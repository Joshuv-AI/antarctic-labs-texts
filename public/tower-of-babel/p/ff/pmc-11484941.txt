ABSTRACT
The reconstruction of MR images has always been a challenging inverse problem in medical imaging. Acceleration of MR scanning is of great importance for clinical research and cutting-edge applications. One of the primary efforts to achieve this is using compressed sensing (CS) theory. The CS aims to reconstruct MR images using a small number of sampled data in k-space. The CS-MRI techniques face challenges, including the potential loss of fine structure and increased computational complexity. We introduce a novel framework based on a regularized sparse recovery problem and a sharpening step to improve the CS-MRI approaches regarding fine structure loss under high acceleration factors. This problem is solved via the Half Quadratic Splitting (HQS) approach. The inverse problem for reconstructing MR images is converted into two distinct sub-problems, each of which can be solved separately. One key feature of the proposed approach is the replacement of one sub-problem with a denoiser. This regularization assists the optimization of the Smoothed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\ell}_{0}$$\end{document} (SL0) norm in escaping local minimums and enhances its precision. The proposed method consists of smoothing, feature modification, and Smoothed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\ell}_{0}$$\end{document} cost function optimization. The proposed approach improves the SL0 algorithm for MRI reconstruction without complicating it. The convergence of the proposed approach is illustrated analytically. The experimental results show an acceptable performance of the proposed method compared to the network-based approaches.

Introduction

Over the past few decades, Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) has become a leading method for diagnosing and investigating various diseases. Additionally, its avoidance of harmful rays like X-rays helps minimize damage to the body1. MRI employs radio frequency waves to non-invasively evaluate soft tissues’ structure, function, and morphology2. However, unlike other clinical imaging methods like computed tomography (CT) or ultrasound, MRI faces the challenge of slow data acquisition. Fast MRI techniques are crucial to accelerate data acquisition while maintaining high-quality image reconstruction. In recent decades, compressed sensing (CS) has emerged as a popular and successful strategy for rapidly reconstructing MR images. Compressed Sensing MRI (CS-MRI), as an effective approach, enables a much lower data sampling rate than the Nyquist rate without significant degradation in image quality3–5.

Compressed sensing exploits the fact that an image is usually sparse in some appropriate transform domains6,7, provided that the acquisition process adheres to principles of incoherence. In this context, incoherence is crucial for disrupting the conventional regularity in sampling patterns, enabling reconstruction methods based on sparsity8. The concept of “incoherent” refers to the deliberate design of sampling patterns when acquiring MRI data. This term indicates a deliberate lack of correlation or regularity in how the data is sampled. The goal is to deviate from the typical systematic or predictable sampling patterns. It facilitates reconstructing high-quality images from a reduced set of non-redundant samples. Within most early CS-MRI methodologies, the model consists of two main components: a data fidelity term in the k-space domain and a regularization term of sparse representation in different domains, such as finite-difference9and wavelet transforms10. However, a key disadvantage of these classical methods is the noticeable loss of structures and details in the reconstructed images, especially when dealing with high under-sampling rates.

Since the introduction of sparse image representation, a significant focus has been on reconstructing MR images using CS. Numerous approaches have been developed to address the inverse problem within this framework11. One such method is the Fast Multiclass Dictionary Learning (FDLCP) algorithm12, which optimally determines a dictionary matrix for image reconstruction. FDLCP’s advantage is its unique approach to multiclass dictionary learning, enabling efficient reconstruction from under-sampled data. However, it struggles with computational complexity and may not generalize well to different types of MR images. Iterative thresholding techniques, including the Smoothing Fast Iterative Shrinkage-Thresholding Algorithm (SFISTA)13and Projected Fast ISTA (PFISTA)14, have also significantly contributed to solving the inverse problem, particularly in the context of CS-MRI.

In recent years, deep neural networks have achieved notable achievements in various domains, such as image classification15, segmentation16, denoising17, and high-speed MRI. The primary advantage of deep learning methods is their ability to automatically learn complex features and patterns from data, making them highly effective for MRI reconstruction. For instance, the Iterative Feature Refinement Network (IFR-Net)18, operating within the Iterative Feature Refinement Compressed Sensing (IFR-CS) model19, integrates insights from parameter learning within the Alternating Directions Method of Multipliers Net (ADMM-Net)20and learning convolutional neural network (CNN) weights. This approach is advantageous due to its ability to refine features iteratively, leading to high-quality reconstructions. However, the need for large amounts of training data and the high computational cost are significant disadvantages. In21, Wu et al. presented the K-space Transformer MRI Reconstruction Model (KTMR), an approach designed to reconstruct magnetic resonance (MR) images from under-sampled k-space data. Drawing upon the Swin Transformer architecture, KTMR provides an effective solution for generating fully sampled MR images across diverse anatomical structures. Combining the advantages of CNNs and Transformer models, their method offers an end-to-end framework that seamlessly transforms under-sampled images into their fully sampled counterparts22.

The main advantage of SL0 is its computational efficiency, being 2-3 times faster than Basis Pursuit (BP) while maintaining comparable or superior accuracy24. Proposed method

Various methods have been developed to obtain the sparse solution. The SL0 method has emerged as a fast and accurate approach. This study aims to improve the SL0 algorithm, enhancing its precision while maintaining low complexity. In pursuit of this objective, we integrate a smoothness condition into the algorithmic framework to minimize the risk of converging to local minimums. However, this smoothness may unintentionally remove structural elements for analysis. This side effect is controlled by two techniques: a decreasing factor reduces the smoothness level in each iteration, and a “sharpening” operator is used to counterbalance the loss. This approach ensures that delicate structures, especially edges crucial for MR image analysis, are returned to the original image during each iteration. The SL0 tries to optimize the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\ell}_{0}$$\end{document} norm directly by gradually decreasing the parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:$$\end{document}; hence, there is a challenge of trapping in a local minimum in this approach. This combination can effectively consider this issue. Therefore, we propose the following Image Smoothness-regularized SL0 (ISRSL0) problem.4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{ISRSL}}0:\quad \hat {{\varvec{s}}}=\mathop {argmin}\limits_{{\mathbf{s}}} {\text{~}}{\left\| {{{\mathbf{\Psi }}^{ - 1}}{\mathbf{s}}} \right\|_{0\sigma }}+{\lambda _{\text{s}}}\,{\text{smoothness}}\left( {\mathbf{s}} \right)\quad s.t\quad {\mathbf{y}}={\mathbf{As}}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\lambda\:}_{\text{s}}$$\end{document} is the regularization parameter that controls the balance between smoothness and sparseness, we recast the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\text{I}\text{S}\text{R}\text{S}\text{L}0$$\end{document} problem in the following mathematical form5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{ISRSL}}0:\quad \hat {{\varvec{s}}}=\mathop {argmin}\limits_{{\mathbf{s}}} {\text{J}}\left( {\mathbf{s}} \right),\quad {{\text{J}}_{IRSL0}}\left( {\mathbf{s}} \right)={\left\| {{{\mathbf{\Psi }}^{ - 1}}{\mathbf{s}}} \right\|_{0\sigma }}+{\lambda _{\text{s}}}\,{\text{smoothness}}\left( {\mathbf{s}} \right)+{{\text{\varvec{\updelta}}}_c}\left( {\mathbf{s}} \right)~$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\updelta\:}}_{c}\left(\mathbf{s}\right)$$\end{document} represents the subspace of measurement data \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:C=\{\mathbf{s}\in\:{R}^{n}|\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}\}$$\end{document}, defined as6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\delta _C}\left( {\mathbf{s}} \right)=\left\{ {\begin{array}{*{20}{l}} 0&{{\text{if}}\quad {\mathbf{s}} \in C} \\ {+\infty }&{{\text{if}}\quad {\mathbf{s}} \notin C} \end{array}} \right.$$\end{document}

The smoothness regularization can be any measure function such as total variation or deep image prior. We don’t define this cost function implicitly because the proposed optimization approach considers its effect in a denoising step.

The HQS approach28 optimizes the ISRSL0 cost function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\text{J}\left(\mathbf{s}\right)$$\end{document}. The HQS method is a well-known technique used in different applications of inverse problems. It divides the minimization function into two components: one related to the model space and a regularization term achieved by introducing an auxiliary variable.

In the HQS method, an auxiliary variable \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbf{z}\in\:{R}^{n}$$\end{document} is introduced to formulate the minimization problem as follows:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat {{\varvec{s}}},{\text{~}}{\mathbf{\hat {z}}}=\mathop {argmin}\limits_{{\mathbf{s}}} {{\text{J}}_{HQS}}\left( {\mathbf{s}} \right),\quad s.t.\quad {\mathbf{z}}={\mathbf{s}}{{\text{J}}_{HQS}}\left( {{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{z}}} \right)={\left\| {{{\mathbf{\Psi }}^{ - 1}}{\mathbf{s}}} \right\|_{0\sigma }}+{\lambda _{\text{s}}}\,{\text{smoothness}}\left( {\mathbf{z}} \right)+{{\text{\varvec{\updelta}}}_c}\left( {\mathbf{s}} \right)$$\end{document}

To solve this problem, the penalty method is employed29, resulting in the following expression:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat {{\varvec{s}}},{\text{~}}{\mathbf{\hat {z}}}=\mathop {argmin}\limits_{{\mathbf{s}}} {\text{J}}_{{HQS}}^{p}\left( {\mathbf{s}} \right),\quad s.t.\quad {\mathbf{z}}={\mathbf{s}}{\text{J}}_{{HQS}}^{p}\left( {{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{z}}} \right)={\left\| {{{\mathbf{\Psi }}^{ - 1}}{\mathbf{s}}} \right\|_{0\sigma }}+{\lambda _{\text{s}}}{\text{smoothness}}\left( {\mathbf{z}} \right)+{{\text{\varvec{\updelta}}}_c}\left( {\mathbf{s}} \right)+\frac{1}{{2{\lambda _p}}}{\mathbf{s}} - {\mathbf{z}}_{2}^{2}$$\end{document}

Where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\lambda\:}_{\text{p}}$$\end{document} is the penalty parameter. This parameter controls deviation from the condition \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{s}$$\end{document}. The HQS approach converts the minimization problem (8) into two disjoint subproblems as follows:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\{ {\begin{array}{*{20}{c}} {{\text{}}{{\mathbf{z}}_{k+1}}=\mathop {argmin}\limits_{{\mathbf{z}}} {\text{J}}_{{HQS}}^{p}\left( {{{\mathbf{s}}_{\text{k}}},{\mathbf{z}}} \right){\text{~~}}} \\ {{{\mathbf{s}}_{k+1}}=\mathop {argmin}\limits_{{\mathbf{s}}} {\text{J}}_{{HQS}}^{p}\left( {{\mathbf{s}},{{\mathbf{z}}_{k+1}}} \right)} \end{array}} \right.$$\end{document}

These steps can be simplified into the following mathematical form, each term with an explicit concept.10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\{ {\begin{array}{*{20}{c}} {{{\mathbf{z}}_{k+1}}=\mathop {argmin}\limits_{{\mathbf{z}}} {\lambda _{\text{s}}}{\text{smoothness}}\left( {\mathbf{z}} \right)+\frac{1}{{2{\lambda _p}}}\left\| {{{\mathbf{s}}_k} - {\mathbf{z}}} \right\|_{2}^{2}{\text{~~~~~~}}} \\ {{{\mathbf{s}}_{k+1}}=\mathop {argmin}\limits_{{\mathbf{s}}} ~{{\left\| {{{\mathbf{\Psi }}^{ - 1}}{\mathbf{s}}} \right\|}_{0\sigma }}+\frac{1}{{2{\lambda _p}}}\left\| {{\mathbf{s}} - {{\mathbf{z}}_{k+1}}} \right\|_{2}^{2}+{{\text{\varvec{\updelta}}}_c}\left( {\mathbf{s}} \right)} \end{array}} \right.$$\end{document}

The first subproblem is dedicated to implementing the smoothing condition, while the second reconstructs the image through SL0 optimization within subspace \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:C$$\end{document}. The following subsections investigate these optimization problems.

First subproblem: denoising and sharpening

A more precise evaluation reveals that the first subproblem of (10) can be expressed as the denoising of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathbf{s}}_{k}$$\end{document}. This parameter is a decreasing sequence. The small \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:g\left(k\right)$$\end{document} is equivalent to decreasing the effect of the smoothness regularization in the optimization problem, eliminating less essential image details and structures. The proposed value of the parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:g\left(k\right)$$\end{document} is proportional to the parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:$$\end{document} in the SL0 algorithm. As the iterations progress, the smoothing effect gradually diminishes, leaving only the SL0 term in the final stages. In the sparse recovery problem, decreasing the sampling rate or the ratio \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\frac{m}{n}$$\end{document} highlights the challenge of local minima. To consider this issue in the proposed algorithm, the denoiser parameter is inversely related to the sampling rate. Our experimental results demonstrate that a smoothing coefficient of 0.08 has a minimal impact on reconstruction quality, with variations within the range of 0.01 to 0.15, leading to a PSNR standard deviation of 0.1dB. This specific range of smoothing coefficients was meticulously chosen to balance the compromise between the intensity of smoothing applied and the preservation of image details. It would enhance the clarity and accuracy of the final output.

However, due to the inherent smoothing effect, the denoising process can result in a loss of relevant data, including edges and fine details. To rectify this issue and prevent the permanent removal of such information by the denoiser, the “sharpening” operator is employed. This operator is pivotal in restoring critical image components, ensuring that only the fundamental elements are reintegrated into the final image.

The main goal is to preserve essential image structures that are inadvertently removed during denoising. The Sharpening function is employed to achieve this, which evaluates local contrast, brightness, and correlation between pre-smoothing and post-smoothing images31. This modifier facilitates the recovery of valuable components and acts like a fine sieve in image processing. Over iterations, a diminishing coefficient gradually reduces the sharpening effect.

Second subproblem: gradient descent and projection

The second optimization step in (10) is a regularized version of the SL0 algorithm. Inspired by the SL0 optimization, this problem is iteratively solved in that each iteration contains the following two steps: gradient descent and projection.14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\{ {\begin{array}{*{20}{l}} {{\mathbf{s}}=\arg \mathop {\hbox{min} }\limits_{{\mathbf{s}}} {{\left\| {~{{\mathbf{\Psi }}^{ - 1}}{\mathbf{s}}} \right\|}_{0\sigma }}+\frac{1}{{2{\lambda _p}}}\left\| {{\mathbf{s}} - {{\mathbf{z}}_{k+1}}} \right\|_{2}^{2}} \\ {{\text{Project}}\,\mathbf{s}\,{\text{onto}}\,{\text{the}}\,{\text{feasible}}\,{\text{set}}\,C~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~} \end{array}} \right.$$\end{document}

According to the above simplification, the projection step is expressed as:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{s}} \leftarrow {\mathbf{s}} - {{\mathbf{F}}^T}\left( {{{\mathbf{M}}^T}{\mathbf{MFs}} - {{\mathbf{M}}^T}{\mathbf{y}}} \right)$$\end{document}

The measurement matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbf{M}$$\end{document} can be defined as the sampling pattern or Mask. According to (18), the following simplification can be concluded to reduce the computational complexity.21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathbf{M}}^T}{\mathbf{y}}={{\mathbf{M}}^T}{\mathbf{MFs}}={\mathbf{Mask}} \odot {\mathbf{Fs}}$$\end{document}

where Mask signifies the sampling pattern, comprising binary values (zero and one), and ʘ indicates element-wise multiplication. Based on the mentioned note, the projection can be expressed in the following form.22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{s}} \leftarrow {\mathbf{s}} - {{\mathbf{F}}^T}\left( {{\mathbf{Mask}} \odot {\mathbf{Fs}} - {\mathbf{\hat {y}}}} \right)$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{\mathbf{y}}\in\:{R}^{n}$$\end{document} is introduced as the zero-padded \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbf{y}$$\end{document}. This is the Fourier transform of the original image, where the acquired coefficients by the imaging process are known, and the remaining coefficients are set to zero. The projection expression (22) does not need any matrix inversion.

IRSL0 algorithm

The IRSL0 algorithm consists of two loops. The outer loop is designed to decrease the parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:$$\end{document} gradually to avoid being trapped in a local minimum. The outer loop will continue until the condition \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\upsigma\:}>{{\upsigma\:}}_{min}$$\end{document} is satisfied. The denoiser and sharpening operations are in this loop. The inner loop employs the Gradient Projection algorithm, encompassing two fundamental components: the gradient descent and projection stage. In the gradient descent step, the objective is to minimize the cost function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\:\|{\varvec{\Psi\:}}^{-1}\mathbf{s}\|}_{0\sigma\:}+\frac{1}{{2\lambda\:}_{p}}{\|\mathbf{s}-{\mathbf{z}}_{k+1}\|}_{2}^{2}\:$$\end{document}for a specified \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:$$\end{document} value. The inner loop is repeated in several iterations (L = 2). Convergence within this inner loop isn’t a strict requirement. The proposed IRSL0 algorithm to reconstruct an MRI image is illustrated in Algorithm 1.

Convergence analysis

In this section, the convergence of the proposed approach is analyzed. In the first step, the convergence of the proposed approach is investigated for a fixed parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:$$\end{document} in the following Lemma.

Lemma 1

The sequence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\left\{{J}_{k}={J}_{HQS}^{p}\left({\mathbf{s}}_{k},{\mathbf{z}}_{k}\right)\right\}}_{k=1}^{\infty\:}$$\end{document} generated by the ISRSL0 algorithm with a fixed parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:$$\end{document} converges.

Proof

We calculate the following difference23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\text{J}}_{\text{k}}} - {J_{k+1}}=J_{{HQS}}^{p}\left( {{{\mathbf{s}}_k},{z_k}} \right) - J_{{HQS}}^{p}\left( {{{\mathbf{s}}_{k+1}},{{\mathbf{z}}_{k+1}}} \right)=\left( {J_{{HQS}}^{p}\left( {{{\mathbf{s}}_k},{{\mathbf{z}}_{k+1}}} \right) - J_{{HQS}}^{p}\left( {{{\mathbf{s}}_{k+1}},{{\mathbf{z}}_{k+1}}} \right)} \right)+\left( {J_{{HQS}}^{p}\left( {{{\mathbf{s}}_k},{{\mathbf{z}}_k}} \right) - J_{{HQS}}^{p}\left( {{{\mathbf{s}}_k},{{\mathbf{z}}_{k+1}}} \right)} \right)$$\end{document}

Two optimization subproblems (9) conclude that two ter on the right side of the above difference are non-negative. Hence, the sequence is decreasing, i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{J}_{HQS}^{p}\left({\mathbf{s}}_{k+1},{\mathbf{z}}_{k+1}\right)\le\:{J}_{HQS}^{p}\left({\mathbf{s}}_{k},{\mathbf{z}}_{k}\right)$$\end{document}. Since all terms in the cost function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{J}_{HQS}^{p}$$\end{document} are bounded, convergence is obtained.

In the ISRSL0 algorithm, a decreasing sequence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:$$\end{document} is used, and the denoiser coefficient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:g\left(k\right)$$\end{document} is directly proportional to the parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:$$\end{document}. Hence, the denoiser coefficient gradually tends to 0, and we can obtain the following approximation for small \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:$$\end{document}:24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$J_{{HQS}}^{p}\left( {{{\mathbf{s}}_{k+1}},{{\mathbf{z}}_{k+1}}} \right) \cong J_{{HQS}}^{p}\left( {{{\mathbf{s}}_{k+1}},{{\mathbf{s}}_{k+1}}} \right)$$\end{document}

Now, according to the definition of the ISRSL0 cost function and the auxiliary variable \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbf{z}$$\end{document} in the HQS approach, we obtain the following inequalities.25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${J_{ISRSL0}}\left( {{{\mathbf{s}}_k}} \right)=J_{{HQS}}^{p}\left( {{{\mathbf{s}}_k},{{\mathbf{s}}_k}} \right)\underbrace { \geqslant }_{{(9)}}J_{{HQS}}^{p}\left( {{{\mathbf{s}}_k},{{\mathbf{z}}_{k+1}}} \right)\underbrace { \geqslant }_{{(9)}}J_{{HQS}}^{p}\left( {{{\mathbf{s}}_{k+1}},{{\mathbf{z}}_{k+1}}} \right)\underbrace { \cong }_{{(24)}}J_{{HQS}}^{p}\left( {{{\mathbf{s}}_{k+1}},{{\mathbf{s}}_{k+1}}} \right)={J_{ISRSL0}}\left( {{{\mathbf{s}}_{k+1}}} \right)$$\end{document}

The above result shows that the sequence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{J}_{ISRL0}\left({\mathbf{s}}_{k}\right)$$\end{document} is a decreasing sequence. This result can also be obtained via Lemma 1 and (24). Since the smoothed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\ell}_{0}$$\end{document} norm and the regularization term are finite, the sequence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{J}_{ISRL0}\left({\mathbf{s}}_{k}\right)$$\end{document} converges.

It’s important to note that this proof doesn’t guarantee global convergence. Since the cost function, especially the smoothed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\ell}_{0}$$\end{document} norm, is non-convex, local minimums remain challenging. Simulation results show the improvement of the proposed approach compared to the SL0 algorithm.

MRI sampling masks

The CS significantly reduces scan time for various MRI applications by minimizing the number of samples required for image reconstruction. These samples are determined through specific sampling masks, with three distinct types examined: Cartesian, Pseudo-radial, and spiral paths. The Cartesian sampling mask is the most frequently employed choice due to its robustness in data acquisition, practical implementation, and ease of reconstruction. Non-Cartesian sampling masks, such as radial and spiral trajectories, can enhance the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) since the center of k-space sampling predominantly influences SNR. However, it’s essential to note that the k-space density is non-uniform, with crucial image information concentrated in the low-frequency components situated at the center of the k-space rather than the high-frequency components at the grid’s periphery.

Radial acquisition paths exhibit reduced susceptibility to motion artifacts compared to Cartesian trajectories. In our experiments, the Pseudo-radial mask is predominantly employed, given its widespread use in k-space sampling. Figure 4illustrates this research’s array of sampling masks33.

The first criterion, Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), is computed as:26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{PSNR}}=10{\log _{10}}\left( {\frac{{{{255}^2}}}{{MSE\left( {{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{\hat {x}}}} \right)}}} \right)$$\end{document}

MSE represents the mean squared error between the reconstructed image \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbf{x}$$\end{document} and the original image \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\stackrel{\sim}{\mathbf{x}}$$\end{document}.

The second criterion, High-Frequency Error Norm (HFEN), evaluates high-frequency image components and is determined by:27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$HFEN={H_{high}}\left( {\mathbf{x}} \right) - {H_{high}}{\left( {{\mathbf{\hat {x}}}} \right)_2}$$\end{document}

In this equation, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{H}_{high}$$\end{document} denotes the Gaussian high-pass filter.

The third criterion, the Structural Similarity Index (SSIM), assesses structural information preservation. It is calculated as follows:28\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$SSIM=\frac{{\left( {2{\mu _{{\mathbf{\hat {x}}}}}{\mu _{\mathbf{x}}}+{c_1}} \right)\left( {2{\sigma _{{\mathbf{x\hat {x}}}}}+{c_2}} \right)}}{{\left( {\mu _{{{\mathbf{\hat {x}}}}}^{2}+\mu _{{\mathbf{x}}}^{2}+{c_1}} \right)\left( {\sigma _{{\mathbf{x}}}^{2}+\sigma _{{{\mathbf{\hat {x}}}}}^{2}+{c_2}} \right)}}$$\end{document}

Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mu\:$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:$$\end{document} represent the mean and variance of the signal, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{1}$$\end{document}​ and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{2}$$\end{document}​ are constant coefficients. Together, these three criteria offer comprehensive insight into the quality of reconstructed images.

Discussion & conclusion

This research focused on achieving the primary objective of reconstruction methods: delivering the best results with minimal complexity. To this end, we proposed a systematic process of enhancing the classic SL0 algorithm. A key element in our approach was the incorporation of image smoothness regularization, which was crucial in addressing challenges related to local minima encountered during the algorithm’s execution. The proposed cost function was optimized by applying the HQS method. The proposed algorithm operates through three fundamental stages: Denoising, Sharpening, and SL0 Cost Function Optimization. Each step preserved essential features and guided the image away from local minima toward the global minimum.

We adjusted the algorithm’s parameters to ensure a gradual reduction in smoothing over iterations. Simultaneously, the influence of the Sharpening was gradually diminished until only the original SL0 algorithm prevailed. Furthermore, we demonstrated the convergence of the algorithm, analytically. The results show the algorithm’s success and superiority over classic reconstruction approaches. Notably, the experimental results demonstrated comparable performance to network-based algorithms, with the added advantages of simplicity and low computational complexity. The results and the comprehensive evaluation against various measurement criteria indicate a marked improvement over the original SL0 algorithm. The proposed approach will be combined with the deep model to improve the reconstruction performance in future work.
