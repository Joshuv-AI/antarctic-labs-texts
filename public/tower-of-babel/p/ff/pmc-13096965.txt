A decade of discovery: Leveraging genomic data in the ABCD study to illuminate adolescent neurodevelopment
Authors: Leanna M. Hernandez, Robert Loughnan, William Cheng, Le Zhang, Chun Chieh Fan, Arpana Agrawal, ABCD Genetics Workgroup
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study provides an unprecedented resource for developmental genomics, offering genetic data for more than 11,000 youth, as well as extensive longitudinal behavioral, neuroimaging, environmental, and real-world digital phenotyping measures. In this review, we summarize the scope and utility of the ABCD genetics data and provide a review of ABCD genetics publications over the past decade. We first describe the genetic data available through the consortium, including pipelines used for genotyping, imputation, and ancestry information, and review commonly used methods for admixture estimation, relatedness, genome-wide association studies, and polygenic score analyses. Key methodological considerations are highlighted to guide analyses, with an emphasis on addressing population structure to promote reproducibility and ensure validity across diverse populations. We then synthesize findings from the first decade of ABCD research leveraging measured genetic data, highlighting progress in several key domains: neurodevelopment, substance use, cognition, sleep, impulsivity and externalizing behaviors, and psychosis. Finally, we discuss key challenges and opportunities including the need for robust analytic strategies to maximize use of admixed and cross-ancestry genetic data to ensure that findings are generalizable to the diverse ancestral distribution of the U.S. population, application of advanced statistical methods to model gene x environment interactions, and the use of real-world digital phenotyping data. Together, these efforts will enhance the translational relevance of ABCD genetics data, enabling identification of genetic and environmental factors that shape adolescent neurodevelopment, long-term health, and wellbeing.

Introduction

Genetic variation contributes to diversity in human traits. Because inherited genetic sequence is established at conception and remains stable throughout life, it precedes and likely influences subsequent brain and behavioral developmental processes, providing opportunities for causal inference. In combination with environmental exposures, the additive and epistatic effects of common inherited genetic variants play an important role in shaping a wide range of neurodevelopmental and behavioral outcomes. A core aim of the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study is to investigate the impact of genetic variation and its interplay with the environment on trajectories of brain development, behavior, and health across the critical transition from childhood to adolescence and early adulthood. This landmark multi-site study has collected in vivo neuroimaging, behavioral, environmental, and genetic data from over 11,000 youth from across the United States (U.S.), beginning at 9–10 years-of-age and following them prospectively for 10 years with repeated assessments. With thousands of deeply phenotyped and genotyped youth, the ABCD Study provides an unparalleled resource for integrating genetics with dense, multi-modal longitudinal data to investigate the biological factors driving adolescent brain and behavioral development.

A growing body of research in psychiatry and neuroscience shows that human brain structure, cognition, and mental health are heritable (e.g., Elliott et al., 2018; Grasby et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2023). Over the past decade, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have transformed our understanding of the genetic architecture of complex human traits, demonstrating that they primarily arise from the additive effects of hundreds of variants across the genome (Visscher et al., 2017). As they are unbiased tests of common variants, GWAS have led to discoveries of novel pathways to brain and behavior development. Consistent with prior twin studies, these GWAS have also revealed the shared genetic architecture underlying multiple traits and conditions (e.g., Agrawal et al., 2025). Another breakthrough from GWAS is the understanding of the polygenic nature of variation underlying most complex traits (Boyle et al., 2017). Polygenic scores (PGS), which use effect sizes from a well-powered discovery GWAS to weight alleles in a separate sample and aggregate the effects of common genetic variants into a single quantitative measure, now provide a convenient index of individual-level genetic liability, relative to a given population, for a given trait (Schwarzerova et al., 2024). These PGS have dramatically enhanced the portability of GWAS discoveries, which require massive cohorts, often with shallow phenotyping, into deeply phenotyped but smaller, longitudinal samples, leading to increases in our understanding of how genetic liabilities shape health across the lifespan (Allegrini et al., 2022). However, the vast majority of both GWAS discovery and PGS research has been conducted in adult populations (Posner et al., 2021), leaving largely unknown the extent of genetic continuity and specificity underlying childhood and adult brain and behavioral traits, the predictive accuracy of PGS derived from adult discovery GWAS for childhood phenotypes, and how genetic liability shapes and is shaped by environmental factors to inform developmental trajectories.

To this end, the extraordinary scale, diversity, and systematic longitudinal follow-up afforded by the ABCD study provide an unprecedented opportunity to study how genetic variation influences adolescent brain development and emerging psychiatric and health outcomes. The study’s school-based recruitment across diverse U.S. communities supports the analysis of ancestry, environmental heterogeneity, and developmental timing using a longitudinal framework (Casey et al., 2018, Garavan et al., 2018). Importantly, the inclusion of twins and siblings through targeted oversampling enables family-based genetically informed designs that help disentangle shared family and environmental influences (Garavan et al., 2018). Further, the comprehensive environmental data available, including social and economic factors, indices of built and natural environment, and chemical exposures enables research that presents exciting avenues to uncover the complex interplay between genes and environment in creating variability in the brain and behavior. At the same time, interpretation of genetic findings in ABCD should also consider the composition of the cohort. ABCD was designed to approximate the sociodemographic distribution of the U.S. (Garavan et al., 2018); accordingly, participants of European genetic ancestry constitute the largest ancestry group (Fan et al., 2023a), and the sample was not designed to over-sample clinical or high-risk populations. These features reflect the study’s population-based design and shape the types of genetic questions that can be addressed, the analytic strategies required, and the contexts in which findings may generalize.

To facilitate the broad use of ABCD genetics data by the research community, in this 10- year review we first summarize the scope and structure of the genetic resources available, then consider key methodological issues that arise in their analysis. We draw on emerging findings from studies using measured genetic data to illustrate the promise of integrating genetic information with ABCD’s rich longitudinal phenotyping. Finally, we discuss ongoing challenges and future opportunities for leveraging this unique dataset to advance understanding of the genetic architecture of adolescent brain and behavioral development.

Overview of genetic data in ABCD

Biospecimen collection in the ABCD study included both blood and saliva samples for genotyping. Details of the genotyping protocol have been described previously (Fan et al., 2023a; Uban et al., 2018) and are available on the ABCD Data Documentation website (https://docs.abcdstudy.org). Briefly, following collection, samples were shipped to the Rutgers University Cell and DNA Repository (RUCDR, later rebranded as Sampled), where DNA extraction and genotyping were performed. Genotyping was performed using the Smokescreen Genotyping Array (Baurley et al., 2016), which is built on an Affymetrix GWAS backbone covering over 700,000 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), along with > 30,000 SNPs associated with substance and nicotine use (National Institute on Drug Abuse, 2016). Blood and saliva samples were genotyped separately, and genotype calling was conducted on raw intensities using the Axiom Analysis Suite. Genotyping was performed in nine batches, and data were aligned to the human genome reference build GRCh37/hg19 (Fan et al., 2023a). Batches were then merged, and quality control of genotype calls was performed at RUCDR and by the ABCD Data Analysis, Informatics, and Resource Center (DAIRC), including filtering for call rate, sample-level missingness (20%), variant-level missingness (10%), and excessive or discordant relatedness. In Data Release 6.0, high-quality genotyping data are available for 11,664 unique participants, covering 515,279 variants. These data are provided through the NIH Brain Development Cohorts (NBDC) Data Hub, with additional files indicating sample IDs that did not pass quality control and batch assignment for each participant.

Genotype data were imputed to the TOPMed-r2 reference panel, which includes over 400 million single-nucleotide variants identified through whole-genome sequencing of individuals from diverse ancestral backgrounds (Taliun et al., 2021). Imputation was performed using the Michigan/TOPMed imputation Server (v1.7.3) with minimac4 (v1.0.3), genome build GRCh38/hg38 and phasing performed using Eagle v2.4. All imputed variants were included in release data along with imputation quality scores to allow users to apply their own quality control metrics. Imputed Variant Call Format (VCF) files include data for 11,664 participants across ∼260 million variants, with post-imputation quality metrics available through the NBDC Data Hub.

ABCD genetic data, along with derived measures such as genetic principal components (capturing the major axes of genetic ancestry) and relatedness variables (ancestry-adjusted kinship coefficients), are available to approved researchers through the NBDC portal. Access is granted to qualified researchers at NIH-recognized research institutions who obtain an approved Data Use Certificate (DUC) and complete required data security training. Additional permissions are required for researchers intending to report findings specifically from American Indian/Alaska Native (AI/AN) participants. Data access is renewed annually and researchers must adhere to the conditions of the approved DUC(s) and follow the NIH Genomic Data Sharing Policy (National Institutes of Health, 2024), which provides requirements on secure handling of genomic data.

Analytical approaches and considerations

The analytic flexibility afforded by the ABCD genetic data enables a wide range of approaches but also introduces important methodological decisions that can substantially influence results. Given the rapidly evolving methodological landscape and diversity of analysis strategies appropriate for the ABCD data, here we highlight common analytic approaches, their strengths and limitations, and practical considerations for their application in ABCD.

Ancestry and population stratification

The ABCD cohort is ethnically and ancestrally diverse by design (Garavan et al., 2018). While ethnicity refers to the cultural, linguistic, and social factors that contribute to an individual’s identity, ancestry refers to the geographic origins of a person’s ancestors, which due to evolutionary mechanisms, contributes to patterns of linkage disequilibrium (i.e., correlations between neighboring genetic variants). Accounting for ancestry is critical in genetic analyses because variation in allele frequencies across population subgroups can lead to confounding if the trait of interest also varies between those groups – a phenomenon known as population stratification. These differences in allele frequencies across ancestries are normative features of human diversity and reflect migration, historical population bottlenecks and isolation, and genetic drift (Auton et al., 2015, Bergström et al., 2020, Novembre et al., 2008, Rosenberg et al., 2002). Failure to fully account for population stratification in genetic analyses can lead to spurious genotype-phenotype associations that are explained by differences in demographic history, rather than true biological relationships between genetic variability and a trait or disease (Cardon and Palmer, 2003, Freedman et al., 2004, Hamer and Sirota, 2000). One widely used approach to account for population stratification is to compute ancestry principal components (PCs), which capture broad patterns of genetic similarity across individuals that can then be used as covariates in statistical models (Patterson et al., 2006, Price et al., 2006). Such analyses involve comparing the genomes of ABCD participants with reference genomes of individuals from various geographic regions, such as the 1000 Genomes Project (1KG). Early releases of the ABCD data included ancestry PCs computed in PLINK (Purcell et al., 2007) by merging ABCD genotype data with data from the 1000 Genomes reference panel (Auton et al., 2015). However, as ABCD also includes genomic data from family members, such as twins and other siblings close in age, and other potential unknown sources of relatedness (due to sampling within schools that multiple second degree relatives may attend), ancestry components were re-estimated using PC-AiR – a method that robustly estimates population structure in the presence of known or cryptic relatedness (i.e., relatedness unknown to study investigators) (Conomos et al., 2015). Further details of PC-AiR settings used to calculate ancestry estimates, as well as file names containing ancestry information can be found on the ABCD Data Documentation website (https://docs.abcdstudy.org).

Admixture

A substantial portion of the ABCD sample is admixed, meaning that their genomes contain DNA segments inherited from multiple ancestries, representing the diversity inherent in contemporary America. This admixture presents exciting opportunities for genetic discoveries since admixed genomes show ancestral heterogeneity within an individual. Ancestral PCs may not fully capture this heterogeneity, leading to potential residual confounding. Several tools are available to glean insights from admixed genomes, including methods to define ancestry at local genomic segments (e.g., RFMix (Maples et al., 2013)), perform admixture mapping to identify loci associated with ancestry-related differences in diseases/traits (e.g., ADMIXMAP; H3ABioNet tools (Hoggart et al., 2004; McKeigue, 2005; Mulder et al., 2016)), and conduct admixture-aware GWAS (e.g., Tractor (Atkinson et al., 2021)). Family structure and relatedness

While it is important to model genetic ancestry to account for potential confounding at the population level, it is equally important to account for genetic relationships at the family level, which can introduce non-independence and bias in association testing if not adequately addressed (Sul et al., 2018). Pair-wise genetic relatedness in the ABCD sample was estimated using PC-Relate, which generates a genetic relationship matrix based on kinship coefficients that are adjusted for ancestry using PCs derived from PC-AiR (Conomos et al., 2015). Available data include identity by descent probabilities, indicating the probability that any pair of individuals has inherited one or two alleles at a given locus from the same ancestor, and pair-wise genetic relatedness representing monozygotic twins, dizygotic twins and siblings, 2nd-, 3rd-, and 4th- degree relatives (expected genetic relatedness values 1.0, 0.5, 0.25, 0.125, and 0.0625, respectively), as well as more distant genetic relationships. Because these values are estimated from genome-wide SNP data rather than observed pedigrees, sampling variability and deviations from expected population-allele frequency assumptions can result in kinship coefficients slightly below 0 or above 1 (observed range −0.125–1.025; (Conomos et al., 2015; Fan et al., 2023a; Manichaikul et al., 2010)). Some of these relationships were known and expected. Specifically, the ABCD cohort includes 838 monozygotic twins, 1288 dizygotic twins, and 1542 non-twin siblings (derived based on genetic information), in addition to more distant relatives identified through genomic relatedness. These relatedness estimates allow researchers to control for family relationships, either by selecting subsets of unrelated participants or preferably, by using mixed-model approaches that account for correlated observations, to ensure the accuracy of genetic findings from the ABCD study. Statistical methods that range from simpler robust variance estimation to explicit inclusion of genetic relatedness matrices can account for these clustered data. Objective determination of genetic relatedness of close relatives is also of particular relevance for the significant embedded twin sample which forms over 10% of the entire ABCD sample (Iacono et al., 2018), enabling analyses that parse within- and between-sibship effects.

Genome wide association studies (GWAS)

Many complex traits have additive polygenic genetic architecture, meaning that thousands of genetic variants across the genome act in an additive fashion to influence variation in phenotypic outcomes. GWAS are a widely-used method to identify these statistical relationships between common genetic variants (i.e., SNPs) and a particular trait or disease. GWAS typically leverage large-scale imputed genetic data in thousands of individuals to test associations between a trait and millions of SNPs across the genome, using either linear or logistic regression at each individual genetic locus. Because GWAS involve testing millions of variants, a widely-used genome-wide significance threshold of p < 5 × 10−8 is applied to account for multiple testing (Chen et al., 2021), and associations are interpreted as regions of the genome that may contain trait-influencing genes. Recent GWAS have shown that many complex brain-based phenotypes, including brain surface area and cortical thickness (Grasby et al., 2020, van der Meer et al., 2020), as well as psychiatric and neurodevelopmental conditions and behavioral traits like sleep, ADHD, and schizophrenia (Agrawal et al., 2025, Austin-Zimmerman et al., 2023, Demontis et al., 2019, Pardiñas et al., 2018) are highly polygenic, highlighting the value of large-scale studies to uncover the genetic architecture of brain and behavioral traits. A critical limitation, however, of these existing GWAS is that they rely predominantly on data collected during adulthood. This limitation presents two opportunities: first, studies like ABCD, especially when meta-analyzed with other large-scale youth cohorts (e.g., MoBA, ALSPAC), could result in discoveries of genetic variation that might uniquely drive youth behavior and disorders. Second, ABCD presents a unique opportunity to evaluate the extent to which discoveries from adult GWAS relate to youth phenotypes.

Another critical limitation of most previous GWAS is their predominant reliance on data from individuals of European ancestry (Martin et al., 2019). Because genetic variants located near one another on a chromosome tend to be inherited together – a phenomenon known as linkage disequilibrium (LD) – and because patterns of LD and allele frequencies differ across ancestral populations (Freedman et al., 2004, Price et al., 2006), genotype–phenotype associations detected in one ancestry may not generalize to another, even when the same genetic mechanism is implicated. As a result, there have been growing efforts to increase the diversity of GWAS, which can improve the generalizability of genomic findings, boost power to detect associations with genetic variants that are rarer in European populations, and aid in fine-mapping by leveraging differences in LD across ancestries.

Although the ABCD sample is small by GWAS standards, this does not preclude GWAS analyses. Indeed, the unique diversity and longitudinal nature of the ABCD data provides researchers with an unparalleled opportunity to perform GWAS of change-over-time in brain and behavioral traits across the adolescent period (Brouwer et al., 2022; Li et al., 2025a; Parekh et al., 2025), for which GWAS samples are limited or non-existent, as well as to test genotype–phenotype associations with measures collected during childhood – a developmental period rarely captured in large-scale GWAS that have historically focused on adults. To maximize validity, GWAS conducted with the ABCD sample should follow current best practices, including performing GWAS separately within each major ancestry group followed by meta-analysis across ancestries, and including appropriate covariates (e.g., ancestry PCs, age, sex, study site) to control for potential confounds.

Polygenic scores (PGS)

A major goal of modern genetics research is to develop robust genetic predictors of liability for health and psychiatric outcomes that can ultimately inform precision medicine approaches aimed at earlier intervention, prevention, and care for those at highest risk (Torkamani et al., 2018). To this end, findings from large-scale GWAS can be leveraged to calculate PGS at the individual level, representing a person’s liability for a disease or trait relative to others in the population. Here, effect sizes for each allele, as reported in GWAS summary statistics, are multiplied by the number of copies of that allele, and the effects are summed across the genome to produce a single additive PGS per individual (Choi et al., 2020, Purcell et al., 2009, Wray et al., 2007). These PGS can then be used to compare genetic liability for a trait or disease between subgroups, as a predictor in regression analyses, or to examine how PGS by environment interactions shape developmental outcomes.

Responsible use of PGS requires careful consideration of ancestry in both the discovery sample (i.e., the GWAS from which effect sizes are obtained) and the target sample (e.g., the ABCD cohort). Differences in LD and allele frequencies across ancestral populations can lead to substantially reduced predictive accuracy when the base and target ancestries are mismatched (Ding et al., 2022, Martin et al., 2019, Schultz et al., 2022). Researchers should therefore verify that the ancestries represented in GWAS summary statistics mirror that of the ABCD subsample used to generate the PGS, applying an ancestry-specific data analysis pipeline, or use emerging methods that model cross-ancestry LD structure in order to improve cross-population prediction (e.g., BridgePRS, PolyPred, PRS-CSx) (Hoggart et al., 2024, Ruan et al., 2022, Weissbrod et al., 2022).

Additionally, the discovery and target cohorts should be free from sample overlap, as such overlap can inflate test statistics and increase the risk of false positives (Ellis et al., 2024). Rigorous harmonization procedures are also needed to ensure that alleles are aligned across datasets and that genome builds are consistent (i.e., that both discovery and target cohorts use the same reference genome – either GRCh37/hg19 or GRCh38/hg38). Finally, as most analyses aim to relate PGS to mental and physical health outcomes, regression models must include appropriate covariates, such as ABCD ancestry PCs and genotyping batch, to reduce residual population structure and confounding (Choi et al., 2020).

Modeling gene x environment interactions

Understanding not only the genetic contributions to traits related to brain development, but also how genes and environments work in tandem, remains a central question in developmental neuroscience. Broadly, there are two types of gene–environment interplay. First, genetic liability may be potentiated/accentuated or suppressed in the presence of certain environments. Such gene–environment interactions (G×E) have been identified for metabolic traits such as obesity, where genetic susceptibility manifests more strongly under certain environmental exposures, such as high sugar or low physical activity contexts (Qi et al., 2012). Similar processes are likely relevant to neurodevelopment, where the expression of genetic propensities related to cortical maturation or connectivity may depend on environmental contexts like socioeconomic status, family stability, or early-life adversity. Second, genetic liability can influence the environments individuals experience, a phenomenon referred to as genotype-environment correlation (rGE). Particularly during childhood, when exposures frequently arise from familial context or niche selection, studies have found that many environments, with the exception of events like natural disasters, show a “heritable” component, reflecting the complex pathways by which our genotypes modify our environmental exposures. Furthermore, such active rGE effects may be enhanced or counterbalanced by passive rGE, in which genotypes are not randomly distributed among trait-relevant environments.

With its dense phenotyping, the ABCD Study provides an unparalleled opportunity to investigate gene–environment interplay. For example, G×E studies applied within this sample are beginning to illuminate how genetic risk may interact with contextual exposures to shape brain developmental trajectories (e.g., Shi et al., 2024). Twin-based designs offer a complementary approach to modelling G×E interactions. Rather than merely partitioning variance into genetic and environmental components, twin G×E models explicitly test how genetic influences on a phenotype vary as a function of measured environments (Briley et al., 2015, Purcell and Sham, 2002 Kendler & Eaves, 1986). These designs provide a critical perspective on developmental plasticity, highlighting contexts in which genetic effects may be amplified or attenuated.

Key findings from the field

A continuously updated catalog of publications authored by ABCD investigators, collaborators, and the wider research community is available through the ABCD Consortium (https://abcd-study.shinyapps.io/abcd-publications/). In this section, we summarize key themes that have emerged from this work, identified through a qualitative review of publications returned by a keyword search for “Genetics” on the ABCD publications website as of September 8, 2025. This search returned 208 publications, of which 153 met the inclusion criteria for this review based on their use of measured genetic data. We focus on studies leveraging individual-level SNP data, excluding classical twin and family-based designs, which are beyond the scope of the current review. Accordingly, the findings summarized here reflect associations based on measured common genetic variation and SNP-based heritability derived from genome-wide data.

Imaging-genetics

Over the last decade, genotype data from the ABCD study have most frequently been used to examine associations between genome-wide polygenic liability for complex traits and phenotypic measures assessed in ABCD participants. Within this area, studies have explored links of PGS for psychiatric, behavioral, and physical health to adolescent brain morphology. These studies suggest that genetic influences on brain structure are already detectable (albeit with small effect sizes) by late childhood. A major strength of the ABCD study is that it was designed to follow a large cohort of children before the onset of risk-related behaviors, providing an opportunity to disentangle the effects of genetic liability for a trait from the consequences of that trait or behavior on brain structure.

Leveraging this design, researchers have used PGS as a causal anchor to examine one of the ABCD consortium’s central aims – assessing the causes and consequences of substance use on adolescent brain development. This work has shown that polygenic risk for substance use disorder show weak associations with variation in brain structure among drug-naïve adolescents, suggesting that previously reported widespread morphological differences in adult substance users likely reflect consequences rather than causes of substance exposure (Kramer et al., 2025). Conversely, PGS for obesity, while modestly correlated with body-mass index in childhood, predict reduced cortical volume and thickness in frontal and temporal brain regions (Morys et al., 2023). The size of these brain regions, in turn, are linked to executive function and impulsivity, suggesting that polygenic liability may contribute to the behavioral disinhibition that is thought to precede excessive weight gain.

In addition to PGS-based approaches, ABCD data have increasingly been integrated into large-scale GWAS aimed at identifying common genetic variants associated with individual differences in brain structure and connectivity. Although the ABCD sample size is considered small by modern GWAS standards, ABCD has served as a primary discovery cohort for studies of cranial vault shape, identifying over 100 independent associations enriched for genes active in neural crest and craniofacial development, including genes previously implicated in the premature fusion of cranial bones in infancy (i.e., craniosynostosis; (Goovaerts et al., 2023, Goovaerts et al., 2025)). A recent lifespan study integrating ABCD with multiple other cohorts performed GWAS of age-related change in brain structure across over 15,000 individuals between the ages of 4 and 99 and identified multiple associations with genes previously implicated in neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative processes, as well as significant genetic correlations with a range of mental health and behavioral traits (Brouwer et al., 2022). Indeed, to increase statistical power, ABCD data have frequently been meta-analyzed with other large population cohorts, including, most commonly, the UK Biobank. In a recent meta-analysis of over 70,000 individuals, ABCD contributed to the identification of over 200 loci associated with subcortical brain volume, as well as significant genetic correlations between subcortical brain volume and psychiatric and neurodevelopmental disorders including ADHD, insomnia, neuroticism, and Parkinson's disease (García-Marín et al., 2024).

ABCD data have also played a key role in the recent generation of population-wide “brain charts” across over 100,000 individuals, which model normative developmental trajectories across the ages of 0–100, and provide a critical resource for understanding brain development and aging processes across the lifespan (Bethlehem et al., 2022). Importantly, centile scores built using these data (indexing an individual’s deviation from age-expected norms) are highly heritable, suggesting that individual differences in brain maturation trajectories are influenced by additive genetic factors.

The broad array of neuroimaging measures available in ABCD has also enabled researchers to evaluate multiple structural MRI metrics to uncover shared and distinct genetic pathways underlying brain surface area and cortical thickness (Kuang et al., 2023, Makowski et al., 2022, Yu et al., 2023), structural brain asymmetry (Zhao et al., 2022), measures of tissue microstructure and cortical folding (Fan et al., 2022, Warrier et al., 2023), as well as multi-modal network-based measures of anatomical similarity across brain regions (Wu et al., 2023). These studies have found overall high concordance between genetic loci associated with neuroimaging traits in children and adults, and enrichment for genes expressed in neurons and glia during pre- and post-natal development. Efforts leveraging ABCD as a replication cohort for GWAS of brain functional connectivity, cortical surface morphometry, white matter microstructure, and brain iron accumulation have also found highly consistent results with adults from the UK Biobank (Chen et al., 2024; Loughnan et al., 2024; Loughnan et al., 2022a; Makowski et al., 2022, Naqvi et al., 2021, Ou et al., 2023, Shadrin et al., 2021, Tissink et al., 2024 S. Wang et al., 2024b; B. Zhao et al., 2021, Zhao et al., 2022). Together, these findings reveal a complex, highly polygenic genetic architecture of the human brain and position ABCD as a key resource for identifying when and how these genomic influences first emerge in childhood.

Genetics and cognitive, behavioral, and psychiatric phenotypes

Genetics findings from the ABCD study have expanded our understanding of how polygenic factors shape cognition, behavior, and psychiatric symptoms. To date, investigations in this area have primarily focused on five major domains: substance use, cognitive processing, sleep, impulsivity and externalizing behavior, and psychosis.

Substance use

Substance use during adolescence is a major public health concern and a key focus of the ABCD study, which aims to disentangle the causes and consequences of substance exposure on brain and behavioral development. Leveraging ABCD’s large, substance-naïve baseline sample, researchers have begun to examine how polygenic liability for substance use contributes to neurodevelopmental vulnerability prior to exposure, providing a causal anchor for interpreting substance-related outcomes. Although overall associations between substance-use liability PGS and brain structure in drug-naïve adolescents appear weak (Kramer et al., 2025), some studies have identified small, region-specific effects for particular alcohol-related PGS (e.g., Hatoum et al., 2021). Importantly, early substance use shows both behavioral and genetic overlap with multiple neurodevelopmental and psychiatric disorders, including ADHD, anxiety, depression, suicide attempt, schizophrenia, and bipolar disorder, underscoring its transdiagnostic nature (Gerring et al., 2024, Toikumo et al., 2024). Similarly, higher PGS for lifetime cannabis use (but not alcohol, tobacco, or cannabis problematic use) have been associated with higher scores on latent factors of general cognitive ability, executive function, and learning/memory (Paul et al., 2022). However, these effects seem to be partially mediated by polygenic load for educational attainment, as including education-related PGS in predictive models attenuates the relationship between lifetime cannabis use PGS and cognition. Longitudinal analyses across ages 9–15 have also revealed that associations between addiction-related PGS and impulsivity change dynamically throughout adolescence. While nicotine/tobacco PGS consistently predict impulsivity across middle childhood and adolescence, PGS for other addiction-related behaviors (e.g., smoking status, alcohol consumption) show an increasing/decreasing magnitude of association with different facets of impulsivity over time (Wade et al., 2025). Additional evidence for a role of self-regulation in the initiation of substance use comes from related work showing that PGS of behavioral undercontrol predict time to first sip and first full drink of alcohol, as well as progression from alcohol sip to first full drink (Choi et al., 2025).

Recent studies have also examined how genetic risk for substance use interacts with environmental factors to influence childhood psychiatric traits. In longitudinal analyses, alcohol use disorder PGS showed no direct effect on externalizing behavior; however, interactions with parental acceptance revealed that Hispanic youth with both high genetic risk and low parental warmth showed elevated rule-breaking behavior (Trevino et al., 2024). Similarly, higher levels of family conflict have been reported to amplify the relationship between PGS for alcohol use disorder and the tendency to act impulsively when experiencing positive emotions (i.e., positive urgency) in youth of African American ancestry (Su et al., 2022). These preliminary findings help to illustrate the role of the family environment in moderating the relationship between genetic predisposition for substance use and the phenotypic expression of problem behaviors. Collectively, these studies highlight ABCD as a unique resource for mapping gene-brain-environment associations that predispose to substance use disorders later in life.

Cognition

Findings from the ABCD study show that cognitive traits measured in childhood are heritable and associated with common genetic variation (e.g., Eising et al., 2022; Li et al., 2024; Q. Li et al., 2025b; Maes et al., 2023; Pedersen et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2022, Wang et al., 2025). For example, recent large-scale investigations of reading and language-related abilities, including data from ABCD, have identified genome-wide significant loci associated with word reading and demonstrated substantial SNP-based heritability for reading, spelling, and language-related measures, accounting for 13–25% of trait variance (Carrión-Castillo et al., 2023, Eising et al., 2022). Other work has similarly found that multiple components of executive function are heritable, with SNP-based estimates ranging from 9% to 12%, and are highly genetically correlated with one another (Yin et al., 2025).

Importantly, however, while SNP-based heritability reflects the proportion of phenotypic variance in the population that is explained by common genetic variants, PGS derived from GWAS capture only a small portion of this signal at the individual level. Accordingly, PGS of intelligence and educational attainment explain only 4–5% of the variability in cognitive performance among 9–11-year-old ABCD participants, with stronger associations for crystallized compared to fluid intelligence (Loughnan et al., 2023). These findings comport with previous results reported in adults (Kan et al., 2013) and suggest that genetic influences on cognition may be amplified though children’s interactions with the environment, which exerts a larger influence on crystalized intelligence (Akshoomoff et al., 2013).

By decomposing genetic liability for educational attainment into PGS for cognitive and non-cognitive factors, ABCD researchers have further demonstrated that both PGS predict higher scores on cognitive tests, lower levels of impulsivity, and reduced severity of psychotic-like experiences (Gorelik et al., 2024). However, findings relating PGS for cognition to brain structure have been mixed. While some studies report associations with larger cortical volume and surface area (Carrión-Castillo et al., 2023, Gorelik et al., 2024, Wang et al., 2024a), others have found a lack of significant associations with white matter microstructure (Wang et al., 2024a) or co-heritability between working memory and regional measures of cortical thickness in ABCD youth (Krogsrud et al., 2021). Importantly, the effect sizes for these PGS associations with neuroimaging traits are typically less than 1%, underscoring that these additive genetic effects, while detectable, are small.

Building on these univariate analyses, researchers have begun to employ multivariate approaches to reduce the complexity of the ABCD data, transforming correlated imaging and behavioral traits into latent components reflecting shared dimensions of variation. Using this approach to both generate a latent factor for general cognition (g-factor) and to combine multimodal MRI data via machine learning, researchers have demonstrated that PGS for adult cognitive ability are associated with childhood g-factor (Monaghan et al., 2025, Pat et al., 2022b), and that multimodal brain-based measures partially mediate this relationship (Pat et al., 2022b). Extending this framework, analyses integrating multimodal neuroimaging, PGS, and socio-demographic factors show that most of the association between PGS and mental health is shared across domains, with neuroimaging accounting for a larger proportion of this shared variance than PGS (Wang et al., 2025). Consistent with these findings, PGS for educational attainment are associated with latent measures of increased cortical surface area and reduced externalizing symptoms, demonstrating that genetic effects on cognition are associated with broad brain and behavioral factors rather than isolated brain regions or behavioral profiles (Fernandez-Cabello et al., 2022). These distributed patterns of gene-behavior associations have also been observed for delayed discounting (i.e., individual differences in preference for smaller immediate rewards versus larger delayed rewards), where both greater PGS for delayed discounting and lower PGS for educational attainment predict preference for smaller immediate rewards (Rabinowitz et al., 2025). Altogether, these studies illustrate that genetic influences on cognition are polygenic and have broad impacts on brain and behavioral development.

Sleep

Sleep is a biological process that is crucial for physical growth, cognitive development and emotional functioning, yet its genetic architecture in childhood remains incompletely understood. Analyses of sleep-related phenotypes in the ABCD study suggest that childhood insomnia is associated with additive genetic liability for sleep problems (Ma et al., 2021) and demonstrates significant SNP-based heritability (15%) in the ABCD cohort at 9–10 years of age (Hernandez et al., 2025), consistent with previous studies in adult populations (Jansen et al., 2019, Lane et al., 2019). Childhood sleep disturbances also appear to be broadly associated with ADHD: At 9–10 years of age PGS for ADHD are significant predictors of insomnia, disorders of initiating and maintaining sleep, and excessive somnolence, and insomnia symptoms show significant genetic correlations with childhood externalizing traits (Hernandez et al., 2023a; Ohi et al., 2021), suggesting that childhood sleep disturbances may lie along the dimensional axis of childhood ADHD.

Building on this work, recent analyses have begun to evaluate how polygenic liability for adult sleep traits are related to sleep in adolescence. For example, longitudinal analyses suggest that adolescent sleep trajectories characterized by increasing sleep disturbances longitudinally across two timepoints are associated with adult-derived insomnia PGS (Yan et al., 2025). Similarly, PGS derived from adult GWAS of chronotype – indexing an individual's preference for earlier versus later sleep-wake timing – predict adolescent chronotype, but only in youth who are in later pubertal stages (Hernandez et al., 2025). These findings suggest that as children age and gain greater autonomy over their sleep-wake routines with less environmental regulation (e.g., parental monitoring, limited screen time), genetic effects on sleep-wake behavior may become more apparent.

In addition to genetic factors, other environmental influences such as SES also impact insomnia symptoms, acting both independently and additively to affect sleep–wake behaviors (Zhang et al., 2025). These additive gene–environment effects are associated with not only insomnia symptoms but also with reductions in cortical thickness in the inferior precentral sulcus, which have been shown to mediate the relationship between gene–environment effects and insomnia symptoms (Zhang et al., 2025). Importantly, however, most GWAS of sleep-related phenotypes have been conducted in adults of European ancestry. As a result, the predictive accuracy of insomnia PGS on non-European ABCD participants is substantially reduced (Ma et al., 2021), pointing to the need for broader representation in genetics research.

Impulsivity and externalizing behaviors

Externalizing and impulsive behaviors emerge early in development and often reflect difficulties with self-regulation, including problems with inhibitory control, emotion regulation, and reward sensitivity (Beauchaine et al., 2017, Krueger et al., 2001). These traits are also well-established risk factors for substance use initiation and dependence (Kozak et al., 2019, Winters et al., 2008), and prior work indicates that they are moderately heritable and highly polygenic (Baselmans et al., 2022, Linnér et al., 2021). Genetic analyses of childhood measures of impulsive behavior and delayed discounting in ABCD indicate that they are heritable and show partially shared genetic architecture with adult samples, suggesting some stability in genetic influences on these traits across the lifespan (Deng et al., 2025, Maes et al., 2023). Indeed, analyses of the ABCD cohort demonstrate that even at ages 9–10 years, PGS for externalizing and ADHD-related traits are associated with both behavioral and neuroimaging phenotypes. For example, ADHD PGS predict ADHD symptoms, conduct problems, and a general factor of psychological problems at baseline and 1-year follow-up, with baseline measures of impulsivity mediating these longitudinal associations (Lahey et al., 2022), and they similarly predict both initial ADHD symptom levels and rate of change over time (Pang et al., 2025). ADHD PGS also show broad associations with multiple other cognitive phenotypes including several subscales from the Child Behavior checklist (Achenbach and Rescorla, 2001, Feng et al., 2025), working memory and reaction time variability (Moses et al., 2022). These findings highlight that ADHD PGS may capture broad axes of liability for externalizing traits rather than disorder-specific risk. However, work testing the association between ADHD PGS and different phenotypic definitions of ADHD suggests that the strength of the genotype-phenotype association depends on how cases are defined. Youth meeting the most stringent case definitions show increased polygenic liability for ADHD, while youth meeting only more relaxed case criteria threshold show a weaker genetic signal (Cordova et al., 2022).

Extending this work to examine impact on the brain structure, higher PGS for comorbid ADHD and disruptive behavior has been linked to reduced total cortical gray matter volume, while PGS for both ADHD and irritability have been linked to lower white matter integrity measured with diffusion MRI (Teeuw et al., 2023, Yang et al., 2022). More recent work also shows that ADHD PGS are associated with connectivity of resting state fMRI networks (Feng et al., 2025). Importantly, although these polygenic effects are generally small (accounting for roughly 0.5% of the behavioral variance in externalizing behavior), the combination of PGS with sociodemographic and neuroimaging measures predicts a more substantial 12% of behavioral variance (Teeuw et al., 2023), while multivariate models including measured genetic and environmental factors explain roughly 9% of the behavioral variance in ADHD symptoms (Mooney et al., 2023).

Similar patterns have been observed for the relationship between PGS for externalizing and impulsive behaviors: higher externalizing PGS predicts conduct problems and elevated impulsivity as measured by the UPPS-P Impulsive Behavior Scale (Lahey et al., 2024, Whiteside and Lynam, 2003), as well as lower gray matter volume and smaller cortical surface area and volume in frontal and temporal brain regions (Gilman et al., 2024, Lahey et al., 2024). Together, these findings suggest that genetic liability for externalizing and impulsive traits impact a broad set of neurodevelopmental processes in brain regions involved in inhibitory control, motivation, and reward sensitivity.

Integrative methods have also been used to parse the shared and unique biology of externalizing-related traits with other co-occurring behaviors. For example, work using wearable-derived phenotypes in ABCD conducted multivariate GWAS on behavioral features generated from smartwatch data, identifying two genome-wide significant loci, with follow-up tests revealing a locus with ADHD-specific effects on sedentary behavior (Liu et al., 2025). By performing GWAS on a combined score of wearable-derived data reflecting an individual’s likelihood of ADHD, this same study identified 10 genome-wide significant loci, several of which have previously been reported in GWAS of related traits (e.g., ADHD case/control, chronotype) (Liu et al., 2025). Extending this work to neuroimaging data, data-driven subtyping of internalizing disorders indicates that youth with co-occurring impulsivity and anxiety show a distinct cortical thickness profile relative to those with lower levels of impulsivity and controls, which is broadly characterized by thicker cortex across multiple regions in the frontal and occipital cortex (Fan et al., 2023b). Subsequent GWAS of cortical thickness in these regions find enrichment for neuronal and glial cell-type specific gene expression, as well as genes previously implicated in autoimmune diseases and immune processes (Fan et al., 2023b).

Complementary trans-diagnostic approaches have demonstrated pleiotropic effects between ADHD and other frequently co-occurring conditions. Among individuals with ADHD, PGS for autism spectrum disorder (ASD) predicts increased verbal cognition, consistent with shared polygenic contributions to these two traits (LaBianca et al., 2024). A better understanding of these shared and distinct genetic pathways will be critical for identifying early signs of risk for externalizing and impulsive behaviors, and for designing interventions that target core self-regulatory processes, as well as disorder-specific symptoms. Importantly, however, this widespread pleiotropy is not specific to ADHD: extensive trans-diagnostic genetic and phenotypic associations are frequently reported in the ABCD data (e.g., Loughnan et al., 2022b; Sun et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025).

Psychosis

Efforts to understand the impact of genetic risk for psychosis on childhood psychopathology have largely focused on whether PGS derived from adult GWAS can aid in the early detection of youth experiencing psychotic-like experiences (PLEs). Because the onset of clinically significant psychosis is often preceded by months or years of gradual changes in cognition, motivation, and perception (Klosterkötter et al., 2001), identifying children at elevated liability for psychosis offers important opportunities for early detection and intervention. PLEs are surprisingly common in childhood, affecting roughly 10% of the preadolescent population (Poulton et al., 2000), though they persist or worsen in only a small subset of individuals. The ABCD Study provides measures of both the frequency and severity (i.e., subjective distress) of PLEs, allowing for dimensional modeling of risk in this large pediatric population.

Using these measures, researchers have taken a transdiagnostic approach, modeling multiple PGS to examine genetic influences on PLEs. These studies have consistently found that the frequency and severity of PLEs are associated with PGS for cross-disorder liability, ADHD, cognitive traits, and educational attainment (Chang et al., 2024, Karcher et al., 2022, Loughnan et al., 2022b, Park et al., 2024), suggesting that these subclinical symptoms of psychosis in childhood show a high degree of pleiotropy and shared genetic influences with broader neurodevelopmental phenotypes. However, findings regarding the impact of schizophrenia-specific PGS have been mixed. While some studies have reported that higher schizophrenia PGS is associated with more distressing and persistent PLEs (Chang et al., 2024, Karcher et al., 2022, Ku et al., 2024, Pries et al., 2025, Yuan et al., 2025), other studies have found no association with PLE frequency and severity (Hernandez et al., 2023b, Loughnan et al., 2022b). Differences across studies may stem from differences in which PLE dimensions are being modeled, for example whether studies examine frequency, severity, or distress, as well as whether PLEs are treated as continuous or binary variables. Results may also vary depending on the covariates included in statistical models, as socioeconomic and biological (e.g., puberty, body mass index) factors are controlled for in some analyses but not others. Because these factors are themselves genetically and phenotypically correlated with psychiatric and cognitive traits, adjusting for them in statistical models has the potential to attenuate PGS-phenotype associations. In fact, analyses of longitudinal ABCD data has shown schizophrenia PGS are associated with lifetime distressing PLEs when controlling for sex and age, but not in models that additionally control for family income and parent education (Di Vincenzo et al., 2025). In addition, differences may reflect how PGS were constructed (e.g., clumping and thresholding versus Bayesian methods), the discovery GWAS used, and the inclusion of genetic-related covariates such as ancestry principal components or genotyping batch in analytic models. Thus, discrepancies across studies likely reflect methodological differences, underscoring the importance of providing clear and reproducible analytic pipelines when using this open-access dataset. In addition, there is evidence that while the relationship between broad neurodevelopmental PGS and childhood PLEs attenuate over time, schizophrenia PGS are associated with PLEs across timepoints (Chang et al., 2024), pointing to the need for careful consideration of the specific ages examined when reading the ABCD literature.

In addition to genome-wide PGS modeling, ABCD data have also been leveraged to investigate specific genetic mechanisms implicated in psychosis, such as structural variation. Although schizophrenia and psychosis are highly polygenic traits (Trubetskoy et al., 2022), one of the largest GWAS signal maps to the complement component 4 A (C4A) locus on chromosome 6 (Pardiñas et al., 2018, Ripke et al., 2014, Sekar et al., 2016). Using SNP-based genetically predicted C4A expression, a phenome-wide analysis in ABCD study found no association with PLEs at ages 9–10, but higher predicted C4A expression was associated with reduced surface area in the entorhinal cortex, which in turn predicted greater frequency and severity of PLEs one to two years later (Hernandez et al., 2023b). These findings highlight the utility of the ABCD cohort to identify genetically mediated changes in brain structure and function that may precede the development of clinically significant PLEs, enabling biologically based stratification of those at elevated risk for developing psychosis. Additional work has identified rare copy number variants present in the ABCD sample – which is unique in that it contains genetic data from a general pediatric population as opposed to a clinically ascertained sample – including a duplication at 10q26.3 that was nominally associated with psychopathology and previously implicated in schizophrenia (Sha et al., 2025).

More broadly, the ABCD data have also been leveraged to study how genetic liability for other neurological and psychiatric disorders shapes brain development, an approach that may help to define the continuum between typical and atypical brain developmental trajectories. For example, higher polygenic risk for epilepsy has been linked to reduced cortical thickness in temporal and frontal regions in children without an epilepsy diagnosis, mirroring the patterns observed in clinical cohorts (Ngo et al., 2025). These findings highlight the power of the ABCD data to examine how genetic vulnerability manifests in the developing brain, before the emergence of clinically significant symptoms.

Multivariate and phenome-wide approaches

Multivariate and phenome-wide approaches are increasingly being used by ABCD researchers to move beyond single-phenotype analyses in order to capture the multidimensional nature of genetic and behavioral risk. Together, these studies highlight how polygenic liability, rather than mapping on to discrete traits, is associated with shared dimensions of youth mental and physical health, cognition, brain structure, and environmental contexts.

A growing set of multivariate studies has focused on associations between PGS and a higher-order latent dimension of general psychopathology (p-factor). Across these analyses, PGS for multiple disorders, including major depression and ADHD, show significant associations with the p-factor (Pat et al., 2022a; Waszczuk et al., 2023). Further work suggests that these associations arise in part through broad cognitive and behavioral pathways: latent indices of behavioral inhibition and the g-factor partially mediate the link between MDD PGS and the p-factor, whereas latent indices of behavioral activation and the g-factor partially mediate the link between ADHD PGS and the p-factor (Pat et al., 2022a). In-line with these observations, work examining links between multiple PGS for psychiatric disorders and dimensional CBCL subscales shows that while MDD and ADHD PGS are both associated with a range of behavioral symptoms, ADHD PGS show the most robust associations with CBCL subscales related to rule-breaking and externalizing-related behaviors (Hughes et al., 2023, Wainberg et al., 2022). Expanding this work to test for associations between 22 psychiatric, cognitive, pain, and physical-health related PGS and general psychopathology indicates that more than half of these PGS are associated with the p-factor, while factors related to internalizing, externalizing, and detachment continue to show associations with multiple PGS even after controlling for associations with the general p-factor, suggesting the presence of more specific gene-behavior associations (Waszczuk et al., 2023).

Additional multivariate work in ABCD integrates genetic risk with brain and environmental measures to uncover how the latent structure of these variables relate to one another. Using genomic structural equation modeling (gSEM), researchers have shown that a narrow neurodevelopmental cross-disorder PGS that is enriched for genes expressed prenatally in the cerebellum and indexing liability for ADHD, autism, Tourette’s disorder, and depression is a more robust predictor of childhood psychiatric symptoms than broader cross-disorder PGS or disorder-specific PGS scores (Hughes et al., 2023). Turning to associations with neuroimaging phenotypes, PGS for transdiagnostic genetic factors related to common mood disorders are associated with the p-factor, while both this PGS and a transdiagnostic PGS related to serious mental illness have been linked to individual differences in personalized functional brain networks (Sun et al., 2025). Other structural equation modeling work suggests that, once environmental factors (i.e., adversity, maternal substance use, parent psychopathology, socioeconomic status, school and family environment) and covariates (i.e., age, sex, ethnicity, sibship) are accounted for, the influence of psychiatric PGS on brain structure is limited, with social-anxiety PGS emerging as the only score associated with reduced structural connectivity in visual and orbitofrontal networks (Qiu and Liu, 2023). Complementing these findings, multivariate data reduction techniques such as canonical correlation analysis and partial least squares regression find two broad genetic dimensions of mental illness – one reflecting liability for general psychopathology and another reflecting neurodevelopmental-specific risk – and show that both genetic liability and adversity-related risk converge on shared cortico-limbic connectivity patterns (Vedechkina et al., 2025). Related multimodal work using parallel independent component analysis (pICA) further demonstrates that coupled fMRI–gene expression components in cortex, cerebellum, and the nucleus accumbens differentiate children with ADHD and predict variability in impulsivity-related cognitive performance (Jeon et al., 2025).

Phenome-wide association studies (PheWAS) extend these findings, taking a data-driven approach by systematically testing associations between thousands of behavioral, physical, and neural phenotypes to identify novel effects of polygenic liability. PheWAS in ABCD show that neurodevelopmental PGS are associated with a wide array of psychological and physical traits in youth (e.g., Hoffman et al., 2024; Paul et al., 2024). For example, a recent PheWAS investigated the association between polygenic liability for elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) in adulthood (a marker of immune activation) and over 2000 psychological, behavioral, physical, and neuroimaging phenotypes (Norton et al., 2025). Findings from this work indicate that while CRP PGS is not robustly related to childhood neuroimaging phenotypes, higher CRP PGS is associated with higher childhood weight (BMI, caregiver reports of overweight status, waist circumference, measured weight), eating (caregiver reports of overeating), and weekday video watching, suggesting modifiable behaviors in childhood may contribute to downstream inflammatory risk in adulthood (Norton et al., 2025).

Taken together, these multivariate and PheWAS approaches indicate that genetic influences on youth mental and physical health are broad, dimensional, and intertwined with cognition, environment, and brain development. These studies make clear that genetic risk rarely maps onto single diagnostic categories and instead manifests across traditional psychiatric boundaries.

Effects of genes and the environment

The comprehensive genomic, neuroimaging, and longitudinal environmental measures available through the ABCD dataset offers an unparalleled opportunity to investigate the interplay of genetic and environmental factors in shaping brain and behavioral development. To this end, recent work has examined multiple forms of gene-environment interplay, including additive models that estimate the independent effects of the environment while accounting for genetic liability, whether socioeconomic and family relationships amplify or buffer genetic risk, and examined gene-environment interactions (GxE) and correlation (rGE) (i.e., when genetic factors influence the environments that individuals experience) across development.

The role of maternal and prenatal factors has been explored in several recent studies that have modeled PGS for cognitive and psychiatric traits as covariates to obtain more precise estimates of the impact of the environment on neurodevelopmental outcomes. Findings in this area have demonstrated that prenatal exposures exert independent effects on cognition, above and beyond the impact of genetic liability (Gu et al., 2024, Nivins et al., 2025). For example, prenatal exposure to substances such as alcohol, tobacco, marijuana, and caffeine are associated with cognitive function, externalizing and internalizing symptoms, and sleep problems, even after controlling for PGS related to educational attainment, and mental health (Gu et al., 2024). In addition to prenatal exposures, maternal age has also previously been linked to multiple neurodevelopmental outcomes, including ADHD (Chang et al., 2014). Indeed, data from the ABCD study suggest that greater ADHD PGS is linked to both younger maternal age and higher odds of a child with elevated ADHD symptomatology (Baker et al., 2023). However, when genetic liability for ADHD is included in predictive models, the association between younger maternal age and elevated ADHD symptoms in children is substantially reduced, suggesting that shared genetic factors partially account for this relationship (Baker et al., 2023).

Additional research has focused on how family relationships and socioeconomic factors impact behavioral outcomes. A handful of studies in this area have found larger effects of the environment on behavioral outcomes compared to genetic liability. For example, higher levels of parental acceptance and monitoring are associated with a reduced likelihood of suicidal ideation in late childhood, whereas suicide-attempt PGS did not predict suicidal ideation at ages 9–10 (Stephenson et al., 2025). However, this absence of early genetic effects does not imply a lack of genetic influence. Longitudinal analyses show that the association between suicide-attempt PGS and lifetime suicide attempt strengthen with age, mirroring an increase in the prevalence of suicide attempt over this time period and suggesting that with increased variance in psychiatric phenotypes over the course of development we might expect the strength of genetic associations to also increase (Lee et al., 2022). Other research suggests that family, sociodemographic, and social environments play a stronger role in predicting alcohol expectancies (i.e., expectations about how one would feel when drinking alcohol) than genetics. In the ABCD cohort, parental education, religious beliefs, childhood experiences, and peer attitudes were more predictive of alcohol expectancies than PGS for problematic alcohol use, depression, or risk-taking behavior (Johnson et al., 2023). These findings suggest that environmental contexts likely play a large role in influencing risky behavior and attitudes in late childhood, potentially outperforming predictions based on additive genetic risk alone. Consistent with these findings, brain structural traits that show stronger SNP-based heritability in the ABCD sample are no less associated with socioeconomic factors than less heritable brain phenotypes, indicating that even highly heritable traits are influenced by environmental contexts (Williams et al., 2024).

Several studies have examined GxE to understand how these factors jointly shape mental health and behavior (e.g., Mooney et al., 2023; Shi et al., 2024; Su et al., 2025; Thomas et al., 2023). For example, a genome-by-exposome–wide analysis spanning 133 environmental variables suggests that GxE interactions account for a substantial proportion of variation in internalizing and externalizing symptoms at 9–11 years of age (Choi et al., 2022). In this study, genome-exposome interactions explained 13% of the variance in internalizing symptoms and 33% of the variance in externalizing symptoms; these effects exceeded the additive genetic component, and for externalizing were also larger than the additive environmental component. These studies suggest that modeling GxE interactions is important to fully capture the mechanisms through which genetic variation impacts neurobehavioral profiles.

Importantly, some environmental exposures themselves show significant heritability, reflecting rGE. Large-scale meta-analysis across ABCD and several other population-based biobanks show that childhood maltreatment is both heritable and polygenic, indicating that genetic factors influence a child's likelihood of being exposed to adverse environments (Warrier et al., 2021). Similarly, both traumatic experiences and symptoms of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) are partially heritable, although environmental factors explain a larger proportion of variance in these phenotypes (Bustamante et al., 2022), and higher PGS for mental health problems are associated with an increase in adverse childhood experiences (Baldwin et al., 2023). Altogether, these findings highlight the need for continued research that models genetic and environmental influences together, which will allow for a more complete understanding of how early experiences and genetic variation work together to shape neurodevelopmental trajectories.

Challenges and opportunities

While there has been rapid growth in the number of studies utilizing ABCD genetics data over the past decade, there remain a number of limitations to the knowledge that has been gained and methodological hurdles to be overcome. One major limitation is the underrepresentation of non-European ancestry individuals in large-scale GWAS, which severely limits efforts to build robust and equitable genetic predictors for mental and physical health. Even within ABCD, many genetics studies have been limited to European participants and there remains relatively less utilization of genetics data from underrepresented groups, limiting generalizability of the reported findings. This inequity not only impacts the reproducibility of scientific findings across diverse groups but also has the potential to perpetuate existing health disparities as these genetic predictors become increasingly leveraged for clinical use by the medical community.

A second limitation is the documented small effect sizes of current PGS, which explain only a small fraction of variability in brain, behavioral, and physical health outcomes – a limitation that is expected to improve with larger and more diverse sample sizes for GWAS. Nevertheless, even with their small effect sizes, PGS can still be misinterpreted in inaccurate deterministic ways, for example, by suggesting that genetic influences are fixed, unchangeable, or more important than environmental factors. Such interpretations are incorrect, as genetic influences are nonspecific, shaped by gene-environment interactions and gene-environment correlations, and are expressed through complex biological pathways that include many potential points for intervention. Inaccurate deterministic interpretations have the particular risk of causing harm in diverse and marginalized populations, potentially exasperating stigma or misrepresenting genetic findings as fixed causes of social or psychiatric outcomes. As the field advances, accurate reporting of genetic findings, as well as their limitations, will be critical to ensure scientific rigor, maintain public trust, and support the responsible application of genetic research in clinical practice.

Looking ahead, there are many opportunities to advance both methods and discovery by leveraging the deep longitudinal behavioral, neuroimaging, and genetic data made available through the ABCD consortium. In particular, the cohort's ancestral diversity provides a unique platform for developing and validating new methods for analyzing mixed-ancestry data and for testing the transferability of genetic findings across populations. At the same time, emerging machine learning approaches also hold promise for integrating genetics with high-dimensional behavioral, neuroimaging, and environmental data, potentially improving prediction through more comprehensive integrative analyses. Importantly, the longitudinal and multimodal design of the ABCD study also allows for novel within-family analyses, such as sibling-comparison and discordant twin designs, that can help to distinguish inherited genetic from environmental impacts on brain and behavioral trajectories. Beyond these analytic opportunities, there is also substantial untapped potential for integrating genetics with ABCD digital phenotyping data, which includes Fitbit data on physiological measures such as daily activity, heart rate, and sleep, as well as with measures of pubertal hormones, and substance and environmental exposures assayed through biosamples. Finally, as the cohort ages into adolescence and early adulthood, which are periods of peak onset for psychiatric and substance use disorders, the relevance of these phenotypes to predictive models and precision psychiatry will only continue to grow.

Conclusion

Over the past decade, the ABCD Study has become a powerful resource for understanding how genetic and environmental factors shape adolescent brain development, behavior, and health. The combination of large-scale genomics, deep longitudinal phenotyping, and rich environmental data position ABCD to inform the development of precision medicine approaches that identify youth at elevated risk for mental, cognitive, and physical health challenges before clinical symptoms emerge. The ABCD data continues to hold great potential for understanding adolescent development through application of cutting-edge statistical models, developing methods to address ancestry bias, and integration of genetics with real-world behavioral and physiological measures, which together can help to realize the full potential of ABCD as a unique resource for developmental genomics. As highlighted throughout this review, some findings across domains have been mixed, likely reflecting differences in analytic decisions, phenotype definition, and covariate selection. Thus, continued emphasis on transparent reporting and reproducible pipelines will be essential for maximizing the scientific value and interpretability of findings from the ABCD dataset. With these considerations in mind, integration of genetic and environmental variables has the potential to help uncover modifiable biological, social, and environmental exposures that influence developmental trajectories and lifelong wellbeing. As data collection continues and expands, there will be increasing opportunities for researchers to collaborate across disciplines, as well as to perform innovative integration and harmonization across large-scale datasets to further improve power for genomic discovery. By embracing open science and collaboration, researchers can ensure that the full potential of ABCD genetics is realized, enhancing both scientific discovery and public health.
