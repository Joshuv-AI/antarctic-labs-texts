Prediction of treatment outcome for branch retinal vein occlusion using convolutional neural network-based retinal fluorescein angiography
Authors: Yupeng Zhang, Zhen Xing, Aijun Deng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeep learning techniques were used in ophthalmology to develop artificial intelligence (AI) models for predicting the short-term effectiveness of anti-VEGF therapy in patients with macular edema secondary to branch retinal vein occlusion (BRVO-ME). 180 BRVO-ME patients underwent pre-treatment FFA scans. After 3 months of ranibizumab injections, CMT measurements were taken at baseline and 1-month intervals. Patients were categorized into good and poor prognosis groups based on macular edema at the 4th month follow-up. FFA-Net, a VGG-based classification network, was trained using FFA images from both groups. Class activation heat maps highlighted important locations. Benchmark models (DesNet-201, MobileNet-V3, ResNet-152, MansNet-75) were compared for training results. Performance metrics included accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1 score, and ROC curves. FFA-Net predicted BRVO-ME treatment effect with an accuracy of 88.63% and an F1 score of 0.89, with a sensitivity and specificity of 79.40% and 71.34%, respectively.The AUC of the ROC curve for the FFA-Net model was 0.71. The use of FFA based on deep learning technology has feasibility in predicting the treatment effect of BRVO-ME. The FFA-Net model constructed with the VGG model as the main body has good results in predicting the treatment effect of BRVO-ME. The typing of BRVO in FFA may be an important factor affecting the prognosis.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Convolutional neural network, Branch retinal vein occlusion, Fundus fluorescence angiographySubject terms: Diseases, Medical research, Signs and symptoms

Branch retinal vein occlusion (BRVO) is a common retinal vascular disease that occurs mainly in the area where the branch of the retinal vein is blocked, leading to impaired blood flow, accumulation of blood and exudates, and resulting in retinal edema, hemorrhage, and ischemia1,2.

Clinically, the main manifestations of BRVO include decreased visual acuity, visual field defects, and retinal hemorrhage. Macular edema (ME) is the most critical factor leading to visual impairment in BRVO, which is mainly associated with an increase in the concentration of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) in the vitreous3–7. The macula is responsible for central vision and fine visual acuity in the retina, and any degree of ME can have a severe impact on visual function. In clinical practice, ME that persists for at least 3 months and remains present after three or more anti-VEGF drug treatments, with central macular thickness (CMT) exceeding 300 µm as shown on optical coherence tomography (OCT), is referred to as refractory ME8. Currently, conventional methods for treating BRVO-ME include laser photocoagulation and drug injections, with the use of anti-VEGF drugs being the most common. The mechanism of action of these drugs is to inhibit VEGF, thereby reducing vascular permeability and the formation of new blood vessels. However, anti-VEGF therapy typically requires repeated injections on a regular basis, leading to high treatment costs. For some patients, despite repeated injections, macular edema may still recur, causing significant economic and psychological burdens5–7.

Therefore, in order to optimize the treatment of BRVO-ME, it is necessary to accurately predict treatment outcomes, which can help doctors better select treatment methods and improve treatment success rates. Currently, artificial intelligence technology has shown great potential in the field of medical image analysis9. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are a type of deep learning model that can effectively learn complex patterns and features, thus making significant progress in medical image diagnosis, treatment response prediction, and other areas10. One particularly noteworthy application area is the analysis of fundus images.

Fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) is an important examination for evaluating the condition of retinal vessels, as it can depict the distribution and status of retinal vasculature. In the diagnosis and monitoring of BRVO, FFA provides detailed images of the affected area, including the location, extent, and degree of vascular occlusion. Currently, there have been successful studies using CNN to detect and classify macular lesions, and even predict the progression of the disease11–14. Given the importance of FFA in assessing BRVO lesions, research combining CNN technology for image analysis is urgently needed. Such a predictive model can help doctors explain treatment plans, engage in effective communication with patients, and help patients establish reasonable treatment expectations.

Therefore, by integrating CNN technology with ophthalmic medical practice and establishing relevant model architectures, we aim to improve the prediction of short-term anti-VEGF treatment outcomes for patients with BRVO-ME.

Model training results

To evaluate the performance of the model, we utilized testing metrics such as accuracy, F1 score, sensitivity, specificity, and weighted error. The FFA-Net model achieved an accuracy of 88.63%. The F1 score was 0.89, with sensitivity and specificity reaching 79.40% and 71.34% respectively.

Table 4 We also compared the training results of other mainstream models, including DesNet-201, MobileNet-V3, ResNet-152, and MansNet-75. The DesNet-201 model achieved an accuracy of 87.23%. The F1 score was 0.79, with sensitivity and specificity reaching 79.32% and 72.96% respectively. The MobileNet-V3 model had an accuracy of 76.47%, an F1 score of 0.69, and sensitivity and specificity of 71.28% and 60.30% respectively. The ResNet-152 model had an accuracy of 70.58%, an F1 score of 0.72, and sensitivity and specificity of 75.68% and 69.97% respectively. Lastly, the MansNet-75 model achieved an accuracy of 23.52%, an F1 score of 0.41, and sensitivity and specificity of 40.33% and 42.37% respectively.

Discussion

The existing literature indicates that current research on the application of artificial intelligence technology in BRVO is limited to disease diagnosis, classification, segmentation, etc. However, there is currently no relevant research on using artificial intelligence technology to predict the treatment outcomes of BRVO-ME.

In this study, we collected FFA images within the standard 55° range of the first eye examination of the patients. Based on the response of the macular thickness (CMT) after three consecutive anti-VEGF treatments, the patients were divided into the good prognosis group and the poor prognosis group. In addition, patients were classified as ischemic or non-ischemic based on the area of non-perfusion in FFA images. The image dataset used in this study consisted of 180 FFA images collected from our hospital. Although the number of images is limited, it serves as a preliminary study to explore the feasibility of using artificial intelligence technology in assessing the prognosis of BRVO-ME. Chen et al. developed a deep learning-based artificial intelligence approach to assist in the screening of retinal vein occlusion (RVO), alleviating the pressure on ophthalmologists, and facilitating early detection and treatment of RVO15. Ren et al. established an AI model for differentiating RVO patients from normal individuals using color fundus photographs, which may have important implications for risk prediction in RVO patients16. In our study, we used convolutional neural networks (CNN) to extract features of the target area in FFA images. FFA images can intuitively reflect vascular signals and non-perfusion area. By analyzing the target area using CNN, we aim to predict the treatment outcomes of BRVO-ME patients who have not undergone treatment.

In addition, we used Grad-CAM to visualize the decision-making process of the CNN. Grad-CAM generates a coarse localization map by propagating the gradient of the target class to the final convolutional layer, allowing visualization of the features. Grad-CAM addresses the issue of requiring model architecture modifications that exists with CAM methods. It reduces computational complexity and increases the interpretability of the model compared to other explanation methods. The Grad-CAM heatmaps in our study highlight the important areas where the deep learning model may focus on extracting features, which are correlated with the treatment outcomes of BRVO-ME patients. The highlighted regions on the heatmaps indicate the sites of venous occlusion in BRVO patients, including blood vessels and non-perfusion areas. By presenting the FFA regions recognized by artificial intelligence, it helps address the “black box” issue in CNN and further explains how the CNN predicts treatment outcomes by analyzing the structures in FFA images of BRVO-ME patients. However, it should be noted that in our machine learning process, we only provided early-stage FFA images. Further research is needed to investigate if there are different results in late-stage FFA images.

In our study, we used VGG, DesNet, MobileNet, ResNet, and MansNet models. The FFA-Net model, primarily based on the VGG model, achieved the best overall performance. The FFA-Net model had an average test accuracy of 88.63%, sensitivity of 79.40%, and specificity of 71.34%.

Due to the small size of the dataset in our study, we utilized transfer learning strategy. To further improve the model performance, we used preprocessed images as inputs to the classification network. Specifically, we utilized the VGG architecture as the base and made corresponding adjustments to build the FFA-Net model according to our requirements. In addition to the mentioned improvements, we decided to freeze the weights of the pretrained model and utilize the general features obtained from large-scale images to train the model for our specific task. The VGG-Net is known for its concise architecture, which constructs deep CNN architectures by repeating small-sized convolutional filters (3 × 3) and max-pooling layers (2 × 2). The most well-known versions are VGG-16 and VGG-19, which contain 16 and 19 layers, respectively. By using this strategy, the VGG models significantly increase the depth of the network without overly complicating the model. By using multiple small-sized convolutional filters instead of a single large-sized filter, the VGG model can increase the network depth and enhance the model’s nonlinearity, thus improving its feature learning ability17–21. This means that the network can capture more complex patterns in the images and extract richer feature representations. Compared to previous network architectures, VGG has significantly reduced error rates, strong scalability, and excellent generalization when applied to other image datasets. Therefore, we obtained good results by utilizing the VGG model. Yoo et al. conducted a retrospective analysis of 59 patients (59 eyes) with age-related macular degeneration (AMD) and used OCT and fundus images combined with deep learning to improve the accuracy of AMD diagnosis. The researchers established a new deep learning (DL) model for diagnosing AMD using a single-image mode. These models consisted of pretrained VGG-19 and transfer learning with the use of random forests. After data augmentation and training, the DL using OCT showed diagnostic efficiency, with an AUC of 0.906 (95% confidence interval, 0.891–0.921) and an accuracy of 82.6% (81.0–84.3%). DL using fundus images exhibited an AUC of 0.914 (0.900–0.928) and an accuracy of 83.5% (81.8–85.0%)22. Due to the excellent performance of the VGG model on large-scale datasets such as ImageNet, its pretrained models are widely used in transfer learning scenarios. In small datasets, researchers often use pretrained VGG models as feature extractors or fine-tune them to adapt to new tasks23. This strategy significantly improves the performance of the model in new tasks, especially with limited data. VGG has shown good image classification capabilities and is widely applied in the medical field.

In addition, there is a statistically significant difference in prognosis between different types of fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) classification in BRVO-ME. In the group with a good prognosis, the proportion of nonischemic BRVO patients is significantly higher than ischemic BRVO patients. Similarly, in the group with a poor prognosis, the proportion of nonischemic BRVO patients is significantly higher than ischemic BRVO patients. The ischemic retina produces high levels of VEGF. The high concentration of VEGF in the vitreous can further exacerbate retinal ischemia, leading to retinal edema and visual loss. FFA findings of NPAs larger than 5PD are diagnosed as ischemic BRVO. Therefore, FFA classification of BRVO patients may be one of the factors affecting the efficacy of anti-VEGF treatment.

There are still limitations in this study. Firstly, the limited sample size and varying data quality require a large amount of high-quality and accurately annotated image data to train a CNN model. However, our study was a single-center study with only 180 patients included. This limited sample size and lack of diversity in the dataset (such as limited variation in race, gender, and age distribution) may restrict the generalizability of the model. Additionally, the quality of the fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) images may be influenced by the operator’s skill level, differences in image acquisition devices, or varying levels of patient cooperation, which can have adverse effects on the training and performance of the model. Secondly, our study did not conduct a multimodal analysis. The included analysis only involved factors such as gender, age, blood pressure, arm-to-retinal circulation time, central macular thickness (CMT), and BRVO classification. While these factors have certain value in assessing the prognosis of BRVO-ME patients, they may not be sufficient. Future research may consider incorporating additional morphological indicators of the retina to further improve the AI model. Thirdly, our study focused on short-term treatment outcomes and still has limitations in predicting long-term effects. A comprehensive predictive model should be able to forecast long-term effects after treatment, including the duration of visual improvement, recurrence rates, and potential long-term complications. Therefore, further research is needed to conduct follow-up studies on the long-term outcomes of patients after anti-VEGF treatment.

In conclusion, the use of FFA-based deep learning technology shows feasibility in predicting the treatment outcomes of BRVO-ME. The FFA-Net model, mainly constructed based on the VGG model, demonstrates good performance in predicting the treatment outcomes of BRVO-ME. The classification of BRVO in FFA may be an important factor affecting the prognosis.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study included a total of 180 patients (180 eyes) who were newly diagnosed with BRVO and received treatment at the Ophthalmology Center of Shandong Second Medical University Affiliated Hospital from January 2020 to December 2023. All patients were diagnosed with BRVO-ME through fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA). A total of 180 FFA images were collected for the included BRVO patients. The patients’ gender, age, and blood pressure at admission were recorded. The brachio-retinal circulation time at the time of FFA examination, the first-eye image within 60 ± 20 s of complete venous filling, and the type of BRVO were determined based on the patient’s FFA image.

The patients received intravitreal injections of ranibizumab once a month for three consecutive months. Eye examinations were conducted one month after the treatment and continued for four months with at least monthly visits. During each follow-up, slit lamp biomicroscopy, intraocular pressure measurement, dilated fundus examination, best corrected visual acuity (BCVA), and OCT imaging were performed. Baseline and monthly follow-up cross-sectional OCT images of the macular center were collected. The central macular thickness (CMT), measured as the vertical distance between the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and the inner surface of the retinal nerve fiber layer, was recorded before and after each injection. Patients who received three consecutive anti-VEGF treatments were followed up in the short term and divided into two groups: a good prognosis group and a poor prognosis group. Patients with persistent macular edema (ME) or CMT > 300 μm at the fourth month follow-up were classified as the poor prognosis group, while patients with normal anatomical structure in the macular area or CMT ≤ 300 μm were classified as the good prognosis group.

The FFA images of patients in the favorable prognosis group and poor prognosis group with BRVO-ME will be subjected to deep learning to construct relevant models. The models will be visualized using class activation maps. The sensitivity and accuracy of the models in predicting patient prognosis will be analyzed, and a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve will be constructed.

Inclusion criteria

(1) Diagnosed with BRVO-ME by at least two experienced ophthalmologists. The OCT examination shows that the foveal depression is absent or elevated, with a CMT ≥ 250 μm. (2) All patients underwent fluorescein fundus angiography (FFA) using a multipurpose retinal camera prior to treatment. (3) All affected eyes received consecutive intravitreal injections of anti-VEGF medication for a minimum of three months after diagnosis. (4) OCT scans of the macular region in both horizontal and vertical orientations were performed before treatment and during follow-up.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Patients with other conditions that can cause macular edema, such as diabetic retinopathy, age-related macular degeneration, polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy, and choroidal neovascularization. (2) Patients who have previously received retinal laser photocoagulation, intravitreal injections of anti-VEGF medications or corticosteroids, or have undergone vitreous and/or retinal surgery. (3) Patients with poor quality FFA images that do not provide clear visualization of vascular structures for assessment. (4) Patients with OCT image quality grade lower than Q20, where structural details are unclear and central macular thickness (CMT) cannot be calculated. (5) Patients with missing imaging data due to loss to follow-up during treatment. (6) Exclusion criteria: Patients with clinical evidence of acute or chronic inflammation or infection, severe cardiovascular or cerebrovascular diseases, autoimmune diseases, malignancies, and those on long-term use of corticosteroids or immunosuppressive drugs. (7) Patients with allergies to fluorescein sodium dye.

Selection of FFA images

Collect FFA images within the standard 55°range of the first eye position of patients. Images should be taken when the veins are fully filled or within 60 ± 20 s of dye injection, as shown in Fig. 3. The contrast and brightness parameters should be consistent for all images. According to the guidelines for the study of branch retinal vein occlusion, the size of the avascular area in the microvasculature observed in FFA determines whether BRVO is ischemic or non-ischemic. If there is an avascular area larger than 5 papillary diameters (PD) in size, it is classified as ischemic. Otherwise, it is classified as non-ischemic24.

Model architecture

In the classification stage of deep learning, due to the limited quantity of the dataset, we employ transfer learning techniques and use preprocessed images obtained from FFA examinations as inputs to the classification network. The FFA-Net model is based on the VGG architecture, with the output layer removed and a fully connected layer added as the output. The dimension of the fully connected layer corresponds to the number of categories the model needs to classify. By freezing the weights of the pretrained model, we utilize the generic features obtained from a large-scale image dataset and simultaneously train the fully connected layer to adapt to the specific task. The specific training process is illustrated in Fig. 4, where Stage 1 represents the preprocessing stage. In this study, the following preprocessing steps were applied to the images: (1) resizing the image dimensions to 224 × 224 × 3, (2) performing data augmentation including random flipping and random cropping, (3) normalizing the pixel values of the images, as shown in Fig. 1. (4) Randomly selecting 20% of the dataset as the test data, while the remaining 80% is divided into training and validation sets in a 3:1 ratio. Stage 2 in the figure represents the model training stage, where the model consists of 16 convolutional layers, 5 max pooling layers, and 3 fully connected layers. Each convolutional layer is equipped with the same number of convolutional kernels and a bias term. The model has a total of 143,667,240 parameters. During the training process, we save the model weights with the highest accuracy by monitoring the changes in accuracy and loss functions. Ultimately, we obtain two output categories, we further categorize the results into a group with good prognosis and a group with poor prognosis. The output results serve as an assistance, providing additional information and reference. In addition, popular models such as DesNet-201, MobileNet-V3, ResNet-152, and MansNet-75 are also trained to compare the training results of different models.

Class activation map (CAM) visualization

Class Activation Maps (CAM) visualization is a technique used to compute a two-dimensional grid of scores for a generated image, indicating the importance of each position for a specific output class. In this heatmap, higher scores indicate regions in the original image that have a stronger response and contribute more to the network’s prediction for that class, thereby reflecting the importance of each position for that class.

Statistical methods

The experiment used the TensorFlow deep learning framework, Windows 11 operating system, AMD Ryzen 95900HX with Radeon Graphics processor, 32.0 GB of memory, and Python 3.9 programming language. The training was conducted for 7–8 days with 1000 iterations. The dataset was split into a training set and a test set in an 8:2 ratio. The model parameters were adjusted and optimized using the training set, with Adam used as the optimizer and cross-entropy chosen as the loss function.

IBM SPSS Statistics 22 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) was used for statistical analysis. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was conducted for continuous variables, and data that followed a normal distribution were represented as mean ± standard deviation. Depending on the distribution, t-test or Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare the two groups of data. Count data were presented as numbers (percentages) and analyzed using the chi-square test. The network model was implemented using the Python language, ROC curves were plotted, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
