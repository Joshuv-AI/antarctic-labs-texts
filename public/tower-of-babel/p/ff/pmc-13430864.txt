Access to palliative care by home care clients with severe and persistent mental illness: a retrospective study of health administrative data
Authors: Emmanuel Yohuno, Michel Bédard, Lynn Martin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThough it is recommended that a palliative approach be considered early during a serious or life-limiting illness, in most cases it is provided very late in the course of illness, if at all. Persons with severe and persistent mental illnesses (SPMI) have been identified as an underserved group with respect to palliative care despite higher rates of morbidity and mortality. This study examined access to palliative care (PC) by home care clients with SPMI and factors associated with access.MethodsThis study employed a retrospective study design using population-level health administrative data based on the interRAI Home Care assessment in Ontario (Canada) Prevalence of access to PC was determined and compared among those with and without SPMI. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression models were fitted to assess the association between PC access and several social-demographic and clinical factors.ResultsOf the 616,296 clients, 25.3% had SPMI and 2.5% accessed PC; 23.5% of those who accessed PC had SPMI. Of note, the association between SPMI and access was modified by gender (p = 0.02) and age (p = 0.04), where females with SPMI under 65 years had 15% lower odds (OR = 0.85, CI = 0.76, 0.95) of access compared to males without SPMI under 65 years. Also, females with SPMI aged 65–74 had 16% lower odds (OR = 0.84, CI = 0.76, 0.93) of access compared to males without SPMI in that age group. Together, these results indicate that women with SPMI under 75 years had lower odds of PC access than women without SPMI; this was not seen for males.ConclusionsOverall, persons with SPMI had lower access to palliative care compared to those without SPMI, and this association was modified by gender and age. Given that persons with SPMI represent approximately one quarter of home care clients, further research is needed to examine timing of provision of palliative care to persons with SPMI, as well as its outcomes in terms of symptom management and quality of life.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-026-02164-3.

Introduction

Background

Globally, an estimated 56.8 million people with life-limiting illness need palliative care (PC), but only 14% of them receive it [1]. Low access can be attributed to many factors, for example health polices, lack of training for healthcare professionals, country-specific socio-cultural issues, and misconceptions about PC [1].

Though it is recommended that a PC approach be considered early during a serious or life-limiting illness, in most cases it is offered or provided very late in the course of illness [2]. For example, a recent Canadian Institute for Health Information (CIHI) report [3] revealed that, of the 153,524 deaths captured between 2021 and 2022 across three provinces and one territory, 58% of those who died had received PC. Overall, there is limited information about the annual estimated number of people in Canada who require PC and what percentage of that population have access to it. This is in part due to lack of data standardization and insufficient measurement of access to PC across the country [2, 3]. However, a key goal of the government of Canada’s Framework on Palliative Care is ensuring Canadians have equitable access to PC [4]. Persons with severe and persistent mental illnesses (SPMI, e.g., schizophrenia, depression, bipolar disorders) have been identified as an underserved group with respect to PC [3], despite higher rates of morbidity and mortality [5].

There are many factors that contribute to reduced access to PC among persons with SPMI. For example, homelessness or incarceration, living alone or in self-isolation [6], and having limited social support [7] can impact access. System-level factors such as late medical diagnosis of life-limiting illness [6, 8], limited staff capacity and scarcity of resources [6], stigmatization and discrimination by healthcare providers [9], may also play a role.

Objectives

While studies exist, there is, overall, relatively little research focused on access to PC by persons with SPMI in Canada. To meet the government’s goals of equitable PC access, a better understanding of access and factors associated with access in the Canadian context is needed. This study aimed to fill that gap.

Methods

The STROBE checklist for cross-sectional studies was used to structure the manuscript [10].

Study design

Population-level anonymized data collected on all adults (i.e., 18 years or older) expected to receive home care services for at least 60 days (i.e., long-stay clients) in Ontario between 2019 and 2023 were used. Data are based on the interRAI Home Care (HC) assessment [11], which is completed at admission and every 6 months thereafter, or upon significant change in status. The most recent interRAI HC assessment of 616,296 unique individuals was used to get the most recent “clinical picture”.

As this study involved secondary analysis of anonymized data it was granted an ethics waiver by the [BLINDED] Research Ethics Board under Article 2.4 of the Tri-Council Policy Statement [12].

Variables

All data are based on the interRAI HC assessment [11].

The main variables of interest are access to PC and SPMI. With respect to PC services, coding options include whether it was not ordered/did not occur (score = 0), was ordered but not yet implemented (score = 1), was received on 1 or 2 of the last 3 days (score = 2) or received daily in the last 3 days (score = 3). For the purposes of this study, we interpreted PC access broadly and included receipt of PC (scores 2 or 3) as well as ordered/scheduled PC though it had not yet been received (score 1). We have added this information to the Methods section (see page 4). As such, access to PC was coded as “yes” if the person was offered or had received PC services.

The presence of SPMI was coded based on presence of any of the following psychiatric diagnoses listed on the assessment: bipolar disorder, depression, or schizophrenia (i.e., any among the three diagnoses were coded as present), regardless of whether active treatment was received. Note that the assessor does make a diagnosis but rather records the presence of a psychiatric diagnosis that is based on documentation, discussion with the person and their informal supports, or with formal healthcare providers who know the person well.

Several personal, social, functional, and clinical characteristics were also considered in examination of the relationship between access to PC among persons with SPMI, based on literature related to PC access, including specifically among persons with SPMI. Personal characteristics included age, gender, and health region. The health regions are based on Ontario’s fourteen Local Health Integration Network regions (LHINs) [13], which were renamed Home and Community Care Support Services in 2023 [14].

Social characteristics. Partners status was coded as “yes” if marital status was married or partner/significant other, and as “no” if never married, widowed, separated, or divorced. Making financial trade-offs was coded as “yes” if the person had made trade-offs among necessities (e.g., food, shelter, medications) because of limited funds in the previous month. The relationship between the home care client and their primary and secondary informal helpers was recorded as: child or child-in-law, spouse, partner/significant other, parent/guardian, sibling, other relative, friend, neighbour, or no informal helper. Informal helper status included three items related to whether helpers are unable to continue in caring activities (yes/no), the primary informal helper expresses distress (yes/no), and whether family or close friends express feeling overwhelmed (yes/no).

Functional characteristics included information on communication and cognition. The Communication scale is a sum of two items related to expressive and receptive communication; each is coded on a scale of 0 = Understood/Understands to 4 = Rarely or never understood/understands, producing a final score ranging from 0 to 8 [15]. Given the skewed nature of these data, scores of 0 were recoded into no impairment, scores of 1 and 2 into mild impairment, and scores of 3 or more into moderate or worse impairment. The Cognitive Performance Scale (CPS) is based on 4 items (daily decision-making, expression, short-term memory, and self-performance in eating), with scores ranging from 0 = Intact to 6 = Very severe impairment [16]. Scores were recoded into 0 = Intact; 1or 2 = Mild impairment; 3 or greater = Moderate or worse impairment.

Clinical characteristics included diagnoses and health instability. Diagnoses are recorded as 0 = Not present, 1 = primary reason for current services, 2 = present and receiving active treatment, or 3 = present but no active treatment (e.g., monitoring). In this study, diagnoses were coded as 0 = Not present and 1 = Present (score 1, 2, or 3). The following were included in this study: Alzheimer disease, dementia, any paralysis (i.e., hemiplegia, paraplegia, quadriplegia), multiple sclerosis, Parkinson’s disease, stroke/CVA, coronary heart disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), congestive heart failure, cancer and diabetes. The Changes in Health, End-stage disease and Symptoms and Signs (CHESS) is a measure of health instability based on signs and symptoms (e.g., vomiting, peripheral edema, dyspnea, weight loss, dehydration), worsening decision-making, worsening in self-performance of activities of daily living, and prognosis of six or fewer months to live (i.e., end-stage disease); scores range from 0 = No health instability to 5 = Very high health instability [17].

Analyses

All analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4. Frequencies were used to present categorical participant characteristics and means and standard deviation were used for continuous variables; chi-square tests were used to inform on differences between those with and without SPMI. The association between access to PC, SPMI, and covariates were identified by performing univariate and multivariable logistic regressions; interaction terms were introduced to the multivariable model assess how age and gender modified the relationship between SPMI and PC access. All variables were entered simultaneously into the multivariable model. Though model development wasn’t a study goal, overall model fit was assessed using the C-statistic. Statistical significance was set at 0.05.

It was decided that items with more than 5% of values missing would be excluded from the statistical analysis; however, no variables in the analysis had more than 5% missingness. As such, all variables were included in the analysis and missingness is reported in the Tables.

Results

Participant characteristics

Table 1 shows that, overall, home care clients were on average 80 years old, most were female (59.1%), had no partner (61.9%), and few had made financial trade-offs in the last month (3.2%). A higher proportion of home care clients lived in Central East (14.7%), Hamilton-Niagara-Haldimand-Brant (12.1%), Central (12%), Champlain (9.2%) and Southwest (8.1%) health regions. Impairment in communication and cognitive functioning were present among more than half of home care clients. Coronary heart disease (30.9%), diabetes (29.7%), dementia (26.2%) and cancer (18.7%) were the most common medical diagnoses. Approximately three quarters of clients experienced some level of health instability, with 12.9% having high or very high health instability. For just under half of clients (48.6%), children or children-in-law were the primary informal helper, followed by spouses (28.7%); the same pattern was seen for secondary informal helpers, though about one third did not have a secondary helper. 32% of informal helpers indicated that they were unable to continue with caring activities, 39.4% experienced distress, and 49% of clients had family or close friends who felt overwhelmed.

Population characteristics by presence of SPMI

aMissing n = 20 among those with SPMI and n = 165 for those without SPMI

Approximately 25.3% of home care clients had SPMI. Table 1 also shows that, compared to persons without SPMI, those with SPMI were significantly younger (75.9 years vs. 80.9 years), more often female (64.8% vs. 57.1%), more often did not have a partner (66.8% vs. 60.2%), and had a higher proportion of people who had made financial trade offs (5.4% vs. 2.4%). The proportion of home care clients with SPMI varied across the LHINs. There was a higher prevalence of caregiver distress among those supporting persons with SPMI on all three measures considered. Fewer persons with SPMI had no impairment in cognitive functioning (13.4% vs. 19.5%) and communication (37% vs. 42.4%). Except for congestive heart failure and cancer, those with SPMI had higher prevalence of all considered medical conditions and more commonly experienced health instability.

Factors associated with PC access

Approximately 2.5% of home care clients (N = 15,057) had accessed PC. Among those who accessed PC, 23.5% had SPMI.

Table 2 presents unadjusted and adjusted associations between the explanatory variables and PC access. SPMI was independently associated with decreased odds of PC access (OR = 0.91, CI = 0.87–0.94). In the multivariable model, age and gender showed a statistically significant interaction with SPMI in relation to PC access. More specifically, the results of the adjusted model showed that the association between SPMI and PC access was modified by gender (p-value = 0.02) and age (p-value = 0.04). The effect modification showed that females who were less than 65 years and had SPMI had 15% lower odds (OR = 0.85, CI = 0.76–0.95) of PC access compared to males of the same age without SPMI. Similarly, females who were aged 65–74 years with SPMI had 16% lower odds (OR = 0.84, CI = 0.76–0.93) of PC access compared to males of same age and had no SPMI. Together, these results indicate that women with SPMI aged less than 75 had lower odds of PC access than women without SPMI, but that no such association was evidenced for men.

Crude and Adjusted Odds ratios for access to PC by persons with SPMI compared to those without SPMI

After adjusting for covariates, several factors were associated with higher odds of access, including having a partner/significant; living in Erie St. Claire, Waterloo Wellington, South East, and North Simcoe Muskoka health regions; having moderate or worse communication impairment; paralysis; COPD; congestive heart failure; cancer; health instability; and having family or friends who report feeling overwhelmed. Conversely, the following variables were associated with lower odds of PC access: making financial trade-offs; living in Hamilton Niagara Haldimand Brant, Mississauga Halton, Toronto Central, Central East and North East health regions; having mild and moderate or worse cognitive impairment; Alzheimer disease; dementia; Parkinson disease; stroke/CVA; coronary heart disease; diabetes; and having a primary informal helper who is unable to continue in caring activities.

Result of the C-statistic (AUC = 0.8288, CI = 0.8253–0.8323) indicates that the overall multivariable regression model showed good discrimination between persons who had access to PC and those who did not.

Discussion

To our knowledge, very few studies have examined population-level access to PC among home care clients with SPMI in the province of Ontario, Canada. In the univariate analysis, those with SPMI had 9% lower odds of PC access compared to those without SPMI; reduced access to PC has been reported by others [5, 6]. The Canadian Institute for Health Information reported that underserved populations, which included persons with SPMI, often have late referrals for PC [3]. This could help to account for lower PC access for persons with SPMI in the current study. Note that studies in other countries have shown the opposite. For example, a population-based study in Taiwan reported that cancer patients with schizophrenia received more PC consultation services than those without schizophrenia [18]. Similarly, in France, cancer patients with schizophrenia had higher chance of receiving PC, though they were less likely to receive chemotherapy [8]. Possible reasons for higher access to PC for persons with SPMI in some countries could be due to integrated healthcare systems in these countries that effectively integrates mental health into their overall healthcare systems, whereas countries with more fragmented and siloed healthcare systems would have the opposite. The implications of the inequity in access to PC for persons with SPMI appears far reaching, as siloed/fragmented care leads to poor pain and symptom management, and other poor health outcomes [9, 19, 20].

This is, to our knowledge, among the first studies to examine how age, gender, and SPMI interact in relation to the association between having SPMI and PC access. In the adjusted logistic regression model, while SPMI was not statistically associated by itself with PC access, gender and age modified the association between SPMI and PC access, pointing to the complex association between gender, age and PC access.

The multivariable model showed that many personal, social, functional, and clinical factors were associated to PC access among home care clients. Compared to persons under 65 years, those 65 years or older had lower odds of access to PC. Current literature on access to PC among different age groups are conflicting. While Evers and colleagues [21] showed that older adults, especially those above 80 years, are more likely to access PC, some have indicated otherwise [3, 22]. The disparities in PC access among older adults could be due to clinical judgment of healthcare professionals in making decisions about prioritizing access to PC [22].

Having a partner/significant other was associated with higher odds of PC access, which has been reported elsewhere [23, 24], and could be due to the partner advocacy for referral to PC services [3, 6]. Making financial trade-offs in purchasing necessities such as food, rent, and heating due to limited funds was associated with lower odds of PC access, an indication that low socioeconomic status could be associated with disparities in PC access for persons with SPMI. Where people lived was associated with access in the multivariable model. For example, results indicated that persons living in the North East LHIN had 32% lower odds of PC access compared to those living in Central region, though there was no significant difference for those living in North West region. This is somewhat different than what was reported by Conlon and colleagues [25]; they found that decedent rural cancer residents in northern Ontario had 10% lower odds of receiving PC compared to those in southern regions. The variation of access across regions in Ontario warrants further investigation.

While presence of moderate or worse communication impairment was associated with higher odds of access, this study showed that persons with mild communication impairment or any impairment in cognitive function had lower odds of PC access, which has been reported elsewhere [26, 27].

Health instability as measured by the CHESS was significantly associated with higher odds of PC access, which, given its association with mortality, was not surprising [17, 28]. Similarly, it was expected that several diagnoses considered would be associated with higher odds of PC access, such as cancer [29] and COPD [30]. That Alzheimer and dementia were associated with lower odds of PC access was also expected, given that persons living with dementia have been identified as an underserved group [3].

This study has several strengths and limitations. The use of a large, population-level dataset provided enhanced statistical power for the study. While it allowed for robust analyses able to detect statistically significant differences, it may have also led to identification of differences that while statistically significant, are less meaningful. Also, the population-level nature of the data means that findings are representative of the current situation in Ontario’s home care sector. A limitation is the inability to know the severity of the diagnosed mental health conditions. We included a diagnosis of depression in the definition of SPMI, but it is possible that some individuals have mild depression. As such, it is possible that we have overestimated the prevalence of SPMI in the home care population. Future work should investigate whether other items or outcome measures embedded in the interRAI HC could provide information helpful to determining severity of depression – for example, medications or specific depressive symptoms. The use of administrative dataset limited the ability to adjust for other individual variables known to affect PC access, such as race or ethnicity and severity or stage of life-limiting illness that are not captured in the dataset. In particular, the absence of information on race or ethnicity is problematic, as it is known that persons who are racialized are underserved in Canada when it comes to access to PC [3]. As such, it is possible that the study results overestimate the impact of SPMI on PC access. Future work should not only consider ethnicity or race in modeling access to PC, but also the impact of the interaction between SPMI and ethnicity or race on PC access.

Also, the analyses are limited by the fact that the dataset does not include information on home care providers (e.g., staffing, experience) or other system-level variables that may impact access. Future research should seek to link system-level factors to individual-level assessments to better understand PC access overall, as well as access by those with SPMI.

Conclusions

This study showed that sex and gender modify the relationship between SPMI and access to PC in home care clients. The lack of access to a palliative approach to care may also increase the risk of poor pain and symptom management as well as other poor health outcomes.

The present study also highlights the need to consider age and gender of a person with SPMI when examining access to PC. Further research that examines PC in persons with SPMI should explore the timing of onset of receipt of PC relative to when they are diagnosed with life-limiting illnesses. Furthermore, future research should explore the impact of PC access on overall quality of life of persons with SPMI overall, and over time.
