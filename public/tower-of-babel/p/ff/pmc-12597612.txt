A Forgotten Foe Returns: The Diagnostic Odyssey of Kala‐Azar Masquerading as Myelodysplastic Syndrome in a Post‐Elimination Era
Authors: Sumaya Khan Mifty, Saiful Islam Chowdhury, Tahmidur Rahman, Tanveer Al Razi Khan, Swagota Roy, Faiza Ahmmed, Md Sabbir Hossain, Ibrahim Khalil
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe report a diagnostically complex case of visceral leishmaniasis (Kala‐azar) in a 73‐year‐old male from a country where the disease was eliminated in 2023. The patient presented with a 2‐month history of low‐grade fever, fatigue, progressive skin hyperpigmentation over 2 years, and hepatosplenomegaly. Initial investigations revealed pancytopenia (hemoglobin 10.4 g/dL, white blood cells 3500/mm3, platelets 60,000/mm3), prompting a differential diagnosis of Kala‐azar versus myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS). The rapid diagnostic test (rK39) for Kala‐azar was positive, but bone marrow examination showed dysplastic changes, hypercellularity, decreased myeloid‐erythroid ratio, binucleate erythrocytes, and maturation arrest at the myelocyte stage without Leishmania donovani (LD) bodies, complicating the diagnosis. Financial constraints precluded fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) testing for MDS confirmation. Repeated peripheral blood film (PBF) and rK39 testing (qDetect Kala‐azar) on February 6, 2025, confirmed Kala‐azar, ruling out MDS as the primary pathology. The patient responded well to liposomal amphotericin B treatment (3 mg/kg/day according to WHO regimen). This case underscores the challenge of diagnosing Kala‐azar in post‐elimination settings, particularly when clinical and laboratory features overlap with hematological disorders like MDS. It highlights the critical need for persistent diagnostic evaluation, including repeated serological and hematological testing, to differentiate infectious causes from malignancies in patients with unexplained pancytopenia. Clinicians must remain vigilant for re‐emerging infectious diseases, even in regions where they are deemed eliminated, to ensure accurate diagnosis and prompt treatment, ultimately improving patient outcomes in resource‐limited settings.Keywords: kala‐azar, myelodysplastic syndrome, pancytopenia, post‐elimination, visceral leishmaniasis

Summary

In post‐elimination settings, visceral leishmaniasis (VL) can mimic myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) with pancytopenia and dysplastic bone marrow changes, even without visible LD bodies. Clinicians must prioritize serological tests like rK39 in resource‐limited areas to avoid misdiagnosis and ensure timely treatment.

Introduction

Leishmaniasis is a vector‐borne parasitic disease caused by obligate intracellular protozoa of the genus Leishmania, transmitted primarily through the bites of infected female phlebotomine sandflies [1]. It is classified as one of the neglected tropical diseases and poses a significant global health burden, with an estimated 700,000 to 1 million new cases annually across its various forms [2]. The disease is endemic in 99 countries, placing over 1 billion people at risk, predominantly in tropical and subtropical regions including the Mediterranean Basin, East Africa, South Asia, and parts of the Americas [2, 3]. In the United States, sporadic autochthonous cases have been reported, though most are travel‐related [3]. Leishmaniasis manifests in three main clinical forms: visceral leishmaniasis (VL), cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL), and mucocutaneous leishmaniasis (MCL) [1, 2]. VL, also known as kala‐azar, is the most severe form, involving systemic infection of the reticuloendothelial system and characterized by irregular fever, anemia, weight loss, hepatosplenomegaly, and immunosuppression, which can be fatal if untreated [2, 4]. In South Asia, including Bangladesh, Leishmania donovani is the primary causative agent for VL, with anthroponotic transmission predominant, though zoonotic cycles involving domestic animals may contribute [5]. Globally, over 90 species of phlebotomine sandflies are proven or suspected vectors, with Phlebotomus argentipes being the main vector in the Indian subcontinent and Lutzomyia longipalpis in the Americas; domestic dogs often serve as key reservoirs in zoonotic transmission [5, 6].

A notable sequela of VL is post‐kala‐azar dermal leishmaniasis (PKDL), primarily associated with L. donovani infection [7]. PKDL typically develops 6 months to 5 years after apparent cure of VL in the Indian subcontinent, but can occur concurrently or de novo (without prior VL history) in 5%–15% of cases, with higher rates reported in East Africa (up to 50%) [7, 8]. In endemic areas like India and Sudan, de novo PKDL may account for 10%–20% of cases, potentially sustaining transmission [8, 9]. The clinical spectrum of PKDL includes hypopigmented macules, erythematous papules, nodules, and plaques, with rarer variants such as verrucous or hypertrophic lesions [7, 10]. Treatment options for leishmaniasis remain limited and challenging. For CL and MCL, pentavalent antimonials like sodium stibogluconate are still used in some regions, while liposomal amphotericin B (L‐AmB) is the first‐line therapy for VL in the Indian subcontinent due to its efficacy and safety [11, 12]. However, issues such as drug toxicity, prolonged regimens, high costs, and emerging resistance hinder effective management [11, 13]. Recent research emphasizes the role of Th1‐mediated immunity in parasite clearance, driving interest in immunotherapies involving cytokines, dendritic cells, and immunomodulators to complement chemotherapy [14]. Enhanced understanding of host–parasite dynamics and novel therapeutic developments are essential to reduce the disease's global impact. This case report details a rare presentation of VL mimicking myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) in a post‐elimination context, underscoring the importance of diagnostic awareness for prompt intervention.

Case History/Examination

A 73‐year‐old male presented to a tertiary hospital in Bangladesh on January 15, 2025, with a 2‐month history of low‐grade, intermittent fever, fatigue, and weakness, and a 2‐year history of progressive, painless, purplish, non‐itchy, non‐palpable, patchy hyperpigmentation on his extremities and abdomen (Figure 1). The fever subsided with paracetamol (500 mg every 6 h as needed) and was associated with a non‐productive cough and chest pain, but without chills, rigors, or evening spikes. He denied recent tuberculosis (TB) contact, abdominal pain, headache, dysuria, hematemesis, hemoptysis, epistaxis, or melena. Bowel and bladder habits were normal. The patient lived in a muddy hut in an endemic Kala‐azar zone, near cattle. On examination, he was underweight, anemic, and febrile but non‐icteric and non‐dehydrated. Peripheral lymph nodes were not palpable, bony tenderness was absent, and vital signs were normal (pulse and blood pressure within reference ranges). Abdominal examination revealed hepatosplenomegaly (liver 15.3 cm, spleen 16.4 cm) without ascites.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

The patient's presentation—fever, anemia, skin hyperpigmentation, and hepatosplenomegaly—raised suspicion of an underlying hematological or infectious disorder. Initial investigations on January 15, 2025, included a complete blood count (CBC) with erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), immunochromatographic tests (ICT) for dengue and malaria, ultrasonography (USG) of the abdomen, serum alkaline phosphatase (ALP), sputum examination for TB, and chest X‐ray. CBC revealed pancytopenia (hemoglobin 10.4 g/dL, white blood cells 3500/mm3, platelets 60,000/mm3) with an elevated ESR (38 mm in 1st h) (Table 1). ICT was negative for dengue and malaria, sputum for TB was negative, and chest X‐ray showed no abnormalities. USG confirmed hepatosplenomegaly (spleen 16.4 cm, liver 15.3 cm) without focal lesions, ascites, pleural effusion, or lymphadenopathy (Figure 2). Serum ALP was elevated (487 U/L). Peripheral blood film (PBF) on January 18, 2025, showed normocytic anemia, mild neutropenia, relative lymphocytosis, and no abnormal cells.

Given the pancytopenia and hepatosplenomegaly, differential diagnoses included Kala‐azar and myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS). Although Kala‐azar was eliminated in Bangladesh in 2023, a rapid diagnostic test (rK39) for Kala‐azar on January 21, 2025, was positive. Bone marrow examination on January 27, 2025, revealed hypercellularity, a decreased myeloid‐erythroid ratio, hyperactive erythropoiesis with normoblastic and megaloblastoid changes, binuclearity (> 10% dysplastic changes), active granulopoiesis with maturation arrest at the myelocyte stage, increased eosinophilic myelocytes, and normal megakaryocyte numbers with few dysplastic changes, but no amastigotes (LD bodies) (Figure 3). This created a diagnostic dilemma between Kala‐azar and MDS. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) to confirm MDS was not performed due to financial constraints. Repeat PBF and rK39 testing on February 6, 2025, again showed pancytopenia and confirmed Kala‐azar positivity (Figure 4), ruling out MDS as the primary pathology. Treatment with liposomal amphotericin B was initiated.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

The patient was diagnosed with Kala‐azar despite the absence of LD bodies in the bone marrow, based on repeated positive rK39 tests and consistent clinical findings. Liposomal amphotericin B, the drug of choice for visceral leishmaniasis in the Indian subcontinent, was administered, and the patient showed significant improvement, with resolution of fever and improved physical status. Follow‐up is ongoing to monitor for clinical and parasitological cure, defined as fever resolution, weight gain, hemoglobin and white blood cell count improvement, splenic regression (> 60%), and absence of parasites in bone marrow or splenic aspirates at the end of therapy.

Discussion

Epidemiological Context and Risk Factors

This case of a 73‐year‐old male in Bangladesh, initially suggestive of myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) but confirmed as visceral leishmaniasis (VL, kala‐azar), illustrates the diagnostic pitfalls of re‐emerging infections in post‐elimination eras. VL, caused by Leishmania donovani and vectored by phlebotomine sandflies, remains a major threat, with 50,000–90,000 new cases annually worldwide and 90% of fatal cases in Brazil, Ethiopia, Sudan, South Sudan, India, and Bangladesh [2, 15]. In 2023, Bangladesh became the first country validated by the World Health Organization (WHO) for eliminating VL as a public health problem, defined as < 1 case per 10,000 population at the sub‐district level, following a 2005 regional initiative with Nepal and India that reduced cases by over 95% from 2007 to 2021 through vector control, early detection, and treatment [16, 17]. By 2024, only 25 VL cases were reported in Bangladesh, but cross‐border risks persist, with 40% of historical cases in such areas [17, 18]. This patient's presentation in 2025 highlights elimination's vulnerability to factors like migration, climate change, and surveillance gaps, where untreated VL carries a near‐100% fatality rate within 2 years [2, 19]. The patient's muddy hut residence in an endemic zone near cattle aligns with risk factors: poor housing with cracks fosters Phlebotomus argentipes breeding, exacerbated by humidity, rainfall, and socioeconomic poverty [20, 21]. These elements emphasize ongoing transmission risks despite elimination status.

Diagnostic Challenges

The clinical features—low‐grade fever, fatigue, hepatosplenomegaly, and pancytopenia (hemoglobin 10.4 g/dL, white blood cells 3500/mm3, platelets 60,000/mm3)—prompted differentials including hematological malignancies (MDS, aplastic anemia, leukemia, myelofibrosis), infections (VL, tuberculosis, malaria, hepatitis), and autoimmune disorders like systemic lupus erythematosus [22]. Investigations ruled out dengue, malaria, and tuberculosis via negative immunochromatographic tests, sputum, and chest X‐ray; aplastic anemia was unlikely due to absent exposures (e.g., toxins, radiation) and present hepatosplenomegaly [23].

Diagnostic overlap between VL and MDS posed challenges. Bone marrow showed hypercellularity, reduced myeloid‐erythroid ratio, dyserythropoiesis (normoblastic/megaloblastoid changes, binuclearity > 10%), granulopoiesis arrest at myelocyte stage, and eosinophilic myelocytes—features mimicking MDS, where blasts are < 20% [24, 25]. No Leishmania donovani (LD) bodies were seen, consistent with VL's ability to induce marrow suppression and dysplasia [26]. The rK39 rapid test (98% sensitivity) was positive twice, confirming VL despite bone marrow's 60%–85% sensitivity [27, 28]. Splenic aspiration (gold standard, > 95% sensitivity) was avoided due to thrombocytopenia and hemorrhage risk, especially with limited expertise [28]. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) for MDS was infeasible owing to costs and availability in Bangladesh [29]. Epidemiological context and rK39 positivity established VL, with MDS‐like changes likely infection‐induced.

Initial VL oversight stemmed from elimination status, stressing vigilance for pancytopenia, hepatosplenomegaly, and fever in post‐elimination areas to prevent delays [30]. Absent LD bodies highlight serological tests' value in resource‐limited settings, where advanced diagnostics are scarce [27, 28].

Treatment and Outcomes

Liposomal amphotericin B (L‐AmB), WHO‐recommended for South Asian VL, led to fever resolution and improvement [11, 31]. FDA‐approved L‐AmB offers superior safety over alternatives like pentamidine or miltefosine; combinations (e.g., L‐AmB with miltefosine/paromomycin) reduce duration and resistance [11]. In East Africa, sodium stibogluconate‐paromomycin is standard [11]. Emerging agents like fexinidazole show promise [32]. Cure metrics include fever abatement, weight gain, hemoglobin/white cell rise, > 60% splenic regression, and parasite absence at treatment end, with six‐month follow‐up [33].

Implications for Clinical Practice and Public Health

Implications include clinician training for VL recognition post‐elimination, integrating risks like housing into algorithms, and addressing diagnostic barriers via affordable tools [18, 20]. Elimination ≠ eradication; sustained vector control, education, and surveillance are vital to avert resurgence [16, 17].

In summary, this case reminds us that VL persists in post‐elimination settings, demanding suspicion to avoid misdiagnosis. Meticulous diagnostics and acumen ensure timely care and prevent unwarranted treatments.
