Central retina thickness measured with spectral-domain optical coherence tomography in Parkinson disease: A meta-analysis
Authors: Yong Woo Lee, Myung-Nam Lim, Jae Yeon Lee, Yung-Ju Yoo
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background:Optical coherence tomography (OCT) can detect visual alterations associated with Parkinson disease, such as damage to the retinal nerve fiber layer or changes in retinal vasculature. Macula thinning in association with Parkinson disease (PD) remains controversial. Therefore, we conducted a meta-analysis to investigate the central retina thickness in PD measured using spectral-domain OCT (SD-OCT).Methods:We searched PubMed and the Excerpta Medica database to identify studies that compared macular thickness between patients with PD and healthy controls published before July 31, 2021. A random-effects model was used to examine PD-associated changes in macular thickness. Meta-regression analysis was performed by assessing heterogeneity, publication bias, and study quality.Results:Thirty-two studies with a cross-sectional design were selected, including 2118 patients with PD and 2338 controls. We identified significant differences in the thickness of the ganglion cell–inner plexiform layer (standardized mean difference [SMD], –0.41; 95% confidence interval [CI], –0.66 to –0.16; I2 = 80%), ganglion cell complex (SMD, –0.33; 95% CI, –0.50 to –0.17; I2 = 0%), and of all inner and outer sectors of the macula (SMD range, –0.21 to –0.56; all P < .05) between patients with PD and controls.Discussion:These results corroborate the increased prevalence of changes in OCT measures in individuals with PD, highlighting the efficacy of SD-OCT–determined macular thickness as a biomarker for PD. Our findings may provide helpful guidelines for clinicians in rapidly evolving areas of PD diagnosis.

1. Introduction

Parkinson disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder predominantly affecting elderly people,[1] which is characterized by the progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra.[2] Whilst motor dysfunction is among the major consequences, nonmotor symptoms are increasingly recognized given their significant impact on the burden PD imposes on affected individuals and on disease progression.[3] Accordingly, visual dysfunction frequently occurs in the early stages of PD and often precedes the occurrence of motor symptoms by years. The accumulation of phosphorylated-synuclein within the inner retinal layer, which is associated with Parkinson disease (PD), has been identified as a causal factor in the disruption of ganglion cell receptive functions, leading to visual impairments.[4–6] Additionally, abnormal expression of α-synuclein in the retina has been confirmed to contribute to retinal degeneration, thereby serving as a potential mechanism underlying the pathological changes observed in the retinas of individuals with PD.[7]

Previous meta-analyses mainly assessed PD-associated differences in the circumpapillary retinal nerve fiber layer and central retinal thickness.[8–10] Automatic segmentation of the macula has allowed a detailed analysis of each retinal layer. PD-associated changes in retinal thickness have been detected in the inner retinal layers, such as the macular ganglion cell–inner plexiform layer (GC-IPL), macular ganglion cell layer, and macular ganglion cell complex (GCC).[11–13] Dopaminergic amacrine cells are damaged due to retinal dopaminergic degeneration.[14] Retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) receive visual input from these amacrine cells through dense interconnections in the IPL.[14] Given that the macular region contains more than 50% of all RGCs, these cells may be more sensitive to pathological alterations occurring in PD than in the circumpapillary retinal nerve fiber layer.[15]

The clinical manifestations of PD are often difficult to diagnose during the early stages, since symptoms may not appear until as many as 50% of the dopaminergic nigral neurons have died. Moreover, direct assessment of clinical symptoms is difficult. Therefore, significant progress has been made toward identifying suitable biomarkers for PD.

To complement the limitations of previous meta-analyses, we performed a meta-analysis to identify differences in macular thickness between patients with PD and healthy controls measured using spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT).

2. Methods

2.1. Eligibility criteria

This study followed the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram for new systematic reviews, which included searches of databases and registers only.[16] Studies were included in this analysis based on the following criteria: original human data; case-control, cross-sectional, or prospective design; inclusion of patients with PD and age-matched controls; measurements made using SD-OCT and reported as mean and standard deviation; and sample size of at least 10 per group.

Studies were excluded based on the following criteria: unrelated to PD or not based on SD-OCT; use of time-domain OCT; language other than English; quality below standard as assessed using the quality assessment of diagnostic accuracy studies (QUADAS)-2 tool; or conference abstracts, letters to the editor, animal studies, reviews, and case studies.

2.2. Study selection and risk of bias assessment

PubMed and EMBASE were searched for potentially eligible studies from their dates of inception to July 31, 2021, including manuscripts published ahead of print. The critical search terms were “Retina” or “Macula lutea” or “Fovea centralis” and “Parkinson disease” and “Tomography,” “Optical coherence,” with details in Supplemental Content (Table S1, Supplemental Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K100). To overcome the low specificity of our computerized literature search, we went through a selection process in 3 steps. First, 2 reviewers (J.Y.L. and Y.J.Y.) independently checked the titles and abstracts to screen for articles related to PD and SD-OCT. Second, the reviewers determined study designs and types of publications by evaluating the entire text of the remaining studies. Third, the methodological quality of the remaining studies was assessed using the QUADAS-2 tool; QUADAS-2 is an evidence-based tool for systematically evaluating the quality of studies that validate diagnostic tests.[17] Additionally, to assess the quality of included studies reporting quantitative OCT measurements, we followed the Advised Protocol for OCT Study Terminology and Elements recommendations.

2.3. Data collection

All data identified in the selected studies were screened independently by 2 reviewers (Y.W.L. and Y.J.Y.). The following variables were collected from each of the included articles: author names and study title, publication year, study design, eye-selection method, OCT model, country in which the study was performed, presence of disease treatment, mean age, sex, number of eyes, and SD-OCT measurement outcome variables (means and standard deviations). Any discrepancies between the results of the 2 reviewers were resolved through discussion with the third author (M.N.L.).

2.4. Ethics and dissemination

This study will not need to provide ethical approval, because this study based on the published data.

2.5. Data synthesis and analysis

The analysis was performed using R statistical software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) and RevMan software (version 5.3). Means and standard deviations were used to determine the standardized mean differences (SMDs) and weighted mean differences (WMDs), including 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We used the inverse variance method of random-effects models. Heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochran Q test. The literature search results were considered heterogeneous if the P value of the chi-square value in the homogeneity test was <0.05. Potential publication biases were assessed using funnel plots and the Egger test.[18]

Meta-regression analysis was performed to assess the effects of the study characteristics and potential confounding factors on the effect sizes. The explanatory variables included the mean Hoehn and Yahr (H&Y) scale, eye-selection methods (i.e., single-eye vs paired-eye data sets), OCT model types, and the proportion of males in each study group. The residual between study variance was estimated using the restricted maximum-likelihood method. We also performed subgroup analyses according to OCT model types and eye-selection methods.

3. Results

Figure 1 shows a detailed summary of the selection process for the inclusion of studies in our meta-analysis. In the electronic literature search, 1450 publications (787 and 663 articles in the Excerpta Medica database and PubMed, respectively) were identified, of which 1117 duplicates were deleted automatically. Finally, 32 studies were included in the meta-analysis (Supplemental Table S2, http://links.lww.com/MD/K101): 11 measured macular GC-IPL thickness,[10,12,19–27] 22 measured macular thickness,[11,19,20,22–24,27–42] 6 measured macular GCC thickness,[26,28,39,43–45] and 6 measured macular volume.[19,23,26,27,29,30] All studies had a cross-sectional and prospective design. There was no significant difference in mean age between patients with PD and control groups in any of the selected studies. The H&Y scale score ranged from 1.21 to 3.92 in the PD group. However, the mean H&Y scale score was not reported in 11 studies.[13,20,25,26,28,30,31,35,39,43,44] All studies excluded patients with a history of glaucoma and age-related macular degeneration. The detailed exclusion criteria for each study are listed in Table S3, Supplemental Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K102. Eleven studies used OCT measurements of either eye of the participants.[12,19,22–24,31–34,44,45] The OCT models used in the included studies were as follows: 14 studies used Cirrus HD-OCT (Carl Zeiss Meditec, Dublin, CA),[12,13,19,20,22–24,31–36,43] 12 used Heidelberg Spectralis (Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany),[11,22,25,27,30,34,37,38,40–42,46] and 6 used other OCT models, including the Optovue RTVue-100 (Optovue), Nidec Co. SD-OCT (Optovue Freemont, CA), and OPKO/OTI (Ophthalmic Technologies, Toronto, Canada).[21,28,32,44,45,47]

3.1. GC-IPL thickness

Changes in macular GC-IPL thickness were investigated in 11 studies, totaling 511 patients with PD and 500 controls.[10,12,19–27] Given the similar reflectivity of the thicknesses of the ganglion cell layer and the IPL, which renders it difficult to detect the boundary between the 2 layers except in the nearest fovea, they were reported together as the GC-IPL thickness.[48] Patients with PD had a significantly lower mean GC-IPL thickness (SMD, –0.41; 95% CI, –0.66 to –0.16; P = .0011; I2 = 71%) than the controls, with an absolute reduction of–3.16 µm (95% CI, –5.03 to –1.29 µm; P = .0009; Figure S1, Supplemental Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K95 and Table 1). The decrease in GC-IPL thickness was observed in all subsectors of the macula: superotemporal (SMD, –0.66; 95% CI, –0.91 to –0.40; P < .0001), superior (SMD, –0.57; 95% CI, –0.78 to –0.37; P < .0001), superonasal (SMD, –0.73; 95% CI, –0.99 to –0.46; P < .0001), inferonasal (SMD, –0.74; 95% CI, –1.03 to –0.45; P < .0001), inferior (SMD, –0.72; 95% CI, –0.97 to –0.47; P < .0001), and inferotemporal (SMD, –0.67; 95% CI, –0.88 to –0.47; P < .0001; Figure S2, Supplemental Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K96 and Table 1).

3.2. Macular GCC thickness

Macular GCC thickness was investigated in 6 studies with a total of 238 patients with PD and 236 controls.[26,28,39,43–45] It was significantly reduced in patients with PD compared to that of the controls (SMD, –0.33; WMD, –2.96 µm; all P < .0001), with an absolute decrease of 2.96 mm (95% CI, –430 to –1.63 µm; P < .0001; Figure S1, Supplemental Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K95 and Table 1).

3.3. Macular volume and total macular thickness

Macular volumes, which were examined in 6 studies, totaling 433 patients with PD and 494 controls,[19,23,26,27,29,30] were not significantly different between these groups (SMD, –0.12; 95% CI, –0.26 to 0.02; P = .0866; Figure S1, Supplemental Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K95 and Table 1). Macular thickness, investigated in 22 studies comprising 1279 patients with PD and 1511 controls,[11,19,20,22–24,28–42] was significantly reduced in all inner and outer macular sectors (SMD range, –0.21 to –0.41; WMD range, –3.87 to –7.44 µm; all P < .05; Figure S3, Supplemental Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K97 and Table 1). The inner temporal sector exhibited the greatest thinning (WMD, –5.92 µm; 95% CI, –8.37 to –3.47 µm; P < .0001). The thickness of outer plexiform layer increased significantly by mean difference of 0.24 μm (95% CI, 0.04 to 0.43 μm; Figure S4, Supplemental Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K98 and Table 1).

3.4. Meta-regression analysis

Meta-regression was performed for 22 studies to compare macular thickness, for 6 studies to compare macular volume, and for 11 studies to compare macular GC-IPL thickness between patients with PD and controls. For other factors, the number of studies using SD-OCT measurements was not sufficient to perform meta-regression. Our analysis revealed that the effect size of the difference in macular thickness between groups was significantly correlated with the type of OCT model (β = –3.286; 95% CI, –3.706 to –2.866; P < .001). However, the mean age difference between study groups, eye-selection method, sex proportion, and H&Y score did not significantly affect the effect sizes of the differences in macular and GC-IPL thickness among patients with PD (P > .05). Except for fovea thickness, no other total macular thicknesses of the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study subsectors were significantly correlated with the OCT model type (Table S4, Supplemental Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K103).

3.5. Subgroup analysis

This analysis was performed to compare the effect sizes between studies with single-eye data sets (i.e., selected measurements of either eye of each participant) and those with paired-eye data sets (i.e., selected measurements of both the eyes of each participant) (Table S5, http://links.lww.com/MD/K104, Figure S5, Supplemental Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K99). Among the studies with both single- and paired-eye data sets, we observed significant differences in macular volume, macular thickness of inner sectors, and mean macular GC-IPL thickness (P < .05). However, the differences in macular volume and thickness of the 2 outer sectors did not significantly differ among studies with a paired-eye data set (Table S5, Supplemental Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K104).

Because the meta-regression results suggested that the OCT model types significantly affected the effect sizes of differences in macular foveal thickness between groups, we performed further subgroup analyses according to OCT devices (Table S6, Supplemental Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K105). Taking into account Cirrus, Spectralis, and RTVue-100 devices, the decrease in macular thickness among patients with PD remained statistically significant when compared with that of the controls.

3.6. Publication bias and quality assessment

Mild heterogeneity was identified in the pooled effect estimates. No publication bias was identified in most analyses according to the Egger test, except for 2 subsectors of GC-IPL thickness and macular outer nuclear layer thickness (Table 1).

The QUADAS-2 tool (Table S7.1, Supplemental Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K106) was used to evaluate the risk of bias and applicability of the results. Most of the included studies had low applicability of the results regarding patient selection, index tests, and reference standards. There were few concerns regarding the risk of bias in patient selection. The risk of bias regarding the flow and timing was unclear in some studies based on a lack of specification of the time interval between diagnosis of PD and OCT examination and missing information on whether the controls underwent neurological examination to exclude underlying neurologic disease.[11–13,19–24,26–43,45–47] Further, the studies of Bayhan et al,[45] Tugcu et al,[41] and Kaur et al[25] featured small sample sizes (N < 25).

Based on the Advised Protocol for OCT Study Terminology and Elements recommendations (Table S7.2, Supplemental Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K107),[49] we identified the OCT acquisition settings, funduscopic imaging, and data selection process after acquisition as the most common sources of bias (Table S7.2, Supplemental Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K107).

4. Discussion

This meta-analysis found that macular dimensions measured using SD-OCT, including total macular, GC-IPL, and GCC thicknesses, as well as macular volume, were significantly reduced in PD patients compared with healthy controls. In subgroup analyses, we observed that the differences in the macular thicknesses of the outer sectors were statistically significant in PD among studies with a single-eye data set, but not among those with a paired-eye data set. These findings improve our understanding of the differences in SD-OCT measurements in PD and indicate the potential of OCT measurements for exploring the mechanisms of neuronal damage in this disease.

Our results accurately demonstrated that the retinal inner layers, such as the macular GC-IPL and GCC, were significantly thinner in patients with PD than in controls. Notably, the strong association between decreases in GC-IPL thickness and PD was maintained in the subsectoral analysis. RGCs are the bridging neurons of the retina, and their cell bodies mostly lie within the retinal ganglion layers and dendrite branches of the IPL in the macular region.[50] A thinner macular GC-IPL therefore implies fewer RGCs in the eyes of patients with PD. RGCs, which convey visual information directly to the central nervous system via the optic nerve, have characteristics similar to those of neurons in the brain. The finding that the number of RGCs is reduced in patients with PD corroborates the hypothesis that PD-associated pathological changes affect both the central nervous system and RGCs, and the damage progresses with increasing disease duration.[51] It was further suggested that the reduction in RGCs may explain the disturbances of the circadian rhythm in patients with PD as it leads to retinal melanopsin system dysfunction.[52]

RGCs are particularly vulnerable to neurodegenerative damage due to associated mitochondrial dysfunction, increased sensitivity to oxidative stress, and energy depletion.[53,54] There are 3 hypotheses regarding the underlying mechanism of retinal thickness reduction in patients with PD. First, accumulation of phosphorylated α-synuclein in the inner retina has been previously reported to directly damage RGCs.[4,5] This is particularly evident in the inner retina, including the areas of the GC-IPL and the interface between the IPL and inner nuclear layer.[4,5] Second, levodopa, a precursor of dopamine, is an intermediate product of the melanin biosynthesis pathways and is produced in retinal cells.[55,56] Cells within the human retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) release levodopa as an intermediate product in the melanin biosynthetic pathway, serving as a precursor for dopamine.[55] Patients with PD have diminished retinal dopamine levels, leading to a decrease in thickness of the inner retinal layers.[56,57] Third, dopaminergic degeneration can damage the circuit connectivity of amacrine cells, resulting in remodeling of RGCs and their dendrites.[58] However, since all of the studies included in the present analysis had cross-sectional designs, this phenomenon could not be definitively confirmed.

Central retinal changes provide objective information on the differential diagnoses of neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer disease, multiple system atrophy, and progressive supranuclear palsy.[58] Alzheimer disease spares the retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) in the temporal quadrant but predominantly affects the superior and inferior quadrants, indicating involvement of retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) projecting to the peripheral magnocellular pathway unrelated to visual acuity. Owning to α-synuclein accumulation in the inner retina and mitochondrial dysfunction, RGC loss preferentially involves the parvocellular cells.[59] The pattern of axonal loss observed in OCT studies points toward the preferential involvement of the temporal quadrant.[8,12,31,34,58,60–63] This pattern is clearly distinguishable from the preferential loss of magnocellular cells documented among other Parkinsonian syndromes, such as multiple system atrophy,[59,64] and resembles mitochondrial optic neuropathies.[53] Recent histological studies and mathematical modeling of retinal ganglion cell (RGC) loss in Leber hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON) clearly describe the higher vulnerability of parvocellular cells to mitochondrial dysfunction. This vulnerability to mitochondrial dysfunction, especially oxidative stress, can be attributed to the high energetic demand relative to a low energetic potential. This is influenced by the high surface area/volume ratio and the absence of myelin around the axons in the unmyelinated portion of the optic nerve.[65]

In a comparative study by Mendoza-Santiesteban et al, multiple system atrophy (MSA) patients exhibited significant thinning of the RNFL and ganglion cell complex (GCL + IPL), primarily in the inferior quadrant. This pattern distinguished MSA from Parkinson disease (PD). Progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP) shows distinct optical coherence tomography (OCT) features, including greater thinning of the superior peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (pRNFL), macular ganglion cell layer, inner plexiform layer, inner nuclear layer, and macular volume compared to PD.

The subgroup analysis showed that the differences in macular thickness between outer sectors observed in patients with PD were statistically significant in studies using single-eye data sets, but not in those based on paired-eye examinations. The selection bias among the included studies may explain these findings. No randomization of eye choice was performed in single-eye studies. The bias resulting from the preferred selection of eyes with a thinner retinal layer may therefore have skewed the findings as compared with those of the studies on paired-eye data. Moreover, the general heterogeneity in retinal thickness between the eyes may also have confounded the obtained results.

Various limitations of the included studies were identified in this meta-analysis, warranting future research. First, while our findings did support the hypothesis that the loss of retinal nerve cells occurs concomitant with brain injury in patients with PD, a definite confirmation cannot be provided exclusively based on studies with cross-sectional designs. Longitudinal studies recruiting preclinical patients with PD are needed for thorough examination of cortical degeneration and additional disease-specific hallmarks to fully understand changes in the retinal layer over the course of this disease. Second, future studies should use appropriate methodologies to validate the clinical usefulness of OCT measurements as an alternative biomarker for diagnosing PD. Such methodologies must include suitable statistical analyses, including receiver operating characteristic curves and false-positive or true-positive fractions.

Although this meta-analysis comprehensively assessed most SD-OCT measurements of the macula,[66] it had several limitations. First, both disease severity and age were heterogeneous among the included studies, which may have affected the estimations of the true effect sizes. The Unified H&Y score of the included patients with PD ranged from 1.21 to 3.92 among the selected studies. The mean age also varied between studies, albeit diagnosis was based on explicit criteria in almost all included studies. Despite age-matching the PD patients in this study with healthy controls, there were variations in disease duration and severity among them, leaving the association with retinal OCT changes uncertain.[40,57] Prior research has indicated a negative correlation between the overall thickness of the central retina and UPDRS motor scores, as well as between the thickness of the inner retinal layers and both the overall UPDRS scale[56] and the Hoehn-Yahr scale.[38] Second, the majority of studies did not report on the presence of absence of cognitive impairment in the included patients. Finally, only a small number of studies included drug-naive patients, which is crucial to avoid possible bias from antiparkinsonian medication. Altogether, future studies should assess longitudinal changes in OCT measurements during disease progression to validate the findings of the current study.

Meanwhile, our study had some limitations. Firstly, most of the included studies did not match axial length or did not report whether there is a difference in axial length between PD patients and healthy controls. However, it has been found that axial length affects the retinal thickness assessed by OCT in normal eyes and in diseased eyes. But whether axial length affects the retinal thickness assessed by OCT in PD has not been reported. In addition, concomitant eye diseases might be present in the participants, potentially affecting the assessment of retinal thickness. Although all the articles, except one, reported that patients with eye diseases possibly affecting OCT assessment results were excluded (Supplemental Table S2, http://links.lww.com/MD/K101), it is not verifiable how well the exclusion criteria were applied in the individual studies.

Secondly, the OCT devices used in the included studies were of various brands produced by different companies. There is wide diversity in analysis protocols, including autonomic, semi-autonomic, or autonomic with manual adjustment and manual segmentation. All these factors contribute to heterogeneity. It has been reported that there is a significant difference in the absolute values of retinal thickness measured by different OCT devices. However, subgroup analysis on the OCT devices showed consistent results. Nevertheless, considering the high heterogeneity in some effect sizes, the results should be interpreted with caution.

The groundbreaking discovery of OCT has contributed significantly to the advancement of ophthalmology, and SD-OCT is a noninvasive, high-speed, stratified tool for analyzing retinal structure. Based on the ability to yield high-resolution images, this technique is ideal for studying retinal and ocular neurodegenerative diseases, including glaucoma and neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorders. Recently, it was demonstrated that GC-IPL thickness was associated with a subsequent decline in general cognition.[67] Therefore, improvements of OCT measurements in sensitivity and specificity, such as combined SD-OCT metric analyses, should be the subject of future research.

In summary, based on 32 carefully selected studies, we found retinal layer thinning in patients with PD at various stages of disease, which was determined using noninvasive SD-OCT techniques. Given the differences in macular thickness observed between patients with PD and controls, changes in SD-OCT measurements may be a suitable biomarker for PD. Thus, this technique could be helpful for the early identification of individuals with a high risk of developing PD, who may benefit from additional examinations, including amyloid positron-emission tomography imaging and detailed neuropsychological evaluations. Our data indicate that SD-OCT measurements are significantly altered in individuals with PD compared to controls.
