KIF26B promotes bladder cancer progression through activating the NF-κB signaling pathway
Authors: Dan-Ni Chen, Jia-Ming Wang, Feng-Hao Zhang, Hong-Ying Ye, Yi-Ru Chen, Feng Zhao, Xiao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryKinesin superfamily plays pivotal role in tumor progression. Our previous study identified KIF26B as a novel biomarker for bladder cancer diagnosis and prognosis. Herein, we illustrated another role of KIF26B in bladder cancer progression through stimulating the NF-κB signaling pathway. Mechanically, KIF26B activated the NF-κB signaling pathway by enabling nuclear localization of NF-κB subunit p65 without influencing its protein stability. Instead, KIF26B could recruit TRIM21 and add K63-linked ubiquitin to p65, which allowed its nuclear localization. Further, PKCε responded to TNFα and mediated the phosphorylation of KIF26B at the S592 site, further increasing the interaction between KIF26B and p65. In turn, the activated NF-κB signaling pathway elevated the transcription of kif26b and her2, forming a KIF26B/NF-κB positive feedback loop and further promoting bladder cancer progression. Combining radiation and KIF26B blockage therapy yielded better anti-tumor effects.

Introduction

Bladder cancer (BCa) is a global critical health concern, ranking as the 10th most commonly diagnosed cancer worldwide.1 A relatively large number of patients with BCa displayed muscle-invasive BCa at the initial diagnosis,2 highlighting the importance of identifying new biomarkers and getting a deeper understanding of BCa.

Kinesin superfamily belongs to motor protein system within cells and shoulders the responsibility of cargo transportation.3,4 In normal condition, expression of kinesin superfamily is strictly regulated to prevent undue activation.5,6 However, in pathological condition, especially in tumor state, their expression is dysregulated and leads to redundant cell survival, growth or survival.3,7 In our previous studies, we identified the kinesin superfamily member KIF26B as a novel diagnostic and prognostic biomarker for BCa.8 Mechanically, we observed that KIF26B responded to Wnt3a stimulation and activated the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway.8 Further, KIF26B could translocate NSUN2 into the nucleus and maintain RNA m5C modification in response to IL-6 stimulation.9 Due to the diversity of cargoes with which kinesin could interact, whether KIF26B could respond to another cytokine stimulation and mediate a totally different signaling pathway deserved to be investigated.

The NF-κB signaling pathway activates from extracellular stimulation including LPS, TNFα, EGF, VEGF, and so on.10 Then, core signaling molecules, NF-κB dimers (e.g., p65 subunit plus p50 subunit), isolate from IκB to escape ubiquitination-mediated degradation. Hence, NF-κB subunits can translocate into nucleus, bind to the promoters of targeted genes, and elevate their transcription.11,12 Among the potential cytokines that could activate NF-κB signaling cascade, patients with BCa showed greater serum level of TNFα, and higher TNFα production often propose worse overall survival probability of BCa.13 Besides, TNFα-activated NF-κB signaling cascade is a promoter for muscle-invasive BCa through increasing occurrence of epithelial-mesenchymal transition and reducing its sensitivity to cisplatin-based chemotherapy, radiotherapy, as well as anti-PD-1-based immunotherapy.14,15,16 Therefore, understanding novel roles of TNFα and the detailed mechanism of NF-κB signaling activation, especially its nuclear translocation in BCa, is urgent and meaningful.

This study unveiled how KIF26B could activate NF-κB signaling pathway in BCa. KIF26B bound to p65 and recruited TRIM21 to modify K63-linked ubiquitination of p65 without influencing p65 protein stability. This allowed p65 nuclear translocation and increased the transcription of kif26b and her2. Also, TNFα elevated the interaction between KIF26B and p65 through PKCε-mediated KIF26B phosphorylation at S592. Combining kif26b silencing and radiation therapy exaggerated the anti-tumor effects of both monotherapies.

Results

KIF26B activates the NF-κB signaling pathway

To explore the novel role of KIF26B in BCa progression, we established kif26b stable knockdown T24 and T5637 cells. Then, we performed RNA-seq with T24 cells to explore the roles of KIF26B in BCa (Figure 1A). According to the results from RNA-seq, we obtained 1,819 genes that exhibited significant changes after silencing kif26b, with 651 genes exhibiting downregulation trend and 1,168 genes exhibiting upregulation trend (Figures 1A and S1A). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of RNA-seq revealed that differentially expressed genes were enriched in cellular processes including “transcription regulator activity,” “translation regulator activity,” “localization,” and so on (Figure S1B). Further, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis identified that differentially expressed genes were enriched in pathway such as “ECM-receptor interaction,” “cytoskeleton in muscle cells,” and “axon guidance” (Figures 1B and S1C). These analyses were in concert with the traditional functions of KIF26B. Notably, the “TNF signaling pathway” was specially enriched, indicating that KIF26B had a potential role in regulating the TNF signaling pathway (Figure 1B). The TNF signaling pathway was closely related to the NF-κB signaling pathway where TNFα functioned as the upstream activator of the NF-κB signaling pathway.10 Silencing kif26b decreased the protein level of phosphorylated NF-κB (p65) and TRAF2 while also decreasing IκB degradation both at basal state or in response to TNFα stimulation (Figures 1D and S2A). However, protein level of total NF-κB (p65) was not influenced (Figures 1D and S2A). To the contrary, overexpression of KIF26B increased the protein level of phosphorylated NF-κB (p65) and TRAF2 while also increasing IκB degradation both at basal state or in response to TNFα stimulation (Figures 1E and S2B). Similarly, protein level of total NF-κB (p65) was not influenced (Figures 1E and S2B). Accompanied with these, genes that were under NF-κB signaling regulation (Il6, Bcl2, and Ccnd1) also showed similar trend. Silencing kif26b decreased their transcription level while overexpression of KIF26B increased their transcription level both at basal state or in response to TNFα stimulation. (Figures 1F, 1G, S1D, S2C, and S2D). Notably, the NF-κB signaling pathway could be activated by other cytokines, including IL-1β and HMGB1.17 However, silencing kif26b impaired IL-1β-mediated rather than HMGB1-mediated NF-κB signaling pathway activation (Figure S2E). Further, silencing traf2 had no significant effect on NF-κB signaling activation caused by KIF26B overexpression (Figure S2F). These results led us to hypothesize that KIF26B activated NF-κB signaling pathway through increasing the nuclear localization of p65. As expected, knockdown of kif26b decreased nuclear localization p65 both at basal state or in response to TNFα stimulation (Figure 1H). Similarly, silencing kif26b abrogated upregulated colony formation ability of BCa cells caused by TNFα stimulation (Figure 1I). Collectively, these results demonstrated that KIF26B activated NF-κB signaling pathway through enabling p65 nuclear translocation.

TNFα facilitated association between KIF26B and p65 through KIF26B phosphorylation

KIF26B belongs to the kinesin superfamily, which helps transport related cargos along microtubule.18 Given this evidence, we asked whether KIF26B-enabled p65 nuclear-translocation was dependent on microtubule dynamics. Herein, we introduced microtubule polymerization reversible inhibitor nocodazole and found that nocodazole truly inhibited p65 nuclear-translocation either at basal state or at KIF26B-overexpressed state (Figure 2A). This result indicated that KIF26B-mediated p65 nuclear-translocation was dependent on normal microtubule dynamics. Based on this, we examined whether there existed a possible interaction between KIF26B and p65. Interestingly, KIF26B was able to interact with p65 either endogenously or exogenously (Figures 2B and 2C). Interestingly, TNFα was able to increase interaction between KIF26B and p65 (Figure 2D). Notably, the subcellular localization of KIF26B was restricted to cytoplasm, and the interaction between KIF26B and p65 only existed in the cytoplasm (Figure S3A). Further, TNFα stimulation failed to enable nuclear translocation of KIF26B (Figure S3B). This phenomenon may suggest that KIF26B translocated p65 to the nuclear pore and released it. Then, we aimed to determine the exact domain that mediated the interaction between KIF26B and p65. Commonly, KIF26B is divided into 3 main domains (N-terminal, motor, and C-terminal) while p65 was divided into 2 main domains (RHD and TAD). We observed that KIF26B utilized its motor domain to associate with the RHD domain of p65 (Figure S3C). As TNFα often increased phosphorylation of related signaling molecules to enhance or reduce binding affinity between different partners,19,20 we conducted molecular docking with the help of AlphaFold3 to target KIF26B and p65 to determine the exact interaction residue (Figure 2E). According to AlphaFold3, the interface area of KIF26B (Uniprot entry: Q2KJY2)-p65 (Uniprot entry: Q04206) complex was 9277.5 Å2 with ΔiG −85.6 kcal/mol and ipTM score 0.76. According to molecular docking and Scansite 4.0, we determined 4 potential phosphorylated sites (Figure 2F). Following this clue, we constructed S592A and S592E KIF26B mutants to mimic non-phosphorylation and constant-phosphorylation states (Figure S3D). In response to TNFα stimulation, S592A could not increase the phosphorylation of KIF26B and its association with p65 (Figures 2G and S3E). To the contrary, S592E exhibited increased phosphorylation of KIF26B and association with p65 whenever stimulated by TNFα, compared with wild-type KIF26B or S592A KIF26B (Figures 2H and S3F). These results indicated that phosphorylation of KIF26B at S592 was necessary for TNFα-mediated binding between KIF26B and p65.

S592 phosphorylation of KIF26B is pivotal for NF-κB signaling activation and BCa progression

To proceed further and to establish linkage between the phosphorylation of KIF26B and NF-κB signaling activation or BCa progression, we found that overexpression of wild-type KIF26B, but not S592A KIF26B, could increase the protein level of phosphorylated p65 in the basal and TNFα-stimulated states (Figure 3A). Neither wild-type KIF26B overexpression nor S592A KIF26B overexpression had significant impact on the protein level of total p65 (Figure 3A). Consistent with this result, wild-type KIF26B responded to TNFα stimulation in increasing nuclear localization of p65, while S592A KIF26B failed to do so (Figure 3B). Additionally, genes that were targeted by NF-κB signaling pathway showed elevated transcription level under TNFα stimulation in wild-type KIF26B group but not in S592A KIF26B group (Figure 3C). Similarly, overexpression of wild-type KIF26B, but not S592A KIF26B, promoted colony formation ability (Figure 3D). Then, we established subcutaneous tumor model and confirmed that overexpression of wild-type KIF26B elevated tumor growth while overexpression of S592A KIF26B failed to elevate tumor growth (Figures 3E and 3F). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining targeting KI67 showed similar results (Figure 3G). PKCε is involved in TNFα-mediated KIF26B phosphorylation

Next, we sought to infer the exact mediator that was involved in TNFα-mediated KIF26B phosphorylation. According to the prediction from Scansite 4.0, it was determined that a kinase PKCε targets KIF26B phosphorylation. Classically, PKCζ and PKCλ/ι are two PKC subunits that participate in TNFα-induced NF-κB signaling pathway activation.21 PKCζ recruits co-transcription factors to phosphorylate NF-κB subunit p65 and induce transcriptional activation in the nucleus.21,22 PKCλ/ι could be recruited to cell surface receptor such as TNF receptor and IL-1 receptor to activate IKK complex and degrade IκB.21,23 Discovered more than three decades ago, PKCε is a novel PKC subunit that also activates NF-κB signaling pathway.24,25 In BCa, PKCε is an oncogene and promotes cell invasion.26 TNFα stimulation is able to activate PKCε.27,28 Herein, we found that TNFα promoted cell membrane-localization of PKCε, which is the active form of PKCε29 (Figure 4A). In vitro kinase assay proved that PKCε could phosphorylate KIF26B at S592 (Figure 4B). TNFα elevates association between KIF26B and PKCε (Figure 4C). In the presence of PKCε, TNFα is able to increase phosphorylation of KIF26B as well as the association between KIF26B and NF-κB (Figure 4D). However, silencing pkcε abrogates the aforementioned effects (Figure 4D). Consistent with these results, in the presence of wild-type PKCε, but not its catalytic-dead K437R mutant, TNFα is able to increase the phosphorylation of KIF26B and its association with p65 (Figure 4E). Further, we found that increased p65 nuclear translocation induced by TNFα could be abrogated by silencing pkcε (Figure 4F). Interestingly, depriving kif26b decreased the ubiquitination level of p65, which was attributed to decreased K63-linked ubiquitination level, but not K48-linked ubiquitination level (Figure 5C). In concert to this result, either overexpression of wild-type KIF26B or S592A-KIF26B did not impact the protein degradation speed of p65 (Figure S4A). K63-linked ubiquitination often mediates interaction between different partners, giving it the potential to mediate nuclear translocation of molecules.30 As KIF26B could bind to p65 and regulate its K63-linked ubiquitination, we asked whether KIF26B could recruit potential ubiquitinase to p65 and catalyze K63-linked ubiquitination. One previous study found that TRIM21 was able to catalyze K63-linked ubiquitination of p65, which was helpful for its nuclear localization.31 In vitro ubiquitination assay further confirmed that TRIM21 could catalyze K63-linked ubiquitination of p65 (Figure S4B). Given this evidence, we explored whether KIF26B was able to recruit TRIM21 targeting p65. As expected, silencing kif26b decreased the association between TRIM21 and p65 (Figure 5E). Compared with wild-type KIF26B, S592A KIF26B reduced the association between TRIM21 and p65 (Figure 5F). Additionally, increased K63-linked ubiquitination of p65 after KIF26B overexpression could be reduced after silencing trim21 (Figure 5G). In trim21-silenced BCa cells, overexpression of KIF26B failed to increase K63-linked ubiquitination of p65 (Figure 5H). Consistent with these results, elevated nuclear localization of p65 after KIF26B overexpression was downregulated by depriving trim21 (Figure S4C). Collectively, these results suggest that KIF26B could recruit TRIM21 to catalyze K63-linked ubiquitination of p65, which enables its nuclear localization.

One of the main functions of the NF-κB signaling pathway is to induce the transcription of targeted genes. Notably, TNFα increased the transcription and expression of KIF26B, which was blocked by NF-κB inhibitor (Figures 6A and 6B). With the help of JASPAR database, we scanned potential p65-targeted sites within the promoter of kif26b. Then we wanted to determine the exact region that facilitated p65-mediated transcription (Figure 6G). HER2 is downstream of the KIF26B/NF-κB signaling axis

To explore the downstream processes of the established KIF26B/NF-κB signaling axis, we conducted ChIP-seq targeting p65 after silencing kif26b in T24. Then, we combined the data from RNA-seq after silencing kif26b in BCa cells and the aforementioned ChIP-seq data to choose genes that showed reduced transcript and reduced p65 enrichment within promoter after silencing kif26b. According to this criterion, one gene, her2, was picked (Figures 7A and 7B). HER2, known as human epidermal growth factor receptor 2, is distributed in the cell surface and is involved in cancer progression and metastasis.32 As the NF-κB signaling pathway is involved in reducing sensitivity to radiotherapy, and HER2 expression was frequently correlated with anti-tumor effects of radiotherapy,33,34 we tried to decipher the link between the KIF26B/NF-κB signaling axis and HER2 expression. We found that overexpression of wild-type KIF26B, but not its S592A mutant, increased the transcription and protein level of HER2 either in the basal state or TNFα-stimulated state (Figures 7C and 7D). Consistent with these results, in the presence of wild-type KIF26B, the enrichment of p65 to the promoter of her2 was greater than that in the presence of S592A KIF26B (Figure 7E). Recently, radiation-based therapy was used to treat multiple types of cancer, including BCa.35,36 As KIF26B was able to upregulate the expression of HER2, which is an obstacle for radiation-based therapy,37,38 we wanted to decipher whether blocking KIF26B was able to increase the efficiency of radiation-based therapy. As shown by colony formation assay, silencing kif26b exaggerated the negative effects of radiation on BCa cells (Figures 7G and S5A). However, this effect was reversed after the overexpression of HER2 (Figures 7G and S5A). Then, we established subcutaneous tumor model and treated the mice models with radiation (Figure 7H). Results showed that combining silencing of kif26b with radiation-based therapy achieved greater anti-tumor effects than monotherapy (Figure 7H). IHC staining targeting KI67 also exhibited similar results (Figure 7I). These results point to a novel strategy to treat BCa.

Discussion

Different from our previous study in elucidating KIF26B-promoted BCa progression through activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway,8 this study proposed another role for KIF26B in mediating BCa progression. Herein, KIF26B was found to activate the NF-κB signaling pathway through promoting nuclear translocation of NF-κB subunit p65. Though it seemed contradictory when compared with our previous study, possible explanation may be put forward as follows. In the previous study, Wnt3a stimulation enabled interaction between KIF26B and Wnt/β-catenin signaling molecules while herein, TNFα stimulation increased association between KIF26B and p65. Different upstream molecules shaped different binding partners of KIF26B, reinforcing its ability as a motor protein in the complex intracellular microenvironment. Following this clue, there were evidences pointing that crosstalk between Wnt/β-catenin signaling and NF-κB signaling indeed existed. For example, β-catenin could directly bind to NF-κB and increase the association between NF-κB and DNA.39 Also, Wnt/β-catenin signaling could stimulate the transcription of tnfrsf19 and indirectly activate NF-κB signaling.40 Further, TNFα activated the NF-κB signaling cascade and induced the transcription of tc1, which could further activate Wnt/β-catenin signaling.41 Also, TNFα-stimulated NF-κB signaling could inactivate GSK3β to induce Wnt/β-catenin signaling activation.42

Herein, several interesting questions arose. Our previous study exhibited that silencing kif26b had no significant impact on NF-κB signaling activation in UM-UC3 and J82, while in the current study, we showed that silencing kif26b could inhibit NF-κB signaling activation under TNFα stimulation in T24 and 5637. This seems to be contradictory, but differences in dominant pathways could explain this phenomenon. In UM-UC3 and J82, PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway (NCBI SRA: SAMN30438059) and GPCR-mediated signaling43,44 predominated, respectively, while in T24 and 5637, NF-κB signaling pathway was relatively predominant.45,46,47 Silencing kif26b could reduce phosphorylated p65 level in UM-UC3 and J82 to some extent under TNFα stimulation (Figures S6A and S6B). These evidences endowed varied sensitivity to kif26b deprivation in different cell lines. However, this phenomenon needed further investigation.

Post-translational modification frequently happens in proteins, especially signaling molecules.48 The interaction between various partners are often regulated by different post-translational modification.48 Post translational modification often influence the subcellular localization of targeted proteins.49,50 In this study, we found that phosphorylation of KIF26B by PKCε enabled binding to p65. The interaction between KIF26B and p65 could recruit ubiquitinase TRIM21 to catalyze K63-linked ubiquitination of p65, enabling its nuclear localization. Previous study also showed that phosphorylation of proteins could influence its ubiquitination level.8,51 For example, TK1 was phosphorylated by ERK1/2. Phosphorylated TK1 recruited USP9X and inhibited its ubiquitination level, which could prevent TK1 from degradation.52 Also, there are studies showing that K63-linked ubiquitination could influence nuclear localization of targeted proteins. For example, K63-linked ubiquitination of FOXP3 enabled its translocation into the nucleus.30 Similarly, K63-linked ubiquitination of NSUN2 enabled it to achieve nuclear localization.9 Our results were consistent with these results and proposed a novel mechanism for nuclear localization of p65.

Kinesin superfamily is traditionally regarded as motor proteins that facilitate cargo transport within intracellular microenvironment. However, recent studies point to novel roles of kinesins, not limited to their classical functions. For example, KIF4A was able to inhibit degradation of AR by isolating AR from STUB1.53 KIF1C could undergo liquid-liquid phase separation to enable RNA periphery localization.54 Following this clue, our study pointed to a novel role for kinesin in modifying K63-linked ubiquitination of its cargo.

Collectively, our study elucidated how KIF26B was able to activate the NF-κB signaling pathway by enabling p65 nuclear localization (please refer to the graphical abstract). In detail, TNFα stimulated association between KIF26B and p65 through PKCε-mediated KIF26B S592 phosphorylation. KIF26B then recruited TRIM21 to catalyze phosphorylation of p65, enabling its nuclear translocation. Activated NF-κB signaling could enhance transcription of kif26b and her2, forming a positive feedback loop. Combining the targeting of kif26b with radiation-based therapy could exaggerate anti-tumor effects.

Limitations of the study

Though our study illuminated the function of KIF26B in NF-κB signaling activation, the clinical usage to target this signaling axis faced a problem—no KIF26B inhibitor has been identified so far. Scanning potential KIF26B inhibitors would be helpful for achieving “bench to bedside” translation. Further, the precise mechanism of how KIF26B promotes NF-κB (p65) nuclear translocation is unknown, especially the exact importin protein that fulfils the related roles. Last, as we exhibited that KIF26B utilized its S592 site to associate with NF-κB (p65), whether patients with BCa carry S592 site mutation deserves to be investigated.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animal studies

All the experiments were conducted under approval of the Joint Ethics Committee of Zhejiang University (202410613).

Subcutaneous tumor models were generated by injecting T24 cells into the right flank of mice. Four-week-old male BALB/C nude mice received T24 cells (2×106 cells/mouse). Animals were randomly grouped using simple randomization method and blindly allocated to experimental groups (n=5 per group). Tumor size was assessed every three days using the formula: Volume = 0.5 × length × width2. No exclusions were made from the study.

For one thing, T24 cells with kif26b stable-knockdown were established. Then wild-type KIF26B expressing plasmids or S592A KIF26B expressing plasmids were transfected into above cells. T24 cells transfected with empty vector were used as control. Cells were injected into BALB/C nude mice and mice were sacrificed 30 days later using cervical dislocation method.

For another thing, T24 cells with kif26b stable-knockdown and control T24 cells were injected into mice. After 14 days when tumor volume reaching 200 mm3, mice were exposure to 8 Gy radiation for the next 3 days. After 7 days mice were sacrificed and tumor samples were collected.

Tumor samples were fixed in 4% PFA and used for subsequent experiment.

Method details

Chemicals and reagents

Chemicals and reagents were used strictly under the guidance of related manufacturers.

Cell lines

T24 and 5637 were used in this study. T24 and 5637 were cultured in RPMI-1640 supplemented with FBS to a final concentration of 10 %. Cells were cultured at 37 °C with 5 % CO2 in cell incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Cell transfection

Transfection of cells was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Polyplus, France). Briefly, cells were seeded in 6-well plates. For each well, 1000 ng of plasmid DNA or 500 ng siRNA was combined with 200 μL of transfection buffer and mixed by vortexing for 10 s at room temperature. Subsequently, 5 μL of transfection reagent was introduced, followed by brief vortexing for 2 s. The mixture was allowed to stand at room temperature for 20 min before being introduced into the cell culture medium. After 24 hr, the medium was refreshed, and cells were incubated for an additional 24–48 hr.

All plasmids utilized in this research were acquired from and verified through sequencing by Yoche Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Western blotting analysis

Following related treatment, the culture medium was removed and cells were rinsed with 1× PBS. Cells were subsequently lysed using RIPA buffer on ice for 20 min. Lysates were transferred to tubes and centrifuged at 4 °C and 13,000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was collected and combined with 5×loading buffer. Protein denaturation was performed at 100 °C for 10 min. Denatured protein samples (30–50 μg) were resolved on 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gels (1.0-mm thick) at 180 V for 50 min. Proteins were then transferred onto 0.22 μm PVDF membranes on ice at 200 mA for 100 min. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 hr at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The following day, membranes were washed three times with TBST (prepared by adding 10 mL of 0.1% Tween-20 to 1 L of 1× TBS), each wash lasting 15 min. They were then probed with appropriate secondary antibodies for 1 hr at room temperature, followed by three additional TBST washes (15 min each). Signal detection was performed using a chemiluminescence imaging system following developer application.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Cells were grown in confocal-compatible 15-mm petri dishes. After a 10-min wash with 1×PBS, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (in PBS) for 15 min at room temperature. Following three 5-min PBS washes, cells were blocked with 2.5% BSA dissolved in PBS for 1 hr at room temperature. Primary antibody incubation took place at 4 °C overnight. The next day, cells were washed four times with PBS (5 min each) and incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies at 37 °C for 2 hr in darkness. After four additional 5-min PBS washes, nuclei were stained with 3 μg/mL DAPI for 20 min in the dark. Imaging was performed using a Leica STELLARIS 5 microscope (Leica, Germany).

Immunoprecipitation (IP)

Treated cells were harvested and lysed in NP-40 buffer on ice for 20 min. After centrifugation at 4 °C and 13,000 rpm for 15 min, the supernatant was collected. Primary antibodies or control IgG were then introduced, and samples were rotated overnight at 4 °C to form antigen-antibody complexes. Protein A/G magnetic beads were washed twice with 1×TBS (5 min each) and added to the mixtures, followed by rotation at room temperature for 2 hr. Beads were washed four times with NP-40 lysis buffer (5 min each), and bound proteins were eluted using 1×SDS loading buffer at 95 °C for 10 min. Analysis was performed via western blotting.

Ubiquitination analysis

For ubiquitination assays, cells were grown in 10-cm culture dishes. Following the indicated treatments, MG132 was applied at a concentration of 10 mM for 6 hr. Cells were subsequently lysed in NP-40 lysis buffer. IP was carried out as outlined in the previous section. Immunoprecipitated samples were heated at 65 °C for 5 min in a loading buffer supplemented with 500 mM imidazole. Protein ubiquitination was detected by immunoblotting using 1.5 mm-thick gels.

RNA extraction and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

After treatment, cells were rinsed with 1× PBS and lysed in TRIzol reagent on ice for 15 min. Chloroform (1/5 time the volume of TRIzol) was introduced, and the mixture was shaken vigorously for 15 s, then incubated on ice for 10 min. Centrifugation was performed at 4 °C and 12,000 rpm for 15 min. The aqueous phase was transferred, and isopropanol (equal to the initial TRIzol volume) was added. After mixing for 15 s, samples were stored at –20 °C for 20 min, then centrifuged again at 4 °C and 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The resulting RNA pellet was washed twice with 75% ethanol at 4 °C and air-dried for 5 min at room temperature. Purified RNA was dissolved in RNase-free water to a final concentration of 500 ng/μL.

For cDNA synthesis, two volumes of RNA were combined with one volume of reverse transcriptase and seven volumes of reaction buffer (250 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.3; 375 mM KCl; 15 mM MgCl2; 50 mM DTT), followed by incubation at 37°C. qPCR was prepared by mixing one volume of cDNA with 0.4 volumes each of forward and reverse primers, 10 volumes of SYBR Premix Ex Taq, and 8.2 volumes of ddH2O. Reactions were run on a CFX96™ Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA). Relative mRNA levels were determined using the 2–ΔΔCt method, normalized to gapdh.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol reagent. RNA integrity and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and by electrophoresis on a 1.5% agarose gel. Then mRNA was isolated using oligo(dT) magnetic beads and fragmented in a dedicated buffer. First-strand cDNA was synthesized with random hexamers, and double-stranded cDNA was generated and purified using AMPure XP beads. The cDNA was end-repaired, A-tailed, and ligated to indexed adapters. Final libraries were prepared through limited-cycle PCR, size-selected, and quality-controlled before sequencing. DNA nanoball-based sequencing was performed on an MGI high-throughput platform.

Differentially expressed genes were identified based on |log2 fold change| > 0.58 and p < 0.05. Transcripts showing reduced expression were further analyzed via Gene Ontology and KEGG pathway annotation. RNA sequencing and bioinformatic analyses were conducted by BIOPROFILE (Shanghai, China).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

Tumor specimens obtained from mice or human patients were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. IHC processing was performed by SHANGHAI OUTDO BIOTECH Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China) according to standard procedures. The workflow included tissue sectioning, baking, deparaffinization, rehydration, antigen retrieval, inactivation of endogenous peroxidase, blocking, incubation with primary and secondary antibodies, chromogenic development, counterstaining, and mounting. Sections were prepared at a thickness of 4 μm. Primary antibody applied was as follows: anti-Ki67 (1:4000).

Subcellular fractionation for cytosolic and nuclear proteins

Following treatment, cells were washed with 1×PBS and harvested mechanically using a cell scraper. Cell pellets were obtained by centrifugation at 4 °C and 1,500 rpm for 5 min. Complete removal of supernatant was emphasized at this step. Trypsin was not used to preserve target protein integrity.

Pellets were resuspended in 200 μL of cytoplasmic extraction reagent A (containing PMSF) per 20 μL of pellet volume, followed by vigorous vortexing for 10 s and incubation on ice for 15 min. Then 10 μL of cytoplasmic extraction reagent B was introduced, samples were vortexed for 5 s, kept on ice for 1 min, vortexed again, and centrifuged at 4 °C and 12,000 g for 5 min. The resulting supernatant (cytoplasmic extract) was promptly transferred to a chilled tube, ensuring minimal disturbance of the pellet.

For nuclear extraction, residual supernatant was carefully removed from the pellet, which was then treated with 50 μL of nuclear extraction reagent B supplemented with PMSF. The pellet was resuspended by vortexing at maximum speed for 30 s, followed by cyclic incubation involving 30-s vortexing every minute over 30 min on ice. After centrifugation at 4 °C and 16,000 g for 10 min, the supernatant (nuclear extract) was collected into pre-cooled tubes. Protein localization was assessed by western blotting.

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

Cells were treated with formaldehyde to covalently link DNA and associated proteins. Then cells were lysed to isolate chromatin. DNA was sheared into 200-1000 bp fragments using sonication. Chromatin was then incubated with anti-NF-κB antibody overnight at 4 °C. Next day, protein A/G beads were added to capture the antibody-protein-DNA complexes and rotated for 2 h at room temperature. Beads were washed to remove non-specific binders. Then immunoprecipitated complexes were eluted from the beads. Eluate was heated to reverse formaldehyde cross-links and the released DNA was purified. Enriched DNA was analyzed by qPCR or ChIP-seq. ChIP-seq was conducted by IgeneBook.

Colony formation assay

Treated cells were harvested and counted, and 1,000 cells per well were seeded into 6-well plates. Culture medium was refreshed every three days. After 14 days, cells were washed with 1×PBS, fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 min at room temperature. Plates were rinsed again with PBS and imaged for colony quantification.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Sample sizes, replication schemes, and interim endpoints were determined based on prior experimental data, ensuring 80% statistical power and a type I error rate of 5% (p < 0.05).55 Analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). All experiments were repeated in triplicate. Two-group comparisons were conducted using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-tests, while multiple groups were compared via one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test, as appropriate. Data were assumed to follow a normal distribution with homogeneous variance across groups. Results are expressed as mean ± SEM. Significance levels were defined as follows: ns (not significant), p > 0.05; ∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001; ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.
