Intrathalamic delivery of adeno-associated viral vector expressing progranulin as gene therapy for GRN-related frontotemporal dementia
Authors: Youn Bok Lee, Do Young Lee, Zoe Walker, Joseph Allison, Aleksandra Kaliszewska, Alinda Fernandes, Katharine Y. Zhang, Yalem Bekele, Paniz Hosseini, Romain Joubert, Joana Furtado, McQuinn Gumus, Olivier Brock, Ali Bienemann, Steven Gill, Samantha Murray, Nadia Mitchell, Page Bouchard, Alex Bloom, John Isaac, Carlos J. Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
GRN mutations leading to progranulin haploinsufficiency can cause frontotemporal dementia. AVB-101, an investigational gene therapy comprising an adeno-associated virus construct expressing codon-optimized human GRN under a neuronal promoter, was delivered intrathalamically to mice, sheep, and non-human primates. AVB-101 reversed pathology in Grn−/− mice and achieved widespread cortical biodistribution in sheep brain, with human progranulin protein detected in the majority of prefrontal cortical neurons. Conversely, human progranulin was undetectable in sheep cortical neurons following intra-cisterna magna administration of AVB-101 or adeno-associated viruses containing progranulin under a ubiquitous promoter. AVB-101 was well tolerated in cynomolgus macaques with no adverse events reported for the 6-month duration of the study. At all doses tested, human progranulin protein was detected throughout the cortex while absent in peripheral tissues. Human progranulin levels in cerebrospinal fluid and prefrontal cortex tissue were closely correlated in sheep and non-human primates, confirming that an increase in cerebrospinal fluid progranulin levels reflects neuronal expression of AVB-101. Thus, AVB-101 is well tolerated in various animal models, and intrathalamic administration delivers progranulin at levels sufficient for cross-correction throughout the brain. These data support the progression of AVB-101 to clinical development in humans with frontotemporal dementia caused by GRN mutations.

Introduction

Frontotemporal dementia (FTD) is characterized by a heterogenous group of symptoms, including uncharacteristic behaviors, personality changes, progressive decline in language, and loss of executive function and cognitive abilities.1,2,3 FTD is an important cause of dementia in those <65 years old,4 and GRN mutations account for ∼10% of all FTD cases and ∼22% of familial FTD cases.5 With no effective disease-modifying therapies currently available, death occurs 3–13 years after symptom onset,6,7,8,9 and the social, financial, and healthcare burden of FTD are significant.10

The molecular pathology of FTD predominantly affects the frontal and temporal lobes and is characterized by the cytoplasmic accumulation of microtubule-associated protein tau (MAPT, ∼45%), transactive response DNA-binding protein 43 (TDP-43, ∼50%), and fused in sarcoma protein (∼5%).11 FTD is highly heritable, and a strong family history is found in ∼30%–50% of all the patients with FTD.5,9,12 Most of the heritability of FTD is due to autosomal-dominant mutations in GRN and MAPT genes and a GGGGCC hexanucleotide repeat expansion in chromosome 9 open reading frame 72; each group causes ∼5%–10% of all the FTD cases.8,9,12,13,14,15

Autosomal-dominant loss-of-function mutations in GRN cause haploinsufficiency; the decrease in progranulin (PGRN) causes mis-accumulation of TDP-43 in neurons, leading to neurodegeneration with symptoms characteristic of FTD.16,17,18

GRN codes for the human PGRN (hPGRN) protein, which is found in a wide range of tissues, but dysfunction and degeneration in heterozygous mutant carriers are largely confined to the brain.19 In the central nervous system (CNS), PGRN is produced by microglia, neurons, endothelial cells, and astrocytes, with neurons and microglia having the highest expression levels.20,21 Importantly, PGRN is secreted into the extracellular space, taken up by neighboring cells and packaged into endosomes, before being trafficked to lysosomes and cleaved into multiple granulins by cathepsins.22,23 Deficiency in PGRN from GRN mutations leads to dysfunction of lysosomal serine proteases and accumulation of insoluble proteins, particularly TDP-43, in the cytoplasm and nucleus. It also increases microglial reactivity, accelerating neurodegeneration.24,25,26,27 Phagocytosis-defective microglia produce high levels of reactive oxygen species and secrete proinflammatory cytokines that have been indicated in both age-related and PGRN-related forms of neurodegeneration.19

AVB-101 is a recombinant adeno-associated virus (AAV9)28 containing DNA encoding a codon-optimized version of the human GRN gene, under the control of a neuronal-specific promoter human synapsin (hSyn),29 with 5′ and 3′ enhancing elements designed to increase the expression and secretion of PGRN. This combination aims to enhance target-tissue specificity and boost secretion of PGRN, thereby minimizing the required dose to reduce off-target effects. The design of AVB-101, combined with an intrathalamic (ITM) route of administration (RoA), aims to maximize biodistribution in the CNS by exploiting thalamocortical projections to transport and release PGRN in the cortex. Uptake by neighboring cortical neurons and glia would enable cross-correction, particularly in the frontal and temporal lobes most affected in FTD-GRN.30 Herein, we describe the optimization of the vector and the preclinical efficacy, biodistribution, and toxicology of AVB-101 delivered to homozygous Grn knockout (Grn−/−) transgenic mice, wild-type sheep, and cynomolgus macaques using the ITM RoA. These extensive preclinical data support the progression of AVB-101 to clinical studies in FTD-GRN.31

Results

AVB-101 is well tolerated and ameliorates pathology in Grn−/− mice

AVB-101 comprises a codon-optimized human GRN cassette with an hSyn promoter packaged into an AAV9 vector (Figure 1A). In a dose-ranging study, young adult Grn−/− mice (7 weeks old) were treated with bilateral ITM injections of AVB-101, and the effect on pathology was compared to same-age, saline-injected control animals. AVB-101 was well tolerated at the doses tested (2.3 × 107 to 2.3 × 109 vector genomes [vgs]/hemisphere), with no adverse events observed up to the scheduled sacrifice at 12 weeks after injection. A dose-dependent increase in vg (Figure 1B) and hPGRN protein (Figure 1C) was observed in the thalamus, with an elevation in PGRN up to 15-fold between low- (2.3 × 107) and high dose (2.3 × 109). Late-stage pathology in Grn−/− mice is typically characterized by microglial activation, as detected by CD68 positivity and a change in IBA1 cellular morphology, whereby fine processes retract to adopt a more ameboid shape. To evaluate AVB-101 efficacy in this late-stage pathology, Grn−/− mice at 40 weeks of age were treated by thalamic injections at doses ranging from 1.1 × 108 to 2.3 × 109 vg/hemisphere and sacrificed at 52 weeks. Immunofluorescence imaging revealed an increase in IBA1-positive microglia in the thalamus (injection site) at the highest dose and a small increase in the prefrontal cortex only at the lowest dose. No significant change in CD68 levels with AVB-101 dosing was observed, although there was a trend toward reduction across all doses, compared with saline-injected animals (Figures S2A and S2B).

Delivery route optimization of AVB-101 and hPGRN expression in sheep

Having shown tolerability and efficacy of ITM AVB-101 in the Grn−/− mouse model, we explored biodistribution in the much larger sheep brain in a pilot study with doses ranging from 2.0 × 1011 to 5.0 × 1012 vg per sheep. Bilateral thalamic injections were performed for the lower dose (1.0 × 1011 vg) or unilateral for the mid-dose (5.0 × 1011 vg) and high dose (5.0 × 1012 vg). A NeuroInfuse catheter (Renishaw, Wooton-under-Edge, UK) was used to deliver 250 μL of AVB-101 into the thalamus of approximately 1-year-old Suffolk cross sheep using a standard convection-enhanced delivery (CED) protocol. Animals were sacrificed 28 days post infusion with alternate coronal sections processed for either histological or biochemical analyses. Immunofluorescence microscopy revealed hPGRN throughout the hemisphere ipsilateral to the thalamic injection; however, hPGRN was almost undetectable in the contralateral hemisphere (in which the thalamus was injected with saline), indicating little delivery across hemispheres (Figure 2B). The hPGRN signal distributed throughout the brain with high levels observed in the thalamus (Figure S3). In addition, robust PGRN expression was observed in the frontal cortex and basal ganglia, at the level of the caudate and putamen in frontal cortex (Figure 2Bc). The PGRN signal was predominantly within neurons and was detected from cortical layers I to VI, including large pyramidal cells in layers V and VI, but was most intense in neuronal layer IV, which receives the most thalamocortical projections (Figure 2Bd-e).

Biopsies taken from the thalamus, caudate, putamen, and multiple sites across the cortex were quantified for vg and the presence of hPGRN protein. ITM infusion of AVB-101 resulted in widespread distribution of vg and hPGRN expression across the cortex and subcortical structures (Figures S4 and S5). Vg and hPGRN levels in the thalamus increased with AVB-101 dose increase, resulting in hPGRN distribution throughout the cortex and hippocampus, as well as in the caudate and putamen.

Widespread cortical biodistribution of vg and hPGRN after ITM AVB-101 in sheep

In a follow-up, dose-ranging study in sheep, bilateral ITM infusions by CED of AVB-101 at doses of 1.0 × 1010, 5.0 × 1010, and 1.0 × 1011 vg/hemisphere produced no adverse clinical observations, and all animals survived until their scheduled sacrifice (28 days post injection). AVB-101 yielded dose-dependent increases in hPGRN levels in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), from 3.8 ng/mL at the lowest dose (1.0 × 1010 vg/hemisphere) to 27.2 ng/mL at the highest dose (1.0 × 1011 vg/hemisphere, Figure 3A), with no significant increase in hPGRN levels observed in serum (Figure 3B). The levels observed in CSF equaled or exceeded human physiological levels of PGRN (4.5 ± 1.5 ng/mL; n = 6, 2.3 to 6.2 ng/mL). As in the pilot study, ITM infusion of AVB-101 resulted in widespread distribution of vg (Figures 3C and S6A) and hPGRN protein expression (Figures 3D and S6B) throughout the brain, as measured from biopsies in the thalamus, caudate, putamen, and multiple sites across the cortex. Vg and hPGRN protein levels in each region were closely correlated, and similar to the levels achieved in the pilot experiment, with a clear gradient of expression in the sub-cortical and cortical regions. We observed higher levels of vgs in the cortex compared to protein expression levels (Figures 3C and 3D). This is likely the result of an active transport of endosomes containing AAV capsids along thalamocortical axons but very little trans-synaptic transduction of neighboring neurons. PGRN protein, however, is actively secreted at synapses and taken up by neighboring neurons and glia, as detected by immunohistochemistry (Figure 3E) and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (Figure 3D), and shown in lower levels than in the vector genome (VG) heatmaps from the brain tissues analyzed. Immunofluorescence imaging confirmed that ITM administration of AVB-101 resulted in neuronal expression of hPGRN in the sheep thalamus and corroborated the results obtained from biochemical analysis quantifying vg and hPGRN expression. Immunostaining in both brain hemispheres showed that hPGRN was abundant in frontal and temporal cortical regions for all animals at all three doses (Figure 3E). The percentage of hPGRN-positive neurons in the frontal cortex ranged from 61% (1.0 × 1010 vg/hemisphere) to 78% (1.0 × 1011 vg/hemisphere) (Figure 3F). Immunostaining of hippocampal regions in both hemispheres showed no significant detection of hPGRN expression except in one animal in the 5.0 × 1010 vg/hemisphere dose group (data not shown), further corroborating protein levels detected by ELISA (Figure 3D). There was no evidence of microglial reactivity, as determined by IBA1 cell morphology, in the thalamus—which had the highest vg and hPGRN levels (data not shown)—nor in the prefrontal cortex (Figure 3E).

Intrathecal cisterna magna administration of AAV-hPGRN vectors fails to deliver robust cortical vg or hPGRN expression in sheep

Since other groups have investigated an intrathecal cisterna magna (ICM) RoA for AAV-hPGRN, we sought to test the performance of our AAV9-Syn-hPGRN construct and two alternative vectors via ICM delivery. To enable a direct comparison, we exchanged our hSyn promoter for the non-cell selective chicken-β-actin promoter (CB7) and packaged the otherwise identical cassettes into AAV1 to generate AAV1-CB7-hPGRN, or AAV9 to produce AAV9-CB7-hPGRN (Figure S8). To confirm intrathecal injections via ICM, 2 mL of CSF was withdrawn, and Omnipaque 240 was injected. Pre- and post-infusion computed tomography (CT) scans of the brain and spinal cord revealed Omnipaque on the exterior surface of the brain parenchyma, permeating into the third and lateral ventricles and surrounding the cervical spinal cord and cerebellum after 10 min (Figure 4A). Using this ICM injection technique, two sheep each were injected with AAV1-CB7-hPGRN, AAV9-CB7-hPGRN, or AAV9-Syn-hPGRN. ICM injections were at 1.0 × 1013 vg total dose, which is 50 times the highest ITM dose tested. As with ITM delivery, no adverse clinical observations or body weight changes were observed, and all animals survived until their scheduled sacrifice (28 days post ICM infusion). Contrasting with ITM delivery, no elevation in hPGRN protein levels was detected in CSF or serum after AAV9-Syn-hPGRN injection, consistent with minimal CNS neuronal transduction following ICM delivery of an AAV9 driving expression in neurons. In the CB7 constructs, CSF hPGRN was detected (average 15.5 ng/mL) in both sheep that received AAV9-CB7-hPGRN and in one sheep that received AAV1-CB7-hPGRN, reaching 111 ng/mL (Figure 4B). Despite using an identical analytical protocol for ICM- and ITM-injected sheep, vg/μg DNA levels were very low in the ICM-injected sheep, averaging 1 × 101 to 1 × 102 vg/μg DNA across all regions of the cortex (Figures 4D and S9A), compared to 1 × 105 to 1 × 106 vg/μg DNA in brain tissue where ITM infusions were used (Figure 3C). The distribution of hPGRN protein matched that of vg and was undetectable above background by ELISA throughout the cortex (Figures 4E and S9B). Immunofluorescence imaging of hPGRN failed to detect any neurons positive for hPGRN in the frontal cortex (Figure S10A) with Purkinje cells in the cerebellum strongly positive, particularly when AAV9-CB7-PGRN was used (Figures S10B and S11). Contrasting with ITM RoA (Figure S7), vgs were detected in the liver in all animals that received ICM injections (Figure 4F).

ITM delivery AVB-101 achieves widespread cortical distribution of hPGRN protein in cynomolgus macaques

To translate mouse and sheep findings, we undertook a biodistribution study of AVB-101 in ten cynomolgus macaques. For each of the following groups, one male and one female received formulation buffer (FB) and AVB-101 at low (8.3 × 109 vg/hemisphere), mid (2.5 × 1010 vg/hemisphere), or high (7.5 × 1010 vg/hemisphere) doses, bilaterally into the thalamus by CED using a Smartflow neuroventricular cannula (Table S1). In addition, we infused one male and one female with high-dose (7.5 × 1010 vg/hemisphere) AVB-101 plus gadolinium, to explore any effect of gadolinium on biodistribution, as gadolinium is the contrast agent typically used for MRI-guided intraparenchymal gene therapy infusions in clinical trials. No adverse events were observed, and all animals survived until their scheduled sacrifice at 8 weeks post injection.

Unlike sheep, macaque PGRN is detected by the antibodies used in immunohistochemistry to detect hPGRN and to some extent in the ELISA. Nevertheless, elevation of hPGRN protein levels was detected in the frontal and temporal lobes and hippocampi of animals injected with all three doses of AVB-101, compared to FB (Figure 5A). As expected, the highest levels were observed in the thalamus (the injection site), and there was robust elevation across all cortical regions as well as in the caudate and cerebellum. There was no evidence that the addition of gadolinium affected biodistribution or hPGRN levels in any brain areas tested. Levels of PGRN for all doses were within or above the physiological levels observed in control human brain tissues as measured using the equivalent assay and extraction protocol (Figure 5A, gray bar). Importantly, the levels of PGRN observed in the frontal cortex showed a significant positive correlation with levels of PGRN in CSF measured in the same animals at the same time point (Figure 5B). ITM AVB-101 is well tolerated with minimal pathology in cynomolgus macaques

Next, to assess the safety of AVB-101, we undertook a toxicology study in 28 cynomolgus macaques under Good Laboratory Practice (GLP) conditions. Males and females received either FB or AVB-101 at a low or high dose delivered bilaterally into the thalamus by CED using a Smartflow neuroventricular cannula (Table S2). No adverse events were observed in a functional observational battery of electrocardiography, ophthalmology, urinalysis, or CSF parameters. There were no meaningful effects on hematology or biochemistry, particularly liver function or coagulation (Tables S3–S8).

PGRN levels in the CSF increased over the first 3 months (Figure 6A), but as seen in sheep, there was no increase of PGRN in serum during this same period (Figure 6B). Neurofilament light chain (NfL), a biomarker of neuronal injury,33 increased in the CSF following the ITM procedure, peaked at 3 weeks, and declined thereafter but had not returned to baseline by 12 weeks (Figure 6C). NfL levels in serum showed a similar profile, but a smaller increase, that returned to baseline by 8 weeks (Figure 6D). A similar profile of NfL levels was observed in the CSF of animals followed for 6 months, with NfL approaching baseline levels by 6 months (Figure S12). At sacrifice, no test article-related changes were observed in organ weight, macroscopic appearance, or microscopic findings in non-nervous tissues of the non-human primates (NHP) at both the 3- and 6-month time points. Human-optimized GRN transcript, as detected by in situ hybridization (BaseScope), revealed that expression was largely confined to thalamic neurons (Figure 6E). PGRN protein expression by immunohistochemistry and ELISA revealed robust expression in the thalamus (Figures 6F and 6G) with a dose-dependent distribution extended to the putamen, caudate, and the frontal cortex (Figure 6G).

No AVB-101-related microscopic findings were observed in the spinal cord or dorsal root ganglia. Histological analysis of brain tissue at 3 and 6 months indicated a reduction over time of mild infusion procedure-related findings across all groups, including FB-infused control animals, associated with transient MRI findings. Discussion

In this study, ITM administration of AVB-101 demonstrated an acceptable safety profile and delivered physiological hPGRN levels sufficient to achieve cross-correction in neurons and glia throughout the brain of three preclinical models, which supports progression of AVB-101 to clinical-stage development for FTD-GRN.

AAV vectors capable of transducing neurons offer a potentially curative “one-and-done” therapy, but the blood-brain and CSF-brain barriers severely limit systemic or intrathecal delivery, even with high vector doses. We developed AVB-101, a cassette containing DNA encoding the human GRN gene, designed to increase expression of hPGRN, under the control of a neuronal-specific promoter. This cassette was packaged in the neurotropic vector AAV9, which is readily manufacturable and has been used in Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved treatments for spinal muscular atrophy.28,34 We hypothesized that stereotactic delivery of AVB-101 would bypass the blood-brain and CSF-brain barriers, allowing hPGRN to be manufactured, distributed via thalamocortical projections, secreted at synapses, and potentially cross-correct hPGRN-deficient neurons and glia in the cortices of FTD-GRN patients. Here, we show that AVB-101 delivered to the thalamus of Grn−/− mice prevents and reverses pathology. ITM-delivered AVB-101 in sheep and NHPs resulted in physiological levels of human PGRN in the prefrontal cortex. The levels of PGRN in the prefrontal cortex correlated with PGRN levels observed in the CSF of the same animals, and ITM-delivered AVB-101 was found to be well tolerated in NHP. Importantly, we found no detectable hPGRN in the prefrontal cortices of sheep following ICM delivery with AVB-101 or other AAV constructs with PGRN under the control of a ubiquitous promoter, even at doses 50-fold higher than the highest ITM-delivered dose, indicating that direct delivery to the thalamus is required to deliver PGRN to the cortex.

Given that there are reports linking high levels of PGRN with potential cellular proliferation or malignant transformation,35 we placed GRN under the hSyn promoter to restrict its expression to post-mitotic neurons. We demonstrated that this approach avoided systemic exposure. The ITM RoA was selected to target neuronal networks that could facilitate the transport of the viral vector and hPGRN protein to the cerebral cortex.30,36 Previous reports of AAV2 and AAV5 delivery to the thalamus had resulted in good transduction of the frontal cortex.37,38 Following ITM delivery of AVB-101, we observed widespread vector and hPGRN protein expression in cortical and subcortical regions in Grn−/− mice. Microglial lysosomal dysfunction and neuronal lipofuscin accumulation are the earliest pathological phenotypes observed in the Grn−/− mouse brain,39 which precede microgliosis, synaptic loss, and TDP-43 pathology.40 All doses were well tolerated by the mice, and there was no evidence of microglial reactivity at doses up to 2.3 × 108 vg/hemisphere. Additionally, all doses of AVB-101 suppressed lipofuscinosis in the thalamus, prefrontal cortex, and hippocampus of young adult Grn−/− mice, as measured by quantifying SCMAS, confirming that this early pathology is readily reversed.

Having shown that ITM AVB-101 can reverse pathology in Grn−/− mice, we tested its biodistribution in sheep and cynomolgus macaques. The ovine model was selected as the sheep brain is closer to the human brain in size than macaques and has similarities in structure and CSF volumes to humans.41 The thalamus is highly connected to a diverse range of brain regions,30 and it was hypothesized that ITM delivery of secretable hPGRN could target the full breadth of brain regions affected by FTD-GRN.42

In sheep, we established the optimal surgical procedure, volume, and rate of infusion. Vg and hPGRN were observed in all sub-cortical and cortical regions at doses ranging from 1.0 × 1010 to 5.0 × 1012 vg/hemisphere, and no adverse events occurred during the study. Even at lower vg doses of 1.0 × 1010 vg/hemisphere, hPGRN was observed in 60% of neurons in the frontal cortex. At higher doses (1.0 × 1011 vg/hemisphere), hPGRN was detected in nearly 80% of neurons across all cortical layers, confirming the potential for functional cross-correction in cortices of FTD-GRN patients. There was also a clear correlation between vg dose and hPGRN CSF levels. As FTD-GRN patients have ≤50% of physiological CSF hPGRN levels, it is predicted that any elevation after AVB-101 injection would reflect therapeutic delivery to the target tissues.2,43

We compared ITM and intrathecal delivery of AVB-101 with an alternative capsid and/or alternative promoter using AAV1-CB7-hPGRN and AAV9-CB7-hPGRN. Having confirmed by contrast CT imaging that an ICM RoA effectively delivered infusate around the brain and inside the ventricles of sheep, we injected all three vectors at 50 times the highest dose used in the ITM RoA. Despite achieving very high CSF hPGRN levels in some animals, vg levels in the cortex were low, and the hPGRN protein was largely undetectable in brain tissues. Poor to moderate cerebral transduction after ICM injection has been previously reported in NHPs44,45 and sheep.46 Furthermore, following ICM delivery, vector was detected in the liver of all animals, an expected finding with this dose and administration route, with hepatotoxicity a known dose-limiting feature of many AAV therapies.47

Lastly, we conducted a biodistribution and GLP safety study of ITM-delivered AVB-101 in 38 cynomolgus macaques. The procedure was well tolerated, and no adverse events were reported. We demonstrated that AVB-101 transduction was largely restricted to the thalamus, but hPGRN protein was distributed throughout the cortex and subcortical structures, in a pattern similar to that observed in sheep. Furthermore, we confirmed that the addition of gadolinium, which is standard for MRI-guided gene therapy infusions, did not affect transduction or biodistribution.48

A dose-related increase in CSF hPGRN was observed and correlated with frontal cortex tissue levels, indicating that any increase above baseline in FTD-GRN patients will reflect hPGRN secretion in the brain, including in the most affected cortical areas. Histological analysis revealed minimal site injection changes, and the inflammatory reaction diminished over time, establishing an NOAEL at the low dose in NHPs.

PGRN supplementation has been of increasing interest as a potentially disease-modifying approach for FTD-GRN, given the known causative relationship between haploinsufficiency and disease. Histone deacetylase inhibition was one of the first proposed therapeutic strategies, and although the program was discontinued following lack of pharmacodynamic effect in the FRM-0334 study,49 it was the first trial in FTD-GRN demonstrating the feasibility of the international multicenter studies required for drug development in this rare disease. More recently, an anti-sortilin antibody approach (latozinemab, Alector) has been shown to inhibit PGRN endocytosis and increase plasma and CSF PGRN levels, and the phase 3 study is ongoing.50 Another antibody-based approach currently under phase 1/2 (DNL593, Denali/Takeda) consists of PGRN fused to an antibody fragment that binds to transferrin receptors to be transported across the blood-brain barrier and replace PGRN in the brain.51

More recently, gene therapy aiming to increase the baseline expression of PGRN in FTD-GRN has emerged as an attractive therapeutic strategy.43,52 A gene therapy approach to restore PGRN may be particularly advantageous as it consists of a single administration, compared to the repeated, chronic administration necessary for the aforementioned treatments, and it is potentially curative for patients who are identified early in the disease course. There are two ongoing phase 1/2 clinical trials using the ICM RoA to deliver AAV vectors encoding the PGRN protein in patients with FTD-GRN: the PROCLAIM study (NCT04408625)53 by Prevail Therapeutics/Lilly and the upliFT-D study (NCT04747431), PBFT02 by PassageBio.54

AVB-101 has been designed to address the underlying cause of FTD-GRN and enhance treatment through a unique design that optimizes hPGRN expression and cellular targeting. The ability to deliver gene therapy directly to the thalamus is expected to offer the additional advantage of a predicted therapeutic dose that would be significantly lower than would be required with an intravenous or intrathecal (including ICM) approach. This may result in little-to-no systemic exposure, thereby limiting the risk of off-target transduction in peripheral organs, and any associated dose-dependent toxicity. The ITM RoA-enhanced protein distribution in mice, sheep, and NHPs provides strong evidence to support the expected safety and efficiency of this AAV administration in humans. ITM AVB-101, at clinically relevant doses, achieved thalamic transduction and widespread cortical hPGRN in all animals, with no significant evidence of toxicity. The infusion resulted in minimal vector distribution to the liver and no increase in serum hPGRN, which are important safety features. Reversal of pathological changes in Grn−/− mice confirms the potential for efficacy. ITM AVB-101 infusion in sheep and NHPs was well tolerated for the dose range and study duration tested, and biodistribution was vastly superior to ICM delivery of AVB-101 and alternative capsids and promoters.

Although this study comprises a large body of work, it does have limitations. Grn−/− mouse models do not mirror the behavioral phenotype or pathology of FTD-GRN patients, which limits the ability for some assessments of efficacy in rodents. We did not investigate the impact of hPGRN supplementation on lysosomal function at a biochemical level, and, while the number of animals in the GLP safety study was substantial, study duration did not extend beyond 6 months. Nevertheless, our results demonstrate that ITM has an acceptable safety profile and that AVB-101 achieved widespread uptake of hPGRN by cortical neurons. These data supported the initiation of the ongoing ASPIRE-FTD study, a phase 1/2 clinical trial of AVB-101 delivered by bilateral ITM infusion in patients with FTD-GRN.31

Materials and methods

AVB-101 cassette design

A substantially optimized AAV9 hPGRN construct was developed through a series of cellular experiments (data not shown). The final construct featured a neuronal-specific promoter (hSyn) to restrict hPGRN expression to neurons, a codon-optimized human GRN sequence, cis-acting 5′ and 3′ elements, and bovine growth hormone poly A signal to enhance transgene transcription. These modifications were made to maximize transgene expression, minimize vector dose, and restrict expression to the CNS.

Viral vector production

The optimized hPGRN cassette under a hSyn promoter was packaged into AAV9 (AVB-101) but was also placed under a constitutive CB7 and packaged into AAV1 and AAV9 for ICM experiments (Figure S7). All constructs were produced in HEK293T cells by triple transfection and purified by AAVX resin prior to formulating in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for mouse and sheep studies.

AVB-101 used for GLP toxicology studies was manufactured using a triple transient transfection process in HEK293 cells at 50L scale. After harvest and lysis, the vector was purified by filtration, affinity chromatography, ultracentrifugation, and ion-exchange chromatography. Purified vector was buffer exchanged using tangential flow filtration into the final formulation buffer at a concentration of 1 × 1013 vg/mL. The vector was titrated using a validated droplet-digital polymerase chain reaction method prior to use.

AVB-101 in vivo efficacy study in a Grn−/− mouse

AVB-101 efficacy was assessed in the Grn−/− mouse model lacking exons 2–8 of Grn.55 Grn−/− mice have no detectable hPGRN protein in all tissues and recapitulate key pathophysiological features of FTD-GRN patients. Vector was delivered via bilateral ITM injection into two age groups selected to represent different disease stages. Mice at 7 weeks of age were selected for treatment before overt disease onset (lipofuscinosis without overt microglia activation). Mice at 40 weeks of age were selected when marked brain disease was present (lipofuscinosis and brain neuroinflammation evidenced by activated microglia). Four dose groups of 2.3 × 107 (7-week-old mice only), 1.2 × 108, 2.3 × 108, and 2.3 × 109 vg/hemisphere were delivered in a volume of 3 μL/hemisphere. Additional control groups of Grn−/− and Grn+/+ mice receiving PBS and untreated Grn−/− mice (early treatment group) were included. Each group consisted of 4 males and 4 females, except the untreated group, which consisted of 2 males and 2 females. Animals were sacrificed 12 weeks after vector administration at both ages. Full surgical, termination, and sample collection methods are described in the supplemental materials and methods (Terminal procedures).

Histological assays

Brain tissue was cut coronally into 30 μm sections with a cryostat (Leica Biosystems, Wetzlar, Germany) and stained with the following antibodies (detailed in Table S9): goat anti-hPGRN and mouse anti-neuronal nuclear protein (NeuN), rabbit anti-glial fibrillary acidic protein, rat anti-cluster of differentiation 68 (CD68), and rabbit anti-Iba1, goat anti-SCMAS, before using the following secondary antibodies: donkey anti-goat Alexa 488, donkey anti-mouse Alexa 550, donkey anti-rat Alexa 568, donkey anti-rabbit Alexa 488, donkey anti-rabbit Alexa 647, and donkey anti-goat Dylight 650. Low magnification (20×), high-resolution images of hPGRN immunostaining were obtained with a slide scanner (Olympus VS120, Olympus, PA). Confocal imaging was performed using the Nikon Upright Ni-E with A1R confocal optics (Nikon, Melville, NY). The region of interest per section was determined using specific landmarks for the frontal cortex region and the thalamic region of both hemispheres (left and right) using 10× magnification and DAPI staining. Representative images were taken using 20× magnification under Galvano 1024 resolution (with 1.2 pin hole). The following laser wavelengths were used: 405 nm (DAPI), 488 nm (IBA1), 561 nm (NeuN), and 640 nm (hPGRN). Laser power (depending on channels, magnification, and brain region) was between 1.00 and 10.00 using a gain between 45.00 and 110.00. All images were analyzed using NIS-Elements (Nikon) and Fiji software. hPGRN, CD68, IBA-1, and SCMAS quantification were performed using Visiopharm A/S software (Hoersholm, Denmark).

Biochemical assays

hPGRN levels in the serum, CSF, and cortical brain tissue were assessed using the Adipogen Life Sciences PGRN (human) ELISA Kit (AG-45A-0018YEK-KI01; Adipogen, San Diego, CA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Vector biodistribution was assessed postmortem using quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) in DNA extracted from tissue from the thalamus, hippocampus, and prefrontal cortex using commercial kits and following manufacturer’s protocols. Areas quantified were the entirety of the thalamus (excluding hypothalamus, as assessed by division of the two by the medial lemniscus and zona incerta) and entirety of the prefrontal cortex (excluding the olfactory cortices). Quantification readouts were divided by the area to give comparable readouts between tissues.

AVB-101 biodistribution and PGRN expression in the sheep

To optimize the RoA in a large animal brain, we measured the biodistribution of AVB-101 vg and PRGN in two cohorts of sheep given an ITM infusion and compared the results to one cohort of sheep given an ICM infusion. In a pilot experiment, six female Suffolk cross ewes (aged 12–15 months weighing 50–60 kg) underwent MRI imaging under general anesthesia to assess thalamic volumes and plan safe trajectories for catheters to be implanted bilaterally in the thalamus. Convection-enhanced delivery infusions into the thalamus were performed using a ramped regime of gradually increasing rates as previously described.56 Three sheep received bilateral ITM infusions of AVB-101 at different doses representing 1.0 × 1011, 5.0 × 1011, and 5.0 × 1012 vg/hemisphere. One sheep received a dose of 5.0 × 1012 vg AVB-101 in the left hemisphere and PBS with gadolinium (Gadovist, Bayer, UK) in the right hemisphere. One sheep received a dose of 1.0 × 1011 vg AVB-101 in the left hemisphere and PBS in the right hemisphere. The final sheep received a dose of 5.0 × 1011 vg AVB-101 in the left hemisphere and 5.0 × 1012 vg of AAV9 with AAV9-Syn-mCherry plus gadolinium (2 μM) in the right hemisphere. Volumetric T1 MRI was obtained upon completion of infusion, to determine the infusate distribution for the two sheep that received gadolinium.

A follow-up study was performed using an identical approach to the CED system to infuse AVB-101 bilaterally into the thalamus of Romney Marsh ewes (aged 18 months weighing 45–66 kg) at doses of 1.0 × 1010, 5.0 × 1010, and 1.0 × 1011 vg/hemisphere (n= 2 per dose). An alternative ICM RoA was also assessed in a separate study of Coopworth ewes (aged 13 months weighing 42–55 kg) at doses of 1.0 × 1013 vg in 2 mL of AVB-101 and in AAV1 or AAV9 vectors with the CB7 promoter and an identical GRN transgene (AAV1-CB7-hPGRN or AAV9-CB7-hPGRN) (N = 2/group). An Omnipaque 240 (GE Healthcare, Australia) PBS vehicle control was also used (N = 1/group). Scheduled sacrifices were at approximately 28 days after vector administration for all animals. Tissues were harvested, and multiple biopsies were performed in specified brain regions and peripheral organs. Blood samples were collected pre-injection (1 mL) and pre-termination (1 mL) and processed to serum. CSF samples (1 mL) were also collected on the day of termination. hPGRN levels in plasma, CSF, and brain tissues were determined by ELISA, vg levels by qPCR, and immunostaining as described earlier, except that isolectin was used as a substitute marker for CD68 as no commercial antibodies could detect sheep CD68.

AVB-101 biodistribution and safety study in cynomolgus macaques

Two studies in cynomolgus monkeys (Macaca fascicularis) were conducted with AVB-101. A non-GLP study was conducted to assess the biodistribution of AVB-101 following a single bilateral ITM injection in cynomolgus monkeys. Ten (5 male, 5 female) 2.1- to 2.4-year-old animals received bilateral intraparenchymal infusions into the thalamus. Doses used were 8.3 × 109 (low dose, n = 2), 2.5 × 1010 (mid dose, n = 2), and 2.5 × 1011 (high dose, n = 2). In addition to formulation buffer, two doses of AVB-101 (low dose, 2.5 × 1010 vg/hemisphere and high dose, 2.5 × 1011 vg/hemisphere), were tested. In-life observations and measurements included clinical observations, body weight, physical examinations, and clinical pathology (blood and CSF) evaluations. Blood and CSF samples were collected for biodistribution, antibody analysis, NfL protein analysis, and hPGRN analysis. Blood was also collected for peripheral blood mononuclear cells for cellular immunogenicity analyses. On day 55 ± 5, the animals were sacrificed, and selected tissues were harvested for biodistribution, gene expression, hPGRN analysis, and possible histopathological evaluation.

A GLP study was designed to assess the safety and biodistribution of AVB-101 following a single bilateral ITM infusion in cynomolgus monkeys. Twenty-eight (14 males and 14 females) cynomolgus monkeys received bilateral intraparenchymal infusions using a 16-gauge SmartFlow cannula into the thalamus on day 1. In addition to formulation buffer, low and high doses of AVB-101 were tested. In-life observations and measurements included clinical observations, body weight, neurological and physical examinations, brain MRI, electrocardiography, ophthalmology, and clinical pathology evaluations. Blood and CSF were collected for bioanalytical analyses. At the necropsy intervals on day 92 ± 3 days or day 183, the animals were sacrificed, and selected tissues were harvested for biodistribution and histopathological evaluation.

Statistical analyses

In the Grn−/− mouse proof-of-concept study, data were analyzed and graphed using GraphPad Prism 9.3.1 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA). A d’Agostino & Pearson test for normality was performed, and if most groups were normally distributed, then one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was performed. If most groups were non-normally distributed, a Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s pairwise comparison test was performed, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical treatment of animals and tolerability

Research involving mouse and sheep studies were performed according to the principles of the Research Council’s UK Policy and Guidelines on the Governance of Research Conduct and the Concordat to Support Research Integrity. Throughout the studies, animals were closely monitored following recovery from anesthesia, assessed for general health, behavior, and neurological changes, and their body weight was monitored. Consideration was given to the welfare of individual animals in terms of the number and extent of procedures to be carried out on each animal. All experimental procedures performed were approved by local animal ethics review committees under project licenses approved by the UK Home Office and the NZ Ministry for Primary Industries.
