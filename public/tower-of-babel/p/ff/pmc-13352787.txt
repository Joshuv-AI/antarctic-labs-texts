Clinical value and regulatory role of lncRNA HOXB-AS3 in gastric cancer
Authors: Yushuang Ding, Lihong Zhang, Yuan Xu, Meini Cen, Pengfei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLncRNA HOXB-AS3 has been shown to exert oncogenic effects in various malignancies. However, its specific role in gastric cancer (GC) remains largely unknown. This study addressed the expression profile and clinical relevance of HOXB-AS3 in GC. Moreover, we dissected its downstream regulatory circuitry in a cell model.MethodsThe cohort comprised 156 GC patients who were followed up for 5 years. Multivariate Cox analysis was employed for risk indicator identification. Relative expression of genes was calculated by RT-qPCR. Cell proliferation was detected by CCK-8 assay, while migration and invasion were assessed by transwell assay. ROC curve was applied for diagnostic efficiency.ResultsHOXB-AS3 was upregulated in GC tissues (P < 0.001) and exhibited promising diagnostic utility (AUC = 0.851, 95% CI: 0.810–0.892). Background

Gastric cancer (GC) ranks as the world’s fifth deadliest malignancy [1]. It exhibits pronounced regional clustering, with Asia bearing a disproportionately high disease burden [2]. By 2040, incident cases and deaths in the region are anticipated to exceed current levels by at least 70%. Most patients are diagnosed at an advanced phase [3]. Biomarkers currently reported for GC progression have limited clinical utility [4] and current GC therapy still faces challenges [5]. While radiotherapy, immunotherapy and targeted therapies confer measurable survival extensions, drug resistance and peritoneal metastasis remain critical barriers to further survival gains [6, 7]. A growing body of evidence implicates microbial infection, dietary patterns, environmental exposures and inherited susceptibility are main contributors [8]. However, the precise molecular circuitry remains incompletely mapped. Thus, exploring new targets and elucidating the pathogenic pathways is the pivotal step toward guiding innovative, evidence-based interventions.

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are defined as RNA transcripts exceeding 200 nucleotides, with stringent tissue specificity and precise temporal-spatial expression. Growing studies have documented widespread dysregulation of lncRNAs in tumor tissue and cell lines, with individual transcripts functioning either as oncogenes or tumor suppressors to drive initiation and progression. Furthermore, distinct lncRNA expression profiles have been shown to hold prognostic value [9]. The upregulated lncRNA SNHG17 in GC partners with worse outcome and it facilitates G1/S transition as well as tumor growth [10]. LncRNA UCA1 accelerates proliferation of GC cells through miR-145/MYO6 [11]. Highly expressed GAPLINC boosts invasion by miR-211-3p/CD44 pathway and results in poor survival [12]. Current studies underscore the potential of lncRNAs in GC development, prognosis and therapy.

HOXB-AS3, an antisense transcript anchored within the HOXB locus, is gaining attention as a dysregulated driver of malignancy. Recent evidence attributes its tumor-promoting role in gallbladder cancer to MEK/ERK-dependent enhancement of cellular proliferation and invasion [13]. Mounting evidence portrays overexpressed HOXB-AS3 as a threat for poor prognosis in epithelial ovarian cancer and mechanically it is reported to fuel oncogenesis through canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling [14], which is a major driver of tumorigenesis, functioning through post-transcriptional RNA regulation [15]. HOXB-AS3 is observed to be overexpressed in endometrial cancer and its upregulation enhances lipogenesis, proliferation as well as invasiveness by interacting with PTBP1 [16]. Bioinformatics approaches are commonly used to identify lncRNA-disease associations in published reports [17]. Based on preliminary bioinformatics analysis, miR-498-5p/EP300 axis may be involved in the regulation of HOXB-AS3. Therefore, we investigated whether the HOXB-AS3 is involved in gastric cancer through miR-498-5p/EP300 axis.

The present investigation addresses the expression profile and clinical relevance of the HOXB-AS3 in GC. Moreover, functionally we dissect its downstream regulatory circuitry using in vitro cell model.

Materials and methods

Study objects and sample preparation

The cohort comprised 156 GC patients from The Affiliated Hospital of Youjiang Medical University for Nationalities, who underwent gastrectomy without prior therapy. Patients included in this study were consecutively enrolled to avoid selection bias. Exclusion criteria were (1) presence of other active malignancies or a history of other malignancies within the past 5 years, (2) severe comorbidities, including but not limited to unstable angina or myocardial infarction within 6 months, heart failure, severe hepatic or renal impairment, uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, interstitial pneumonia, or active infectious diseases, (3) targeted therapy or chemotherapy, (4) severe cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease, and (5) incomplete clinical or follow-up data. The GC tissues and adjacent normal tissues were collected intraoperatively and stored in liquid nitrogen. A post-hoc power analysis was conducted using the observed hazard ratio (HR) for HOXB-AS3 expression. In G*Power 3.1 software, with a two-sided significance level (α) of 0.05, the calculated statistical power exceeded 0.80. This indicated that the current sample size was sufficient to detect a significant difference in overall survival between the HOXB-AS3 high-expression and low-expression groups. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The research protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Affiliated Hospital of Youjiang Medical University for Nationalities and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Cell culture

The purchased GC cell lines (SGC-7901, BGC-823, AGS, MKN45, and HGC-27) and the normal gastric epithelial cell line GES-1. These cells from the Cell Resource Center (Shanghai, China) were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) of the final volume. The above cells were incubated in a humidified incubator under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂).

Cell transfection

The expression level of HOXB-AS3 was modulated by si-HOXB-AS3 for knockdown and HOXB-AS3-OE for overexpression with negative control si-NC and HOXB-AS3-NC, respectively. The miR-498-5p was downregulated by miR-inhibitor (inhibitor-NC as its negative control). Additionally, EP300 was knocked down by specific small interfering RNA (si-EP300). Transient transfection was carried out once the confluence reached 80%, with Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen).

RT-qPCR

RNA isolation was conducted with Trizol (Invitrogen) method. A reverse transcription kit was applied for cDNA synthesis under specific conditions set based on the instructions. A subsequent fluorogenic quantitative PCR kit (Takara) was employed to assess the transcriptional level. Expression levels of HOXB-AS3 (F: 5’-TGCTTGTCTGGAGATGGAGC-3’, R: 5’-GATAAGAGCGATGAGGCGCT-3’) and EP300 were normalized to GAPDH (F: 5’-GACAGTCAGCCGCA TCTTCT-3’, R: 5’-GCGCCCAATAC GACCAAATC-3’) while miR-498-5p was normalized to U6 (F: 5’-CTCGCTTCGGCAGCACA-3’, R: 5’-AACGCTTCACGAATTTGC GT-3’). They were calculated using the method. Values represent fold change relative to the control group (set to 1). The efficiency of cell transfection was validated by RT-qPCR.

Cell proliferation

Cell proliferation of BGC-823 along with MKN45 was accomplished by CCK-8 kit (Beyotine, China). Cell suspension 48 h post-transfection (100 µL/well) was added to a 96-well plate. CCK-8 reagent was administered at 24-hour intervals with 10 µL for a well. After 2 h, absorbance (450 nm) was measured by a microplate reader.

Transwell assay

Cell suspension of BGC-823 or MKN45 was prepared 48 h post-transfection. For migration detection, 6000 cells were seeded into the top chamber while 9000 cells were used for invasion analysis. After an incubation of 24 h, they were fixed with paraformaldehyde (PFA, 4%) for cell number counting. Manual cell counting was performed in five random fields. Each condition was tested in triplicate wells, and the entire experiment was independently repeated three times.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays

A Magna RIP RNA-Binding Protein IP kit (Millipore) was employed for RIP assay. GC cells were lysed in lysis buffer. Then RIPA buffer with magnetic beads was added and placed at 4℃ overnight. Notably, the beads were conjugated with human anti-Ago2 or anti-IgG (control) antibodies. Then washing buffer was added to the beads. After probing the relative enrichment of HOXB-AS3, miR-498-5p and EP300 were quantified by RT-qPCR.

Western blot

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Thermofisher) on ice, and supernatants were collected by centrifugation. Following concentration determination, samples were mixed with loading buffer (Bio-Rad) and denatured at 100 °C for 8 min. proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE. After transfer and blocking, membranes were incubated with specific primary antibodies for EP300 (Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000) and β-Actin (Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000) overnight at 4 °C, followed by secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. For the negative control, the primary antibody was omitted and replaced with blocking buffer and all other steps were performed identically to the experimental groups. Target proteins were visualized using chemiluminescence imaging.

Target prediction

Databases including miRDB (https://mirdb.org/index.html), ENCORI (https://rnasysu.com/encori/index.php), miRWalk (http://mirwalk.umm.uni-heidelberg.de/) and TargetScan (https://www.targetscan.org/vert_80/) were employed for downstream prediction for miR-498-5p. The intersection was applied for PPI analysis with the online STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/). The interaction parameter was 0.7 to establish a clear PPI network.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The wild-type (WT- HOXB-AS3, WT-EP300) and mutation (MUT-HOXB-AS3, MUT-EP300) were inserted into a luciferase vector pmirGLO, respectively. At appropriate confluence, these plasmids were transfected with miR-mimic, miR-498-5p inhibitor (miR-inhibitor), or the negative control (miR-NC). Cells were lysed 48 h later to detect the luminescence intensity.

Statistical analysis

Data from the present study were processed with SPSS 21.0 along with GraphPad Prism 7.0. Statistical differences between groups were evaluated through chi-square test, one-way ANOVA or Student’s t-test. The correlation of HOXB-AS3 and miR-498-5p was indicated by Pearson analysis. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis with area under the curve (AUC) was applied for the diagnostic performance of HOXB-AS3.

Results

Clinical characteristics of patients

The cohort comprised 156 GC patients, with 93 males and 63 females. Among them, 91 patients (55.1%) were over 55 years old. Poorly differentiated tumors were observed in 82 patients (52.6%). Lymph node metastasis was present in 95 patients (60.90%). As for TNM stage, 89 individuals (57.05%) were divided into stage III–IV. A summary of baseline characteristics was provided in Table 1.

Expression of HOXB-AS3 in GC tissues and cell lines

To investigate the role of HOXB-AS3 in GC, its expression level was quantified in 156 patients and exhibited an apparent elevation in GC tissues in contrast with adjacent normal ones (Fig. 1A, P < 0.001). ROC curve analysis revealed an AUC of 0.851(95% CI: 0.810–0.892), with sensitivity and specificity of 70.51% and 83.33%, respectively (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, the in vitro experiments showed that HOXB-AS3 expression was significantly upregulated in different GC cell lines (SGC-7901, BGC-823, AGS, MKN45, and HGC-27) compared with the normal gastric epithelial cell line GES-1 (Fig. 1C, P < 0.001). Compared with other cell lines, the differences in HOXB-AS3 expression levels were most pronounced in BGC-823 and MKN45 cells. To ensure high transfection efficiency and to guarantee that gain-of-function and loss-of-function experiments would yield distinct phenotypic changes, we selected these two representative cell lines as our primary candidates.

Correlation between HOXB-AS3 expression level and clinical characteristics

Shapiro-Wilk normality test on the expression levels of HOXB-AS3 in our cohort (n = 156) indicated that the data followed anormal distribution (P = 0.057). The enrolled patients were stratified into low HOXB-AS3 (n = 62) and high HOXB-AS3 (n = 94) groups based on the mean HOXB-AS3 expression level (Table 2). Statistical analysis demonstrated significant correlations between HOXB-AS3 with differentiation type (P = 0.031, phi = 0.173), lymph node metastasis (P < 0.001, phi = 0.289) and TNM stage (P = 0.006, phi = 0.222). These findings implicated the close involvement of HOXB-AS3 in GC progression.

Follow-up results

The GC patients were followed for 5 years. Statistical data indicated that 27 individuals survived in high HOXB-AS3 group, showing worse overall survival compared to the low HOXB-AS3 group with 30 survivors (Fig. 1D, P = 0.005). Multivariate Cox analysis identified TNM stage (HR = 1.594, 95% CI:1.041–2.4401, P = 0.032), HOXB-AS3 (HR = 1.905, 95% CI:1.209-3.000, P = 0.005) and lymph node metastasis (HR = 1.642, 95% CI:1.054–2.557, P = 0.028) as independent risk factors for poor prognosis (Fig. 1E). Notably, high HOXB-AS3 carriers demonstrated a 1.905-fold mortality, establishing HOXB-AS3 as an independent predictor of adverse outcomes.

Effect of HOXB-AS3 on cell proliferation, migration and invasion

Regulation of HOXB-AS3 expression was achieved via transient cell transfection, with efficiency validated by RT-qPCR. The HOXB-AS3 was successfully downregulated by si-HOXB-AS3 and upregulated by overexpression in BGC-823 cells (Fig. 2A, P < 0.001). The downregulation suppressed proliferation while the upregulation promoted proliferation capacity under these experimental conditions (Fig. 2B, P < 0.001). Consistently, HOXB-AS3 knockdown inhibited migration and invasion, while its overexpression promoted these processes (Fig. 2C-D, P < 0.01). Another GC line MKN45 was employed to validate these regulatory effects. The expression modulation of HOXB-AS3 was confirmed by quantitative analysis (Fig. 2E, P < 0.001). Briefly, HOXB-AS3 knockdown suppressed proliferation, migration, and invasion, while overexpression facilitated these phenotypes (Fig. 2F-H, P < 0.01).

miR-498-5p potentially targeted by HOXB-AS3

Potential downstream miR-498-5p of HOXB-AS3 was predicted via lncBase database and preliminary evaluated by RIP assay. Data manifested an increased relative enrichment for HOXB-AS3 and miR-498-5p in anti-Ago2 group (Fig. 3A, P < 0.001), revealing a coexistence relationship. In dual-luciferase reporter system, WT-HOXB-AS3 luciferase activity increased with miR-498-5p inhibition and decreased with miR-498-5p analog, whereas the mutation (MUT-HOXB-AS3) abolished responsiveness (Fig. 3B, P < 0.001). Clinically, miR-498-5p expression was significantly lower in GC tissue (Fig. 3C, P < 0.001). Moreover, a consistent downward trend was found in 5 GC lines (Fig. 3D, P < 0.01). Pearson correlation analysis identified a strong inverse relationship between miR-498-5p and HOXB-AS3 in GC tissue (Fig. 3E, r = -0.702, P < 0.0001).

Reverse effect of miR-498-5p inhibition on the negative influence from HOXB-AS3 knockdown

Transcriptional quantification following transfection showed that miR-498-5p level was elevated by HOXB-AS3 knockdown, which was subsequently restored to baseline upon co-transfection with miR-498-5p inhibitor (Fig. 4A, P < 0.001). Functionally, HOXB-AS3 knockdown hindered cell proliferation, migration and invasion, which were counteracted by subsequent miR-498-5p inhibition (Fig. 4B-D, P < 0.001). MiR-498-5p levels were increased by HOXB-AS3 knockdown and reversed by miR-498-5p inhibitor (Fig. 4E, P < 0.001). HOXB-AS3 knockdown attenuated proliferation, whereas subsequent miR-498-5p inhibition reversed this negative effect (Fig. 4F, P < 0.01). Similarly, migration and invasion were impaired by its knockdown but were partially restored to aproximately 85% of normal levels following miR-498-5p inhibition (Fig. 4G-H, P < 0.05).

EP300 covalently linked to miR-498-5p

The targets were screened by miRDB, ENCORI, miRWalk and TargertScan databases (Fig. 5A). The intersection encompassed 346 common genes, which were applied for PPI analysis (Fig. 5B). Ten candidates with high node degree were further selected. Based on existing reports, we selected genes associated with GC and the expression level appeared to be increasing (EP300 and PIK3R1) for further validation in dual-luciferase reporter system. EP300 emerged as a promising target of miR-498-5p (Fig. 5C). In WT-EP300 construct, miR-498-5p inhibition enhanced luciferase activity, whereas miR-498-5p mimic suppressed it (P < 0.01). However, the mutation (MUT-EP300) rendered luciferase activity unresponsive to miR-498-5p modulation (Fig. 5C). To validate the HOXB-AS3/miR-498-5p/EP300 regulatory axis, we performed co-transfection experiments across 5 GC lines. Data demonstrated that HOXB-AS3 knockdown markedly downregulated EP300 expression, which was substantially rescued by simultaneous inhibition of miR-498-5p (Fig. 5D, P < 0.05). A full rescue experiment involving EP300 knockdown in the context of HOXB-AS3 modulation was performed in BGC-823 cells. In the context of HOXB-AS3 knockdown, miR-498-5p inhibition increased the EP300 expression level, which was then negated by the specific interference of EP300 (Fig. 6A, P < 0.01). The increased capacity of proliferation, migration and invasion under conditions of knocking down both HOXB-AS3 and miR-498-5p was reversed by subsequent interference of EP300 (Fig. 6B-D, P < 0.05).

Discussion

As the world’s fifth most common cancer with a declining age of onset, the main survival limitations in GC remain postoperative recurrence, distant metastasis, and surgical complications. Clarifying its pathological mechanisms is essential for advancing clinical practice and accelerating the shift from empirical to precision-based GC care. This study demonstrated that upregulated HOXB-AS3 exhibited significant diagnostic utility. and was positively correlated with poor prognosis. Mechanistically, HOXB-AS3 overexpression promoted cell proliferation, migration, and invasion through the miR-498-5p/EP300 axis under experimental conditions.

In this study, quantitative analysis showed HOXB-AS3 was upregulated in GC tissues and exhibited promising diagnostic utility. This aberrant expression aligned with that in many tumor lesions. For instance, its upregulation has been documented in colon adenocarcinoma [18]. Similarly, clinical RNAseq data reveals its elevated expression in metastatic serous ovarian cancer [19]. The diagnostic value of circulating lncRNAs in GC is further corroborated by existing literature. A meta-analysis of 40 studies involving 6,772 patients reports that circulating lncRNAs for GC diagnosis yield a pooled AUC of 0.85 [20]. By comparison to conventional biomarkers such as CEA and CA19-9, HOXB-AS3 presented better diagnostic potential [21, 22].

Our follow-up data demonstrated an elevated mortality in the high HOXB-AS3 group, which was a risk factor. Our results implied the correction of elevated HOXB-AS3 and adverse prognosis. Supportive evidence can be observed in previous reports. As reported in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, HOXB-AS3 is implicated in predicting prognostic risks and survival outcomes [23]. Besides, acute myeloid leukemia patients with high HOXB-AS3 expression experienced shorter survival than low-expression patients, establishing it as a poor prognostic marker [24]. Additionally, a multivariate Cox regression analysis independently corroborates its association with adverse prostate cancer prognosis [25].

The regulatory role of HOXB-AS3 in GC was subsequently explored. Its upregulation was observed in multiple GC cell lines, associated with enhanced proliferation, migration and invasion. Accumulating evidence also emphasizes its oncogenic role. Results from lung cancer manifested that HOXB-AS3 enhances proliferation, migration and invasion via PI3K/AKT pathway [26]. HOXB-AS3 is also reported to drive epithelial ovarian cancer progression via glycolytic pathway, while its knockdown significantly suppresses proliferation, invasion and migration [27]. A study concerning hepatocellular carcinoma indicated that increased HOXB-AS3 facilitates cell proliferation through p53 suppression [28]. Thus, HOXB-AS3 functioned as an oncogenic driver capable of regulating diverse signaling pathways.

Here, miR-498-5p was recognized as a target of HOXB-AS3 and was negatively regulated by it. Their target binding has been reported previously [29]. Ubiquitous downregulation of miR-498-5p in GC tissues and cell lines has been observed [30], [31]. Based on existing reports, miR-498-5p downregulation drives GC progression via multiple mechanisms, including targeting LIN28B to relieve proliferation inhibition [30], activating AKT pathway to enhance malignant phenotype [32], and targeting CEACAM5 to facilitate metastasis [31]. Thus, miR-498-5p downregulation represented a critical driver of GC development.

E1A-binding protein p300 (EP300) is a lysine acetyltransferase and transcriptional co-activator. Studies indicate that variability in EP300 expression bears a close relationship to GC development and progression [33, 34]. Published reports have identified it to be overexpressed in various GC lines and its inhibition suppresses the survival and invasion of GC cella [35]. It has also been demonstrated to drive chemoresistance by forming a TWIST1 transcriptional complex that activates COL1A2 [36]. Previous studies have demonstrated that the GC progression involves multiple molecular mechanisms, primarily including the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [37], autophagy and oxidative stress responses [38] and the immune microenvironment and macrophage polarization [39]. Given that the regulatory role of EP300 in these pathways has been widely reported [40], [41], the deeper regulatory mechanism of EP300 in GC may involve these key pathways. Above results suggest that EP300 knockdown may provide a potential target for cancer therapy. Considering new tool (SEanalysis 2.0) for studying HOXB-AS3 [42], it is necessary to conduct in-depth investigations into the regulatory functions of HOXB-AS3 through various approaches, such as inflammation and oxidative stress [43, 44].

Collectively, this study found HOXB-AS3 was a potential diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target in GC, acting via the miR-498-5p/EP300 axis. The translational implications (diagnostic value and therapeutic targets) are promising; however, our results should be interpreted with caution as they rely solely on in vitro data.

This study has several limitations. First, while our results suggest a central role for the ceRNA axis, the potential contribution of other regulatory mechanisms (transcriptional control, protein-protein interactions) cannot be ruled out. Comprehensive future investigations integrating transcriptomic and proteomic approaches (e.g., ChIRP-MS) alongside multi-pathway functional assays (e.g., inflammation, oxidative stress) are needed to delineate the full spectrum of HOXB-AS3’s regulatory functions in GC. Second, this study is primarily based on in vitro cell models. To validate the clinical relevance of our findings, we plan to construct xenograft tumor models in nude mice in subsequent work to further confirm the functions and regulatory mechanisms of key molecules.
