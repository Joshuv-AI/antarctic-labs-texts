From unwelcome to supportive. Patients’ conceptions of being prescribed a glucose-lowering drug at type 2 diabetes diagnosis – a phenomenographic study
Authors: Sofia Dalemo, Yvonne Carlsson, Johan Alsterhag, Susanne Andersson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeGuidelines recommend starting glucose-lowering drug upon type 2 diabetes mellitus diagnosis together with lifestyle changes. Lifestyle changes are as essential as the drug, earlier recommendations allowed some months of lifestyle changes while being drug-free. Prescription on diagnosis may interfere with patients’ understanding and motivation for lifestyle changes if they cannot evaluate the effect on blood glucose.MethodsA phenomenographic approach and interviews were conducted with patients who started a glucose-lowering drug at diagnosis.ResultsThree qualitatively different conceptions of being prescribed glucose-lowering drugs in connection to type 2 diabetes mellitus diagnosis were found: “Drugs as something unwelcome,” “Drugs as a support,” and “Drugs as a means to reach the goal”. These conceptions range broadly from drugs as unwelcome to drugs as a support for lifestyle changes and an opportunity to influence the course of the lifelong disease to reach a goal.ConclusionsThis study has identified various perspectives of patients’ understanding of the role of lifestyle changes in managing their disease. The patients undergo a process, and the perspectives vary, providing a more extensive and nuanced understanding. It is, therefore, impossible to apply a routine protocol and a person-centred approach is required when prescribing a glucose-lowering drug.KEYWORDS: Diabetes mellitus type 2, drug therapy, lifestyle, metformin, primary healthcare, qualitative research, phenomenography

Introduction

The number of patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) has increased globally over the past three decades, making it a significant healthcare problem (Chen et al., 2011). It is estimated that 783 million adults will have diabetes by 2045 (Sun et al., 2022). This trend can also be seen in Sweden, where approximately 5% of the population has known T2DM diabetes (The Swedish National Diabetes Register, 2022). An underlying reason is the increasing number of elderly people, as T2DM susceptibility increases with age. T2DM is often associated with lifestyle-related factors but also with genetic factors (International Diabetes Federation, 2021). The basis of its treatment is lifestyle intervention, such as a healthy diet, weight loss, and improved physical activity, often in combination with pharmacological treatment (International Diabetes Federation, 2021).

T2DM is a serious disease that can lead to severe complications and premature death (Goff et al., 2007; Norhammar et al., 2016). Early treatment of elevated glucose levels leads to fewer complications and reduced mortality (UK Prospective Diabetes Study UKPDS Group, 1998). Metformin, a glucose-lowering drug, has a preventive effect on cardiovascular disease, which is another reason to prescribe the drug (Holman et al., 2008). The Swedish Medical Products Agency recommends starting metformin immediately upon a T2DM diagnosis (Swedish Medical Products Agency, 2017). The same guidelines also recommend increased physical activity and necessary lifestyle changes because lifestyle changes are as essential in managing T2DM as the drug. Previous guidelines offered patients a few months to introduce and evaluate lifestyle changes’ effect on blood glucose levels while being drug-free before starting drug treatment (International Diabetes Federation, 2021; National Board of Health and Welfare, 1996).

In Sweden, patients with T2DM usually receive care at primary healthcare centres, where they meet specialist diabetes nurses and general practitioners. The diabetes nurse primarily educates patients with T2DM and strengthens their self-management ability regarding diet, physical activity, and lifestyle (Kjellsdotter et al., 2020). To meet the different needs of patients, person-centred care is appropriate with a partnership that incorporates listening to the patient’s story, sharing information, and joint decision-making (Ekman et al., 2011). For patients with recently diagnosed T2DM, an initial period focused on lifestyle changes can offer a pedagogical experience to evaluate the effect of these changes on blood glucose levels while free from drugs. Reflecting on and experiencing a direct impact on blood glucose levels could promote learning and increased awareness of one’s responsibility for healthy lifestyle changes (Johansson et al., 2016).

Having T2DM changes one’s living conditions. To manage the new situation with illness and the consequences for the current life situation and health in the longer term, the illness and treatment must be integrated into daily life (Hörnsten et al., 2011). This can be understood as a process of learning grounded in the new life situation (Berglund, 2011; Johansson et al., 2016; Kneck et al., 2011).

Although the effects of glucose-lowering drugs on complications and mortality are well studied, there is a lack of studies on how patients’ conceptions of being prescribed a glucose-lowering drug in direct connection to a T2DM diagnosis vary. There are concerns that prescription on diagnosis may interfere with patients’ understanding of the role of lifestyle changes in managing their disease. By understanding how patients perceive being prescribed a drug and simultaneously implementing the necessary lifestyle changes, healthcare professionals can adapt their practice to the patients’ responses to the new situation. This study can better highlight patients’ conceptions of being prescribed a glucose-lowering drug while simultaneously implementing necessary lifestyle changes and lead to healthcare professionals providing person-centred care.

Therefore this study aims to describe variations in patients’ conceptions of being prescribed a glucose-lowering drug in direct connection to a T2DM diagnosis, and how it affects the motivation for lifestyle changes.

Material and methods

Design

We used semi-structured interviews and a phenomenographic approach to understand how patients with newly diagnosed T2DM describe being prescribed a glucose-lowering drug. The phenomenographic approach is appropriate for exploring the broad range of experiences of a specific phenomenon (Sjöström & Dahlgren, 2002). This study intends to describe different ways of understanding the phenomenon and not merely describe how things “really are” because it is how patients experience the phenomenon that influences their perception of drugs and lifestyle changes (Larsson & Holmström, 2017). The phenomenon examined in this study was the collective variations of conceptions to identify different aspects of the experience of being prescribed a glucose-lowering drug at T2DM diagnosis (Marton & Booth, 1997). Identifying the variations of the described phenomenon can highlight how the drug treatment is understood and reflected upon by patients struggling with T2DM.

Settings and participants

Potential participants were identified by nurses or physicians or through the Swedish National Diabetes Register (2016) in three primary healthcare centres in three cities in southwestern Sweden. The primary healthcare centres were all rural and had between 13,200 and 17,700 registered patients. One healthcare centre was privately run, while the other two were publicly run. To enable as many variations of experiences as possible, patients who met the inclusion criteria were strategically included from the three healthcare centres. The number of participants was considered large enough for diverse experiences to be explored.

The inclusion criteria for participants were a recent diagnosis of T2DM within the past six months before the interview, age above 18 years, a glucose-lowering drug prescription in connection with the diagnosis, and sufficient language proficiency. The characteristics of the interviewees are described in Table 1. Overall, 22 individual interviews were conducted, with one interview later being excluded due to insufficient language proficiency.

Data collection

All the interviews were conducted between October 2020 and February 2022. They were initially intended to be done face to face, but due to COVID-19 restrictions, 12 interviews were done via telephone. Three of the authors (SD, JA, SA) conducted the interviews, which were numbered in the order they were done. The participants were between 30 and 75 years old. A majority were men born in Sweden. Many of the participants had symptoms at diagnosis, including elevated average glucose and HbA1c levels (see Table I).

The semi-structured interviews were recorded digitally and transcribed verbatim. An interview guide was used, and some of the questions included were “Tell me about your feelings and thoughts when you received a drug for your diabetes”, “What information have you received from your doctor/nurse?” and “Have you made any efforts for your health besides taking the glucose-lowering drug after the T2DM diagnosis?”. Follow-up questions, such as “Please, tell me more” and “What is your experience?”, were used to clarify the intended meanings behind the patients’ answers.

Analysis

Initially, the three authors (SD, JA, SA) performed the data analysis. During the later steps of the analysis, another author (YC) joined to give methodological advice. To reach a common understanding, an ongoing discussion took place during the process. The transcribed interviews were analysed using a phenomenographic approach following a seven-step analysis method described by Sjöström and Dahlgren (see Table 2) (Berglund, 2011). The consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative studies were applied (see supplementary file) and adhered to as a checklist for reporting the findings.

Ethical consideration

The study was performed according to the Declaration of Helsinki (WMA, 2013) and was approved by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority (reference number 2020–02024). All participants gave oral informed consent, which was recorded, to participate in the study. It was emphasized that participation was voluntary and anonymous.

Result

Three qualitatively different conceptions, describing the patients’ overall understanding of the phenomenon of being prescribed a glucose-lowering drug in connection to a T2DM diagnosis were found. These are described below as “Drugs as something unwelcome”, “Drugs as a support”, and “Drugs as a means to reach the goal”. The first conception reflects that the patients only do as they are instructed and take the drug. The second conception, reflects that they are engaged in the treatment of the disease and see opportunities to combine the drug with diet changes and exercise habits. The last conception conveys a learning perspective, an opportunity to reflect on and influence the course of the lifelong disease with drugs as one of several ways to reach set goals. Each conception is illustrated using direct quotes from the patients. Conceptions

Three conceptions found from the analysis are detailed in this section.

Drugs as something unwelcome

The conception that the drug is unwelcome includes an understanding that T2DM is a disease that patients can develop since it runs in the family. When a glucose-lowering drug was prescribed in connection with a T2DM diagnosis, the patients felt it was presented by the physician/nurse as a part of the necessary treatment. Patients perceived the situation as leaving no room for other choices, presenting the drug as compulsory to take. In contrast, according to the patients, it could be good to first make lifestyle changes and wait to begin using the drug to evaluate the effect of the lifestyle changes. Taking the drug can be perceived as a benefit, but at the same time, they are aware of the potential side effects of the drugs. However, to not risk getting sicker, the patients perceived no other choice but to start taking the drug.

Drugs as a support

The conception is that the drug supports structure and is a compensation as a part of the treatment. It is also a reminder that one has a disease that does not always have symptoms. A glucose-lowering drug can be perceived as a support to gain knowledge about how and what it means to have T2DM. It can also be understood that by starting the prescribed treatment, one is confident that the doctor/nurse has the required knowledge. According to the patients, the drug could also act as a support to gain time to implement lifestyle changes. The patients acknowledged that the drug did not cure the disease but supported them in bringing about the required changes. The patients perceive a responsibility to make the necessary lifestyle changes. There is an insight that the progression of the disease can be delayed and that both the drug and lifestyle changes may affect blood glucose levels.

Drugs as a means to reach the goal

The last conception is that the drug helps to reach the goal of maintaining blood glucose values in the long term through a balanced diet, exercise, and drugs. The glucose-lowering drug is perceived as a life-long drug treatment, not as a temporary cure. Taking the drug regularly is not in itself positive, but if the drug counteracts the disease, it is perceived as an opportunity. The patients have undergone a process and learn more about themselves and the new situation. They see both the drug and lifestyle changes as ways to reach the set goal. Taking the drug may provide opportunities to reflect and influence the course of the lifelong illness with drugs. The patient feels that it clarifies their own responsibility and offers an opportunity to take control of the disease. It is up to the patient to ensure that the goal is reached and to gain life-long health.

Dimensions

The three conceptions were further analysed across five dimensions. The dimensions represent key aspects of how patients experience the conceptions as a part of the whole in the different ways of understanding the phenomenon. The following dimensions were analysed: “Degree of responsibility”, “Lifestyle habits”, “Participation”, “Health”, and “Experiences”. The relationships between the three conceptions and the five dimensions of variation are represented in an outcome space (Table III). The table shows a hierarchy of increasing complexity of understanding of being prescribed a drug in connection with T2DM diagnosis. The following subsections present an overview of the five dimensions that emerged from the iterative analysis process.

Degree of responsibility

There was variation in the breadth of understanding of the patients’ responsibility for their treatment. The understanding ranged from only being compliant with the drug prescription and doing as the healthcare providers say. They then do not see that they have any choice of their own (Drugs as something unwelcome). Some see the drug as a support, along with other lifestyle changes, to achieve acceptable blood glucose levels and compensate with the drug for “something that is missing” (Drugs as a support). The more advanced understanding view was goal-oriented and saw opportunities to reduce the number of tablets and influence the disease’s course (Drugs as a means to reach the goal).

Lifestyle habits

There are big differences in how patients perceive the meaning of lifestyle habits in relation to the drug. Some perceive taking their drug on time as the effort they make. Others see the glucose-lowering drug as a complement to necessary changes in lifestyle, such as diet changes and exercise habits (Drugs as something unwelcome). Some see the drug as a daily reminder and help for finding a structure for food intake (Drugs as a support). Others see opportunities to manage their life with the support of the drug, and changed lifestyles are described as a way forward—a way to continue to live one’s life despite the illness with a wish to decrease or become free from drugs (Drugs as a means to reach the goal).

Participation

Participation can be perceived as not being involved in the decision and only doing as one is told (Drugs as something unwelcome). It can also be understood as an openness and sense of security to discuss the treatment with their healthcare provider. Some patients started with the prescribed treatment and trusted that the physician/nurse had the knowledge required to choose the best treatment (Drugs as a support). A high degree of participation can be described as patients having the opportunity to influence and be part of the treatment process, not just taking drugs (Drugs as a means to reach the goal).

Health

The study revealed variations regarding health and taking a glucose-lowering drug in the long term. Patients could perceive taking the drug as an extra burden since there is an uncertain aspect regarding the drug and its need (Drugs as something unwelcome). Others perceive taking the drug for a preventive purpose, even if they have no symptoms yet and their health is perceived as good now (Drugs as a support). Some perceive lifestyle changes and taking the drug as necessary to stay healthy over time since there is an awareness that health does not improve with age (Drugs as a means to reach the goal).

Experiences

There is an awareness of the risks of developing diabetes and what it means, both now and in the future. Some patients had relatives with diabetes and feared to develop the disease, and some had heard about the struggles (Drugs as something unwelcome). Other patients perceive that elevated glucose levels contribute to their benefiting from the drug.

The patients perceived that, together with lifestyle changes, the drug helped them understand that there may be consequences of the disease that could lead to complications (Drugs as a support). Therefore, some patients see the drug as a new chance to live their life under different conditions. The drug and lifestyle changes offer an opportunity to take control of the disease (Drugs as a means to reach the goal).

Discussion

Studies of patients’ experiences of being prescribed a glucose-lowering drug in connection with a T2DM diagnosis affect the motivation for lifestyle changes have not previously been described in the literature, so the current study fills this gap. An interesting outcome of this study was the broad range of patients’ conceptions on being prescribed a glucose-lowering drug. It illuminates a spectrum from “Drugs as something unwelcome” to “Drugs as a support” to “Drugs as a means to reach the goal”.

The first conception was that the drug was unavoidable and unwelcome. Many patients have previously seen themselves as someone who does not use medication, so starting with a glucose-lowering drug is a big deal, although unwelcome. The glucose-lowering drug was not described as a miracle cure. A contrast may be that as a patient, it is more important to be understood as a person, a patient with T2DM, not primarily cured (Ekman et al., 2011). This perspective differs from physicians and nurses, who primarily see their tasks as curing diseases (Anden et al., 2005). When the patients tried to make sense of being prescribed a glucose-lowering drug in connection with the diagnosis, they described it as unwelcome and accompanied by demands to change their lifestyle. Many patients do not realize that lifestyle changes are as essential treatment as the drug.

In this study, the patients perceived that the medical side effects could be troublesome, and that there was always a risk of side effects that may appear now or later. This led to some scepticism about the drug. Another study showed that simply being prescribed long-term drug treatment seems to have a strong influence on both identity and self-perception. Shared decision-making when starting drug treatment seems to be important for the outcome (Hultberg & Rudebeck, 2017). A person-centred approach to care sets the person’s conceptions at the centre, and the narrative may be the starting point for partnership (Ekman et al., 2011). Furthermore, how patients make sense of their new situation with a glucose-lowering drug is affected by experiences in life; thus, it is impossible to apply a routine treatment protocol when starting medical treatment. Therefore, healthcare professionals have a crucial role to play in telling and listening to the patients in an open manner as a way to create a common understanding (Ekman et al., 2011) and not only be focused on the laboratory levels of the disease (Sørensen et al., 2020). In this study, the experience of not participating in the decision-making process may have been reinforced by the COVID-19 pandemic when physical meetings were limited (Florin et al., 2008; Strobel et al., 2023).

The second conception is that patients experience the glucose-lowering drug as supportive, which motivates and inspires the implementation of essential lifestyle changes. The patients have already worked their way through the process and reached the level at which taking medicine is no longer an issue, as medication has already been incorporated into daily life (Hultberg et al., 2021).

Taking a life-long drug can be seen as a daily reminder of the disease. The medication times become like a mealtime schedule that gives structure to their food intake and physical activities. Another Swedish study showed that reflecting on the drug and its effects contributes to the understanding of the disease’s complexity. This understanding increases the motivation to be responsible for the treatment and implementation of new habits (Kjellsdotter et al., 2020).

The third and last conception is described by the patients as an opportunity to reflect on and influence the course of the lifelong disease. Drugs are one of several ways to reach set goals. Lifestyle habits such as a healthy diet and increased physical activity are essential as the drug in managing the disease. The consequences for health in the longer term mean that the disease and its treatment must become a natural part of life; it must be integrated with the person one is (Hörnsten et al., 2011). To manage the new situation of having T2DM with changed life conditions, a process of learning grounded in the new life situation must occur (Berglund, 2011; Johansson et al., 2016; Kneck et al., 2011). Most patients with T2DM in Sweden are registered in the Swedish National Diabetes Register. Through the register, it is possible to follow, for example, the average level of blood glucose (HbA1c) at different healthcare centres. These monitoring statistics may contribute to healthcare professionals feeling pressured to introduce medication for blood glucose control in connection with the diagnosis. The monitoring statistics may also contribute to healthcare professionals not presenting a healthy diet and increased physical activity as being as important as the glucose-lowering drug.

Strengths and limitations

The chosen qualitative method using interviews and a phenomenographic approach is appropriate when describing the collective variations of experiences among patients with newly diagnosed T2DM and their conceptions of having a glucose-lowering drug prescribed. The intention of the phenomenographic approach is not to describe how things really are (Sjöström & Dahlgren, 2002) but to understand a phenomenon and establish a structure of the different understandings (Åkerlind, 2023). Credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability build trustworthiness, which forms the basis for qualitative research.

When using a phenomenographic approach, it is common that no new experiences are identified after 10 to 12 interviews (Larsson & Holmström, 2017), and this assumption is strengthened by the result of the analysis. The data from the study were rich and varied and resulted in findings that increase the understanding of how patients with recently diagnosed T2DM perceive having a glucose-lowering drug prescribed. Thus, when it comes to transferring the findings to similar settings, it is important to consider the possibility that other experiences will be found since humans vary in how they perceive situations (Åkerlind, 1981).

Semi-structured interviews were chosen as the data collection method and were conducted within six months of the diabetes diagnosis. This is considered a strength since the respondents had time to reflect on the situation but not forget. The interviews were performed by three of the authors, which may be a weakness. However, with support from an interview guide with similar interview questions and techniques, dependability was achieved. Furthermore, all interviews were conducted by persons with several years of experience meeting patients in a primary care context. Most of the individual interviews were conducted over the phone due to the COVID-19 pandemic, which may be seen as a limitation of the analysis process, as facial expressions or body language could not be observed. However, a strength may be the increased level of comfort, as the patients could be in their home environment during the phone call. Thus, some patients with limited social contacts due to the pandemic may have been more outspoken than they would normally have been, as there was little else requiring their attention.

Due to the pandemic, several patients in this study received information about the glucose-lowering drug in a letter or through a telephone call, whereas they might otherwise have been offered a visit to a nurse or physician at the healthcare centre. The National Board of Health and Welfare reported a large reduction in patients with long-term diseases who received care according to national guidelines during the pandemic (National Board of Health and Welfare, 2021).

In this study, many patients were diagnosed with T2DM due to symptoms. The patients had clearly elevated average glucose levels with a high mean HbA1c level (69 mmol/mol). This was likely an effect of the pandemic when patients waited longer to seek medical care and had chosen to ignore or cancel visits to a physician/nurse. It could certainly also be the case that patients had to wait longer than normal to get help at the healthcare centre due to the pandemic. This situation may have affected the result in this study.

Three of the authors participated as interviewers, and all authors participated in all the different stages of the analyses. To strengthen the trustworthiness of the present study, the analysis process was performed by all the authors, and the conceptions were developed through peer discussions. Quotations are presented in the findings in Table III to enable validation of the relevance of the experiences.

Conclusion

This study has identified and described various perspectives of patients’ understanding of the role of lifestyle changes in managing their T2DM disease when being prescribed a drug on diagnosis. The patients undergo a process, and the perspectives vary from elementary to more refined conceptions, providing a more extensive and nuanced understanding. As the experiences varied significantly among patients, it is impossible to apply a routine protocol and treat everyone in the same way when starting glucose-lowering drug treatment. A person-centred approach is required when prescribing glucose-lowering drugs as part of the care for T2DM. Professionals and patients must develop a partnership to reach mutually agreed goals based on joint decision-making.
