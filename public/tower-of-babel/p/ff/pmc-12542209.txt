Platelet-derived biomimetic nanoplatforms: a promising drug delivery strategy for cardiovascular disease
Authors: Simin Zhang, Mei Han, Shan Li, Yixuan Wang, Lihua Wu, Li Dong, Pan Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOver the past few decades, research has demonstrated that the progression of cardiovascular diseases is related to platelets, and the associated pathological responses include bleeding and hemostasis, antithrombotic and prothrombotic effects, and immune and inflammatory reactions. Significant platelet-derived biomimetic nanotechnology (PBN) has garnered considerable interest as a drug delivery system due to its excellent biomimetic properties, leading to the enhanced development of innovative therapies. In this review, we have organized the research progress of three types of technologies: platelet receptor-mediated technology, platelet membrane-derived application technology, and artificial platelet-like nanomimetic technology. This review focuses on the use of PBN in cardiovascular diseases, including myocardial ischemia-reperfusion, coronary atherosclerosis, and stenosis, aiming to highlight its potential benefits and uniqueness in this field. This review provides an overview of the main types of current laboratory PBNs and their applications in different cardiovascular diseases. Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) represent a complex group of systemic disorders, primarily encompassing myocardial infarction, coronary artery disease, atherosclerosis, and thrombosis. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), CVDs account for approximately 17.9 million annual deaths globally, representing one-third of total mortality [1, 2]. Notably, CVD incidence continues to rise annually, with an emerging trend towards younger affected populations. Current therapeutic strategies include interventional procedures, thrombolytic therapy, and pharmacological interventions (e.g., anticoagulants, antiplatelet agents, and lipid-lowering drugs) [3]. Despite ongoing refinements in clinical management driven by the complexity of the cardiovascular system, significant challenges persist. While percutaneous coronary intervention demonstrates benefit for patients with acute myocardial infarction or severe coronary stenosis, postoperative complications remain a critical area of investigation. The profound impact of chronic CVD on patient quality of life and the associated substantial economic burden underscore the paramount need for safer and more efficacious treatments.

The pathophysiological link between platelets and the progression of CVD has garnered increasing attention from researchers. As essential circulating anucleate cells, platelets play pivotal roles in multiple CVD-associated processes, including hemostasis, thrombosis (both pro- and anti-thrombotic effects), immune responses, and inflammation [4]. Critically, platelets function as rapid responders to vascular injury, enabling them to target adhesion to damaged endothelium and subsequently form a thrombus [5, 6].

Advances in nanotechnology offer novel opportunities for innovative CVD therapeutics and drug delivery systems. Nanoparticles exhibit the distinct advantage of traversing biological barriers to access specific sites, thereby facilitating direct interactions with molecular targets (e.g., DNA, proteins) within the blood, organs, tissues, or cells [7]. An ideal delivery platform achieves precise targeting, minimizing off-target distribution and systemic clearance. Conventional nanocarriers, including polymeric nanoparticles, liposomes, and inorganic nanomaterials, enable targeted therapy within the cardiovascular system [8]. Biomimetic strategies utilizing natural human cell membranes offer further advantages, such as inherent cellular mimicry, passive/active targeting capabilities, negligible cytotoxicity, prolonged circulation half-life, and superior biocompatibility and biodegradability [9, 10].

Research into biologically inspired systems, particularly those that mimic the complex functions of circulating blood cells, has intensified. Notably, the application of cell membranes as protective carriers and functional components has advanced significantly [11]. Diverse membrane sources—including erythrocytes [12], platelets [13], leukocytes [14], cancer cells [15], stem cells [16], and hybrid membranes [17])—demonstrate unique properties. Among these, PLT membranes present distinct advantages, including a relatively low antigenic profile, high availability, reduced immunogenicity, and extended circulation persistence compared to nucleated cells [18, 19]. Furthermore, the anucleate nature of platelets facilitates membrane isolation [20]. Consequently, PBN leverages its inherent site-specific targeting and favorable pharmacokinetics [21], holding significant promise for targeted CVD therapies [22] (Table 1).

Current PBN production methodologies and research foci continue to evolve in tandem with technological advancements [21]. PBN research primarily encompasses three domains: (1) platelet receptor-mediated technology, (2) platelet membrane-derived application technology, and (3) artificial platelet-like biomimetic technology. This review aims to elucidate the classification of PBN strategies and their recent preclinical advancements for CVD treatment [23].

Biological effects of the platelet

The complex structure of PLTs is anatomically divided into four central regions from the periphery inward [6, 24]:

Peripheral Zone: Mediates PLT adhesion, activation, and participation in plasma coagulation. This zone comprises the glycocalyx, plasma membrane, surface-connected canalicular system, membrane receptors (e.g., integrins, glycoprotein Ib-IX-V), and the submembrane cytoskeleton [25].

Peripheral Zone: Mediates PLT adhesion, activation, and participation in plasma coagulation. This zone comprises the glycocalyx, plasma membrane, surface-connected canalicular system, membrane receptors (e.g., integrins, glycoprotein Ib-IX-V), and the submembrane cytoskeleton [25].

Sol-Gel Zone (Cytoskeletal Matrix): Forms the cytoplasmic matrix, rich in microtubules and microfilaments (actin), responsible for maintaining platelet shape and facilitating structural changes.

Organelle Zone: Contains secretory granules (primarily α-granules and dense granules (δ-granules)), mitochondria, lysosomes, and peroxisomes. Notably, α-granules store factors critical for adhesion, aggregation, and coagulation.

Membrane System Zone: Primarily consists of the dense tubular system, a residual network from megakaryocyte rough endoplasmic reticulum, serving as the primary site for thromboxane A synthesis and calcium storage.

This unique physiological architecture underpins platelet functions, including adhesion, aggregation, and granular release [26] (Fig. 1). The phospholipid bilayer of the platelet membrane (PM) hosts specific receptors that enable direct interactions with vascular injury sites, contributing to immune evasion, and mediating drug responses [4]. Initial platelet adhesion to exposed subendothelial collagen is facilitated by the integrin α2β1, followed by firm adhesion and transmembrane signaling via the glycoprotein VI receptor [27]. Concurrently, the glycoprotein Ibα component of the glycoprotein Ib-IX-V complex binds to collagen-adherent von Willebrand Factor (vWF), which is adherent to collagen or exposed matrix, promoting platelet tethering and rolling under shear stress. This specific receptor ultimately enhances adhesion to damaged vessel walls and aggregation [26, 28].

Platelets exhibit multifaceted roles in both physiological homeostasis and cardiovascular pathologies [26, 29]. They participate critically in innate and adaptive immunity, atherosclerosis, angiogenesis, and inflammation [29]. Through dynamic interactions with endothelial cells, platelets can induce and propagate chronic vascular inflammation, thereby contributing to the initiation and progression of atherosclerotic lesions [30, 31]. Activated platelets accumulate at inflammatory foci, releasing mediators that recruit leukocytes and modulate tissue repair processes [31]. The distinctive advantages of platelets, particularly for nanotechnology applications, have spurred significant research interest [32, 33]. Surface-functionalized proteins enable the engineering of platelet-mimetic nanoparticles with enhanced biocompatibility, increased mobility, prolonged circulation, inherent biodegradability, and intrinsic pathological tissue targeting [13]. These properties facilitate the development of safe, efficient, and potentially cost-effective drug delivery strategies, which hold considerable promise for treating vascular diseases [34, 35].

Consequently, leveraging platelet physiology, pen research is categorized into three primary domains based on production strategy:

(1) Platelet Receptor-Mediated Technology: Exploits glycoproteins and membrane receptors (primarily in the peripheral zone) via receptor-ligand interactions. This approach promotes targeted platelet aggregation at disease sites, enabling the co-delivery of diagnostic or therapeutic agents [36].

(2) Platelet Membrane-Derived Application Technology: Utilizes the inherent biological properties of platelet membranes for nanoparticle “cloaking”. Nanoparticles are encapsulated within intact platelet membranes, platelet-derived extracellular vesicles (PEVs), or hybrid membranes (fused with other cell membranes) [37]. This strategy leverages membrane biocompatibility to evade immune clearance (e.g., by macrophages) prior to reaching the target site, thereby minimizing immunogenicity [38, 39].

(3) Artificial Platelet-Like Biomimetic Technology: Involves the design of fully synthetic or biohybrid particles that mimic key platelet structural and functional features (e.g., adhesion, aggregation, targeting). These artificial systems aim to achieve intelligent, targeted drug delivery [40]. Key methodologies for coupling nanoparticles to platelet membranes or components in PBN include co-extrusion and sonication [19](Fig. 2and Fig. 3).

Application of platelet-derived biomimetic nanotechnology

Platelet receptor-mediated technology

The PM surface displays an array of receptor proteins, including integrins, prostaglandin receptors, lipid receptors, and glycoproteins [24, 41]. These receptors facilitate platelet adhesion and aggregation through ligand-specific interactions. For instance, the glycoprotein Ib (GPIb) complex binds vWF associated with exposed collagen at vascular injury sites, initiating tissue repair cascades [42]. Glycoprotein IIb/IIIa (GPIIb/IIIa) plays a pivotal role in platelet aggregation and adhesion, serving as a critical therapeutic target for bleeding disorders [43]. Liposomes functionalized with platelet membranes expressing glycoprotein IIb (GPIIb) and glycoprotein Ib-β (GPIb-β) demonstrate enhanced binding affinity for circulating monocytes and exhibit prolonged accumulation at myocardial infarction sites [44].

Activated platelets represent ideal targets for directing regenerative cells to ischemic myocardium. Single-chain variable fragments targeting the activated conformation of GPIIb/IIIa bind to unique epitopes with high specificity and sensitivity [45, 46], which enables the precise aggregation of Single-chain variable fragment GPIIb/IIIa conjugates within ischemic cardiac tissue [47, 48].

The presence of integrin-associated protein (“do not eat me” signal) within the PM enables nanoparticles like PM@Se/Rb1 to interact with macrophage signal regulatory protein alpha, effectively inhibiting phagocytosis [49]. Ligand binding to platelet membrane receptors can trigger diverse downstream biochemical signaling pathways [50, 51]. Exploiting these receptors enhances nanoparticle targeting to pathological sites or specific cell types, promoting cellular internalization, improving therapeutic efficacy, and minimizing off-target effects [20, 39].

Clinical studies implicate Galectin-3 in CVD pathogenesis, including atherosclerosis, acute coronary syndrome, and myocardial infarction [52–55], supporting its role as an independent biomarker for coronary artery disease [56]. Consequently, the Galectin-3 inhibitor (i.e., TD139) was developed. Preclinical and Phase II clinical trials (e.g., in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis) demonstrate TD139’s potent antiplatelet effects, reducing arterial thrombosis and myocardial ischemia/reperfusion (MI/R) injury without increasing the risk of bleeding [57, 58] (Fig. 4).

Receptor-mediated targeting extends beyond platelets. Antibody coating against extracellular epitopes of the transmembrane glycoprotein, such as platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule-1, enables the specific delivery of drugs to vascular endothelial cells [51]. Furthermore, activated platelets surface-express P-selectin, an adhesion molecule that links innate immunity, inflammation, endothelial activation, and coagulation [59, 60]. P-selectin serves as a potential molecular target for the diagnosis and treatment of CVD [36]. For example, EGCG-Mn-Fuc nanoparticles exhibit excellent hemocompatibility, anticoagulant properties, and high affinity for P-selectin, enabling specific thrombus targeting and accumulation within platelet-rich regions [61]. EGCG-Mn-Fuc nanoparticle targeting of P-selectin exemplifies the successful application of platelet membrane receptor-mediated targeting mechanisms.

Platelet membrane-derived application technology

A prominent research focus involves platelet membrane-camouflaged nanoparticles (PNP), comprising a biodegradable polymeric core (e.g., poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid), PLGA) enveloped by plasma membranes derived from human platelets [62, 63]. This biomimetic coating, achieved through membrane fusion onto nanodelivery platforms, confers enhanced immune evasion and reduced systemic clearance [12]. Hu et al. demonstrated this by fusing Human platelet membranes onto 100-nm PLGA cores to create PNPs [64].

Platelet membrane-coated nanoparticles

Nanoparticles encapsulated within platelet membranes leverage inherent cellular properties for targeted vascular therapy [22, 65] (Fig. 5A). Diverse core materials (organic polymers, inorganic compounds, synthetic/biologic molecules) [39] are cloaked by a biomimetic platelet membrane layer. This encapsulation: (1) protects therapeutic cargo (e.g., proteins, peptides, nucleic acids) from enzymatic degradation/inactivation, (2) enhances subendothelial adhesion, immune evasion, and pathogen interaction, (3) promotes pathological site homing and retention via specific molecular recognition [66].

Consequently, PNP exhibits improved targeting, superior biocompatibility, prolonged drug retention, and optimized release kinetics [39, 67]. Studies confirm PNP efficacy in atherosclerosis, stenosis, thrombosis, hemostasis, and MI/R injury [68, 69]. The PNP approach enhances biocompatibility, minimizes immune recognition/adverse effects, prolongs circulation, promotes target site accumulation, and improves therapeutic potential for vascular diseases [70].

Platelet-derived extracellular vesicles

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) facilitate intercellular communication, reflecting the identity and pathophysiological state of the parental cell [71, 72]. They transfer functional cargo to recipient cells, offering advantages including stability, retention capacity, low immunogenicity, and efficient drug delivery, thereby positioning EVs as “cell-free therapies” for cardiovascular, oncological, and immune disorders [73, 74].

Platelet-derived extracellular vesicles (PEVs), also termed platelet microvesicles or granules (100–1000 nm diameter) [68, 75, 76], express platelet glycoproteins (e.g., GPIIb/IIIa, GPIa/IIa) [77]. Circulating PEVs originate primarily from platelets via physiological in vivo and in vitro mechanisms [78–80], increasing in number upon activation, inflammation, shear stress, or apoptosis [81–83]. PEVs promote angiogenesis [73]. They preferentially accumulate in the damaged endothelium of ischemic hearts, significantly enhancing angiogenic capacity in MI/R models. PEVs show promise for hemostasis, tissue regeneration, and immune modulation. Engineering EVs with platelet membrane fusion enhances targeted therapeutic angiogenesis in MI/R [75, 84, 85]. In CVD, PEV fusion with mesenchymal stem cells may exert cardioprotection through immunomodulation and anti-inflammatory effects [86–88] (Fig. 5B). PEVs loaded with MCC950 (an NLRP3 inflammasome inhibitor) reduced atherosclerotic plaque formation and localized vascular inflammation [89–92], highlighting PEVs as biocompatible, autologous drug carriers [93]. Further research into PEV mechanisms and clinical applications offers significant therapeutic potential for CVD.

Hybrid membrane fusion technology

Fusing platelet membranes with other cellular membranes creates hybrids with novel functionalities. Platelet membrane modification of mesenchymal stem cell-derived EVs mimics platelet-monocyte binding capacity [94]. Mesenchymal stem cell-derived EVs stimulate post-MI angiogenesis via pro-angiogenic effector/miRNA delivery [87]. Mesenchymal stem cell-derived EVs hybrids show emerging potential for immunomodulation during cardiac repair [95], vascular regeneration [96], and the treatment of coronary atherosclerosis [97, 98] (Fig. 5C).

Hybrid membrane systems extend beyond EVs. Dehaini et al. pioneered red blood cell-platelet hybrid membrane-coated nanoparticles ([RBC-P]NPs), which combine the biological properties of both cell types. [RBC-P]NPs exhibit prolonged circulation and target atherosclerotic tissue in vivo [99, 100]. Yang et al. developed a red blood cell-platelet hybrid membrane-coated nanosystem (PM/RM@PLGA@P/R) encapsulating ginsenoside Rg1 and perfluorohexane. This system demonstrated ultrasound-enhanced targeted delivery, immune evasion, self-targeting for thrombosis, superior ROS scavenging, anticoagulant activity, and reduced macrophage uptake compared to uncoated controls [101]. Zhou et al. engineered nanocomplexes reversibly cloaked in platelet-macrophage hybrid membranes for efficient Sav1 siRNA delivery into cardiomyocytes, thereby inhibiting the Hippo pathway and inducing regeneration [97] (Fig. 5D).

Artificial platelet-like biomimetic technology

Artificial platelet-like biomimetic technology replicates natural platelet functions—adhesion, activation, and aggregation—through structural and functional mimicry [103]. This approach involves engineering synthetic analogs of key platelet surface receptors (e.g., GPIb, GPIIb, GPIb-β, GPIIb/IIIa) [104, 105], enabling PBN platforms to emulate these proteins’ targeting capabilities.

For instance, artificial platelet systems utilize platelet-specific affinity peptides (targeting vWF and collagen) combined with fibrinogen-mimetic peptides that bind to the activated GPIIb/IIIa receptor [106]. Such designs offer significant clinical advantages: early transfusion (within 1–3 h post-injury) improves trauma survival rates, suggesting potential for pre-hospital hemorrhage management. Being fully synthetic, these systems eliminate blood-type matching requirements (universal applicability) and extend to veterinary applications (e.g., canine/equine trauma). Furthermore, they serve as versatile platforms for co-delivering adjuvant therapeutics to enhance hemostatic efficacy [101, 106].

The hetero-multivalent surface decoration principle—pioneered in liposomal nanoparticles [107]—extends to diverse platforms, including albumin nanoparticles. Representative advances include:

Advances in cardiovascular disease based on PBN

Targeting cardiovascular inflammation via PBN

Inflammation serves as a fundamental immune defense mechanism underlying diverse cardiovascular pathophysiological processes [113]. Platelets significantly contribute to immune and inflammatory responses through membrane protein-mediated immunomodulation and adhesion [114, 115]. Consequently, PBN offers promising strategies to mitigate cardiovascular inflammation [116–118].

Key mechanisms include: (1) Receptor-Ligand Interactions: Engagement of platelet receptors (e.g., programmed death-1) modulates thrombotic inflammation [119]. The immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motif - bearing receptors C-type lectin-like receptor 2 (CLEC-2) and glycoprotein VI represent novel therapeutic targets; CLEC-2 binding podoplanin promotes venous thrombosis [120, 121]. (2) Microparticle Signaling: Platelet-derived microparticles directly activate monocytes, endothelial cells, and other immune effectors [116]. (3) Engineered Nanocarriers: Platelet-derived nanocarriers (e.g., PL720 encapsulating arginine and FTY720) enable targeted delivery to post-MI/R myocardium. Controlled release promotes macrophage polarization (M2 phenotype) and accelerates inflammatory resolution during late reperfusion [122, 123] (Fig. 7).

PBN and cardiovascular inflammatory response: Schematic representation of the PL 720 cascade targeted therapy for MI/R in mice. Use platelet-encapsulated nanoparticles to attenuate the inflammatory response process in the late phase of myocardial ischemia-reperfusion [122]. Copyright Feb 12, 2024. Advanced Science

Clinical evidence further supports this approach: Dynamic monitoring in a canine model of acute type A aortic dissection revealed the concurrent onset of platelet activation and systemic inflammation [124]. Activated platelets release proinflammatory mediators (IL-1β, IL-6, PAF, P-selectin), while TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-10 levels correlate with acute type A aortic dissection inflammatory severity [125, 126]. This temporal association establishes a “golden intervention window” for PBN-based anti-inflammatory strategies early post-event.

PBN therapeutic strategies for cardiovascular inflammation encompass three key approaches: (1) Time-window precision leverages the temporal synchrony between platelet activation and inflammatory onset (e.g., post-acute type A aortic dissection surgery) to administer antiplatelet nanotherapeutics within defined intervention windows (e.g., ≤ 2 h), maximizing therapeutic efficacy [124]. (2) Multi-target convergence integrates complementary mechanisms: (i) CLEC-2 inhibitors disrupt thrombo-inflammatory cascades [120, 121], (ii) PL720 nanocarriers deliver arginine/FTY720 to reprogram macrophages and accelerate inflammation resolution [122], and (iii) anti-CXCR2 [RBC-P]NPs suppress p38α (Mapk14)-mediated M1 polarization to halt atherosclerotic plaque progression [99, 100]. (3) Translational expansion exploits PBN’s modular design for inflammation modulation beyond cardiology, including oncology, neuroinflammatory disorders, and autoimmune conditions [76, 127, 128].

PBN strategies for coronary atherosclerosis

Atherosclerosis, fundamentally an inflammatory disease [129], involves platelet activation as a key driver of plaque formation and progression [130, 131]. Inflammatory mediators (e.g., hs-CRP, IL-6) predict atherosclerotic severity and cardiovascular events [132–134]. Activated platelets contribute through multiple mechanisms: (1)Early Plaque Initiation: glycoprotein Ibα binding to vWF on damaged endothelium facilitates platelet entry into nascent plaques [135], augmented by GPIa-IIa interactions [136, 137]. (2) Leukocyte Recruitment: platelet surface antigens (e.g., PSGL-1) bind lymphocyte function-associated antigen-1 (LFA-1), promoting leukocyte-endothelial adhesion under high shear stress [20, 138]. These mechanisms establish platelets as critical therapeutic targets. PBN leverages its inherent plaque-targeting capabilities, biocompatibility, and sustained drug retention for atherosclerosis intervention [35].

PBN therapeutic platforms for atherosclerosis encompass three strategic approaches: (1) Bioinspired targeted delivery systems leverage platelet membrane interactions for enhanced site specificity, which includes: (i) platelet membrane-camouflaged Janus aminated mesoporous silica nanoparticles that minimize premature drug leakage while improving plaque penetration and retention [139]; (ii) rapamycin-loaded PNPs (RAP-PNPs) demonstrating 4.98-fold greater plaque targeting efficiency versus non-biomimetic counterparts [140]; and (iii) platelet membrane-hybridized liposomes (P-Lipos) exploiting natural platelet-plaque binding mechanisms [140] (Fig. 8). (2)Intelligent anti-inflammatory regulation is exemplified by tannic acid-Fe(III)-curcumin nanoparticles (Curc@Lignin@TA@PL NPs), where platelet membrane functionalization enhances endothelial/macrophage interactions and significantly suppresses pro-atherogenic mediators (NF-κB, TGF-β1, IL-6, IL-1β) in inflamed vasculature [141]. (3) Stage-adaptive targeting addresses evolving plaque biology [142], with PBN’s unique hemodynamic stability enabling: (i) endothelial activation/neutrophil recruitment during early atherogenesis, (ii) foam cell/collagen fiber targeting in progressive plaques, and (iii) thrombus/necrotic core engagement in unstable lesions [99].

Precise ligand selection for stage-specific plaque components (inflamed endothelium, inflammatory cells, proteases, thrombotic material) remains critical for optimizing PBN diagnostic/therapeutic efficacy [50, 142, 143].

PBN interventions for myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury

MI/R injury involves microthrombotic vascular occlusion and inflammatory cascades [48, 144, 145]. Platelets undergo rapid activation post-reperfusion, accumulating in the ischemic myocardium, where their density correlates with infarct size [146, 147]. While early studies emphasized the detrimental effects of platelet infiltration, emerging evidence suggests that contextually beneficial platelet aggregation may facilitate repair [45]. Critically, activated platelets serve as an ideal vector for redirecting regenerative cells to ischemic tissue [45].

Activated platelets release mediators—serotonin, thromboxane A₂, adenine nucleotides, stromal-derived factor-1α, and transforming growth factor-β—that mitigate cardiomyocyte death during ischemia [148–150]. A fusion protein called Targ-CD39 binds specifically to activated GP IIb/IIIa to activate platelets [48]. The therapeutic application of the PBN mechanism in MI/R involves three key strategies: (1) Targeted enzymatic therapy employing the Targ-CD39 fusion protein, which binds activated GPIIb/IIIa to enhance capillary density in infarcted myocardium while concurrently preventing thrombosis and vascular occlusion [45, 46, 48]. (2) Inflammatory reprogramming via mesoporous silica nanoparticles loaded with anti-inflammatory miR-21, which target circulating platelet-monocyte aggregates post-MI/R—membrane fusion enables direct monocyte delivery, reprogramming inflammatory macrophages toward reparative phenotypes [108]. and (3) Dual-action therapeutics exemplified by Targ-TAP (scFvTarg-tick anticoagulant peptide fusion), demonstrating synergistic antiplatelet/anticoagulant activity that reduces infarct size and preserves cardiac function in vivo [151–153].

Collectively, these advances underscore how PBN is propelling MI/R treatment into an era of “precision damage control and repair”. This paradigm shift operates through: (i) dual-function targeting (simultaneous antithrombotic action and regenerative promotion), (ii) spatiotemporal inflammatory reprogramming (controlled macrophage polarization), and (iii) synergistic regenerative factor delivery (orchestrated release of cardioprotective mediators) [62, 94](Fig. 9).

Thrombus-Targeted PBN strategies

Platelets orchestrate hemostasis through rapid clot stabilization via thrombosis cascades, positioning them as pivotal targets in thrombosis therapy [154]. Biomimetic approaches include: (1) Platelet membrane-enveloped nanoparticles (PMENs) loaded with earthworm kinase enhance thrombolytic efficacy while reducing bleeding risk. In murine carotid thrombosis models, PMENs demonstrate superior thrombus affinity versus free lumbrokinase, overcoming its short half-life and enabling low-dose efficacy [65]. (2) Platelet membrane-stealth nanotubes functionalized with the arginine-glycine-aspartic acid (RGD) peptides exhibit exceptional clot penetration. These nanotubes prolong the half-life of urokinase, achieving 60% thrombolysis in FeCl₃-induced thrombosis models with minimal hemorrhage [154]. (3) Thrombus-responsive biomimetic nanoparticles (PTPN) co-deliver tissue plasminogen activator, phenylboronic acid, and protocatechualdehyde. Upon thrombus arrival, PTPN disintegration releases protocatechualdehyde to scavenge reperfusion-induced ROS, protecting cardiomyocyte mitochondria [155] (Fig. 10).

PBN and Thrombus: Schematic illustration of PTPN preparation and rescue of ischemia and reperfusion injury [155]. Copyright 13 December 2022, Advanced Science

For venous thrombosis, neutrophil-targeted nanoparticles exploit α1-antitrypsin-derived peptides to specifically bind activated neutrophils/platelet-neutrophil aggregates, attenuating inflammation-driven thrombosis [156]. Similarly, engineered platelet nanoparticles (AMSNP@PMrH/A) deliver recombinant hirudin/apixaban for the treatment of deep vein thrombosis, with P-selectin-mediated targeting validated via multimodal imaging (ICP-MS/FI/MALDI-MSI) [157].

The cardinal challenge—balancing thrombolysis against bleeding risks—is addressed through: (1) Arterial thrombosis: Optimized GPIIb/IIIa targeting (RGD density tuning) for platelet-rich thrombi; (2) Venous thrombosis: Enhanced fibrin affinity (cysteine-arginine-glutamic acid-lysine-alanine peptide modification) for fibrin-dominant clots. This thrombus heterogeneity-responsive design enables spatiotemporal drug release with multi-organ protection, charting future directions toward integrated thrombosis monitoring-thrombolysis-repair systems.

PBN strategies for vascular restenosis

Vascular restenosis critically impacts angioplasty prognosis, with inflammation driving its pathogenesis. While interleukin-10 (IL-10) nanoparticles modulate local inflammation, their targeting efficacy requires optimization [62]. To address this, glycosylation-engineered platelet membrane nanoparticles (IL10-GE-PNPs) were developed through sialic acid removal and α(1,2)/α(1,3)-fucose incorporation into platelet membrane glycoproteins [158]. In vivo carotid injury models demonstrated IL10-GE-PNPs’ dual functionality: (1) Enhanced vascular injury targeting with reduced hepatic/splenic sequestration versus non-glycosylated counterparts. (2) Potent immunomodulation via macrophage polarization and suppression of smooth muscle cell proliferation/migration. (3) Promotion of endothelial repair in inflammatory microenvironments. These mechanisms collectively inhibited neointimal hyperplasia, establishing IL10-GE-PNPs as promising candidates for targeted anti-restenosis therapy and innovative platforms for PBNs in vascular stenotic diseases.

PBN applications in hemorrhagic disorders

While endogenous/exogenous coagulation critically involves platelets [159], clinical platelet transfusion for acute hemorrhage, thrombocytopenia, and surgical management faces significant limitations: supply constraints, portability issues, bacterial contamination risks, specialized storage requirements (≤ 5-day shelf life), and functional degradation during cryopreservation/lyophilization [160–166]. To address these challenges, PBN offers targeted hemostatic alternatives [167, 168]:

Procoagulant Nanoparticles : Liposome-based systems mimicking platelet phosphatidylserine exposure enhance fibrin stability and clot robustness under fibrinolytic conditions. In thrombocytopenic hemorrhage models, procoagulant nanoparticles reduced bleeding and improved survival comparably to platelet transfusion [169].

Emerging evidence suggests broader applications: Platelet-rich plasma improves post-MI ventricular function via antioxidant delivery (Catalase/Superoxide Dismutase) [144], while PBNs may mitigate antiplatelet-aggregation drug-induced bleeding [176]. Collectively, these advances demonstrate PBN’s unique capacity for precision hemostatic modulation across traumatic, neurovascular, and immune-mediated bleeding disorders through biomimetic coagulation potentiation, ROS-mediated neuroprotection, and pathological antibody neutralization [128, 171].

Diagnostic applications of PBN

Advances in molecular imaging nanotechnology enhance contrast agent stability and lesion-specific targeting, improving contrast resolution while reducing off-target accumulation [177]. PBN enables the molecular/cellular-level characterization of atherosclerotic plaques – detecting necrotic cores, intraplaque hemorrhage, fibrous cap integrity, inflammation, and microcalcifications – facilitating early atherosclerosis detection and progression monitoring [178]. Integrated with multimodal imaging (MRI, ultrasound, CT, and optical), PBN platforms demonstrate unique diagnostic superiority for thrombotic and atherosclerotic events [179].

Representative systems include: (1) Multifunctional platelet nanoparticles for targeted molecular imaging and thrombolysis of coronary microthrombi [180]. (2) Platelet membrane-coated porous PLGA particles enabling early detection of myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury, exhibiting significantly enhanced adhesion to human umbilical vein endothelial cells and collagen IV versus erythrocyte-mimetic controls (p < 0.01) [63]. These platforms validate PBN’s exceptional potential for cardiovascular diagnostics through pathological site-specific contrast enhancement, cellular-level resolution, and real-time tracking of progression.

The studies, as mentioned above, suggest that PBN may play a more significant role in the treatment of cardiovascular diseases, facilitating the assessment of disease onset, progression, and prognosis. The convergence of receptor-mediated targeting, membrane-derived carriers, and artificial platelet systems heralds the emergence of next-generation theranostic platforms capable of comprehensive disease assessment from onset through prognosis.

Discussion and future perspectives

This review synthesizes advancements in PBN across three primary categories—platelet receptor-mediated technology, platelet membrane-derived application technology, and artificial platelet-like biomimetic technology—highlighting their translational potential for CVD [181]. While PBN offers promising therapeutic strategies, critical challenges require resolution prior to clinical implementation.

Comparative analysis and translational barriers

A systematic comparison of PBN platforms (Table 2) reveals an incomplete understanding of nanoparticle bioactivity mechanisms, necessitating the elucidation of precise modes of action and therapeutic optimization [182]. Clinical translation faces three key barriers: (1) Limited validation in higher mammalian models beyond rodents; (2) Insufficient large-scale efficacy and biosafety data; (3) Unresolved long-term biocompatibility and pharmacokinetic profiles. These knowledge gaps necessitate expanded Phase II clinical trials and rigorous toxicological assessments, despite the associated cost/time burdens [183].

Multiparametric safety assessment

Comprehensive PBN evaluation requires integrated analysis of [95, 184, 185]: (1) Hemocompatibility: Prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time, fibrinogen, platelet aggregation assays [38]. (2) Immunogenicity: Serum IL-6, TNF-α, IgM/IgG quantification. (3) Organotoxicity: ALT/AST (liver), creatinine/urea (kidney), histopathological staining [186]. Exemplifying this approach, the platelet-like particles platform (e.g., PLP-RvD1)—a ROS-responsive liposome wrapped with platelet membranes—demonstrates rigorous drug loading/release kinetics alongside coagulation and immune safety profiling [184]. Such multifactorial assessment is essential given PBNs’ inherent thrombotic/hemorrhagic risk profiles.

Membrane protein bottlenecks

PBN development remains constrained by platelet membrane protein complexities [187] : (1)Structural Limitations: Only 3,880 membrane protein structures are resolved at atomic resolution, impeding rational drug design [188, 189]. Critical knowledge gaps persist regarding: (i) protein-decorated membrane biophysics (fluctuation spectra, bending stiffness); (ii) tissue-specific protein interactions [187, 190]; (iii) membrane tension effects on protein conformation/function [191–193]. (2) Expression Challenges: Low heterologous yield, solution instability, and aggregation hinder purification [194]. Emerging techniques, such as fluorescence detection and size exclusion chromatography, offer functional analysis beyond structural studies, enabling the characterization of oligomeric states and ligand screening [194].

Despite these hurdles, PBN represents a transformative platform for CVD management [13, 195–197]. Future efforts should prioritize: resolving membrane protein structure-function relationships, standardizing multiparametric safety assessment, and advancing combinatorial PBN designs. These developments will accelerate clinical translation of precision cardiovascular nanomedicine.
