A review of the clinical efficacy and adverse effects of disease modifying osteoarthritic drugs (DMOADs) in dogs with special focus on glucosamine and pentosan polysulfate; do they work?
Authors: Eugene C BWALYA, HM Suranji WIJEKOON, Masahiro OKUMURA
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Osteoarthritis (OA) or degenerative joint disease (DJD) is a degenerative joint disease that progressively causes loss of joint function and is the most common and costly form of arthritis. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are currently one of therapeutic treatment options for OA. Because NSAIDs do not alter the underlying pathophysiological process on the structural degradation of joint tissue but merely control signs of pain and inflammation, recent developments have allowed the use of treatments termed disease modifying osteoarthritic drugs (DMOADs) aimed at targeting the pathophysiologic processes of OA with the view of preventing, retarding progression of, or reversing morphologic changes associated with OA. Several in vitro and experimental studies have reported potential efficacy of glucosamine (GlcN) and pentosan polysulfate (PPS) as novel therapeutic agents of OA. This review will evaluate the clinical efficacy and safety of DMOADs with particular focus on GlcN and PPS based on the prescribed outcome measures by European Medicines Agency.

INTRODUCTION

Osteoarthritis (OA) or degenerative joint disease (DJD) is a degenerative joint disease that progressively causes loss of joint function [29, 57] and is the most common and costly form of arthritis [24]. In dogs, OA/DJD may be classified as primary or secondary depending on the cause. Primary OA is a disorder of aging in which cartilage degeneration occurs for unknown reasons. Secondary OA which is more common in dogs, occurs in response to abnormalities that cause joint instability, abnormal loading of articular cartilage or in response to other recognizable joint disease [18]. Cranial cruciate ligament (CCL) rupture and hip dysplasia are some of the common causes of OA/DJD in dogs due to joint instability and developmental abnormalities respectively. The disease does not only affect articular cartilage but also involves the entire joint including the subchondral bone, ligaments, capsule, synovial membrane and menisci [24, 32]. The mechanisms responsible for OA progression are very complex and poorly understood [4]. OA/DJD occurs when the equilibrium between breakdown and repair of the joint tissues becomes unbalanced. This imbalance is driven by cytokine cascades and the production of inflammatory mediators. OA chondrocytes and synoviocytes in affected joints produce increased levels of inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), which in turn decrease anabolic collagen synthesis and increase catabolic (including matrix metalloproteinases or MMPs) and other inflammatory mediators such as IL-6, IL-8, prostaglandin E2 and nitric oxide (NO) [29, 40].

Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) as well as intraarticular injections of steroids and hyaluronans are the current therapeutic treatment options for OA. However, NSAIDs do not alter the underlying pathophysiological process on the structural degradation of joint tissue but merely control signs of pain and inflammation [29, 47, 57]. Recent developments have allowed the use of treatments termed disease modifying osteoarthritic drugs (DMOADs) aimed at targeting the pathophysiologic processes of OA with the view of preventing, retarding progression of, or reversing morphologic changes associated with OA [13, 44,45,46]. These drugs include; polysulfated glycosaminoglycan (PSGAG), glucosamine (GlcN), pentosan polysulfated (PPS), diacerein, doxycycline, hyaluronan etc. and are available in certain parts of the world in oral and injectable forms. Despite their discovery, currently, there is no single treatment that when administered alone is of much benefit and capable of restoring the original structure and function of damaged cartilage and other synovial tissue [13, 46, 50].

The aim of the review was to evaluate the clinical efficacy and safety of GlcN and PPS in the treatment of canine OA/DJD based on the prescribed outcome endpoint criteria by European Medicines Agency (EMA).

METHODOLOGY

Study design

A review study was undertaken to evaluate the clinical efficacy of GlcN and PPS in dogs based on clinical trials and experimental studies conducted from 2000 to 2013. A systematic thorough literature search was carried out based on a Medline (via PubMed) search using the following key words or combined search method; ((Glucosamine OR GlcN OR “Pentosan Polysulfate” OR PPS) AND (osteoarthritis OR OA OR “degenerative joint disease”) AND (dog* OR canine) AND (efficacy OR safety OR treatment OR therapy OR “disease-modifying osteoarthritis drugs” OR DMOADs*)) AND (“Randomized Controlled Trial”[Publication Type] OR randomized OR “double blind” OR “parallel group” OR clinical trial) AND (“2000/01/01”[Date−Publication]: “2013/12/31”[Date−Publication]).

Only published studies that matched the search criteria were selected and entered in the Microsoft Excel® spreadsheet (Microsoft, Redmond, Washington DC, USA).

EMA definition and methods of assessing efficacy of symptomatic and structure modifying osteoarthritic drugs were used to evaluate clinical efficacy of these agents [22]. For the purpose of this review, DMOADs refers to PPS, GlcN, PSGAG, chondroitin sulfate (Cs), doxycycline and strontium ranalate; study focus means studies that either evaluated DMOADs or NSAIDs; whereas type of study means clinical trial or experimental study.

Data entry and analysis

The data was coded and summarized using Excel® Spreadsheet and SPSS 16.0 to generate descriptive statistics (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

RESULTS

Only 12 articles published from 2000 to 2013 qualified as RDBPD. However, only eight (8/12) articles evaluated the efficacy of DMOADs with six (6/8) being clinical trials and two (2/8) experimental studies. The mean sample size from the eight studies was 32 dogs (minimum=14, maximum=71) and the mean study duration was 147 days (SD ± 139, Minimum=30, Maximum=378).

PSGAG was shown to improve functional disability in dogs (1/1). GlcN was reported to improve pain and functional disability in one study (1/2) while one study (1/2) did not report any improvement with regard to pain or functional disability. No study (0/2) involving PPS reported significant difference in improvement of pain or functional disability and slowing or preventing of structure damages associated with OA between dogs on placebo and in the treatment group. However, one (1/2) study observed that the group on PPS appeared to have significant improvement in pain and functional disability when the dogs were grouped based on radiographic changes. Doxycycline (1/1) reported improvement in functional disability and to some degree slowed or prevented further structural damage to articular cartilage while strontium ranalate (1/1) reduced the progression of OA structural changes, both cartilage lesions and subchondral bone sclerosis (Table 1). Only one study (1/8) reported adverse effects and all of the studies (8/8) expressed hope at the prospect of developing these agents as potential novel therapeutic agents for the treatment of canine OA.

DISCUSSION

There is paucity of randomized placebo controlled clinical trials and this presents one of the challenges in evaluating the efficacy of most DMOADs besides other challenges which have already been reported by other researchers in the development of these agents [5, 33, 55]. Randomized placebo controlled clinical studies present numerous challenges in the dog population especially in trying to achieve homogeneity and later on control of the studies. The inclusion criteria targeted to specific joints has presented challenges and finding dogs of a particular age range with the specified conditions is also difficult. Compared with observational studies, randomization is considered an effective method to balance confounding factors between treatment groups and helps to eliminate the influence of confounding variables. To obtain significant and reliable results for these types of studies, it is very important to consider the sample size in the design [61]. The mean sample size of 32 (range of 14–71) observed from the eight (8) randomized studies equally depicts the challenges and limits the reliability power of these studies in view of available literature and what is prescribed by Regulatory Agencies for a comprehensive clinical trial designed to evaluate the efficacy of various DMOADs categories. It is possible that most of these studies may have failed to answer their research questions on the efficacy of the various DMOADs because of the limited sample sizes used in the studies. Generally, studies with a small sample size tend to have large confidence intervals and only show up as statistically abnormal if there is a large difference between two treatment groups being compared [27]. Sample size calculation, which was observed to be lacking in most of the studies reviewed, is considered a key component of clinical trials and should be part of an assessment since the emphasis in most of these studies is in finding the magnitude of difference between therapies [27, 60]. The mean duration of study was 147 (range of 30–378) days. We agree with observations made by McCarthy et al. [34] who recommended that future clinical trials be monitored for a minimum period of 70 days after demonstrating that clinical signs of OA in dogs only improved significantly after 70 days of oral treatment with Glu/Cs (Synoquin SA®, VetPlus Ltd., Lytham, Lancashire, England-Each gram of the Glu/Cs formulation contained the following active ingredients; GlcN HCL 475 mg/g, CS 350 mg/g, N-acetyl-D-GlcN 50 mg/g, ascorbic acid 50 mg/g and 30 mg/g of Zn sulfate). Similarly, a study of 180 days’ duration by Nganvongpanit et al. [41] echoes the importance of long term studies when they previously demonstrated that dogs treated with doxycycline showed statistically significant improvements in lameness, joint mobility, pain on palpation, weight-bearing and overall score at 2, 6, 4, 4 and 4 months, respectively, after treatment. Furthermore, unlike the fast acting symptomatic NSAIDs, most DMOADs are slow acting agents that require more time for their effect to be observed. Interestingly, research by Moreau et al. [38] with study duration of 60 days reported no significant improvement in the clinical signs in dogs that were treated orally with nutraceuticals (Each capsule contained 500 mg of glucosamine hydrochloride, 400 mg of chondroitin sulphate and 75 mg of manganese ascorbate). This observation further emphasizes the importance of a longer duration of therapy with the objective of ascertaining efficacy especially of slow acting DMOADs. The slow acting mechanism of DMOADs requires a much longer duration of treatment and observation period for any meaningful effect size to be observed. This observation has already been recognized in human clinical trials [1, 42].

The study by Fujiki et al. [19] reported improvement in functional disability in dogs following treatment with PSGAG administered IM twice a week for a total of 8 treatments and this observation is generally in agreement with other findings in human [3, 26, 43] and equine [9, 58]. PSGAG has been reported to improve pain and functional disability of a chemically induced carpitis in horses [58] and its use is regarded as moderately effective and most useful in the treatment of subacute degenerative joint disease [9]. In humans, IA injections of 50 mg PSGAG have been reported to be useful for OA of the knee although the clinical symptoms improved only gradually following the injections [3, 26, 43]. Some studies however, have failed to show the usefulness of administration of PSGAG for OA of the knee joint [10, 28]. These observations with PSGAG have to some extent demonstrated its efficacy as a slow acting symptomatic OA drug although more randomized placebo controlled long term clinical trials are required to validate the reliability of these findings including its long term safety.

McCarthy et al. [34] demonstrated statistically significant improvements in pain and functional disability with orally administered Glu/Cs in dogs with hips or elbows OA. Furthermore, a controlled randomized experimentally-induced OA study by Ajadi et al. [2] showed that dogs treated with oral GlcN HCL (Glycoflex®, Vetri- Science Laboratories, Williston, ND, USA) daily for four weeks following OA had significantly lower plasma IL-6 and MMP-3 up to week 4 of treatment when compared with the control group. Because efficacy of GlcN in the latter was based on changes in plasma IL-6 and MMP-3, the study does not provide clinical evidence as prescribed by EMA. Moreover, there are no recognized biomarkers currently with any correlation with OA disease progression [12]. On the contrary, Moreau et al. [38] reported that orally administered CS-G-M (CosequinDS®; Nutramax Laboratories, Lancaster, SC, USA-Each capsule contained 500 mg of GlcN hydrochloride, 400 mg of CS and 75 mg of manganese ascorbate) had no significant improvement in pain and functional disability in dogs with elbows, stifles or hips OA. These studies give an indication that the direction in the evaluation of GlcN as DMOADs are streamlined to its potential symptomatic effects and not structure modifying. This and of course the anticipated high cost of carrying out such clinical trials for not less than two years, could probably be the main reason why almost all clinical trials involving this agent in dogs have focused on evaluating its symptomatic effects other than the structure effects. The current studies may actually be misguided because symptomatic or pain relief which is achieved with NSAIDs is not really the primary target of this agent but rather as a concomitant outcome of its structure modifying effects not achievable in short term studies. GlcN mechanism of action although not very well elucidated, has been shown to regulate gene expression and synthesis of nitric oxide (NO) and PGE2 [11], prevent experimentally induced cartilage degradation in vitro [17], inhibit inducible NO synthesis via inhibition of iNOS protein expression [36] and has also been shown to alter TGF signaling which explains its anabolic effect on chondrocytes [56]. In addition, it has been shown to attenuate the development of OA, concomitantly reduce nociception and modulate chondrocyte metabolism possibly through inhibition of cell p38 and JNK and increase of ERK expression [59]. Furthermore, GlcN has been shown to be partly involved in the direct scavenging of cellular radical species and alteration of oxidation mediated destructive events of OA [37] and is highly effective in preventing IL-1β–mediated suppressive effects and prevents the production of inflammatory mediators induced by the cytokine [21]. These mechanisms could fairly account for a possible anti-inflammatory beneficial effect of GlcN on OA articular cartilage. But as stated, improvement in pain is clearly not the primary effect but occurs as a concomitant outcome associated with the slight tilt in the OA balance which favors the anabolic process and reduces on the catabolic cartilage degradation. Other researchers have equally argued that as long as the adverse mechanical environment that perpetuates OA exists in a joint, it is unlikely that a pharmacological or biological agent like GlcN that inhibits a specific enzyme or cytokine in the pathways of cartilage degradation or further stimulates the already increased synthesis of cartilage matrix molecules by chondrocytes will be effective in pain improvement [6, 12]. This observation is also supported by the fact that long term clinical trials (three to five years) in humans have previously demonstrated combined structure-modifying and symptom-modifying effects of GlcN [42, 49] whereas a study of two years’ duration failed to demonstrate similar effects [52].

None of the studies involving PPS reported significant difference in improvement of pain or functional disability and slowing or preventing of structure damages associated with OA in the treatment group compared to those on placebo. Budsberg et al. [8] and Innes et al. [25] did not report any significant difference in the change in radiographic score and lameness scores between the PPS and placebo groups. Budsberg et al. [8] however, observed that when the dogs were grouped and evaluated only by initial radiographic score, PPS-treated dogs improved significantly faster in braking GRFs than placebo-treated dogs. These findings are similar to what Read et al. [48] previously reported in dogs with chronic OA and McIIwraith et al. [35] reported recently in horses with induced OA. A recent clinical trial in humans that assessed the efficacy and safety of PPS in mild radiographic knee OA and OA-associated symptoms found that PPS was able to quickly reduce hydrarthroses in all cases and the range of motion (ROM) of the knee joint improved significantly with the clinical benefits continuing for almost one year [30]. The radiographic findings however, detected neither improvement nor degeneration when compared before the treatment and end of the study and this is almost similar to previous findings in dogs [8, 25] and horses [35]. In some dogs, OA grading deteriorated in both the treatment and placebo group whereas in the horses, significantly higher enthesophyte and lysis scores in OA versus non-OA limbs (both groups combined) were observed, although there was no significant effect of treatment on radiographic scores between treated and control groups. The mechanism of action of PPS has also not been fully elucidated although its beneficial effects are strongly attributed to its properties. These include; prevention of inflammatory intracellular responses induced by IL-1 β through inhibition of phosphorylation of certain MAPKs, p38 and ERK [54], preservation of cartilage integrity by supporting chondrocyte anabolic activities, including biosynthesis of aggrecan and suppression of catabolic MMPs [14, 16, 20, 39, 51]. However, radiographic evaluation results in clinical trials for PPS as a potential structure modifying DMOADs have been disappointing.

Measurement of JSW in humans’ knee have been recommended as being reliable and may be useful when evaluating the change in minimum JSW as outcome of interest [54]. But as observed by other researchers [7, 15], because of the complexity of OA and the insensitivity of JSN to early changes in cartilage and meniscus, the relevance of JSW alone as an outcome measure may not be of significance in the absence of defined clinical relevance. The EMA recommends that such agents must also show a trend in improvement of symptoms and/or a correlation between structural outcome and pain/function evolution to support the surrogacy value of radiographic changes. However, this type of outcome in dogs may be a limitation currently because of the paucity of reference clinical trials data. For future clinical trials, it would be prudent to consider JSW measurement including nature and magnitude of structural changes especially for stifle OA as a parallel clinical outcome conducted over two years to effectively evaluate PPS.

On the other hand, pain intensity characterization is an important outcome that is measured by self-assessment with validated methods such as the visual analogue (VAS) or numerical rating scales. A number of studies currently use the SLS method compared to the GFR plate method and this is evident from the few studies we reviewed. However, SLS methods have in the past received criticism about their reliability especially when evaluating symptomatic slow acting DMOADs and the fact that most are modifications from short-term trials focusing on the efficacy of OA treatment on symptoms especially those of NSAIDs. The use of force plate analysis has shown to be sufficiently repeatable, reproducible and appropriate for the quantitative evaluation of lameness in dogs and horses. Therefore, a comprehensive objective assessment of clinical signs, using both SLS sheet and force plate gait analysis should be considered in future clinical trials in order to objectively elucidate the efficacy of most of the DMOADs.

Almost all the DMOADs reviewed appeared to be relatively safe with very low incidence of adverse effects. Only McCarthy et al. [34] reported adverse effects in two (2) cases with Glu/Cs which presented as gastrointestinal upsets. Adverse effects associated with gastrointestinal system have been reported in long term clinical trials in human using only GlcN sulfate besides other side effects [42, 49]. Similarly, Cs alone or that combined with GlcN sulfate has also been reported to cause very mild to transient GIT related effects [31, 47] although recent studies reported no adverse effects associated with Cs alone [60]. In humans, these adverse effects related to GlcN sulfate or Glu/Cs have also been reported in people on placebo and most of them were transient and of mild to moderate severity [31, 42, 47, 49]. Furthermore, PPS which is traded as Cartrophen Vet® (Biopharm, New South Wales, Australia) has been found to have a low incidence of side effects. This safety profile for PPS has a history of over 20 years of safe use in the dog [23, 53]. However, this safety observation for Cartrophen Vet® has limitations regarding the reliability of the reported findings because the studies were conducted by the authors who have a direct interest in the company that manufactured the product and as such biasness of the results cannot be overlooked. Although, a few studies currently report safety of PPS, we recommend that more long term comprehensive clinical trials without the direct involvement of the manufacturers of these products are conducted that will effectively evaluate its clinical efficacy and safety.

This review looked at the current clinical trials and experimental studies on DMOADs in dogs with special focus on PPS and GlcN using outcome measures as prescribed by EMA. Numerous preclinical and review studies had previously reported GlcN and PPS as potential therapeutic agents for OA treatment [13, 17, 21, 33, 37, 55]. However, based on our observations from these few studies, both GlcN and PPS have shown an inconsistent efficacy in decreasing pain, improving joint function and retarding the progression of OA although they demonstrate excellent safety profile. The current evidence is relatively weak to suggest a therapeutic value, which may imply that these agents are not effective to combat OA when used alone and require to be combined with other interventions. Because OA is multifactorial with both mechanical as well as biochemical factors driving the process, probably the research approach should also be multimodal. Although the prospect of DMOADs development as potential novel OA therapeutic agents appears to be heading in the positive direction, an approach targeted at the whole joint components involved in OA appears to be more realistic and objective bearing in mind the complexity of the condition. Furthermore, because of the limitations of inferential statistical analyses to convey information on the size of an observed effect when comparing two treatment groups, we recommend that effect sizes (with confidence intervals) be reported in all future randomized placebo controlled clinical trials evaluating the efficacy of DMOADs. This will be a useful adjunct to the traditional hypothesis-testing using inferential statistics and will help to determine how these potential therapeutic OA agents compare in relative terms to the placebo treated group for OA.
