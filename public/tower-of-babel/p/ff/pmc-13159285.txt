Time use patterns and activity sequences among older adults in India: socio-demographic correlates from time use survey
Authors: Robin Raj, Dipti Govil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study investigated elderly individuals’ time use and its association with socio-economic and demographic factors. The study relies on data from the 2019 Time Use Survey conducted by the Indian National Statistical Office, encompassing 52,414 individuals aged 60 and above.MethodsTime use was classified into nine categories and recombined as leisure, maintenance and subsistence activities. Descriptive statistics and ordinary least squares (OLS) were used to address the objective.ResultsThe findings revealed that elderly people in India primarily allocate their time to activities such as sleep (10 h), self-care, social participation, and passive leisure. Furthermore, a significant gender gap in time use among the elderly is observed, with females spending less time on leisure and subsistence activities but more time on maintenance activities than males. Urban elderly allocate more time to leisure activities and less time to maintenance activities than their rural counterparts, although differences in subsistence time are not significant. Higher educational attainment is associated with increased leisure and reduced subsistence time, while maintenance time shows no consistent educational gradient. Similarly, higher economic status is correlated with greater leisure and lower maintenance time. Household size is positively associated with leisure and negatively with maintenance, but shows no clear relationship with subsistence. Elderly residing in pucca dwellings spend significantly more time on leisure and less time on both maintenance and subsistence than those living in kutcha dwellings.ConclusionBy shedding light on the time allocation patterns of older adults, this research enhances our understanding of the factors influencing their daily routines. These insights have several implications for ageing-related policy in India. The positive association between education and leisure time suggests that expanding access to adult education and digital literacy programs may promote social participation and help reduce isolation among older adults. In addition to that, income security and housing improvement schemes may enable older adults to engage more in leisure and social activities, thereby enhancing their overall well-being and quality of life.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27169-8.

Background

Demographic context

The global population is not only increasing but also ageing, with a notable increase in both the proportion and number of elderly individuals. As of 2022, the United Nations reports a global count of 1.1 billion people aged 60 and above, constituting around 14% of the total global population [1] . India is also undergoing a similar demographic transformation comprising 104 million people aged 60 years and above, making up 8.6% of the overall population [2]. A significant surge in the elderly population is evident, coupled with a decline in the number of people aged younger than 15 years, indicating a swift ageing process in India. By 2050, the proportion of the elderly is predicted to jump from 10.5% in 2022 to 20.8%, and by the end of the century, the elderly will make up more than one-third of the nation's population [3].

Ageing and well-being

Old age is a phase of several challenges related to both the physical and mental well-being of individuals. The demographic shift toward an ageing population underscores the prevalence of chronic illnesses, cognitive deterioration, and limited access to essential healthcare services. Additionally, elderly people often struggle to adapt to rapid digital advancements, which exacerbates feelings of social isolation and mental health concerns. Sedentary habits and insufficient nutrition further escalate these difficulties, increasing susceptibility to conditions such as obesity and cardiovascular diseases and consequently impacting overall physical health. Understanding the lifestyle of elderly people is crucial in this context. One method of analysis is through examining the time use of elderly.

The way in which elderly people live their lives affects their well-being. The main factor contributing to elderly people’s physical health and mental state vulnerabilities is their daily schedule. The phase of old age in the life of an individual involves many modifications and transformations. Individuals' physical, social, and emotional identities change as they age, creating challenges for them to carry out previous duties and responsibilities, work, and interact with others, among other things [4]. All these transformations are reflected by the manner in which they use their time.

Time use as analytical lens

Time use describes the order, duration and characteristics of various types of daily activities. It describes the pattern and sequence of activities performed by individuals in a day. It tells how an individual utilises their time in a day. Research on time use was developed in the late 1920 s to highlight the economic value of women’s homemaking activities [5]. It primarily originated in developed countries and gradually expanded in developing nations in the 1990s.

Time Use Studies serve various purposes, from measuring and understanding informal work to aiding in employment planning and assessing child labor. Recently, time use statistics have been instrumental in formulating effective macroeconomic policies and evaluating their impact on the unpaid work of both men and women [6, 7]. Studies on the use of time capture facets of human existence that are hard to quantify in terms of money or other resources [8]. Good health outcomes and bad health consequences are determined by the chronology, duration, and features of the many daily activities (such as paid labor, physical activity, eating, and sleeping), as well as by their intensity and context [5].

According to the retirement and unemployment literature, it becomes difficult for unemployed elderly to maintain a routine and fill their days with different activities which may lead to negative outcomes such as depression and loss of meaning [9, 10]. Retirement from the workforce frees up a significant amount of time of individuals in a day, which is then usually spent on passive activities [11]. It has been observed that having too much free time can also be harmful to well-being, particularly as people age. The amount of life pleasure in old age is negatively correlated with having limited confined time and excessive discretionary time in relative terms [12]. Old age, characterized by frequent life changes, involves shifts in time use patterns and transitions from subsistence activities to leisure, social participation, or self-maintenance. Despite retirement, some seniors continue working, driven by economic considerations or personal preferences.

Leisure and social participation

Leisure plays a significant impact in the process of ageing. It provides an environment for older adults to create purpose in life, adjust to age-related changes, and enhance or preserve physical, social, mental, and emotional well-being [13]. Participating in physically active leisure activities can lower the risk of all-cause mortality, falls, and chronic illnesses (such as diabetes and osteoporosis). Additionally, engaging in physically active leisure gives older adults a place to experience self-renewal and maintenance [13].

Older adults’ participation in their communities makes them healthier and more active than those who do not [13]. Age-related difficulties such as isolation and loneliness may be addressed by elderly people actively participating in activities that advance social production and social worth [14, 15].

Objective of the study

The primary objective of this paper is to gather insights into the sequence and pattern of time use among elderly people. In this study, an activity sequence refers to the ordered succession of activities undertaken by an individual over a 24-h period. Further, this paper aims to investigate variations in the time use of elderly people based on factors such as gender, age, place of residence, educational level, and economic status. Analysing the time-use behaviours of elderly people provides us with insight into the full range of activities carried out by Indian elderly people. We can describe the lifestyle and living situations of elderly people based on the activities they engage in. This can help to determine whether the existing environment promotes active ageing. The time use pattern examines the fraction of elderly people who are dependent on others and those who are functioning as a second demographic dividend. The second demographic dividend refers to the long-term economic benefits that may arise from the ageing of the population, as individuals accumulate assets, savings, and human capital over the life course and invest these resources productively in later life.

Method

Data and sample description

This study utilizes the data from the Time Use Survey (TUS) 2019 conducted by India’s National Statistical Office (NSO). India’s TUS 2019 is the first nationally representative time use survey, with an earlier pilot survey conducted in 1998–99. It was conducted from January 2019 to December 2019, using the time diary method through personal interviews. This survey covered the whole country, excluding the villages in the Andaman and Nicobar Islands because of difficulties in accessing those areas.

The Time Use Survey (TUS) covers 1,38,799 households and collects data of individuals aged six and above. This survey mainly collected information on various activities performed by individuals on a complete day. Data on the participants' activities were gathered for a full day, starting at 4:00 a.m. the day before the interview and ending at 4:00 a.m. on the day of the interview.

The raw TUS 2019 data is publicly available on the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MoSPI) website. The raw data consists of multiple unit-level files, which we merged to create a single dataset. The key variables, mentioned in the Read-me file of TUS 2019, were identified and combined to create a common identifier, which was used to merge all the unit-level files.

The original data structure was in a long format where each individual was repeated across multiple time slots. To facilitate our analysis, the dataset was transformed from a long to a wide format, so that each row uniquely represented a single observation unit i.e., an individual.

Furthermore, the raw data recorded time slots of non-uniform duration, with 30 min being the minimum interval. To facilitate our analysis, we created a uniform daily time-use schedule of 48 discrete 30-min time slots using a loop command in Stata.

The survey observed 518,744 individuals, 71,494 of whom were children younger than six years of age and 1951 of whom had not given time use information. Thus, the time use information was collected from 4,45,299 individuals. As the paper focuses on elderly individuals, 52,414 individuals aged 60 and above were selected for the analysis.

Classification of activities

In the Time Use Survey, the activities were classified according to the International Classification of Activities for Time Use Statistics (ICATUS) 2016. In this paper, we first classified the activities into nine major categories, including Wage Earning Activities, Unpaid Activities, Household Chores, Sleep, Self-care, Care-giving, Social Participation, Active Leisure, and Passive Leisure. Furthermore, these nine activities were clubbed into three broad categories. Our classification of activities into three broad categories, i.e. Subsistence, Maintenance and Leisure, was based on Becker’s [17] household production framework and Ironmonger’s [18] third-party principle. The third-party principle distinguishes between activities that can be outsourced to a third party versus those that are non-substitutable.

Subsistence activities include wage-earning or income-generating activities comprising all the primary, secondary and tertiary activities.

In our classification, maintenance activities include both those that are necessary for the upkeep of the household and the biological maintenance of the individual. Household-related maintenance includes unpaid production for own use, household chores, and caregiving. Personal maintenance includes sleep and self-care. Becker’s [17] theory of time allocation suggests that any activity that helps sustain physical functioning and capacity to engage in other activities should be part of the production function,thus, we have considered sleep and self-care as part of the maintenance activity.

Leisure activities cover discretionary uses of time beyond income generation and household or self-maintenance. These include social participation, active leisure, and passive leisure. These activities are neither strictly necessary for survival nor generally substitutable by third-party services.

Further details on the activities can be found in the report (Report_TUS_2019_0.pdf (mospi.gov.in) (Pg42-48)).

Explanatory variables

The explanatory variables considered in the study include gender (male, female), place of residence (urban, rural), educational level (illiterate, primary, secondary, 12th and above), Monthly Per Capita Expenditure (MPCE) quintile (bottom 20%, 20–40%, 40–60%,60–80%, top 20%), kind of dwelling unit (pucca, semi-pucca, kutcha dwellings), religion (hindu, muslim, others), and social group (Scheduled Caste (SC)/Scheduled Tribe (ST), Other Backward Class (OBC), others) and age group. The elderly sample was divided into three categories according to age; the elderly individuals belonging to the 60–69, 70–79, and 80 + age groups were categorised as youngest old, older old, and oldest old, respectively.

MPCE quintiles serve as a proxy for household income, with households grouped into five categories: bottom 20%, 20–40%, 40–60%, 60–80%, and top 20%. Social group was classified into Scheduled Caste/Scheduled Tribe (SC/ST), Other Backward Class (OBC), and others, representing India’s official classification system for marginalized communities. The type of dwelling unit refers to the structural quality of the individual's house and is categorized into three types of dwellings:Pucca: walls and roof made of permanent materials such as brick, cement, or concrete.Semi-pucca: a mix of permanent and temporary materials for the walls and roof.Kutcha: walls and roof made of temporary materials such as mud, thatch, or unburnt bricks.

We also classify living arrangements into five categories: living alone, living with spouse, living with spouse and children, living with children (without spouse), and living with others. Other household-level variables include the presence of children younger than six years of age and the type of cooking fuel used (non-solid, solid, or other fuels). The use of non-solid fuels (e.g., LPG, electricity) is associated with higher income levels and a cleaner household environment, while solid fuels (e.g., wood, coal, cow dung) are often used by lower-income households and are a significant source of indoor air pollution. Thus, this variable captures an aspect of the household environment that can impact an individual’s health and well-being. The individual’s status as the head of the household was also considered.

The selection of explanatory variables in this study is guided by socio-demographic frameworks commonly used in social and health sciences to analyze quality of life and well-being among the elderly. These variables are categorized based on their ability to capture key aspects of an individual's socio-economic status, demographic profile, and living environment.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics and regression models were used to address the study’s objectives. For all 9 major activities, the mean duration of time (in minutes) spent was calculated.

This is average across the entire observed sample, regardless of whether a person participated in a specific activity. In TUS data, multiple activities within a single time slot were also recorded. Among the multiple activities, one activity was designated as major activity in the dataset, and all others were considered minor activities. The major activity was identified as the one the informant considered the most important during that slot. In our analysis, we focused exclusively on these major activities to derive the time-use patterns. This method ensures each time slot is uniquely assigned to a single activity, preventing the summation of time from exceeding 24 h and providing a clear focus for the analysis of primary time allocation.

The associations between elderly people's background characteristics and the amount of time they spend engaging in maintenance, leisure and subsistence activities were examined using three separate linear regression models. As daily time use is constrained to a fixed total of 1440 min (24 h), the outcomes are compositional, and coefficients from the separate models should be interpreted as reflecting reallocations of time across activity categories rather than independent changes.

Figure 3 maps the sequence of time use among elderly people by gender, age, place of residence, literacy level and MPCE quantile. In a complete day, a greater proportion of elderly females than elderly males were engaged in household chores, passive leisure and care-giving activities, while the opposite was true for wage-earning activities, social participation and active leisure (Fig. 3a).Fig. 3Time Use Pattern among Elderly according to a Gender, b Age, c Place of Residence, d Literacy Level, e MPCE Quantile

Time Use Pattern among Elderly according to a Gender, b Age, c Place of Residence, d Literacy Level, e MPCE Quantile

In all three phases of old age, the duration of engagement in different activities remained the same, but the proportion of elderly people’s engagement in different activities varied significantly. With increasing age, involvement in wage-earning activities, unpaid activities, and household chores decreases. In the older phase, most elderly individuals allocate their time to self-care and maintenance activities, social participation, and passive leisure. In the oldest old phase, the elderly individuals were mainly involved in sleep and passive leisure (Fig. 3b).

Elderly people who live in rural areas are more likely to wake up early than those who live in urban areas. During the course of a day, a higher percentage of elderly living in rural areas were involved in unpaid work than elderly people living in urban areas. Conversely, in urban areas, elderly people were more involved in passive leisure and sleep than rural elderly people were. In particular, rural elderly residents spent more time in social activities than did urban residents (Fig. 3c).

Elderly people who are literate and illiterate show almost the same daily pattern, with minor differences, such as illiterate elderly people spending a few more hours on household chores, while passive leisure is performed by a greater proportion of literate elderly people (Fig. 3d).

The elderly individuals belonging to all the quantiles also exhibited similar kinds of daily schedules with minor differences (Fig. 3e); for example, the proportion of elderly individuals engaged in unpaid activities decreased with increasing MPCE, and a greater proportion of elderly individuals belonging to the richest category engaged in active leisure.

The findings show that there is a significant gender gap in the time use of elderly people. Compared with elderly males, elderly females spent significantly less time on leisure and subsistence activities, while they spent significantly more time on maintenance activities. Age plays a significant role in the time use patterns of elderly people. With increasing age, elderly people prefer spending more hours on leisure but less time on subsistence activities, though the rate of change slows at older ages.

The time use behaviour of elderly people varies significantly between urban and rural areas. Elderly people living in urban areas spend significantly less time on maintenance activities, while they spend considerably more time on leisure activities, as compared to elderly living in rural areas.

Elderly people living alone spend significantly more time on maintenance activities and less time on subsistence activities as compared to the elderly people living with spouse; spouse and children, children (without spouse) or others. The findings also suggest that as the size of a household increases, elderly people spend significantly more time on leisure activities and significantly less time on maintenance activities. It is also found that if an elderly individual is the household head, then he/she spends significantly more time on subsistence activities and less time on leisure and maintenance than the elderly who do not occupy such a position. Elderly people living in households with children spend significantly less time on leisure and subsistence activities and more time on maintenance activities than do households with no children.

Elderly people with a higher educational status spend less time on subsistence activities and more time on leisure activities. With increasing wealth status, elderly spend less time on maintenance activities. The elderly individuals in the top 20% MPCE quintile spend significantly more time on leisure activities than those in the bottom 20% quintile. Elderly individuals living in pucca dwelling units spend significantly more time on leisure but less on maintenance and subsistence activities. Additionally, households using solid fuels spent significantly more time on maintenance activities. Muslim elderly people tend to spend less time on subsistence and maintenance activities, while they spend more time on leisure activities than Hindu elderly people.

Discussion

Time Use Surveys (TUSs) represent a vital tool in the collection of comprehensive data regarding human activities. TUSs are unique in their ability to gather detailed information across all types of human activities, encompassing paid and unpaid work, leisure, and personal care [19]. This methodology stands out for its ability to offer an exhaustive portrayal of individual daily lives, a level of detail that traditional surveys fail to achieve [20]. It is now recognised as a trustworthy and accurate tool for gathering data on the patterns of activities of large populations [21, 22].

The present paper identified time use patterns and sequences of activities among elderly people according to socio-economic and demographic characteristics and the determinants associated with time use in different activities.

Our analysis revealed that elderly people spent more than half of their day sleeping (10 h), self-care (3 h, 11 min), social participation (3 h, 10 min) and passive leisure (2 h, 55 min). They also spent almost the same number of hours in wage earning activities and household chores. Spending time on active leisure and unpaid activities is less common. The literature also supports our results and suggests that a maximum number of hours are spent sleeping and resting by elderly people [23]. The more significant percentage of time spent on sleep and self-care may be influenced by health conditions and the need for adequate rest and personal care, which become more critical in old age.

Gender differences

The gender division of labor influenced by social and cultural conventions leads to the differences in how men and women allocate their time to different activities [24] . Generally, women have more favorable attitudes toward household chores, such as cleaning, cooking, and childcare, than men. They maintain higher standards for these tasks and feel more responsible for them [25]. This sense of responsibility extends beyond their own households,older female individuals often bear care responsibilities for their parents and grandchildren [26]. Regardless of their employment status, women remain solely responsible for domestic work and care-related activities, including both childcare and elder care [27, 28]. Our findings also suggest that time use patterns of male and female elderly differ. According to the analysis, older female adults were more likely to perform household chores and activities that are confined to their homes, such as caregiving and passive leisure. However, older male adults were more involved in activities that were usually accomplished outside the household, such as wage earning activities and social participation. This finding was also supported by our regression analysis, which indicated that females spend less time on leisure and subsistence activities and more time on maintenance activities than males. This finding might be attributable to traditional gender roles and societal expectations. The patriarchal cultural norms may assign domestic responsibilities to women, while society might encourage men to participate in activities outside the home. Indian women who are less educated, socially marginalised, jobless, and from lower-income families spend more time in unpaid domestic activities; on the other hand, women who are highly educated, socially marginalized, not in the labour force, and who have more children at home spend more time in "unpaid care-giving" activities [29].

Age-based shifts

As people age, their regular daily activities, health, and social connections diminish, but their level of life accomplishment doesn't change over time. However, when all domains are combined, well-being tends to go down with advancing age [30]. Variability in the time use pattern is also observed depending on the age of the elderly individual. As individuals age, they reallocate their time by decreasing their working hours and performing more leisure activities [31]. Our findings reveal that with increasing age, the amount of time that elderly people spend performing wage-earning activities, unpaid activities, and household chores decreases significantly, and they start allocating more time to passive leisure and social participation. Engaging in leisure activities can improve the overall well-being of elderly people [32–34]. It has also been observed that elderly people who spend their time on social participation and leisure activities are more likely to experience active ageing [23]. Thus, we can say that elderly people in India are progressing toward the active ageing concept.

Rural–urban divide

Variation in the time use patterns of elderly people based on place of residence was also observed. Older adults living in urban areas live with more ease than older adults living in rural areas. Urban older residents spend more time on active and passive leisure and less time on wage earning activities, unpaid work, and household chores than do rural older residents. The greater ease of living for urban residents can be attributed to better economic opportunities, access to social infrastructure, and technological advancements, which might allow older urban individuals to provide more time for active and passive leisure [35, 36]. On the other hand, some researchers have suggested that elderly people living in rural areas have greater chances to experience active ageing [37] because of greater involvement in various labor activities, whether paid or unpaid [38].

The patterns of time use among elderly Indians highlight the need for targeted social policies. Support for unpaid domestic and caregiving work, particularly for older women, could include caregiving allowances, community services, or time-saving technologies, such as shared washing or cooking facilities, and services that reduce the burden of household chores. To promote health and well-being, especially in rural areas, programs could encourage leisure, social participation, and awareness of activities like yoga, meditation, and other low-cost recreational options for older adults.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides crucial insights into the time use patterns of elderly people in India, offering a comprehensive understanding of how individuals aged 60 and above distribute their time to different activities. As this is the first nationwide time use survey in 2019, the findings illuminate the daily routines of elderly people, laying the groundwork for informed policy formulation and social interventions to enhance their well-being.

The identified dominance of sleep, self-care, social participation, and passive leisure in elderly people's daily lives underscores the need for policies promoting a balanced and active lifestyle. Notably, the study reveals a gender divide, with elderly females dedicating more time to maintenance activities within the confines of their homes. At the same time, males engage more in wage-earning and outdoor social participation. This insight is pivotal for tailoring interventions that address both genders' specific needs and dynamics in their later years.

The impact of age on time use patterns is evident, reflecting a natural shift from subsistence activities to increased engagement in leisure and self-care as individuals age. As elderly people face challenges associated with physical and mental well-being, policies should be designed to facilitate an environment that supports and encourages active ageing. Encouragingly, this study emphasizes the importance of social participation and leisure in the ageing process, highlighting opportunities for older adults to find meaning, adapt to changes, and sustain good health.

The urban‒rural disparity in time use patterns indicates the necessity for context-specific interventions. Urban elderly residents exhibit distinct patterns, emphasizing the importance of tailored strategies for different living environments. This study underscores the role of education, household characteristics, and living arrangements in shaping time use behaviors among elderly people, providing valuable insights for policymakers.

Looking forward, India needs proactive policies and programs that not only recognize evolving time use patterns but also actively promote active ageing. By considering the identified variations in time use based on socio-economic factors, policymakers can craft initiatives that foster inclusivity, support financial independence, and address the unique needs of the ageing population. As India braces for a significant increase in its geriatric demographics, understanding and addressing the time use patterns of the elderly are essential steps toward creating a society where individuals age with dignity, purpose, and good health.

Limitations

In this study, the analysis includes only primary (major) activities, which understate multi-tasking and the time spent on some of the activities. Incorporating minor activities would require subdividing time slots or allowing multiple activities within the same interval, potentially resulting in overlapping time allocations that exceed the 24-h daily constraint and complicate interpretation. Accordingly, the results should be interpreted as reflecting primary time allocation rather than total engagement across concurrent activities.

Furthermore, the study could not establish the causation and consequences of time use patterns because of the limitations of the available data. However, it was the first all-India Time Use data and is thus in its evolving phase.
