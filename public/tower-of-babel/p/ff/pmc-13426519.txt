Preserved contextual modulation of eye movements in psychosis prone individuals
Authors: Anna-Chiara Schaub, Alexander Goettker, Stijn Adriaan Nuiten, Mika Kanana, Philipp Sterzer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The framework of predictive processing conceives perception as the integration of prior predictions and sensory data. An important source of priors is contextual information, including semantic context (i.e., the meaningful relationships between objects) and temporal context (i.e., the information provided by recent perceptual experiences). Alterations in the integration of priors and sensory data have been proposed to underlie psychotic experiences. Here, we examined the effects of semantic and temporal contextual information on eye movements in healthy individuals with low and high psychosis-proneness. Healthy participants were assigned to a low or a high psychosis-proneness group based on a questionnaire measuring delusional ideation. Effects of semantic and temporal context on eye movements were assessed using a naturalistic semantic task and a temporal task, respectively. Mixed models were used to investigate group-by-context condition interactions. Replicating previous findings, both tasks yielded robust effects of contextual information on eye movements. Crucially, however, no differences in the context effects in either task were detected between individuals with low and high psychosis proneness. Psychosis proneness in healthy individuals does not alter the impact of context information on eye-movement behavior. These findings are in contrast with previous reports of altered eye-movement behavior in clinical and subclinical psychosis, highlighting the need to delineate the experimental conditions and sample characteristics in which contextual modulation of eye movements is impaired.

Introduction

Hierarchical predictive processing models suggest that our brains infer the state of the world by combining hierarchically organized priors (i.e., predictions shaped by prior experiences and contextual information) with incoming sensory input (Friston, 2005; Friston, 2009). In psychosis, this inference process is thought to be disrupted, with a reduced influence of perceptual priors relative to sensory data (Adams, Stephan, Brown, Frith, & Friston, 2013; Sterzer et al., 2018). This shift leads to an abnormally strong weighting of sensory data, which may result in an exaggerated assignment of salience to otherwise inconspicuous stimuli. Such aberrant salience may underlie delusional thinking and perceptual abnormalities (Kapur, 2003). Evidence supporting the notion of a reduced weighting of perceptual priors includes reduced susceptibility to visual illusions in psychosis (Notredame, Pins, Deneve, & Jardri, 2014). Such illusions are thought to reflect the effect of long-learned perceptual priors from regularities in the sensory environment. Less is known about the role of short-term, context-dependent priors in psychosis. Context-dependent priors may stem from various sources, including semantic or temporal contextual information. In regard to visual information processing, semantic context refers to meaningful relationships between the objects in a visual scene, such as a player's movement in a ball game and the motion trajectory of the ball. Temporal context, on the other hand, arises from recent perceptual experiences and can produce serial dependence, where current perception is biased by previous perceptual choices (Cicchini, Mikellidou, & Burr, 2024; Manassi & Whitney, 2024; Pascucci et al., 2023).

Eye-movement behavior provides valuable insights into predictive processing and its potential alterations (Bakst & McGuire, 2021). Schizophrenia has long been associated with characteristic eye-movement abnormalities, particularly during smooth pursuit and anti-saccade tasks (Calkins & Iacono, 2000; Levy, Sereno, Gooding, & O'Driscoll, 2010). Individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia also present impaired predictive pursuit during target occlusion (Thaker, Ross, Buchanan, Adami, & Medoff, 1999) but superior performance in tracking unpredictable target motion compared to neurotypical individuals (Hong, Avila, & Thaker, 2005). Within the framework of predictive processing, these impairments have been interpreted as reflecting reduced precision of priors regarding target trajectories and thus an overreliance on bottom-up sensory data (Adams et al., 2013).

Based on the notion of a continuous distribution of psychotic traits in the general population (Van Os, Linscott, Myin-Germeys, Delespaul, & Krabbendam, 2009) and evidence on shared neurobiological, cognitive, and genetic mechanisms with schizophrenia-spectrum disorders (Nelson, Seal, Pantelis, & Phillips, 2013), some studies have also investigated eye movement abnormalities in subclinical samples. Smooth pursuit eye-tracking (Van Kampen & Deijen, 2009) and predictive pursuit to occluded targets are impaired in individuals with high schizotypy (Faiola, Meyhöfer, & Ettinger, 2020; Kattoulas et al., 2011).

In the present study, we examined how semantic and temporal contextual information influences eye movements in healthy individuals with varying levels of psychosis-proneness, as measured with the Peters Delusions Inventory (PDI, Peters, Joseph, & Garety, 1999). To assess the role of semantic context, we used a naturalistic scene paradigm in which a moving ice hockey puck is shown either in isolation on a gray background or embedded in the video of an ice hockey game (Figure 1A, Goettker, Pidaparthy, Braun, Elder, & Gegenfurtner, 2021; Goettker, Borgerding, Leeske, & Gegenfurtner, 2023). Observers were asked to track the puck with their eyes. Previous work has shown that there is a substantial delay between the location of the puck and the observer's gaze when the puck is shown in isolation (Goettker et al., 2021; Goettker et al., 2023). If the puck is embedded in the video, however, the semantic context provided by the ice hockey players’ movements substantially reduces this delay. We reasoned that this delay reduction may be used to quantify the degree to which an individual uses priors derived from semantic context in their oculomotor behavior. In a third additional condition, the video was played reversed meaning that semantic context was available, but not helpful because of its impaired causality. This led to a significant increase in the delay (Goettker et al., 2023). We use the term semantic to denote the presence of meaningful, real-world context in the naturalistic stimuli (e.g., structured action sequences), while acknowledging that this manipulation also involves broader spatiotemporal coherence.

To assess the role of temporal context, we examined serial dependence in eye movement velocity (Darlington, Beck, & Lisberger, 2018; Deravet, Blohm, De Xivry, & Lefèvre, 2018) using a simple oculomotor task in which observers track two subsequently presented moving target stimuli with their eyes (Figure 2A, Goettker, 2021; Guan & Goettker, 2024). This trial structure creates an attractive serial dependence effect, whereby the velocity of the prior target biases the eye velocity when tracking the subsequently presented probe target. We measured this bias to quantify the degree to which an individual's oculomotor behavior is influenced by priors derived from temporal context.

Temporal task eliciting serial dependence. (A) Task structure. The prior moves at either 5°/s or 15°/s, and the probe always moves at 10°/s. (B) Mean eye velocity as a function of time, shown separately for prior speeds of 5°/s and 15°/s for the low and high psychosis-proneness group. (C) Group comparisons for the mean eye velocity and serial dependence effect (SD, difference in velocity of fast-slow prior).

Based on the notion of reduced prior weighting as suggested by predictive processing accounts of psychosis (Adams et al., 2013; Sterzer et al., 2018), we hypothesized that psychosis-proneness would be associated (1) with a reduced benefit from predictive semantic context as reflected by a smaller delay reduction when tracking the puck in the semantic task; and (2) with a reduced influence of temporal context on eye movements as reflected by a decreased attractive bias in the temporal task.

Material and methods

The study was conducted in line with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the local ethics committee (Ethikkommission Nordwest- und Zentralschweiz; Project ID 2023-01428). All participants gave written informed consent before participating. We collected data between January and October 2024.

Sample

As part of a larger project, we recruited 32 participants per group via advertisement on local online platforms. Based on data from a related study investigating sensory evidence sensitivity in patients with schizophrenia compared to healthy control individuals (Weilnhammer et al., 2020), we computed an η2 effect size of 0.05 for the group*condition interaction. Using α = .05 and power = .80 and a correlation of r = 0.18 for the within-subject factor condition, a sample size of n = 32 participants per group was computed using G*Power (Faul et al., 2009). When dropouts occurred, new participants were recruited to fill up the sample size.

Participants were required to be between 18 and 65 years old, fluent in German, able to read and understand the study information and consent. In the screening, interested individuals had to have a PDI score of ≤3 or ≥11 to be assigned to one of the two study groups (low and high psychosis-proneness, respectively). We selected the cut-off values based on empirical evidence from Eckert, Gounitski, Guggenmos, and Sterzer (2023) who found a normal distribution with a mean of 6.6 and standard deviation (SD) of 3.1 in a sample of 113 participants. Based on this distribution, we decided to define the cut-offs at ±1 SD of the mean, leading to the values of ≤3 or ≥11, allowing us to include the lowest and highest 16% of the population. Exclusion criteria were current or previous psychiatric or neurological diseases, non-correctable visual impairment, glasses, and hearing impairment.

Procedures

After giving written informed consent, participants first performed an online screening procedure. This online screening contained a demographic questionnaire and the German version of the PDI (Lincoln, Keller, & Rief, 2009). Participants were assigned to one of the two study groups (low versus high psychosis-proneness) and invited to attend the experimental session in person. During the experimental session, participants completed the eye-tracking tasks and further cognitive tests (see below). After completing the study, participants received a reimbursement of CHF 20.00 per hour or, alternatively, psychology students got course credit.

Setup

Participants sat at a table in a dimly illuminated room with their head positioned on a chin rest. In this position, their eyes were roughly at the height of the center of a monitor (1920 × 1080 pixels, 60 Hz refresh rate, 23.8-inch, G24e-20 monitor, Lenovo, China) at a distance of 53 cm. The tasks were programmed and controlled with MATLAB (MathWorks, USA) using Psychtoolbox (Kleiner, Brainard, & Pelli, 2007). Gaze was recorded from the left eye with a desk-mounted eye tracker (EyeLink 1000 Plus; SR Research, Kanata, ON, Canada) at a sampling frequency of 1000 Hz. In both tasks, a nine-point calibration was used, and an additional drift check was performed at the start of each trial.

Semantic task

We systematically manipulated the available semantic context information in the task using videos of an ice hockey game as in Goettker et al. (2023). In the “video” condition, predictive semantic context was provided by showing the original ice hockey video. The participants were instructed to follow the moving puck with their eyes. In the “disk” condition, no context was available; a small black disk moved on the grey screen and participants got the same instruction to follow the moving disk. The trajectories of the puck and the disk were equal allowing direct comparison of the eye movements. In the “reverse” condition semantic context was available, but not predictive since the causal structure of the scene dynamics was abolished by playing the video backwards. Participants performed two blocks of each of the three conditions and the six blocks were in a pseudo-random order. A block contained 12 different videos lasting 10 s each. In three participants, the duration of the video was 12 s, as the refresh rate of the monitor was altered. Per person, 72 videos were presented. Completing all six blocks took approximately 30 min. We preregistered the task and analysis at OSF (https://osf.io/y94ge).

Measures and pre-processing

The main eye-tracking measure of interest was the delay between target and eye movements, which was calculated as the magnitude of the cross-correlation between target and gaze. Secondary eye-tracking measures were the number of saccades, proportion of pursuit, position error, position error at pass, timing at pass delay for saccades, and delay for pursuit. These measures were extracted using a MATLAB processing script (adapted from Goettker et al., 2023). A basic version of the code is available on GitHub: https://github.com/AlexanderGoettker/CuesforNaturalisticVideos. In short, we linearly interpolated blinks and filtered gaze position data with a second-order Butterworth filter (30 Hz cutoff frequency). Eye velocity was then computed based on the difference in horizontal and vertical eye positions in consecutive samples. Then, eye velocity (°/s) was calculated as the first derivative of the filtered position traces. The hand-labeled puck position (see Pidaparthy & Elder, 2019) was upsampled via linear interpolation to match the sampling rate of eye position, because it was only available at the frame rate of the video (30 Hz). EyeLink criteria were applied to detect saccades (velocity >30°/s and acceleration >4000°/s2).

The delay between target movement and eye gaze was estimated using a cross-correlation approach with a sliding window with 500 ms width. We computed the correlation between eye and target position for each window for varying delays (−200 to +400 ms in steps of 10 ms), separately for horizontal and vertical eye positions. The window was then repeatedly shifted by 250 ms until the end of the video. At the end of the video, we computed the median correlation across the different sliding windows for each delay. Autocorrelation within the data was accounted for by estimating the correlation within each clip by performing 1000 samples for 500 ms windows for eyes and pucks at random time points (for example, the eye vector after 1 s and the target vector after 8 s) again for horizontal and vertical eye position. Those were then subtracted from the median. Lastly, we obtained one correlation value for each of the different delays between gaze and target movement for a given trial by averaging the correlations obtained for horizontal and vertical movements. This approach allowed us to quantify the delay between eye and target movement independent of the specific oculomotor response. Extraction details of the secondary measures are described in detail in Goettker at al. (2023).

Individual trials were excluded during the eye-tracking processing if there was missing data for more than 500 ms (due to blinks or tracking error) and the mean position error was bigger than 5° of visual angle. From the valid trials, means were calculated per person and condition. If more than 30% of data were missing across conditions, the participant was excluded from the analysis.

Temporal task

Each trial included two subsequently presented horizontally moving targets, a prior target and a probe target. The prior target moved at either 5°/s or 15°/s and the probe target at 10°/s (Figure 2A). The movement direction was randomized and remained the same for both the prior and probe targets within one trial. Participants were asked to track the movement of both targets with their eyes. At the start of each trial, a red fixation cross appeared in the center of the screen for drift correction. Participants were required to fixate on it and press the space bar to initiate the trial. This was followed by a red fixation circle (0.2° diameter), displayed at the center of the screen for a random duration ranging from 1 to 1.5 s. Once the fixation circle had disappeared, the prior target (Gaussian blob, SD = 0.4°) appeared and crossed the screen horizontally. To minimize the need for corrective saccades, the target was initially presented with a slight offset in the direction opposite to its motion before beginning its movement. The offset was chosen so that the target crossed the initial fixation point 200 ms after the start of the movement. The target always moved for 1 s before disappearing. After a short delay, a new fixation circle signaled the upcoming probe target, which followed the same sequence, with a random interval of 1 to 1.5 s before appearing. Another fixation cross was then introduced to mark the start of the subsequent trial. While the target contrast of the prior target was always high (1), it was always low in the probe target (0.1) to increase uncertainty and thus potential serial dependence effects (Pascucci et al., 2023). Each prior target (5 and 15°/s) was presented 32 times, resulting in 64 trials overall.

Measures and pre-processing

The mean eye velocity during the probe was the main measure of interest. The serial dependence effect was quantified as the difference between the mean velocity in the fast prior trials minus the slow prior trials from 100 to 400 ms after motion onset. Eye movement data was processed offline using MATLAB code adapted from Guan and Goettker (2024). As in the semantic task, linear interpolation of eye blinks, and filtering of eye position data with a second-order Butterworth filter (30 Hz cutoff frequency) was applied. Eye velocity was determined as the first derivative of the filtered position traces. Saccades were detected based on velocity and acceleration thresholds of 30°/s and 4000°/s2, respectively. To reduce the impact of saccadic movements, velocity values were interpolated within a 70 ms window surrounding each saccade (35 ms before saccade onset and after saccade offset). Additionally, a low-pass Butterworth filter (20 Hz cutoff) was applied to remove high-frequency noise. Trials with more than 500 ms of missing eye-tracking data (due to head movements or system artifacts), trials where the eye velocity exceeded 30°/s after saccade interpolation, and any trials in which a pursuit response with a delay of less than 400 ms could not be identified were excluded from the analysis.

Cognitive tests

After completing the eye-tracking tasks, the Brief Assessment of Cognition in Schizophrenia (BACS) (Keefe et al., 2004), and the d2-test (Brickenkamp, 1962) were performed. The BACS measures the following cognition aspects: verbal memory (list learning), working memory (digit sequencing), motor speed (token motor task), verbal fluency (semantic and letter), attention and speed of information processing (symbol coding) and executive functions (tower of London). From the d2-test, which measures selective and sustained attention, we used a score calculated as the number of correctly crossed-out characters minus the number of confusion errors.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in R. We used conventional two-sample Wilcoxon tests to compare demographic, cognitive and eye-tracking measures between groups. We additionally conducted Bayes t-tests (R package BayesFactor, Morey & Rouder, 2012) to detect evidence for the absence of group differences for the eye-tracking measures. Bayes Factors are reported to present evidence for the absence of an effect (BF01) and interpreted according to Kass and Raftery (1995).

Our main analysis was a linear mixed model on aggregated data (mean per condition and person) with a group-by-condition interaction and a random intercept. In the semantic task, the analysis was conducted for each of the eight eye-tracking measures and in the temporal task for the mean velocity. Group and condition were included as factors; in the semantic task were three condition levels (disk, video, reverse), in the temporal task two (slow and fast prior). Processing speed as measured with the d2 test, age (both scaled) and gender (factor) were included as covariates. The nlme package was applied for both tasks as it was required to control for heterogeneity in the condition in some of the dependent variables (Pinheiro, Bates, & R Core Team, 1999). We computed a marginal ANOVA over the model and compared the model to a similar model without interaction using a likelihood ratio test. To compare the models, they were fitted using maximum likelihood estimation (ML), but the final model was then fitted using restricted ML (REML). Post-hoc tests on significant main effects were computed using estimated marginal means with Bonferroni correction (emmeans package, Lenth, 2017). As an additional exploratory analysis, we computed models using trial-wise data to control for intra-individual changes over time. In the semantic task, this analysis was only computed for the six trial-related measures delay, number of saccades, proportion pursuit, position error, delay for saccades and delay for pursuit but not the pass related measures.

Associations between the eye-tracking measures of the two tasks (delay in the semantic task and serial dependence effect in temporal task) were computed using spearman rank correlations. Additionally, for the semantic task, a semantic context effect was created using the difference in the delay of the disk minus the video condition. Furthermore, eye-tracking measures were correlated with cognitive measures per study group. Due to their exploratory nature, these analyses were conducted without corrections for multiple testing. They should therefore be interpreted with caution and considered hypothesis-generating. If significant correlations occurred, correlation coefficients were compared between the groups using Fishers z transformation (cocor package; Diedenhofen & Musch, 2015).

The cognition measures showed reduced processing speed as measured with the d2 test, increased verbal memory and digit sequencing in the high compared to the low psychosis-proneness group (Table 1).

Semantic task

In total, 232 trials (∼5%) were discarded from the analysis based on our predefined criteria. The grouped mixed model for the delay showed a main effect of condition (Table 2), indicating smallest delay in the video condition and largest in the reverse condition (Figure 1B; Appendix Table A1). There was no main effect of group and no condition-by-group interaction (Table 2). Comparing the model with interaction term with the model without interaction term showed no superiority of the model with interaction (Χ2(1) = 4.23, p = 0.121). Effects of covariates in secondary measures are reported in the Appendix Tables A1 and A2. In addition, we computed regression models for each condition predicting delay with the PDI score. None of the models showed a significant effect of PDI (all p > 0.188).

On the trial level, the delay and all five trial-wise secondary measures showed a significant main effect of condition (all p < 0.001, Appendix Table A3). The group-by-condition interaction was not significant in the delay model and did also not improve model fit (Χ2(1) = 0.973, p = 0.615). However, there was significant interaction in the models for number of saccades and for pursuit proportion (Appendix Table A3). The post-hoc test on the number of saccades indicated greater reduction of saccades from video to disk and from disk to reverse in the high psychosis-proneness group (disk-video: estimate = −1.379, t(4308) = −3.921, p < 0.001, disk-reverse: estimate = −1.052, t(4308) = −3.008, p = 0.008). The last contrast for video versus reverse by group was not significant (Appendix Table A4). Regarding the pursuit proportion, post-hoc testing showed a smaller decrease from the disk to the reverse condition in the high compared to the low psychosis-proneness group (estimate = 0.022, t(4308) = 2.536, p = 0.034). The two other interaction contrasts were not significant (Appendix Table A4). Effects of covariates on secondary measures are reported in the Appendix Tables A3 and A4.

Temporal task

In the temporal task, 211 (5.15%) trials were excluded from the analysis. The main model predicting eye velocity per person and condition did present a significant effect of condition (Table 2), meaning that the eye velocity during tracking of the probe target was increased in the fast prior condition compared to the slow prior condition (estimate = −1.16, t(62) = 23.537, p < 0.001). The group and interaction effects were not significant and comparing the model to a model without interaction did not indicate superiority of the model with interaction (Χ2(1) = 0.084, p = 0.771). For the serial dependence effect (difference between velocity in the fast - slow prior), there was substantial evidence for the absence of a group difference (BF01 = 3.78, group comparisons in Appendix Table A1). An additional regression model predicting the serial dependence effect with the PDI score showed no significant effect (p = 0.630). The model on the trial-level showed similar results, i.e., a significant main effect of condition but no group-by-condition interaction (Appendix Table A3 and A4). The model comparison showed no improved fit by the interaction (Χ2(1) = 0.533, p = 0.465).

Associations between eye-tracking measures and cognition

Additional exploratory analyses were performed to investigate the relationship between the two types of contextual priors investigated in the two experiments. We correlated the semantic context effect (difference between the delay of the disk and video condition) with the serial dependence effect in the temporal task. There was no significant correlation, neither across groups (rs = −0.119, p = 0.348) nor within groups (low: rs = −0.130, p = 0.479; high: rs = −0.079, p = 0.666). However, the delay in the disk condition correlated with the serial dependence effect, indicating less delay with a stronger serial dependence effect. This effect was marginally significant (rs = −0.242, p = 0.054) and driven by the low group (rs = −0.339, p = 0.058; high group: rs = −0.139, p = 0.449, group difference not significant z = −0.811, p = 0.417). The delay in the video condition did not correlate with the serial dependence effect (across groups: rs = 0.042, p = 0.739; low group: rs = −0.036, p = 0.846; high group: rs = 0.063, p = 0.732).

Since there were some group differences in the cognitive measures, correlations with eye-tracking measures were conducted per group. The semantic context effect was negatively related to working memory as measured with the digit sequencing task in the high psychosis-proneness group (rs = −0.399, p = 0.024, Appendix Figure A1). This indicates that individuals with greater delay reduction from disk to video showed lower digit sequencing scores. The correlation in the low psychosis-proneness group was not significant but the effects between the two groups did not differ (z = 1.567, p = 0.117). None of the other cognitive measures correlated with the semantic context effect. In the disk condition, processing speed was negatively correlated with delay in the high (rs = −0.503, p = 0.003) but not in the low psychosis-proneness group (rs = −0.037, p = 0.839). The correlations differed significantly between the two groups (z = 1.965, p = .049). In the temporal task, the serial dependence effect did not correlate with any of the cognitive measures per group (Appendix Figure A2). However, processing speed (d2 and symbol coding test) was positively correlated with eye velocity in both conditions in the high but not in the low psychosis-proneness group (Appendix Figure A2).

Discussion

This study aimed to elucidate the effects of visual semantic and temporal context-related priors on eye movements in relation to psychosis proneness. In two visual tasks, we replicated previously reported contextual effects on eye movements. In the semantic task, the semantic context provided by player and scene information led to significantly reduced tracking delays compared to the control condition with an isolated disk. In the temporal task, eye velocity when tracking a target stimulus was influenced by the temporal context, specifically by the velocity of the preceding stimulus. However, our hypotheses with regard to psychosis proneness were not confirmed: The influence of contextual information on eye movements was not attenuated in participants with high psychosis proneness in either task. In particular, individuals with high psychosis proneness neither showed smaller delay reduction in the semantic task, nor a diminished serial dependence effect in the temporal task, compared to those with low psychosis proneness.

The semantic task allows the investigation of eye movements in a more naturalistic and complex setting compared to artificial stimuli. Inter-individual differences in eye movements have been observed both in the present study and in previous work using this task (Goettker et al., 2021; Goettker et al., 2023). The only known factor explaining such variability so far is prior experience, namely familiarity with watching ice hockey games, which reduces tracking delays across all task conditions investigated (Goettker et al., 2023). We did not systematically assess ice hockey familiarity because we did not expect a group difference in this regard. However, given the previous evidence, it is theoretically conceivable that unbalanced levels of expertise between groups may have confounded our results. Future studies using similar experimental paradigms should therefore control for ice hockey familiarity. Although the semantic task uses naturalistic stimulus material, it targets processes that are highly automatic and likely involve low-level sensory processing. However, the absence of an effect of psychosis proneness in our experiment is in apparent contradiction to previous work showing impaired predictive pursuit performance during target occlusion in schizotypy and schizophrenia (Faiola et al., 2020; Kattoulas et al., 2011; Thaker et al., 1999). This discrepancy may be due to the fact that, in contrast to these previous studies, we actively manipulated semantic context with the different task conditions used, in which the stimulus was always present. Moreover, the contradictory results might be due to the complexity of the visual scene stimuli, as in a naturalistic setting in which participants could look at self-chosen targets, no significant gain difference has been detected between patients with schizophrenia and healthy individuals (Dowiasch et al., 2016). The influence of semantic context on perception has previously also been studied using speech stimuli and showed increased weighting of high-level priors in high schizotypy, especially when priors were imprecise or the relevant sensory information unexpected (Knolle, Sterner, Demler, MacGregor, & Mathys, 2025). Interestingly, this overweighting of priors was related to increased cingulate glutamate, which may reflect altered neural signaling supporting top-down predictions, consistent with hierarchical predictive processing accounts (Corlett et al., 2019; Petrovic & Sterzer, 2023; Sterzer et al., 2018).

The temporal task used in our study is highly controlled and designed to elicit positive serial dependence by applying stimuli with high contrast as priors followed by low-contrast probes (Pascucci et al., 2023). The positive serial dependence effect we observed is comparable to previous findings with the same task (Guan & Goettker, 2024). While substantial inter-individual variability was present in the current data, no explanatory variable has yet been identified. Interestingly, serial dependence strength in the eye-tracking task was previously not associated with serial dependence strength in perceptual tasks involving color or orientation judgements (Guan & Goettker, 2024). In perceptual tasks, reduced serial dependence has been reported in individuals with high psychosis proneness (Eckert et al., 2023; Schaub, Eckert, Nuiten, Weilnhammer, & Sterzer, 2025) and in a working memory task in individuals with psychotic disorders (Stein et al., 2020). However, there is also conflicting evidence. Pascucci et al. (2024) found no differences in serial dependence in patients with schizophrenia compared to healthy control individuals, albeit using different stimulus and task conditions. Importantly, the absence of group differences in our oculomotor serial dependence measure does not necessarily imply that group differences in behavioral performance would be absent. Given that eye-tracking and perceptual judgment measures of serial dependence appear to be dissociated (Guan & Goettker, 2024), it is plausible that psychosis proneness-related alterations in predictive processing manifest selectively at the level of perceptual decisions rather than oculomotor control, or that they require different task conditions or outcome measures to become detectable.

Possibly, the tasks applied in the present study, including their specific stimulus characteristics and demands, did not optimally target processes implicated in psychosis proneness with optimal sensitivity. For example, in the temporal task, we employed a single uncertainty configuration (low-contrast probe, high-contrast prior), and it remains unclear whether different contrast manipulations, potentially inducing lower tracking uncertainty, might reveal group differences in serial dependence that were not detectable under the current task design. Also, future studies might benefit from task manipulations that better disentangle low-level sensory mechanisms from higher-level cognitive contributions. In addition, combining eye-tracking with imaging methods such as EEG or fMRI could provide insights into the neural correlates of semantic and temporal context integration. Another potential explanation for our failure to confirm our hypotheses may lie in our assessment of psychosis proneness. We relied on the PDI, which focuses on delusional ideation and selected cut-offs values based on empirical evidence with the aim of preserving ecological validity, ensuring that the high psychosis-proneness group remained within the healthy continuum. It is possible that discriminant sensitivity was therefore reduced and true group differences were not detected. Kattoulas and colleagues (2011) reported deficient predictive pursuit performance only in individuals with disorganized schizotypy, identified using a subscale of the Schizotypal Personality Questionnaire (SPQ; Raine, 1991), but not in the total SPQ score. This highlights that eye-movement alterations may be specific to particular subdimensions of psychosis proneness. Future studies should therefore adopt a more comprehensive assessment of psychosis proneness, incorporating additional and more specific measures to capture distinct dimensions, such as the disorganized subtype. Taken together, the effects of psychosis proneness on eye movements appear highly dependent on the sample characteristics, the specific tasks employed, and the eye-movement measures analyzed. This emphasizes the need to further delineate the exact conditions in which contextual modulation of eye movements is impaired.

Besides our main aim to investigate alterations in psychosis proneness, our findings point to an association between general cognitive functioning and eye-movement behavior. The two study groups differed in some cognitive functions, consistent with previous work showing that high psychosis proneness or schizotypy is associated with deficits in domains such as attention, memory, and inhibitory control (Ettinger et al., 2015). In our study, processing speed was related to eye movements in both tasks, albeit only in the high psychosis-proneness group. Greater processing speed correlated with smaller delay in the disk condition of the semantic task and with faster prior and probe tracking in the temporal task suggesting a processing speed modulation of eye-movement efficiency. These findings align with evidence that attention and processing speed are altered in schizophrenia (e.g., Nuechterlein et al., 2004) and support the view that they cut across both cognitive and oculomotor domains. The absence of broader associations between both tasks and with cognitive measures may partly reflect the substantial differences between the two tasks in stimulus characteristics, task demands, and derived eye-movement measures, suggesting they capture partially distinct sensorimotor and cognitive processes rather than a single unified index of contextual prior integration. More generally, these findings underscore the limited variance that individual behavioral tasks can explain with respect to complex constructs such as psychosis proneness, a challenge compounded by the typically small effect sizes associated with measures like serial dependence. Given the established link between cognition and eye movements, and their alterations in psychosis, future research could profitably investigate their interactions more deeply.

Conclusions

Our study used a novel approach to probe predictive processing alterations in psychosis proneness by actively manipulating priors within the tasks and by using eye-movement measures, which implicate predictive mechanisms and reflect internal dynamics of decision processes (Cavanagh, Wiecki, Kochar, & Frank, 2014; Henderson, 2017). The findings emphasize that eye-movement measures reliably capture contextual effects, although their sensitivity to psychosis proneness appears to depend on task design, sample characteristics, and assessed cognitive domains. Future research should investigate whether individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia or other clinically manifest psychotic disorders—as opposed to varying levels of psychosis proneness in healthy individuals—show altered effects of contextual priors on eye movements, as predicted by predictive processing models, and use multimodal approaches to disentangle the mechanisms linking cognition, perception, and eye movements.
