Is Work‐Related Hearing Loss Associated With Dementia? Evidence From a High‐Risk Population
Authors: Marianne Cloeren, John Dement, Jane Quackenbush, Patricia Quinn, Knut Ringen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAge‐related hearing loss is associated with increased dementia risk. We examined the association between hearing loss and dementia in a population at high risk for hearing loss from occupational noise exposures.MethodsWe conducted cross‐sectional and longitudinal analyses using logistic regression and interval‐censored Cox models using data from the Building Trades National Medical Screening Program (BTMed), from inception in 1996 through March 2024. Hearing loss was defined as a speech‐frequency pure‐tone average ≥ 20 decibels (dB) in the better ear and categorized as mild (20–34 dB), moderate (35–49 dB), moderately severe (50–64 dB), or severe to complete (≥ 65 dB). Dementia was defined using criteria from medical history, physical exams, and medication data across all medical screening examinations.ResultsThe study included 44,000 exams in 24,958 BTMed participants; 54.6% had hearing loss. Hearing loss was strongly associated with dementia prevalence (211 cases, p < 0.001), with prevalence increasing by severity. Cross‐sectional analysis found a significant association between hearing loss ≥ 20 dB and dementia (adjusted odds ratio = 1.88, 95% confidence interval (CI) = 1.15–3.07). In longitudinal analysis, a Cox model adjusted for confounders estimated a hazard ratio of 1.60 (95% CI = 0.99–2.59, p‐trend = 0.0928) for incident dementia.DiscussionCross‐sectional results support an association between occupational hearing loss and dementia, consistent in direction with findings for age‐related hearing loss; longitudinal estimates were not significant but were directionally similar. If confirmed in other high‐risk cohorts with repeated audiometry, these findings underscore the potential for hearing conservation and hearing loss rehabilitation in dementia prevention.Keywords: dementia, hearing loss, occupation

Introduction

Hearing loss is a common condition, impacting an estimated 73 million or 22.2% of the United States population as of 2019 [1]. Hearing loss has been linked to multiple negative social, physical, and cognitive health outcomes [2]. While disabling hearing loss has been defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as hearing loss > 35 decibels (dB) in the better hearing ear, 20 dB is used as the threshold to define hearing loss that may be mild. Hearing loss may be mild, moderate, moderately severe, or profound, using the WHO classification [3].

A growing body of research has demonstrated an association between hearing loss and dementia. Early studies have found cross‐sectional evidence of a link between both self‐reported and audiometric hearing loss and dementia diagnosis [4, 5, 6]. A meta‐analysis found an overall odds ratio (OR) of 2.42 (95% confidence interval (CI) = 1.24–4.72) for dementia in hearing loss assessed by audiometry [7]. Most studies have been cross‐sectional, and many have assessed hearing loss based on self‐reported indications of hearing impairment. However, recent reviews and meta‐analyses support a link between hearing loss by audiometry and incident dementia, with a summary relative risk of 1.28 (95% CI = 1.02–1.59) [7, 8, 9].

There are several theories for the mechanism linking hearing loss to dementia. These include common pathology between dementia and hearing loss, decreased input processing/neural stimulation from loss of auditory input, increased neural demand from increased effort of hearing, and alterations in pattern recognition [10]. Although the exact mechanism has not been fully elucidated, a prevailing theme across current theories and animal model studies is the link between hearing loss, changes in neural processing or pathway maintenance, and resulting decline in memory and cognition [10, 11].

Most human studies of hearing loss and dementia have focused on age‐related hearing loss. The population attributable fraction (PAF) of dementia due to age‐related hearing loss in the United States (US) has been estimated at 8.9% based on an estimated population prevalence of hearing loss of 10.8% [12]. However, the PAF among occupational groups at increased risk of noise‐induced hearing loss, such as construction workers, manufacturers, and miners is likely to be much greater [13]. Previous retrospective and prospective studies have demonstrated a significant link between increased prevalence of hearing loss and construction work, with some studies finding an increase in hearing loss with years worked and a decrease with occupational use of hearing protection [14, 15, 16]. In one study, construction workers were found to have a hearing loss prevalence ratio of 1.52 when compared to the reference group with low occupational noise exposure [17].

Animal studies of the link between hearing loss and dementia have primarily modeled noise‐induced hearing loss rather than age‐related hearing loss [18]. These studies provide support for noise‐inducing hearing loss by damaging memory centers in the brain via depressed hippocampal neurogenesis, hippocampal glucocorticoid receptor expression, and hippocampal oxidative‐inflammatory injury [11, 19, 20]. Animal studies have demonstrated that noise induces hearing loss and cognitive deficits, including diminished perceptual acuity, reduced attentional focus, altered choice bias, slowed‐down evidence accumulation speed, impaired recognition memory and reduced myelination in the corpus callosum, social memory deficits, and other effects [21, 22, 23, 24]. However, no human studies to our knowledge have examined the association between hearing loss and dementia in occupational populations at increased risk for noise‐induced or chemically‐induced hearing loss. Animal models provide some insight but noise‐induced hearing loss in these models is typically induced through single, intense sound exposures, which may not be the most appropriate model for the exposures more commonly found in occupational environments. Understanding links between noise‐induced and ototoxic hearing loss and dementia in a human model is vitally important as exposure‐induced hearing loss is likely to occur earlier in life than age‐related hearing loss [25, 26]. This earlier onset of hearing loss has the potential to impact both overall risk of dementia development and time at onset.

The Building Trades National Medical Screening Program (BTMed) was established in 1996 with funding from the US Department of Energy to provide periodic occupational medical surveillance examinations to construction workers employed in 35 nuclear weapons facilities in the USA. Prior studies of the BTMed population have found construction trade workers to be at significantly increased risk for noise‐induced hearing loss [27, 28]. Exposure to ototoxic chemicals, including solvents and heavy metals, is also common in the construction industry. Recent studies have offered evidence that occupational hearing loss is more common in industries with coexposure to noise and ototoxic chemicals, including construction [29, 30, 31]. We aimed to assess whether audiometrically measured hearing loss was associated with both prevalent and incident dementia in a high‐risk occupational cohort.

Materials and Methods

Medical Surveillance Protocol

Since program inception in 1996, BTMed has provided over 44,000 surveillance examinations. BTMed collects a detailed occupational exposure and medical history, followed by an examination by contract medical providers who adhere to a detailed protocol. The occupational history questionnaire and medical protocol have been previously described [32, 33, 34]. Information used for this study included demographic, medical history, physical exam, and audiometric testing data. Workers are offered repeat examinations every 3 years; however, audiometric testing is only offered during the initial examination. Audiometric testing is performed according to procedures recommended by the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) at frequencies of 500–8000 Hertz (Hz) [35].

Study Cohort, Case Definitions, and Risk Factors

BTMed data from program inception in 1996 through March 2024 were used for this study. The cohort for this study was comprised of all BTMed participants from 1996 to 2024 who had baseline audiometry at initial examination. All dementia (vascular and Alzheimer's) was defined using the BTMed medical history, physical exam, and medications data. We developed an algorithm for identifying dementia cases based on similar research limited to available medical record data; this approach misses cases of dementia not yet diagnosed but has shown high sensitivity and positive predictive value [36, 37, 38]. Noncases were workers without a positive finding of dementia by this algorithm on their baseline or follow‐up exams.

A significant body of research has found an association between the use of anticholinergic drugs with cognitive decline and incident dementia [42, 43, 44, 45]. Studies of anticholinergics have supported potential links between anticholinergics and neural changes such as increased cortical atrophy, increased neural inflammation, and changes in memory pathways [46, 47, 48]. We assessed reported use of anticholinergic drugs at baseline using the 2012 update of Anticholinergic Cognitive Burden (ACB) scale and classified individual use as none, use of possible anticholinergics (ACB Group 1), and use of definite anticholinergics (ACB Group 2 or 3) [49, 50].

Speech‐frequency pure‐tone average hearing levels (HL) were calculated for each ear using measurements at 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 kHz. Most published studies of dementia and hearing loss have defined normal hearing as having a HL ≤ 25 dB in the better ear [6, 51, 52, 53]; however, recent studies have found cognitive impairment among those with subclinical hearing loss (i.e., < 25 dB) [54]. For this study audiometry‐based hearing loss was defined as a pure‐tone average HL ≥ 20 dB in the better hearing ear, consistent with current recommendation by the WHO and our prior study of speech‐frequency hearing loss in this population [28, 55]. Hearing loss was further categorized into mild (20–34 dB), moderate (35–49 dB), moderately severe (50–64 dB), and severe to complete loss (≥ 65 dB). Self‐reported hearing impairment was defined as a positive answer to the question “Do you have problems hearing or understanding normal conversation?”.

Dementia risk factors included in the analyses were determined a priori from the literature and included demographic data (age, gender, and race/ethnicity), cigarette smoking status (current, former, never), body mass index (BMI), worker‐reported physician‐diagnoses of hypertension, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, or stroke, self‐reported hearing impairment, use of hearing aids, use of anticholinergic drugs, and hearing loss by audiometry [43, 45, 56, 57]. These risk factors were taken from physical examination and medical history. A history of having smoked was based on a “Yes” response to the question—“Have you ever smoked cigarettes? (NO means < 20 packs of cigarettes or 12 oz. of tobacco in a lifetime or < 1 cigarette a day for 1 year).” Use of hearing aids at initial examination was based on a positive answer to the question—“Have you ever used a hearing aid?” or a notation of hearing aid use in the audiometry or physical exam reports. BMI was missing for 105 individuals (0.4%) and was imputed by single imputation; all covariates were included as predictors in the imputation model.

Statistical Analyses

Cross‐sectional analyses investigated risk factors for prevalent dementia, with prevalent dementia defined as those meeting criteria for dementia on the baseline examination. The longitudinal analyses investigated incident dementia, defined by those free of dementia at baseline and having audiometric data but meeting criteria for dementia at later follow‐up. Dementia prevalence by risk factor strata was calculated and univariate associations were evaluated. Categorical variables were tabulated by number and prevalence (percent) and means and standard deviations were calculated for continuous variables. Continuous variables were compared by analysis of variance (ANOVA). Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests were employed for comparing continuous variables that departed significantly from a normal distribution. Categorical variables were compared using a χ 2 test of general association.

Logistic regression models were developed to evaluate the association between audiometric hearing loss or self‐reported hearing impairment and prevalent dementia at baseline, controlling for other risk factors and potential confounders. Separate models were developed based on self‐reported hearing impairment and hearing loss by audiometry. Functional forms of the continuous variables age and HL were evaluated in the models as both linear terms and restricted cubic spline functions with selection of best‐fitting models based on Akaike Information Criterion (AIC).

Longitudinal analyses investigated risk factors for incident dementia. The longitudinal cohort included those with audiometry, without dementia by our algorithm on the baseline exam, and having at least one repeat examination. To improve internal validity, the longitudinal analyses were restricted to those ≤ 65 years of age at baseline, because a high proportion of participants older than 65 (26.6%) reported cognitive symptoms despite no dementia diagnosis [58]. This restriction helps ensure that participants were truly dementia‐free at entry. This cohort definition is consistent with a recent Lancet Commission report that found the greatest impact of hearing loss occurred among those 45–65 years of age [9]. The longitudinal models included the same set of risk factors considered in the cross‐sectional logistic models and a covariate to indicate use of hearing aids on baseline exam. Data from each BTMed examination were used; therefore, all covariates except sex, race/ethnicity, hearing aid use, and audiometry status were treated as time‐varying covariates with repeated determinations of dementia status at each exam. Once an individual developed dementia, all subsequent examinations from that individual were excluded from the analysis. We treated dementia onset as interval‐censored, occurring between the last dementia‐free exam and the first exam where dementia was detected.

We used the Statistical Analysis System (SAS) Interval‐Censored Proportional Hazards Regression procedure for proportional hazards modeling of interval‐censored data [59]. The assumption of linearity for the effects of age and HL was evaluated in the models by incorporation of restricted cubic spline functions with selection of best‐fitting models based on AIC. Best‐fitting parameterization of the baseline hazard function in the semi‐parametric model was also selected based on model AIC.

All statistical analyses used SAS/STAT 9.4 [59]. p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant and all statistical tests were two‐sided.

Human Subjects Protection

All study procedures and materials were reviewed and approved by the Central Department of Energy Institutional Review Board (CDOE IRB). All participants provided informed consent. The use of data for the current analyses was approved by the CDOE IRB (DOE001053, approved August 5, 2024).

As shown in Table 2, dementia prevalence increased significantly with increasing age with a rapid increase in prevalence among those older than 75 years of age (p < 0.001). No statistically significant differences were observed by gender, race/ethnicity, or smoking status; however, dementia prevalence was significantly associated with a history of diabetes (p = 0.0327), hypertension (p < 0.001), stroke (p < 0.001), cardiovascular disease (p < 0.001) and use of anticholinergic drugs (p < 0.0001). Obesity was found to be significantly associated with decreased prevalence of dementia (p < 0.0001). Both self‐reported hearing impairment and hearing loss by audiometry were significantly associated with increased prevalence of dementia in the univariate analyses (p < 0.001). Increasing severity of audiometric hearing loss was significantly associated with increasing dementia prevalence in the univariate analyses.

Multivariate analyses were conducted for self‐reported hearing impairment (Table 3) and audiometric testing‐based hearing loss (Table 4). Age was retained as a linear function in both models as restricted cubic spline functions for age did not improve model fit. Prevalent dementia was found to be significantly associated with self‐reported hearing impairment (OR = 1.47, 95% CI = 1.09–1.98) and hearing loss by audiometry (OR = 1.88, 95% CI = 1.15–3.07) after controlling for other risk factors and confounders. Additionally, the risk of dementia was found to increase with hearing impairment severity category although the trend by category was not statistically significant (p‐trend for hearing loss severity category = 0.11). Incorporation of HL as a continuous variable in the audiometry model found a significant trend with the risk of dementia, increasing ~11% for each additional 10 dB of hearing loss (OR = 1.11, 95% CI = 1.01–1.22). Entry of HL as a restricted cubic spline in the cross‐sectional model did not improve model fit by AIC criteria.

Incident Dementia Analysis

The dementia incidence analyses included 6933 individuals who completed 19,954 exams. The number of follow‐up exams by individual varied (1 follow‐up exam = 49.2%, 2 follow‐up exams = 25.8%, 3 follow‐up exams = 15.3%, > 3 follow‐up exams = 9.8%). The mean age at start of follow‐up was 53.4 years (standard deviation = 7.9) and 2819 (40.7%) of workers had audiometric hearing loss at baseline. The median follow‐up time was 9.9 years (interquartile range 5.5–14.8 years). Of the 80 incident dementia cases, 49 (61.3%) had hearing loss on their baseline exam.

The inclusion of baseline age in the proportional hazards model as a cubic spline function did not improve model fit. A discrete function for the baseline hazard was found to provide the best model fit [60]. Semi‐parametric proportional hazards model results are shown in Table 5. A history of diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, stroke, or definite use of anticholinergics was positively but not significantly associated with dementia incidence while being obese had a protective but not significant effect. The hazard ratio (HR) for incident dementia among those with hearing loss at baseline was 1.60 (95% CI = 0.99–2.59). The model found some evidence of a trend with increasing hearing loss severity (p‐trend = 0.0928) and inclusion of hearing loss as a continuous variable in the model found the risk of dementia to increase by ~10% for each 10 dB increase in HL (HR = 1.10, 95% CI = 0.93–1.31). Entry of HL as a restricted cubic spline function did not improve model fit. Only one incident case of dementia was observed among 357 individuals with hearing loss who reported using hearing aids at baseline. The small number of cases among hearing aid users resulted in an HR with high variability which precluded meaningful estimation of the magnitude of an apparent protective effect. However, hearing aid use is likely an effect modifier for the effect of hearing loss, as inclusion in the model reduced the HR for hearing loss among those with a HL of 35–49 dB by 5.9% and 20.4% among those with a HL ≥ 50 dB.

Discussion

The cross‐sectional analyses provide support for an association between audiometric hearing loss and dementia as well as self‐reported hearing impairment and dementia. Although audiometry is a more objective measure, the results from both methods are consistent with prior cross‐sectional studies [7, 61]. The observed protective effect of obesity is consistent with recent research that has shown obesity to be a risk factor for dementia during mid‐life but protective among older individuals [62].

The dementia incidence analyses also yielded a positive association with hearing loss by audiometry that was not statistically significant but was consistent in the trend with existing studies. The observed HR 1.60 (95% CI = 0.99–2.59) is slightly higher than the summary ORs from recent meta‐analyses of cohort studies (OR = 1.28, 95% CI = 1.02–1.59) [7]. The increased risk per 10 dB increase in hearing loss, 1.10 (95% CI = 0.93–1.31), is comparable to a result found in a comprehensive meta‐analysis (HR = 1.16, 95% CI = 1.07–1.27) [63]. The positive association found in this high‐risk occupational cohort is consistent with the 2024 Lancet Commission on Dementia Prevention which cites hearing loss as a major modifiable risk factor for dementia in the general population.

In addition to hearing loss, the relationship between definite anticholinergic use and dementia results was found to be significant in the cross‐sectional and positive but not significant in the analyses of incidence. The observed OR of 4.13 (95% CI = 2.64–6.46) and the HR of 2.26 (95% CI = 0.96–5.32) demonstrate the largest relationship to dementia observed for the clinical variables in this study. The association between definite anticholinergic drug use and incident dementia is consistent with a recent Cochrane review [44].

Anticholinergic drugs are used for the treatment of a variety of both acute and chronic conditions, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), and Parkinson's Disease, many of which have a significant impact on quality of life. As the scope of focus for this study did not allow for inclusion of these conditions in the statistical model, the measured relationship between anticholinergics and dementia may be partially or fully attributable to the underlying conditions being treated. However, given the extent of the measured relationship and corroborating literature, these findings merit further study as anticholinergics are widely prescribed for long‐term treatments, and the extent and mechanism of their relationship to dementia have yet to be fully explored.

Proton pump inhibitors (PPI) are regularly prescribed for long‐term treatment of gastrointestinal conditions such as acid reflux and stomach ulcers, and some cohort studies have shown evidence of an association between PPI use and dementia [64, 65, 66, 67, 68, 69], while others have not [70]. PPI medications were used by 10.1% of individuals in the longitudinal analyses. In a post hoc analysis, we investigated the effect of PPI medication use in the final longitudinal model. A covariate for use of PPI medications was found to be significantly associated with risk of incident dementia (HR = 1.72, 95% CI = 1.04–2.85). However, use of PPI medications was not a confounder for the association between hearing loss and dementia, as the HR for hearing loss changed negligibly (< 1%) with the PPI covariate in the model. As with the observed association with anticholinergics, some of the measured association between PPIs and dementia may be attributable to the presence of underlying medical conditions being treated. However, the scale of the association should prompt further investigation as these medications are widely prescribed and in some forms available over the counter.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths include a large high‐risk occupational cohort with hearing loss determined by audiometry as well as having data for many dementia risk factors and potential confounders identified in the literature. The longitudinal cohort also had a reasonable follow‐up time (median 9.9 years) after the baseline exam.

A limitation is that our dementia case definition was based on self‐reported diagnoses or the use of a specific list of medications for dementia treatment. This case definition is likely subject to underreporting and misclassification as a diagnosis of dementia often requires several years of observation and objective testing. A recent study of Americans with a cognitive impairment consistent with dementia assessed with a validated instrument found that 91.4% reported no physician dementia‐related diagnosis during a telephone interview [71]. Additionally, our inclusion of patients reporting more broad diagnoses, such as cognitive impairment or decline, selected to decrease the number of missed cases, could have led to the capture of very mild cases or false positives for dementia. However, our case definition was based on both self‐reported diagnoses and use of a prescribed set of medications, similarly used in other cohort studies and shown to improve case ascertainment [39, 72]. Second, audiometric tests were only performed on baseline BTMed exams and the relevance of the baseline audiometric data to incident dementia likely diminishes as follow‐up time increases, especially in this older population at increased risk of progression to hearing loss due to advancing age. Increasing incidence and severity of hearing loss over cohort follow‐up without repeat audiometric assessment likely results in some cases of misclassification of hearing loss, dampening the associations observed in this study. Lastly, information on hearing aid use was only available for all cohort members on the baseline exam and the possibility of increased use over follow‐up exists. Increasing hearing aid use over study follow‐up also would likely dampen the effects of hearing loss in our analyses.

This study population is limited in its generalizability to the general population due to the primarily white, male cohort composed of predominantly construction workers. However, given our focus on occupational noise exposure, this demographic profile is reflective of the demographic trend in many of the highest risk industries for hearing loss, such as construction work, manufacturing, and mining [73]. There is also the potential for residual confounding from socioeconomic or educational factors that were not measured in‐depth for this cohort. Because our cohort of predominantly construction workers is relatively homogeneous socioeconomically and educationally, the likelihood of significant confounding by these factors would appear small.

It is possible that hearing loss was misdiagnosed at entry in participants who had cognitive impairment at baseline, if this affected their ability to perform the test correctly. It is unlikely that this problem affected our incident dementia analysis, because any measured hearing loss preceded measured dementia in these cases. Compared to the general population, our cohort had greater occupational noise exposure and likely earlier hearing loss development, further reducing the likelihood that dementia would have preceded and contributed to the hearing loss.

Our estimates of the association between speech‐frequency hearing loss and dementia found the cross‐sectional estimate (OR = 1.88) to be higher than the estimate based on incidence (HR = 1.60). This observation is consistent with prior research which found higher rates of age‐related cognitive decline in cross‐sectional versus longitudinal analyses [74]. While cross‐sectional studies may suffer bias due to participation rates, longitudinal studies may be biased due to nonparticipation in follow‐up assessments (dropout) and death during follow‐up, both of which may be especially important in older populations [74]. Unfortunately, these two factors are not independent of cognitive function at baseline. In our study, a covariate for self‐reported memory loss was found to be significantly associated with incident dementia when inserted in our final model (HR = 2.42, 95% CI = 1.52–3.84). Additionally, those with memory loss at baseline had a greater probability of death or dropout during follow‐up, defined as not having at least one follow‐up exam (48.3% without memory loss vs. 52.3% with memory loss). These observations suggest that our incidence analyses may have underestimated the risk associated with hearing loss.

Implications

This study adds to the literature on the association between hearing loss and dementia using both cross‐sectional and longitudinal study designs. Cross‐sectional findings support an association between occupational hearing loss and dementia, whereas longitudinal estimates were directionally similar but did not reach statistical significance, consistent in direction with reports for age‐related hearing loss. These results need confirmation in other occupational high‐risk cohorts using prospective cohort designs with audiometry repeated at defined intervals over follow‐up. Further research is needed to determine whether dementia should be considered a compensable condition secondary to occupational noise‐induced hearing loss. Our results, if confirmed, suggest that dementia should be considered an occupational disease of concern among those with excessive noise exposure. Potential compensability will depend on jurisdictional considerations, such as whether the applicable workers' compensation system recognizes “consequential conditions” arising as a result of other accepted conditions, and causation thresholds that apply. Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) standards require establishment of Hearing Conservation Programs, including annual hearing tests and provision of hearing protection, whenever employee noise exposures equal or exceed an 8‐h time‐weighted average sound level of 85 dB [75]. However, literature estimates that about 47% of noise‐exposed workers do not use hearing protection [76]. Additionally, recent studies offer estimates ranging from 9.7% to 29.2% for the percentage of individuals with hearing loss utilizing hearing aid devices, suggesting that over two‐thirds of individuals with clinical hearing loss do not currently use hearing assistance [1, 61, 77].

This study has both individual and system‐level implications. The risk of dementia as a complication of hearing loss may persuade employers and workers to invest more in protecting against hearing loss. Individuals with hearing loss may be more likely to use hearing aids if evidence continues to support this intervention as one that can protect cognitive health. Consistent hearing aid use, especially for high‐risk populations, has been found in recent studies to offer up to a 19% reduction in dementia risk [78, 79, 80]. This protective effect aligns with prevailing theories for the link between hearing loss and cognitive decline as hearing aid use allows for reduced effort of hearing, greater engagement with auditory stimulation, and improved social connectedness [78]. However, hearing aids are not broadly covered by health insurance in the United States. Dementia care is costly with estimates of the annual cost of dementia care around $23,796–$56,290 per person with dementia, compared to the average cost of hearing aids which range from around $2000–$7000 [81, 82]. Further evidence of the link between hearing loss and dementia supports the argument for improving health insurance coverage of hearing aids to reduce downstream healthcare expenses. However, it is also important to acknowledge that improvements in insurance coverage of hearing aids may lead to a reduction in workers' compensation claims for hearing loss due to reduced reliance on the workers' compensation system for this benefit. This effect has the potential to create a reduction in reports of work‐related hearing loss not indicative of improved occupational protection. If confirmed, our results may also provide evidence for dementia as a secondary (also called “consequential”) work injury from occupational noise‐exposure hearing loss, eligible for workers' compensation coverage of dementia care costs.

Our study includes a cohort with a high risk for noise‐induced hearing loss but does not exclude those with age‐related hearing loss. To better understand differences in cognitive impacts of noise‐induced versus age‐related hearing loss, future studies should examine differences in dementia incidence and risk between these two different hearing loss populations. Additionally, because this study was unable to directly analyze the protective effect of hearing aid use due to sample size limitations, future research should further examine the degree of protective effect of hearing aid use against dementia development in noise‐induced hearing loss.

Conclusions

Hearing loss has been identified as a significant risk factor for dementia with great potential for prevention [9]. Carefully designed studies aimed at prevention of dementia through reduction in occupational noise exposures should be conducted. If confirmed, the findings of this study support the need to (1) emphasize access to hearing aids in medical benefits in both personal and workers' compensation insurance and (2) anticipate covering claims for subsequent dementia associated with accepted hearing loss claims in workers' compensation.
