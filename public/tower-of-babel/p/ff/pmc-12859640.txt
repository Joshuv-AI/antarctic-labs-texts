A viscosity-activable nano-micelle with aggregation-induced emission for boosting NIR-II fluorescence imaging-guided synergetic photodynamic/photothermal therapy of breast cancer
Authors: Wenwen Cai, Jinyin Ge, Jie Dong, Xinyu Guo, Bo Fan, Qian Wang, Peng Yang, Dongqiang Guo, Dong Wang, Ben Zhong Tang, Ruiping Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In view of the fast-growing achievements of cancer phototheranostics, the exploration of advanced materials shows inexhaustible and vigorous vitality. To overcome those limitations of existing materials, such as aggregation-caused fluorescence quenching, inferior selectivity, and high O2-dependence, the utilization of specific activatable agents with aggregation-induced emission (AIE) features is highly desirable yet remains challenging. Herein, we have designed an organic small-molecule microenvironment activated AIE agent, namely DPXBI. After encapsulation into amphiphilic DSPE-PEG-RGD through self-assembly, the biocompatibility, water solubility, targeting effect, and circulation time in the blood of obtained nano-micelle RM@DPXBI are improved. RM@DPXBI showed powerful fluorescence in the second near-infrared (NIR-II) region after being activated by viscosity in the tumor microenvironment, which could accurately identify the boundary of the tumor and determine the optimal accumulation time for following phototherapy. Subsequently, RM@DPXBI exerts inhibiting on breast cancer growth through synergistic photodynamic (PDT) and photothermal (PTT) therapy under single laser irradiation. Meanwhile, the phototherapy-induced cellular death increases intracellular viscosity, which further restricts the intramolecular motion of RM@DPXBI, resulting in the amplifying of PDT effect. The activated strategy provides a new exploration for the design and development of AIE and viscosity activation agents in tumor theranostics.

Introduction

Phototherapy is nowadays growing into a sparkling frontier in cancer treatment compared to traditional approaches such as surgery, radiotherapy and chemotherapy [[1], [2], [3]], by virtue of its various intrinsic advantages including noninvasiveness, minimal side toxicity, low medication resistance, as well as the dual-selectivity offered by spatiotemporal manipulation and optical control [4]. The two most common phototherapies are photodynamic (PDT) and photothermal (PTT) therapy, which utilize exogenous or endogenous photosensitizers to generate cytotoxic reactive oxygen species (ROS) or enhance local temperature at the target tissue upon light irradiation, respectively [5]. Despite sharing technical similarities and possessing safe, effective local tumor eradication, PDT and PTT each have inherent limitations that require comprehensive considerations to ensure optimal therapeutic effect.

PTT refers to the utilization of both photothermal agents and laser irradiation to achieve protein denaturation and cell apoptosis or cell necrosis. It has been demonstrated that PTT-mediated tumor ablation is characterized by significant limitations, including the non-selectivity damage to the adjacent tissues, inadequacy tumor aggregation, insufficient temperature for tumoricidal tissue and the safety and logistical concerns from high-energy laser irradiation [6]. Moreover, the intracellular heat shock protein (HSP) undergoes upregulation during the heat shock response as a heat resistance mechanism to protect and restore homeostasis [7]. In case of PDT, the key therapeutic factor, ROS, is the output of highly O2-dependent PSs following a series of photophysical and photochemical processes [8]. The primary concern revolves around the hypoxic-tumor microenvironment, which would be a hindrance for ROS generation during the PDT procedure. While employing O2-carrying systems to deliver exogenous O2 into the tumor area in situ to alleviate hypoxia, may paradoxically facilitate cancer cell proliferation and inhibit cell apoptosis simultaneously [9]. It has been recognized that the synergetic implement of PDT and PTT may achieve complementary shortcomings and improve safety and efficacy [10,11]. PTT-induced heat can enhance blood circulation, potentially boosting the tumor's oxygen levels to facilitate PDT. Conversely, the ROS generated from PDT can impair the functionality of HSP, thereby increasing tumor cells' susceptibility to heat [12]. The integration of PTT and PDT merits offers a synergistic strategy for enhanced cancer treatment, advancing the field of theranostics [13,14].

Traditional PSs are beset by aggregation-caused quenching (ACQ), which leads to diminished fluorescence intensity and inferior ROS generation efficiencies when forming nano-aggregates [15]. Restricted intramolecular motion (RIM) refers to the constrained internal motions (e.g., rotation and vibration) of molecules in the aggregated state. This phenomenon is responsible for the AIE effect, as it eliminates non-radiative decay pathways and preserves excitation energy, thereby enhancing fluorescence [16]. Owing to these properties—including anti-quenching, boosted emission, and enhanced ROS generation in the aggregate state—the AIE strategy leveraging the RIM mechanism is regarded as a promising tool for amplifying PDT sensitization [[17], [18], [19]]. Recently, AIE PSs, demonstrating synergistic advantages in both high-efficiency photothermal conversion and robust ROS generation, have been developed and applied for cancer cell imaging and phototherapy [[20], [21], [22]]. Specifically activatable AIE PSs, harnessing the abnormally upregulated biomarkers or the tumor microenvironment, enable an on-demand regulation of ROS generation [23]. Upon specific biomarker response, the PS could transform from a quenched state to an intensified state, or from an inert to an active one, which significantly mitigates the risk of indiscriminate accumulation, thus preventing collateral damage to normal tissue while preserving the potent antitumor efficacy against tumor cells [24,25]. Intracellular viscosity serves as a critical biophysical parameter within the tumor microenvironment (TME), intricately intertwined in the myriad of multitudinous cellular processes encompassing signal transduction, biological species diffusion, chemical signal propagation, protein aggregation, metabolite diffusion, and so on [26,27]. It's also a potential biomarker for metastatic cancer cells [28]. Meanwhile, the condensation of cytoplasm and cell shrinkage during cell death would further induce an increase cytoplasmic viscosity [29]. To date, a series of small-molecule fluorophores have been applied to detect intracellular viscosity in a variety of diseases [30,31]. A growing repertoire of fluorescent probes engineered for precise mapping of microenvironmental viscosity have been reported [32,33]. Most of them are visible and NIR-I window fluorescent materials, which limit tissue penetration depth, and it is difficult to achieve the early evaluation of cancer diagnosis and therapeutic efficacy in vivo. The emerging second near-infrared fluorescence imaging (NIR-II FI) technology, due to its high penetration depth into biological tissues, greatly reduced autofluorescence, and improved the signal-to-noise ratio perfectly to solve the above difficulties [[34], [35], [36]]. Currently, AIE PSs with NIR-II emission are explored mostly in the form of small molecules, which exhibit precise molecular architectures, versatile chemical tunability, good biocompatible, minimal side effects, and facile metabolism [37]. Its limited water solubility, reduced selectivity and targeting, and limitation of cellular uptake vastly hinder the adoption and application in biomedicine [38,39]. The new generation of nano-photosensitizers, featuring prolonged blood circulation and tumor-specific accumulation, addresses these limitations while enhancing functionality [[40], [41], [42]].

Herein, we use DSPE-PEG-RGD as a matrix to encapsulate DPXBI to form nano-micelle (RM@DPXBI) through a self-assembly strategy for NIR-II FI-guided synergistic PTT and PDT therapy. Scheme 1A illustrated the synthetic pathway of RM@DPXBI. DPXBI, an organic small-molecule AIE PS, emits strong NIR-II fluorescence signal after activated by the physicochemical property of viscosity in the TME due to the RIM (Fig. S1). DSPE-PEG-RGD, a biocompatible and amphiphilic polymer with non-immunogenic and tumor-targeting properties, is used to encapsulate hydrophobic DPXBI, forming RM@DPXBI nano-micelles that enhance systemic circulation by reducing aggregation, opsonization, and phagocytosis [[43], [44], [45]]. RM@DPXBI exhibits robust absorbance within the NIR region (700–900 nm), accompanied by a fluorescence peak centered at 928 nm that extends up to 1200 nm. As shown in Scheme 1B, the nano-micelle also possesses AIE, viscosity-activation characteristics, and ROS generation, thereby endowing them with excellent NIR-II FI, PTT, and PDT capability. It's worth noting that the efficacy of PDT is significantly enhanced through a cascade effect. The high viscosity in the TME enhances PDT effectiveness. The combination of PTT and PDT induces tumor cell apoptosis, which further increased viscosity, resulting in a self-reinforcing positive feedback loop. RM@DPXBI enhances the targeted accumulation at the tumor site through the capability of both active and passive targeting, long circulation in blood, and viscosity activation to precisely shape the boundary of the tumor. Results and discussion

The synthesis and characterization of the RM@DPXBI

The viscosity-activated and AIE small molecular dye DPXBI was constructed through the double bond linkage of a benzindole sulfonate skeleton functioning dually as an electron-accepting moiety, a molecular rotor and an electron-donating diphenyl xanthene group [46]. DSPE-PEG-RGD was utilized as amphiphilic copolymers for the loading of insoluble DPXBI to form water-soluble and targeted nano-micelle structure by self-assembly. The outer spherical structure was primarily composed of self-assembled DSPE-PEG-RGD, while DPXBI constituted the inner core matrix. As depicted in Fig. S2, the encapsulation efficiency of DPXBI reached 91.33 % at a ratio of 1:5 (DPXBI: RGD-DSPE-PEG), which is significantly higher than the efficiency observed at a ratio of 1:1 (61.67 %). Besides, the latter possessed a larger, supramolecular aggregates, a population of poorly formed micelles and higher PDI value, which rendered it unsuitable for further development. Furthermore, the drug loading capacity of RM@DPXBI, synthesized at a 1:5 ratio, was measured to be 9.87 %.

As displayed in the transmission electron microscope (TEM) image, the RM@DPXBI nano-micelles exhibited a spherical morphology, featuring an average diameter of approximately 46.8 nm (Fig. 1A, Fig. S3). The average hydrodynamic diameter (Dh) of RM@DPXBI, as determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS) in an aqueous solution, was 58.8 nm, exhibiting excellent dispersibility with a PDI of 0.279 (Fig. 1B). This favorable dispersion enables RM@DPXBI to actively and passively accumulate at the tumor sites. RM@DPXBI exhibited excellent stability over a 7-day period in PBS, DMEM, and DMEM supplemented with 10 % FBS, with no observed precipitation, aggregation, color changes or change of hydrodynamic diameter (Fig. S4 and S5). These results confirmed its robust solubility and long-term stability under physiologically relevant conditions. The critical micelle concentration (CMC), a key indicator of micellar stability, was determined to be 7.14 μg/mL for RM@DPXBI. This low value indicates its high stability and resistance to dilution in the systemic circulation (Fig. S6). The zeta potential of RM@DPXBI was −29 mV. Optical response of RM@DPXBI to viscosity

The AIE characteristic of DPXBI was examined in the DMSO and toluene mixed solvents with different fractions ranging from 0 to 50 %. As shown in Fig. 1D, fully solvated DMSO solutions of DPXBI demonstrated attenuated fluorescence signal in the NIR-II window. The emission wavelength enhanced gradually within the increasing toluene fraction, definitely indicating a typical AIE behavior for non-destructive monitoring. The absolute NIR-II QY of ᴿM@DPXBI was 6.4 % using an integrating sphere method. The aqueous solution of RM@DPXBI exhibited a NIR-II fluorescence emission (peak = 928 nm), as the concentration was increased from 0 to 200 μg mL−1 under an excitation power of 2.0 W (Fig. 1E), which may effectively minimize background fluorescence and facilitate deep-tissue imaging for biological applications. The viscosity-activation characteristic of RM@DPXBI was examined in the ethanol-glycerol simulated viscosity conditions. The NIR-II region fluorescence emission of RM@DPXBI generally rose within the increasing solvent viscosity, indicating a characteristic of viscosity activation and respondent, which could be attributed to the mechanism of intramolecular charge transfer (TICT) and integration of molecular rotor leading to fluorescence intensity increase (Fig. 1F) [47]. Besides, a fluorescence emission peak at 764 nm was observed by xenon lamp excited (Fig. S7). Similarly, the fluorescence emission intensity also showed significant viscosity-activation characteristics.

Subsequently, the selectivity and specificity of RM@DPXBI were evaluated by recording its reactions with various ROS, reactive nitrogen species (RNS), metal ions, polarity solvents, and proteins under simulated physiological conditions. Encouragingly, all of these ROS, RNS, metal ions, proteins, and solvents caused no significant fluorescence signal changes except for glycerol (Fig. 1G), demonstrating that RM@DPXBI had excellent selectivity and specificity for viscosity. Furthermore, the fluorescence intensity of RM@DPXBI showed no obvious fluctuation in PBS buffer under varying pH conditions (pH 4.0−9.0), confirming pH stability for in vivo applications (Fig. 1H). Hence, RM@DPXBI possesses strong anti-interference ability against complex environments.

1O2 generation and the evaluation of photothermal conversion efficiency

DCFH was used as an indicator of total ROS, which itself had a weak fluorescence signal [48]. In the presence of RM@DPXBI and laser irradiation, the mixture occurred a rapidly increased fluorescence signal and noticeable changes. As the irradiation time progressed, the intensity of the fluorescence signal at 525 nm gradually escalated, positively correlating with the production of total ROS (Fig. 2A and S8). Commercial DPBF as a ROS indicator can be oxidized by 1O2 accompanied by a time-dependent attenuation of absorption spectrum. In the common conditions, the absorption intensity at 410 nm was progressively diminished with prolonged irradiation duration, in sharp contrast to the nearly changed DPBF itself absorption under laser irradiation (Fig. S9). Both the DPBF solution containing RM@DPXBI in ethanol and that in glycerol generated 1O2 upon laser irradiation, and each showed statistically significant differences compared to the DPBF-only group. Although the difference between the ethanol and glycerol group was not statistically significant, the glycerol-simulated viscosity group exhibited a noticeable increase in 1O2 production, with an average enhancement of approximately 61.7 % (Fig. 2B and C). The PDT efficacy of RM@DPXBI was enhanced with the occurrence of viscosity response, which could be attributed to the aggregation-induced intersystem crossing (AI-ISC). The high-viscosity induced a hydrophilic-to-hydrophobic transition in the RM@DPXBI molecules and paved them to form aggregates. Encouraged by the long wavelength absorption, the photo-thermal capability and photo-stability of RM@DPXBI were recorded through the real-time temperature variation in aqueous solution. As revealed in Fig. 2D and E, the temperature of RM@DPXBI solution was markedly elevated compared to deionized water during 808 nm laser irradiation within 5 min (1.0 W/cm2). As expected, the temperature was gradually elevated when the concentration of RM@DPXBI increased from 50 to 400 μg/mL. The temperature of RM@DPXBI (100 μg/mL) rapidly increased by approximately 27 °C within 5 min after irradiation, sufficient to create a hyperthermia environment for damaging cancer cells. In addition, the laser power-dependent (0.6–1.2 W/cm2) temperature increase was depicted in Fig. 2F and G. More importantly, after five on/off cycles of NIR laser irradiation, the RM@DPXBI had no considerable temperature drop, which retained virtually intact photothermal stability (Fig. 2H). The photothermal conversion efficiency of RM@DPXBI was calculated through the heating and cooling curves within continuous laser irradiation. The linear time data and –lnθ were exhibited in Fig. 2I and the value of photothermal conversion efficiency (η) was to be about 34.77 %. These findings unequivocally demonstrate that the RM@DPXBI exhibits quite high robust colloidal photothermal capability and stability, thereby being favorable to promoting PTT-mediated anticancer efficacy.

PDT/PTT-mediated cytotoxicity analysis and anti-cancer effect in vitro

The cytotoxicity and anticancer efficacy of nanomaterials are crucial indicators for assessing the potential for further utilization in biological applications [49]. As shown in Fig. 3A, 4T1 cells exposed solely to RM@DPXBI (0 to 200 μg/mL) retained over 95 % viability. When subjected to the same concentrations and 5 min of the 808 nm laser irradiation, the cell viability exhibited a clear dose-dependent cytotoxicity behavior. The cytotoxicity implied that 808 nm laser-mediated phototherapy strategy significantly enhanced the lethal effect on cancer cells. Subsequently, the cellular internalization capability of RM@DPXBI was conducted in 4T1 cells. PDT/PTT-mediated anticancer effect assessment in vitro was further elaborated through the live/dead staining, mitochondrial membrane potential, and intracellular ROS generation using a fluorescence microscope (Fig. 3C and S10B-D). In the control groups comprising PBS, PBS with laser, and solely the RM@DPXBI, the cells were successfully stained with Calcein AM and emitted a vivid green fluorescence, indicating viable and metabolically active cells. Conversely, nearly all cells subjected to RM@DPXBI and laser irradiation were stained with PI and emitted intense red fluorescence, suggesting damage to the cell structure. Subsequently, the mitochondrial membrane potential was conducted to evaluate the cellular apoptosis by the cationic dye JC-1. Compared to control groups, 4T1 cells within RM@DPXBI and laser irradiation resulted in a significant reduction in a marked fluorescence shift from red to green (elevated JC-1 monomer ratio), signifying mitochondrial membrane depolarization associated with apoptotic processes. DCFH-DA inherently has no fluorescence but emits bright green fluorescence when triggered by endogenous ROS to formation 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein (DCF). There was no prominent ROS generation in the control groups, indicating that RM@DPXBI cannot be activated to produce ROS without NIR laser irradiation. While 4T1 cells treated with RM@DPXBI and NIR laser irradiation displayed green fluorescence, verified that RM@DPXBI has a high ROS generation capacity after irradiation in vitro. Apoptosis quantification was further conducted using Annexin V-FITC/PI dual staining followed by flow cytometric analysis. The cells treated with both RM@DPXBI and laser group exhibited elevated levels of cell apoptosis, reaching approximately 39 %, which is higher than another groups (Fig. 3D). Above mentioned assessments in vitro implied the high photothermal conversion efficiency and ROS generation ability of RM@DPXBI, thereby validating the PDT/PTT-mediated anticancer effect in vitro, which holds promise for further tumor theranostic applications in vivo.

In vivo NIR-II fluorescence imaging

After the evaluation of the anticancer properties of RM@DPXBI at the cellular level, the study of the tumor imaging and treatment performance in vivo was carried out in 4T1 tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 4A). To explore the viscosity activation performance of RM@DPXBI, the NIR-II FI images of tumor-bearing mice were obtained at different times (10 min, 2 h, 6 h, 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h) after intra-tumoral injection. As shown in Fig. 4B, the tumor in mice was detected in NIR-II fluorescence imaging. The tumor site fluorescence signal gradually increased due to the viscosity activated and response compared to 10 min and reached the highest at 6 h post-injection. The quantitative assessment of NIR-II fluorescence signal intensity is illustrated in Fig. S11. To explore the optimal phototherapy time, the NIR-II fluorescence images of tumor-bearing mice were obtained at different times (pre, 2 h, 6 h, 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h) after intravenous injection. The peek intensity within the tumor site occurred 24 h post-injection, which could be attached to both active and passive targeting (EPR effect), and response process to viscosity microenvironment (Fig. 4C and S12). To validate the active targeting capability of the RGD peptide in RM@DPXBI, M@DPXBI was intravenously administered the tumor-bearing mice via tail vein and acquired NIR-II fluorescence images at same time points (Fig. S13). Despite reaching the tumor site via the EPR effect with accumulation peaking at 24 h, M@DPXBI exhibited a significantly lower fluorescence intensity in tumor than the RM@DPXBI group. PTT/PDT-mediated phototherapy in vivo

Building on the favorable biodistribution of RM@DPXBI following intravenous administration, the synergetic PTT/PDT-mediated phototherapy effects were assessed with an 808 nm laser (1.0 W cm−2, 5 min) at 24 h post-injection. 4T1 tumor-bearing mice were allocated to four groups: PBS, PBS with laser irradiation, RM@DPXBI, and RM@DPXBI with laser irradiation (n = 5 per group). As depicted in Fig. 4D, the infrared thermal images demonstrated that the temperature at the tumor site treated with RM@DPXBI and laser irradiation rapidly soared above 47.9 °C within just 1 min, and almost subsequently plateaued at around 53 °C at 3–5 min post-irradiation. Compared to the above group, the tumor site temperature of mice treated with PBS and laser increased by only around 3 °C. The quantitative analysis at the tumor site is displayed in Fig. S14.

The PDT/PTT-mediated phototherapy efficacy in vivo was studied by the measurement of relative tumor volume and body weight. The body weight of control group mice gradually decreased, primarily attributed to physical cachexia stemming from uninhibited tumors. In contrast, the body weight of therapeutic group mice showed a slight increase, potentially indicative of bodily recovery after the tumor suppression (Fig. 5A). Compared with another groups, the mice treated with both RM@DPXBI and laser showed a suppressive effect on tumor growth, with no recurrence throughout the entire observation period (Fig. 5B). The images of extracted tumor tissues and tumor-bearing mice both showed the convincingly suppressive effect of PDT/PTT-mediated phototherapy by RM@DPXBI (Fig. 5C, D and S15). To further substantiate the detrimental therapeutic effects caused by RM@DPXBI on tumors, H&E, TUNNEL, and Ki67 staining assays were conducted. As shown in Fig. 5E and S16, the tumor sections demonstrated that the majority of tumor cells in the treatment group had severe damage, upregulation of TUNEL, and downregulation of Ki67 expression. The fluorescence quantitative analysis of Ki67 in the experimental group and control groups is shown in Fig. S17. Conversely, tumor cells in the control group still maintained their regular physiological morphology, which aligns well with the above findings. Biosafety in vivo

Hemolytic assay was quantified as a prerequisite for in vivo application. As shown in Fig. S18, the hemolysis rate of the highest concentration (200 μg mL−1) was 4.76 ± 0.24 %, which ensured blood compatibility and safe biological applications.

To further examine the in vivo safety of RM@DPXBI, tissue and hematologic specimens were harvested for analysis 24 h post-intravenous injection with RM@DPXBI. All tested indices (WBC, Lym, Neu, RBC, HGB, PLT, ALT, AST, ALB, BUN, CREA, UA) related to blood routine and biochemical remained within normal ranges, indicating the absence of acute hematological toxicity (Fig. 6A). In addition, no significant morphological alterations, tissue damage, or abnormal reactions of major organs in the RM@DPXBI group compared to the normal group (Fig. 6B). To investigate the metabolic pathway of RM@DPXBI in vivo, major organs were collected from mice at designated time intervals following tail vein administration, and subsequently analyzed for NIR-II fluorescence signals. As shown in Fig. S19, the fluorescence signal in the liver peaked at 6 h post-injection and gradually decreased thereafter. Notably, clear gallbladder imaging was also detected, suggesting that the majority of RM@DPXBI particles were metabolized via the liver and excreted into the intestines through bile, ultimately being excreted in the form of feces. Additionally, weak fluorescence signals were observed in the kidneys, indicating that smaller RM@DPXBI particles may also be cleared through renal filtration and excreted via urine.

Conclusion

In summary, a single NIR light-triggered multifunctional self-assembly nano-micelle, RM@DPXBI, was engineered for tumor theranostics. The nano-micelle exhibited AIE effect, viscosity activation, superior biocompatibility, high photostability, anti-interference ability, and good tumor targeting properties. Under NIR light excitation, RM@DPXBI could serve as a NIR-II FI agent to offer accurate tumor information and describe tumor with high selectivity and imaging resolution, overcoming the ACQ quenching effect. In addition, the PDT effect of RM@DPXBI could be amplified in tumor environment, contributing to its excellent viscosity activation ability through RIM. The integration of enhanced PDT and PTT mediated anticancer efficiency surpasses the limitations posed by the insufficient therapeutic effects of single treatment modalities. This research provides a new exploration for the design and development of AIE and viscosity activation agents in tumor theranostics, further promoting the engineering of nano-platforms tailored for intravital biological imaging and cancer theranostics.
