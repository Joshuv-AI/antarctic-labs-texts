Bootstrap-based K-means feature selection strategy for fuzzy regression functions
Authors: Aylin Ucan, Dogan Yildiz, Nihat Tak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recent years, the increasing complexity of datasets has highlighted the limitations of traditional feature selection methods and motivated the search for more stable and robust alternatives. This study proposes a bootstrap-stabilized feature selection method based on the k-means clustering algorithm, where features are repeatedly grouped, and stable representative features are identified across multiple resamples. In the proposed approach, each feature is assigned to its nearest cluster center, and the feature closest to that center is selected as the representative, reducing sensitivity to random initialization and improving generalizability. The selected features are subsequently integrated into a Fuzzy Regression Function (FRF) model, forming a hybrid framework that combines interpretable feature selection with flexible fuzzy regression modeling. The method is evaluated on ten real-world datasets and compared with widely used feature selection techniques, including Correlation based Feature Selection (CFS), Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE), Genetic Algorithm (GA) based Feature Selection, LASSO, and Ridge regression. In the proposed framework, the number of feature clusters was primarily determined using a grid-search strategy, while the Silhouette Index (SI) and Davies Bouldin Index (DBI) were additionally considered as alternative cluster validity criteria for k-means based feature selection. These findings demonstrate that the proposed bootstrap-stabilized clustering approach achieved either superior or competitive performance across the ten datasets considered, highlighting its effectiveness and suitability for complex data environments.Keywords: Feature selection, k-means clustering, Bootstrap, Regression, Fuzzy functionsSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Many contemporary datasets contain large numbers of features and often display complex structures, making it difficult for regression models to perform reliably without some form of dimensionality reduction. Feature selection has therefore become an essential step in modern data analysis, helping to remove irrelevant or redundant features, reduce model complexity, and improve predictive accuracy1. By focusing on the most informative attributes, feature selection methods also mitigate overfitting and enhance interpretability, which is increasingly important in applied machine learning.

A wide range of feature selection strategies has been proposed in the literature. Comprehensive reviews in the literature1–3 categorize these techniques into filter, wrapper, embedded, hybrid, and ensemble approaches. Filter methods select features based on statistical criteria and operate independently of the final predictive model4. Wrapper methods evaluate subsets of features using a trained model, often achieving good performance but at the cost of increased computation5. Embedded approaches, such as those used in LASSO6 and Ridge regression7, perform feature selection during model training and exploit algorithm specific regularization structures8. Hybrid methods combine complementary aspects of filters and wrappers to balance efficiency and accuracy3, while ensemble based feature selection aims to aggregate multiple feature subsets to improve robustness9. Despite their widespread use, these methods may struggle when the feature space contains highly correlated features, leading to instability in the selected subsets or unnecessary redundancy.

As an alternative, clustering based feature selection has gained attention due to its simplicity and scalability. In particular, k-means clustering offers a straightforward way to group similar features and choose a representative from each cluster. However, classical k-means suffers from sensitivity to initialization and may produce inconsistent results across different runs, especially in high-dimensional or noisy settings. Bootstrap resampling provides a natural mechanism to improve stability by aggregating information across repeated partitions of the feature space. Although bootstrap techniques have been successfully applied in various machine learning contexts, their use within clustering driven feature selection has received limited attention.

To address these issues, we propose a bootstrap-stabilized k-means feature selection algorithm that repeatedly clusters bootstrap-resampled datasets and identifies features that consistently appear nearest to their cluster centers. This approach reduces the randomness inherent in k-means and yields a more reliable and interpretable subset of representative features. The selected features are then incorporated into a Fuzzy Regression Function (FRF) model, originally introduced by10, which provides a flexible framework for modeling uncertainty through membership based weighting. By employing bootstrap-based clustering in the FRF modelling structure, we aim to construct a feature selection and regression framework that is both interpretable and robust. The bootstrap mechanism stabilizes the k-means step, while the FRF model captures local variations in the relationship between predictors and the response feature. The resulting hybrid methodology reduces the dimensionality of the problem, lowers the computational cost of FRF parameter tuning, and improves overall predictive performance. In this study, the proposed method is evaluated on multiple real-world datasets and compared with widely used feature selection techniques to demonstrate its stability and effectiveness.

Literature survey

This section reviews several methodologies proposed in the literature that integrate feature selection and clustering strategies, particularly k-means based approaches, to improve clustering effectiveness.

Hancer et al.11 presented a comprehensive survey on feature selection approaches for clustering, systematically reviewing existing unsupervised and evolutionary computation based methods while highlighting their strengths, limitations, and potential future research directions. Alimoussa et al.12 proposed a clustering based sequential feature selection framework that removes irrelevant features, groups redundant ones through clustering, and sequentially selects representative features, demonstrating effective and efficient performance compared with benchmark methods. Xu et al.13 proposed a multi-scale supervised clustering based feature selection algorithm (MCBFS) for genomic data analysis, demonstrating robust and effective performance in gene selection, classification, and biomarker identification compared with benchmark methods. Zhu et al.14 proposed a joint spectral clustering framework with embedded feature selection and graph optimization, achieving superior clustering performance and improved robustness against redundant features and noise. Wang et al.15 proposed a feature clustering assisted differential evolution based feature selection method that identifies multiple optimal feature subsets and improves classification accuracy while reducing feature subset size. Mei et al.16 proposed a cooperative hybrid breeding swarm intelligence algorithm for feature selection, demonstrating superior convergence speed, classification accuracy, and feature subset stability on high dimensional benchmark datasets. Tripathi et al.17 proposed a scalable distributed Laplacian Score framework for unsupervised feature selection, significantly improving computational efficiency and scalability while preserving the original method’s accuracy and interpretability. Dwivedi et al.18 presented a taxonomy of unsupervised feature selection methods, categorizing existing approaches and discussing their advantages, limitations, and practical challenges . Recent studies have also emphasized the importance of clustering quality evaluation through validity measures such as the Silhouette Index (SI) and Davies Bouldin Index (DBI). For example, Dwivedi et al.19 proposed a scalable alignment free feature extraction framework for genomic data clustering by incorporating chemical property based entropy measures into clustering analysis . In another study, Dwivedi et al.20 developed a multi-objective incremental clustering framework for dynamic data analysis by integrating inter cluster distance, intra cluster distance, and cluster density within a weighted optimization structure.

Based on the above literature, it can be observed that existing clustering based feature selection approaches mainly focus on optimization quality, scalability, or clustering performance. However, an important limitation remains in k-means based representative feature selection. Since k-means relies on random initialization of cluster centers, representative features selected in a single run may vary significantly across different executions, reducing the stability and robustness of the selected feature subset.

To address this limitation, this study proposes a bootstrap-stabilized k-means feature selection framework, where k-means clustering is repeatedly applied to bootstrap-resampled datasets and representative features are aggregated across multiple iterations. In this way, the proposed method aims to improve the stability of representative feature selection while preserving the simplicity and scalability advantages of k-means clustering. The selected features are subsequently integrated into the FRF modeling framework to improve predictive performance under uncertainty. FRF models have been successfully applied in a variety of regression problems27–35, and have been further enhanced through regularization techniques such as Ridge based FRF36–38 and Elastic Net FRF39. These studies demonstrate that FRF models can effectively capture nonlinear interactions and graded transitions in noisy environments.

Proposed method

The primary goal of the proposed method is to form a meaningful subset of features by grouping similar ones using the k-means clustering algorithm. Unlike conventional clustering based feature selection, the proposed approach employs a bootstrap-based repeated k-means strategy to alleviate sensitivity to random initialization. By selecting the most consistently observed representatives across multiple resampled runs, the method enhances robustness against randomness and improves the stability of the selected feature subset. In this way, the preprocessing phase of the prediction model is simplified, and its generalizability, interpretability, and predictive performance are improved. The k-means clustering algorithm was introduced by MacQueen (1967) and aims to partition data points into homogeneous subgroups based on their similarities. For k-means clustering analysis, the input matrix is constructed from the observation values in the dataset to be analyzed. The distance measure is typically taken as Euclidean distance; however, alternative distance metrics that are frequently used in the literature may also be employed40.

The proposed method is based on clustering the feature space in order to identify representative features from cluster of similar features. In the proposed framework, the training data matrix is first transposed so that each feature is represented as a vector in the observation space, and k-means clustering is applied to partition these feature vectors into homogeneous groups. A known limitation of the classical k-means algorithm is its sensitivity to the random initialization of cluster centers, which may lead to instability in the selected representative features across different runs. To reduce this sensitivity, the proposed approach incorporates a bootstrap-based resampling strategy41, where bootstrap samples are repeatedly generated from the transposed training feature matrix and k-means clustering is independently applied at each bootstrap sampling. In each bootstrap sampling, the property with the smallest Euclidean distance from the center of each cluster is recorded as the representative property of that cluster. After completing all bootstrap samples, the final representative feature set is determined by selecting the most frequently occurring representative feature in each cluster across all bootstrap runs. This aggregation mechanism improves the stability, robustness, and representativeness of the selected feature subset while reducing the variance caused by random initialization.

Determining the optimal number of feature clusters in k-means remains a challenging problem, since there is no universally optimal cluster number for all datasets. Various cluster validity indices, such as the Elbow Method, Calinski Harabasz Index, Silhouette Index (SI), and Davies Bouldin Index (DBI), have been proposed in the literature to evaluate clustering quality42–44. Although the SI and DBI were employed in this study to determine the optimal number of clusters by evaluating cluster compactness and separation, these indices do not necessarily guarantee optimal predictive performance in subsequent regression tasks. Therefore, in this study, the primary methodology for determining the optimal number of feature clusters is based on a grid-search strategy over a predefined range of candidate cluster numbers. For each candidate value of , the bootstrap-stabilized feature selection and FRF modelling procedure is executed, and the model with the minimum validation MAE is selected as the final model. Finally, using the optimal number of feature clusters , the selected feature subset and the corresponding FRF model are applied to the test dataset to evaluate the final predictive performance of the proposed framework.

Experimental results

The performance of the proposed method is evaluated on ten real-world datasets with varying numbers of observations and features. Prior to analysis, all predictor features are standardized using Z-score normalization to ensure comparability across features and to improve the performance of clustering based feature selection. For each dataset, the observations are randomly partitioned into training (), validation (), and testing () subsets. The training set is used for model prediction, the validation set is used for hyperparameter tuning and model selection, and the testing set is reserved for final performance evaluation. To ensure a fair comparison, the same data partitions are used for all competing methods within each dataset. All analyses are performed in the R programming environment.

In the proposed framework, the number of clusters () is treated as a tuning parameter and optimized iteratively. For datasets with fewer than 100 features, where denotes the total number of features, the search interval is defined as , whereas for datasets with more than 100 features, the search interval is restricted to in order to reduce computational cost. For each candidate , the proposed bootstrapped k-means feature selection procedure is applied to the training data. At each bootstrap sampling, k-means clustering is performed on the transposed training matrix, and representative features are identified as the features closest to the cluster centers. The final feature subset for each candidate is determined by taking the mode of the representative feature indices obtained across bootstrap iterations for each cluster. Subsequently, the selected feature subset is used to construct the FRF model, and the predictive performance of each candidate is evaluated on the validation dataset. The optimal number of clusters is then determined based on the minimum MAE obtained on the validation dataset, and the corresponding FRF model and selected feature subset are used for final evaluation on the test dataset.

For performance comparison, the proposed method is evaluated against both regression based and feature selection based benchmark methods, including Multiple Linear Regression (MLR), FRF, LASSO, Ridge Regression, Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE), Genetic Algorithm based Feature Selection (GA), and Correlation based Feature Selection (CFS). In the FRF model, the optimal number of fuzzy clusters (c) is determined using the FSI, while the fuzziness parameter is fixed at throughout all analyses. The optimal cluster numbers are provided in Table 1 of the Supplementary Information. For LASSO and Ridge regression, the regularization parameter () is determined by cross-validation using the cv.glmnet function from the glmnet package45. The optimal values obtained for each dataset are reported in Table 1 of the Supplementary Information. RFE and GA based feature selection procedures are implemented using the caret package46. For RFE, the rfe function is used, where the candidate subset sizes are defined over the range , with representing the total number of independent features. The regression model is specified using the ”functions = lmFuncs” function set. The optimal subset size selected for each dataset corresponds to the number of selected features reported in the feature reduction tables. For GA based feature selection, the gafsControl function is used with the underlying regression model specified as “method = lm”. The crossover probability is searched over the values , the mutation probability over , and the elite proportion over the interval using a grid-search strategy. The optimal parameter settings for each dataset are reported in Table 1 of the Supplementary Information. CFS is implemented using the FSelector::cfs function with its default settings47.

Discussion

Empirical findings from ten real-world datasets demonstrate that the proposed feature selection framework provides competitive or superior predictive performance. To better understand the behavior and practical effectiveness of the proposed method, several aspects are discussed in this section, including the relationship between the number of selected features and bootstrap iterations, the overall ranking of the competing methods, the results of the ablation study, the paired comparison test results, and its robustness under different data partitioning ratios.

Table 3 in Supplementary Information summarizes the optimal bootstrap numbers identified for each dataset together with their corresponding feature numbers. As shown in the table, no clear or monotonic relationship can be observed between the number of available features and the optimal number of bootstrap iterations. For instance, some low-dimensional datasets such as BodyFat require a relatively large number of bootstrap repetitions, whereas some high-dimensional datasets such as Gasoline achieve their best performance with fewer bootstrap iterations. This finding suggests that the optimal bootstrap size is not determined solely by feature dimensionality, but also depends on the intrinsic characteristics of the dataset, such as feature redundancy, and the complexity of the relationship between predictors and the response feature. This relationship could be further explored in future studies using a larger number of datasets with different feature dimensions.

Table 4 in Supplementary Information reports the ranking performance and average rankings of the competing methods in terms of MAE across ten datasets. The results show that the proposed method variants consistently achieve superior ranking performance compared to the benchmark methods. In particular, BKMFS(500) achieves the best overall average ranking (4.35), closely followed by BKMFS(250) (4.50). Among the benchmark methods, LASSO exhibits a competitive performance with achieving the best average ranking (7.2). Overall, the average rankings across the datasets considered in this study suggest that the proposed method framework achieves the highest overall ranking among the competing methods.

Table 25 presents the ablation study results in terms of the percentage MAE improvement achieved by each feature selection method relative to the baseline FRF model. Overall, the bootstrap-based version of the proposed method provides higher performance gains across most datasets compared with the competing feature selection methods, which suggests the contribution of bootstrap sampling to the feature selection process. For example, on the Mtcars dataset, the proposed BKMFS method achieves a 48% improvement in MAE relative to the baseline FRF model. Similar performance patterns can also be observed in the Ethanol, Gasoline, and Corn datasets, where BKMFS yield the highest improvement rates. These findings indicate that bootstrap sampling can contribute to identifying more informative feature subsets and, consequently, improving predictive performance.

To further evaluate the robustness and predictive performance of the proposed method against different data partitioning ratios, additional analyses are conducted using alternative train-validation-test split ratios of 70%-15%-15% and 60%-20%-20%, in addition to the 80%-10%-10% setting reported in the experimental results. The detailed results, including MAE, RMSE, MAPE, and the number of selected features, are presented in Supplementary Information Tables 10–19. The purpose of this additional analysis is to investigate whether the predictive performance and feature selection behavior of the proposed method remain stable under different data partitioning conditions. For each alternative partitioning ratio, the complete analysis pipeline, including feature selection, model analyses, and performance evaluation, is repeated from the beginning to ensure a fair and independent assessment. The results show that the proposed framework consistently achieves either the best or highly competitive predictive performance across different split configurations, indicating its robustness to variations in data partitioning. For example, on the mtcars dataset (Supplementary Information Table 10), BKMFS achieves the best MAE values under both alternative split settings (3.1182 and 2.8421). Similarly, for the Midwest dataset (Supplementary Information Table 13), BKMFS again produces the lowest MAE values under both split configurations (0.1095 and 0.1806). In the Ethanol dataset (Supplementary Information Table 15), the proposed method also maintaines the best predictive performance across both partitioning ratios. These findings suggest that the proposed feature selection framework maintains its predictive performance under different train-validation-test partitions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study presents a new feature selection approach and evaluates its performance in regression problems using multiple datasets. Compared with widely used feature selection techniques such as CFS, RFE, GA, LASSO, and Ridge regression, as well as alternative cluster number determination criteria based on SI and DBI within the k-means framework, the proposed method demonstrates superior or competitive performance primarily in terms of MAE, with supporting evidence from RMSE and MAPE results.

The contribution of the proposed method lies not only in eliminating less informative features but also overcoming a fundamental limitation of feature selection with k-means clustering. Because k-means initializes cluster centers randomly, classical clustering may lead to unstable or even suboptimal results due to random initialization. In this sense, this study makes two main contributions. First, by integrating bootstrapping with repeated runs, the proposed approach mitigates the effects of randomness, enhances robustness, and improves the probability of identifying feature subsets with superior generalization performance. Second, the method assigns each feature to its nearest cluster center and selects the feature closest to this center as the cluster representative, representing the first application of this strategy in the feature selection literature.

Future studies may extend the proposed bootstrap-stabilized feature selection framework in several directions. First, the selected feature subsets may be integrated with alternative regression models, such as LASSO, Ridge regression, support vector regression, neural network based regression methods, to evaluate their predictive performance under different modeling assumptions. Second, the effectiveness of the proposed bootstrap-based stabilization strategy may be investigated using alternative clustering paradigms, such as fuzzy clustering, hierarchical clustering, and spectral clustering, to assess its generalizability beyond hard partitioning methods such as k-means. Finally, applying the proposed framework to large scale and more complex real-world datasets, such as genomic, financial, and industrial data, may provide further insights into its scalability, robustness, and practical applicability.
