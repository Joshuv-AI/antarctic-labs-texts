Refractory epilepsy from lead poisoning
Authors: Casey Singleton, Wei Kang Yap, Ming Lai, Mark Anderson, Albert Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLead exposure remains a significant yet under-recognised public health concern in the UK, particularly among vulnerable paediatric populations. Its non-specific, multisystemic involvement and rare neurological presentations make recognition and timely treatment of lead poisoning especially challenging.Case presentationWe report a case of a 6-year-old boy with autism spectrum disorder and pica presenting with new-onset refractory status epilepticus, unresponsive to standard antiepileptic therapy. Diagnostic evaluation demonstrated microcytic anaemia with basophilic stippling on blood film, diffuse cortical changes on MRI and markedly elevated blood lead levels, establishing the diagnosis of acute-on-chronic lead poisoning. Treatment with intravenous sodium calcium edetate and polyethylene glycol bowel irrigation led to progressive clinical improvement and seizure resolution. Serial measurements confirmed a declining lead burden, and the patient was discharged on oral chelation therapy.ConclusionsThis case highlights the importance of considering lead toxicity in unexplained encephalopathy or refractory seizures in children, with early recognition to prevent irreversible neurological sequelae.Keywords: PAEDIATRIC NEUROLOGY, TOXICOLOGY, EEG

Introduction

Status epilepticus is a neurological emergency with significant morbidity and mortality, requiring prompt and aggressive management. In children, underlying causes vary by age, with febrile seizures common in preschool children and trauma or infections more prevalent in older children.1 In refractory cases, it is crucial to evaluate for metabolic, structural or environmental causes, including rare toxicological exposures.

Lead poisoning, although uncommon, is an important differential diagnosis in children presenting with refractory seizures. Its diagnosis is often delayed due to non-specific presentations that mimic other neurological conditions. We report a case of severe lead encephalopathy in a 6-year-old child presenting with status epilepticus. The presence of microcytic anaemia with basophilic stippling prompted targeted blood lead testing, confirming acute-on-chronic lead toxicity.

Case presentation

A previously healthy 6-year-old boy presented to the emergency department after his mother observed generalised tonic-clonic seizures during sleep. The convulsions began approximately 1 hour before presentation and were described as generalised jerking movements. He was afebrile, with no history of preceding illness, recent vaccinations or head trauma. Initial treatment with benzodiazepines, levetiracetam and phenytoin failed to terminate seizure activity. Ongoing seizure activity prompted endotracheal intubation and urgent transfer to a tertiary paediatric intensive care unit (PICU).

His medical history included a presumed diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and pica. He was born at term following an uncomplicated perinatal course and had relocated from Nigeria to the UK 6 months before admission. There was no family history of epilepsy or metabolic disorders.

On admission to the PICU, empirical antimicrobial therapy with intravenous ceftriaxone and acyclovir was initiated for a possible meningoencephalitis but discontinued following a full negative septic screen and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) cultures. Lumbar puncture revealed clear CSF with normal opening pressure, normal white cell count and elevated protein concentration. Chest radiography and non-contrast CT brain imaging were unremarkable.

Despite maximal supportive measures, the patient continued to experience breakthrough seizures, necessitating the escalation of sedation with a continuous infusion of midazolam, alongside intermittent doses of propofol and ketamine. Electroencephalography (EEG) demonstrated subconvulsive status epilepticus with asynchronous epileptiform discharges over both hemispheres. Intermittent myoclonic jerks of the head and shoulder, likely corresponding to EEG bursts, were observed.

The patient was subsequently administered a loading dose of phenobarbitone, followed by a maintenance regimen, which resulted in cessation of seizure activity. He remained intubated and sedated for ongoing neurocritical care management.

Initial laboratory investigations revealed hypochromic microcytic anaemia with a haemoglobin concentration of 77 g/L (mean cell volume 62.9). White cell and platelet counts, C-reactive protein, serum electrolytes and blood ammonia levels were within normal limits. Brain MRI demonstrated extensive cortical abnormalities involving grey matter with mild subjacent white matter changes and cerebral oedema, consistent with encephalopathic changes (figure 1A).

Given the anaemia and history of pica, a haematology consultation was sought. Blood film examination revealed basophilic stippling and confirmed sickle cell trait. The presence of basophilic stippling raised suspicion for heavy metal toxicity, particularly lead poisoning. A whole-genome sequencing for an underlying genetic epilepsy predisposition returned negative.

Subsequent toxicology testing confirmed a markedly elevated blood lead concentration of 12.46 µmol/L or 258 µg/dL (reference range <0.23 µmol/L or 11.1 µg/dL). Abdominal radiography demonstrated radiopaque material within the colon, consistent with ingested lead-containing substances (figure 1B). Long bone radiographs revealed dense metaphyseal bands, characteristic of chronic lead exposure (figure 1C). In addition, intraoral examination revealed a bluish gingival line (Burton’s line), a classical sign of lead poisoning (figure 1D).

A diagnosis of acute-on-chronic lead poisoning was established following toxicology results. The National Poisons Information Service was consulted, and chelation therapy with intravenous sodium calcium edetate (NaCaEDTA) was commenced for 10 days. Simultaneously, gastrointestinal decontamination was performed with polyethylene glycol electrolyte solution and phosphate enemas.

Further history revealed habitual chewing of electrical wires and possible ingestion of paint flakes from the family’s Grade II listed flat. Environmental assessment by Public Health England confirmed degraded lead-based paint as the likely chronic exposure source.

Serial EEGs demonstrated progressive reduction in epileptiform activity, permitting gradual sedation weaning and successful extubation by day 7. Seizure activity progressively declined following initiation of treatment (figure 1E). Antiepileptic therapy was de-escalated to oral levetiracetam. At discharge, blood lead concentrations had decreased to 4.82 µmol/L (100 µg/dL), with resolution of encephalopathic features, enabling transition to oral dimercaptosuccinic acid for ongoing chelation.

During outpatient follow-up, he experienced only two febrile generalised tonic-clonic seizures in the context of intercurrent illness. These events were not attributed to residual lead burden and were considered typical febrile seizures despite the relatively late age of onset. He remained free of unprovoked seizures on brivaracetam. Serial monitoring showed a continued decline in blood lead levels, and he remained neurologically stable. Although the theoretical effect of residual lead on seizure threshold cannot be entirely excluded, this was regarded as unlikely given the substantially reduced lead levels, the absence of further unprovoked seizures and the lack of supportive genetic diagnosis.

Discussion and conclusions

Lead remains a ubiquitous environmental toxin despite regulatory controls, posing a continued public health concern. Children are particularly susceptible due to increased gastrointestinal absorption and mouthing behaviours.2 Exposure typically occurs via ingestion of lead-based paint, contaminated dust or soil. Pica, commonly seen in children with ASD and developmental delay, significantly increases risk, with up to 23% of children with ASD demonstrating pica behaviours.3 This reinforces the importance of considering lead toxicity in children with autistic regression and pica.

Neurological effects of lead are particularly severe in children due to their immature blood-brain barrier and the metal’s preferential central nervous system distribution.4 Several mechanisms of lead-induced neurotoxicity have been proposed, including oxidative stress, apoptosis and impaired neurotransmitter release.5 Lead encephalopathy, a severe manifestation, typically occurs at blood lead levels ≥4.8 µmol/L (≥100 µg/dL).6 In our case, the significant lead burden resulted in refractory status epilepticus unresponsive to multiple antiepileptic medications.

Talbot et al7 described a 2-year-old initially treated for viral tonsillitis who later developed afebrile seizures and raised intracranial pressure; lead poisoning was diagnosed late, and the child deteriorated despite chelation therapy. Similarly, George et al8 reported a 4-year-old with severe autism presenting with vomiting, pallor and behavioural change, later confirmed to have acute lead encephalopathy. Unlike these cases, where diagnosis was delayed and outcomes were poor, early recognition in our patient facilitated timely chelation therapy with rapid neurological recovery. This comparison underscored the diagnostic value of recognising microcytic anaemia and pica behaviours as potential indicators of lead toxicity.

An intriguing aspect of our case was the rapid clinical improvement following intravenous sodium calcium edetate (NaCaEDTA) and polyethylene glycol whole bowel irrigation, accompanied by declining blood lead levels and seizure resolution. This observation raises the possibility that higher blood lead levels may enhance the efficacy of chelation. While not traditionally linked to altered pharmacokinetics, emerging evidence suggests that chelation therapy may preferentially mobilise and facilitate the excretion of more labile blood lead pools that are less tightly bound to cellular targets in those with high blood lead levels.9 Further mechanistic studies are warranted to elucidate this potential interplay.

Once lead encephalopathy is diagnosed, it warrants high-dependency admission for seizure control and intracranial pressure management. Prompt initiation of chelation therapy, with input from toxicology services, is critical as it binds circulating lead for renal excretion, reducing neurological sequelae. Blood and urine lead levels should be monitored to guide treatment duration and intensity. In cases with gastrointestinal lead burden, as evidenced by our patient’s abdominal x-ray, whole bowel irrigation is indicated to reduce ongoing absorption.10

Kamal et al11 and Berry et al12 previously reported paediatric cases of lead encephalopathy successfully managed with combined parenteral chelation using dimercaprol and calcium disodium EDTA. In contrast, our case employed a treatment strategy incorporating whole bowel irrigation in combination with intravenous sodium calcium edetate (NaCaEDTA) alone. Notably, despite a blood lead concentration approximately twofold higher than that reported by Kamal et al, our patient achieved a comparably favourable neurological outcome.

In summary, this case underscored the enduring public health threat posed by lead exposure, particularly in homes constructed before regulatory restrictions in 1987. Lead-induced status epilepticus presents significant diagnostic challenges due to its non-specific features and risk of delayed recognition. Insights from this case reinforce the necessity of considering environmental toxins in refractory paediatric seizures and inform evidence-based strategies for the management of lead-induced encephalopathy in neurological practice.
