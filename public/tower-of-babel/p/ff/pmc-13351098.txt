Leveraging artificial intelligence to streamline documentation and support patient-centered gynecologic oncology outpatient visits
Authors: Madison Kieffer, Jarom Morris, Britton Trabert, Katelyn Tondo-Steele
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aims to evaluate provider-perceived accuracy, completeness, and clinical utility of ChatGPT-generated assessment and plans for outpatient gynecologic oncology encounters. A secondary aim is to identify prompt elements associated with accurate outputs to optimize the reliability of artificial intelligence (AI) integration.MethodsSix clinical scenarios across a spectrum of outpatient gynecologic oncology visits were entered into ChatGPT (GPT-4) using a standardized prompt to generate electronic health record assessment and plan sections. Outputs were reviewed by gynecologic, radiation, and medical oncology faculty at a National Cancer Institute–designated tertiary referral center. Reviewers were blinded to other's evaluations. Accuracy was rated on a 5-point Likert scale and completeness on a 3-point Likert scale. Reviewers also indicated whether they would consider clinical use. Descriptive statistics summarized responses.ResultsFourteen faculty evaluators (response rate 77.8%) completed reviews, providing 84 accuracy ratings and 84 completeness ratings. Median accuracy was 4.0 (IQR 4.0–5.0), with 91.7% of ratings “mostly correct” or higher. Median completeness was 2.0 (IQR 2.0–3.0), with 91.7% of ratings “adequate” or “comprehensive.” 80.6% of responses indicated willingness to use the AI-generated outputs in clinical practice. Analysis of the prompt-development process and reviewer feedback also identified key elements for generating reliable outputs such as role specification and guideline parameters.ConclusionsChat-GPT is capable of producing accurate, complete, and clinically useful outpatient gynecologic oncology plans as rated by oncology faculty. Specific prompt engineering to reflect subspecialty clinical context and established treatment standards may improve the consistency and reliability of AI-generated documentation.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, AI, Chat-GPT, Documentation, Electronic health record

Introduction

Gynecologic oncology clinical encounters often involve detailed histories, complex treatment plans and multidisciplinary care coordination. These visits require substantial documentation, which may reduce the time available for direct patient care. In response, there has been growing interest in leveraging artificial intelligence (AI) to support clinical workflows. Large language models (LLMs), a subset of natural language processing technologies, are capable of generating human-like text and synthesizing complex information from large datasets (Biesheuvel et al., 2025). These models have been explored as tools to streamline clinical documentation, improve the consistency and completeness of medical records, and assist clinicians in organizing patient-specific information (Chen et al., 2025; Bracken et al., 2025).

Within gynecologic oncology, AI applications have shown promise in areas such as early cancer detection, histopathologic classification, risk stratification, and outcome prediction (Mysona et al., 2021; Paiboonborirak et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2023; Gumilar et al., 2024). In addition, ChatGPT, a widely used LLM, was shown to outperform healthcare providers in delivering medically accurate responses to patient questions about ovarian cancer (Chou et al., 2025). Despite increasing interest and early evidence supporting AI applications, there are no published studies specifically evaluating the clinical efficacy of LLM-generated treatment plans and associated electronic health record (EHR) documentation in the field of gynecologic oncology. Using LLMs to efficiently apply treatment guidelines to patient-specific information and generate a template for clinical documentation has the potential to increase clinician time available for other tasks including counseling and shared decision-making. However, before LLM-generated clinical content can be meaningfully integrated into oncologic practice, its accuracy, completeness, and clinical usefulness must be evaluated.

Accordingly, this pilot study aims to evaluate physician-perceived accuracy, completeness, and clinical utility of ChatGPT-generated assessments and plans for outpatient gynecologic oncology encounters. A secondary aim was to identify prompt design elements associated with higher-quality outputs to provide practical guidance for AI prompt development in gynecologic oncology clinical and research settings.

Methods

The study was exempt from the Institutional Review Board as it did not meet qualifications for human subjects research. Six clinical scenarios were developed to represent a spectrum of outpatient gynecologic oncology visits. These included new patient visits for low-grade endometrial cancer, advanced ovarian cancer, and locally advanced cervical cancer as well as return patient visits for recurrent metastatic vulvar cancer, recurrent metastatic endometrial cancer, and post-operative counseling following surgical staging of early-stage endometrial cancer.

An iterative prompt refinement process was used to develop a standardized prompt for entering each clinical scenario into ChatGPT. Initial prompt versions included patient-specific information (age, available imaging, laboratory and pathology data, and prior treatment history) along with instructions to generate an assessment and plan suitable for inclusion in the electronic health record. Early prompt outputs frequently produced assessment and plan sections more appropriate for a general medical audience rather than a gynecologic oncology subspecialist and occasionally included clinical details not provided in the original prompt. To address these limitations, additional constraints were incorporated into subsequent prompt iterations. These included explicit instructions for the model to assume the role of a board-certified gynecologic oncologist, to adhere to National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines, and to restrict outputs to clinical information explicitly provided within the prompt. After each prompt was entered using the final standardized prompt, the clinical scenarios and associated AI-generated outputs were then independently reviewed by gynecologic, radiation, and medical oncology faculty at a National Cancer Institute–designated tertiary referral center. Reviewers were blinded to each other's evaluations. Accuracy was rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = completely incorrect, 2 = mostly incorrect, 3 = mix of correct and incorrect elements, 4 = mostly correct, 5 = completely/nearly completely correct). Completeness was rated on a 3-point Likert scale (1 = incomplete, 2 = adequate, 3 = comprehensive). Reviewers also indicated whether they would consider using each output in clinical practice (Yes, No, or Not Applicable). Score results were listed descriptively. Evaluators were also given the option to provide free-text comments identifying missing content or suggested improvements to the AI-generated assessments and plans.

Results

Survey responses were received from 14 faculty evaluators (response rate 77.8%), providing 84 accuracy ratings, 84 completeness ratings, and 84 clinical utility ratings across scenarios. Among accuracy ratings, 41.7% (n = 35/84) were marked as completely/nearly completely correct, 50% (n = 42/84) were marked as mostly correct, and 8.3% (n = 7/84) were marked as a mix of correct and incorrect elements. No accuracy ratings (n = 0/84) were marked as mostly incorrect or completely incorrect (Fig. 1). Median accuracy was 4.0 (IQR 4.0–5.0) on the 5-point accuracy Likert scale, with 91.7% of ratings designated as mostly correct or completely/nearly completely correct.

Among completeness ratings, 44% (n = 37/84) were marked as comprehensive, 47.6% (n = 40/84) were marked as adequate, and 8.3% (n = 7/84) were marked as inadequate (Fig. 2). Median completeness was 2.0 (IQR 2.0–3.0) on the 3-point completeness Likert scale, with 91.7% of completeness ratings designated as adequate or comprehensive.

Across scenarios applicable to respondents' individual practice, 80.6% of responses indicated willingness to use the AI-generated documentation in a clinical setting. In total, 69% (n = 58/84) of responses would consider clinical use, 14.3% (n = 14/84) of responses would not consider clinical use, and 14.3% (n = 12/84) of responses deemed the AI-generated documentation as inapplicable to their clinical practice. The optional question regarding missing content or suggested improvements to the AI-generated documentation was completed by one evaluator, who suggested that incorporating references within the assessment sections would strengthen the documentation. No additional content-specific deficits or suggestions were reported.

Informal analysis of the prompt-development process identified key elements for generating reliable AI outputs such as role specification, guideline parameters, and exclusion of unprovided information.

Discussion

Our findings demonstrate that ChatGPT can produce accurate, complete, and clinically useful assessments, plans, and associated electronic health record documentation in response to standardized clinical prompts. These results suggest that large language models, when guided by intentional prompt design, may represent a viable adjunct for clinical documentation and treatment planning in gynecologic oncology.

To our knowledge, this is a novel study evaluating ChatGPT's ability to respond to a given clinical scenario in gynecologic oncology with a written assessment and plan designed for the EHR. Prior studies regarding the role of AI in clinical documentation are not specific to gynecologic oncology and have primarily focused on ambient listening, creation of clinical notes from audio recordings, or generation of operative reports or discharge summaries (Bracken et al., 2025). Within gynecologic oncology, several applications of AI have been evaluated, including AI-assisted responses to patient questions, recurrence risk prediction, screening and early detection, as well as interpretation of cytology, histopathology, and radiology findings (Paiboonborirak et al., 2025; Chou et al., 2025; Reicher et al., 2025). Additional work within gynecologic oncology has assessed the ability of various AI chatbots to generate accurate and consistent medical recommendations to clinical vignettes, but primarily focused on clinician decision-making support and did not address generation of associated clinical documentation (Gumilar et al., 2024). Our focus on practical documentation workflows distinguishes this work from prior studies and has the potential to address a critical source of clinical burden (Moy et al., 2021).

Additionally, several practical considerations for AI prompt design emerged during this study that may be informative for future evaluations of large language models in gynecologic oncology. Role and guidelines specification, achieved by instructing ChatGPT to act as a board-certified gynecologic oncologist and adhere to National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines, yielded outputs that were more specialty-specific and guideline-concordant. Additionally, explicit instruction to ChatGPT to only use clinical information included in the original prompt minimized AI hallucinations, a common pitfall of AI-generated content resulting from its predictive (as opposed to knowledge-based) nature (Howell et al., 2024). Optimal prompt design should be considered in future studies evaluating the use of AI in gynecologic oncology given its impacts on generated output.

This study has several strengths, including a high provider response rate and evaluation by multidisciplinary faculty, which enhance the clinical relevance of the findings. Additionally, the use of standardized prompts allowed for consistent assessment of AI-generated outputs across cases. The case content spanned multiple disease sites and included both new and recurrent cancer diagnoses, enabling evaluation of ChatGPT responses across the spectrum of gynecologic oncology. Several limitations should be considered. First, a formally validated instrument to assess AI-generated documentation was not used. While validated tools designed for AI-generated medical content do exist, including the QAMAI tool and the PDSQI-9, neither is validated to evaluate the subspeciality-specific assessment and plan documentation generated in this study (Vaira et al., 2024; Croxford et al., 2025). We therefore assessed accuracy, completeness, and clinical utility using a structed Likert-based approach that aligns closely with several domains captured by these validated instruments and is consistent with much of the existing literature evaluating large language models in medicine. Second, we did not systematically capture the specific content deficits of the AI-generated assessments and plans. Our survey's optional free-text field yielded only one comment and therefore did not characterize what evaluators found missing. This is an important gap, as the value of AI-generated documentation depends not only on overall accuracy but on consistent capture of treatment risks and benefits, side effects, surveillance recommendations, and shared decision-making considerations, as large language models are prone to omission of nuanced discussion and fabrication of unsupported details. Third, the single-site design of this study limits generalizability to other institutions with potentially different practice approaches or documentation preferences. This could be remedied by including providers from multiple institutions and geographic locations in future studies. Additionally, the absence of a direct comparator, such as physician-authored documentation, precludes conclusions about relative quality or efficiency that should be investigated in future work to better assess the utility of these tools in clinical practice.

Additional research should also examine implementation strategies including HIPAA compliancy safeguards and potential EHR integration, as well as ethical considerations including mitigation of bias, data privacy, and patient perspectives on AI-assisted treatment planning and documentation. As applications of AI within medicine expand, it is crucial to evaluate its utility in improving patient care and provider workflows. Our work demonstrates that ChatGPT can produce accurate, complete, and clinically useful treatment plans and documentation, underlining a potential application of AI to streamline documentation frameworks and increase clinician time for counseling and shared decision-making while preserving individualized care.
