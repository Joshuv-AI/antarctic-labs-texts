The Impact of an Optimised Environmental Noise Management Protocol on Sleep Quality and Fatigue in Patients with Colorectal Cancer
Authors: BiQin Wu, XiaoRong Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:This study aimed to examine the effects of an optimised environmental noise management protocol on sleep quality and fatigue levels in patients with colorectal cancer.Methods:A retrospective analysis was conducted. Patients admitted before the implementation of the optimised protocol (January 2023 to December 2023, receiving conventional noise management) were assigned to the conventional care group (n = 63). Those admitted after implementation (January 2024 to December 2024, receiving optimised environmental noise management) were assigned to the optimised management group (n = 64). A comparison was made between the two groups regarding noise decibel levels (daytime and nighttime), sleep quality (Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index [PSQI], fatigue (Cancer Fatigue Scale [CFS] and comfort status (General Comfort Questionnaire [GCQ].Results:The optimised management group demonstrated significantly lower noise decibel levels during daytime and nighttime compared with the conventional care group (P < 0.05). Significantly reduced scores were observed in all PSQI domains and the global PSQI score in the optimised management group (P < 0.05). Similarly, scores across all domains of the CFS and its total score significantly decreased in this group (P < 0.05). Conversely, scores for all domains of the GCQ and its total score were significantly elevated in the optimised management group (P < 0.05). Pearson correlation analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between noise decibel levels and PSQI (r > 0, P < 0.05) and CFS scores (r > 0, P < 0.05). A significant negative correlation was observed between noise decibel levels and GCQ scores (r < 0, P < 0.05).Conclusion:Implementing an optimised environmental noise management protocol can effectively reduce noise levels in patient care units, thereby improving sleep quality, reducing fatigue, and enhancing overall patient comfort during hospitalisation.

INTRODUCTION

Colorectal cancer is a common malignant neoplasm of the digestive tract, for which surgical resection is frequently employed. This procedure involves the removal of diseased colonic tissue to inhibit tumour proliferation and metastasis, aiming to prolong patient survival.[1] However, surgery inflicts significant physical trauma, necessitating a period of convalescence to facilitate recovery. This underscores the importance of a supportive inpatient environment, yet environmental noise is often overlooked in clinical care. Excessive noise levels can adversely affect sleep quality.[2] Adequate sleep promotes the secretion of growth hormone, maintains autonomic nervous system stability and regulates endocrine metabolism. Poor sleep quality following colorectal cancer surgery may disrupt endocrine regulation and growth hormone secretion, which can impede postoperative wound healing, compromise immune function, increase the risk of postoperative infections and ultimately delay recovery.[3]

Noise is characterised by a subjective assessment as any sound within a specific environment that adversely affects others.[4] It exerts negative effects on the human endocrine system, cardiovascular health, auditory function and other physiological aspects. Patients, often in a debilitated and agitated state due to illness, exhibit heightened sensitivity to noise, which consequently increases their physical and psychological burden.[5] An investigation and analysis by Jiang et al.[6] in a general hospital revealed that noise levels at all measured points exceeded established standards, with the highest exceeding by 84% and the lowest by 4%. Similarly, Feng et al.[7] reported that noise levels in wards of a tertiary general hospital surpassed recommended standards during day and night, with the highest exceedance reaching 48.10% and the lowest 29.08%. Furthermore, multiple noise sources within the wards were identified, all exceeding 62 dB. This evidence indicates that noise has become a significant contributor to environmental pollution in healthcare settings and a substantial barrier to patient recovery. Noise management represents a critical, yet challenging, component of overall environmental control in hospitals. A study by Zhu et al.[8] demonstrated that a noise management scheme for cardiology inpatient wards, developed based on project management theory, effectively reduces environmental noise levels. Building upon this foundation, the present study implemented an optimised noise management protocol for 64 patients hospitalised with colorectal cancer between January 2024 and December 2024, to investigate its effects on sleep quality and fatigue levels. The details are reported as follows.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design

Clinical data were retrospectively collected from 178 patients who underwent surgical treatment for colorectal cancer at The First Hospital of Putian between January 2023 and December 2024. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) fulfilment of diagnostic criteria for colorectal cancer confirmed by postoperative histopathological examination[9]; (2) completion of surgical resection for colorectal cancer; (3) age between 18 and 80 years; (4) tumour-node-metastasis (TNM) stage I or II disease; (5) consciousness during hospitalisation and ability to complete relevant assessments and questionnaires; and (6) availability of complete clinical records. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosis with any other concurrent malignant tumours; (2) readmission due to recurrent colorectal cancer; (3) presence of haematological diseases or coagulation disorders; (4) severe dysfunction of major organs (e.g., heart, liver or kidneys); (5) pre-existing sleep disorders or a history of hypnotic drug use; (6) presence of physical disabilities or hemiplegia; (7) a previous history of cardiovascular or cerebrovascular events, such as myocardial infarction or cerebral haemorrhage and (8) transfer to another facility during treatment, voluntary withdrawal from treatment or incomplete treatment for any other reason.

On the basis of the aforementioned inclusion and exclusion criteria, an eligibility assessment was conducted on the 178 initially identified patients. Of these, 16 were excluded due to transfer to another hospital or voluntary withdrawal from treatment, 20 were excluded because of incomplete clinical records, 5 were excluded as they were admitted for recurrent colorectal cancer and 10 were excluded due to a pre-existing history of sleep disorders or use of hypnotic medication. Consequently, a final cohort of 127 patients was included in the analysis. Patients admitted prior to implementation (January 2023 to December 2023) were assigned to a conventional care group (n = 63), whereas those admitted after implementation (January 2024 to December 2024) were assigned to an optimised management group (n = 64). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Hospital of Putian (Approval No: 2024-140). All subjects participated in this study have provided informed consent.

Conventional Care Group

Patients in the conventional care group received standard nursing practices during their hospitalisation. The specific protocols were as follows. Preoperatively, detailed explanations were provided regarding the necessity of the surgical procedure, the process itself and postoperative precautions. Successful case histories were also shared to bolster patient confidence and promote cooperation with the treatment plan. Conspicuous ‘quiet’ signs were posted at ward entrances/exits, on patient room doors and inside the rooms. Patients and their family members were instructed to maintain a quiet environment, refraining from loud conversations or noisy activities. Patients were guided to consume a liquid diet 1 day prior to surgery, adhere to fasting for 8–12 hours and fluid restriction for 4 hours before the operation, and undergo cleansing enemas as part of the preoperative preparation. Postoperatively, vital signs were monitored closely, and any abnormalities were promptly addressed with symptomatic management and reported to the attending physician. A multimodal analgesia protocol was actively implemented, utilising non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, intravenous patient-controlled analgesia pumps and distraction techniques to alleviate pain intensity. The stoma or surgical incision site was kept dry and clean, with dressings changed regularly to prevent wound infection.

Optimised Management Group

1. Development of the optimised environmental noise management workflow: On-site observational assessments were conducted to identify and catalogue sources of noise, which primarily included conversations within patient rooms, door closure sounds, mobile phone ringtones and elevated volumes during healthcare provider–patient interactions. Following the identification of these noise sources, team members performed a literature review using keywords such as ‘colorectal cancer’, ‘noise reduction’, ‘sleep quality’ and ‘fatigue’ in Chinese databases (e.g., Wanfang, China National Knowledge Infrastructure) and keywords like ‘noise’ and ‘quality of sleep’ in PubMed. The retrieved evidence was systematically organised and reviewed to define the core components of an optimised noise management strategy. A revised noise management workflow and specific interventions were subsequently developed through team consensus and implemented starting in January 2024.

2. Noise management interventions:

(a)Noise management policies: The ‘Ward Noise Management’ protocol was strictly enforced, incorporating regulations for ward noise, management of medical equipment and visitor control policies. Healthcare staff were required to set personal mobile devices to silent or vibrate mode during shifts and rigorously adhere to the ‘Four Light’ principle: speak lightly, operate lightly, walk lightly and close doors lightly. Upon admission, nurses provided each patient and their family with a ‘Ward Noise Management Handbook’, explaining its contents in detail. A strict policy was enforced, limiting each patient to one designated caregiver per day and discouraging non-essential visits to minimise noise from social interactions.

(b)Physical environment modifications: Sound-absorbing ceiling tiles or acoustic panels were installed in patient rooms and corridors. Soundproof doors and windows were utilised to attenuate external noise transmission. Designated daily quiet hours (14:00–16:00) were established, during which only essential medical care was provided, and family visits were prohibited. A ‘quiet culture’ was promoted throughout the unit by displaying the ‘Quiet Ward Demonstration Zone’ signage at room entrances and distributing informational pamphlets on maintaining silence.

(c)Equipment maintenance: A dedicated medical equipment officer was assigned within the department. This officer was responsible for conducting weekly inspections and maintenance of all mobile carts, therapeutic instruments and patient room doors. Wheels on all mobile carts required to enter patient rooms were replaced with silent or soft rubber casters. Lubrication levels in casters were regularly checked and replenished. Felt pads or rubber cushions were installed on the legs of companion chairs, beds, cabinets and other furniture to prevent noise generated from friction or impact with the floor.

Outcome Measures

(1)Environmental noise: Noise levels in patient rooms were measured continuously using wall-mounted noise meters (Manufacturer: Shenzhen Yunyi Industrial Control Co., Ltd. Bao’an District, Shenzhen, China.; Model: TA654B) throughout the hospitalisation period for both groups. The average decibel (dB) values were calculated for daytime (06:00–22:00) and nighttime (22:00–06:00 the next day) periods separately.

(2)Sleep quality: Sleep quality during hospitalisation was assessed for both groups using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), as revised by Buysse et al.[10] and translated into Chinese by Liu et al.[11] The PSQI evaluates seven components as follows: sleep latency, subjective sleep quality, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, daytime dysfunction, sleep duration and sleep efficiency. Each component is rated on a 0–3 scale, yielding a global score ranging from 0 to 21, with elevated scores indicating inferior sleep quality. The internal consistency (Cronbach’s α) for the scale in this context was 0.837, and its test–retest reliability was 0.964.

(3)Fatigue levels: Fatigue levels during hospitalisation were assessed for both groups using the Cancer Fatigue Scale (CFS), which was modified by Okuyama et al.[12] and subsequently translated and culturally adapted into Chinese by Zhang et al.[13] This 15-item instrument comprises three subscales as follows: cognitive fatigue (four items), affective fatigue (four items) and physical fatigue (seven items). Each item is scored on a 0–4 point scale. The maximum possible scores for the cognitive fatigue, affective fatigue and physical fatigue subscales are 16, 16 and 28 points, respectively, with a total maximum score of 60 points. Higher scores indicate more severe fatigue. The scale demonstrated an internal consistency of 0.857 and a test–retest reliability of 0.891.

Statistical Methods

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 25.0) IBM Corporation, Ammonk City, New York State, USA. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data conforming to a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared between groups using the independent samples t-test. Categorical data are expressed as percentages (%) and were compared using the chi-square (χ2) test. The relationship between noise levels and the sleep quality, fatigue and comfort of patients with colorectal cancer was determined via Pearson correlation analysis. A two-sided P-value of less than 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

DISCUSSION

Optimised Environmental Noise Management Reduces Ward Noise Decibels

The findings of this study demonstrated that the optimised management group exhibited significantly lower noise decibel levels during daytime and nighttime compared with the conventional care group (P < 0.05). Thus, the implementation of an optimised environmental noise management protocol can effectively reduce noise levels in the inpatient environment for patients with colorectal cancer. This reduction can be attributed to the comprehensive approach employed before implementation, which involved real-time monitoring of ward noise over 3 consecutive days to confirm and quantify noise pollution. Subsequent direct observation identified the primary sources of noise, which included insufficient awareness among healthcare staff and patients’ families regarding noise, noise from medical equipment and devices and inadequacies in existing noise reduction policies. An evidence-based optimisation of the noise management strategy was developed following a systematic review of relevant literature on noise reduction retrieved from domestic and international databases to address these factors. Enhanced training and education on noise reduction for medical staff and patients raised awareness and promoted self-management for noise control. This led to voluntary reductions in speaking volume among staff and improved management of in-room noise, contributing to an overall quiet environment.[16] The refined ‘Ward Noise Management’ protocol effectively mitigated noise at its source by regulating conversation volumes among staff and patients. The strict enforcement of the ‘Four Light’ principle among healthcare workers minimised noise generated during professional interactions. Furthermore, stringent restrictions on the frequency and duration of daily visits reduced noise from conversations between patients and visitors. Regular weekly maintenance of medical equipment, coupled with the installation of silent casters or soft rubber wheels on mobile carts, minimised friction-induced noise during equipment movement, thereby lowering ambient noise levels.[17] Environmental modifications, including the installation of noise alert indicators, strict enforcement of visiting policies and the prominent display of signage such as ‘Quiet’, ‘No Visitors’ and ‘Quiet Hours’ throughout the ward, served as constant reminders for all individuals in the unit to control noise, which significantly reduced decibel levels.[18]

Optimised Environmental Noise Management Improves Sleep Quality in Patients with Colorectal Cancer

The results of this study revealed significantly lower scores in the optimised management group across all measured domains of PSQI, as well as a lower global PSQI score, compared with the conventional care group (P < 0.05). Thus, the optimised environmental noise management protocol effectively improved sleep quality during hospitalisation for patients with colorectal cancer. This finding was consistent with the research results of Duggan et al.,[19] who reported that ‘non-pharmacological noise intervention measures can reduce noise decibels and improve sleep quality’. The improvement observed is likely attributable to the protocol’s effectiveness in creating a quiet and comfortable inpatient environment by reducing ambient noise exposure. This optimised acoustic environment facilitates easy sleep initiation, shortens sleep latency, reduces the reliance on hypnotic medication and minimises sleep disruptions caused by noise, thereby enhancing overall sleep quality and efficiency. Consequently, patients experience prolonged and less disrupted sleep, achieve deep sleep stages and secure restorative rest, mitigating daytime dysfunction.[20] The implementation of scheduled quiet periods as part of the environmental management strategy specifically reduced potential disruptions during critical rest times, safeguarding sleep continuity and contributing significantly to improved sleep outcomes.

Optimised Environmental Noise Management Alleviates Fatigue and Enhances Comfort in Patients with Colorectal Cancer

Fatigue is a prevalent and distressing symptom among patients with malignant tumours, representing a persistent subjective feeling of exhaustion resulting from the disease, its treatment and associated psychological distress. It often presents as tiredness, weakness and general debilitation, which may be partially alleviated by adequate sleep and rest.[21] Excessive noise in the hospital environment can disrupt daytime rest and nocturnal sleep in patients with colorectal cancer, preventing necessary physical and mental recuperation, exacerbating feelings of fatigue and agitation and ultimately diminishing overall comfort.[22] The results of this study demonstrated significantly reduced scores in the cognitive, affective and physical fatigue domains, as well as in the total score of CFS, in the optimised management group compared with the conventional care group (P < 0.05). This suggests that the optimised environmental noise management protocol effectively mitigated fatigue and enhanced comfort levels. This improvement was attributed to the quiet restful environment fostered by the protocol, which facilitated improved sleep and effective rest. By ameliorating the exhaustion and irritability caused by the illness and a noisy environment, patients’ fatigued bodies and minds were allowed to fully recover. This supports the restoration of physical function, reduces disease-related weakness and tiredness, improves daytime functional status and increases overall comfort during hospitalisation. Consequently, patients can more effectively engage with clinical treatments, possibly enhancing recovery and leading to improved prognosis.[23] From a public health perspective, hospital noise represents a frequently overlooked form of environmental pollution. Effective noise management not only improves patient recovery outcomes but also enhances staff well-being and the overall quality of care, aligning with global initiatives advocating for healthy hospital environments.

Limitations

Firstly, the effects of environmental noise are complex; nevertheless, this investigation was conducted as a pilot project solely among hospitalised patients with colorectal cancer, limiting the generalisability of the findings. Furthermore, as a retrospective analysis constrained by the available sample size and data completeness, the absence of an a priori sample size calculation may have resulted in insufficient statistical power. Secondly, the two groups were assessed over different time periods, during which various confounding factors may have been present, potentially compromising the reliability of the outcomes. Thirdly, sleep quality and fatigue are influenced by numerous factors. Important confounders, such as postoperative pain, psychological status and medication use, were not fully controlled, possibly introducing bias into the results. Future research should adopt a prospective design with an appropriately calculated sample size and randomised allocation. Incorporating comprehensive and standardised documentation of postoperative pain, along with structured psychological assessments, may control for potential confounding variables and validate the findings of this study.

CONCLUSION

In summary, the implementation of an optimised environmental noise management protocol, through the systematic identification of noise sources and the application of evidence-based noise reduction strategies, effectively reduced noise levels in the inpatient environment for patients with colorectal cancer. The provision of a quiet and restful setting significantly improved sleep quality, enhanced comfort and alleviated fatigue. These findings provide robust empirical support for the broad adoption of such interventions in healthcare settings. Promoting such protocols not only alleviates the burden on medical systems but also results in significant public health benefits, demonstrating considerable socio-economic value.
