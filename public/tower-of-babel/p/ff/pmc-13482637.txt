Time-restricted feeding improves functional capacity of adipose-derived stem cells with activation of OSK-associated transcriptional programs
Authors: Rui Zhang, Xingxiang Duan, Wanyang Zhong, Yan Deng, Ziping Wang, Junnan Wang, Yan He, Shuang Rong, Qingsong Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTime-restricted feeding (TRF), a circadian-based dietary intervention, has emerged as a promising strategy to counteract metabolic and age-related dysfunctions. However, how TRF can reverse stem cell aging and restore tissue regenerative potential remains unclear. In this study, we investigated the effects of long-term TRF on senescent adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) in a high-fat diet (HFD) induced aged mice model. Mice were assigned to standard or HFD diets under ad libitum or TRF (8 h/day) regimens for 7 months. TRF effectively attenuated HFD-induced weight gain and metabolic inflexibility. Functionally, TRF preserved ADSC morphology and mitochondrial integrity, restored proliferation and migration capacity. Restored balanced lineage differentiation and markedly reduced senescence markers, reactive oxygen species, and inflammatory cytokines. TRF was associated with increased expression of Oct4, Sox2, and Klf4 (OSK) in ADSCs. Lentiviral overexpression of OSK partially recapitulated restoration-associated phenotypes in vitro. However, while OSK overexpression was sufficient to induce these changes, the present data do not establish a necessary role for OSK in mediating TRF-induced effects. Analysis of adipose tissue was consistent with the cell assay, confirming that TRF alleviated fibrosis and inflammation in aged adipose tissue. We find TRF as a noninvasive, physiologically safe intervention to restore aged stem cell function and tissue homeostasis during aging. Subject terms: Cell biology, Stem cells

Introduction

Aging is a complex biological process characterized by degenerative changes and functional decline across multiple tissues and organs, making it a major risk factor for various chronic diseases, including cancer, cardiovascular diseases, and metabolic disorders. With the rapid aging of the global population, research aimed at delaying or even reversing aging has become an urgent priority in biomedical science1. Although chronological aging and metabolic stress-associated conditions, such as those induced by a high-fat diet, are distinct biological processes, they share certain overlapping features, including increased cellular senescence and inflammatory activation.

Among the cellular and tissue-level changes that accompany aging, stem cell exhaustion plays a central role. Stem cells possess self-renewal and multipotent differentiation capabilities; however, their functional decline and depletion with age lead to reduced physiological functions in multiple organs and tissues, profoundly impacting regenerative medicine and tissue engineering2. Adipose tissue exhibits a particularly strong response to age-related perturbations, with adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) being among the most sensitive cell types to aging3. The aging of ADSCs is directly linked to adipose tissue dysfunction and the progression of age-related diseases. Studies across multiple species have confirmed that age-associated changes in visceral fat depots contribute to systemic inflammation and metabolic dysfunction4,5.

When adipose tissue undergoes abnormal expansion due to excessive nutrient intake or physical inactivity, adipocyte dysfunction occurs, triggering both local and systemic inflammation and exacerbating metabolic disorders6. Restoring the balance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory adipokines holds promise for preventing obesity-related metabolic diseases. Excess energy intake from a high-fat diet is a primary driver of obesity, and dietary interventions involving diet restriction have been widely used to investigate the effects of diet on fat deposition and glucose-lipid metabolism in aged mice7. Dietary restriction has been shown to restore multiple antioxidant metabolites and reduce oxidative stress in the intestines of aged mice. These findings highlight the complexity of adipose tissue aging and its systemic consequences.

Time-restricted feeding (TRF) is a dietary intervention that modulates energy intake by restricting the feeding window, and it has been shown to effectively delay aging, improve metabolic disorders, and extend lifespan8. Emerging evidence suggests that TRF influences lipid metabolism and ameliorates aging phenotypes. As ADSCs progressively lose their proliferative and differentiation potential with age, their decline contributes to adipose tissue dysfunction and systemic metabolic dysregulation. TRF has been shown to reduce inflammation-associated factors such as IL-1 in adipose tissue and inhibit excessive macrophage infiltration, thereby mitigating chronic systemic inflammation9. Unlike caloric restriction, TRF maintains normal caloric intake while aligning feeding with circadian rhythms, thus offering better clinical feasibility and compliance10. Furthermore, recent studies indicate that dietary intervention can modulate the expression of key stemness-related transcription factors, including Oct-4, Sox-2, Klf-4, and Myc (OSKM). These factors play essential roles in maintaining the self-renewal capacity and multipotency of stem cells, and their age-associated downregulation is linked to stem cell exhaustion and impaired tissue regeneration11,12. TRF-mediated restoration of OSKM expression may contribute to improved stem cell function, potentially reversing age-related declines in ADSC proliferation and differentiation. However, the precise mechanisms by which TRF improves metabolic parameters and extends lifespan remain incompletely understood, as multiple aging-associated signaling pathways are involved in these interventions13.

This study aims to investigate how TRF modulates lipid metabolism to counteract aging, with a specific focus on reversing the aging of ADSCs. We will examine the effects of TRF on ADSC proliferation, differentiation potential, and overall aging phenotypes, while elucidating its underlying molecular mechanisms, particularly in relation to OSKM expression. Through this research, we seek to provide novel insights into aging reversal strategies and offer a potential therapeutic approach for aging-related diseases. Our findings will establish whether TRF restores ADSC function by improving lipid metabolism, promoting adipocyte remodeling, reducing oxidative stress, and enhancing the expression of stemness-related transcription factors, ultimately reversing the aging process.

Results

Long-term TRF mitigates HFD-induced metabolic dysfunction and preserves metabolic flexibility

HFD consumption is a well-established inducer of metabolic dysregulation and has been closely associated with accelerated aging phenotypes14. 14-month-old male mice were fed either standard chow or HFD under ad libitum or TRF regimens for 7 months (Fig. 1a). Consistent with previous studies, mice in the Old + HFD group exhibited significant increases in body weight and adiposity compared with age-matched controls, as confirmed by both body weight trajectories and CT-derived fat mass percentage. TRF intervention (Old + HFD + TRF) partially reversed these HFD-induced changes, attenuating both body weight gain and adipose tissue accumulation, although it did not fully return to those of young controls (Fig. 1b, c).

TRF alleviates HFD-induced obesity and improves metabolic parameters in aged mice.

a Experimental design of dietary interventions: aged mice were subjected to normal diet (Old), normal diet with TRF (Old + TRF), high-fat diet (Old + HFD), or high-fat diet with TRF (Old + HFD + TRF) for 7 months. b Body weight changes throughout the intervention period. c Body fat percentage measured by CT imaging. d–g Serum lipid profiles: d triglycerides (TG), e total cholesterol (TC), f high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and g low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C). Data are shown as mean ± SD; n = 8 per group. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01.

Serum lipid profiling revealed no significant differences in triglyceride (TG) levels among groups. By contrast, total cholesterol (TC), LDL-C, and HDL-C were significantly elevated in HFD-fed aged mice (Old+HFD) relative to age-matched controls, reflecting impaired lipid homeostasis under chronic nutrient overload (Fig. 1d–g). This coordinated pattern may reflect broader remodeling of systemic lipid metabolism. Mice receiving TRF in combination with HFD (Old+HFD + TRF) displayed only mild elevations in these parameters, suggesting the protective effect of TRF against diet-induced dyslipidemia15. Within the LDL-C profile, we observed that TRF-fed aged mice (Old+TRF) exhibited slightly higher LDL-C levels compared with naturally aged controls (Old), while showing no significant difference from young mice. TRF restored LDL-C to levels indistinguishable from those of young mice. This may be linked to TRF’s circadian alignment and its improving effects on ADSCs. Reinvigorated ADSCs enhance adipose tissue remodeling, support healthier lipid turnover, and sustain systemic lipid homeostasis.

TRF improves activity rhythms and partially restores metabolic flexibility

To evaluate systemic metabolic adaptations, mice were subjected to metabolic cage analyses measuring food intake, locomotor activity, and respiratory exchange ratio (RER). Total daily food intake was not significantly different among groups, suggesting that the metabolic benefits of TRF were not attributable to changes in caloric consumption (Fig. 2a, b). Locomotor activity analysis revealed that naturally aged mice (Old) and HFD-fed mice (Old + HFD) exhibited significantly reduced spontaneous activity compared with young controls, reflecting age-related behavioral decline (Fig. 2c). TRF intervention restored locomotor activity toward youthful levels, particularly during the dark phase, when mice are naturally more active (Fig. 2d). Nocturnal activity in TRF-treated mice closely resembled that of young controls, indicating a restoration of circadian behavioral rhythms. RER analysis showed that HFD-fed mice (Old + HFD) displayed a consistent bias toward lipid oxidation, with mean RER values approximating 0.7, indicating impaired substrate flexibility and reduced carbohydrate utilization (Fig. 2e). TRF partially altered this metabolic pattern in HFD-fed mice (Old + HFD + TRF), restoring limited diurnal oscillation; no statistically significant differences were observed compared with the Old + HFD group (Fig. 2f). While TRF substantially improved feeding rhythm and locomotor activity, its ability to restore metabolic flexibility in the context of long-term HFD feeding was only partial.

TRF improves locomotor activity rhythms and partially restores metabolic flexibility.

a, b Total daily food intake measured by metabolic cage analysis showed no significant differences among groups (n = 8). c Locomotor activity was significantly reduced in aged (Old) and HFD-fed (Old + HFD) mice compared with young controls. d TRF restored locomotor activity to near-youthful levels, particularly during the dark phase, with activity patterns closely resembling those of young controls. e Respiratory exchange ratio (RER) profiles demonstrated that Old + HFD mice exhibited a predominant reliance on lipid oxidation (mean RER ≈ 0.7). f TRF partially restored diurnal oscillations in RER in HFD-fed mice, though no statistically significant differences were observed compared with Old + HFD. Data are presented as mean ± SEM, n = 8 per group. Metabolic parameters were continuously monitored with a temporal resolution of 6.5 min.

Age- and diet-induced reductions in physical activity and metabolic flexibility contribute to a pro-inflammatory and lipotoxic microenvironment that accelerates ADSC senescence and impairs their regenerative potential. By restoring circadian feeding rhythms and enhancing nocturnal activity, TRF likely reduces metabolic stress and inflammatory signaling, thereby creating a more supportive niche for ADSCs. This systemic reprogramming may underlie the observed restoration of ADSCs in TRF-treated mice, linking behavioral and metabolic improvements to stem cell preservation and tissue homeostasis.

TRF reverses the aging and metabolic stress-associated phenotype of ADSCs

ADSCs were isolated from the subcutaneous adipose tissue of mice subjected to various interventions. Senescent ADSCs exhibited distinct morphological alterations under phase-contrast microscopy, including increased surface secretions and transformation from spindle/triangular shapes to flattened, irregular configurations with reduced refractivity (Fig. 3a). Ultrastructural analysis via transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that aged ADSCs displayed cellular enlargement accompanied by abundant rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER), apoptotic lysosomes (ASS), and significantly increased secondary lysosomes compared to young counterparts16. Mitochondrial abnormalities were prominent in senescent cells, manifesting as fragmentation, vacuolization, and swelling. Conversely, TRF mice maintained ADSCs with mitochondrial characteristics resembling young cells, exhibiting dense and numerous mitochondria (Fig. 3b).

TRF restores ADSCs’ morphology and reduces senescence markers.

Aged cells exhibited mitochondrial swelling, vacuolization, and ER dilation. TRF maintained dense and intact mitochondrial architecture. c Western blot analysis and densitometric quantification of p16, p21, and p53 in ADSCs. d–f Densitometric quantification of CDKIs. g Representative images of SA-β-gal staining. h Quantification of SA-β-gal-positive cells. Scale bars: 20 µm. Data are presented as mean ± SD. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01.

Western blot analysis confirmed that TRF-mediated suppression of cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors (CDKIs) was associated with restored proliferative capacity. Cell cycle profiling revealed significant upregulation of CDKIs in senescent ADSCs, with p21 exhibiting the most pronounced elevation, followed by p16 and p53 (Fig. 3c–f). This aligns with established senescence markers, as p21high populations are recognized hallmarks of aged tissues in both physiological aging and obesity models4,17. TRF effectively normalized p21 expression and attenuated HFD-induced p21 hyperactivation alone. In addition, no significant changes were found in the surface markers (CD44, CD105) of senescent fat stem cells (Fig. 3c). CD45 expression remained negligible across groups, consistent with maintenance of mesenchymal identity. HFD mice exhibited accelerated ADSCs senescence phenotypes18, which were partially attenuated by TRF, although the magnitude of improvement appeared less pronounced compared to the normal diet group. Senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining confirmed these findings, with TRF-induced groups showing staining intensity similar to young controls (Fig. 3g, h).

TRF mitigates inflammation and restores stemness in aged and stressed ADSCs

Both aging and HFD were associated with increased inflammatory activation in ADSCs, although these represent distinct biological contexts, contributing to their functional decline. Western blot analysis of isolated ADSCs revealed markedly elevated expression of inflammatory cytokines IL-1, IL-6, and IL-10 in the Old + HFD group, reflecting heightened inflammatory stress14. TRF intervention significantly suppressed these cytokines in aged ADSCs, restoring their expression to levels close to those observed in young controls (Fig. 4a, b). Consistent with inflammatory findings, reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were significantly increased in ADSCs from Old and Old + HFD mice. TRF treatment substantially alleviated this oxidative burden, with ROS fluorescence intensity returning to near-baseline levels (Fig. 4c), further supporting TRF’s antioxidative effects at the stem cell level. These results demonstrate that TRF attenuates inflammation and oxidative stress in aged ADSCs and contributes to improved cellular homeostasis and regenerative potential.

TRF restores proliferative, migratory, and differentiation capacity of aged and stressed ADSCs

To assess the impact of TRF on ADSCs’ function, we evaluated cellular proliferation, migration, and lineage differentiation potential in aged and diet-induced senescent ADSCs. CCK-8 assays demonstrated a marked reduction in the proliferative capacity of ADSCs isolated from Old and Old + HFD mice, while TRF intervention significantly restored this capacity to levels comparable with young controls (Fig. 5a). Consistently, wound healing assays revealed impaired migratory ability in Old and Old + HFD ADSCs, which was substantially ameliorated by long-term TRF treatment (Fig. 5b, c). EdU (5-Ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine) staining assay further confirmed the proliferative benefit of TRF. Aged ADSCs exhibited reduced S-phase entry, indicative of diminished proliferative potential, whereas TRF restored the proportion of EdU-positive cells, even under HFD-induced aging (Fig. 5d, e).

TRF rescues the proliferation, migration, and differentiation capacity of aged ADSCs.

a CCK-8 cell viability assay. b Scratch assay showing migration capacity. c Quantification of wound closure area. d Quantification of EdU-positive cells. e EdU staining of proliferating cells. f, g mRNA expression of adipogenic markers (Cebpa, Pparγ). h, i mRNA expression of osteogenic markers (Alp, Runx2). Scale bars: 20 µm. Data are presented as mean ± SD. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001.

Lineage differentiation assays revealed that aging and HFD exposure biased ADSCs toward adipogenesis at the expense of osteogenesis. qRT-PCR analysis showed elevated expression of adipogenic markers Cebpa and Pparγ (Fig. 5f, g), accompanied by a reduction in osteogenic regulators Alp and Runx2 (Fig. 5h, i). TRF intervention effectively reversed this imbalance, restoring a more youthful differentiation signature characterized by enhanced osteogenic and attenuated adipogenic marker expression19. These findings indicate that TRF exerts a multifaceted restoring effect on ADSCs, promoting proliferation, enhancing migration, and restoring multipotent differentiation potential. These effects are maintained even under metabolic stress induced by HFD, supporting the robustness of TRF as an anti-aging intervention in stem cell biology.

TRF mitigates adipose tissue aging and restores stemness signatures in vivo

ADSCs from TRF-treated mice exhibited enhanced expression of key pluripotency transcription factors. We assessed the expression of reprogramming OSKM transcription factors, qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated that TRF significantly upregulated the expression of Oct4, Sox2, and Klf4 (OSK), whereas c-Myc expression exhibited a non-significant change (Fig. 6a–d). While c-Myc is essential for reprogramming, it is also associated with oncogenic potential. The lack of c-Myc induction under TRF suggests that TRF preferentially enhances stemness through activation of the OSK axis without promoting tumorigenic risk.

TRF ameliorates adipose tissue aging and restores stemness markers in vivo.

a–d qRT-PCR expression of Oct4, Sox2, Klf4, and c-Myc. e H&E staining of inguinal adipose tissue. f p21 immunohistochemical staining. g IL-1 immunohistochemistry. h Sox2 immunostaining. i Pdgfrα immunostaining. j–m Quantitative analysis of IHC staining intensity (n = 8). Scale bars: 200 µm. Data are presented as mean ± SD. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001.

To determine whether the TRF-mediated functional capacity of ADSCs is mirrored at the tissue level, we performed histological analyses on the inguinal subcutaneous adipose tissue across different treatment groups. H&E staining revealed substantial adipocyte hypertrophy and increased interstitial fibrosis in aged mice subjected to a high-fat diet (Old + HFD), indicative of pathological remodeling (Fig. 6e). TRF intervention significantly reduced adipocyte size and mitigated fibrosis, restoring tissue morphology to a state comparable to that observed in young controls20.

IHC staining for the senescence marker p21 showed robust upregulation in adipose tissue of Old and Old + HFD mice, consistent with age- and obesity-related cellular senescence. TRF treatment markedly suppressed p21 expression (Fig. 6f), as quantified in Fig. 6j, supporting its anti-senescent efficacy in vivo. The inflammatory cytokine IL-1 was significantly elevated in the adipose tissue of HFD-fed mice, reflecting chronic low-grade inflammation (Fig. 6g). Quantification analysis (Fig. 6k) demonstrated that TRF significantly downregulated IL-1β expression, suggesting effective attenuation of the pro-inflammatory microenvironment.

To evaluate the impact of TRF on tissue stemness, we assessed the expression of Sox2, a key transcription factor regulating pluripotency. Sox2 expression was significantly reduced in Old and Old + HFD adipose tissues, while TRF restored its expression to near-youthful levels (Fig. 6h), with statistical analysis presented in Fig. 6l. These findings are in line with our in vitro results and underscore the capacity of TRF to preserve stem cell identity in vivo. We further analyzed Pdgfrα, a well-established marker for adipose-derived progenitor cells21, which plays a critical role in regulating lipid storage and metabolic homeostasis. In both Old and Old + HFD groups, Pdgfrα expression was notably decreased, indicating compromised progenitor cell presence or function. TRF significantly upregulated Pdgfrα expression (Fig. 6i), as shown in the quantification (Fig. 6m), suggesting a restoration of progenitor abundance and multipotency within the adipose niche. TRF preserves progenitor cell populations and stemness markers, reinforcing its potential as a systemic anti-aging intervention.

OSK partial reprogramming-like transcriptional activation rejuvenates senescent ADSCs

To further elucidate the long-term fate and plasticity of ADSCs subjected to TRF, we investigated whether direct reprogramming of senescent ADSCs could recapitulate a youthful phenotype11. Senescent ADSCs isolated from the Old group mice were transfected with a lentivirus vector (LV) carrying the OSK plasmid. The design of the experimental strategy is illustrated in Fig. 7a. Western blot analysis confirmed successful overexpression of OSK proteins in Old@Transfection ADSCs (Fig. 7b), with statistical quantification provided in Fig. 7c. Parallel qRT-PCR validation further confirmed robust upregulation of Oct4, Sox2, and Klf4 transcripts (Fig. 7d–f), consistent with effective cellular reprogramming.

Exogenous OSK expression mimics TRF-induced ADSC rejuvenation.

a Schematic of LV-mediated OSK delivery in senescent ADSCs. b Western blot analysis of OSK expression post-transfection. c Quantification of OSK protein levels. d–f qRT-PCR of Oct4, Sox2, and Klf4 mRNA expression. g Quantification of EdU-positive proliferating cells. h EdU immunofluorescence images. i Quantification of SA-β-gal-positive cells. j Representative SA-β-gal staining images. Scale bars: 20 µm. Data are presented as mean ± SD. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001.

Functionally, OSK overexpression restored proliferative capacity in senescent ADSCs, as indicated by EdU staining assays (Fig. 7h), with quantitative data revealing a significant increase in S-phase cell proportion of Old@Transfection ADSCs (Fig. 7g). This reversal of proliferation decline highlights the potential of OSK activation in Old@Transfection ADSCs. SA-β-gal staining demonstrated a pronounced reduction of senescent ADSCs upon OSK expression (Fig. 7j), with quantitative analysis confirming a statistically significant decrease in SA-β-gal-positive cells compared to non-reprogrammed controls (Fig. 7i). These data reveal that targeted expression of OSK in senescent ADSCs is sufficient to reverse cellular senescence phenotypes and restore regenerative functionality 22.

Our prior findings demonstrated that long-term TRF alone was sufficient to induce endogenous upregulation of Oct4, Sox2, and Klf4. This pattern of selective OSK activation suggests that TRF could promote a partial reprogramming-like activation state through physiological mechanisms, thereby enhancing stem cell plasticity and function in a safer, non-integrating manner. In contrast to exogenous reprogramming strategies that carry the risk of genomic instability or malignant transformation23, TRF-induced OSK modulation represents a more moderate and controllable approach to enhancing stem cells. Our findings highlight the potential of TRF as a translationally viable intervention to restore stem cell function and delay tissue aging through intrinsic, low-risk reprogramming pathways.

Discussion

Aging is characterized by progressive stem cell exhaustion and chronic inflammation, which together undermine tissue homeostasis and regeneration24. In adipose tissue, age-related alterations in the immune milieu markedly impair ADSC self-renewal and lineage plasticity, thereby accelerating adipose degeneration14. The similar directional changes observed in HDL-C and LDL-C may represent global metabolic normalization rather than isolated lipoprotein-specific effects. Here, we identify TRF as a non-genetic intervention that alleviates systemic and local inflammation, accompanied by selectively reactivating core stemness transcription factors25. TRF reversed the senescence-associated expression of p16, p21, and p53, mitigated oxidative stress, and normalized the aberrant lineage commitment of ADSCs. Transcriptomic data and immunodetection collectively confirmed that aged ADSCs displayed activated inflammatory signaling and downregulation of immune clearance pathways—patterns significantly restored by TRF26. This suggests that TRF acts, at least in part, through remodeling of the immune niche.

It is important to distinguish between the effects of chronological aging and those induced by HFD-associated metabolic stress. While both conditions resulted in impaired ADSC function and increased inflammatory activation, they represent biologically distinct processes. Chronological aging reflects time-dependent cellular and systemic changes, whereas HFD induces metabolic perturbations, including lipid overload and inflammation. Therefore, findings observed in HFD models should not be interpreted as equivalent to physiological aging. In this study, we interpret HFD-related effects as reflecting metabolic stress-associated impairment, which may partially overlap with, but is not identical to, aging-related changes.

Our data identify that TRF may act as a non-genetic inducer of stemness-related transcriptional programs27. Specifically, TRF upregulated Oct4, Sox2, and Klf4 (OSK), without significantly altering c-Myc transcriptional expression. It should be noted that c-Myc expression was assessed at the mRNA level only. Protein abundance, downstream transcriptional activity, and potential transient activation were not evaluated in this study. Therefore, no conclusions regarding oncogenic safety can be drawn from the present data, and further investigation is required. The OSK-only reactivation implies that TRF may induce a partial reprogramming-like activation state sufficient to restore ADSC function, while circumventing the safety concerns associated with full iPSC reprogramming11. To further probe causality, we introduced OSK into aged ADSCs via lentiviral delivery. This exogenous reprogramming restored proliferative activity and reduced senescence markers to levels comparable to TRF intervention. Although increased OSK expression was observed following TRF, and ectopic OSK expression was sufficient to induce restoration-associated phenotypes in vitro, these findings do not establish a necessary role for OSK in mediating TRF effects. Loss-of-function approaches would be required to determine causality. Due to the limited viability of senescent ADSCs under further genetic perturbation, such experiments were not feasible in the current study and remain an important direction for future investigation.

Mechanistically, the TRF-OSK axis may involve epigenetic modifications23,28, including promoter histone acetylation and DNA demethylation, although this remains to be directly investigated in our model. Prior studies have implicated NAD⁺/SIRT1 and circadian regulators in similar contexts, suggesting possible upstream convergence24,29,30. The TRF-induced OSK reactivation may be mediated by epigenetic remodeling. Future studies integrating ChIP-seq and methylome profiling are needed to confirm this. While our study did not directly evaluate these pathways, they provide plausible targets for future mechanistic dissection. The translational implications of our findings merit emphasis. Unlike integrative reprogramming approaches, TRF represents a noninvasive, physiologically compatible intervention that modulates stem cell function without genomic manipulation14,22. Given the complexity of aging and the heterogeneity of stem cell compartments, it will be critical to assess whether TRF-mediated restoration extends to other mesenchymal or tissue-specific stem cell populations7,31,32. For instance, skeletal muscle satellite cells or intestinal stem cells may respond similarly, given their shared regulation by systemic metabolic and circadian cues.

We identify TRF as a promising anti-aging intervention that restores ADSC function associated with selectively activating endogenous OSK expression. By restoring metabolic flexibility, reducing oxidative and inflammatory stress, and preserving lineage balance, TRF enhances ADSC function both in vitro and in vivo. These findings identify TRF as a physiologically relevant intervention that mimics aspects of OSK activation and offer new insights into metabolism-based strategies for stem cell restoration. These findings support TRF as a safe strategy and conceptual framework for the development of dietary or pharmacologic mimetics targeting the transcriptional axis to combat aging and age-associated diseases. Clinical implementation of TRF still faces challenges, including dietary compliance, individual metabolic variability, and sustainability of its anti-aging effects. Furthermore, the duration and timing of TRF required to maintain stem cell fitness in humans remain undefined33. Whether TRF improves the functional capacity of other stem cell populations (e.g., muscle satellite cells, bone marrow MSCs) warrants further exploration. Future studies integrating longitudinal omics profiling and functional assays across multiple stem cell types will be essential to evaluate the long-term safety and efficacy of TRF in translational aging models.

Methods

Animals and treatment

Male C57BL/6 mice were obtained from the Hubei Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention. To establish an aging model, mice were fed a standard chow diet from 3 months of age until 14 months of age. At this point, animals were randomly assigned to the following experimental groups to construct a high-fat diet (HFD)-induced accelerated aging model: naturally aged control group (Old), aged mice with HFD (Old + HFD), aged mice with time-restricted feeding (Old + TRF), and aged mice with both HFD and TRF intervention (Old+HFD + TRF). The dietary intervention lasted for 7 months. During this period, food intake and body weight were monitored weekly. The HFD contained a fat content approximately ten times greater than that of the standard chow diet. In TRF groups, mice were allowed access to food only during an 8-hour window in the dark phase, from Zeitgeber time (ZT) 15 to ZT23. All mice were housed under standard laboratory conditions (21 °C, 55% relative humidity, 12-h light/dark cycle). During tissue harvesting, mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane in oxygen using a calibrated vaporizer. At the end of the procedure, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation under deep anesthesia. All experimental procedures involving animals were conducted in strict accordance with the guidelines and regulations set forth by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Wuhan University of Science and Technology (Approval No. WUST202020). This study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines for animal research.

Respiratory exchange ratio (RER) analysis

Respiratory exchange ratio (RER) was measured using a comprehensive laboratory animal monitoring system. Oxygen consumption (VO2) and carbon dioxide production (VCO2) were continuously recorded, and RER was calculated as VCO2/VO2. Mice were acclimated to metabolic cages for 24 h prior to data collection.

Isolation and culture of mouse ADSCs

Freshly extracted adipose tissue was stored in Dulbecco’s Phosphate-Buffered Saline (DPBS; Gibco, USA) with 100 U/mL penicillin-G(Servicebio, China), 100 mg/mL streptomycin (Servicebio, China) at 4 °C, and transported to the laboratory within 6 h of extraction. The adipose tissue was cut by hydraulic forceps, minced, and digested in a solution of 4 mg/mL neutral protease (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) and 3 mg/mL collagenase type I (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 30 min at 37 °C. The digested mixtures were centrifuged to remove digestive enzymes and cultured in Minimum Essential Medium α (α-MEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA). Cells were maintained in an atmosphere of 5% CO2 at 37 °C and collected into cell clones when the cell colony formation units reached 80% confluence. The cells were maintained in α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS, penicillin, and streptomycin and passaged every 3–4 days. Cells in passages 3 were used for this study. The microstructure of ADSCs was detected by Transmission electron microscopy (HITACHI, HT7700, Japan).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis

ADSCs were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer overnight at 4 °C, post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated through graded ethanol, and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections (70–90 nm) were cut and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Images were acquired using a transmission electron microscope (HT7700, HITACHI) at 80 kV.

Western blotting

A Western blot was performed to identify the specific markers contained in ADSCs. Using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, CN), protein concentrations were determined. In total, 30 mg of total protein was loaded onto a 10% SDS-PAGE gel for each sample. NC membranes were incubated with primary CD44, CD45, CD146, p16, p21, p53 (Abcam, USA, 1:1000), IL-1, IL-6, IL-10, Oct-4, Sox-2, Klf4 (Proteintech, CN, 1:1000) and β-actin (1:5000, CN, Servicebio) overnight at 4 °C, after being blocked with protein free rapid blocking buffer (Epizyme, CN) for 15 min at room temperature to prevent non-specific absorption.

RNA isolation and real-time polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was extracted from ADSCs using RNAiso easy reagent (TaKaRa, JPN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (TaKaRa, JPN). Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was carried out using a Bio-Rad Real-Time System. Each experiment was repeated three times to determine differences in relative gene expression. Primer sequences have been included in Supplementary Table S1.

Cell migration assay

ADSCs were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured to 90% confluence. Then the cells were scratched with a pipette tip. Images of the cells were obtained at 0, 24, and 48 h via a microscope. Migration areas were measured by the ImageJ software.

Cell proliferation rate

For the cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8; Life-iLab, China) assay, ADSCs were seeded in a 96-well plate at 103 cells/well. After incubation for 1–4 days, add 10 μL solution of CCK-8 to each well, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The absorbance of various groups was measured at 450 nm using an enzyme-labeling device (PerkinElmer EnSight, USA).

EdU staining

ADSCs were inoculated into 6-well plates with a density of 1 × 105 cells per well, and after incubation for 24 h, EdU (Beyotime Biotechnology) was added to the medium until the final concentration was 10 μM. The cells were incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 48 h. The cells are immobilized, infiltrated, treated with a reaction solution, and finally stained. The samples were observed and imaged by fluorescence microscopy.

SA-β-Gal staining

ADSCs (1 × 105) were seeded per well in 6-well plates. Cells were fixed and stained with an SA-β-gal staining kit (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells positive for the SA-β-Gal activity were determined by observing cells under an inverted microscope (Olympus, Japan).

ROS analysis

The ROS Assay Kit (Beyotime, China) was used to detect the intracellular ROS generation. ADSCs (1 × 105) were seeded in a 6-well plate. The cells were incubated in 5 μM of DCFDA for 20 min at 37 °C followed by washing 3 times with 1× PBS and after that, the plates were fixed with DAPI for 10 min. The images of each group of cells were captured under an inverted microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Histological and immunofluorescence analyses

The inguinal adipose specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. After washing with PBS for 2–3 times, the sample is embedded in paraffin wax for sectioning. Paraffin sections (4 μm thick) were stained by HE and immunofluorescence according to instructions. The stained sections were observed under Leica light. Immunofluorescence staining was used to evaluate the expression of p21, IL-1, Sox-2, and Pdgfrα in the specimens. Nuclei were stained with DAPI. Microscopic images were collected and analyzed using ImageJ. Staining intensity was quantified in high-magnification fields, with emphasis on nuclear signal distribution for transcription factors.

Cell transfection

The stably overexpressing cell lines were constructed by transferring lentivirus into ADSCs according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Beyotime, China). The expression plasmids encoding Oct4, Sox-2, and Klf4 were purchased from Sangon Biotech. 1 × 105 ADSCs were seeded into 12-well plates. After adhering, cells were transfected with plasmids using Lipo3000 reagents according to the protocol from the manufacturer.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted utilizing Prism 8.0 (Graphpad Software, California, USA). The unpaired Student’s t-test was utilized for statistical comparisons between two groups. For multiple comparisons, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.
