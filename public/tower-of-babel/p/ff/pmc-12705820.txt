At-home self-collection device offers an effective and preferred method to engage high BMI women in cervical cancer screening: method comparison study
Authors: Clair Kaplan, LaShonda Crane, Ann Collins, R. Lamar Parker, Ronald Orso, Colleen McNicholas, Elizabeth Sutton, Lisa Memmel, Christine Conageski, Youri Hwang, Lindsay Kuroki, Jenell Coleman, Sangini S. Sheth, Kimberly Harshberger, Ashley Jennings, Emeline Aviki, Diane Harper, Meghna Mukherjee, Karl Hibler, Megan Burke Fitzpatrick
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionCervical cancer (CxCa) screening rates are lower among women with high BMI in the US, leading to increased prevalence and worse outcomes from cervical dysplasia and cancer in this group. The main barriers to participation in screening relate to the in-clinic speculum exam, which could be overcome with an at-home self-collect (SC) screening device optimized for differing body types.MethodsThis prospective method comparison study recruited 609 screening-eligible participants aged 25 to 65 years, enrolled from November 20, 2023, to April 5, 2024. SC was performed by the participant in a simulated home environment, using a novel device optimized for differing body sizes. Eligible participants collected a vaginal sample with the SC device, followed immediately by a clinician-collection (CC) sample using a speculum and broom. The sample pairs were shipped to the lab, where the SC sample was eluted into PreservCyt and tested on an FDA-approved high-risk human papillomavirus (hrHPV) test approved for primary screening. Participants completed usability and preference surveys. Endpoints included the detection of hrHPV between SC and CC samples, as well as other study measures, such as clinical sensitivity for high-grade cervical dysplasia, usability, and preferences by BMI category. Five hundred ninety (590/599) participants had data available for this sub-analysis, based on body mass index (height and weight were recorded via self-report). Results and preferences of participants with a higher BMI (≥ 35) are highlighted herein.ResultsBMI ranged from 16 to 66 for the entire study group, with 114/590 (19.3%) representing a BMI of 35 or greater, comprising the analysis group. The agreement for detection of hrHPV was equivalent across BMI categories. Over a third (36.8%) of participants in Class II (BMI: 35–40) and Class III (BMI > 40) categories had delayed or avoided their cervical cancer screening, and 91% reported that they would be more likely to stay up to date with routine screening if an at-home self-collect option were available. Preferences for self-collect among women in Class II + categories were higher for a self-collect option for screening, reporting that SC made them feel more in control of their experience.DiscussionIn women with a BMI ≥ 35, cervical cancer screening by SC with this uniquely designed device demonstrated equivalent or superior HPV detection performance and was strongly preferred by participants. Offering this option could improve screening rates in women with BMI ≥ 35, a group that represents a significant portion of the US population who delay screening and help reduce disparities in cervical cancer incidence and outcomes.

Introduction

Cervical cancer (CxCa) rates have been decreasing for decades due to vaccination, screening, and early treatment of precancerous lesions. Still, among certain groups of women in the US, these rates remain elevated. This is particularly true among women with excess weight, where detection of precancers is lower and cervical cancer incidence increases with increasing body mass index (BMI) [1, 2]. Overweight is defined as a BMI of 25–29.9, with obesity starting at a BMI of 30 and severe obesity at a BMI of 40 or higher [3, 4]. In the United States, 41.9% of adult women are estimated to have a BMI of 30 or higher, with 11.5% having a BMI of 40 or higher. Obesity (BMI 30 +) is further classified into three distinct categories: Class I (BMI: 30–34.9), Class II (BMI: 35–39.9), and Class III (severe), which includes a BMI of 40 or higher [5].

Women with excess weight are less likely to participate in CxCa screening due to their reported worse experience with speculum exams, perceived lack of respect from healthcare providers, and reluctance to undergo the exam [2, 6–8]. Providers may delay or avoid the exam in women with higher BMIs due to inadequate tools (length of speculum and difficulty of the exam) [9]. Patients delay due to embarrassment, discomfort, and biases experienced from the provider [6, 7, 9–11]. The stigma experienced during healthcare visits is highest among those with weight categorized in Class II (BMI: 35–39.9) and Class III (BMI: 40 +), with worse health outcomes due at least in part to the increased stress and avoidance of health maintenance due to this societal stigma [11, 12]. The combination of these factors leads to women in BMI Classes II and III being less likely to participate in CxCa screening, by 25% and 40%, respectively [13]. Weight stigma also contributes to delayed screening due to provider behavior, where 83% of physicians are reluctant to perform a speculum exam on high BMI patients due to the difficulty of the exam, time spent, and challenge visualizing the cervix, and biased attitudes towards this group are reported among 69% of physicians and 46% of nurses [12]. Providers also experience difficulty identifying precancerous lesions during cervical screening in high BMI participants, while cervical cancers are diagnosed more frequently [1, 2, 13].

Advances in technology for cervical screening in the last decade have enabled primary screening for CxCa with HPV testing, which is possible via self-collection (SC) [14–16]. SC is only recently FDA approved in the US (May 2024) for the healthcare setting and for the at-home setting (May 2025). Most SC devices have not been designed with differing body habitus in mind, nor data to demonstrate performance for screening and preferences among high BMI participants.

SELF-CERV, a prospective method comparison study, evaluated the performance of a SC device optimized for at-home use in differing body types (the Teal Wand, Fig. 1) for detection of HPV primary CxCa screening, an acceptable screening method endorsed by United States Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) and American Cancer Society (ACS). Methods

The study design was a prospective, multi-center, method comparison study, with a primary outcome to measure agreement between self-collected (using the Teal Wand, designed for at-home use) and clinician-collected samples for hrHPV test results. Eligible participants were recruited from 16 US investigational sites representing academic and community health practices and were enrolled from November 20, 2023, to April 5, 2024 [17]. Participants were required to be within primary HPV guideline-indicated screening ages (25–65 years old) [1, 4], with an intact cervix. Participants were enrolled from two groups: (1) enriched, meaning they had a recent (within 6 months) positive hrHPV test and/or an abnormal cytology result, or were being referred for LEEP or other excisional procedures (with presumptive positive hrHPV status) and (2) a general screening group. Those with pregnancy, vaginal bleeding, and an excisional procedure in the prior 5 months were excluded from participation. All participants were required to be willing and able to provide informed consent, which they did before engaging in any study activities. Participants received IRB-approved reasonable compensation for their time. A central IRB (Advarra) and two institutional IRBs provided oversight for the protection of human subjects. The trial was registered on clinicaltrials.gov (NCT06120205).

Self-collected (SC) samples were first obtained by the participant using written and pictorial instructions for use (IFU) for the Teal Wand. Participants were provided a private area for SC, simulating an at-home environment, and followed the steps in the IFU. The participant then detached the collection sponge from the device and placed it in an empty vial. No fluid or media was required. The vagina and cervix were examined prior to clinician collection to assess for any adverse events from use of the SC device. A clinician-collected sample (CC) was obtained following the SC for method comparison using a Rovers Cervex-Brush (Rovers Medical Devices B.V., Lekstaat, the Netherlands), then transferred into a 20 mL vial of PreservCyt (ThinPrep®, Hologic, Inc., Marlborough, MA). Following the CC procedure, safety was reassessed, and any notable events were recorded. When biopsy or LEEP/excisional procedures were indicated, the histopathology results were used to calculate clinical sensitivity for detection of high-grade cervical dysplasia (HSIL including cervical intraepithelial lesion 2 [moderate severity] or more: CIN2+), and the highest severity diagnoses present were used to calculate clinical sensitivity (a non-powered study measure).

HPV testing

SC samples were stored dry in the empty vial for up to 9 days before a laboratory technician eluted the sponge into 20 mL of PreservCyt. Samples from self- and clinician-collection were tested using the Roche cobas® hrHPV test on the Roche cobas 8800 System(s) (Roche Molecular Systems Inc., Rotkreuz, Switzerland), which detects 14 hrHPV types with partial genotyping for HPV16, HPV18, and 12 other hrHPV (a pooled result inclusive of HPV31/33/35/39/45/51/52/56/58/59/66/68).

Demographics, usability, safety and preferences

Self-reported participant information, including height, weight, race, ethnicity, income, education, prior cervical screening history, and preferences, was recorded. Surveys about the usability of the Teal Wand were given to participants following their SC procedure. The survey consisted of questions evaluating the participants’ ability to follow written instructions and their perception of the device’s use. Responses were either binary (yes/no) or a 5-point Likert scale. Some questions allowed for open-ended fields to expand on the selected response, and a general open-ended response field was included for additional comments at the end. Results were tabulated as percentages (binary) or averages. In this study, participants in Classes II and III (BMI ≥ 35) were grouped into a single analytical category, which aligns with prior research methodologies and enhances the statistical power of the experiences, preferences, and performance data presented. This grouping also enables a more comprehensive analysis of the self-collect device and experience, addressing the specific needs of this 35 + BMI population to overcome their shared barriers to screening [11, 12]. An additional survey about patient preferences was also collected and tabulated. Safety was assessed after the collection procedures and between 6 and 14 days after study procedures through an email survey or phone call to the patient.

Statistical analysis

The sample size for the overall study was determined based on agreement statistics for positive and negative agreement between SC and CC samples, with point estimates corresponding to a relative sensitivity exceeding 0.95 and a relative specificity exceeding 1.00. Positive percent agreement (PPA) of 87% and negative percent agreement (NPA) of 88% with a lower bound of 80% (95% CI) yielded a minimum sample size of n=225 HPV+ and n=198 HPV- subjects. Enrollment continued until a minimum number of HPV16-positive cases was met, as an FDA requirement. In this study, dichotomous categories of BMI <35 (Less than or equal to Class I) and Class II+ were used to observe evident differences based on BMI.

This approach is supported by literature, which suggests that the healthcare experiences of Class I obesity are different than those with BMI ≥35. Classes II and III (BMI≥35) share similar experiences, including lower participation in screening, increased stigma, and increased rates of CaCx incidence [11, 12]. Furthermore, this analysis approach has been used in primary studies (e.g., NHANES, ACS reports) due to shared outcomes and determinants [3, 4]. The primary purpose of these groupings was to gain a better understanding of the experiences of individuals in Class II and III, their preferences for self-collect, and the performance of the self-collect device in this specific cohort.

Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 29.0.2.0 (20). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics. Basic summaries of diagnostic test results were tabulated, including the dichotomized test result and adequacy of the sample collected. A statistical significance test (Chi-squared, Fisher's exact, or linear-by-linear, where applicable) was conducted to ascertain significance between groups. P<.05 was considered statistically significant.

Clinical sensitivity for HSIL/CIN2+

As a non-powered study measure, clinical sensitivity for detection of high-grade cervical dysplasia (HSIL including cervical intraepithelial lesion 2 [moderate severity] or more: CIN2+) was evaluated as follows: if a biopsy/loop electrosurgical procedure (LEEP) was collected during or closely following the study visit, the de-identified histopathology results were collected, and the highest severity diagnoses present were used to calculate clinical sensitivity. The clinical sensitivity for detection of HSIL/CIN2+ was comparable between SC and CC among participants with a BMI < 35. Usability

There were no significant differences in the usability of the Wand when comparing the BMI categories (Class II + , Class I, and Normal weight), as indicated by survey data. The data show that the Wand was easy to use and that instructions were equally comprehensible. For example, in both groups, 99% of participants relied only on the instructions provided to complete their sample collection, over 98% stated that the instructions on how to position the Wand and guide it into their vagina was clear, and 93% reported that the overall process to use the instructions to self-collect a sample with the Wand was easy or very easy. On average, participants in both groups took between 3 and 5 min to complete their sample collection (which included reading the instructions). They reported feeling “confident” that they had successfully followed the instructions to collect a sample. The invalid rate for the entire group was 2.3%, and only 0.9% of samples were invalid among SC samples in participants with a BMI ≥ 35 (1/114). This indicates that the device design and instructions are adequate for women with higher BMIs.

Preferences

Participants reported a strong preference for self-collect at home to stay up to date with screening: When asked, “If at-home self-collect were an option, would you be more likely to stay up to date with routine cervical screening?” 84.4% (401/475) of participants with a ≤ Class I responded “yes” compared to 91.2% (103/113) of participants with a Class II + (p = 0.41). Participants with a BMI categorized as Class II + specifically provided additional comments on the SC option being favorable to them due to the comfort (citing specifically pain of the speculum and prior sexual trauma), privacy (citing embarrassment), and the convenience of at-home for time and other logistics that cause barriers (child care, ride to clinic, flexible timing of at-home SC). Over a third (36.8%) of participants in Class II (BMI: 35–40) and Class III (BMI 40 +) categories had delayed or avoided their cervical screening, while 91% reported they would be more likely to stay up to date with routine screening with an at-home self-collect option.

Adverse events

For all participants, there were no serious or unexpected adverse events. Only two events were attributed to the use of the SC device, which were mild and transient (one participant with mild cervical abrasion noted on CC exam without associated bleeding and the other participant with self-reported spotting following SC), neither of which occurred in a subject with a BMI Class II + . The SC device and procedure were safe in all subjects.

Discussion

Overall, our study demonstrated that SC is comparable to CC and preferred by people with high BMI. This study’s population was representative of the US population; 37.6% (222/590) of participants had a BMI over 30, and 7.8% (46/590) had a BMI over 40. The performance of the Teal Wand was equivalent to the clinician-collection for detection of HPV, showing high PPA and NPA for the entire study population, and this did not differ for participants who reported Class I < BMI and those with Class II + BMI [17]. BMI was not a factor that influenced the results of SC using the Teal Wand, nor the usability of this uniquely designed device, which is longer than other SC devices available. There was no difference in invalid rates in this population. Furthermore, clinical sensitivity for CIN2+ among those with a BMI ≥ 35 showed equivalent performance for detection of HPV and dysplasia, showing the potential for SC to overcome barriers experienced by CC, which include difficulty reaching the cervix and inadequacy of existing tools (speculums and collection devices) in addition to the reluctance of high BMI women to undertake the speculum exam. Women with excess weight (Class II + BMI) in the study strongly preferred SC as an option. They indicated that their experience was preferable to the in-clinic speculum exam and clinician collection, reporting they found the SC device to be much more comfortable than the clinician collection. As a result, 91.2% of participants with a BMI ≥ 35 would be more likely to engage in CxCa screening with this option.

The rates of obesity are steadily increasing in the US, where 39.2% of women have a BMI over 30, and 9.4% have a BMI over 40 [5]. People with a BMI ≥ 35 show significantly lower rates of CxCa screening compared to those with a lower BMI [2, 7–9, 13, 18, 19]. The lower screening participation among those with a BMI ≥ 35 and higher rates of CxCa are likely due to a confluence of factors, including lower screening participation, increased weight stigma, difficulty in visualizing precancerous lesions, and inadequate removal of precancerous lesions [8, 9, 12, 13, 19].

Innovative and practical solutions designed to engage people with excess weight in CxCa screening are imperative, given the increased rates of delayed diagnoses resulting in poorer outcomes, and the substantial portion of the US population in these categories [2, 13]. SC for HPV screening presents an opportunity to engage more women in the screening process. Still, the performance and acceptability of the available devices have not been rigorously evaluated, and other FDA-approved devices are not optimized for the specific requirements of SC in people with high BMI. The Teal Wand vaginal SC device is FDA-approved and designed for varying vaginal lengths and body sizes, to ensure that more women can engage in screening, thereby increasing overall participation in CxCa screening. The authors note that other SC devices currently FDA-approved in the United States (Rovers Evalyn Brush and Copan Floq swab) lack supporting performance and usability data among high BMI participants with redundant labial tissue, which is a constraint that this device was designed to address. These other devices are also currently only approved for use in healthcare settings and do not offer the advantage of an at-home SC experience, for which the Teal Wand is designed.

This SELF-CERV study demonstrated high accuracy across study participants, regardless of body habitus, showing that the use of the Teal Wand produces equivalent results to those of clinician-collected samples and equivalent clinical sensitivity for CIN2+ [17]. When evaluating participants by BMI, these results hold for individuals with a Class II + BMI, a group that may be more reluctant to screen due to difficulty with the speculum exam. As a result, these women are at higher risk for later detection of dysplasia, based upon screening avoidance and the inadequacy of tools that accommodate their body habitus. Women in this study, classified as Class II + , strongly preferred SC to CC and reported that they would be more likely to screen with this SC option (91%). The approval and adoption of self-collect with a user-friendly design optimized for different body types will increase options that overcome the most frequently cited barriers to engagement in screening among those with high BMI.

Limitations

We were unable to completely rule out the role of confounding variables, including income and education, which may also contribute to the differences in participants’ experiences and performance.
