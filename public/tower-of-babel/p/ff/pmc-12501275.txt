One-step degradation of 4 classes of β-lactam using β-lactamase enzyme cocktail
Authors: Jiafu Lin, Tiantian Wang, Yaliang Zhou, Tao Cheng, Haisha Liu, Xueke Chen, Wan Luo, Qiyue Liu, Yiwen Chu, Xinrong Wang, Feng Xie, Jingzhou Sha, Tao Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntibiotics, particularly β-lactams, are emerging environmental contaminants that induce antibiotic-resistant bacteria or genes, which threaten public health. Enzymatic degradation is an environmentally friendly approach to reducing residual antibiotics; however, its application is limited by enzyme’s narrow substrate spectrum, which cannot deal with the co-occurrence of multiple β-lactam classes in the environment. To address this issue, four representative β-lactamases were biochemically characterized and two of them (CTX-33 to VIM-1) were selected because of their broader substrate spectrum and better stability. Compared to a single enzyme that can only degrade the ring structure of three classes of β-lactam antibiotics at most, the enzyme cocktail (with a ratio of CTX-33 to VIM-1 of 4:1) was able to hydrolyze nineteen antibiotics from four different β-lactam families. In laboratory experiments, the enzyme cocktail demonstrated degradation efficiencies of over 99% for the four classes of β-lactams (penicillin, cephalosporin, carbapenem, and monobactam) within the concentration range of 1 to 100 mg/L, both individually and simultaneously. Moreover, when applied to pharmaceutical industry wastewater, pig farm wastewater, and river water, over 99% simultaneous degradation of β-lactams was observed at 1 mg/L within 60 min and at 100 mg/L within 5 h. This is the first report of an enzymatic method capable of simultaneously removing antibiotics from four β-lactam classes. This study offers a novel and eco-friendly approach to β-lactam degradation.Keywords: Emerging contaminants, Antibiotic, β-lactamase, Enzyme degradationSubject terms: Biological techniques, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Hydrology

Introduction

Β-lactam antibiotics, which possess a β-lactam ring, make up over 55% of the global antibiotic market and are widely used as antimicrobial agents to treat bacterial infections1. Researchers have developed 4 classes of β-lactams (penicillin, cephalosporin, carbapenem, and monobactam) to broaden their antimicrobial efficacy and spectrum2. However, extensive use of β-lactam antibiotics in humans and livestock results in more than 46% being excreted unaltered into environments. Additionally, the use of β-lactam antibiotics as growth promoters, along with pharmaceutical industrial discharges and improper disposal of expired medications, contributes to contamination of ecosystems, including rivers, soil, and air3. The presence of β-lactam antibiotics in the environment exerts selective pressure on environmental bacteria, compelling them to mutate or acquire resistance genes through conjugation, transduction, and transformation, thereby enhancing their survival4,5. Consequently, environmentally resistant bacteria become reservoirs of resistance genes, elevating the risk of these genes being transferred to clinically significant bacteria, thereby significantly undermining the therapeutic effectiveness of β-lactams6. Hence, diminishing the levels of residual β-lactam antibiotics in the environment is crucial for lessening the prevalence of resistant bacteria and their genes, which is essential to preserve the clinical efficacy of β-lactam antibiotics.

Presently, β-lactam antibiotics could be removed from contaminated water using methods such as physical adsorption, membrane filtration, photolysis, chemical bioelectrical systems, and oxidation7. These conventional physical and chemical techniques often involve high operational costs due to expensive equipment and materials8,9. On the other hand, biological methods are gaining attention for their specificity, environmental friendliness, and cost-effectiveness10. The application of enzymes, specifically β-lactamases, offers two benefits11. Firstly, enzymatic treatment is a green and environmentally friendly approach as enzymes are natural biodegradable products. Secondly, β-lactamase are highly specific to β-lactam antibiotics without potential off-target by-products12. Thirdly, using a cell-free expression system or a yeast expression system can avoid the spread of antibiotic resistance gene and reduce the cost13. Currently, there is limited research on the enzymatic removal of β-lactam antibiotics, greatly limiting the application of enzymatic methods for the removal of β-lactam antibiotics14,15.

β-lactamases are known for their ability to hydrolyze the lactam-ring, and reduce antimicrobial ability16. Based on sequence similarities, β-lactamases are divided into 4 main classes: serine-β-lactamases (class A, C, and D) and metal-dependent β-lactamases (class B)17. Substrate spectrum of β-lactamases varies across these different enzyme classes. For instance, class A β-lactamases predominantly degrade penicillin and cephalosporin18, while class B β-lactamases are known for their broad activities against penicillin, cephalosporins, and carbapenems19. Class C β-lactamases can degrade cephalosporins and certain carbapenems20, and class D β-lactamases are effective against penicillin, cephalosporin, and carbapenem21. A single enzyme is insufficient for the simultaneous removal of antibiotics from all different β-lactam families. For example, the enzymes used for β-lactam degradation are from class B or class A and cannot simultaneously remove antibiotics from all four β-lactam families12.

Enzyme’s narrow substrate specificity presents a obstacle in degradation various β-lactams from the environment. This study selected four representative β-lactamases and investigated their enzymatic properties. Subsequently, a cocktail combining two of these β-lactamases was formulated to broaden the substrate spectrum. The effectiveness of this enzyme cocktail in simultaneously removing β-lactams from various sources, including rivers, pharmaceutical industry wastewater, and pig farm effluents, was evaluated. The ecological risks of the enzymatic degradation products were also evaluated. The exploration of β-lactamase cocktail offers valuable insights for other researchers and may accelerate the development of effective environmental antibiotic removal processes.

Results and discussion

Biochemical characterization of β-lactamase from class A/B/C/D families

To systematically screen for β-lactamases with potential industrial applications, we retrieved a total of 8,154 β-lactamases from the BLDB database (Fig. 1A). The criteria for lactamases selection were based on three key factors: occurrence in E. coli (as E. coli was used for subsequent expression), solubility in expression (to enhance the success rate of protein expression), and the number of β-lactam classes they can degrade (to broaden the application range) (Supplementary material 1). From 8154 lactamases, we chosen 4 enzymes: CTX-33 from Class A, VIM-1 from Class B, ACT-3 from Class C, and OXA-65 from Class D. Each of these enzymes was not only previously reported in E. coli but also demonstrated soluble expression and the ability to degrade more than two classes of β-lactam antibiotics. SDS-PAGE analysis verified that the purified enzymes were solubly expressed and had > 90% purities. The estimated molecular weights of CTX-33, VIM-1, ACT-3, and OXA-65 in SDS-PAGE, were 28, 25, 40, and 29 kDa, respectively, consistent with their predicted molecular weights (Fig. 1B–E and Supplementary material 2). The inhibition zone experiment demonstrated that enzyme treated ampicillin no longer had inhibition zone while untreated ampicillin had (Fig. 1F–I). This indicated that the four enzymes CTX-33, VIM-1, ACT-3, and OXA-65 are capable of inactivating ampicillin and abolishing its antimicrobial activity. This result suggested that enzymatically degraded antibiotics may no longer exert selective pressure on bacteria, a critical precondition for reducing the emergence and spread of antibiotic-resistant bacteria or genes22.

The optimal temperatures for β-lactamases CTX-33, VIM-1, ACT-3, and OXA-65 were found to be 30, 40, 40, and 30 °C, respectively (Fig. 2A). Regarding the thermal stability of the enzymes, CTX-33 and VIM-1 exhibited thermal stability, retaining 85 and 65% of their activity, respectively, after 1 h of treatment at 100 °C (Fig. 2B). The optimal pH for these enzymes were 8, 7, 8, and 7, respectively (Fig. 2C). Among them, ACT-3 were active in a broad pH range (3 to 11), with 45% to 100% activity. ACT-3 was the most pH-stable enzyme, exhibiting minimal activity loss across diverse pH levels (Fig. 2D). The optimal conditions for all 4 β-lactamases—CTX-33, VIM-1, ACT-3, and OXA-65—were within a temperature range of 30–40 °C and a pH of 7–8, aligning with the temperatures of their ecological environments and natural habitats23.

Heavy metal ions have a significant impact on enzyme activity. Cu2⁺ completely inhibited CTX-33, VIM-1, and OXA-65 at 1 mM, and inhibited ACT-3 at 10 mM. Cr3⁺ inhibited ACT-3 and OXA-65 at both 1 and 10 mM, and inhibited CTX-33 at 10 mM; however, VIM-1 maintained enzyme activity at both 1 and 10 mM (Supplementary material 3). While, CTX-33 and OXA-65 maintained 20–50% activity in SDS, DTAB, and Tween 20. VIM-1 showed partial activity in 10 mM SDS, DTAB, and 5 mM Tween 20, while ACT-3 was active in 15 mM DTAB (Supplementary material 4A–C). Ethanol, methanol, and DMSO affected enzyme activity differently, inhibiting all four enzymes at concentrations above 50%. Interestingly, VIM-1 retained 25% activity at 90% methanol concentration (Supplementary material 4D,E). VIM-1’s tolerance to methanol may be related to its three-dimensional structure, such as the flexibility of the catalytic pocket and the high charge distribution on the protein surface24. However, the exact mechanism underlying this tolerance requires further validation.

In our study, CTX-33 showed the capacity to degrade 16 β-lactam antibiotics, including 4 penicillin-class antibiotics, 11 cephalosporin-class antibiotics, and 1 monobactam (Supplementary material 5). OXA-65 was capable of degrading 13 β-lactam antibiotics, including, 3 penicillin-class antibiotics, 9 cephalosporin-class antibiotics, and 1 carbapenem (Supplementary material 5). ACT-3 efficiently degraded 14 β-lactam antibiotics, including 3 penicillin-class antibiotics and 11 cephalosporin-class antibiotics (Supplementary material 5). Notably, VIM-1 demonstrated a broader range, 16 β-lactam antibiotics, including 4 penicillin-class antibiotics, 11 cephalosporin-class antibiotics, and 1 carbapenem (Supplementary material 5). Based on their capacity to degrade antibiotics across different classes, the enzymes can be ranked in terms of substrate spectrum as follows: VIM-1 > CTX-33 > ACT-3 > OXA-65. It is worth noting that both VIM-1 and CTX-33 can only degrade antibiotics from three β-lactam families, not all four.

Optimization of enzyme cocktail proportions

Selection of enzyme cocktail

As a single enzyme could only degrade three classes at most, an enzyme cocktail was proposed to achieve the simultaneous degradation of antibiotics from four β-lactam classes. The broad substrate spectrum and enzyme stability were crucial for their application, guiding our selection process. Radar results showed that CTX-33 and VIM-1 had high thermostability scores (0.8644 and 0.8281, respectively) and good substrate spectrum scores (0.8889 and 0.8889, respectively) (see Supplementary Material 6 and Fig. 2E). Notably, the substrates degraded by VIM-1 and CTX-33 are complementary, and their combination can degrade 18 antibiotics in four β-lactam classes. Based on these results, we selected CTX-33 and VIM-1, the enzymes with the highest thermostability score and substrate spectrum scores, to achieve the simultaneous degradation of four classes of β-lactam antibiotics.

Optimization of enzyme cocktail proportion

To further analyze the competitive or synergistic effects between CTX-33 and VIM-1, cocktail ratios were investigated. Enzyme cocktails with different ratios could degrade 18 β-lactam antibiotics from 4 β-lactam families (Supplementary Material 7). Meanwhile, enzyme activities increased by 6.0, 6.7, 7.3 and 10.0% at different enzyme cocktail ratios (4:1, 3:2, 2:3, and 1:4) (Fig. 2F). Among them, enzyme cocktail (4:1) could had synergistic effect against 12 substrates, which was more than those of other three. Therefore, we used an enzyme ratio of CTX-33: VIM-1 = 4:1 for the enzyme cocktail.

Degradation of β-lactam antibiotics in laboratory

We investigated the degradation efficiency of 4 representative β-lactam antibiotics individually in laboratory. When inactivated enzyme was used as control group, degradation efficiencies of ampicillin, cefotaxime, imipenem, and aztreonam were 5.5–4.0%, 8.8–14.3%, 4.9–6.8%, and 3.5–8.1%, respectively. When enzymes cocktail was added, after 60 min, the degradation efficiencies of 100 mg/L ampicillin and cefotaxime reached 99.95 and 99.17%, respectively (Fig. 3A,B). When the concentration of these antibiotics was reduced to 10 mg/L, their degradation efficiencies escalated to 99.89% for ampicillin and 99.94% for cefotaxime within 40 min (Fig. 3A,B). At a lower concentration of 1 mg/L, the degradation efficiency for cefotaxime and ampicillin reached 99.97 and 99.04% respectively, in 30 min (Fig. 3A,B). The degradation efficiency of imipenem by the β-lactamase was comparatively slower. At a concentration of 100 mg/L, the degradation efficiency was 53.59% at 60 min and reached 99.00% by 120 min (Fig. 3C). For concentrations of 10 mg/L, it took 45 min to achieve a degradation efficiency of 99.00%, and at 1 mg/L, degradation time was 15 min (Fig. 3C). In the case of 100 mg/L aztreonam, degradation efficiency was 99.59% at 105 min, while degradation efficiency of 10 mg/L or 1 mg/L aztreonam was 99.98% in 30 min (Fig. 3D). According to the Kd values at a substrate concentration of 100 mg/L, the effectiveness degradation was as follows: cefotaxime (0.064 min-1) > aztreonam (0.060 min-1) > ampicillin (0.028 min-1) > imipenem (0.018 min-1) (Supplementary material 8).

Given the presence of different classes of β-lactams in environments, we further investigated the efficacy of enzymes cocktail in removing 4 classes of β-lactam antibiotics simultaneously. When inactivated enzyme was added as control, 91% ampicillin, 86% ceftriaxone, 94% imipenem, and 90% aztreonam were detected after 5 h (Fig. 3E,F). When the concentration of 4 antibiotics were 100 mg/L, the degradation efficiencies for ampicillin and cefotaxime reached 99.50% and 99.78%, respectively, after 210 min (Fig. 3E). In contrast, the degradation efficiencies for imipenem and aztreonam at the same time point were 79.89 and 66.90%, respectively. However, after extending the duration to 300 min, the degradation efficiencies for these two antibiotics increased to 99.15 and 99.44% (Fig. 3E). According to the Kd values at a substrate concentration of 100 mg/L, the effectiveness of degradation was as follows: ampicillin (0.012 min-1) > cefotaxime 0.009 min-1) > imipenem (0.005 min-1) > aztreonam (0.004 min-1) (Supplementary material 9).

It is noteworthy that when imipenem and aztreonam are present simultaneously, Kd value of imipenem (0.005 min-1) is higher than that of aztreonam (0.004 min-1). However, when they exist separately, Kd value of aztreonam (0.060 min-1) is higher than that of imipenem (0.018 min-1). This difference is likely due to the substrate competition effect between the different β-lactams in the mixture, and the specific reasons will require further investigation in the future.

Current research about enzymatic removal of β-lactam antibiotics focused on single, two or three classes of β-lactam antibiotics. β-lactamase was used to degrade ampicillin (penicillin class), amoxicillin (penicillin class), and meropenem (carbapenem class) at the same time12,25. For instance, researchers used a β-lactamase for penicillin degradation (penicillin class), and another β-lactamase was used to remove penicillin (penicillin class), cefotaxime (cephalosporin class), and ceftriaxone (cephalosporin class)14,15. Therefore, as far as we know, there has been no enzymatic method could remove all 4 classes of β-lactam antibiotics simultaneously. Given the widespread use of these 4 classes of β-lactam antibiotics in clinical and veterinary fields, and reports of their concurrent residues in the environment26, the technique for simultaneous removal of these 4 classes of β-lactam antibiotics developed in this study holds significant promise. They could be applied in hospitals, pharmaceutical companies, and livestock farms where the concentration of β-lactam antibiotics is relatively high27, as well as in rivers where the concentration is relatively low28. This approach could avoid the need to develop new enzyme for different classes of β-lactam and had an application potential.

Degradation products of antibiotics by the β-lactamase

To confirm the catalytic process of β-lactamase, ESI–MS was used to study the β-lactam (ampicillin, ceftriaxone, imipenem, and aztreonam) before and after degradation. As shown in the Fig. 4, the molecular weights of 4 β-lactam antibiotics changed. For example, prior to degradation of ampicillin, a signal peak at 349 m/z can be detected, while the hydrolysis product (367 m/z) is absent. After degradation, the mass spectrum shows the disappearance of the 349 m/z peak and the emergence of the open-ring product at 367 m/z. Similar observations can be made for cefotaxime, which increased from 456 to 473 m/z, aztreonam, which changed from 434 to 452 m/z, and imipenem, which increased from 300 to 318 m/z (Fig. 4 and Supplementary material 10). Following enzymatic degradation by β-lactamase, the molecular weights of the antibiotics all increased by 18, indicating that upon hydrolysis of the β-lactam ring, an additional water molecule was added.

Simultaneously degradation of antibiotics in different water samples

Considering that real-world environments were more complex than laboratory conditions, we further explored the effectiveness of enzymes cocktail in pharmaceutical wastewater (pH 8.4), pig farm wastewater (pH 8.9), and river water (pH 7.6). Not surprisingly, when inactivated enzyme was used as control, the degradation efficiency of ampicillin, cefotaxime, imipenem and aztreonam at 100 mg/L or 1 mg/L were detected 9.3–15.6%, 6.0–13.7%, 2.7–15.9% and 6.8–14.8% respectively (Fig. 5) The degradation of β-lactam antibiotics is likely due to the relative instability of their β-lactam ring structure. This instability causes β-lactam antibiotics to degrade faster than tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones. However, the presence of residual β-lactam antibiotics and β-lactam-resistant bacteria in the environment indicates that β-lactam degradation is not complete, which leads to the emergence of antibiotic-resistant bacteria.

When the concentrations of the 4 antibiotics were at 100 mg/L, the degradation efficiency of cefotaxime reached 99.99% within 1 h in all three types of water samples (Fig. 5A,C,E). After 1 h of treatment with enzymes cocktail, the degradation efficiencies of ampicillin were 97.68% in river water, 92.40% in pharmaceutical wastewater, and 98.39% in pig farm wastewater. Extending the treatment time to 5 h led to a degradation efficiency of 99.99% for ampicillin in all three wastewater types. The degradation efficiencies of imipenem in the three types of water samples reached 96.00% within 2 h after adding enzymes and increased to 99.9% with prolonged treatment up to 5 h. For aztreonam, after 1 h of enzyme treatment, the degradation efficiencies were 82.64%, 82.34%, and 70.40% in river water, pharmaceutical wastewater, and pig farm wastewater, respectively. While the degradation efficiency reached 99.99% in river water and pharmaceutical wastewater after 5 h, the degradation efficiency in livestock wastewater reached 99.13% (Fig. 5A,C,E). The slower degradation efficiency of aztreonam in livestock wastewater compared to river water and pharmaceutical effluents was likely due to the higher concentrations of ammonia–nitrogen and organic matter, which may affect enzyme activity. When the concentrations of each antibiotic were reduced to 1 mg/L, all 4 antibiotics achieved a degradation efficiency of 99.00% within 60 min in 3 water samples (Fig. 5B,D,F).

The simultaneous enzymatic degradation method could remove four classes β-lactam antibiotics at the same time, which was seldomly investigated. It achieved or even surpasses the performance of other methods in terms of degradation efficiency (> 99%), degradation time (1–5 h), and antibiotic concentration (1–100 mg/L) (Table 1). Compared with existing methods, enzymatic degradation offers the following advantages: (1) environmental friendliness: enzymatic method only added proteins into the system, which could be easily degraded by microbes. While other methods added substances which might cause secondary pollution, for example Fenton oxidation added Fe ions, photocatalysis needed TiO2 or ZnO, and adsorption methods needed adsorbents. (2) low cost: it is low-cost because it does not require specialized equipment (like UV light for photocatalysis) or additional reagents (such as Fe ions for Fenton oxidation, adsorbents for adsorption, or nutrients for biodegradation). The cost of enzymes can be further reduced by using engineered bacteria that can be cultured with inexpensive carbon sources. (3) simplicity of process: enzymatic method only needed to add enzyme into the system, whereas Fenton oxidation requires repeated pH adjustments, and adsorption methods need the removal of adsorbents, which may require extra processes and equipment.

Conclusion

Simultaneous removal of antibiotics from four β-lactam families is crucial for enzyme application. This study compared representative β-lactamases from 4 β-lactamase families and selecting enzyme cocktail (CTX-33: VIM-1 at 4:1 ratio) for β-lactam degradation. The enzyme cocktail could degrade4 classes of β-lactam antibiotics simultaneously from diverse water samples, underscores the practical applicability and efficiency of this novel approach. The success of enzymatic method not only provides an alternative approach for β-lactam antibiotic removal but also is indicative for the degradation of other antibiotic classes.

Materials and methods

β-lactamase screen, expression and purification

Information on β-lactamases was retrieved from the BLDB database, including experimental evidence such as occurrence in Escherichia coli, solubility of expression, and the types of substrates that can be degraded. In this study, 4 β-lactamase genes representing different lactamase classes were selected for analysis. These included blaCTX-33 (Class A, from Escherichia coli, accession ID: KF696720.1), blaVIM-1 (Class B, from Achromobacter denitrificans, accession ID: AJ278514.1), blaACT-3 (Class C, from Klebsiella pneumoniae, accession ID: EF125013.1), and blaOXA-65 (Class D, from Acinetobacter baumannii, accession ID: AY750908.1). β-lactamase genes were synthesized into the BamH I and Not I restriction sites of the pET-28a( +) vector. The vectors were subsequently transformed into E. coli TOP10 for plasmid maintenance and into E. coli BL21 (DE3) for protein expression29.

E.coli BL21 (DE3) colonies containing synthesized plasmids were inoculated into LB medium supplemented with 100 μg/mL kanamycin. When the OD600nm reached 0.5–0.6, 1 mM IPTG (Isopropyl β-D-Thiogalactoside) was added and incubated at 25 °C for 12 h. Following induction, cells were harvested by centrifugation at 6500 g for 10 min. The cell pellet was disrupted using sonication and then centrifuged at 12000 g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cell debris. The supernatant was subjected to purification using the IMAC method. The purity of the β-lactamase was visualized by SDS-PAGE30.

Inhibition zone analysis

Antimicrobial activity of enzyme hydrolyzed β-lactam antibiotics was assessed using the agar well diffusion method31. Initially, 2 μg of purified β-lactamase was mixed with 120 μg/mL ampicillin, incubated at 37 °C for 30 min and filtered by 0.22 μm membrane. Concurrently, LB medium with E. coli BL21 (DE3) was gently mixed and poured onto plastic petri dish and left to solidify. Wells of 8 mm diameter were aseptically punched into the agar and 50 μL of the sample mixture was added to each well. The plates were incubated at 37 °C overnight. The inhibition zones were measured using a ruler.

Enzyme assay of free β-lactamase

The enzymatic activity of purified β-lactamase was quantified using ampicillin as substrate. For the assay, 50 μL of purified β-lactamase was added to 950 μL of ampicillin solution (120 μg/mL) in 20 mM Tris–HCl buffer (pH 7.0). The mixture was thoroughly mixed and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. The reaction was terminated by adding methanol. For enzymes that are tolerant to methanol, heat inactivation at 100℃ for 10 min was used. The optical density at 235 nm (OD235nm) was measured using a spectrophotometer to determine the enzyme activity32. Enzyme activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to produce 1 μmol of penicilloic acid per minute under standard conditions14. All experiments were performed in triplicate, unless otherwise specified. The concentration of β-lactamase used for enzymatic property studies was 0.04 mg/mL.

Biochemical characterization of β-lactamase

Optimum temperature and thermostability

After purified β-lactamase was added to a solution containing 120 μg/mL ampicillin in 20 mM Tris–HCl buffer (pH 7.0), and the reaction was performed at 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80, 90, and 100 °C for 15 min29. Then the enzyme activity was measured by the method described Sect. “Enzyme assay of free β-lactamase”. To test the thermal stability, purified β-lactamase was incubated in a water bath at10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80, 90 and 100℃ for 1 h, respectively. Then the enzyme activity was tested as described in Sect. “Enzyme assay of free β-lactamase”.

Optimum pH and pH stability

Ampicillin solutions were prepared using Britton-Robinson pH buffers (pH 3–11), and enzyme activity was measured using the method described in Sect. “Enzyme assay of free β-lactamase”. To test pH stability, purified β-lactamase was added to different pH buffer and incubated at 4 ℃ for 1 h. Then the enzyme activity was tested as described in Sect. “Enzyme assay of free β-lactamase”.

Effect of metal ions and chemical reagents on enzyme activity

The effects of 12 metal ions (Li+, K+, Zn2+, Ca2+, Ni2+, Mg2+, Mn2+, Cr2+, Co2+, Ba2+, Cu2+, Al3+), For each reagent, 100 mM stock was added to the reaction mixture to achieve a final concentration of 1 mM and 10 mM,—and the enzyme activity was assayed as described in Sect. “Enzyme assay of free β-lactamase”. The positive control was defined as 100% enzymatic activity in the absence of metal ions. The blank control was identical to the experimental group, except that an inactivated enzyme solution was used.

Effect of detergent and organic solvents on enzyme activity

The impact of three detergents (SDS, DTAB, and Tween20) and three organic reagents (methanol, ethanol and dimethyl sulfoxide) on enzyme activity was investigated. In the reaction system, 5 mM, 10 mM, 15 mM and 20 mM of SDS and DTAB were added, respectively. Additionally, 2.5, 5, 7.5 and 10 mM of Tween20, and 10, 30, 50, 80, and 100% of organic reagents were added. And the enzyme activity was assayed as described in Sect. “Enzyme assay of free β-lactamase”. The positive control was defined as 100% enzymatic activity in the absence of organic reagents and detergents. The blank control was identical to the experimental group, except that an inactivated enzyme solution was used.

Substrate specificity of β-lactamase

To investigate the substrate specificity of β-lactamase, 18 β-lactam antibiotics from 4 classes were selected. These included 4 penicillins (Penicillin, Penicillin V, Carbenicillin, Ampicillin), 12 cephalosporins (cephalexin, cephalothin, cefazolin, cefathiamidine, cefaclor, cefonicid sodium, ceftizoxime, ceftriaxone sodium, cefotaxime, moxalactam sodium, ceftazidime and cefepime), 1 carbapenem (imipenem), and 1 monobactam (aztreonam). Purified β-lactamase was added to each β-lactam antibiotic at a concentration of 120 μg/mL and inactivated β-lactamase was used as control group. The reactions were conducted at 30 °C for 30 min. The procedure for the enzyme activity assay was similar to that described in Sect. “Degradation of β-lactam antibiotics in laboratory”. The optical density was measured at different wavelengths appropriate for each antibiotic, and the antibiotic concentration was calculated using its corresponding molar absorptivity (Supplementary material 11).

Comprehensive score of enzyme characteristics

We comprehensively scored enzymatic characteristics of different β-lactamases. For each enzymatic characteristic, each condition is scored with the maximum relative enzymatic activity being 100 points and the minimum being 0 points. If the enzymatic activity is 10%, it is scored as 10. The scores for each enzymatic property are summed up and then normalized using the MAX–MIN method. After calculating the scores for each enzymatic property for each enzyme, radar charts are plotted.

Optimization of enzyme cocktail

To investigate the potential synergistic or competitive effects and optimize the activity of the enzyme cocktail, different ratios of CTX-33 and VIM-1 were tested for their enzymatic activity on 18 β-lactams. A certain amount of the β-lactamase cocktail, varying in proportions of CTX-33 and VIM-1, was added to 1 mL of β-lactams (concentration: 20–120 μg/mL). The enzyme activity was assessed using the method described in Sect. “Degradation of β-lactam antibiotics in laboratory”.

Enzymatic degradation of β-lactams in laboratory

To investigate the effectiveness of enzymes cocktail in removing antibiotics from aqueous solutions, the dynamic changes in antibiotic concentrations during the hydrolysis process were monitored. The enzymes cocktail was added to solutions containing β-lactam antibiotics (ampicillin, cefotaxime, imipenem, and aztreonam) at varying concentrations (1, 10, and 100 mg/L) prepared in 20 mM Tris–HCl buffer (pH 7.0). The reaction systems were incubated at 37 °C, and supernatants were collected at specified time intervals. Subsequently, to further evaluate the degradation capability of the enzymes cocktail against multiple mixed antibiotics, a 30 mL mixed antibiotic solution was prepared in Tris–HCl buffer (pH 7.0) containing all four antibiotics (ampicillin, cefotaxime, imipenem, and aztreonam), with each antibiotic set at a final concentration of 1 or 100 mg/L. To terminate the enzymatic reaction, a final concentration of 50% methanol was added14,33. Inactivated enzyme complex preparation was used as the control group under identical experimental conditions.

ESI–MS analysis of enzymatic hydrolysis products

To explore the degradation mechanism, the enzymes cocktail were added to 20 mM Tris–HCl buffer (pH 7.0) contained 100 μg/mL β-lactam antibiotics (ampicillin, ceftriaxone, imipenem, and aztreonam), and then incubated at 37 °C for 3 h, followed by filtration through a 0.22 μm micromembrane filter. Then ESI–MS was utilized to investigate the presence of antibiotic mass spectral signals before and after degradation, as well as the signals of the degradation products. This approach is primarily used for the qualitative identification of products before and after the degradation of antibiotics.

Enzymatic removal of β-lactams in different water samples

To evaluate the degradation efficiency of enzymes cocktail on antibiotics in various environmental water samples, we collected effluents from a pharmaceutical company, wastewater from a pig farm, and river water samples in Chengdu, Sichuan Province, China. After removing sediments through centrifugal filtration, 4 β-lactam antibiotics were added to each sample at concentrations of 100 and 1 mg/L. Subsequently, 6 U enzyme cocktail was introduced into 30 mL of antibiotic-containing wastewater. The degradation efficiency of antibiotics was assessed at 37 °C over different time intervals. Antibiotics were then extracted using solid-phase extraction and quantified using UPLC-MS/MS (A-30/ABSciex Qtrap 3200, Japan). Inactivated enzyme complex preparation was used as the control group under identical experimental conditions.
