E2F1-mediated 53BP2 lactylation stabilizes p53 to induce cochlear hair cell apoptosis in mouse age-related hearing loss
Authors: Xiaoqin Luo, Jun Liu, Chanyuan Zhang, Wei Yuan, Gang Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Age-related hearing loss (ARHL) is a prevalent sensory deficit characterized by cochlear hair cell apoptosis, yet the underlying epigenetic mechanisms remain unclear. This study investigated the role of E2F1-mediated 53BP2 lactylation in ARHL pathogenesis using naturally aging C57BL/6J mice and HEI-OC1 cochlear hair cell-like cells. We found that E2F1 was significantly upregulated in aged cochleae, correlating with elevated ABR thresholds, hair cell loss, and apoptotic marker expression. In vitro, E2F1 overexpression promoted HEI-OC1 cell apoptosis by stabilizing p53, while E2F1 knockdown attenuated p53 accumulation and cell death under oxidative stress. Mechanistically, mass spectrometry identified 53BP2 lactylation at lysine 476 (K476), which was enhanced by E2F1. The K476R mutation abolished 53BP2-p53 binding, reduced p53 stability, and inhibited apoptosis. Further, E2F1 transcriptionally upregulated p300, a lactyltransferase that directly mediates 53BP2 K476 lactylation. p300 knockdown reversed E2F1-induced 53BP2 lactylation and p53-dependent apoptosis. These findings reveal a novel E2F1-p300-53BP2 lactylation-p53 signaling axis driving cochlear hair cell apoptosis in ARHL, highlighting potential therapeutic targets for age-related hearing loss.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13148-026-02091-3.

Introduction

Age-related hearing loss (ARHL), or presbycusis, represents the most prevalent sensory deficit in the elderly population, affecting approximately 40–50% of individuals over 70 years of age worldwide [1, 2]. The condition is characterized by progressive deterioration of auditory function, primarily resulting from irreversible damage to the cochlea, including the loss of sensory hair cells, spiral ganglion neurons, and stria vascularis atrophy [3]. Among these pathological changes, cochlear hair cell apoptosis is recognized as a central event in the pathogenesis of ARHL [4]. Hair cells, responsible for converting mechanical sound vibrations into electrical signals, lack regenerative capacity in mammals, making their survival critical for maintaining auditory function [5, 6]. Existing research has identified multiple risk factors for ARHL, including oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and inflammation [7, 8]; however, the molecular mechanisms governing cochlear hair cell apoptosis, particularly the role of epigenetic regulation, remain incompletely understood. Recent advances in epigenetics have highlighted post-translational modifications (PTMs) as key regulators of cellular homeostasis [9, 10], yet their specific involvement in determining hair cell survival during aging remains unclear. Elucidating the epigenetic mechanisms underlying cochlear hair cell apoptosis could provide novel therapeutic targets for ARHL.

E2F1, a member of the E2F family of transcription factors, plays a pivotal role in coordinating cell cycle progression, differentiation, and apoptosis [4, 11]. As a downstream effector of the retinoblastoma protein (pRB) pathway, E2F1 activates the transcription of genes involved in DNA replication and cell cycle progression (e.g., cyclin E, c-Myc) under physiological conditions [12, 13]. However, under stress conditions such as DNA damage or oxidative stress, E2F1 shifts its transcriptional program toward pro-apoptotic targets [14]. Accumulating evidence links E2F1 dysregulation to age-related diseases, including neurodegenerative disorders and cancer, where its upregulation is associated with increased apoptotic cell death [15, 16]. In the context of oxidative stress, E2F1 is known to be stabilized through post-translational modifications [17, 18], leading to the activation of apoptotic signaling cascades. Notably, oxidative stress is a common feature of multiple forms of acquired hearing loss, suggesting E2F1 could have a broader role in auditory pathology. Despite these insights, the specific function of E2F1 in the auditory system, particularly its role in cochlear hair cell survival during aging, remains poorly characterized. Previous studies have reported increased E2F1 expression in the aging brain and retinal tissues [16, 19], but its involvement in ARHL and cochlear hair cell apoptosis has not been systematically investigated.

53BP2, also known as TP53BP2 or ASPP2, is a critical modulator of the tumor suppressor p53, primarily through its ability to regulate p53 transcriptional activity and protein stability [20]. Structurally, 53BP2 contains ankyrin repeats, a SH3 domain, and a proline-rich region, which mediate interactions with p53 and other signaling molecules [21]. By binding to the DNA-binding domain of p53, 53BP2 enhances p53-dependent transactivation of pro-apoptotic target genes, thereby promoting apoptotic cell death [22]. In addition to regulating transcriptional activity, 53BP2 influences p53 stability through modulation of its PTMs. For instance, 53BP2 has been shown to inhibit p53 ubiquitination by MDM2, a key negative regulator of p53, thereby prolonging p53 half-life [23]. While phosphorylation and ubiquitination of 53BP2 have been well-documented [24, 25], emerging evidence suggests that other PTMs, such as lysine lactylation (Kla), may also regulate 53BP2 function. Notably, the role of lactylation in 53BP2-mediated p53 stabilization has not been previously reported, representing a critical gap in our understanding of 53BP2-p53 signaling.

Lysine lactylation (Kla) is a recently identified PTM characterized by the covalent attachment of a lactyl group to lysine residues of proteins [26]. Discovered in 2019, lactylation is enzymatically regulated, with evidence suggesting that histone acetyltransferases such as p300 may function as lactyltransferases [27], while sirtuins (e.g., SIRT1) may act as delactylases [28]. Importantly, lactylation is metabolically linked to cellular pyruvate metabolism, as lactyl-CoA, the donor molecule for lactylation, is derived from lactate, a byproduct of glycolysis [29]. This metabolic dependency positions lactylation as a potential “metabolic sensor” that integrates cellular energy status with gene expression and protein function [29]. To date, lactylation has been implicated in diverse biological processes, including transcriptional regulation, stress responses, and cancer progression. For example, lactylation of histones promotes the transcription of genes involved in glycolysis under hypoxic conditions [30], while lactylation of non-histone proteins, such as p53 [31], has been shown to modulate their activity. Despite these advances, the role of lactylation in the auditory system, particularly in the context of age-related hair cell loss, remains entirely unexplored.

In the present study, we revealed a novel E2F1-p300-53BP2 lactylation-p53 signaling axis that drives cochlear hair cell apoptosis in ARHL. We found that E2F1 was significantly upregulated in the aging cochlea and promoted p53 protein stabilization. Mechanistically, E2F1 transcriptionally activated p300, which in turn enhanced lactylation of 53BP2 at lysine 476 (K476). This lactylation modification strengthened 53BP2 binding to p53, inhibited p53 ubiquitination, and thereby prolonged p53 half-life, ultimately leading to increased expression of pro-apoptotic genes and hair cell loss. Our findings uncovered an epigenetic-metabolic link in cochlear aging and identified potential therapeutic targets for preventing age-related hearing loss.

Materials and methods

Animal models and ARHL induction

C57BL/6J mice (male, 1–12 months old) were used in this study. Mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12-h light/dark cycle and ad libitum access to food and water. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Southwest Medical University (Approval No. swmu20250098). To confirm age-related hearing loss (ARHL), auditory brainstem response (ABR) thresholds (click and tone-burst) were measured at 8, 16, and 32 kHz using a TDT System 3 (Tucker-Davis Technologies, FL, USA) as previously described [32]. Mice were anesthetized with ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg), and subcutaneous electrodes were placed at the vertex (active), pinna (reference), and back (ground). Click and tone-burst stimuli were presented, and ABR thresholds were defined as the lowest intensity that evoked a reproducible waveform.

Cell culture and oxidative stress treatment

The HEI-OC1 cell line, an immortalized mouse cochlear hair cell-like cell line, was cultured in DMEM high-glucose medium (Gibco, CA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco) at 33 °C with 10% CO2. To induce oxidative stress, HEI-OC1 cells were treated with hydrogen peroxide (H2O2; Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA) at final concentrations of 0 (control), 1, 2, or 5 mM for 1 h, after which the medium was replaced with fresh culture medium, and cells were incubated for a total of 24 h post-treatment. For E2F1 overexpression, cells were transfected with the pcDNA3.1-E2F1 plasmid (Addgene, MA, USA) based on the pcDNA3.1 (+) vector (Addgene #12020) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For E2F1 knockdown, cells were transfected with E2F1-specific shRNA (sequences provided in Supplementary Table S1) or scrambled siRNA control. Transfected cells were incubated for 48 h before H2O2 treatment or functional assays.

Reagents, antibodies, and plasmid constructs

The following primary antibodies were used: anti-E2F1 (1:1000, Proteintech, 66515-1-Ig), anti-53BP2 (1:1000, Abcam, ab236448), anti-p53 (1:1000, Proteintech, 10442-1-AP), anti-p300 (1:1000, Abcam, ab275378), anti-lactylated-lysine (1:1000, PTM Bio, PTM-1429RM), anti-β-actin (1:5000, Sigma-Aldrich, A5441), anti-Myo7a (1:500, Abcam, ab150386), anti-cleaved caspase-3 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, #9661), anti-ubiquitin (1:5000, Proteintech, 80992-1-RR) and anti-Bax (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, #2772). Secondary antibodies included HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:5000, Cell Signaling Technology, #7074) and HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (1:5000, Cell Signaling Technology, #7076).

Plasmids used included pcDNA3.1-E2F1 (wild-type, WT), pcDNA3.1-53BP2-WT, pcDNA3.1-53BP2-K476R (mutant), and empty pcDNA3.1 vector (control), all constructed by GenScript (NJ, USA). All sequences were synthesized by Sangon Biotech Company (Sangon, Shanghai, China), and cloned into pcDNA3.1 vector (Addgene, MA, USA, #129020). The 53BP2-K476R mutant was generated by site-directed mutagenesis, with the lysine codon (AAG) at position 476 mutated to arginine (CGG). The sequences for siRNAs used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Western blot and co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Total protein was extracted from cochlear tissues or HEI-OC1 cells using RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche, Switzerland). Protein concentration was determined using the BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein (30–50 μg) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, MA, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, followed by detection using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL; Thermo Fisher Scientific). For Co-IP, cell lysates (500 μg) were incubated with anti-53BP2 antibody or IgG control overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with Protein A/G agarose beads (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) for 4 h. Beads were washed with lysis buffer, and immunoprecipitated proteins were eluted and analyzed by Western blot using anti-p53 or anti-lactylated-lysine antibodies.

Mass spectrometry analysis for lactylation site identification

HEI-OC1 cells were treated with 1 mM H2O2. Cells were lysed in 8 M urea, and proteins were digested with trypsin (Promega, WI, USA) at 33 °C overnight. Lactylated peptides were enriched using anti-lactylated lysine antibody-conjugated agarose beads (PTM Bio, #PTM-1404) for 2 h at 4 °C. Enriched peptides were desalted using C18 columns and analyzed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) on an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled with an Easy-nLC 1200 system. MS data were acquired in data-dependent acquisition mode with a full scan range of 350–1500 m/z. Raw data were processed using MaxQuant software (v1.6.17.0) with the UniProt mouse database. Lactylation sites were identified with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 1%, and the modification site was localized to 53BP2 K476 based on MS/MS spectra and database matching.

Site-directed mutagenesis and functional validation assays

Site-directed mutagenesis of 53BP2 K476 was performed using the QuikChange Lightning Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Agilent, CA, USA). The forward primer for K476R was 5′-GCTGCTGAAGCGCGCGGCTGGACGAGAAG-3′ and the reverse primer was 5′-CTTCTCGTCCAGCCGCGCGCTTCAGCAGC-3′ (mutated bases underlined). PCR amplification was performed using PfuUltra High-Fidelity DNA polymerase, followed by Dpn I digestion to remove parental DNA. Mutant plasmids were transformed into E. coli DH5α cells, and positive clones were verified by Sanger sequencing (GenScript). For functional validation, HEI-OC1 cells were transfected with 53BP2-WT or 53BP2-K476R plasmids. Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay (Dojindo, Japan) by measuring absorbance at 450 nm. Apoptosis was detected by Annexin V-APC/PI staining (BD Biosciences, NJ, USA) using flow cytometry (BD FACSCanto II). For p53 stability assays, transfected cells were treated with 100 μg/mL cycloheximide (CHX; Sigma-Aldrich), and p53 protein levels were analyzed by Western blot at 0, 30, 60, and 90 min post-treatment.

Cochlear hair cell apoptosis assessment

Cochleae were acutely isolated from euthanized animals under sterile conditions. Under a dissecting microscope, the cochlear shell was carefully opened, and the basilar membrane was microdissected to separate the organ of Corti. Isolated basilar membranes were gently transferred onto poly-L-lysine-coated glass slides and flattened to ensure complete spreading of hair cell layers (no overlapping or folding).

Basilar membranes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature, followed by three washes with PBS (5 min each). For antigen retrieval, samples were incubated in 0.01 M citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 10 min and cooled to room temperature. Nonspecific binding sites were blocked with 5% BSA in PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100 for 1 h at room temperature. The samples were then incubated with primary anti-Myo7a antibody (dilution optimized for whole-mount staining) overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber. After three washes with PBS, Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500, Thermo Fisher Scientific) were applied for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1 μg/mL) for 5 min.

After final washes, basilar membranes were mounted with antifade mounting medium. Images were captured using a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM 880) with a 40 × oil immersion objective, covering the entire length of the basilar membrane from apex to base. Apoptotic hair cells were quantified as the number of Myo7a⁺ cells per 100 μm segment of the basilar membrane using ImageJ software. At least three non-overlapping regions per basilar membrane and three basilar membranes per experimental group were analyzed for statistical significance.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR assays

Total RNA was isolated from tissues or cell lines using TRIzol reagent (Takara, Dalian, China). RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Dalian, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using TB Green Premix Ex Taq (Takara, Dalian, China). Relative gene expression levels were quantified using the 2ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Luciferase reporter assay

To investigate whether E2F1 transcriptionally regulates EP300 (encoding p300), a dual-luciferase reporter assay was performed. A fragment of the mouse p300 promoter (approximately -1500 to + 200 relative to the transcription start site) was cloned into the pGL4.10[luc2] firefly luciferase reporter vector (Promega, WI, USA). HEI-OC1 cells were seeded in 24-well plates and co-transfected at 70–80% confluence. Each well received: 400 ng of the p300 promoter-luciferase reporter plasmid (pGL4.10-p300-promoter), 100 ng of the pcDNA3.1-E2F1 overexpression plasmid or empty vector (pcDNA3.1) as control, and 20 ng of the pRL-TK Renilla luciferase plasmid (Promega) for normalization of transfection efficiency. Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, cells were lysed with Passive Lysis Buffer (Promega). Firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured sequentially using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) on a GloMax 20/20 Luminometer (Promega). Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to the corresponding Renilla luciferase activity for each well.

Lactate assay

Determine the lactate content in tissues and cells using the instructions provided with the Lactate Assay Kit (Sigma, catalog no. MAK570). Briefly, prepare a series of lactic acid standard solutions with varying concentrations in accordance with the kit’s instructions to construct a standard curve. Tissues or cells should be homogenized in 4 × the volume of Lactate Assay Buffer. Then, adjust the final volume of each sample to 50 µL per well using Lactate Assay Buffer. Prepare the reaction mixture to a final volume of 50 µL per well, and add 50 µL of the prepared reaction mixture to each well. Mix thoroughly using a horizontal oscillator or repeated pipetting up and down. Incubate the reaction mixture for 30 min at room temperature (18–35 ℃) in the dark. For colorimetric assays, measure the absorbance at 570 nm (A570). For fluorometric assays, measure the fluorescence intensity at an excitation wavelength of 535 nm and an emission wavelength of 587 nm. The assay background is determined by measuring the signal from the 0-concentration (blank) lactate standard. Subtract the blank value from all sample readings to eliminate background interference. Finally, use the signal values corresponding to different lactate concentrations to plot a calibration curve and calculate its slope.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with a minimum of three independent biological replicates (n ≥ 3), and data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 9 software. Differences between two groups were analyzed using the unpaired Student’s t-test. Multiple group comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. Correlation analysis was performed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

E2F1 upregulation correlates with cochlear hair cell apoptosis in mouse ARHL

To investigate the role of E2F1 in age-related hearing loss (ARHL), we first established a naturally aging mouse model and assessed its auditory function, as described in previous studies [32, 33]. Detailed validation of the ARHL model is provided in Supplementary Figs. Next, we sought to assess the relationship between E2F1 upregulation and hair cell apoptosis. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that cochlear hair cells had been partially lost in old mice compared to young mice (Fig. 1F and I). Furthermore, apoptosis-associated proteins (e.g., p53, Bax, Bcl2, Caspase-3) were dysregulated in mouse cochlear tissues (Fig. 1G and H), indicating increased apoptotic activity. Consistent with structural deterioration, ABR thresholds at 16 kHz were significantly elevated in 12-month-old mice, and linear regression analysis confirmed a positive correlation between E2F1 mRNA levels and hearing thresholds (Fig. 1J). Collectively, these findings suggest that E2F1 upregulation correlates with cochlear hair cell apoptosis and hearing loss in mouse ARHL.

E2F1 promotes HEI-OC1 cell apoptosis by elevating p53 protein abundance

To determine if E2F1 directly regulates hair cell apoptosis, we performed gain- and loss-of-function experiments via E2F1 overexpression and knockdown (Fig. 2C and D). Compared to empty vector controls, cells transfected with pcDNA3.1-E2F1 (E2F1-OE) exhibited a significant increase in apoptotic rate under basal conditions; conversely, E2F1 knockdown (E2F1-KD) inhibited cell apoptosis under 1 mM H2O2 treatment (Fig. 2E–H). E2F1-OE increased p53 protein levels and upregulated its target gene Bax (Fig. 2I and J), while E2F1-KD decreased p53 protein levels and downregulated Bax under H2O2 stress (Fig. 2K and L). We then investigated the impact of E2F1 on p53 stability using cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays. Interestingly, as shown in Fig. 2M and N, the half-life of p53 was approximately 85 min in E2F1-overexpressing cells, compared to approximately 65 min in control cells. These results indicate that E2F1 promotes p53 stabilization at the post-translational level in HEI-OC1 cells.

Next, we performed lactylation-targeted MS analysis in HEI-OC1 cells treated with H2O2 (1 mM), which identified lactylation modifications on multiple proteins (Supplementary Table S4). Notably, 53BP2—a p53-binding protein—was found to undergo lactylation at lysine 476 (K476), whereas p53 itself showed no lactylation (Fig. 3C). To validate this, Co-IP followed by western blotting with anti-Pan-Kla antibody confirmed that 53BP2 lactylation levels were significantly higher in cochlear tissues of old mice compared to young mice (Fig. 3D).

To determine if E2F1 regulates 53BP2 lactylation, we transfected HEI-OC1 cells with pcDNA3.1-E2F1 or empty vector. Co-IP followed by western blotting assays showed that E2F1 overexpression significantly increased lactylated 53BP2 levels compared to controls under H2O2 stress (Fig. 3E). Conversely, E2F1 knockdown reduced 53BP2 lactylation under H2O2 stress (Fig. 3F). To understand whether there is an interaction between E2F1 and 53BP2 as a potential mechanism, we performed reciprocal Co-IP, and the results revealed that immunoprecipitation of HEI-OC1 cell lysates with anti-E2F1 or anti-53BP2 antibodies followed by western blotting for the reciprocal protein revealed no co-precipitation (Supplementary Fig. 3C and D).

To confirm the specificity of E2F1-mediated lactylation at K476, we constructed a flag-tagged K476R mutant 53BP2 expression vector (lactylation-deficient at K476). E2F1 overexpression failed to enhance lactylation levels of the K476R-mutant 53BP2 compared to wild-type (Fig. 3G and H). Together, these findings demonstrate that E2F1 specifically enhances 53BP2 lactylation at the K476 residue.

To determine if 53BP2 lactylation affects p53 stability, we performed CHX chase assays. In cells transfected with 53BP2-WT, the half-life of p53 was extended to approximately 90 min, whereas it was shortened to approximately 55 min in 53BP2-K476R-transfected cells (Fig. 4B and C). We next examined the effect of 53BP2 on p53 ubiquitination: 53BP2-WT overexpression markedly reduced p53 ubiquitination levels compared to vector controls, whereas 53BP2-K476R overexpression had no such effect (Fig. 4D). These results suggest that K476 lactylation enhances 53BP2-mediated p53 stability.

Functionally, cell viability and apoptosis assays demonstrated that 53BP2-WT overexpression significantly reduced HEI-OC1 cell viability under H2O2 stress, while 53BP2-K476R overexpression had no significant impact (Fig. 4E). Consistently, 53BP2-WT significantly increased the apoptotic rate, whereas 53BP2-K476R showed no such effect (Fig. 4F and G). To validate downstream signaling, qPCR analysis revealed that mRNA levels of p53 target genes (Bax and PUMA) were significantly upregulated in 53BP2-WT cells compared to 53BP2-K476R cells (Fig. 4H and I). Collectively, these findings indicate that lactylation of 53BP2 at K476 strengthens its binding to p53, inhibits p53 ubiquitination and degradation, and stabilizes p53 protein, then promoting its downstream pro-apoptotic genes expression.

Notably, qPCR and Western blot analyses revealed that E2F1 overexpression in HEI-OC1 cells led to a significant increase in p300 mRNA and protein levels compared to vector controls (Fig. 5C and D). Conversely, E2F1 knockdown reduced p300 expression under H2O2-induced oxidative stress (Fig. 5E and F). Given E2F1’s role as a transcription factor, we hypothesized that E2F1 may regulate EP300 transcription. Luciferase reporter assays confirmed that E2F1 overexpression significantly enhanced p300 promoter activity (Fig. 5G), demonstrating transcriptional upregulation of p300 by E2F1.

Functionally, specific siRNA-mediated p300 knockdown in E2F1-overexpressing cells reduced 53BP2 lactylation, accompanied by decreased p53 protein levels and rescued apoptotic rates (Fig. 5H–K). Collectively, these findings suggest that E2F1 transcriptionally upregulates p300, which in turn acts as a lactyltransferase to enhance 53BP2 lactylation at K476.

Discussion

ARHL remains a prevalent and irreversible sensory deficit, with its pathogenesis attributed to a multifactorial interplay of genetic susceptibility, environmental exposures, and age-dependent cellular decline [1, 35]. Extensive research has established oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and chronic inflammation as central drivers of cochlear damage, ultimately leading to the apoptotic loss of non-regenerative hair cells [7, 8, 36]. Recent investigations have further illuminated the role of metabolic dysregulation. For instance, studies in primate models have identified deficiencies in nucleoside transporters like SLC35F1 as signatures of cochlear aging, while the therapeutic potential of metabolic modulators [37]. Concurrently, the tumor suppressor p53 has been recognized as a critical executioner of hair cell death in response to ototoxic insults and DNA damage [38, 39]. However, a significant gap persists in understanding how age-related metabolic changes are coupled to the epigenetic and transcriptional machinery that governs the pro-apoptotic function of p53 in the inner ear.

We demonstrate that the transcription factor E2F1, whose upregulation we correlate with cochlear aging and hearing loss, transduces oxidative stress signals into an epigenetic program. Rather than regulating cell cycle genes, E2F1 transcriptionally activates EP300, encoding the histone acetyltransferase p300, which we and others identify as a lactyltransferase [40, 41]. This leads to the specific lactylation of 53BP2 at lysine 476. While lactylation of p53 itself has been implicated in tumorigenesis [31], our work reveals a distinct mechanism whereby lactylation of its binding partner, 53BP2, enhances their interaction, shields p53 from ubiquitin-mediated degradation, and amplifies its pro-apoptotic output. Our observation of elevated lactate levels in aged cochleae and stressed hair cells supports the metabolic plausibility of this pathway, suggesting that an age- or stress-associated shift in glycolytic flux may fuel this pathogenic modification.

This work integrates several previously disparate strands of research. First, it expands the functional repertoire of E2F1 beyond cell cycle control, aligning with emerging evidence of its context-dependent roles in stress response and age-associated tissue dysfunction [16, 42]. Second, it provides a direct mechanistic link between a metabolic byproduct (lactate) and the stabilization of a key apoptotic regulator (p53) via a precise epigenetic modification, offering a concrete example of metabolic-epigenetic crosstalk in post-mitotic sensory cells. Third, it resonates with growing literature implicating protein lactylation in other neurodegenerative and age-related conditions [43, 44], suggesting possible conserved mechanisms across sensory and neural systems. The potential relevance of this axis may extend beyond ARHL to other forms of acquired hearing loss where oxidative stress is a common feature, such as noise-induced or ototoxic hearing loss.

Despite these insights, our study has limitations that highlight important future directions. The reliance on the HEI-OC1 immortalized cell line, while valuable, may not fully capture the physiology of primary, terminally differentiated hair cells. Validation of 53BP2-K476 lactylation in human ARHL cochlear specimens is crucial for translational relevance. Although we used a well-established aging mouse model, cell-type-specific genetic manipulations in vivo would provide definitive proof of the pathway’s autonomous role in hair cells. Furthermore, the precise source of lactyl-CoA in the aging cochlea and the potential interplay (cross-talk) between lactylation and other well-characterized modifications on 53BP2 or p53, such as acetylation [45], remain open and fascinating questions. Finally, while we identify E2F1, p300, and 53BP2 lactylation as potential therapeutic targets, the development and efficacy of specific inhibitors, such as p300 lactyltransferase inhibitors or molecules disrupting the 53BP2-p53 interface, require extensive future investigation in preclinical models.

Conclusion

We have identified a previously unrecognized E2F1-p300-53BP2 lactylation-p53 axis that drives cochlear hair cell apoptosis in ARHL. This pathway serves as a mechanistic nexus linking transcriptional regulation, metabolic state, and epigenetic protein modification to a decisive cell fate outcome. By providing this integrated perspective, our work not only advances the molecular understanding of auditory aging but also pinpoints novel nodes for therapeutic intervention in a condition currently lacking disease-modifying treatments.
