Association between anticipated follow‐up duration and treatment outcomes in anorexia nervosa: Insights from a Japanese medical prison
Authors: Wataru Nakagawa, Etsuko Miyamoto, Atsumi Tonai, Marika Okamura, Yusuke Okumura, Takayuki Okada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimRecovery rates in eating disorders generally improve with longer follow‐up periods. However, the effect of a longer follow‐up period itself on prognosis remains unclear due to high dropout rates. This study examined whether treatment outcomes in anorexia nervosa are influenced by the anticipated follow‐up duration, using a unique cohort from a Japanese medical prison, where follow‐up periods are predetermined by sentence length.MethodsData from 149 patients diagnosed with anorexia nervosa were analyzed. Logistic and Cox regression analyses were performed to identify predictors of favorable outcomes, defined as sufficient clinical improvement to allow transfer back to the original correctional facility.ResultsLonger remaining sentence length, representing an anticipated follow‐up duration, was associated with favorable outcomes (p < 0.001), independent of the actual hospitalization duration. Higher body mass index at admission and a lifetime diagnosis of an eating disorder also predicted favorable outcomes. Factors previously associated with treatment dropout, such as low educational attainment and adverse childhood experiences, were not predictive of outcomes.ConclusionLonger remaining sentence length was associated with favorable treatment outcomes. Although this variable was conceptualized as a proxy for anticipated follow‐up duration, physicians' expectations were not directly assessed. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as observational and hypothesis‐generating rather than causal. Future research is needed to determine whether physicians' expectations regarding anticipated follow‐up duration influence treatment processes and outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Recovery rates of eating disorders (EDs) generally improve with longer follow‐up periods. 1 , 2 Steinhausen 2 reported that more than 70% of patients achieve recovery after over 10 years of follow‐up. However, EDs are characterized by extremely high dropout rates. Although estimates vary among reports, dropout rates are high in outpatient (20%–40%) 3 and inpatient (≥20%–50%) 4 , 5 settings. These high rates indicate that maintaining long‐term follow‐up is challenging.

When evaluating the prognosis of patients who have been followed up over extended periods, the risk of dropout cannot be overlooked. Patients followed up for more than 10 years successfully avoid dropout and continue treatment for over a decade, which introduces survival bias. The issue of survival bias has been previously noted in ED prognostic studies. 6 , 7 As the follow‐up period can only be confirmed retrospectively, its effect is difficult to assess under ordinary clinical conditions. However, this effect can be objectively examined in a setting where the follow‐up period is predetermined at the start of treatment.

In a medical prison, each patient has a fixed prison term. Therefore, physicians can precisely anticipate the maximum follow‐up duration at the start of treatment, as it is automatically determined by the remaining sentence at admission (Figure 1). For example, if the remaining sentence is 3 months, discharge (upon completion of the sentence) is predetermined regardless of symptom improvement. Conversely, if the remaining sentence is 10 years, the patient cannot discontinue treatment or leave the prison at their own discretion during that period. Consequently, treatment can be planned without considering dropout risk. Using this unique setting, we hypothesized that a longer remaining sentence (i.e., a longer anticipated follow‐up period) would be associated with better treatment outcomes.

Furthermore, the correctional medical setting allows prognosis to be investigated without the confounding effect of dropouts. In conventional treatment environments, dropout is unavoidable when conducting prognostic studies, leading to statistical issues and survival bias. 6 , 7 By contrast, in medical prisons, patients cannot self‐discharge, eliminating dropouts. Accurate prognostic data in such controlled follow‐up settings are indispensable for establishing treatment plans, and evaluating outcomes under predetermined follow‐up periods may inform more effective treatment strategies.

Accordingly, this study aimed to examine whether treatment outcomes in anorexia nervosa (AN) are influenced by the anticipated follow‐up duration, using a unique cohort of patients treated in a Japanese medical prison, where follow‐up periods are predetermined by sentence length.

METHODS

Participants

Participants were individuals diagnosed with AN who were admitted to and subsequently discharged from the Medical Correction Center in East Japan (MCC East Japan) between July 14, 2020, and March 31, 2025. Recruitment and questionnaire administration were conducted upon admission. Only the most recent dataset without missing values was used for patients with multiple admissions during the study period. After removing duplicates, 150 participants were identified. One participant was excluded due to an unexpected death, leaving 149 participants for analysis. The excluded participant experienced sudden deterioration, which did not align with the study's aim of examining the effects of a fixed follow‐up period.

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of MCC East Japan (approval number 2020‐1) and was conducted in accordance with recognized ethical standards. All participants provided written informed consent prior to inclusion for the use of their questionnaire responses and medical record data for analysis.

Two self‐report questionnaires were used, as described below.

Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire

The Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire (EDE‐Q) 8 is a self‐report questionnaire that assesses ED pathology over the past 28 days. The Japanese version, translated from EDE‐Q 6.0, was validated by Mitsui et al. 9 Of the 28 items, 22 assess ED pathology severity. The original version consists of four subscales: Restriction, Eating Concern, Shape Concern, and Weight Concern. However, Mitsui et al. 9 reported that an alternative four‐factor model (Restriction, Eating Concern, Fear of Obesity, and Self‐Esteem Based on Shape and Weight) had higher internal consistency and validity for the Japanese version. Accordingly, this model was used in the present study.

Adverse Childhood Experiences score

The Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs) framework 10 assesses the effect of adverse childhood experiences on various outcomes. ACEs increase the risk of dropout, 11 although their influence on prognosis remains unclear. 12 , 13 Following the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention guidelines, 14 10 items were used, covering three categories: Abuse (emotional, physical, and sexual), Household Challenges (mother treated violently, substance abuse, mental illness, parental separation or divorce, and incarcerated household member), and Neglect (emotional, physical).

Admission criteria and inpatient treatment

Admission to MCC East Japan was indicated for patients with severely low body weight or worsening symptoms, such as vomiting or overactivity, that made management in general correctional facilities difficult. Treatment included psychotherapy, behavioral therapy, nutritional management, and cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)‐based programs. The treatment goal was to improve life‐threatening underweight and/or severe ED symptoms that led to admission. As shown in Figure 1, patients were transferred back to their original correctional facility once treatment goals were achieved. In practice, readiness for transfer was determined based on clinical stabilization, including a body mass index (BMI) of approximately 16 kg/m2 and reduction of ED‐related behaviors to a level that no longer required continuous medical monitoring or intensive management within a medical prison. Decisions regarding readiness for transfer were made by the treating physician in accordance with institutional approval procedures. If treatment goals were not achieved by the end of the prison term, patients were released upon completion of their sentence, with subsequent care transferred to another medical facility outside correctional medical settings.

Statistical analyses

Logistic regression analysis was performed, followed by post hoc Cox regression analysis. In both analyses, a favorable outcome was defined as clinical improvement leading to transfer back to the original correctional facility (“Transfer With Improvement”), whereas an unfavorable outcome was release before improvement due to sentence completion (“Sentence Completion Release”) (Figure 1).

Explanatory variables included remaining sentence length, age, AN binge‐eating/purging type (AN‐BP), illness duration, comorbidities (depression, substance use disorder [SUD]), BMI at admission, educational level (below bachelor's degree vs. bachelor's degree or higher), lifetime diagnosis of an ED, EDE‐Q subscale score, and ACE score. Explanatory variable selection was informed by Roux et al. 15 on dropout risk during inpatient and Castellini et al., 11 who reported that childhood abuse increases dropout risk. Continuous explanatory variables were z‐standardized prior to analysis, whereas binary variables were entered in their original 0/1 form to facilitate the interpretability of odds ratios.

All statistical analyses were performed using Python (v3.12.3, Python Software Foundation, Beaverton, OR, USA). Logistic regression analysis was conducted using statsmodels (v0.14.5), 16 and Cox regression using lifelines (v0.30.0). 17

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

Of the 149 participants, 11 (7.4%) were male. The BMI at admission was 13.9 ± 1.7 kg/m2 (mean ± standard deviation), and the remaining sentence length was 479.7 ± 289.3 days. Logistic regression analysis

Logistic regression analysis (Table 2) revealed that longer remaining sentences were significantly associated with “Transfer With Improvement” (p < 0.001). In this model, continuous predictors were standardized; thus, the reported odds ratio for remaining sentence length represents the effect of a one standard deviation (1 SD) increase. As the remaining sentence length was positively correlated with hospitalization duration (r = 0.416, p < 0.001), this result may reflect insufficient treatment duration for participants with shorter sentences. To address this, an additional analysis was performed using only participants with remaining sentence length above the 25th percentile (≥254 days, N = 112). In this subset, the correlation between hospitalization duration and remaining sentence length was no longer significant (p = 0.091), whereas the association between remaining sentence length and favorable outcomes remained significant (p = 0.001). In addition, variance inflation factors (VIFs) for the predictors included in the regression model were below 3.1, indicating no problematic multicollinearity. These findings suggest that the observed association between remaining sentence length and favorable outcomes was not attributable to collinearity with hospitalization duration and are consistent with our hypothesis. BMI at admission significantly predicted outcomes, with higher BMI associated with “Transfer With Improvement” (p = 0.001 in the full sample; p = 0.004 in the subset). A lifetime diagnosis of an ED was also significantly associated with favorable outcomes in both the full sample (p = 0.023) and the longer remaining sentence subset (p = 0.004).

DISCUSSION

Effect of remaining sentence length

The logistic regression analysis indicated that patients with longer remaining sentences (conceptualized as a longer duration of anticipated follow‐up) were more likely to achieve “Transfer With Improvement.” In participants with remaining sentences above the 25th percentile, the significant correlation between hospitalization duration and remaining sentence disappeared, whereas the association between remaining sentence and favorable outcomes persisted. These findings suggest that the association was not fully explained by hospitalization duration alone. Thus, treatment outcomes were not solely attributable to the actual length of hospitalization, but may be related to whether follow‐up duration was structurally guaranteed in advance. Previous studies 1 , 2 demonstrated that longer follow‐up periods are associated with better outcomes; however, because follow‐up duration is typically determined retrospectively, its independent contribution is difficult to evaluate. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to examine outcomes in a setting where follow‐up duration is predetermined at treatment initiation. Considering the high dropout rate among patients with EDs, it is possible that a longer compulsory treatment period may allow patients to engage more fully in treatment and achieve better outcomes. All patients admitted to MCC East Japan were transferred under instructions from their original correctional facilities rather than voluntarily, indicating that hospitalization is equivalent to compulsory treatment in general hospitals. In this context, a longer remaining sentence implies a longer period during which treatment cannot be avoided. However, Rienecke et al. 18 reported that compulsory treatment is effective only when patients understand the necessity of treatment and may be counterproductive otherwise. Patients admitted to MCC East Japan were typically transferred because their symptoms were incompatible with daily prison life, despite reluctance or resistance in their original facilities. Therefore, it is unlikely that they possessed sufficient treatment insights upon admission, and compulsory treatment alone would not explain the favorable outcomes. Other studies have reported that compulsory treatment does not yield better outcomes than voluntary treatment and is associated with longer hospital stays. 19

This study did not include questionnaire items assessing treatment reluctance or avoidance; therefore, the direct psychological impact of remaining sentences on patients could not be verified. Given that treatment is compulsory and patients occupy a vulnerable position, they likely experience strong psychological pressure against explicitly refusing treatment. Moreover, for security reasons, correctional officers may inspect questionnaires, introducing bias if treatment attitudes were directly probed. These items were therefore excluded. This limitation highlights the need for future studies with refined designs to examine how remaining sentence length affects patient attitudes and engagement.

Overall, the finding that longer remaining sentences predict favorable outcomes cannot be fully explained by patient‐related factors alone. Considering their potential influence on therapists, Fichter and Quadflieg 1 reported that negative prognostic expectations increase the likelihood of poor outcomes, even though psychotherapists' predictions are otherwise no more accurate than chance. These findings suggest that physician bias regarding prognosis may influence actual outcomes. The present findings indicate that assumptions about the possible follow‐up period may be one such factor.

Reported average hospitalization lengths for EDs widely vary, but only a few exceed the cutoff sentence length in this study (≥254 days): 129.76 ± 102.49 days, 15 91.6 ± 42.1 days, 20 23–254 days, 21 96.69 ± 45.96 days, 22 and 157.8 ± 97.0 days in Japan. 23 Thus, a cutoff length of 254 days represents the longer end of typical durations. When physicians are aware that a long follow‐up period is guaranteed, they may be more inclined to plan time‐intensive interventions such as CBT. In usual clinical settings, designing an 8‐month‐long treatment plan is rare; therefore, the observed effect cannot be solely explained by having more treatment options. Further studies are needed to explore how physicians conceptualize the follow‐up period and to develop strategies such as setting expected treatment timelines at the outset of care.

Predictors of treatment outcome

Cox regression analysis indicated that higher BMI at admission and a lifetime diagnosis of an ED were associated with “Transfer With Improvement.” Previous studies 20 , 22 , 24 report comparable findings for BMI. Although research on diagnostic history is limited, patients with a lifetime diagnosis of an ED may have a greater understanding of their condition and treatment processes, which could facilitate improvement.

Although many studies report that longer illness duration predicts poorer prognosis, 21 , 24 , 25 this study did not find a significant effect. A trend toward longer illness duration being associated with “Sentence Completion Release” (non‐improvement) was observed, possibly due to limited sample size or cohort heterogeneity; some participants received continuous treatment since onset, whereas others had never been treated.

SUD was not significantly associated with prognosis. Ackard et al. 26 reported that alcohol or drug misuse predicted poorer outcomes; however, their sample consisted of younger individuals (mean age = 20 years). Amianto et al. 27 suggested that although psychopathology influenced prognosis in adults, behavioral and social factors (delinquency) were more influential in children, implying that age differences may explain discrepancies with the present results. Moreover, while patients with a history of SUD remain at risk of relapse in standard treatment settings, patients in correctional settings cannot access alcohol or drugs until the end of their sentence, potentially attenuating the impact of SUD on outcomes.

Relationship with dropout risk

This study included several previously identified predictors of dropout as explanatory variables: AN‐BP subtype, 5 adverse childhood experiences, 11 lower educational attainment, 15 lower or absent depressive symptoms, 5 , 15 low EDE‐Q Fear of Obesity scores, 15 and low self‐esteem. 28 None were significant predictors of treatment outcome, suggesting that when dropout risk is controlled for, these factors may not directly influence prognosis.

Certain variables may function differently in inpatient settings. Although multiple studies report that the AN‐BP subtype is a poor prognostic factor, 2 , 24 Quadflieg et al. 22 reported that higher EDI‐2 Bulimia scores were associated with greater weight gain in inpatient settings. However, they reported that AN‐BP predicted poor long‐term outcomes, often in studies with extended follow‐up periods. This suggests that the absence of such effects may be due to the inpatient context. Similarly, although ACEs have been associated with higher dropout risk, 11 some studies report they do not influence treatment outcomes, 12 , 13 particularly in inpatient environments. 12 Long‐term post‐discharge outcomes may differ from those observed here.

Other variables showed nonsignificant but suggestive trends. First, the EDE‐Q Fear of Obesity score tended to be higher among those who achieved “Transfer With Improvement.” Roux et al. 15 reported that lower Fear of Obesity scores predicted a higher dropout risk, which they attributed to a state of denial; higher scores may therefore reflect greater awareness of one's psychopathology. This explanation may apply to the present findings. Second, higher EDE‐Q Self‐Esteem scores tended to predict “Sentence Completion Release.” Although previous studies have linked low self‐esteem to poorer outcomes, 28 this discrepancy may be explained by differences in constructs, as the EDE‐Q subscale specifically measures weight‐ and body‐related self‐esteem. The present findings suggest that self‐esteem may influence prognosis independently of dropout risk. Finally, comorbidity with depression tended to predict “Sentence Completion Release.” Although previous studies have shown that low depressive scores are associated with a higher dropout risk, 5 , 15 depression itself has been shown to predict a poorer prognosis. 29 , 30 These findings suggest that patients with comorbid depression may be easier to follow longitudinally but more difficult to treat, which is consistent with the present results.

Roux et al. 15 identified low educational attainment as a risk factor for dropout, whereas Schlegl et al. 31 reported poorer outcomes among patients with higher education, suggesting inconsistent findings. According to a systematic review by Lopez et al., 32 patients who recovered from AN tended to have a higher intelligence quotient (IQ). Educational attainment may therefore partially reflect cognitive ability, complicating the interpretation of Schlegl et al.'s findings. Kästner et al. 28 further proposed that the combination of high academic achievement and low self‐esteem may adversely affect prognosis. In addition, Dura and Bornstein 33 reported a discrepancy between school achievement and IQ in patients with AN, noting that academic performance was often markedly higher than expected. These findings highlight the importance of the relationships between academic achievement, personality traits, and prognosis. Patients in correctional settings are no exception; although this study targeted a correctional population, 42.3% of participants had completed high school or higher education, substantially exceeding the 15.4% reported among female inmates overall. 34 This observation is consistent with reports that individuals with EDs tend to have higher IQ. 32 Further studies are required to clarify the relationship between educational attainment and treatment outcomes.

Limitations

Remaining sentence length is structurally determined at the time of incarceration and is not influenced by subsequent clinical course or behavioral changes during imprisonment. Therefore, it reflects the legally determined duration of incarceration, which defines the maximum possible follow‐up period within this system. However, remaining sentence length likely reflects multiple factors beyond anticipated follow‐up alone, including institutional constraints, selection processes for transfer to a medical prison, and pre‐existing differences in clinical or background characteristics at the time of incarceration that were not directly measured in the present study. Accordingly, remaining sentence should not be interpreted as a direct proxy for anticipated follow‐up duration, but rather as a multifactorial institutional variable whose association with outcome may partly reflect unmeasured structural and case‐related influences.

The present study was conducted in a compulsory correctional medical setting, which differs structurally from standard ED treatment services in the general healthcare system. Although involuntary admission may also occur in general psychiatric care, the legal framework, institutional constraints, and correctional context surrounding incarceration represent a distinct environment that may influence treatment processes and outcomes. Therefore, the findings should not be directly extrapolated to non‐correctional ED treatment settings without caution. Further research is needed to examine whether similar associations would be observed in general psychiatric or voluntary treatment contexts.

Prior studies have identified autism spectrum disorder, 35 obsessive‐compulsive disorder, 36 , 37 and personality disorders 2 as predictors of poor outcomes; however, the small number of such diagnoses here precluded meaningful analysis. Increasing the sample size in future studies will allow more refined modeling. Additionally, although depression and low self‐esteem are negative prognostic indicators, they did not reach statistical significance, possibly due to limited power. A larger sample size may demonstrate such effects. This was a single‐center study, and multicenter research is necessary to generalize findings. Furthermore, as physicians' perspectives were not directly assessed, future studies should explicitly examine how their prognostic expectations and perceptions of follow‐up duration influence treatment outcomes.

CONCLUSION

Longer remaining sentence length was associated with better treatment outcomes. Although this variable was conceptualized as reflecting the anticipated follow‐up period, physicians' expectations were not directly assessed; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as observational and hypothesis‐generating rather than causal. Furthermore, many factors previously identified as predictors of dropout did not affect prognosis, indicating the need for future studies to distinguish between factors influencing dropout risk and those related to treatment responsiveness.

While caution is warranted in generalizing these findings beyond compulsory correctional medical settings, the present findings nonetheless suggest the potential relevance of physician‐related factors, offering a new perspective on ED prognosis. Advancing research that incorporates both patient and physician perspectives may help refine treatment strategies and improve outcomes in EDs.
