The RPD3L deacetylation complex is required for facultative heterochromatin repression in Neurospora crassa
Authors: Colleen C. Mumford, Hideki Tanizawa, Elizabeth T. Wiles, Kevin J. McNaught, Kirsty Jamieson, Kenta Tsukamoto, Eric U. Selker
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificancePolycomb group (PcG) proteins are critical for repression of silenced genes in facultative heterochromatin in many eukaryotes. The PcG repressive complex PRC2 catalyzes methylation of histone H3 lysine 27 (H3K27me), but not all genes with this repressive mark are derepressed upon loss of H3K27me, indicating that other factors are involved in their repression. Here we identify Neurospora crassa genes sds3, rlp1, and rlp2 and demonstrate they are required for silencing numerous H3K27-methylated genes. SDS3, RLP1, and RLP2 act in a histone deacetylase complex, RPD3L, and absence of any of these factors leads to higher histone acetylation at derepressed genes without significantly decreasing H3K27 methylation. This establishes histone deacetylation by RPD3L as a repressive mechanism required for silencing in facultative heterochromatin.

Plasticity of gene expression is essential for proper development of many organisms. Facultative heterochromatin, genomic regions that are subject to tight silencing but can be derepressed in response to environmental or developmental cues, is characterized by methylation of histone H3 lysine 27 (H3K27me) in the fungus Neurospora crassa and in numerous other eukaryotes (1, 2). H3K27me is deposited by the Polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2) but only about 10% of H3K27-methylated genes are up-regulated upon loss of the PRC2 methyltransferase SET-7 in Neurospora, suggesting that other factors are involved in the repression of facultative heterochromatin genes (3). Moreover, the repressive mechanism of H3K27 methylation remains incompletely understood. This prompted us to search for additional factors involved in maintaining silencing of H3K27-methylated genes using a forward genetics approach (4–6). This led us to find a previously unrecognized PRC2 subunit (PAS) involved in repression of subtelomeric H3K27-methylated regions (4); a previously uncharacterized repressive factor, EPR-1, which mediates H3K27 repression through its BAH domain (6); and the ACF chromatin remodeler complex, which we showed maintains repressive positioning of the +1 nucleosome at a sizeable subset of H3K27-methylated genes (5). The present study implicates another class of transcriptional regulators, namely histone deacetylases.

In contrast to H3K27 methylation, which is typically repressive, acetylation of lysine residues on the N-terminal tails of histones H3 and H4 is associated with transcriptional activity (7, 8). Acetylation levels are controlled through a balance of acetyl deposition by histone acetyltransferase (HAT) complexes and acetyl removal by histone deacetylation complexes (HDACs) (7, 9). N. crassa contains four known histone deacetylases (10) including HDA-3, a homolog of a highly conserved deacetylase known as HDAC3 in humans and Rpd3 in Drosophila melanogaster and Saccharomyces cerevisiae (11, 12). Rpd3, homologs of which have been found in vertebrate and invertebrate animals and plants, has been extensively studied in S. cerevisiae, where it is known to form two complexes (11). The small complex (Rpd3S) consisting of Rpd3, core subunits Sin3 and Ume1, and accessory subunits Eaf3 and Rco1, represses spurious transcription at H3K36-methylated genes by elongating RNA Polymerase II (13–15). Rpd3 also forms a large complex (Rpd3L) with Sin3, Ume1, and seven accessory subunits including Sds3, which is known to be recruited by sequence-specific factors to upstream regions of silenced genes, where it represses transcription (16–22). However, the protein interactions, function, and genomic targets of Rpd3 homologs in other model organisms such as N. crassa have not been thoroughly explored to date.

In this study, we identified three additional genes that are required for silencing of H3K27-methylated genes in Neurospora. These previously uncharacterized genes function without significantly impacting H3K27 methylation at facultative heterochromatin genes. We found that their protein products interact with HDA-3 and homologs of the S. cerevisiae Rpd3L complex in Neurospora. One of the genes codes for a protein homologous to Sds3 in the S. cerevisiae Rpd3L complex, and we therefore named it sds3. The names of the other two genes, rlp1 and rlp2, were assigned based on our finding that their products, RPD3L protein 1 (RLP1) and RPD3L protein 2 (RLP2), associate with homologs of the Rpd3L complex. The yeasts S. cerevisiae and Schizosaccharomyces pombe do not possess PcG proteins or H3K27 methylation, precluding studies of the relationship between Rpd3L and Polycomb function at repressed areas of the genomes in these fungi (23, 24). Thus our work identifies RPD3L-mediated deacetylation as an additional form of repression required for facultative heterochromatin silencing and highlights N. crassa as a simple model system to study the interplay of the histone-modifying complexes PRC2 and RPD3L.

Results

Selection for Factors Required for Facultative Heterochromatin Silencing Identifies Roles for sds3, rlp1, and rlp2.

To identify additional factors required for Polycomb silencing in N. crassa, we previously generated a strain, N6279, in which the open reading frames (ORFs) of two silenced H3K27-methylated genes, NCU05173 and NCU07152, were replaced with hygromycin B and nourseothricin antibiotic resistance genes, respectively (4–6). We UV-mutagenized N6279 and selected for colonies resistant to hygromycin B and nourseothricin (Fig. 1A). To look for causative mutations, the drug-resistant strains, which were in an Oak Ridge genetic background, were crossed to the polymorphic Mauriceville strain and genomic DNA from antibiotic-resistant progeny was pooled for whole-genome sequencing (25). Mutations were mapped by identifying regions of the genome enriched for Oak Ridge single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and then genetic variants within the regions of interest were scrutinized to identify likely causative mutations (26, 27). We had successfully used this approach previously to identify genes involved in PRC2-mediated silencing (4–6), but we had not saturated the genome for mutations that derepressed our reporter genes, prompting us to search for additional mutants. Here we report mutations that implicate three genes that had not previously been identified, which we name sds3, rlp1, and rlp2, as described below.

Four of our mutations mapped to the right arm of Linkage Group I (Fig. 1B), including three small deletions predicted to cause frameshift mutations within the ORF of NCU02898, which we named rlp2 (Fig. 1E). Additionally, one large deletion was found to span this entire gene (Fig. 1E). Another mutation resulted in a premature stop codon at position K34 of NCU01599 upstream of a predicted Sds3 domain in the expected protein product and we therefore named this gene sds3 (Fig. 1C). In the same way, we identified a single nucleotide deletion in a mutant predicted to cause an early frameshift mutation at position A217 of NCU09007, which we named rlp1 (Fig. 1D). None of these genes had previously been characterized in Neurospora. The EMBL Simple Modular Architecture Tool (SMART) predicts that both the RLP1 and RLP2 proteins contain many short disordered regions and that RLP2 has a coiled-coil region (28). BLASTP of peptide sequences for both RLP1 and RLP2 failed to identify homology to previously characterized functional domains. In contrast, as noted above, the third previously undescribed gene (sds3) showed homology with the repressive Rpd3L subunit Sds3 in S. cerevisiae, which has homologs in multiple organisms ranging from the filamentous fungus Fusarium pseudograminearum to mammals (29, 30). Analysis of strains bearing deletions of sds3, rlp1, and rlp2 showed that none of the three genes are essential for viability and no gross morphological defects were observed in deletion mutants grown on agar (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). However, we found that the Δsds3 strain grew markedly slower than wild type in linear race tube assays; the Δrlp1 and Δrlp2 strains showed only slight decreases in growth rate (Fig. 1F).

To verify that the mutations were responsible for loss of silencing at the reporter genes, we deleted the entire ORF of each of the three identified genes (sds3, rlp1, and rlp2) in a PNCU07152::nat-1 background and tested whether the deletion conferred antibiotic resistance. We found that Δsds3, Δrlp1, and Δrlp2 strains were all able to grow on medium containing nourseothricin, like the original strains with the mapped mutations and a Δset-7 strain that lacks H3K27 methylation (Fig. 1 G−I). Moreover, we tested for complementation of each of the mutant alleles with a wild-type copy of the gene and found that in each case the wild-type copy restored silencing of both antibiotic resistance genes, albeit only partially for rlp2 (Fig. 1J), confirming that sds3, rlp1, and rlp2 are all required for repression of these H3K27-methylated genes.

sds3, rlp1, and rlp2 Repress a Shared, H3K27 Methylation-Enriched, Set of Genes.

To determine the global effects on transcription, we performed mRNA-seq in Δsds3, Δrlp1, and Δrlp2 strains (31). Deletion of each gene led to substantial changes in mRNA levels of numerous genes (Fig. 2 A−C). Upregulation was much more frequent than downregulation, suggesting that all three genes primarily play repressive roles (Fig. 2 A−C). Interestingly, in all three mutants, a substantial fraction of the up-regulated genes were H3K27-methylated in wild type—24.8% (117/471) in the Δsds3 strain, 26.5% (115/408) in Δrlp1, and 34.5% (59/171) in Δrlp2 (Fig. 2 A−C), significantly more than expected by chance since roughly 8% of Neurospora genes are marked by H3K27 methylation (3). Furthermore, there was strong overlap between the sets of up-regulated genes in Δsds3, Δrlp1, and Δrlp2 (Fig. 2D). We also observed significant overlap when we narrowed our focus to only H3K27-methylated and up-regulated genes (Fig. 2E), indicating that sds3, rlp1, and rlp2 are all responsible for repressing a shared set of H3K27-methylated genes.

To gain insight into the nature of the up-regulated genes and into possible relationships of sds3, rlp1, and rlp2 with other genes implicated in repression of H3K27-methylated genes, we created a clustered heatmap of transcript changes at H3K27-methylated genes with differential expression in deletion mutants for these three genes and for null mutants of other genes known to be required for repression of other sets of H3K27 methylated genes, namely ash1, acf1, set-7, and epr-1 (Fig. 2F). We identified four clusters, the first consisting of genes at the top of the sorted heatmap that were generally up-regulated in all mutants except Δepr-1. The second cluster consists mostly of genes that are strongly up-regulated in Δsds3, Δrlp1, and Δrlp2 but not consistently up-regulated in the other mutants. A third cluster is marked by strong upregulation in ash1Y888F and Δacf1 but not in other mutants, and the fourth cluster consists of genes with no general patterns of expression changes. We performed MNase-seq (32) and found that loss of sds3, rlp1, or rlp2 does not disrupt the position of the +1 nucleosome at up-regulated genes in these mutants (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A) unlike the nucleosome shifts observed upon loss of the ACF chromatin remodeler complex (5). Although Δsds3, Δrlp1, and Δrlp2 strains up-regulated additional genes not changed in Δset-7 (Fig. 2F and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 B and C), the strong overlap of genes up-regulated in these mutants suggests that SDS3, RLP1, and RLP2 play important roles in H3K27me-mediated gene repression.

Loss of Repression in Δsds3, Δrlp1, and Δrlp2 Strains Is Not Due to Loss of H3K27 Methylation.

To test whether the gene upregulation in Δsds3, Δrlp1, and Δrlp2 was largely or entirely due to loss of H3K27 methylation, we performed H3K27me2/me3 ChIP-seq in these mutants (33). We observed no striking decrease in H3K27 methylation (SI Appendix, Fig. S3—Supplement 1) and actually noticed numerous regions marked by greater H3K27 methylation in Δsds3 and Δrlp1 strains compared to wild type (Fig. 3 A and B). Indeed, when we plotted the H3K27 methylation level at each gene in Δsds3 against the wild-type level we found only seven genes with a twofold decrease in H3K27 methylation (Fig. 3C) and Δrlp1 and Δrlp2 showed similar decreases at only 16 and 15 genes, respectively (Fig. 3 D and E). Twenty-five genes gained H3K27 methylation in Δrlp2 but strikingly, 767 genes had significantly more H3K27 methylation in Δsds3 and 929 genes had more H3K27me2/3 in Δrlp1 (Fig. 3 C–E). Most of these gains were within 1 Mb of the end of chromosomes (SI Appendix, Fig. S3—Supplement 2A) where most genes marked by H3K27 methylation are located in wild type (3). Interestingly, 30.8% (236/767) of the genes in Δsds3 with significant increases in H3K27me2/3 were not methylated in wild type and for Δrlp1 72.8% (676/929) of such genes were unmethylated in wild type (SI Appendix, Fig. S3—Supplement 2B).

Notably, the vast majority of up-regulated H3K27-methylated genes gained or had no change of H3K27 methylation compared to wild type in the three mutants: 99% (116/117) in Δsds3, 91% (105/115) in Δrlp1, and 88% (52/59) in Δrlp2 (Fig. 3F). Moreover, we found that both of the up-regulated antibiotic resistance genes (pNCU05173::hph and pNCU07152::nat-1) retained H3K27 methylation in Δsds3, Δrlp1, and Δrlp2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S3—Supplement 1), indicating that changes in H3K27 methylation were not responsible for the observed transcriptional upregulation in these mutants. This suggests that sds3, rlp1, and rlp2 are responsible for repressing a subset of H3K27-methylated genes via an additional pathway that is downstream or parallel to the deposition of H3K27 methylation by PRC2. To explore this possibility further, we performed mRNA-seq in a Δrlp1 Δset-7 strain (31) and found the up-regulated genes (n = 356) mostly overlapped with genes up-regulated in a Δrlp1 strain (SI Appendix, Fig. S3—Supplement 2C). We compared the average transcript level at genes up-regulated in both strains and found they had a slightly higher transcript level in the absence of SET-7 (Fig. 3G) but the difference was not statistically significant, indicating that RPD3L-mediated deacetylation does not appreciably depend on SET-7-mediated H3K27 methylation for repression of its target genes.

SDS3, RLP1, and RLP2 Interact with Members of the RPD3L Complex.

Since NCU01599 (SDS3) is homologous to a subunit of the Rpd3L complex in S. cerevisiae, we investigated whether it might be part of a similar complex in N. crassa with HDA-3, the homolog of the yeast Rpd3 deacetylase. We immunopurified HDA-3-3xFLAG and NPF-3xFLAG (a homolog of S. cerevisiae Ume1) from N. crassa cellular lysates and performed mass spectrometry on digested peptides to identify copurifying proteins. We found that HDA-3 and NPF strongly copurified with each other and with SIN3 (SI Appendix, Fig. S4—Supplement 1). These three proteins represent the core subunits of both the Rpd3L and Rpd3S complexes of S. cerevisiae (SI Appendix, Fig. S4—Supplement 1). HDA-3 and NPF interacted with NCU06788 and NCU00423, which we named EAF-3 and RCO1 because they are homologous to the two additional yeast Rpd3S subunits Eaf3 and Rco1, respectively (Fig. 4A and SI Appendix, Fig. S4—Supplement 1). HDA-3 and NPF also copurified with homologs of seven Rpd3L subunits, which we named after their S. cerevisiae counterparts: SAP30 (NCU05240), PHO23 (NC03461), RXT2 (NCU05979), RXT3 (NCU08417), CTI6 (NCU02121), DEP1 (NCU09130) and SDS3 (Fig. 4A and SI Appendix, Fig. S4—Supplement 1). Interestingly, HDA-3 and NPF also interacted with RLP1 (NCU09007) and RLP2 (NCU02898), neither of which has a homolog in S. cerevisiae (Fig. 4A and SI Appendix, Fig. S4—Supplement 1). We confirmed that HDA-3 interacts with NPF, SIN3, SDS3, and EAF-3 by coimmunoprecipitation experiments (Fig. 4B); in contrast, EAF-3 and SDS3 do not appear to interact with each other (Fig. 4B), suggesting that N. crassa forms two distinct HDA-3-containing complexes as in yeast.

To explore this indication further, we immunoprecipitated FLAG-tagged EAF-3 and FLAG-tagged SDS3 and performed mass spectrometry. We found that EAF-3 showed strong interactions with the three core subunits and with RCO1, but not with the seven RPD3L-specific subunits or with either RLP1 (NCU09007), or RLP2 (NCU02898) (Fig. 4A and SI Appendix, Fig. S4—Supplement 1). Conversely, when SDS3 was used as bait, it strongly copurified with the core subunits, RPD3L-specific subunits, and RLP1 and RLP2, but only weakly interacted with the two Rpd3S subunits (Fig. 4A and SI Appendix, Fig. S4—Supplement 1). We did not detect homologs of Ash1 or Ume6, two gene-specific repressive DNA-binding factors incorporated in yeast Rpd3L (22). As noted above, since NCU01599 interacts with two core RPD3 homologs and contains an Sds3 domain found in Sds3 homologs in other species, we designated it SDS3. Similarly, we chose the names RPD3L protein 1 (RLP1) for NCU09007 and RPD3L protein 2 (RLP2) for NCU02898 because both proteins interact with the RPD3L subunit SDS3 but not the RPD3S subunit EAF-3, and both regulate a shared gene set with SDS3 (Fig. 2D and SI Appendix, Fig. S4—Supplement 1).

To test whether the two HDA-3-containing complexes regulate distinct gene sets, we also performed mRNA-seq in a Δeaf-3 strain. Loss of eaf-3 up-regulated 988 genes, only 214 of which were also up-regulated in Δsds3, Δrlp1, or both (Fig. 4D). Although Δeaf-3 up-regulated some of the same genes as Δsds3, Δrlp1, and Δrlp2, it was not limited to this subset of genes, as illustrated by a heatmap of transcript abundance for all genes, sorted by most to least increased in Δsds3 (Fig. 4E). Taken together, these mass spectrometry and mRNA-seq results indicate that N. crassa forms two distinct HDA-3-containing complexes homologous to Rpd3S and Rpd3L in S. cerevisiae (Fig. 4A and SI Appendix, Fig. S4), and that RLP1 and RLP2 interact with RPD3L but not RPD3S (Fig. 4C). We measured the linear growth rate of strains bearing deletions of all RPD3L-specific subunits, and found that all showed minor to moderate growth defects compared to WT, with Δsds3 displaying the greatest decrease (SI Appendix, Fig. S4—Supplement 2). Attempts to generate a homokaryotic Δsin3 strain have failed, suggesting SIN3 may be essential for viability, but we found that Δnpf and a null mutant of HDA-3 (HDA3H157AH158A) had a dramatically decreased growth rate compared to WT (SI Appendix, Fig. S4—Supplement 2). These findings underline the importance of the RPD3L complex for normal development of Neurospora.

Loss of sds3, rlp1, and rlp2 Leads to Higher H3K14 Acetylation at Up-Regulated Genes.

To investigate whether a defect in deacetylation could explain the derepression of facultative heterochromatin genes in Δsds3, Δrlp1, and Δrlp2 strains, we performed H3K14ac and H3K9ac ChIP-seq in these mutants and in a wild-type control (33). We did not see gross hyperacetylation, probably because many genes are marked with both acetyl marks even in wild type. However, we found that up-regulated genes in Δsds3, Δrlp1, and Δrlp2 strains showed significantly higher H3K14ac levels than genes that were down-regulated or that did not change their expression (Fig. 5A). Genes up-regulated in Δrlp1 and Δrlp2 but not Δsds3 also showed significantly more H3K9ac than genes with no change or a decrease in expression (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A–C). These results suggest that SDS3, RLP1, and RLP2 play a role in repressing certain genes by decreasing their levels of acetylation at specific residues including H3K14. However, up-regulated H3K27-methylated genes did not consistently have higher H3K14ac and H3K9ac levels than up-regulated genes not marked by H3K27 methylation (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 E and F). This indicates the increased acetylation observed at up-regulated H3K27-methylated genes in RPD3L mutants is not directly due to the H3K27 methyl mark at these genes but due to defective deacetylation at RPD3L target genes which include, but are not limited to, H3K27-methylated genes.

HDAC Inhibition Disrupts Silencing of H3K27-Methylated Genes and Increases H3K14ac at Up-Regulated Genes Similarly to RPD3L Mutants.

Since the deacetylation activity of HDAC complexes like RPD3L is known to repress genes in yeast (19, 22, 34), we wondered whether the gene upregulation observed in Δsds3, Δrlp1, and Δrlp2 was due to defects in deacetylation. To explore this possibility, we tested whether the HDAC inhibitor Trichostatin A (TSA) can derepress the hygromycin and nourseothricin resistance cassettes inserted at H3K27-methylated genes in strain N6279, in which these genes are normally silenced and thus not permissive of growth in the presence of these antibiotics. We plated approximately 1,000 conidia on solidified medium containing hygromycin and nourseothricin and applied TSA (or water) to the center of the plates. While no colonies were found on the plate containing hygromycin and nourseothricin treated without TSA, indicating that the antibiotic resistance genes remained silent, we observed a ring of colonies growing around the site of TSA application (Fig. 5B). We conclude that this drug can derepress both genes similarly to mutations in sds3, rlp1, and rlp2.

To test the broader effects of TSA on gene expression, we performed mRNA-seq after TSA treatment (31) and found that exposure to TSA up-regulated 185 genes, and nearly half of these (91/185) were up-regulated in Δsds3, Δrlp1, or both (SI Appendix, Fig. S5G). The same held true when we narrowed our focus to only up-regulated H3K27-methylated genes, with 54% (27/50) also up-regulated in Δsds3, Δrlp1, or both (Fig. 5C), demonstrating that these mutants have defects similar to those observed when the deacetylation machinery is inhibited chemically. To explore whether this could be due to impaired deacetylation, we performed H3K14ac and H3K9ac ChIP-seq in samples treated with TSA (33). We found that following HDAC inhibition, up-regulated genes had significantly more H3K14ac, although not H3K9ac, than genes with no change or a decrease in expression, similar to the pattern observed in Δsds3 (Fig. 5A and SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A–D). As in Δsds3, Δrlp1, and Δrlp2 strains, up-regulated H3K27-methylated genes did not consistently have greater increases in acetylation than up-regulated unmethylated genes in samples treated with TSA (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 E and F), indicating that the increased acetylation is not specific to H3K27-methylated genes. Together, these results imply that impeding RPD3L function, either by deletion of key subunits or by chemical inhibition, leads to defective deacetylation which increases histone acetylation at up-regulated genes and derepresses facultative heterochromatin.

UMAP Analysis Puts RPD3L-Mediated Repression in Context with Other Repressive Mechanisms Acting on Facultative Heterochromatin.

We wanted to gain insight into how SDS3, RLP1, and RLP2 contribute to repression genome-wide when compared to other mechanisms that are involved in repression of facultative heterochromatin. We therefore performed UMAP (uniform manifold approximation and projection) dimension reduction to generate a model of expression change patterns of DE genes in relevant mutant backgrounds (35). We plotted DE genes in Δsds3, Δrlp1, and Δrlp2 strains and other mutants previously demonstrated to affect H3K27-methylated genes in N. crassa (5, 6, 36) according to their similarity based on H3K27 methylation levels in wild type (Fig. 6A) and their expression profiles (Fig. 6 B–I).

RPD3L-mediated deacetylation is a previously unrecognized repressive mechanism acting at facultative heterochromatin. (A) UMAP modeling depicting H3K27me2/3 score at all genes up-regulated in at least two conditions used for modeling (n = 4,317). Genes are colored by H3K27me2/3 levels in wild type. Merged ChIP-seq biological replicates were used for analysis. (B–I) UMAP modeling depicting mRNA-seq score [log2 (mutant/WT)] at all genes up-regulated in at least two conditions used for modeling for each indicated mutant genotype (n = 4,317). Genes are colored by expression change from wild type with dark red dots marking genes that increased expression in mutant and dark blue dots marking genes that decreased expression in mutant relative to wild type. Merged mRNA-seq biological replicates were used for analysis. (J) Model of mechanisms currently known to be involved in repression of facultative heterochromatin in Neurospora: H3K27 methylation deposited by the PRC2 complex, silencing mediated by EPR-1, H3K36 methylation deposited by ASH1, a 50 bp upstream shift of the +1 nucleosome by the ACF complex, or H3K14 deacetylation by the RPD3L complex. Although each mechanism is depicted acting on a different genomic region, they often overlap in the genes regulated. Loss of any one mechanism is sufficient to derepress a subset of, but not all, H3K27-methylated genes, implying that facultative heterochromatin silencing is mediated by this network of overlapping factors rather than a linear pathway.

As expected, we found that the Δset-7 mutant mostly up-regulates the group of H3K27-methylated genes, shown in the bottom left corner of the model plots; the Δset-7 mutant also down-regulates a group shown at the top (Fig. 6 A and B). Loss of epr-1 modestly up-regulates some of the same genes as the Δset-7 mutant but also up-regulates a unique group of genes shown at the center top and down-regulates a smaller set of genes represented in the bottom right corner (Fig. 6 B and C). Consistent with previous data, we found that ash1Y888F up-regulates many of same genes as Δiswi and Δacf1 including those regulated by SET-7 as well as others depicted at the bottom of the plot (Figs. 2F and 6 D–F). The shared set of genes up-regulated by Δsds3, Δrlp1, and Δrlp2 mutations also encompasses the SET-7-regulated group (Fig. 6 B and G–I) as well as a group seen on the left side of the graph, while loss of each of these genes down-regulates a group of genes up-regulated in ACF complex mutants and ash1Y888F (Fig. 6 D–I).

This clustering analysis extends our model (Fig. 6J), adding RPD3L-mediated deacetylation to the nonlinear constellation of repressive mechanisms that control facultative heterochromatin genes in Neurospora. Loss of any one of the mechanisms—including H3K27 methylation deposited by SET-7, EPR-1-mediated silencing, H3K36 methylation deposited by ASH1, proper nucleosome positioning by ACF, or deacetylation by RPD3L—is sufficient to disrupt silencing at numerous H3K27-methylated genes. This indicates that silencing of H3K27-methylated genes is not enforced by a singular linear pathway. Rather, an array of repressive mechanisms maintain silencing of different subsets of H3K27-methylated genes, together encompassing the entire set of genes in facultative heterochromatin (Fig. 6J).

Discussion

Carefully controlled gene silencing is essential for cell fate specification and proper early development in many organisms. Repression by Polycomb group (PcG) proteins is one common silencing mechanism (1, 37). Although PcG proteins are highly conserved, how PcG complexes establish and maintain silencing of their target genomic regions is not fully understood (2, 38). We previously demonstrated in Neurospora that PRC2 deposits both position-dependent H3K27 methylation (at subtelomeric regions) and position-independent H3K27 methylation (at interstitial sites), and that an accessory PRC2 subunit, PAS, may recruit PRC2 to subtelomeric regions (4, 39). Loss of the H3K27 methyltransferase SET-7 only releases repression of approximately 100 of the roughly 800 H3K27-methylated genes, indicating that this methyl mark is not the only factor responsible for silencing facultative heterochromatin (3). In the study reported here, we identified three additional factors, encoded by the genes sds3, rlp1, and rlp2, that are required to maintain silencing at a subset of PRC2 target genes in N. crassa.

We found that loss of SDS3, RLP1, and RLP2 did not lower H3K27 methylation at up-regulated genes, indicating that their repressive function stems from an additional mechanism controlling expression of H3K27-methylated genes. SDS3, RLP1, and RLP2 interact with the PRC2 subunit NPF as well as histone deacetylase HDA-3 (Rpd3 homolog) and all members of the RPD3L complex. Genes up-regulated upon loss of the RPD3L subunits sds3, rlp1, or rlp2 are rich in H3K27-methylated genes and have higher levels of H3K14 acetylation in Δsds3, Δrlp1, and Δrlp2 strains, suggesting deacetylation mediated by the RPD3L complex is required for repression of these H3K27-methylated genes. Consistent with this, we also found that chemical inhibition of HDAC function with the histone deacetylase inhibitor Trichostatin A (TSA) disrupts silencing at H3K27-methylated regions and increases H3K14 acetylation at up-regulated genes, reinforcing the notion that histone deacetylation is important for repression of facultative heterochromatin in N. crassa. Furthermore, we found that derepression in an RPD3L mutant was not appreciably greater in the absence of SET-7, indicating that RPD3L can repress facultative heterochromatin independently of H3K27 methylation.

Our forward genetics search did not identify mutations in the six other RPD3L subunits even though strains bearing deletions of these genes are viable. This is consistent with the possibility that SDS3, RLP1, and RLP2 play more important roles in RPD3L deacetylation silencing at facultative heterochromatin than do other members of the RPD3L complex. Sds3 is known to be required for structural integrity and deacetylase activity of the RPD3L complex in yeast (40). Based on the reduced growth rate and large number of genes with differential expression upon deletion of sds3, it seems likely that SDS3 is also important for RPD3L complex integrity and deacetylation activity in N. crassa. Loss of rlp1 causes greater changes in H3K27 methylation and gene derepression than loss of rlp2; however, deletion of either gene alone is sufficient to derepress H3K27-methylated genes and increase histone acetylation, indicating that each plays a necessary role in gene silencing. Further study of the unique roles of each of the three subunits identified here is warranted.

Histone acetylation antagonizes Polycomb silencing in mammals and Drosophila (41, 42), and previous studies indicated that Rpd3 is involved in deacetylation to support repression of PRC2 target genes (43, 44). The human Rpd3 homologs HDAC1 and HDAC2 interact with the PRC2 subunit EED and the deacetylase inhibitor TSA interferes with EED-mediated silencing (45, 46); Rpd3 also forms a complex with the SET-7 homolog E(z) and NPF homolog p55, and is required for Polycomb response element (PRE)-mediated silencing in Drosophila (47). A recent paper reported that the core Rpd3 member Sin3 is required for maintenance of H3K27 methylation and loss of Sin3 or PRC2 subunits increased global histone acetylation in the filamentous fungus Magnaporthe oryzae (48). However, these studies did not examine acetylation at native Polycomb target genes or the effects of loss of additional Rpd3 subunits. Here we show that the RPD3L subunits SDS3, RLP1, and RLP2 are required for repression of many H3K27-methylated genes and that loss of these RPD3L subunits increases histone acetylation specifically at up-regulated genes, further underlining the role of deacetylation in repression of Polycomb target genes.

Based on this work and other recent studies, it is clear that Polycomb repression does not simply involve a linear pathway (49). Instead, at least in Neurospora, multiple mechanisms silence genes marked by H3K27 methylation. PRC1 proteins, which act together with PRC2 complexes to silence Polycomb target genes in some organisms (1, 50), have not been detected in fungi (51, 52), raising the possibility that simple eukaryotes utilize other repressive mechanisms such as deacetylation and nucleosome remodeling to reinforce silencing at facultative heterochromatin. The diverse mechanisms that we have found to operate on facultative heterochromatin in Neurospora may have remained undetected in metazoans because of redundancy or because of their necessity for viability. It will be valuable to identify factors necessary for repression of H3K27 methylated regions in other systems to establish whether the overlapping mechanisms that we have found in Neurospora are conserved and to better understand the evolution of Polycomb repression.

Materials and Methods

All N. crassa strains were grown as previously described (5) and are listed in SI Appendix, Table S1. Technical replicates are experimental repeats using the same strain; biological replicates are experiments performed using different strains of the same genotype. All Illumina sequencing was performed by the University of Oregon Genomics and Cell Characterization Core Facility on NextSeq500 and NovaSeq 6000 sequencers.

Selection and Identification of Mutants Defective in Polycomb Silencing.

The selection was carried out as previously described (5). Whole-genome sequencing, SNP mapping, and identification of mutants were performed as previously described (5). Mapping of the critical mutations was performed as previously described (53, 54) using FreeBayes and VCFtools (26, 27).

ChIP and ChIP-seq Data Analysis.

H3K27me2/3 ChIP using α-H3K27me2/3 antibody (Active Motif, 39536), which recognizes di- or trimethylated H3K27, was performed as previously described (5) except samples were lysed for 2 × 90 s on a Mini-beadbeater-16 (Biospec Products) before Bioruptor (Diagenode UCD-200) treatment (2 × 5 min, 30 s on/30 s off). H3K9ac ChIP and H3K14ac ChIP were performed similarly but with α-H3K9ac antibody (Diagenode, C15410004) and α-H314ac antibody (Millipore, 07-353), respectively. The isolated DNA was prepared for sequencing as previously described (5). Mapping, visualization, and analysis of ChIP-sequencing reads were performed as previously described (5). ChIP-seq tracks were normalized using RPKM. For details on how ChIP-seq scatter plots and box plots were constructed, see SI Appendix, Extended Methods.

RNA Isolation, mRNA-seq, and mRNA-seq Data Analysis.

Total RNA was extracted from germinated conidia and used for mRNA-seq library preparation as previously described (5, 55). Sequence reads were aligned to the N. crassa genome (OR74A), quantified and normalized as previously described (5), and are available in Dataset S2. Genes with an FDR < 0.05 and an absolute log2 FC greater than 2 were identified as significantly up- or down-regulated. The RNA-seq analysis for comparison of Δrlp1 and Δrlp1 Δset-7 data followed the standard RNA-seq analysis protocol with the following modifications. Instead of using all sequence reads, 10 million reads were randomly sampled using seqkit (version 2.4.0). To mitigate batch effects between the two experiments, the “removebatcheffect” function from the R package Limma was used. For details on how mRNA-seq heatmaps and box plots were constructed, see SI Appendix, Extended Methods.

Immunoprecipitation Followed by Mass Spectrometry.

Proteins were extracted from strains N5263 (HDA-3-3xFLAG), N5401 (pQA2-3xFLAG-NPF), N5456 (pQA2-3xFLAG-SDS3), and NCU07510 (EAF-3-3xFLAG), and purification of 3xFLAG-tagged protein was performed as previously described (5). Immuno-purified samples were analyzed by mass spectrometry by the UC Davis Proteomics Core. All raw spectrum counts are listed in Dataset S1.

Coimmunoprecipitation.

HA- and FLAG-tagged N. crassa strains supplemented with 0.4% quinic acid were grown for 2 d. Co-IPs were performed as previously described (56) except that mycelia were lysed by sonication on a Branson 450 digital sonifier for 40 pulses (output 2, duty cycle 80) and centrifuged for 5 min at 14,000 RPM at 4 °C to separate the soluble fraction. Blots were probed with mouse anti-FLAG antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, F3165) and rat anti-HA antibody (Roche, 11 867 423 001) or mouse anti-HA antibody (MBL 180-3).

MNase Digestion and Sequencing.

N. crassa cells were grown and digested with micrococcal nuclease as previously described (5, 57) and gel-purified mononucleosome DNA was prepared for sequencing as previously described (5). MNase (Takara) concentration was optimized for each strain to yield ~80 to 90% mononucleosomes (20 units for N8185, N8186, N8193; 40 units for N3752, N6706, N8194; 60 units for 3753; 80 units for N6708). For details on MNase-seq data analysis, see SI Appendix, Extended Methods.

UMAP Modeling.

FC values from the union of significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in Δacf1, Δepr-1, Δiswi, Δrlp1, Δrlp2, Δsds3, Δset-7, ash1Y888F and normalized H3K27me enrichment score in wild type (average = 0, SD = 1) were used, totaling 4,317 DEGs. These values were subjected to the UMAP algorithm to map the DEGs and H3K27me scores onto 2D coordinates, allowing DEGs with similar expression changes to be positioned closely (35).
