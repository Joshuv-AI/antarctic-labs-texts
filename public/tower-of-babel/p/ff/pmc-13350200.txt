Arsenic (As) and Lead (Pb) in soils of Shirak region (Armenia): distribution patterns, soil-to-potato transfer and dietary risk assessment
Authors: G. Tepanosyan, M. Beglaryan, D. Pipoyan, Zh. Poghosyan, A. Gevorgyan, N. Ricca, I. Guagliardi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recent findings on the toxicity of arsenic (As) and lead (Pb) have led to lowered reference values for these elements. This study aims to investigate As and Pb contents and spatial distribution patterns in soils from the Shirak region (Armenia), their contents in potato skin and flesh, the soil-to-potato transfer, and assess the dietary health risks. Arsenic and Pb contents in soils were determined using XRF, while potato samples by ICP-MS. The spatial distribution patterns of soil As (mean-15.1 mg/kg) and Pb (mean-12.5 mg/kg) contents varied widely at the regional level, influenced by both geology and anthropogenic factors. Three distinct subsamples distributed across the region were identified and a significant positive correlation between As and Pb was observed in each subsample. As and Pb contents in the potato tuber skin (mean: 0.226 and 0.093 μg/kg) were greater than in the tuber flesh (mean: 0.098 and 0.036 μg/kg) respectively. The transfer factors indicated low As and Pb bioaccumulation in potato. The estimated daily intake (EDI) values for iAs and Pb (1.24 × 10−4 μg/kg bw/day and 6.5 × 10−5 μg/kg bw/day, respectively) based on occurrence levels and potato consumption rates were low for both iAs and Pb, therefore indicating no public health concern. The substantially higher margin of exposure (MOE) values (Pb: 2.23 × 104 to 9.69 × 103; iAs: of 4.69 × 102 to 5.23 × 102) observed in this study suggest that the studied toxic elements exposure through potato consumption alone do not pose a health risk to the adult population of the Shirak region. Nevertheless, given the cumulative nature of the elements’ toxicity, their contribution to the total dietary exposure remains relevant, particularly for populations with high reliance on locally produced staple foods. This work emphasizes the necessity of regular monitoring of As and Pb contents in potatoes.

Introduction

Soil health is a prerequisite for food quality and safety (Cicchella et al., 2023). As the primary medium for crop growth, soil not only supplies essential macro- and micronutrients but also accumulates and transfers potentially toxic elements (PTEs) (Celis et al., 2024; Evans et al., 2022; Guagliardi et al., 2022; Ohno & Hettiarachchi, 2018). Among these elements, arsenic (As) and lead (Pb) are of particular concern because they are non-essential elements that can negatively affect plant growth (Collin et al., 2022; Guagliardi et al., 2015; Su et al., 2025) and, more critically, pose risks to human health through dietary exposure (Commission of the European Communities, 2006; Council of the European Union 2023).

Potato (Solanum tuberosum, L.) ranks as the fourth most important staple crop worldwide, providing carbohydrates, vitamins, and minerals for billions of people while sustaining livelihoods across diverse agroecosystems in over 150 countries (FAO, 2025). Its adaptability to different climatic conditions and tolerance of different altitudes (from sea-level conditions to above 4000 m above sea level), combined with its high productivity, make it a strategic crop for global food security and biodiversity conservation (CIP, 2024; FAO, 2025). Despite these benefits, potato safety can be affected by PTEs (Pb, As, Cd, Cr, Ni, Cu, Zn, etc.), which are common contaminants of agricultural soils originating from both anthropogenic activities and natural sources, including mineralized zones (Galstyan et al., 2023; Kundu et al., 2012; Norton et al., 2013; Sixto et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024). The transfer of PTEs from the soil environment to potatoes occurs through root uptake, followed by translocation to tuber tissues. This process is influenced by soil pH, organic matter, clay content, cultivar specificity, and rhizosphere interactions. Some studies have reported that potato skins may contain 2–10 times higher concentrations of PTEs than the flesh, partly due to direct soil contact and the lignified structure of peel tissues which facilitates surface adsorption and element binding (Aksouh et al., 2024). Importantly, due to this transfer and subsequent consumption of potato, risk assessment studies have identified potential adverse health effects (Resz-Hoaghia et al., 2022; Selahvarzi & Sobhan Ardakani, 2022; Voica et al., 2025). In some cases, risk is identified when combined exposure to several metals is considered (Aboubakar et al., 2023).

Potatoes are also an essential crop for Armenia’s food security and economic sustainability (Caucasus Watch, 2024). The annual gross production reached 337.7 thousand tons in 2024, enabling the country to transition from a net importer to an exporter of this staple crop (British Potato, 2025; PotatoPro, 2024). According to the latest data from the Statistical Committee of Armenia, potato self-sufficiency reached 106.2% in 2024, indicating that domestic production exceeds internal demand. Per capita annual consumption surpasses 63.4 kg, confirming the potato’s important dietary role (ARMSTAT, 2026).

Despite the importance of potato in the Armenian diet and agriculture, quantitative data on the transfer of PTEs from soil to potato remain fragmented, and no prior study in Armenia has considered peel to flesh partitioning. While earlier investigations addressed soil-to-potato transfer in relatively large agricultural lands, they did not account for local geochemical specificities. This gap is particularly relevant for the Shirak region, which is the second largest potato-producing region in Armenia (accounting for 72.4% of gross production), where 14.5% of the population consumes highly monotonous diets in which bread and potato together account for 70% of the total daily intake (ARMSTAT, 2026). Such dietary reliance, combined with the region’s specific soil geochemical characteristics, may increase the population’s vulnerability to PTEs exposure.

To address these knowledge gaps, this study aimed to investigate the As and Pb contents and spatial distribution patterns in soils from the Shirak region, their contents in potato skin and flesh, the soil-to-potato transfer, and assess the dietary health risks. By integrating geochemical modelling with toxicological evaluation, this study identified potential contamination hotspots and provided insights for enhancing sustainable potato production and public health resilience. Adopting a “One Health” approach (Resz-Hoaghia et al., 2022), chemical risk assessment integrates data on soil contamination, crop uptake, human dietary exposure, and associated health impacts.

Materials and methods

Study sites

The Shirak region lies in the northwestern part of Armenia, bordering Turkey and Georgia. It covers an area of 2680 km2, accounting for 9% of the country’s territory, of which 78.7% is agricultural land. In 2024, Shirak’s share in the gross output of Armenia’s main industrial branches was as follows: 9.3% in agriculture, 4.1% in industry, 4.5% in construction, 4.0% in retail trade, and 1.7% in services (Armstat, 2025). The region’s geological base consists of various volcanogenic rocks, including basalt, tuff, dacite, and basanite, distributed throughout the area. Dolerite basalts are found in the northern part of the region, whereas alluvial, deluvial, and proluvial sediments dominate the central and northern areas. Chernozems are the predominant soil type, with Phaeozems also present in some northern zones (Vardanyan & Valesyan, 2007). The region hosts numerous quarries of construction materials, including tuff, basalt, and gravel, as well as three sites of mineral resources containing tin (Sn), antimony (Sb), nickel (Ni), and arsenic (As) (Republican geological fund, 2025).

Soil and potato sampling

This study was conducted in two stages of sampling activities.

First stage: A regional-scale soil geochemical survey of the Shirak region was conducted in 2021 as part of Armenia’s national soil survey. A uniform sampling grid of 5 × 5 km was applied (one sample per 25 km2), corresponding to the regional geochemical scale of sampling (De Vivo et al., 2018). Soil samples from the upper 20 cm of the soil layer were collected using a stainless-steel shovel. In total, 120 samples (Fig. 1) were collected from the central point of each grid cell. Each sample consisted of five subsamples collected from the corners and center of a 10 × 10 m square and stored in polyethylene bags for transportation and storage.Fig. 1Spatial distribution of soils and soil-potato sampling sites in the Shirak region of Armenia

Spatial distribution of soils and soil-potato sampling sites in the Shirak region of Armenia

Second stage: A targeted soil and potato sampling plan was designed using the data obtained from the first stage. Specifically, 10 sampling sites were selected to investigate the soil-to-potato transfer of selected chemical elements. Soil and potato samples (10 samples for each category) were collected from these sites in September 2025. Depending on the site and the number of potatoes per plant, both soil and potato samples were composed of 5–7 subsamples.

In both stages, the soil samples were oven-dried at 40 °C, homogenized, and sieved (< 2 mm). Subsequently, they were pulverized using a VG 6 vibratory grinder (Vibrotechnik, St. Petersburg, Russian Federation) and stored for chemical analysis. In the case of potato samples, the samples were washed with deionized water to remove soil particles. Afterwards, the potato samples were peeled using a ceramic knife to separate the skin from the flesh for the following analysis.

Analysis methods and QA/QC

The contents of arsenic (As) and lead (Pb) in soils of the Shirak region, as well as in 10 selected soil and potato sampling sites, were determined using X-ray fluorescence spectroscopy (Innov X-5000, USA). Each sample was analyzed in the 3-beam soil mode with a total beam counting time of 360 s (three repeats of 120 s each), resulting in an overall analysis time of 18 min (1080 s). Certified reference materials NIST 2710a and NIST 2711a were analyzed at a ratio of one per 10 samples to verify analytical accuracy, while duplicate samples were used to assess reproducibility. The accuracy and precision of the analytical procedures were 6.9% and 3.2% for As (LOD: 0.79 mg/kg) and 4.1% and 5.9% for Pb (LOD: 1.1 mg/kg). Potato samples were analyzed by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (PerkinElmer ELAN DRC-e, Canada) following aqua regia digestion in an ISO 17025 accredited laboratory.

Soil pH determination

The pH of the soil samples collected adjacent to the potato sampling sites in the Shirak region was measured following the procedure described in ISO 10390:2021 (ISO, 2021). Specifically, 10 g of dried and sieved (< 2 mm) soil was placed in 250 ml Erlenmeyer flasks, to which 50 ml of distilled water was added. The suspension was then placed on a shaker and mixed for 1 h. The pH measurements were carried out using a pH 60 VioLab Benchtop pH Meter.

PTEs risk assessment

Dietary exposure to As and Pb through potato consumption in Shirak region was assessed by calculating the estimated daily intake (EDI), expressed as μg/kg body weight per day:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{EDI}} = {\mathrm{C}} \times \frac{{{\mathrm{IR}}}}{{{\mathrm{BW}}}}$$\end{document}EDI=C×IRBWwhere C is the mean content of As and Pb in potato flesh (µg/kg fresh weight), IR is the mean daily potato consumption, and BW is the mean body weight obtained from the national 24-h recall survey (Pipoyan et al., 2023). Since potatoes are predominantly consumed peeled, only the contents determined in the potato flesh were used in the exposure calculations.

Inorganic As is a genotoxic carcinogen, whereas Pb was assessed with respect to its non-carcinogenic critical effects (nephrotoxicity and cardiovascular effects). Chronic exposure was considered with an exposure frequency (EF) of 365 days/year, an exposure duration (ED) corresponding to the entire lifetime, and an average time (AT) equal to ED × 365 days. Because, under chronic exposure conditions, EF × ED equals AT, these parameters are cancelled in Eq. (2) and are stated here for completeness.

Potato consumption data (mean of 125 g/day) and body weight values (75 for males, 67 for females, and 69 for the overall studied population) of adult respondents from the Shirak region were obtained from the national 24-h recall survey conducted in urban and rural communities in Armenia (Pipoyan et al., 2023). Only consumption levels among adult population were available, so the vulnerable groups such as children were not included in the current study.

Risk assessment and characterization for Pb and iAs is based on the margin of exposure (MOE) approach (Bennekou et al., 2025). MOE is the ratio between the reference point (RP) and the estimated exposure (EDI):3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{MOE}} = \frac{{{\mathrm{RP}}}}{{{\mathrm{EDI}}}}$$\end{document}MOE=RPEDI

For iAs, the health-based guidance value of 0.06 μg/kg/day, established in the latest EFSA scientific opinion (2024) based on human epidemiological data (skin cancer endpoint), was used as the RP (Schrenk et al., 2024). For Pb, two benchmark dose lower confidence limits (BMDLs) were used as RP (EFSA, 2010). The BMDL10 of 0.63 μg/kg/day for nephrotoxicity (chronic kidney disease) and BMDL01 of 1.5 μg/kg/day for cardiovascular effects. An MOE value greater than 10 indicated no health concerns for Pb (Boon et al., 2017). All risk calculations were performed separately for males, females and the overall adult population residing in the Shirak region.

Results and discussion

Data analyses and spatial distribution of PTEs in soil

The descriptive statistics of As and Pb contents in Shirak region soils, as well as in soils from potato sampling sites and in potato flesh and skin grown in these soils, are presented in Table 1. The Shapiro–Wilk test of normality indicated that both As and Pb in the Shirak region soils exhibit non-normal distributions, whereas soils from potato sampling sites showed normal distributions for both elements. After removal of an extreme value identified by boxplot analysis (Fig. 2), As displayed a normal distribution in the Shirak region soils. The coefficient of variation (CV) in all cases was < 60%, which is a typical pattern for background sites (Beus et al., 1976). This suggests the absence of significant anthropogenic or natural sources of contamination by As and Pb. Arsenic mean content in the Shirak region soils (15.1 mg/kg) was 1.7 times higher than the mean content (8.91 mg/kg) observed in soils from potato sampling sites. In contrast, the mean Pb content in Shirak region soils (12.5 mg/kg) was 1.6 times lower than the mean content (20.3 mg/kg) observed in soils from potato sampling sites (Table 1).Fig. 2Boxplots of As and Pb contents (mg/kg) in soil of the Shirak region, potato sampling site soils, potato flesh and skin

The pH of soil in the potato sampling sites ranged from 5.90 to 8.00, with a mean value of 7.28 (Table 1). Except for one site, where the minimum pH was recorded, the soils in all other locations were characterized as neutral to slightly alkaline (Weil & Brady, 2017).

The spatial distribution maps (Fig. 3) of As and Pb contents in the Shirak region soils show that relatively higher values (> 75%) for both elements are mainly concentrated in the southern and northern parts of the region, while the central part is characterized by lower contents (< 25%). The > 95% values of As observed in the northern part of Shirak coincide with known Sb–Ni–As ore manifestations (Republican geological fund, 2025).Fig. 3Spatial distribution of As and Pb contents (mg/kg) in the Shirak region (Armenia) soils

Correlation analysis revealed no significant relationship between As and Pb contents. However, after removing several outliers and extreme values (five samples in total), a detailed inspection of the As–Pb scatterplot indicated the presence of three subsamples within the main dataset. Further analysis of these subsamples (Fig. 4) showed that in all three cases, As and Pb followed a normal distribution. Moreover, each subsample exhibited a significant positive correlation (p < 0.01): r = 0.873 for subsample I (S1), r = 0.720 for subsample II (S2), and r = 0.701 for subsample III (S3). These findings suggest that different origins or conditioning factors may influence the contents of As and Pb in the studied soils.Fig. 4Scatterplots of As versus Pb in the soils of the Shirak region and identified subsamples

The spatial distribution map of these subsamples shows that S1 is mainly located in remote settlement areas characterized by minimal agricultural activity and the presence of highland pastures. S2 is primarily distributed near major regional roads, Gyumri city, and its surroundings, which are characterized by villages and intensive agricultural activity. This suggests some anthropogenic alteration of As and Pb soil chemistry. The geological bedrock in these areas is mainly composed of tuff, ignimbrite tuff, tuff lava, and alluvial, diluvial, and proluvial sediments (Vardanyan & Valesyan, 2007). S3 is spatially distributed throughout the region, occupying areas where volcanic rocks (basalt, andesite, rhyolite, obsidian, dacite, etc.) dominate in the geological base (Vardanyan & Valesyan, 2007). A spatial cluster of sampling sites representing S3 is located in the northern part of the region, characterized by the dominance of dolerite basalt in the geological composition and known Sb–Ni–As ore manifestations (Republican geological fund, 2025). Among these three subsamples (Fig. 5), the lowest mean content (13.0 mg/kg) of As was observed in S1 while in S2 and S3 the mean contents were approximately equal (15.5 and 15.6 mg/kg, respectively). As and Pb contents in potato flesh and skin, and their relationship to soil contents of these elements

In the Shirak region, the mean contents of As and Pb in potato skin were 2.3 and 2.6 times higher than in the flesh, respectively. The maximum contents of both elements in potato flesh and skin were observed at sampling sites located near the Sb–Ni–As mineralization zone. Spearman correlation analysis revealed significant positive correlations (p < 0.05) between As in potato flesh and Pb in soil (r = 0.636), Pb in soil and Pb in potato skin (r = 0.685), and Pb in potato flesh and As in potato skin (r = 0.661). Significant negative correlations (p < 0.05) were detected between Pb and As in soil (r = − 0.685), and between As in soil and Pb in potato skin (r = − 0.721). This finding is in line with the pattern of As and Pb mobility and absorption in soil minerals depending on the soil pH (Weil & Brady, 2017). Particularly, As mobility in soil decreases under acidic to neutral conditions․ In contrast, when soil environments tend to become alkaline which is the case of the soil of potato sampling sites, the increase in As mobility can be expected. In the case of Pb, soil acidity promotes Pb mobility, which tends to decrease under alkaline conditions. This can be a potential explanation for the observed difference and identified relationships of As and Pb contents in soil, potato flesh, and skin in the studied territories.

PTEs soil to potato transfer

Transfer factors highlight that potato tubers are not considered as bio-accumulators of As and Pb (TF < 1). The transfer factors (TF) were calculated for skin and flesh separately (Table 2). For arsenic, the mean TF to skin was 1.3 × 10−5, while TF to flesh was comparable at 1.6 × 10−5, though the flesh values exhibited site-to-site variability (range: 8.1 × 10−⁷ to 1.2 × 10−4). Lead transfer was even more restricted, which is in line with the other research findings (Collin et al., 2022; Faculty et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2024), highlighting the low accumulation level of Pb in potato. Mean Pb TF to skin reached only 5.0 × 10−6, and Pb TF to flesh dropped further still to 1.8 × 10−6. Compared to the flesh, skin demonstrated a significant capacity to accumulate both metals. This indicates that the potato periderm or skin functions as an effective biogeochemical barrier, restricting the movement of metals into the edible inner tissues. The observed accumulation patterns highlight the importance of considering plant tissue-specific metal partitioning when evaluating environmental exposure and human health risks (Wang et al., 2024). It can also have practical implications that peeling potatoes reduces the contaminant load, as evidenced by transfer factors observed in flesh and skin (Table 2). In food safety terms, this simple processing step may reduce dietary As and Pb exposure (Davies et al., 1983). For populations consuming unpeeled potatoes whether by tradition or preference, will provide higher exposure levels, so our data support peeling as a prudent public health recommendation. Codling et al. (2016) examined four potato cultivars grown in legacy lead-arsenate-contaminated orchard soils (soil Pb 350–961 mg kg−1, soil As 43–290 mg kg−1). Despite soil metal loads 20–50 times higher than ours, they detected no Pb in tuber tissue whatsoever (< 0.015 mg kg−1) and As in peeled tubers of 0.24–1.44 mg kg−1. Dietary risk assessment for potato consumption

Due to fact that ICP-MS quantifies total arsenic (tAs), while toxicity is driven by the inorganic fraction (iAs), the iAs content of potato flesh was estimated from tAs using a conversion scenario based on variability of the inorganic fraction in plant origin food. In accordance with the approach proposed by EFSA, 70% of tAs was assumed to be present as iAs, reflecting the proportion considered most representative for risk assessment purposes in foods by EFSA. This conversion factor was applied to estimate the iAs content in potato flesh samples before the EDI calculations (EFSA, 2009). Dietary exposure estimates of As and Pb varied across population groups (Table 3), primarily reflecting differences in body weight and potato consumption patterns. Females had slightly higher EDIs for both Pb and As compared to males, and the total adult population EDI values were intermediate. Inorganic As is a genotoxic and carcinogenic substance, therefore, the MOE is interpreted here as a characterization of carcinogenic risk (Bennekou et al., 2025; Schrenk et al., 2024). The reference point (RP) used (0.06 µg/kg bw/day) is derived directly from human epidemiological data on skin cancer, so the generic benchmark of 10,000 was not applied. In scientific opinion of the EFSA (2024), it is stated that for adult consumers iAs MOEs of approximately 0.2–2 range were considered to raise a health concern (Schrenk et al., 2024). The mean iAs MOE obtained in the present study (4.84 × 102 for the 70% scenario) was substantially higher than this concern range, indicating that iAs exposure through potato consumption alone is unlikely to pose a carcinogenic risk to the adult population of the Shirak region. Nevertheless, given the genotoxic and carcinogenic properties of iAs and the importance of long-term cumulative exposure from the overall diet, continued monitoring of As in staple foods remains warranted.

For Pb, the critical effects (nephrotoxicity and cardiovascular effects) are non-carcinogenic. The estimated MOE values for Pb ranged from 9.38 × 103 to 1.05 × 104 based on the BMDL10 of 0.63 μg/kg/day (chronic kidney disease) and from 2.23 to 2.49 × 104 based on the BMDL01 of 1.5 μg/kg/day (cardiovascular effects). These MOE values are substantially higher than the reference value of 10 (Boon et al., 2017), and remain two to three orders of magnitude above the more conservative MOE benchmark of 100 applied to non-carcinogenic effects (Bennekou et al., 2025), indicating that Pb exposure through potato consumption alone is unlikely to pose a non-carcinogenic health concern for the adult population. Nevertheless, considering the cumulative toxicity of Pb and the absence of a safe exposure threshold, the contribution of potatoes to total dietary Pb exposure should not be neglected.

Conclusion

This study provides the first insight into As and Pb soil geochemistry of the Shirak region (Armenia) and soil-to-plant transfer of these elements to potato, being the region’s primary agricultural product. It was found that the highest values of As and Pb detected in potato flesh and skin were observed in the sampling sites characterized by the relatively high contents of these elements in soil. A relatively high (> 75%) values of both elements were observed in soil developed in areas characterized by volcanic rocks. Three subsamples were identified based on the relationship of As and Pb and the spatial distribution of these subsamples showed a distinct pattern related to the level of anthropogenic activities and composition of the region’s geological bedrock. The assessment of soil-to-potato transfer factor (TF) for both potato flesh and skin is particularly important from a dietary exposure perspective, as the potato flesh represents the primary edible portion, whereas skin contains higher element contents due to direct soil contact and surface adsorption. Therefore, TF estimates provide valuable information to understand the potential contribution of potatoes to human dietary exposure and highlight peeling as a possible risk mitigation measure. The substantially higher MOE values observed in this study suggest that the studied toxic elements exposure through potato consumption alone do not pose a health risk to the adult population of the Shirak region. Nevertheless, given the cumulative nature of the elements’ toxicity, their contribution to the total dietary exposure remains relevant, particularly for populations with high reliance on locally produced staple foods. This work emphasizes the necessity of regular monitoring of As and Pb contents in potatoes. In the future, additional sampling sites, as well as other elements such as Cd, Cu, Zn, should be considered for ensuring a comprehensive characterization of toxic elements in the soil-to-plant system and ensuring food safety and ecosystem health.
