Impact of the 2017 mental health act revision on suicide attempts: evidence from emergency department data in Korea
Authors: Nary Hong, Sun Hwa Lee, Jong Seok Oh, Duk Hee Lee, Ji Yeon Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSouth Korea has one of the highest suicide rates among OECD countries, and suicide and self-harm among young adults continue to increase. In 2017, the Mental Health Promotion and Welfare Act was revised to strengthen the protection of patient rights by introducing stricter procedural requirements for involuntary psychiatric hospitalization, including mandatory external psychiatric evaluations. This study evaluated whether these policy revisions were associated with changes in patterns of suicide and self-harm.MethodsThis study analyzed nationwide multicenter surveillance data from the Emergency Department-based Injury In-depth Surveillance (EDIIS) system, which includes the EDs of 23 tertiary hospitals across South Korea, from 2014 to 2020. A quasi-experimental Regression Discontinuity in Time (RDiT) design was applied to estimate changes in monthly suicide- and self-harm-related ED visits following implementation of the revised law in June 2017.ResultsFollowing the policy revision, suicide- and self-harm-related ED visits increased significantly, with an estimated increase of approximately 95 additional cases per month. Concurrently, discharges against medical advice due to refusal of psychiatric treatment increased, whereas psychiatric inpatient admissions decreased. These findings remained consistent across multiple model specifications and bandwidth selections.ConclusionsThe 2017 revision of the Mental Health Promotion and Welfare Act was temporally associated with a significant increase in suicide- and self-harm-related ED visits. The findings suggest that stricter procedural requirements for involuntary psychiatric hospitalization may have unintentionally created barriers to timely psychiatric intervention for high-risk individuals. These results highlight the importance of balancing the protection of patient autonomy with access to effective and timely mental health care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27986-x.

Background

The suicide rate in South Korea (24.1% in 2020) exceeds the OECD country average (11.3% in 2020) by more than twice and has consistently vied for the top position with Lithuania over the past two decades, rendering it a highly concerning issue [1]. Particularly alarming is the recent substantial increase in self-harm and suicide rates among teenagers and individuals in their twenties [2], highlighting the nation’s growing mental health burden.

Amid this backdrop, concerns regarding potential violations of patient rights during involuntary psychiatric hospitalization led to a comprehensive legislative overhaul through the enforcement of the revised Mental Health Promotion and Welfare Act (hereinafter “the revised Act”) on May 30, 2017. This reform was prompted by a 2016 Constitutional Court ruling that declared certain provisions of the previous law unconstitutional [3]. The revised Act significantly strengthened the criteria for involuntary admission in order to protect patient autonomy. It mandated that involuntary hospitalizations extending beyond two weeks require evaluations by two independent psychiatrists affiliated with public or designated institutions. Furthermore, it narrowed the legal definition of mental illness to include only individuals with substantial impairments in daily functioning due to delusions, hallucinations, or mood and thought disorders.

Several countries have reported ongoing debate regarding stricter regulations for involuntary psychiatric hospitalization. Although these reforms were intended to strengthen patient autonomy and protect civil rights, concerns have been raised about unintended consequences, including delayed access to psychiatric treatment for high-risk individuals. Previous studies have suggested that mental health laws requiring evidence of dangerousness before involuntary admission may prolong the duration of untreated psychiatric illness and worsen clinical outcomes, including suicide risk [4]. International comparative studies have also highlighted persistent tension between protecting patient rights and ensuring timely access to emergency psychiatric care [5]. Similar concerns have been raised in Korea following the 2017 revision of the Mental Health Promotion and Welfare Act, particularly regarding barriers to early intervention for patients with acute psychiatric crises.

Although the revised Act was designed to protect patients’ rights, it has raised concerns regarding its impact on timely access to psychiatric care, especially in emergency situations. The requirement to secure external psychiatric evaluations from other institutions imposes a considerable administrative burden on medical professionals. Although the final decision for hospitalization still rests with the attending physician, the potential legal consequences of misjudgment may lead clinicians to hesitate in admitting patients or to prematurely discharge those in need of treatment [6].

Mental health professionals have expressed concern that the combination of limited availability of certified external psychiatrists, increased administrative demands, and heightened legal liability has undermined access to appropriate psychiatric care and contributed to early discharges. While the revised Act was intended to protect patients reluctant to undergo involuntary admission, its complexity and workforce limitations may impede the provision of timely and effective treatment.

The Act also stipulates that both treatment necessity and risk of harm to self or others must be proven to justify involuntary admission. As a result, extending hospitalization beyond two weeks has become increasingly difficult, complicating preventive care strategies aimed at avoiding symptom escalation.

The World Health Organization (WHO) advocates for international standardization in mental health systems. Its 1991 “Principles for the Protection of Persons with Mental Illness” recommend assessments by two independent mental health professionals before involuntary admission [7].

Although the revised Korean law aligns with this principle in spirit, the requirement that evaluations be conducted by psychiatrists from public or designated hospitals presents practical obstacles, particularly in emergency department settings where rapid decisions are often required.

Given Korea’s persistently high suicide rate, evaluating the revised Act’s impact on access to psychiatric care among individuals in crisis is an important policy question.

This study aimed to analyze changes in suicide attempts and hospitalization patterns before and after the 2017 revision of the Mental Health Promotion and Welfare Act in Korea. In particular, we hypothesized that the revised law may have unintentionally increased barriers to psychiatric treatment for individuals requiring intensive mental health care following suicide attempts, thereby contributing to an increase in suicide attempts. By examining these changes using nationwide emergency department data, this study sought to provide empirical evidence to inform future suicide prevention and mental health policy improvements.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This paper uses data spanning 2014 to 2020 obtained from the Emergency Department Based Injury In-depth Surveillance (EDIIS), a comprehensive initiative developed by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KCDA), covering twenty-three major tertiary hospital emergency departments. EDIIS collects injury-related information, including causes, types, and outcomes, alongside demographic details like age, gender, occupation, education, and income. Focusing on individuals admitted due to suicide and self-harm attempts, six emergency departments conduct detailed monitoring of attempts, recording methods, prior attempts, and discharge reasons.

The registry was established through a prospective method of face-to-face surveys of injured patients visiting emergency departments (EDs) and the assessment of medical records from January to December every year. This mix of features inherent in surveys and medical records enhances the number of observations and increases data reliability, as noted by [8]. The inclusion criteria involved patients with a primary diagnosis code or other diagnosis codes falling within the range of S00 to T79, which pertains to injuries, addiction, and specific consequences resulting from external causes. The classification was based on the Korean Standard Classification of Diseases [9].

The EDIIS data distinctly records patients visiting emergency departments for either ‘suicide attempts’ or ‘self-harm attempts.’ Each term is separately categorized based on comprehensive analyses of face-to-face surveys and medical records. Individuals are classified as ‘suicide attempts’ when they are determined to have intended to end their lives. In contrast, those categorized as ‘self-harm attempts’ are individuals who aim to harm themselves without the intention of ending their lives.

Data collection

The study analyzed a range of variables extracted from the Emergency Department-based Injury In-depth Surveillance (EDIIS) database to characterize individuals who attempted suicide or self-harm. The collected variables included demographic and clinical factors such as gender, age, mode of emergency department (ED) visit, season, time of arrival, alcohol consumption, prior suicide attempts, causes of the attempt, substances involved in intoxication, and medical outcomes.

Patients were categorized into four age groups: adolescents (< 20 years), young adults (20–40 years), middle-aged adults (40–65 years), and elderly adults (> 65 years). Arrival time was divided into six-hour intervals to analyze temporal patterns of ED visits.

The causes of suicide or self-harm attempts were classified as follows: conflicts with family or friends, bereavement (i.e., death of family or close acquaintances), psychiatric problems, physical health issues, financial difficulties, work or school-related problems, abuse, and other unspecified causes.

Substances involved in intoxication were categorized into therapeutic drugs—including sedative-hypnotics, analgesics, psychotropic drugs, and cardiovascular agents—and toxic substances such as pesticides, carbon monoxide (CO), corrosive agents, alcohol, and solvents.

Medical outcomes were classified into five categories: discharge, discharge against medical advice, hospital admission, transfer to another hospital, and death.

Statistical analysis

This study employs the RDiT method to evaluate the impact of the revised Act on suicide and self-harm attempts. The empirical strategy focuses on examining whether the legislative change, particularly the introduction of an additional diagnosis by an external psychiatrist, has a differential effect on the incidence of the negative outcomes. The RDiT method is chosen due to the temporal nature of the intervention and the uniform implementation of the law across Korea on May 30, 2017, which makes time the running variable in the analysis. Also, the analyses are stratified by gender and age to account for variations in self-destructive behaviors across demographics.

The Regression Discontinuity in Time (RDiT) design was considered appropriate for this study because the revised Mental Health Promotion and Welfare Act was implemented nationwide on a fixed date (May 30, 2017), creating a clear temporal threshold for comparison. This design allows assessment of whether suicide and self-harm attempts changed discontinuously immediately after the policy implementation, while accounting for underlying time trends. Since the data were aggregated at the monthly level, June 2017 was defined as the first full post-implementation month and was therefore selected as the cutoff point (t = 0).

To assess the robustness of the findings, we employed the data-driven bandwidth selection procedure proposed by Calonico et al. [10, 11] and additionally examined multiple bandwidth specifications ranging from 10 to 16 months. The estimated effects remained highly consistent across alternative bandwidths, supporting the stability of the observed discontinuity following the policy implementation.

To visually inspect for any discernible discontinuities around the threshold, RD plots for the number of monthly individuals with suicide and self-harm attempts are constructed using a fourth-order polynomial and uniform kernel.

Subsequently, the RDiT analysis is conducted following the approach outlined by Calonico et al. [9, 10] employing the nonparametric local linear method and utilizing triangular kernel weights along with data-driven bandwidth selections. Subgroup analysis by sex and age (Fig. 2 and Appendix A1)

Figure 2 explores the heterogeneous effects of the law revision across sex and age subgroups. A pronounced and statistically significant increase in suicide attempts was observed in both males and females aged 20–39 and 40–64 years. In particular, women aged 20–39 exhibited the most marked increase. No significant changes were noted among those under 20 or over 65 years of age. Among women aged 20–39, the proportion of suicide attempts attributed to depression increased substantially from 22.2% in 2014 to 43.8% in 2020. In contrast, the proportion of cases attributed to “other” causes (e.g., bereavement, abuse) decreased over the same period.

The overall number of psychiatric admissions exhibited a gradual decline from 2014, with a notable 14.32% decrease observed between 2017 and 2018. These findings suggest a growing difficulty in securing psychiatric inpatient care for high-risk individuals—particularly those at risk of suicide or self-harm—in the aftermath of the law revision.

Discussion

This study applied a Regression Discontinuity in Time (RDiT) design, a well-established quasi-experimental method for causal inference from time-series data, to estimate the effect of the 2017 revised Mental Health Promotion and Welfare Act on ED presentations for suicide and self-harm attempts. RDiT exploits the sharp, pre-determined implementation date of the policy as an exogenous threshold, identifying the causal effect under the assumption that no other systematic shock coincided with that threshold, an assumption we verify empirically below. Importantly, our findings should not be interpreted as evidence that the revised Act directly increased suicidal ideation in the general population. Rather, the observed increase in suicide- and self-harm-related ED visits is more plausibly explained by changes in psychiatric care access and hospitalization processes following the policy revision. The procedural requirements for involuntary admission may have created barriers to timely psychiatric intervention for high-risk individuals, potentially contributing to increased premature discharge and repeated ED presentations.

We further examined whether safeguards for involuntary admissions may have inadvertently obstructed access to necessary psychiatric care. Given the known variation in self-harm behavior by demographic subgroups, analyses were stratified by sex and age. This approach aligns with prior studies that have investigated age-specific determinants of rising suicide rates in the United States [12].

There is ongoing debate regarding whether inpatient hospitalization is beneficial for patients following a suicide attempt. Some studies suggest that hospitalization may be ineffective or potentially harmful. For instance, Goldman-Mellor et al. [13] questioned the efficacy of hospitalization and raised concerns about iatrogenic effects. In contrast, Large et al. [4] emphasized that delays in psychiatric treatment (DUP) negatively affect prognosis, cautioning that legal criteria requiring imminent risk (ODC) for admission may hinder timely treatment.

Mental health care continues to be socially underrecognized relative to physical illness, underscoring the critical role of mental health policy. Lang [14] found that U.S. states mandating mental health parity legislation experienced reductions in suicide rates, highlighting the importance of ensuring equitable access to mental and physical health care.

To date, definitions of self-harm and suicide remain inconsistent across countries [15], which limits cross-study comparability. Terms such as ‘suicidal ideation,’ ‘parasuicide,’ ‘aborted suicide attempt,’ and ‘non-suicidal self-injury’ are used interchangeably in the literature [8]. In this study, suicide attempts were defined as actions with the intent to end one’s life, distinguishing them from non-suicidal self-harm, and the dataset was classified accordingly.

RDiT analysis is commonly employed in resource and environmental economics, where high-frequency time series data are available [16–19]. More recently, this method has been adopted in health economics, including work by Hartojo [20] and Yudhistira [21]. Although traditional regression discontinuity (RD) methods are more prevalent in health policy research [22, 23]. RDiT has gained traction due to its temporal sensitivity [24, 25]. In this study, we applied RDiT to monthly nationwide ED data, leveraging the uniform implementation date of the revised law.

RDiT analysis revealed that the number of ED visits related to suicide and self-harm increased following the legislative amendment, with the largest increase observed among women aged 20–39. While the underlying causes of these differences across subgroups remain unclear, these findings suggest that further research is needed to determine whether groups already at high risk for depression and self-harm exhibit varying degrees of vulnerability regarding barriers to accessing mental health care. Analysis of the causes of these attempts suggests that increased difficulty in admitting patients to psychiatric wards may have contributed to a rise in discharges against medical advice. Patients presenting after suicide attempts often refuse psychiatric treatment despite remaining at high risk for repeated self-harm [26]. Prior to the 2017 revision, involuntary admission could be initiated more readily with guardian consent. However, the revised Act introduced stricter procedural requirements for involuntary hospitalization, making compulsory admission more difficult in practice. Consequently, high-risk individuals refusing treatment were more likely to leave against medical advice without adequate psychiatric intervention.

The validity of the causal estimate rests on the RDiT identification assumption that no other major systematic shock occurred precisely at the June 2017 threshold. To our knowledge, we identified no major nationwide socio-environmental events likely to explain the abrupt discontinuity observed after the policy implementation. Specifically, there were no acute macroeconomic crises or highly publicized celebrity suicides during this period that would be expected to trigger a nationwide surge in suicidal behaviors through the Werther effect. The RDiT design also helps mitigate confounding by time-invariant factors and smooth time trends, as these are flexibly controlled for thorough local linear fitting on either side of the cutoff. The identified discontinuity—approximately 91–98 additional ED presentations for suicide and self-harm attempts per month—is temporally associated with the Act and is consistent with the proposed access-barrier mechanism. The access-barrier mechanism provides the theoretically coherent pathway: The findings suggest that the procedural requirements introduced by the Act may have created barriers to timely involuntary psychiatric admission for high-risk individuals. This interpretation is further corroborated by the concurrent rise in against-medical-advice discharges and the 14.32% decline in psychiatric inpatient admissions between 2017 and 2018 documented in this study.

An important alternative explanation for the observed increase in ED presentations is that it may reflect changes in healthcare utilization, reporting practices, or case capture rather than a true rise in suicide attempts. Several observations support the interpretation that the observed discontinuity is unlikely to be explained solely by changes in reporting or healthcare utilization. First, the EDIIS surveillance system underwent no major structural or coding changes in 2017; case ascertainment protocols remained constant throughout 2014–2020, making a sudden discontinuity in reporting practices at precisely the June 2017 threshold implausible. Second, the RDiT methodology is specifically designed to distinguish sharp, discrete discontinuities from smooth trends; a gradual drift in help-seeking or recording behaviour would not produce the abrupt step-change observed in our data. Third, the corroborating clinical indicators strongly support a genuine rise in suicide attempts rather than a statistical artefact. Voluntary discharges against medical advice due to refusal of psychiatric treatment rose from 40.5% to 53.5% of all voluntary discharges between 2014 and 2020 (as shown in Table 2), and total inpatient psychiatric admissions fell by 14.32% between 2017 and 2018. This systemic shift suggests that high-risk individuals who would previously have been protected through inpatient admission were more likely to be discharged without adequate psychiatric intervention, consistent with the proposed access-barrier mechanism. The observed spike therefore most plausibly reflects a genuine increase in actual repeated suicide attempts among these vulnerable patients. These converging lines of evidence are further corroborated by the independent findings of Kim et al. [6], who documented reduced psychiatric inpatient utilization and increased ED revisits following the same policy change, and together favor the interpretation that the observed increase reflects a genuine rise in suicide attempts driven by the access-barrier mechanism described above.

This study has several limitations. This study utilized data from the emergency departments of 23 tertiary hospitals across South Korea. Because the dataset is based on tertiary hospital emergency department data rather than data from all medical institutions, it may not fully represent the overall population structure. However, there were no major changes in either the number of participating tertiary hospitals or the tertiary hospital system itself during the study period. In addition, the functions and roles of tertiary hospitals in providing intensive emergency care and definitive treatment for patients with suicide and self-harm attempts remained largely stable over time. Therefore, although the dataset may not completely represent the entire population, it can still be considered to possess a certain degree of representativeness in reflecting severe cases of suicide and self-harm attempts and patterns of emergency healthcare utilization.

Moreover, while the revised law mandates external psychiatric evaluation for involuntary admission, we lacked data on whether psychiatric specialists were available at the time of ED presentation. However, given the observed decline in inpatient admissions and the prominent policy shift mandating external evaluations, it is plausible that this provision contributed to reduced treatment access and increased suicide attempts.

Another limitation is the inclusion of data from the COVID-19 pandemic period, during which mental health concerns were heightened due to prolonged restrictions. Nevertheless, our findings indicate that the sharp rise in suicide attempts began immediately after the policy change, preceding the pandemic. This suggests that the observed trends are more likely attributable to the law revision than to pandemic-related factors.

This study highlights the lack of realistic alternatives to inpatient care for individuals with severe mental illness and exposes a critical gap in the revised law. The post-revision increase in suicide attempts underscores the need for policy modifications to ensure effective mental health treatment and reduce suicide risk.

A further limitation concerns the possibility that the observed increase in ED presentations reflects changes in healthcare utilization, help-seeking behavior, or case capture rather than solely a true rise in suicide attempt incidence. We cannot entirely exclude, for example, that some individuals who would previously have been admitted and managed as inpatients instead re-presented to EDs multiple times, thereby inflating the count of distinct presentations without a proportionate increase in the number of individuals affected. Nor can we fully rule out secular trends in help-seeking behavior coinciding with the policy period.

Conclusion

This study identified a significant increase in suicide attempts following the 2017 revision of the Mental Health Promotion and Welfare Act. Although the legislation was designed to safeguard patient rights, it may have inadvertently impeded timely access to psychiatric care. These findings highlight the importance of revisiting current policies to balance the protection of individual rights with the need for timely and effective mental health interventions.
