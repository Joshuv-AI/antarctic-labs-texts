Promoter G-quadruplex structures regulate tissue-specific gene expression and functional differentiation in Bombyx mori
Authors: Lijun Xiang, Jin Li, Bingxian Wu, Tingting Yao, Xiaojuan Zhang, Keling Wu, Mingxi Zheng, Qili Feng, Kangkang Niu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDNA G-quadruplex structures (G4s) are noncanonical nucleic acid structures that play crucial roles in gene transcription regulation, particularly when located within promoter regions. Tissue-specific gene expression underlies the development and functional specialization of multicellular organisms. However, the potential role of G4s in tissue-specific transcriptional regulation remains largely unexplored. In this study, we conducted an integrative analysis of G-quadruplex (G4) distribution and function in the silkworm brain and fat body, two substantially differentiated tissues in terms of structure and function, using G4-CUT&Tag, ATAC-seq, RNA-seq, and histone modification datasets.ResultsWe found that promoter G4s were highly enriched in regions of open chromatin and active histone modification, and that G4 folding showed a correlation with transcriptional activation in a tissue-specific manner. Even at identical genomic DNA sequences, G4 folding patterns differed markedly between the brain and fat body and were associated with tissue-specific gene expression profiles. Genes marked by brain-specific G4s were enriched in neural pathways, whereas genes with fat body-specific G4s were enriched in metabolic pathways, closely aligning with the physiological roles of each tissue. Moreover, chemical stabilization of G4s by pyridostatin disrupted gene expression and impaired tissue development, including neurogenesis in the brain and lipid metabolism in the fat body.ConclusionsOur results suggest that G4s may function as dynamic epigenetic modulators that shape tissue-specific gene expression and coordinate functional differentiation in insects.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12915-026-02696-3.

Background

DNA G-quadruplex structures (G4s) are complex noncanonical DNA structures that form in guanine-rich regions of the genome. These structures consist of four-stranded conformations, which are stabilized by Hoogsteen hydrogen bonds [1] and monovalent cations (e.g., potassium or sodium ions) [2–4]. G4s are evolutionarily conserved and widely distributed across eukaryotic genomes, with notable enrichment in regulatory regions, including promoters, untranslated regions (UTRs), telomeres, and replication origins [5–7]. Functionally, G4s have been implicated in multiple aspects of genome regulation, including transcription, replication, and genome stability [8–11]. In particular, genome-wide analyses have revealed that a substantial proportion of human gene promoters harbour putative G4-forming sequences (PQSs), for example, oncogenes such as MYC [12], BCL2 [13, 14], and KIT [15], highlighting their potential role in controlling critical cellular processes and disease states [16, 17].

The regulatory functions of G4s are increasingly supported by experimental studies. Small molecules that selectively stabilize G4s and G4-specific antibodies have been employed to perturb G4 formation in cells, revealing effects on transcriptional activity, chromatin accessibility, and genome organization [18]. These studies indicate that G4s can modulate gene expression both directly, by affecting transcription factor binding, and indirectly, by altering higher-order chromatin structures [19, 20]. Notably, G4 formation is highly dynamic and context dependent and is influenced by local chromatin states, transcriptional activity, and the presence of specific binding proteins [9, 21]. Regions of open chromatin, often nucleosome-depleted, provide favourable conditions for G4 folding, enabling interactions with transcription factors, chromatin remodellers, and epigenetic modifiers [22–24]. High-throughput sequencing techniques, such as G4 ChIP-seq [22], G4 CUT&Tag [24], and G4access [23], have revealed that genomic G4s exhibit cell type-specific distribution patterns in different cells, despite having identical genomic sequences [25]. These G4-formation variations have been linked to cell-specific gene expression programs during cell differentiation and development [26, 27]. In particular, G4s tend to localize near transcription start sites (TSSs) of highly expressed genes [28], suggesting that G4 folding may serve as an epigenetic regulatory mechanism for gene transcription in a cell type-specific manner.

Despite these advances, most studies on G4 function have been conducted in cultured mammalian cells, and relatively little is known about how G4s regulate gene expression across tissues within multicellular organisms. Investigating tissue-specific G4 landscapes provides an opportunity to understand whether and how identical genomic sequences can give rise to distinct transcriptional outputs through differential G4 formation. In this study, we present a comprehensive investigation of G4 distribution and function in two structurally and functionally distinct tissues of the silkworm, the brain and fat body, in an attempt to understand whether genomic G4s are associated with or contribute to the tissue-specific expression of functional genes. By integrating G4-CUT&Tag, ATAC-seq, RNA-seq, and histone modification data, as well as G4 ligand-based functional assays, we characterize the tissue-specific G4 landscapes in the brain and fat body, which have identical genome sequences. Our findings demonstrate that promoter G4s are closely associated with active chromatin states and elevated gene expression and may contribute to tissue-specific gene transcription in various tissues.

Results

Immunofluorescence detection of G4 distribution in the brain and fat body

To investigate the presence of G4s in different tissues, we used the antibody BG4 to detect G4s in the brain and fat body of Bombyx mori (B. mori) larvae on day 3 of the fifth instar (L5D3), a model lepidopteran insect. BG4 is a single-chain antibody fragment that specifically recognizes G4s and has been widely used for G4 detection [29, 30]. Immunofluorescence staining with BG4 revealed the widespread presence of G4 signals in both the brain and fat body (Fig. 1A). G4 signals appear in almost all fat body cells, whereas significant intercellular variation in G4 abundance was observed in the brain, where much stronger G4 signals were detected in the cerebral cortex. To explore the cellular specificity of G4 enrichment, we performed coimmunostaining of G4 signals with synaptotagmin 1 (SYT1), a presynaptic membrane protein that regulates the fusion of synaptic vesicles with the cell membrane by binding to calcium ions and is therefore often used as a mature neuronal cell marker [31, 32]. Coimmunostaining demonstrated a clear lack of colocalization of the G4 and SYT1 signals, indicating that fewer G4s were formed in mature neuronal cells (Fig. 1B). To further quantify this relationship, we dissociated the larval brains into single cells and performed fluorescence intensity analysis. A negative correlation was detected between G4 (red) and SYT1 (green) fluorescence intensities (Fig. 1C, D), suggesting that as neuronal cells mature, the number of genomic G4 structures decreases.

Promoter enrichment and tissue specificity detected by genome-wide G4 mapping

Although immunofluorescence qualitatively indicated the abundance of intracellular G4s in different tissues, it could not precisely map the genomic locations of the G4s. Therefore, we performed G4-CUT&Tag to further investigate the G4 genomic distribution in the brain and fat body. G4-CUT&Tag has been used to quantitatively detect genomic G4s in various cell lines [8, 24, 33]. We first optimized the G4-CUT&Tag protocol by utilizing cell nuclei extracted from the cells of fresh fat body and brain tissues (Fig. 2A). Genome browser views of the genome-wide analysis (Fig. 2B) and of individual loci (Fig. 2C) revealed strong enrichment of G4 signals in the promoter regions. By intersecting peaks called from biological replicates, we identified 4071 and 4174 G4 peaks in the brain and fat body, respectively (Q value = 1e-5) (Fig. 2D, Additional file 1: Fig. S2A). To examine the specificity of G4-CUT&Tag detection for G4s, we analysed the top 1000 peaks in these two tissues using the MEME suite [34] and found a predominant enrichment of G-rich sequences that were characteristic of G4s (Additional file 1: Table S1), although they were somewhat different (Fig. 2E), indicating the effectiveness of the G4-CUT&Tag detection method used. We also found that the G4 peaks closely overlapped with those of the PQSs, in contrast to their overlap with random sequences (Additional file 1: Fig. S2B-D). Genomic distribution annotation of all the G4 peaks identified in these two tissues revealed that the majority of the G4 peaks were localized to gene promoters in both tissues (Fig. 2F), indicating the potential role of these G4s as cis-regulatory elements in the modulation of gene expression.

Notably, a comparison of the G4 peak distribution between the brain and fat body genomes revealed a tissue-specific distribution. These two tissues had only ~ 40% shared genomic G4 peaks, whereas 60% of the genomic G4s were separated, although they had identical nucleotide sequences (Fig. 2G), and the overall distribution patterns were consistent, with promoter regions being the predominant sites of G4 localization (Fig. 2F). These findings suggest that G4 formation is dynamic and depends not only on the genomic sequence itself but also on tissue and cell physiological status.

Association of promoter G4s with increased gene expression

To determine the relationship between promoter G4s and gene transcription, we divided all the genes in a tissue into two groups, one with G4s in their promoter regions and the other without G4s in their promoter regions (Fig. 3A). Comparative analysis revealed that genes harbouring promoter G4s presented significantly higher transcription levels than those lacking promoter G4s in both brain and fat body tissues (Fig. 3B, C). Notably, the presence of G4s within 1 kb around the TSSs was positively correlated with higher gene expression, whereas this trend disappeared when G4s were more distant from the TSSs (Fig. 3B, C). This finding was further confirmed when we ranked genes by expression levels and G4-CUT&Tag signals near TSSs. To further examine the relationship between G4s and individual gene transcription, we compared the transcriptional and G4 profiles for the same genomic loci between the brain and fat body (Fig. 3E). The gene expression levels differed markedly between the two tissues, with 5763 genes upregulated and 1324 genes downregulated in the brain relative to the fat body (Additional file 2). Importantly, genes whose expression level was higher in one tissue tended to harbour folded G4 in their promoters, whereas when the same genes did not have a folded G4, their expression level was downregulated (Fig. 3F), which was also visualized in the heatmaps of RNA-seq and G4-CUT&Tag signals (Fig. 3G), where stronger G4 signals were observed near the TSSs of highly expressed genes. Using MAnorm2 software [35], we quantitatively identified differential G4 peaks in promoter regions, with 607 and 777 unique peaks in the brain and fat body, respectively (Fig. 3H). Notably, genes that were associated with tissue-specific G4 peaks presented higher transcription levels than those that did not contain tissue-specific G4s (Fig. 3H, I). Collectively, these results indicate a robust positive correlation between promoter G4s and elevated gene transcription in both tissues.

Association of G4s with active chromatin and tissue-specific gene expression

To investigate whether the chromatin context of G4s in various tissues differs and the relationship between the chromatin context of G4s and gene expression in differentiated tissues, we performed ATAC-seq to identify accessible chromatin regions and CUT&Tag to identify G4s in the brains of L5D3 larvae. The results revealed strong overlaps between the G4 signals and ATAC-seq signals, with a positive correlation in signal intensity (Fig. 4A). Further analysis revealed that 93% of the G4 peaks in promoter regions coincided with accessible chromatin regions (Fig. 4B, C). To further confirm the association of G4s with nucleosome-depleted regions (NDRs), we performed immunofluorescence to detect G4 foci in BmN cells treated with the Histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor entinostat, which stabilizes transcriptionally active chromatin [36]. Entinostat-treated cells exhibited a marked, dose-dependent increase in G4 foci compared to untreated control (Additional file 1: Fig. S3A, B). Transcriptome analysis revealed that compared with G4-free promoters, promoters that contained folded G4s consistently exhibited higher transcriptional activity, regardless of chromatin openness, in these two tissues (Fig. 4D). Comparative analysis of the promoter regions of the brain and fat body genomes indicated that the simultaneous presence of folded G4s and accessible chromatin corresponded to enhanced tissue-specific transcriptional activity (Fig. 4E, bars 3 and 4, 4 F) and that the transcription level was greater than that for the promoters with open chromatin alone (Fig. 4E, bars 1 and 2). Notably, for genes with open chromatin signals in both tissues, the presence of folded G4s further increased transcriptional activity (Fig. 4E, bars 5 and 6, 4G).

Chromatin accessibility in a DNA region is often manipulated by histone modification in that region [37]. For example, H3K9ac, H3K4me2, and H3K4me3 are believed to result in active transcription, whereas H3K9me3 results in inactive transcription [38]. To elucidate the relationships among G4s, chromatin accessibility, and histone modification, we compared the genomic G4 distribution and gene transcription levels in BmN cells with those in published histone modification datasets and found that G4s were substantially enriched in the promoters marked by H3K9ac, H3K4me2, and H3K4me3 (Additional file 1: Fig. S4). Metaplot analysis revealed that most G4s were located in nucleosome-free regions between two H3K4me3-marked sites (Additional file 1: Fig. S4). These results suggest that G4s are closely associated with active transcription regions where histones are modified by H3K4me3 and where chromatin is accessible. It is not yet clear whether G4 folding facilitates histone modification and chromatin accessibility or whether histone modification and chromatin accessibility facilitate G4 folding.

To verify whether G4s are responsible for tissue-specific functions, we performed pathway enrichment analysis of genes with unique folded G4s and high transcription levels in the brain and fat body (Fig. 4H). Markedly distinct pathways for these genes were identified between these two tissues. In the brain, highly expressed G4-containing genes, such as those encoding nicotine acetylcholine receptors 1/2 (nAChRa1/2), the GABA-gated chloride channel protein RDL1, and potassium ion transport-related proteins, were enriched primarily in signal transduction pathways, including neural signal transmission pathways. In addition, development-related transcription factors, such as Deformed (DFD), developing brain homeobox (DBX1), visual system homeobox 2 (VSX2) and BmSOX-14, also exhibited G4-associated transcriptional promotion. In contrast, in the fat body, the enriched pathways involved energy metabolism, nutrient metabolism and immune responses, including fatty acid synthases (FAS) and acyl-CoA desaturase (DESAT3) for lipid metabolism, phosphoserine aminotransferase 1 (PSAT1) and phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH), and β−1,3-glucan recognition proteins (βGRP1), which bind to β−1,3-glucan in the cell walls of gram-negative bacteria and fungi. These results imply that active metabolic pathways are different in the brain and fat body, which reflects functional differentiation in these two tissues or organs. More importantly, this functional differentiation appears to be related to G4 formation in the related genes. In other words, the formation of G4s in particular genes in a tissue may contribute to the high level of transcription of these genes, resulting in tissue-specific functional differentiation.

Involvement of G4s in tissue-specific physiological functions

The above results indicate that genomic G4s in promoter regions may affect the tissue-specific expression of genes. To further investigate whether changes in the G4 landscape can affect tissue function, we treated L5D3 larvae with pyridostatin (PDS), a small molecule known to selectively bind and stabilize G4s via π-π stacking interactions (Fig. 5A). By day 7 following the injection of PDS, all individual larvae in the control group had pupated, whereas less than 50% of the PDS-treated larvae had completed pupation (Fig. 5B, C). Insect metamorphosis is precisely regulated by the interplay between the nutritional and nervous systems [40, 41]. To determine whether PDS-induced G4 interference impairs tissue function, we dissected the fat body and conducted in vitro culture experiments (Fig. 5D). We observed that the lipid content significantly decreased after PDS treatment (Fig. 5E, F). Quantitative PCR detection of selected genes containing promoter G4s (Additional file 1: Fig. S5) revealed that the expression of Bm-30K-19g1-like, Apolipophorin III (which is involved in lipid synthesis and antibacterial function) [42, 43], and phosphoserine aminotransferase (PSAT1) (which is associated with amino acid metabolism) significantly decreased upon PDS treatment (Fig. 5G). These findings suggest that interference with G4s may lead to transcriptional suppression of metabolic genes, thereby causing abnormal energy synthesis in the fat body.

The insect brain undergoes continuous morphological remodelling during embryonic, larval, and pupal development [44]. In silkworms, one of the most distinctive developmental features is the dramatic differentiation and formation of optic lobe tissue during the pupal stage (Fig. 5H). Transcriptome and G4-CUT&Tag analyses revealed that the expression of several neurodevelopment-related transcription factors in the silkworm brain may be regulated by G4s (Fig. 4H). To further validate the functional role of G4s in nervous system development, we injected PDS into silkworm brains at the onset of the pupal stage (Fig. 5I). Compared with controls, PDS-treated individuals exhibited significantly reduced optic lobe volume (Fig. 5J, K). Moreover, the transcription levels of several optic lobe development-related transcription factor genes whose regulatory regions contained G4s markedly decreased (Fig. 5L, Additional file 1: Fig. S5B). Collectively, these results demonstrate that G4s act as novel regulatory elements in nervous system development and function in the brain.

Discussion

G4s are complex noncanonical DNA structures that can form under physiological conditions, particularly in the presence of monovalent cations such as K+, Na+, and NH4+ [45]. In vivo, G4 formation can be facilitated by transcription-induced negative supercoiling and molecular crowding [46, 47]; however, these G4 structures can be unwound by enzymes, such as helicases, allowing G4s to exist in a dynamic state [48, 49]. Live-cell imaging has confirmed the dynamic nature of G4s [50, 51], and recent advances in high-throughput methods such as ChIP-seq, CUT&Tag, and G4access have enabled genome-wide analyses of G4 distribution, location, and differences between various cell types of the same species [22–24]. However, most of these studies were performed with cell lines, and G4 profiling studies at the tissue, organ, and individual levels remain limited. Compared to cell lines, genomic analysis of G4s at the tissue and organ levels could better uncover the physiological functions of G4s in developmental and disease events.

In this study, we applied G4-CUT&Tag to directly profile genomic G4s in the brain and fat body, two structurally and functionally distinct tissues. By analysing the sequence composition of the detected G4 peaks, we found that most G4 peaks in silkworm contain only two G-tetrads, whereas peaks harbouring three canonical G-tetrads (G3+N1–12G3+N1–12G3+N1–12G3+) account for only a small fraction (~ 7%) (Additional file 1: Fig. S2B). This proportion is much lower than that observed in humans, where approximately half of the identified G4 peaks contain three canonical G-tetrad PQSs [52]. Similar observations have been reported in other lower organisms, including Drosophila and bacteria, where G4s with two G-tetrads appear to predominate [23]. These findings suggest that in lower organisms, G4s may deviate from the canonical three-tetrad configuration and instead primarily adopt two-tetrad conformations.

To better understand the intrinsic relationship between G4s and gene expression, we compared G4 formation at the same genomic positions in two distinct tissues under physiological conditions using G4-CUT&Tag in combination with RNA-seq. In this case, the same genomic positions are assumed to have identical nucleotide sequences, and the only difference is the alteration of the physiological states of the cells, which may result in G4 folding or unfolding at these positions. We demonstrate a positive correlation between the presence of G4 in promoter regions and high gene transcriptional activity, similar to that observed in human cells [28]. We also demonstrate that although these two tissues shared the same genome, not all the PQSs formed G4s, even under the same physiological conditions (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, genes that contain PQSs in their promoters and fold into G4s in a tissue appear to have higher transcription levels in that tissue (Fig. 3F); otherwise, their transcription level was low (Fig. 3F).

One interesting question is how G4 formation facilitates gene transcription. G4s have been reported to be functionally associated with various chromatin features, such as histone modifications [26], R-loops [53], and liquid‒liquid phase separation [54]. Notably, G4s identified through G4 ChIP-seq [22], G4-CUT&Tag [24], and G4access [23] have been found to substantially overlap with open chromatin detected by ATAC-seq, indicating their preferential localization within NDRs. NDRs are accessible DNA fragments that act as key hubs for transcription-related protein binding and are considered crucial for regulating high-level gene expression [55]. G4s are believed to either directly interact with transcription factors to promote gene transcription [21] or bind to epigenetic remodelling factors, which then modify nearby histones and alter chromatin structure [19, 56]. Recent work has further demonstrated that G4s can function as intrinsic nucleosome exclusion elements, promoting chromatin opening and facilitating RNA polymerase II pause-release at promoters [57]. We explored the relationship between G4s and chromatin accessibility in the brain and fat body of silkworms. The results revealed that most G4s were located in the open chromatin regions of the genomes, where gene transcription was active (Fig. 4A, D). For the same genomic regions, if the chromatin in that region is open in a tissue, the genes in that region are actively transcribed in the tissue (Fig. 4E). Otherwise, if the chromatin in that region is closed in the other tissue, the genes are inactive in that tissue (Fig. 4E). These results indicate that there is a close relationship between G4 formation and chromatin accessibility, although we still do not know whether G4 formation causes chromatin opening or whether chromatin opening facilitates G4 formation. Furthermore, such an association of G4 formation with chromatin accessibility in a genomic region is tissue specific. When G4 formation and chromatin accessibility are available in a particular region of the genome in a tissue, the genes in this region are actively transcribed (Fig. 4D, E). In other words, G4 formation and chromatin accessibility in a certain region are closely associated with the tissue-specific transcription of the genes in this region. Recent studies have discussed a potential bias of G4-CUT&Tag toward accessible chromatin. Thompson and Byrd suggested that untargeted CUT&Tag reads are enriched in open chromatin and may contribute to signal enrichment at G4 loci, thereby complicating the identification of real G4 sites [58]. In contrast, Melidis and colleagues argued that this confounding effect may be overestimated because untargeted libraries are typically low-yield and poorly reproducible, making direct normalization between targeted and untargeted libraries problematic [59]. In our study, G4 signals were also detectable in closed chromatin regions, although at much lower abundance than in open chromatin (Fig. 4B, D). This pattern is also consistent with previous G4 ChIP-seq studies [26, 52, 60], which are not based on Tn5-mediated accessibility profiling, suggesting that the higher abundance of G4s in accessible chromatin is likely a genuine biological feature rather than a technical artefact of G4-CUT&Tag. These observations suggest that chromatin accessibility may influence the sensitivity of G4-CUT&Tag, but the method is not restricted to accessible chromatin. Whether these G4s form within closed chromatin regions or persist following chromatin remodelling requires further investigation. It is important to note that the chromatin features and regulatory effects associated with G4-containing regions may not be attributable exclusively to G4s. Other non-canonical nucleic acid structures, such as i-motifs and R-loops, may also occur in these regions and could contribute, at least in part, to the observed signals and regulatory patterns [61, 62]. Moreover, increasing evidence suggests that these structures can interact with one another and may function in a coordinated manner within chromatin [61, 63]. Therefore, future studies will require a more integrative framework to dissect the individual and combined contributions of different nucleic acid structures to chromatin organization and gene regulation.

The contribution of G4 formation to cell-specific functions has been reported. During the differentiation of human embryonic stem cells into neural stem cells, dynamic changes in G4s are associated with the pluripotency of embryonic stem cells and the establishment of neural stem cell identity [26]. In this study, we found that at the genomic level, G4 formation is closely associated with gene transcription in a tissue-specific manner. It is reasonable to hypothesize that G4 may contribute to tissue-specific functional differentiation by regulating gene transcription. The results support our hypothesis. GO functional analysis revealed that in the fat body of silkworms, these highly expressed G4-associated genes were enriched in energy metabolism and immune functions (Fig. 4H), which is consistent with the functions of the fat body. Similarly, in the brain, brain function-related pathways and genes were enriched in the highly expressed G4-associated genes, for example, transcription factors for neural signalling and neuron development (Fig. 4H). Thus, G4 formation in the genome of a tissue is closely associated with the functional differentiation of the tissue through the activation of tissue-specific transcription of certain genes. In addition, functional perturbation using the G4-stabilizing ligand PDS, which blocks the structures and function of G4s [33], provided additional evidence for the involvement of G4 in tissue-specific functions. PDS injection into the brain significantly inhibited the development of the optic lobe, and PDS injection into the fat body impaired energy metabolism and induced premature pupation. These phenotypes underscore the relevance of genomic G4s in the regulation of physiological function and developmental processes. However, we acknowledge that our interpretation of the PDS-based experiments has certain limitations, given the broad-spectrum binding of PDS to G4 structures and its potential cytotoxic effects. In the future, more precise insights could be obtained through complementary approaches, such as genetic manipulation of G4 helicases [23], CRISPR-based structure-specific mutagenesis of G4-forming sequences [64], or perturbation of G4-associated chromatin contexts [28].

Conclusions

We propose a mechanistic model for the association of G4 formation with tissue-specific gene expression and function. In tissue A, specific genomic PQSs fold into G4s within open chromatin regions, which may facilitate the recruitment of transcription factors (TFs) and RNA polymerase II (Pol II), thereby activating transcription of the genes in these regions and resulting in the specific development and function of that tissue (Fig. 6). In contrast, the same PQS in tissue B fails to form a stable G4 and is associated with lower transcriptional activity. Conversely, G4s may form at alternative loci of the genome in tissue B to activate a distinct set of genes involved in tissue B-specific functions (Fig. 6). This model suggests a novel role for genomic G4 formation may contribute to tissue-specific gene transcription and functional differentiation. Methods

Experimental insects and treatments

The B. mori strain P50 was obtained from the Research and Development Center of the Sericultural Research Institute, Academy of Agricultural Sciences of Guangdong Province, China. Larvae were reared on fresh mulberry leaves under 14:10 h light/dark cycles at 26 °C. PDS (HY-15176A, MCE, Shanghai, China) was dissolved in sterile deionized distilled water (ddH2O) to prepare a 30 mM stock solution. For PDS injection, 5 µL of 30 mM PDS was injected into the lateral thorax between the second and third segments of 3-day-old fifth-instar larvae (L5D3). An equal volume of ddH2O was used as control injection. Additionally, 5 µL of 30 mM PDS was injected into the heads of the pupae immediately after moulting. The brains were subsequently isolated for analysis.

Cell culture and treatment

The B. mori BmN cell line, originally derived from ovarian tissues, was maintained at 28 °C in Grace’s medium (Gibco, New York, USA) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Scientific, MA, USA). Entinostat (HY-12163, MCE, Shanghai, China) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). BmN cells were seeded on glass coverslips and cultured overnight at 28 °C, followed by treatment with 10 or 20 µM entinostat or an equal volume of DMSO as a control for 48 h. All the treatments were repeated three times.

In vitro fat body dissection and culture

Fat body tissues were dissected from L5D3 B. mori larvae. The tissue fragments were cultured in 24-well plates containing 1 mL of Grace’s medium (Gibco, New York, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Thermo Scientific, MA, USA). After 24 h of incubation at 28 °C, the tissues were treated with 10 μM PDS or an equal volume of ddH2O for an additional 24 h. All the treatments were repeated three times.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT‒PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using the FastPure Total RNA Isolation Kit (RC112-01; Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Silkworm fat body or brain tissues were immersed in 500 µL of Buffer RL and homogenized thoroughly. The homogenates were then transferred to FastPure gDNA-Filter Columns III and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 1 min. Absolute ethanol was added to the filtrate, mixed thoroughly and transferred to FastPure RNA Columns III. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 1 min, the filtrate was discarded. The columns were subsequently washed with 700 µL of Buffer RW1 and 500 µL of Buffer RW2, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 1 min. The RNA-binding columns were then transferred to RNase-free 1.5 mL collection tubes. To elute the RNA, 50 µL of RNase-free ddH2O was added to the centre of the membrane without touching it, which was incubated at room temperature for 1 min and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 1 min. Reverse transcription was conducted using HiScript IV All-in-One Ultra RT SuperMix for qPCR (R433, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The mRNA expression levels of target genes were normalized to those of the reference gene RP49, and relative transcription levels were calculated using the 2−∆∆Ct method [65]. Three replicates were performed for each gene. The sequences of the primers used for qRT‒PCR are listed in Additional file 1: Table S2.

Immunostaining

Immunofluorescence staining of G4s was performed as previously described [8]. Briefly, brains and fat body tissues from 5th-instar larvae were dissected in ice-cold PBS, transferred into 500 µL Eppendorf tubes and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 2 h at room temperature with gentle agitation. After fixation, the tissues were washed three times in PBST (PBS containing 0.5% Triton X-100) for 20 min each at room temperature. After being rinsed with PBST, the tissues were blocked for 1 h at 37 °C in blocking buffer (3% bovine serum albumin in PBS). The samples were then incubated with a BG4-Flag antibody (MABE917; Sigma‒Aldrich, MO, USA) at a 1:100 dilution in blocking solution for 1 h at 37 °C, followed by three 5 min washes with PBST. Afterwards, the tissues were incubated with a mouse anti-Flag antibody (F3165; Merck, Darmstadt, GER) at a 1:800 dilution for 1 h at 37 °C, followed by three 5 min washes with PBST. Next, the samples were incubated with an Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated secondary antibody (A-11005; Thermo Fisher, MA, USA) at a 1:500 dilution for 45 min at RT. After three additional washes with PBST (5 min each), the second wash contained 2.5 µg/mL DAPI (HY-D1738, MCE, Shanghai, China), and coverslips were mounted using RapiClear1.47 solution (RC147001, SunJin Lab, Hsinchu, Chinese Taiwan). For coimmunostaining with SYT1, SYT1 antibody (14511–1-AP; Proteintech, Wuhan, China) was co-incubated with BG4-Flag antibody. An Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (A-11008; Thermo Fisher, MA, USA) at a 1:500 dilution was used to detect SYT1.

For single-cell immunofluorescence, larval brains were carefully dissected using fine forceps and transferred to low DNA-binding tubes containing 250 µL of ice-cold, RNase-free PBS. The samples were subsequently centrifuged at 800 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was removed and replaced with 200 µL of 1 mg/mL collagenase (C2674; Sigma‒Aldrich, MO, USA). Digestion was carried out for 1 h at 25 °C with gentle agitation, and the suspension was pipetted every 10 min to facilitate dissociation. The enzymatic reaction was stopped by the addition of 1 mL of PBS supplemented with 0.04% BSA. The cells were subsequently washed and filtered through a 40 µm Flowmi cell strainer (BS-40-XBS; Biosharp, Anhui, China) and then centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The cell pellet was resuspended in 100 µL of Grace’s medium and seeded onto poly-L-lysine-coated coverslips (YALy14; Acmec, Shanghai, China), followed by a 30-min incubation to allow cell adhesion. The medium was subsequently removed, and immunostaining was performed as described above, with modifications: the duration of fixation was shortened to 10 min, and the duration of PBST washes was shortened to 5 min each. Coverslips were mounted with ProLong™ Diamond Antifade Mountant (P36970; Thermo Fisher, MA, USA).

Fluorescence images were acquired using an Olympus Fluoview FV1000 confocal microscope. Z-stack images were obtained using a pinhole size of 0.6 Airy units, and identical acquisition parameters were applied to all samples for comparison. Quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software. For the correlation analysis of single-cell immunofluorescence signals (BG4 vs SYT1), 50 cells from two biological replicates were analyzed. For quantification of BG4 immunofluorescence signals in BmN cells, 100–200 nuclei from three biological replicates per condition were analysed. The data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was assessed using Student’s t test in GraphPad Prism. The fluorescence intensity values were normalized to the nuclear area.

BODIPY staining

To detect lipid droplet accumulation in the fat body, in vitro-cultured fat body tissues were washed three times with PBS and then incubated with 10 µM BODIPY (HY-W090090; MCE, Shanghai, China) in DMSO and 2.5 µg/mL DAPI for 10 min at room temperature. After staining, the tissues were washed three times with PBS, mounted on glass slides and covered with coverslips for microscopic observation. Imaging was performed using a Nikon Eclipse Ni DIC microscope (model YTV55).

Cleavage of tissue cell nuclei under targets and tagmentation (CUT&Tag)

Fat body (20 mg) or 20 larval brains were dissected in ice-cold PBS and transferred into 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes. The samples were subsequently centrifuged at 800 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. After the supernatant was removed, 1 mL of nuclear lysis buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.4), 10 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl₂, 0.1% NP40, 0.1% Tween 20, and 0.01% digitonin) was added to resuspend the tissue. The samples were then homogenized using a Dounce homogenizer with 10 strokes each using a pestle A and a pestle B. The nuclear suspension was filtered through a 40 µm Flowmi cell strainer, followed by centrifugation at 800 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The resulting pellet was resuspended in wash buffer. Nuclear integrity was assessed by methylene blue staining, and nucleus concentration was determined using a haemocytometer (BS-QT-1103; Biosharp, Anhui, China) under a Nikon Eclipse Ni DIC microscope (model YTV55).

G4-CUT&Tag was performed based on a previous protocol [20], with minor modifications according to the Hyperactive Universal CUT&Tag Assay Kit protocol (TD903, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Briefly, 100,000 nuclei were immobilized on conA beads by rotation at room temperature for 10 min. The bead-bound nuclei were incubated overnight at 4 °C in 50 µL of primary antibody buffer (containing 0.05% digitonin) supplemented with a 1:50 dilution of BG4-Flag or normal mouse IgG (12–371; Sigma‒Aldrich, MO, USA). On the following day, the primary antibody buffer was removed, and the nuclei were washed three times with 200 µL of dig-wash buffer (containing 0.05% digitonin). The samples were then incubated for 1 h at room temperature with slow rotation in 50 µL of wash buffer containing a 1:100 dilution of mouse anti-FLAG antibody (F3165; Merck, Darmstadt, GER). After being washed three times with 200 µL of dig-wash buffer, the samples were further incubated for 30 min at room temperature with a 1:100 dilution of donkey anti-mouse secondary antibody (TD903, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) in 100 µL of wash buffer. After an additional three washes, the samples were resuspended in 100 µL of dig-300 buffer containing 0.04 µM pA-Tn5 adapter complex and incubated for 1 h at RT with slow rotation. The nuclei were then washed three times with 500 µL of dig-300 buffer. Genome fragmentation was induced by resuspending the samples in 50 µL of 1 × TTBL buffer diluted in dig-300 buffer and incubating them at 37 °C at 800 rpm in a ThermoMixer Comfort. DNA was extracted and purified using magnetic DNA extraction beads in 100 µL of Buffer L/B containing 5 µL of proteinase K and incubated at 55 °C for 1 h. The purified DNA was amplified using the i5 and i7 indexing primers according to the CAM protocol. Libraries were cleaned using VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (#N411, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and sequenced on a NovaSeq X platform, generating 150 bp paired-end reads.

Assay for transposase-accessible chromatin with high-throughput sequencing (ATAC-seq)

ATAC-seq was performed as previously described [20], with minor modifications according to the High-Sensitivity Open Chromatin Profile Kit 2.0 protocol (N248; Novoprotein, Shanghai, China). A total of 50,000 nuclei, isolated during the CUT&Tag procedure, were resuspended in 1 mL of cold wash buffer and centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The nuclear pellet was resuspended in 40 µL of fragmentation buffer (0.3 × PBS, 0.1% Tween-20, 0.01% digitonin, 1 × TD buffer, 1 × pA-Tn5 transposome mix) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. The reaction was stopped by the addition of 10 µL of stop buffer, followed by incubation at 55 °C for 5 min. DNA was then purified using 100 µL of Tagment DNA Extraction Beads and eluted in 37 µL of elution buffer. The purified DNA was amplified with 5 × AmpliMix using the N5 and N7 index primers. The purified DNA libraries were size selected using NovoNGS DNA Clean Beads (N240; Novoprotein, Shanghai, China) and sequenced on the NovaSeq X platform.

RNA-seq processing

Total RNA was extracted using a TRIzol reagent kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA quality was assessed on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA) and checked using RNase-free agarose gel electrophoresis. After total RNA was extracted, eukaryotic mRNA was enriched by oligo (dT) beads. Afterwards, the enriched mRNA was fragmented into short fragments using fragmentation buffer and reverse transcribed into cDNA by using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB #7530; New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). The purified double-stranded cDNA fragments were end repaired, a base was added, and the fragments were ligated to Illumina sequencing adapters. The ligation reaction was purified with AMPure XP Beads (1.0 ×). Ligated fragments were subjected to size selection by agarose gel electrophoresis and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification. Library sequencing was performed by Berry Genomics (Beijing, China). Each sample was analysed in biological triplicates.

Putative G4 prediction

Putative G4 sequences (PQSs) were computationally predicted across the whole genome using pqsfinder [66] and G4Hunter software [67], pqsfinder with parameters specifying the number of guanines in each G-tract, the loop lengths between G-tracts, and the allowed structural imperfections (defects). For example, the parameters run_min_len = 3, loop_min_len = 1, loop_max_len = 12, max_defects = 0, and min_score = 1 were used to identify canonical PQSs with the general motif G3+N1–12G3+N1–12G3+N1–12G3+ [68, 69]. G4Hunter were performed with a window size of 25 bp (default value) and a relatively conservative prediction threshold score of 1.2 and 1.6.

Data analysis

CUT&Tag reads were aligned to the B. mori genome using Bowtie2 [70]. The aligned reads were removed using Picard MarkDuplicates, and the deduplicated BAM files were normalized using the bins per million mapped reads (BPM) to generate bigwig files with the bamCoverage command in deepTools 3.3.0 [71]. Peaks were called with MACS2 (v2.2.6) [72]. Sequences from peak regions were extracted for G4 motif analysis using MEME-ChIP [34], with a minimum motif width of 15 bp and a maximum width of 30 bp. The top significantly enriched motifs with the lowest E values are reported in the text. For the intersection of peaks, the bedtools intersect function was employed. HOMER’s annotatePeaks.pl script was applied to associate peaks with nearby genes and determine their genomic annotation [73]. By default, promoter regions were defined as − 1000 nt to + 100 nt relative to the transcription start site (TSS). Quantitative comparisons of CUT&Tag signals were performed using MAnorm, with differential peaks defined by a P value < 0.05 and an |M| value > 1 [74].

ATAC-seq reads were aligned to the B. mori genome using Bowtie2 and deduplicated using Picard MarkDuplicates. BAM files were normalized using the BPM to generate bigwig files with the bamCoverage command in deepTools 3.3.0. Genomic annotations and peak-to-gene associations were determined using Homer annotatePeaks.pl [73]. RNA-seq reads were aligned to the B. mori genome using HISAT2 [75]. A count table file indicating the number of reads per gene in each sample was generated using FeatureCounts [76]. The number of transcripts per million (TPM) of each gene was subsequently calculated on the basis of the gene expression unit, which was normalized for sequencing depth. Differentially expressed genes were identified using DESeq2 [77], and genes with a P value < 0.05 and a |log2FC| value > 1 were considered differentially expressed.
