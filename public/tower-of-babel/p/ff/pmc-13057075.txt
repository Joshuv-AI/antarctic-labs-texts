Explainable and secure federated learning for privacy-enhancing skin cancer classification using a lightweight multi-scale CNN
Authors: Abdullah Siddique Mohammad Sayeed, Shaikh Afnan Birahim, Md Shafiq Ullah, Yamina Islam, Avijit Paul, Mohammad Asif Hasan, Mominul Ahsan, Marcin Kowalski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Skin diseases pose a major public health challenge, with skin cancer being the most prevalent malignancy. Early detection is critical for improving patient outcomes, yet traditional diagnostic methods rely on expert evaluation, which is limited by clinician variability, image quality, and accessibility constraints. Deep learning (DL)-based models offer automated diagnostic capabilities but require large-scale centralized data aggregation, which conflicts with privacy regulations and poses security risks. Federated Learning (FL) enables collaborative training across multiple hospitals without sharing raw patient data, yet it introduces communication overhead, security vulnerabilities, and interpretability challenges, hindering its real-world deployment. To address these issues, an Encrypted FedAvg-Based Explainable Federated Learning approach has been proposed utilizing a Lightweight Deep Learning Multi-Scale Convolutional Neural Network (LWMS-CNN) for efficient, privacy-enhanced, and interpretable skin cancer diagnosis. The proposed method integrates Homomorphic Encryption (HE) in a simulated federated system for secure model aggregation, privacy enhancements while maintaining strong diagnostic performance. Additionally, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and GradCAM enhance interpretability, enabling clinicians to understand AI-driven predictions. Experimental evaluations on the HAM10000 dataset demonstrate that the proposed LWMS-CNN-FL model achieves 98.62% accuracy, with only a 0.3% tradeoff when encryption is applied, ensuring robust security without compromising diagnostic reliability. The model’s generalization ability is further confirmed through assessments on additional benchmark datasets, including ISIC 2019, where it achieved an accuracy of 96.22%, and PAD-UFES-20, where it reached 89.84%. By integrating lightweight deep learning, federated learning, encryption, and explainability, this study presents a scalable, privacy-enhanced, computationally efficient, and clinically interpretable AI-driven solution for secure and accurate early skin cancer detection in world healthcare applications.

Introduction

Skin diseases represent a major public health challenge and constitute the fourth leading cause of nonfatal disease burden worldwide1. In the United States alone, approximately 84.5 million people (one in four) are affected by skin diseases, imposing an estimated $75 billion in annual cost on the healthcare system. Among these conditions, skin cancer stands out as the most common malignancy: current estimates indicate that approximately one in five Americans will develop some form of skin cancer in their lifetime. This high prevalence translates into a substantial financial burden, with recent analyses showing that U.S. skin cancer treatment costs reach $8–9 billion per year2. These statistics underscore the urgent need for effective strategies for the early detection and management of skin diseases, especially skin cancers.

Traditionally, visual inspection, enhanced by dermoscopy, has been the primary method for diagnosing skin cancer3,4. However, this method’s accuracy is compromised by variability in image quality, clinician expertise, and subjective judgment. Low-resolution images and subtle early-stage cancer indicators further complicate detection, emphasizing the need for automated and objective approaches. Machine learning (ML) methods, particularly tree-based algorithms, have been explored for automating skin cancer classification5. However, these approaches rely heavily on manual preprocessing steps, such as contrast enhancement and feature extraction, introducing variability and limiting scalability. Deep learning (DL), specifically convolutional neural networks (CNNs), address these limitations by learning directly from raw images without extensive preprocessing. However, many deep learning models, such as ResNet50, while achieving high classification accuracy, have a significantly large number of parameters (23.8 M parameters)6. In Federated Learning (FL) environments, where training occurs across distributed hospital networks, transferring such large models introduces substantial communication overhead, increasing bandwidth usage, latency, and synchronization costs7. In resource-constrained hospitals with limited computational power and unstable networks, frequent synchronization of heavy models slows down training, increases operational costs and strains available infrastructure, making a lightweight model essential for practical deployment. Beyond computational challenges, data privacy regulations (e.g., HIPAA) restrict the centralized sharing of sensitive patient data, making FL a promising solution8. In FL, hospitals train models locally and share only aggregated updates rather than raw data, effectively “sharing knowledge, not data”9. However, FL alone does not eliminate privacy risks, as recent studies revealed vulnerabilities to gradient reconstruction, model inversion, and membership inference attacks, which could expose sensitive patient information8. Addressing these risks necessitates robust aggregation and encryption mechanisms to ensure both security and efficiency in medical AI applications.

To mitigate these risks, cryptographic and privacy enhancing techniques, including secure aggregation protocols based on homomorphic encryption (HE), have been integrated into the simulated FL workflow. HE enables aggregation of encrypted model updates, preventing the exposure of individual institutions’ contributions10. Securing encryption-based communication thus allows hospitals to leverage federated learning while maintaining strict data privacy.

While privacy is essential, ensuring the interpretability of AI models is equally critical for clinical adoption. Complex deep learning models often operate as “black boxes,” limiting clinician trust and regulatory compliance11. To address this, explainability techniques such as SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) and Grad-CAM (Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping) have gained prominence. SHAP quantifies individual feature contributions to model predictions, providing intuitive and transparent explanations12. On the other hand, Grad-CAM offers visual interpretability by highlighting the spatial regions in an image that most influences the model’s decision. When used together, SHAP and Grad-CAM provide complementary forms of insight, with SHAP emphasizing numerical relevance and Grad-CAM focusing on spatial attention. This combined interpretability framework enhances clinical understanding of AI predictions, supports informed discussions between healthcare providers and patients, and ultimately promotes greater trust, transparency, and integration of AI in medical decision-making.

To date, most federated learning implementations in healthcare focus on privacy and performance, whereas issues of secure communication and explanatory transparency have largely been addressed separately. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to introduce a federated learning framework for cancer diagnosis that ensures secure communication between stakeholders and simultaneously integrates explainability into the model’s decisions. Literature review

Over the years, researchers have presented various strategies to detect skin cancer through advanced DL and ML models. Mavaddati et al.13 introduced a hybrid deep learning approach that integrates ResNet50 and LSTM to enhance skin cancer classification accuracy. They utilized the HAM10000 dataset comprising 10,015 dermatoscopic images from the Medical University of Vienna and a dermatology clinic in Queensland, Australia which was split into 70% training and 30% testing and evaluated using a 5-fold cross-validation scheme. This framework employs ResNet50 for feature extraction and LSTM to capture sequential dependencies, leveraging transfer learning to improve generalization. When a 99.09% accuracy rate is achieved, the model outperforms the baseline methods such as the MLP, CNN, and standalone ResNet50. While advantages include high accuracy and robust generalization, the approach’s increased computational demands and the hurdles involved in clinical deployment remain notable limitations.

Pandey et al.14 introduced a hybrid transfer learning model by integrating ResNet152V2, VGG19, and MobileNetV3Large to increase accuracy in skin cancer diagnosis. The HAM10000 dataset was used, with a 10-fold cross-validation strategy. Their model leverages convolutional layers, pooling layers, and pre-trained models to extract robust features. The training accuracy reached 99.53%, while the validation accuracy reached 97.52%. The model effectively reduces false positives and enhances diagnostic efficiency, but it depends on high-quality datasets and may face generalization issues across diverse skin types. Alotaibi et al.15 developed a skin cancer classification framework based on the Xception architecture, which is enhanced by self-attention, hard attention, and soft attention techniques. They trained their model via the HAM10000 dataset with an 80–20% training–testing split and applied 10-fold cross-validation. Their proposed Xception model variations outperformed baseline models such as EfficientNetV2-M and DenseNet-169, with the highest accuracy reaching 94.11%. Strengths include improved detection accuracy and computational efficiency, whereas limitations involve potential overfitting owing to the dataset’s limited size.

Djekoun et al.16 applied deep learning and transfer learning for classifying skin lesions, using models such as VGG16, VGG19, ResNet50, and InceptionV3. The HAM10000 dataset was used, with training and evaluation subsets generated via random oversampling. They utilized data augmentation and ImageNet pre-trained weights, achieving a validation accuracy of 96.67%. Advantages include the effective use of transfer learning to enhance classification and reduce training resources. However, the approach remains dependent on large amounts of training data and may struggle with real-time clinical deployment. Attallah et al.17 proposed Skin-CAD, an explainable AI-based deep learning classification model that utilizes Inception, Xception, ResNet-50, and ResNet-101 for feature extraction. They used both the HAM10000 and Kaggle’s Skin Cancer: Malignant vs. Benign datasets, with a 70%-30% training-testing split. Their model employed PCA and feature selection techniques such as MRMR and Relief-F to enhance classification. The highest accuracy achieved was 97.2% on one dataset and 96.5% on the other dataset. While dual-layer feature extraction improves accuracy, biases may arise from imbalanced datasets, and performance may vary across diverse skin tones.

Al-Rasheed et al.18 proposed an ensemble transfer learning strategy combining VGG16, ResNet50, and ResNet101, augmented with Conditional Generative Adversarial Networks (CGANs) for data expansion. The HAM10000 dataset was employed and partitioned into 70% training, 10% validation, and 20% testing. Their ensemble model achieved a 90% accuracy rate, surpassing the performance of the individual models. While this approach effectively addresses data imbalance and enhances classification accuracy, generating highly realistic synthetic images through CGANs remains a significant challenge.

Pacal et al.19 developed a deep learning model based on the CAFormer architecture, incorporating focal self-attention mechanisms. They used both the ISIC 2019 and HAM10000 datasets, which were split into 70% training, 20% validation, and 10% testing. Their model outperformed ten CNNs and twenty state-of-the-art ViT models, with accuracies reaching 92.54% on ISIC 2019 and 95.01% on HAM10000. The strengths of these methods include robust generalization and effectiveness in identifying tumor regions, but their performance may decline for classes with fewer samples. Hu et al.20 presented a Federated Learning (FL) approach for skin cancer detection built around the FedAvg algorithm. Their method employed a DenseNet201-based model trained on the ISIC Skin Cancer dataset and used rotation, zooming, and flipping for data augmentation. Local models were trained on equally partitioned data subsets and then iteratively aggregated to refine the overall model performance. The model with FedAvg improved the training speed but showed a slight decrease in accuracy (from 98% to 81%). The primary advantage is data privacy measures, though communication overhead and network stability remain challenges.

Li et al.21 introduced a CNN-based Federated Learning framework for skin cancer diagnosis using the HAM10000 dataset. Their 21-layer model employed techniques such as Federated Averaging, SMOTE for class imbalance, and KBest feature selection. The highest test accuracy achieved was 98.16%. This approach maintains privacy by keeping data decentralized but suffers from communication latency and lower accuracy than centralized models do. Nam et al.22 implemented a federated learning system with CNNs for classifying skin diseases using the HAM10000 dataset. They addressed class imbalance through SMOTE and achieved an accuracy increase from 79% to 95% after data balancing. Their approach enhances privacy and scalability but requires high-quality data and stable network communication for optimal performance.

Fan et al.23 proposed a Personalized Multimodal Federated Learning (PMM-FL) framework for skin cancer diagnosis that addresses heterogeneous and missing modalities using frequency-adaptive aggregation, attention-based fusion, and modality imputation, achieving 92.32% accuracy on a custom ISIC (2018–2024) dataset with only a 2% drop under 30% missing data while reducing communication overhead by 32.9%, albeit with increased system complexity. Naz et al.24 presented a privacy-preserving and explainable federated learning approach for benign and malignant skin disorder classification using a VGG19 model trained with FedAvg across three clients, integrating Grad-CAM for interpretability and demonstrating strong performance, though scalability and communication efficiency remain challenges.

Several studies beyond skin cancer classification have shown strong performance in broader healthcare tasks. Munir et al.25 proposed PneuX Net, an ensemble feature extraction framework combining Random Forest, Gaussian Naïve Bayes, and K Nearest Centroid classifiers to capture nonlinear patterns and improve class separability, achieving 99.91% accuracy on pneumonia chest X ray images. In a related direction, Tanveer et al.26 explored hybrid diagnostic frameworks for automated brain stroke detection that use pretrained CNNs such as VGG16 for feature extraction, followed by classical machine learning classifiers and dimensionality reduction, reporting accuracies of up to 99.96% and demonstrating the effectiveness of hybrid deep learning–based medical diagnosis.

Although numerous studies have been conducted on skin cancer classification, models that achieve both state-of-the-art accuracy and minimal model complexity are lacking, making real-world deployment challenging, especially in resource-constrained environments27, or that focus on lightweight design but compromise classification performance28. Furthermore, data security in AI-driven healthcare remains a critical concern, as medical data are naturally distributed across multiple institutions, each containing diverse patient demographics and clinical cases29. Machine learning models require access to diverse data to improve generalization across different use cases; however, centralized training approaches pose significant privacy risks and regulatory challenges, making data sharing impractical. Federated Learning (FL) offers a promising solution by enabling decentralized training without sharing raw data30; however, FL itself is susceptible to privacy attacks, such as model inversion and gradient leakage, which could expose confidential medical information31. To ensure robust privacy protection, integrating Homomorphic Encryption (HE) within FL is crucial, as HE enables secure model aggregation by allowing computations on encrypted data without decryption, significantly reducing the risk of data leakage32.

Methodology

This section offers detailed exploration of the datasets employed in this study, as well as the different strategies utilized to develop an accurate model for skin cancer detection and to implement a simulated federated learning system. It includes details on the dataset selection and preprocessing methods. Additionally, this section describes the design of the proposed LWMS-CNN model, the federated learning architecture, aggregation techniques, Homomorphic Encryption (HE), the integration of XAI, and the evaluation metrics used. The entire workflow is depicted in Fig. 1. The workflow comprises several critical stages, beginning with the server initializing the proposed LWMS-CNN model and distributing patient image data to multiple client devices. Each client independently trains the model on its local dataset using the Federated Averaging (FedAvg) algorithm, while maintaining data confidentiality through homomorphic encryption. The encrypted local model updates are then transmitted to the central server, where they are securely aggregated to update the global model. Following convergence of the training process, SHAP-based explainability techniques are employed in the post-training phase to provide interpretable insights into model predictions. This comprehensive framework facilitates secure, efficient, and transparent skin cancer classification in decentralized healthcare settings.

Dataset description

Research has focused on one of the well-known datasets in the field of skin cancer, the HAM10000 dataset. These multiclass datasets have been widely used for training and evaluating ML and DL models for skin cancer detection. HAM10000 is a widely used and well-structured dataset among researchers working on skin cancer. It contains 10,015 labeled images of seven distinct classes. In addition to the dermoscopic images, the dataset al.so includes rich metadata about patients, such as age, sex, and lesion position. The dataset consists of 327 images of Actinic Keratoses and Intraepithelial Carcinoma (akiec), 514 images of Basal Cell Carcinoma (bcc), 1,099 images of Benign Keratosis-like Lesions (bkl), 115 images of Dermatofibroma (df), and 1,113 images of Melanoma (mel), 6,705 images of Melanocytic Nevi (nv), and 142 images of Vascular skin lesions (vasc)34. Figure 2 shows one image of each class from the dataset. The images are captured in full color using three RGB channels, and the dataset exhibits a significant imbalance: the NV class contains approximately 58 times more samples than the DF class does, which is the smallest.

Dataset preprocessing & splitting

Data Standardization

Standardization, which is also called z score normalization, is a data preprocessing method that adjusts feature values so that their mean becomes zero and their standard deviation equals one. This method is particularly beneficial when dealing with data that have varying scales, as it reduces the risk of certain features dominating others, thereby enhancing the performance of many algorithms35. where z refers to the standardized value, x is the initial feature value, µ is the initial feature mean, and σ is the feature standard deviation. Standardization brings all features to a uniform scale, preventing any single feature from overpowering the learning process. This ensures a balanced input for the model, improving its learning efficiency. Moreover, standardization enhances the stability and speed of optimization algorithms, leading to better model accuracy and performance. Data splitting

The dataset was split into two parts, training and testing, following the widely used 80:20 ratios. The training set, comprising 80% of the data, was used to train the model, allowing it to learn patterns and features. The remaining 20% was reserved as the testing set, ensuring that the model was evaluated on unseen data. This separation is crucial for assessing the model’s generalization ability, as all the performance metrics and validation processes were conducted using the testing data, providing an unbiased estimate of the model’s effectiveness.

Dataset oversampling

The datasets used in this research, HAM10000, exhibit significant class imbalance, which can lead to bias toward the majority class if not properly addressed. To address this imbalance, RandomOverSampler was used to create a more balanced dataset. This technique oversamples the minority class with replacement, adding instances until all classes reach an equal number of samples36. The technique was used after splitting the data to ensure there is no data leakage. Notably, RandomOverSampler does not generate synthetic data, making it a suitable choice for this study, as generating realistic synthetic images of skin cancer was not feasible.

Lightweight multi-scale CNN architecture

The lightweight multi-scale CNN model is both computationally efficient and architecturally sophisticated, striking a balance between complexity and lightweight design. It features a single input layer of dimensions 224 × 224, followed by three identical custom CNNBlocks, which serve as the core building blocks of the network. These CNNBlocks are subsequently followed by Flatten layers, fully connected layers, and dropout layers, which are incorporated to mitigate the risk of overfitting. The central feature of this model is the multi-scalefeature, where multiple convolutional layers are connected with each other and features are shared. The multi-scale architecture is illustrated in Fig. 3.

First, the input is entered into a 1 × 1 convolutional layer with a specified number of filters, followed by a 2 × 2 max pooling layer and a batch normalization layer, which uses the ReLU activation function. The convolutional layer is employed to reduce the input dimensionality while effectively capturing key features of the data. By downsampling the feature maps, the max pooling layer helps the model concentrate on critical features while reducing the computational load. Moreover, the batch normalization layer standardizes activations from the previous layer, improving both training stability and overall performance. By mitigating internal covariate shifts, batch normalization fosters smoother, more stable training and ultimately accelerates convergence. The second input goes into 3 × 3 convolutional layers with a specified number of filters, followed by a 2 × 2 max pooling layer and a BatchNormalization layer, utilizing the ReLU activation function. These convolutional layers capture increasingly complex spatial details in the input data. The max pooling layer subsequently reduces the spatial dimensions of the feature maps, lowering the computational overhead. Finally, BatchNormalization standardizes the activations, preserving consistency in their distribution and contributing to more stable model training. The third input is passed through three distinct convolutional operations. A 1 × 1 convolution applies the same number of filters. The fourth input is a 3 × 3 convolution, followed by another 3 × 3 convolution, to enhance feature extraction at intermediate scales. The fifth input is a 3 × 3 convolution followed by a 5 × 5 convolution, followed by an additional 5 × 5 convolution, enabling the network to capture larger receptive fields and more global features. These convolutions are followed by skip (residual) connections, which add the input directly to the combined output of the convolutions. This mechanism ensures that important features can bypass the convolutional operations and be passed directly to subsequent layers without alteration. If there is a mismatch in the number of filters between the input and the output, a 1 × 1 convolution is applied to align the filter sizes. After the residual addition, the output undergoes batch normalization to stabilize the activations. Next, a 2 × 2 max pooling layer downscales the feature maps, followed by a concluding convolution that aligns the number of filters across all blocks. An elementwise addition operation is performed on the outputs of the three blocks. This operation combines the information from the three blocks by adding their corresponding values. The addition allows the model to integrate features from different sources, enabling more effective feature learning and improving gradient flow, which is particularly useful in models with residual or skip connections. The proposed model has very low computational complexity, which should make it suitable and practically deployable. With only 0.88 M trainable parameters, it significantly reduces memory consumption. This lightweight design ensures faster inference and efficient deployment on resource-constrained devices such as mobile and edge-based healthcare systems.

In Fig. 3 the Lightweight Multiscale CNN model introduces a custom architecture that is both efficient and capable of capturing rich visual information by combining parallel convolutional pathways within each block to process input features at multiple scales. By using a multiscale design—specifically through the CNNBlock module that integrates 1 × 1, 3 × 3, and mixed-kernel convolutions in separate branches—the model extracts feature at different levels of abstraction, enabling it to detect fine details such as edges and textures through shallow convolutions while simultaneously capturing higher-order semantic patterns via deeper, composite convolutional paths. This layered understanding is reinforced by the additive fusion of multiscale feature maps, allowing the network to effectively balance precise localization with broader contextual interpretation. Its lightweight structure is maintained through the use of compact convolutional blocks, selective dropout, and batch normalization, which together stabilize training and reduce overfitting without incurring heavy computational cost. These design choices make the model well-suited for real-time and low-resource environments, offering strong classification performance with minimal latency and reduced memory requirements.

Federated learning

Integrating the LWMS-CNN into an FL framework enables privacy enhancements and efficient skin cancer classification without compromising accuracy. While LWMS-CNN is designed for low computational overhead and high classification performance, transitioning it into an FL setup ensures that model training occurs directly on distributed client devices and reduces privacy risks. In FL, multiple clients independently train models on their private datasets, sending only encrypted updates to a central server for aggregation. This design ensures that raw data remain with each client, mitigating confidentiality risks while facilitating collaborative learning37. Through this setup, distributed clients collectively build a global model without revealing their sensitive datasets. Before training, each client’s data undergo preprocessing, including modifications, digitization, and standardization, ensuring consistency across all contributors. In this setup, the dataset is evenly allocated to clients in an IID manner, providing each client with an equal share. This structured distribution ensures balanced training while enhancing data privacy and maintaining dataset integrity.

Upon collecting model parameters from clients, the central server aggregates and updates them according to its predefined framework. It then refines the global model and stores it for the next training cycle, where it will once again gather parameters from participants before distributing the updated model. This iterative approach is fundamental to FL and forms the core of the proposed method training methodology.

However, in federated learning, clients may have varying amounts of data. To account for this imbalance, model aggregation follows a weighted approach, where each local model’s contribution is proportional to the number of training samples it contains.

This ensures that models trained on larger datasets have a greater impact on the global model than those trained on smaller datasets do. Despite its simplicity, this weighted aggregation method has proven highly effective in federated learning, leading to a more balanced and robust global model. In each federated round, the process consists of three primary phases: parallel training, parameter aggregation, and model distribution. During parallel training, multiple clients concurrently train local models on their private datasets. After they complete this step, the clients send their updated model parameters to a central server, which combines them to enhance the global model. The refined model is subsequently shared with all the clients, initiating the next training cycle.

The primary factors influencing this process include C, which represents the number of contributors in each update cycle; E, signifying the number of epochs each contributor trains their local model before submitting updates; and B, denoting the minimum batch size used for local training. These parameters are critical in shaping the efficiency and convergence speed of the federated learning process.

During regional model optimization, each client runs a fixed number of epochs using the Adam optimizer, which adapts learning rates based on both the first-order moment (m) and the second-order moment (v) of the gradients. The hyperparameters β1 and β2 control the decay rates of these moving averages. β1, which is typically set at approximately 0.9, controls the first moment (gradient mean), whereas β2, which is often set at 0.999, controls the second moment (gradient variance). These hyperparameters help in overcoming local minima and ensuring stable and efficient convergence38. Federated Averaging (FedAvg) algorithm was eployed to aggregate locally trained client models into a unified global model. The Federated Averaging (FedAvg) algorithm unifies the locally trained models into a single global model. After each client transmits its updated weights to the central server, a weighted average is performed, where each client’s contribution is determined by the size of its local dataset. Once this aggregation is complete, the refined global model is distributed back to the clients for the next training iteration. This process is repeated iteratively until convergence is achieved. FedAvg helps reduce communication costs by allowing multiple local updates before synchronization, and it is robust to IID data distributions, making it a popular choice in privacy-enhancing machine learning applications39.

CKKS-based secure aggregation

This study adopts the Cheon-Kim-Kim-Song (CKKS) homomorphic encryption scheme40, to enable secure aggregation of neural network model parameters in a federated learning setting. CKKS supports approximate arithmetic over real-valued vectors, making it particularly suitable for deep neural network training, where floating-point computations and limited numerical imprecision are inherently tolerated.

Prior to federated training, a CKKS encryption context is initialized using a polynomial modulus degree of 16,384 and a coefficient modulus configuration of40,41, providing sufficient multiplicative depth and numerical stability for encrypted aggregation. The resulting public parameters are shared with all participating clients and the aggregation server.

During each federated communication round, participating clients locally train a cloned version of the global model and compute updated model weights. Instead of transmitting plaintext updates, each client encrypts its local model parameters using the CKKS public key. Due to the fixed slot capacity of CKKS ciphertexts, large weight tensors are flattened and partitioned into smaller chunks prior to encryption when necessary. These encrypted weight vectors (or chunks) are then transmitted to the server.

The server performs homomorphic federated averaging (FedAvg) directly in the encrypted domain, without accessing any plaintext model updates. For each model layer, encrypted weights received from participating clients are homomorphically summed and scaled by the inverse of the number of selected clients, as expressed here :5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathcal{E}\left(\varDelta\:{W}_{agg}\right)=\frac{1}{\left|C\right|}\left({\sum\:}_{\left\{i\:\in\:C\right\}}\mathcal{E}\left(\varDelta\:{W}_{i}\right)\:\right)\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:$$\end{document}

This aggregation process is executed independently for each layer and for each ciphertext chunk, ensuring correctness while preserving confidentiality. The aggregated encrypted model parameters are then forwarded to the trusted decryption authority, where decryption is performed using the shared key. The decrypted parameters are subsequently reshaped to their original tensor dimensions and used to update the global model.

By confining all aggregation operations to the encrypted domain and restricting access to the secret key, the proposed approach prevents both the server and participating clients from inspecting individual model updates in plaintext. This design significantly mitigates risks associated with information leakage, gradient inversion, and inference attacks. Although CKKS introduces additional computational and communication overhead due to ciphertext expansion and homomorphic operations, the resulting privacy guarantees align well with stringent data protection requirements such as GDPR and HIPAA.

Encrypted FL training workflow

This section outlines the implementation of the proposed Encrypted Federated LWMS CNN model. It explains how each client device trains data locally and how the global server consolidates the resulting model weights, as illustrated in Fig. 4. In this federated learning setup with homomorphic encryption, clients do not pose a threat to each other, primarily because they operate independently and never directly share raw data or decrypted model updates. Each client encrypts its model updates using a shared homomorphic encryption context and sends the encrypted data to the server, which performs aggregation without decryption. Since the same aggregated encrypted update is broadcast to all the clients and only decrypted locally, no individual client gains access to another’s local model or data. This uniform and privacy-enhancing process ensures that even though clients collaborate to improve a global model, they remain mutually secure and isolated, minimizing any risk of model or data leakage among peers42.

Communication protocol and rounds

To balance accuracy and efficiency, communication rounds were set at 30, 40, and 50 iterations. In each round, the global model provided initial weights to clients holding the prepared datasets. The clients then conducted local training and evaluated their models’ accuracy, sending refined weights back to the central server often termed the ‘global model’ for aggregation. The aggregation process applied an algorithm to the FL model, producing new global weights. In the following round, clients updated their weights based on these revised global parameters. To improve the process, a weight scaling factor was introduced to facilitate more effective weight adjustments. Throughout each communication cycle, clients kept updating their models based on the global weight, trained them on local datasets, and transmitted their refined models to the central server. The aggregation step employed various averaging techniques to improve the performance of the FL model.

Explainable AI

SHAP

To improve transparency and trust in AI models, XAI techniques have been applied to interpret decision-making process of the ensemble method used in this study43. XAI bridges the gap between complex AI systems and human understanding by offering interpretable explanations for model predictions. This ensures that AI decisions are explainable, making the internal workings of the models more accessible and understandable. This approach enhances clarity and enables users to comprehend how and why particular outputs are generated44. In this research, explainability is incorporated during the inference phase to enhance the interpretability of model predictions. Unlike approaches that modify the training process to enforce interpretability constraints, this method retains the model’s predictive performance while making its outputs more explainable. By applying SHAP post-training, a clear and interpretable evaluation of predictions without altering the underlying learning dynamics has been achieved.

The SHapley Additive exPlanations(SHAP) framework is employed in this study to enhance interpretability by evaluating how individual features contribute to the model’s predictions. Rooted in cooperative game theory, SHAP values provide a systematic approach to understanding the impact of each feature on a given instance. While accuracy is a fundamental performance metric, it does not reveal the reasoning behind a model’s decision-making process, particularly in complex models. To address this, SHAP offers visualization tools that illustrate the influence of each feature, making model outputs more transparent45,46. SHAP explains model predictions by allocating each feature’s contribution to a given data point, leveraging coalitional game theory to derive Shapley values. These values provide a fair allocation of contributions among features, treating them as players in a coalition. Features may be examined individually or in clusters, such as super-pixels for image classification. SHAP employs an additive feature attribution model, representing Shapley values as a linear function, which enables locally interpretable, model-agnostic explanations. This approach allows for a detailed breakdown of how each feature influences predictions47. where, g is the explanation model, z′ ∈ {0, 1}M is the coalition vector, M is the maximum coalition size, and ϕj ∈ R represents the feature attributions. This structured approach ensures a transparent and interpretable analysis of the model’s decision-making process48.

Grad-CAM

Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) is critical in high-stakes domains such as dermatological diagnostics, where understanding the rationale behind model predictions is essential for clinical trust and adoption. Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) has emerged as one of the most widely used tools for visual explanation of model decisions, enabling practitioners to verify whether automated systems are attending to medically relevant image regions. Grad-CAM generates heatmaps that highlight the spatial regions in an input image that most strongly influence a model’s output by computing gradients flowing into the last convolutional layers. This method allows researchers and clinicians to observe which parts of a skin lesion image—such as asymmetrical edges, color variations, or irregular structures—triggered a particular diagnostic classification. Unlike earlier techniques that required architectural modification or extensive retraining, Grad-CAM can be applied directly to existing models, offering a practical and scalable approach to visual interpretability47,49.

Moreover, Grad-CAM’s ability to produce class-discriminative explanations aligns it closely with the goals of XAI: not only enhancing transparency but also enabling model auditing and bias detection. In the context of skin cancer classification, this means that when a model misclassifies an image, Grad-CAM can reveal whether it was influenced by background artifacts, lighting patterns, or non-lesion features—issues that would remain opaque in black-box models. The integration of Grad-CAM with Guided Backpropagation further results in Guided Grad-CAM, which preserves both high spatial resolution and class specificity, crucial for localizing subtle dermoscopic patterns. Human-subject studies confirm that such visualizations help users, even without deep technical knowledge, to discern between reliable and unreliable model behavior, reinforcing appropriate trust in AI-assisted diagnosis50. Grad-CAM thus serves not merely as a post-hoc visualization tool but as a foundational component in making clinical AI systems safe, interpretable, and ethically deployable.

Experimental setup and hyperparameter settings

The experimental setup follows a fully federated learning paradigm, where training occurs in a distributed manner across multiple client devices. Analysis of the proposed LWMS-CNN-FL model with homomorphic encryption

To further strengthen data privacy and security in federated learning, HE was incorporated into the LWMS-CNN-FL model. By allowing computations to proceed on encrypted data without decryption, HE keeps sensitive patient information secure during the entire training phase. This capability is particularly critical in healthcare, where safeguarding privacy is paramount. Homomorphic encryption provides a crucial advantage in federated learning by enabling secure computations on encrypted data. In healthcare applications, patient confidentiality is a significant concern, and HE ensures that sensitive medical data are never exposed, even during model training. Furthermore, HE facilitates secure multi-party learning by enabling institutions to contribute to a global model without compromising their individual datasets. This is particularly beneficial when working with diverse and geographically distributed datasets, as it allows for improved generalization without data sharing. Despite its privacy-oriented advantages, homomorphic encryption introduces a computational overhead that affects model performance. As observed in Table 7, the accuracy of the LWMS-CNN-FL model with homomorphic encryption is slightly lower at 98.34% than that of the model without encryption at 98.62%. This reduction in performance can be attributed to the increased computational complexity associated with encrypted data operations. Another notable tradeoff is training time. HE-based computations require significant additional processing power because of the complex mathematical operations performed on encrypted data. This results in longer training times than without the HE models. In conclusion, while homomorphic encryption enhances privacy and security in federated learning, it involves a minor tradeoff in accuracy and computational efficiency.

Discussion

Comparison with previous works

Table 16 presents a comparative overview of methods previously adopted for this dataset, highlighting approaches both with and without federated learning. This analysis is vital for evaluating the distinct advantages and drawbacks of each technique, particularly concerning the LWMS-CNN and federated learning for skin cancer detection. The proposed LWMS-CNN + FL (FedAvg) model demonstrated notable advantages over the previously applied methods. Compared with the traditional deep learning approaches of Mavaddati et al.56, Pandey et al.57, Alotaibi et al.17, Djekoun et al.58, etc., which rely on traditional deep learning, the proposed model leverages FL, ensuring that sensitive patient data remain private and secure. Additionally, methods such as ResNet50 + LSTM, Xception + Attention, and CAFormer have high accuracy but are more complex than the proposed model and lack privacy-aware mechanisms, making them vulnerable to data breaches and regulatory constraints. The ResNet50 + LSTM model outperformed the proposed model due to due to its ability to capture both spatial features through deep residual learning and temporal or sequential dependencies across image regions via the LSTM, enabling richer contextual understanding. Among FL-based methods, the proposed model outperformed existing works (Hu et al.22, Li et al.23, Nam et al.24) in accuracy, achieving 98.62%, whereas earlier FL models, such as FedAvg-based CNNs, scored lower by 81%, 95%, and 98.16%, respectively. The proposed model achieved better accuracy, as the better LWMS-CNN was integrated with the FedAvg aggregation strategy, which enhances robustness against noisy or adversarial clients. Additionally, the other existing methods in FL do not apply HE, whereas the proposed model secures data during computation, eliminating the risk of exposure even during training. Another key differentiator is the integration of XAI through SHAP, which is absent in all FL-based methods except Attallah et al.19 SkinCAD. However, the author did not utilize FL or encryption, making it unsuitable for privacy-sensitive applications. Furthermore, ensemble methods such as Al-Rasheed et al. employ CGANs for data augmentation, but their accuracy of 90.00% is significantly lower because of dataset imbalance issues, which the proposed model addresses by up sampling. Overall, the proposed model effectively balances high classification accuracy with fewer trainable parameters, privacy, security, and interpretability, making it a more comprehensive and robust solution for skin cancer diagnosis than all previously studied approaches.

Potential strength of the proposed model

The LWMS-CNN model presents significant advantages in skin cancer classification, particularly within a federated learning framework. Its lightweight architecture, with significantly fewer trainable parameters, allows for efficient training and deployment on edge devices such as smartphones and embedded systems. This can make it ideal for real-time applications where computational resources are limited. Additionally, its multi-scale convolutional approach enhances feature extraction, enabling accurate detection of intricate patterns in skin lesion images, which outperformed all other state-of-the-art models. Furthermore, its consistent results across multiple benchmark datasets underscore the model’s strong generalization ability. The ability to train collaboratively across centralized devices while preserving data privacy makes it a robust candidate for secure and scalable healthcare solutions. By leveraging federated learning, the model is intended to be trainable across multiple institutions without sharing sensitive patient data; however, in this work, this setting is simulated using a single device to emulate a federated learning environment. The integration of homomorphic encryption further strengthens data security, making it highly resilient against potential cyber threats. Although the research is conducted on a single machine where FL system is simulated, FL enables multiple medical institutions or devices to collaboratively train machine learning models without sharing raw patient data, thus ensuring data locality and regulatory compliance. Integrating HE further strengthens this setup by allowing encrypted data to be used in computations without decryption, ensuring that even intermediate model updates or gradients remain confidential. This dual protection mechanism is designed to stop several classes of attacks, including reconstruction attacks, membership inference attacks, and model inversion attacks, by preventing adversaries from performing inference on gradients or model updates that could otherwise be used to reconstruct sensitive training data. By ensuring both the data and the learning process, this approach enhances trust among data holders and facilitates the development of robust, accurate, and privacy-enhancing models for skin cancer detection across different datasets. In clinical settings, instant and accurate predictions can aid dermatologists in early-stage cancer detection, improving patient outcomes, which can be achieved by the LWMS-CNN. Using XAI enhances the interpretability of the model by highlighting which image pixels contribute most to its predictions, allowing clinicians to understand and trust AI-driven diagnoses. Additionally, SHAP and Grad-CAM help in detecting biases and misclassifications, enabling researchers to refine the model for more accurate and fair predictions across diverse datasets and skin types.

Limitations

Despite the promising outcomes and successful cross-dataset validation, the LWMS-CNN-FL model still presents certain limitations that require further investigation. Although the model demonstrates strong generalization across multiple benchmark datasets, its performance may still vary under highly heterogeneous real-world conditions such as variations in imaging devices, lighting environments, and data acquisition protocols. In addition, the proposed FL framework in this study was evaluated in a simulated environment rather than deployed across multiple physical machines, which may limit the assessment of its real-world applicability. Furthermore, it was observed that the model exhibits a performance degradation under non-IID data distributions, a condition that is more representative of real-world federated environments where client data are inherently heterogeneous. Moreover, the federated learning setup assumes stable network connectivity and a balanced distribution of data across participating nodes, which may not always be achievable in practical clinical settings. The integration of encryption and privacy-oriented mechanisms also introduce a minor computational overhead that could affect its deployment on resource-constrained edge devices. Future work should therefore focus on improving domain adaptation, enhancing robustness under non-IID data distributions, increasing communication efficiency in federated learning, and optimizing real-time performance to strengthen the model’s scalability and reliability in diverse healthcare scenarios.

Discussion

This study introduced an Encrypted FedAvg-Based Explainable Federated Learning approach using an LWMS-CNN for early detection of skin cancer. The proposed model effectively balances high classification accuracy with lower computational cost. The integration of FL with Homomorphic Encryption ensures enhanced data privacy by allowing centralized training without exposing sensitive patient data. The FedAvg aggregation strategy proved to be superior to the traditional FedTrimmed and FedMedian approaches. Additionally, the implementation of XAI techniques, such as SHAP and Grad-CAM, enhances model interpretability, fostering greater trust among medical practitioners. With accuracy of 98.62% without homomorphic encryption (HE) and accuracy of 98.34% with HE, it outperformed other state-of-the-art models in both accuracy and efficiency, achieving superior classification performance. The model’s consistent performance across external datasets, including ISIC 2019 and PAD-UFES-20, further demonstrates strong generalization capability. Overall, the proposed LWMS-CNN-FL framework represents a significant step toward developing secure, efficient, and interpretable AI-driven solutions for dermatological diagnostics. Future research should focus on advanced privacy-enhancing techniques, communication-efficient federated architectures, and broader cross-institutional validations to enhance scalability and strengthen its applicability in diverse healthcare environments.
