Choroid plexus remodeling linked to impaired CSF‐mediated clearance and Alzheimer's disease progression
Authors: Haolin Yin, Yuepeng Deng, ZiHao Lu, Yu Jiang, Chao Zuo, Jianyu Li, Linlin Guo, Binwu Ying, Giuseppe Battaglia, Xiaohe Tian, Qiyong Gong, for the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI)
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONImpaired cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) ‐mediated clearance has been implicated in Alzheimer's disease (AD), but how choroid plexus (ChP) remodeling relates to these processes in vivo remains incompletely understood.METHODSIn a large ADNI cohort, we integrated structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), amyloid/tau positron emission tomography (PET), and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) ‐derived analysis along the perivascular space (ALPS) to characterize ChP changes and their associations with ventricular measures, glymphatic marker, CSF biomarkers, and cognition across diagnostic stages. Our experiments in APP/PS1 mice complemented human analyses.RESULTSChP volume increased with disease stage, while PET signal decreased. Larger ChP volume was associated with ventricular enlargement, a lower ALPS index, higher cortical amyloid/tau burden, and worse cognitive performance. Glymphatic impairment partially mediated ChP effects on cognition and pathology. APP/PS1 mice recapitulated the key ChP and glymphatic MRI phenotypes observed in humans, alongside altered expression and localization of key proteins.DISCUSSIONChP remodeling linked to impaired CSF‐mediated clearance, highlighting its role as an early diagnostic and therapeutic target.

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD), the most common form of dementia, is characterized by extracellular amyloid plaques and intracellular tau tangles. Normally, the brain continuously produces and clears Aβ and tau proteins. These pathological changes begin decades before symptoms appear, leading to neuronal loss and cognitive decline. 1 Imbalance between protein production and clearance may be an important factor contributing to protein aggregation. 2 Early‐onset AD often involves increased Aβ production and reduced clearance, while late‐onset AD primarily shows decreased clearance. 3 , 4 The normal circulation and function of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) are crucial for maintaining essential clearance pathways in the brain, including interstitial fluid (ISF) circulation, lymphatic system transport, and clearance across the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and blood–CSF barrier (BCSFB). 2 The choroid plexus (ChP), located in the ventricles, produces CSF and maintains immune function and barrier homeostasis. 5 , 6 , 7 It transports nutrients into CSF and removes toxins. 8

In AD, the ChP shows significant morphological and functional changes, 9 , 10 with studies indicating declined CSF production and turnover. 11 Structural alterations include thickened basement membranes and tissue atrophy. 12 Neuroimaging reveals progressive ChP enlargement in AD, linked to increased permeability and reduced perfusion. 13 , 14 Age‐related changes like volume increase and reduced blood flow further highlight its vulnerability. 7 ChP volume correlates with AD pathology severity and clinical status. 14 , 15 , 16 The BCSFB at the ChP is crucial for brain homeostasis. Composed of tight epithelial layers and a vascular stroma, it regulates compound transport and clears toxins like Aβ and tau. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 The glymphatic system, which clears waste via perivascular spaces1 7, relies on CSF‐ISF exchange to remove proteins like Aβ and tau. 22 , 23 Dynamic positron emission tomography (PET) studies measuring CSF kinetics indicate that CSF‐related clearance defects mediate amyloid deposition in AD patients. 24 , 25 Impaired clearance may reflect lower CSF protein levels, correlated with ChP enlargement. 14 The glymphatic system function, assessed using the diffusion tensor image analysis along the perivascular space (DTI‐ALPS), is reduced in AD patients. 26 , 27 In summary, AD involves ChP structural and functional changes, declined CSF circulation, impaired glymphatic clearance, and BCSFB transport defects. These abnormalities are interwoven that collectively promotes the accumulation of toxic substances such as Aβ and facilitates disease progression.

Aβ is cleared from the brain through an elaborate efflux network: intramural peri‑arterial drainage, perivascular drainage, perineural drainage, absorption via arachnoid granulations/villi into the venous sinuses, meningeal lymphatic drainage to cervical lymph nodes, and transport across the BCSFB and BBB. 2 , 28 Compared with Aβ, the transport of tau across the BBB and its underlying mechanisms are less clearly defined, though the other pathways remain accessible to tau. 2 , 28 Most of these efflux pathways involve CSF as a central medium. The ChP, being the primary source of CSF and the site of the BCSFB, 7 , 23 holds a close yet incompletely understood relationship with the entire CSF‑mediated clearance process. Elucidating these issues holds significant promise: it could fundamentally reshape our understanding of the ChP's pivotal yet underappreciated role and facilitate the development of tailored clinical diagnostic markers alongside therapeutic strategies designed to enhance the clearance of pathological proteins from the brain. 29 , 30 , 31 , 32

This study aims to establish an association framework linking ChP remodeling to impaired CSF‐mediated clearance, supported by multimodal convergent and cross‐species evidence. First, in a large‐sample cohort, we characterized multimodal imaging differences in the ChP and other CSF‐mediated clearance structures across varying stages of AD progression. Furthermore, we elucidated the relationships between ChP, these structural changes, intracranial pathological protein burden, and cognitive status. Second, through mediation analysis, we examined whether ChP remodeling influences cerebral amyloid deposition, cognitive function, and CSF pathological protein levels via the glymphatic system as a potential mediator. Finally, to complement the findings from human studies, we employed APP/PS1 mice to investigate whether the association of key in vivo magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) phenotypes between the ChP and glymphatic system observed in humans could be replicated, and to explore associated molecular alterations.

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: We conducted a comprehensive literature review identify studies on choroid plexus (ChP) structure and function, glymphatic system activity, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) dynamics in Alzheimer's disease (AD). Existing evidence indicates alterations within the CSF‐mediated clearance structures in AD, but their interrelationships and clinical significance remained incompletely. Interpretation: Our findings, integrating large‐scale multimodal human neuroimaging with cross‐species experimental data, establish ChP remodeling as a central hub within the impaired CSF‐mediated clearance network in AD. Convergent evidence supports a unified associative framework that positions the ChP as a critical nexus for early detection and intervention. Future directions: Future studies should investigate: (a) longitudinal ChP and glymphatic changes across the AD continuum; (b) therapeutic strategies targeting ChP transport proteins to restore CSF turnover and Aβ handling; and (c) the utility of ChP‐focused imaging biomarkers for early diagnosis and intervention.

METHODS

Participants

The patients included in this study were sourced from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) database (https://adni.loni.usc.edu). ADNI was established in 2003 by Dr. Michael W. Weiner. ADNI is a comprehensive project that encompasses participants from multiple centers. The primary objective of ADNI is to evaluate a variety of biomarkers (clinical, multimodal imaging, and biospecimens) and their combinations to develop methods for the early detection of AD. The project data is accessible to researchers worldwide. The ADNI project obtained approval from the Institutional Review Board at each participating site, and all participants provided written informed consent for data sharing and analysis. 33 In this study, the total sample consisted of 968 participants, including 382 healthy controls, 433 mild cognitive impairment (MCI) patients, and 153 AD patients. Participants were subjected to rigorous classification diagnostics based on a standardized framework from ADNI. The core elements of the diagnostic criteria encompass both subjective cognitive reports and objective cognitive assessments. For the objective cognitive assessment, Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) and Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) are used as key indicators: for the MCI group, an MMSE score between 24 and 30 and a CDR score of 0.5 is required; for the AD group, an MMSE score between 20 and 26 and a CDR score of ≥0.5 is required. It is worth noting that all participants in the MCI and AD groups must report subjective cognitive decline. In contrast, normal control (NC) participants not only require the absence of subjective cognitive issues but also need to have all cognitive scale assessment results within the normal range. Researchers can refer to the official ADNI operations manual (http://www.adni‐info.org) for detailed implementation methods and scoring rules regarding these diagnostic criteria. The subsets for imaging analyses included 835 participants for amyloid PET, 214 for tau PET, and 90 for DTI MRI. The included variables in the study encompassed baseline demographic data such as age, sex, and years of education; CSF biomarkers including Aβ42, t‐tau, and p‐tau181 (p‐tau); and clinical data such as MMSE total score, Global CDR, and MRI and PET measurements. We only included participants with a time interval of 30 days or less between CSF pathological protein measurements and PET and MRI imaging.

PET and structural MRI

The acquisition methods for amyloid PET are as follows: Florbetapir 370 MBq (10 mCi) ± 10% is injected, and imaging is performed 50–70 minutes after injection for 20 minutes (4 × 5 minute frames). For Tau PET, the acquisition methods are as follows: [18F] Flortaucipir 370 MBq (10.0 mCi) ± 10% is injected, and imaging is performed 75–105 minutes after injection for 30 minutes (6 × 5 minute frames). Structural imaging was performed using a 1.5 or 3.0 Tesla MRI scanner. For detailed parameter information, please refer to the following link: (https://adni.loni.usc.edu/data‐samples/adni‐data/neuroimaging/mri/mri‐scanner‐protocols/). Subsequently, further processing of the amyloid PET and Tau PET data includes frame realignment and averaging, linear transformation for head straightening, voxel size normalization, and smoothing to a uniform resolution (https://ida.loni.usc.edu/). A structural MRI scan that is closest in scan time to the Amyloid PET and Tau PET scans is used. The MRI data are segmented and parcellated using Freesurfer v7.1.1 to define different brain regions (Desikan–Killiany Atlas). The amyloid PET images are then registered using SPM. To generate the standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR), each Amyloid PET scan and Tau PET scan is co‐registered with the corresponding segmented/parcellated MRI, and intensity normalization is performed on the PET scans using bilateral volume weighting with respect to the whole cerebellum. For the amyloid PET data, we utilized the SUVR of [18F] Florbetapir for the cerebral cortex, cerebral white matter, lateral ventricles, third ventricle, fourth ventricle, and ChP, with the cerebellum serving as the reference region. For structural MRI data, volumetric data is obtained for the corresponding cerebral cortex, cerebral white matter, lateral ventricles, third ventricle, fourth ventricle, and ChP. For the Tau PET data, we utilized the SUVR of [18F] Flortaucipir for the cerebral cortex, hippocampus, cerebral white matter, lateral ventricles, third ventricle, fourth ventricle, and ChP. These regions are all normalized to the intensity of the lower gray matter of the cerebellum. The SUVR for the cerebral cortex is calculated as the mean value of all cortical regions.

CSF biomarkers

The concentrations of Aβ42, t‐tau, and p‐tau in CSF were measured using an electrochemiluminescence immunoassay on an Elecsys cobas e 601 instruments (Roche Diagnostics, Penzberg, Germany). The immunoassays were conducted according to the instructions provided by the kit manufacturer and as described in previous studies. 34 , 35 Detection of each biomarker was performed using reagents from the same batch, and each run included quality control samples to ensure precision and accuracy (for detailed information, please refer to https://adni.loni.usc.edu/data‐samples/).

DTI and assessment of ALPS index

DTI data were acquired using multiple MRI scanners from Siemens, GE Healthcare, and Philips, with acquisition parameters specifically optimized for each scanner model. The acquisition parameters varied across scanners, including b‐values ranging from 0 to 2000 s/mm2, field of view (FOV) matrix dimensions of 116×116, 128×128, or 256×256, repetition time (TR) between 4.2 s and 16.7 s, echo time (TE) ranging from 56 to 105 ms, and slice thicknesses of either 2 mm or 2.7 mm. Detailed imaging acquisition parameters are provided in the supplementary materials. For comprehensive information on DTI acquisition, please refer to the imaging protocols (https://adni.loni.usc.edu/data‐samples/adni‐data/neuroimaging/mri/mri‐scanner‐protocols/).

DTI data processing was performed using the FMRIB Software Library (FSL, version 6.0.7.14; Oxford Centre for Functional MRI of the Brain; https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/). The processing pipeline for diffusion images was implemented as follows to obtain various diffusion metric parameter maps for the ALPS index. First, motion correction and eddy current correction were applied to the raw DTI data to minimize distortions caused by head motion and magnetic field inhomogeneities. Subsequently, the orientation of diffusion vectors in the gradient table was rotated to align with the corrected images. The b0 images, representing non‐diffusion‐weighted data, were extracted from the corrected 4D dataset to generate brain masks. Diffusion tensor fitting was then performed using the corrected data, brain masks, and adjusted gradient directions to produce various parameter maps, including diffusion tensor maps, fractional anisotropy (FA) maps, and primary eigenvector (V1) maps. The JHU ICBM‐DTI‐81 White Matter Atlas from the Johns Hopkins University International Consortium for Brain Mapping was overlaid onto the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) standard space to localize the association fibers (superior longitudinal fasciculus [SLF]) and projection fibers (superior corona radiata [SCR]) of the lateral ventricular body. 36 Spherical regions of interest (ROIs) with a 4 mm radius were placed at the corresponding locations of the SCR and SLF in the left hemisphere. A two‐step registration procedure was then executed to align both ROIs and the JHU ICBM‐DTI‐81 atlas to the native brain space of individual subjects. In the first step, a linear affine transformation was applied using the FLIRT (FMRIB's Linear Image Registration Tool) algorithm to register the individual's b0 brain image to the MNI standard space. This step generated a transformation matrix that mapped the individual brain space to the MNI standard space. The inverse of this transformation matrix was then computed using the convert_xfm tool to achieve reverse mapping from the MNI standard space to the individual brain space. In the second step, the inverse transformation matrix was utilized to register the MNI standard space template, ROIs, and JHU ICBM‐DTI‐81 white matter atlas to the individual's native brain space. This was accomplished by applying the precomputed inverse transformation matrix using FLIRT with the ‐applyxfm option. The ROIs were validated and fine‐tuned by an experienced clinician concerning the V1 maps modulated by FA maps, focusing on the trajectory regions of white matter fibers. Finally, the ROIs on the projection and association fibers were utilized to extract diffusivity values along the principal axes (x, y, and z) from the tensor maps, enabling the calculation of the ALPS index based on the ALPS formula, with batch effect correction applied to the ALPS index to mitigate impacts from multi‐scanner data sources. 37

Animal study

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University. Studies were conducted in strict accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. APPSwe/PS1dE9 (APP/PS1) transgenic mice on a C57BL/6J background and their wild‐type (WT) littermates were obtained from Jinzhihe Animal Technology Co., Ltd. in Nanjing (Batch 1) and Shulaibao Biological Technology Co., Ltd. in Wuhan (Batch 2). In this model, Aβ plaques begin to deposit steadily in the neocortex and hippocampus by 6 months of age, with plaque burden increasing significantly by 12 months, demonstrating progressive aggravation, although prominent tau pathology is generally not observed. Both male and female mice were included in this study. Mice were housed under controlled environmental conditions: a constant temperature of 22 ± 1°C, 54 ± 8% relative humidity, and a 12‐h light/12‐h dark cycle. Animals received standard laboratory chow and sterile water ad libitum. Four experimental groups were utilized: 6‐month‐old APP/PS1 mice, 12‐month‐old APP/PS1 mice, 6‐month‐old WT mice, and 12‐month‐old WT mice. The first batch (males, n = 5 per group) and the second batch (males, n = 4 per group; females, n = 6 per group) of mice were used for the following various experiments and analyses. Both animal numbers and procedural suffering were minimized.

Mouse MRI acquisition

Mouse brain MRI experiments were conducted on a 9.4T uMR scanner (Wuhan United Imaging Life Science Instrument Co., Ltd.). Initial anesthesia in mice was induced using 4% isoflurane, followed by initial sedation achieved via intraperitoneal injection of dexmedetomidine (0.016 mg/kg). Animals were then positioned on an MRI‐compatible bed equipped with a heating system and secured using a bite bar with ear bars. Subsequently, the isoflurane concentration was gradually reduced and maintained at 0.6%, while dexmedetomidine was continuously administered subcutaneously at a rate of 0.096 mg/kg/h. Throughout the experimental procedures, mice remained under stable, light anesthesia, with core body temperature consistently maintained at 37.5 ± 0.5°C (SA Instruments Inc.). For DTI imaging, a 2D echo planar imaging (EPI) sequence was employed with the following parameters: isotropic voxel size of 0.2×0.2×0.2 mm3, TR of 4000 ms, TE of 24.2 ms, and flip angle of 90°. Diffusion weighting was applied along 16 directions using three b‐values (0, 850, and 1550 s/mm2), with each direction averaged 8 times to improve signal‐to‐noise ratio. The acquisition matrix was 96 × 96 points, with a FOV of 19×19 mm. The bandwidth was 3370 Hz/pixel, and the echo spacing was 0.34 ms. The total acquisition time for DTI was 9 mins and 28 s. For T2‐weighted imaging (T2W), a 2D fast spin echo (FSE) sequence was used with the following parameters: voxel size of 0.10×0.10×0.40 mm3, TR of 3000 ms, TE of 39.34 ms, flip angle of 90°, and 38 slices with a thickness of 0.4 mm and no gap between slices. The acquisition matrix was 208 × 208 points, with a FOV of 20×20 mm. The bandwidth was 260 Hz/pixel, and the echo spacing was 5.62 ms. The total acquisition time for T2‐weighted imaging was 1 min and 39 s.

Mouse T2W image segmentation and volumetric measurement

The processing of mouse T2W data utilized a semi‐automatic segmentation method implemented in the 3D Slicer software (version 5.6.2; https://www.slicer.org). First, the boundary of the brain parenchyma was roughly outlined using the level tracing tool within the segmentation module, which employs a region‐growing algorithm based on intensity thresholds. Due to potential inaccuracies in edge detection in areas with similar pixel intensity values, systematic manual corrections were applied to all axial slices to ensure the precision of the boundary delineation. The generated brain mask was then exported for further processing. Custom MATLAB scripts (R2022b, MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) were written to extract specific brain regions based on the generated mask. Subsequently, image intensity and contrast were adjusted to enhance the visualization of the ventricular system and brain parenchyma boundaries. The same protocol was applied to segment the ventricular system, producing a ventricular mask. For ChP segmentation, given the differing voxel intensity distributions between CSF and ChP tissues within the ventricles, custom MATLAB scripts were developed to calculate the distribution of voxel values and select appropriate thresholds to maximally exclude CSF voxels, generating a preliminary ChP mask. This preliminary mask was then registered with the original T2‐weighted images in 3D Slicer for verification. An experienced researcher manually corrected the mask to exclude isolated voxel clusters that were discontinuous with the ChP structure and lacked basal attachment within the ventricular cavity. A second experienced researcher independently validated the corrected ChP mask. The ChP mask was used to extract the ChP, calculate the number of voxels, and convert this to actual volume based on the voxel size of the scanning parameters.

Mouse DTI image preprocessing and ALPS index calculation

The processing pipeline for mouse brain DTI data was conducted using FSL software, following a workflow analogous to that employed for human data. Due to the limitations of FSL's automated skull‐stripping algorithm when applied to mouse DTI data, we opted to extract the b0 images following eddy current and motion correction, and subsequently processed these images in 3D Slicer. A brain mask was generated using the same methodology applied to mouse T2‐weighted data. Tensor fitting was then performed to derive diffusion tensor maps, FA maps, V1 maps, and mean diffusivity (MD) maps. Region of Interest (ROI) selection was guided by prior literature 38 , 39 , 40 and the anatomical characteristics of white matter tracts in the mouse brain (https://atlas.brain‐map.org/). Two ROIs were delineated within the fimbria region based on the following criteria: (1) localization within white matter tracts exhibiting a close anatomical relationship with ventricular structures, and (2) sufficient volume and stable anatomical positioning to ensure reliable ROI placement. Using FSLeyes, structural images were overlaid with V1 maps modulated by FA values as a reference. Two spherical ROIs (radius = 0.2 mm) within the fimbria (Fimbria ROI1 and Fimbria ROI2) were precisely placed by an experienced researcher. For Fimbria ROI1, where white matter fibers are aligned along the z‐axis, the Dxx and Dyy components were extracted, with Dxx representing the principal diffusion direction. Similarly, for Fimbria ROI2, where white matter fibers are aligned along the x‐axis, the Dyy and Dzz components were extracted, with Dyy representing the principal diffusion direction. A mouse ALPS (mALPS) was subsequently calculated using the formula. The fimbria ROI1 and fimbria ROI2 were placed in the left and right hemispheres, respectively. Subsequently, the mALPS index for each hemisphere was calculated, resulting in the left mALPS index and the right mALPS index. By computing the average of the left and right mALPS indices, the mean mALPS index was obtained. In addition, we measured the MD in the ChP and ventricular regions. By leveraging the registration between structural and DTI images, the ChP and ventricular ROIs defined on the structural images were transferred to the MD maps, and the MD values within these ROIs were calculated.

Tissue collection from mice

For immunofluorescence and immunohistochemical analyses, mice were deeply anesthetized with 3% isoflurane, euthanized by cervical dislocation, and transcardially perfused with ice‑cold 0.1 M phosphate buffered saline (PBS) followed by freshly prepared 4% paraformaldehyde (in 0.1 M PBS, pH 7.4). Brains were harvested, embedded in paraffin, and coronally sectioned at 3 µm thickness for subsequent staining. For CSF collection, mice were anesthetized with 3% isoflurane. Using a 10 µL microsyringe, approximately 5 µL of CSF was aspirated from the midline depression between the occipital protuberance and the first cervical vertebra. The sample was transferred into a pre‑cooled 0.5 mL microcentrifuge tube, centrifuged at 500 × g for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was snap‑frozen in liquid nitrogen. Immediately after CSF collection, the same mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation and perfused with ice‑cold 0.1 M PBS. Brains were removed, and the ChP from the lateral ventricles was carefully dissected under a microscope. The isolated ChP and the remaining brain parenchyma tissue were snap‑frozen separately in liquid nitrogen for subsequent molecular analyses.

Immunofluorescence

Twenty serial sections encompassing the lateral ventricle ChP were selected per animal. Sections were baked at 65°C for 5 h, deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval by microwaving in pre‐boiled sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 8 minutes at high power, followed by 8 minutes at medium‐low power, followed by natural cooling to room temperature. Non‐specific binding was blocked by incubation with 10% normal goat serum (Zhongshan Jinqiao, #ZLI‐9056) in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. After three PBS washes, sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies diluted in antibody diluent (Zhongshan Jinqiao, #ZLI‐9030): rabbit polyclonal anti‐transient receptor potential vanilloid 4 (TRPV4) (1:200, Abmart, #TD8624); mouse monoclonal anti‐aquaporin‐1 (AQP1) (1:100, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #sc‐25287); rabbit polyclonal anti‐potassium‐chloride cotransporter 3 (KCC3) (1:200, Affinity, #DF15430); rabbit monoclonal anti‐low‐density lipoprotein receptor‐related protein 1 (LRP1) (1:50, HUABIO, #ET1601‐1); mouse monoclonal anti‐Aβ (1:100, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #sc‐28365); rabbit polyclonal anti‐aquaporin‐4 (AQP4) (1:1000, Servicebio, #GB11529‐50); and mouse monoclonal anti‐β‐dystroglycan (β‐DG) (1:100, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #sc‐33702). The following day, sections were washed three times in PBS and incubated for 1 h at room temperature with appropriate Alexa Fluor®‐conjugated secondary antibodies diluted in PBS: AffiniPure® donkey anti‐rabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG_ (H+L) Alexa Fluor® 488 (1:200, Jackson ImmunoResearch, #111‐545‐003) or CoraLite® 594 (1:200, Proteintech, #SA00012‐7) or Alexa Fluor® 647 (1:200, Abclonal, #AS075) for rabbit primaries, and AffiniPure® donkey anti‐mouse IgG (H+L) Cyanine3® (1:200, Jackson ImmunoResearch, #115‐165‐003) for mouse primaries. Following three final PBS washes, sections were counterstained with DAPI (1 µg/mL, Sigma, #D8417‐10MG) in deionized water for 15 minutes in the dark, then sealed with a coverslip with antifade mounting medium.

Immunofluorescence measurement

Immunofluorescence images of lateral ventricle ChP sections were acquired using a Leica STELLARIS 5 confocal microscope equipped with 10× and 20× objectives. Three distinct FOVs were captured from each lateral ventricle ChP side per mouse, yielding a total of six FOVs per animal. Identical digital imaging parameters were maintained throughout the entire experimental series. Quantification was performed by a blinded observer using Fiji software (v1.53q). ROIs encompassing the ChP area were manually delineated within each FOV. These measurements were averaged across the six ROIs (corresponding to ROIs 1, 2, and 3 per lateral ventricle ChP side) to obtain single representative values per mouse for downstream statistical analysis. Protein expression was quantified based on mean fluorescence intensity and percentage of positive fluorescence area (positive area %) within the ROIs. Colocalization analysis was conducted using both line ROIs and area ROIs, with quantification performed via Pearson's correlation coefficient.

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin‐embedded sections were subjected to deparaffinization, rehydration, and antigen retrieval under high‐pressure conditions. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked, followed by a blocking step to reduce nonspecific binding. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4°C with a primary antibody against Aβ1‐42 (mOC64, Abcam, China). Subsequently, a horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated goat anti‐rabbit IgG (H+L) secondary antibody (AS014, ABclonal) was applied and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Color development was performed using a 3,3’‐diaminobenzidine chromogen, followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin. The stained tissues were mounted and examined under a light microscope (CX41, Olympus, Japan). Finally, whole‐slide bright‐field scanning was carried out using an Olympus VS200 slide scanning system.

Enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay

The concentration of Aβ1‐42 protein in mouse CSF was measured using a commercial enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (RXW201468M, Scientific Research Mouse β‐Amyloid 42). Each CSF sample was diluted by adding 50 µL of phosphate‑buffered saline per tube, and the diluted mixture was transferred to a 96‑well plate followed by gentle shaking for 1 h. Subsequently, enzyme conjugate reagent was added, and the plate was shaken gently for another 2 h. After incubation (37°C, 30 min), the plate was washed five times (30 s per wash), followed by the addition of chromogenic substrate. Finally, the optical density at 450 nm was measured using a Rayto RT‑6100 microplate reader.

Polymerase chain reaction

The isolated ChP and the remaining brain parenchyma tissues from mice were homogenized in an appropriate buffer. Total RNA was extracted and reverse‐transcribed into complementary DNA using a reverse transcription kit (TaKaRa, RR047A, China). Subsequently, mRNA levels were quantified with SYBR quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) Mix (Novoprotein, E096‐01B, China). The relative mRNA expression of TRPV4, KCC3, AQP1, and LRP1 was normalized to β‐actin and calculated using the 2^(‐ΔΔCt) method (gene‐specific primers are listed in the Table S1). Results are presented as relative quantification (RQ) values; however, statistical analysis was performed based on ΔCt values.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.1 for Windows; GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA). Unless otherwise specified, a two‐tailed p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene's test. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non‐normally distributed data were presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were described as frequencies (percentages). Based on the results of normality and homogeneity of variance tests, continuous variables were analyzed using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal–Wallis test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson's chi‐squared test. Post‐hoc multiple comparisons were performed using the Holm‐Bonferroni or Tukey's method. multivariable‐ordinal logistic regression analysis was used to evaluate the effect of regional brain volumes and PET measurements of amyloid SUVR and tau SUVR on AD progression, adjusting for age, sex, and years of education. Multiple linear regression analysis was used to determine the regression coefficients between ChP volume and various neuroimaging measures (including PET metrics of the ChP, ventricular system volumes and PET measurements, brain parenchyma volumes and PET measurements), MMSE scores, CSF biomarkers (Aβ42, t‐tau, and p‐tau), and the ALPS index, with adjustments for age and sex (years of education were included based on the significance in univariate analysis). In mice, simple linear regression was used to evaluate the relationships between ChP volume and ventricular system volume, left mALPS index, right mALPS index, and mean mALPS index. Bland–Altman analysis was performed to assess the agreement between left and right mALPS indices. Mediation analysis was conducted using a three‐variable regression model, decomposing the total effect into average direct effect (ADE) and average causal mediation effect (ACME), with age and sex included as covariates. The significance of mediation analysis was tested using the Bootstrap method with 5,000 repetitions. Two types of mediation analyses were performed: (1) to examine whether the ALPS index mediates the pathway from ChP volume to MMSE and neuroimaging measures, with ChP volume as the independent variable, MMSE and neuroimaging measures as the dependent variables, and the ALPS index as the mediator; (2) to examine whether PET measurements of the ChP and brain regions mediate the pathway from ChP volume to CSF pathological protein levels, with ChP volume as the independent variable, CSF pathological protein levels as the dependent variable, and PET measurements as the mediator.

RESULTS

Demographic and clinical characteristics

We first characterized baseline demographic, cognitive, and biomarker differences across the AD continuum to provide context for subsequent analyses. This large cohort (n = 968) comprised cognitively NC (n = 382), MCI (n = 433), and AD dementia patients (n = 153) (Table S2). The groups showed significant differences in age and sex distribution (both p < 0.001). AD patients were slightly older on average (median 75 years, vs 72 in NC and MCI) and had a higher proportion of males (63.40% in AD, vs 41.10–54.73% in NC/MCI). Education level also differed modestly across groups (p = 0.042).

Cognitive performance declined progressively with disease stage, as expected (p < 0.001). The AD group had the lowest MMSE scores and the highest proportion of participants with a CDR ≥ 1 (about 57% in AD, compared to none in NC). Consistent with clinical severity, CSF biomarkers showed the hallmark AD profile (p < 0.001 for all). AD patients had markedly lower CSF Aβ42 levels (median 386.30 pg/mL) and higher tau levels (t‐tau 238.00 pg/mL, p‐tau 39.35 pg/mL) than NC and MCI, reflecting greater Aβ deposition and neurodegeneration. These baseline differences in demographics, cognition, and CSF pathology establish the clinical context for interpreting neuroimaging findings. (Detailed characteristics of sub‐cohorts used for amyloid‐PET, tau‐PET, and ALPS analyses are provided in Table S3–S5).

ChP and ventricular volumes: ChP volume increased significantly with disease stage (p < 0.001). Mean ChP volume was 1.63 cm3 in NC, 1.83 cm3 in MCI, and 1.99 cm3 in AD, indicating progressive ChP enlargement from normal aging to dementia. The ventricular system also expanded in later stages. Lateral ventricle volume was 28.49 cm3 in NC, increasing to 33.97 cm3 in MCI and 45.36 cm3 in AD (p < 0.001). Third ventricle volume similarly enlarged (1.46 cm3 in NC to 1.91 cm3 in AD, p < 0.001). Fourth ventricle volume did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.389).

ChP and ventricular tracer distribution: PET signal in the ChP itself declined with advancing disease. ChP amyloid PET SUVR decreased from 0.86 in NC to 0.78 in AD (p < 0.001), and ChP tau PET SUVR was lower in AD (2.70) than NC (3.35, p = 0.009). Thus, the enlarged ChP in AD showed diminished distribution of both amyloid and tau tracers. Likewise, CSF spaces showed reduced tracer distribution in disease. The lateral ventricle amyloid SUVR dropped from 0.76 (NC) to 0.66 (AD, p < 0.001), indicating less free tracer diffusing into ventricles as amyloid accumulates in the brain. Tau PET signal in ventricles also showed subtle reductions: for example, lateral ventricle tau SUVR was slightly lower in AD (0.19) vs NC (0.22, p = 0.013), and third and fourth ventricle tau SUVR values trended lower with disease (p = 0.001 each for group differences). These patterns indicate that the progression of AD is accompanied by an increase in ChP volume alongside a reduced distribution of PET tracers within the ChP itself and the ventricular system.

Cortical pathology and brain volumes: As expected, AD patients exhibited substantially higher pathological protein burdens in the brain parenchyma. The mean cortical amyloid PET SUVR rose from 1.04 in NC to 1.47 in AD (p < 0.001), and the proportion of amyloid‐positive individuals increased from 31.91% (NC) to 84.06% (AD). Cortical tau PET SUVR similarly increased (1.11 in NC to 1.42 in AD, p < 0.001), reflecting heavy tau accumulation in AD cortex. These molecular changes coincided with structural atrophy. Total cortical volume was significantly reduced in AD (mean 260.53 cm3) compared to NC (282.17 cm3, p < 0.001). White matter volume showed a mild increase in MCI relative to NC, followed by a slight decline in AD (overall group difference p = 0.025). Notably, white matter amyloid SUVR increased slightly yet significantly in AD (1.72 vs 1.64 in NC, p < 0.001), whereas white matter tau SUVR did not differ across groups (p = 0.128). In summary, cross‐sectional imaging reveals that AD is characterized by an enlarged ChP and ventricles, reduced tracer distribution in these CSF‐related structures, and markedly elevated cortical amyloid/tau with accompanying brain atrophy.

Identifying predictors of AD progression from neuroimaging

We further investigated which imaging measures are significantly associated with AD progression. To this end, we employed multivariable‐ordinal logistic regression with disease stage (NC, MCI, AD) as the dependent variable. After adjusting for age, sex, and years of education, we identified neuroimaging predictors (Table S8). An odds ratio (OR) > 1 indicates that a one‐unit increase in the imaging measure raises the odds of being in a later disease stage, whereas an OR < 1 suggests a protective association.

Several ChP and ventricular metrics emerged as significant stage‐related factors. Larger ChP volume was strongly associated with disease progression (OR = 2.42, p < 0.001). Similarly, ventricular enlargement predicted a more severe disease stage: increased lateral ventricle volume was associated with higher odds of a later stage (OR = 1.02, p < 0.001), and third ventricle volume was also a significant predictor (OR = 1.90, p < 0.001). Conversely, greater amyloid distribution in CSF‐related compartments and ChP was protective. Higher ChP amyloid SUVR was significantly associated with lower odds of a later stage (OR = 0.06, p < 0.001); elevated lateral ventricle amyloid SUVR also showed a protective effect (OR = 0.09, p < 0.001). Ventricular tau SUVR similarly demonstrated protective associations (e.g., lateral ventricle tau SUVR OR = 0.01, p = 0.010). As expected, cortical pathology measures were among the strongest predictors. Increased total cortical amyloid SUVR corresponded to an OR = 22.91 (p < 0.001), and elevated cortical tau SUVR corresponded to an OR = 12.26 (p < 0.001). White matter amyloid SUVR could also independently differentiate disease stages (OR = 3.78, p < 0.001).

To verify whether the associations of ChP and ventricular PET signals with AD stages were independent of overall parenchymal tracer uptake, we performed another ordinal regression after including total cortical and white matter PET SUVR as confounding variables (Table S9). After this adjustment, ChP amyloid SUVR remained significantly protective (OR = 0.22, p = 0.010); lateral and third ventricle amyloid SUVR also retained significance (OR = 0.24, p = 0.010; OR = 0.22, p = 0.020, respectively). ChP tau SUVR remained protective (OR = 0.80, p = 0.040), whereas the protective effects of ventricular tau SUVRs were not statistically significant (all p > 0.05). In summary, these ordinal models indicate that, after controlling for confounders, ChP enlargement and decreased tracer distribution in CSF compartments are significant markers of progression across the AD continuum.

To understand how ChP remodeling might impact the broader AD pathology network, we examined associations between ChP volume and key clinical/imaging measures. Using multivariate linear regression (adjusted for age, sex, and years of education), we tested whether ChP enlargement correlates with cognitive decline, ventricular expansion, and cortical pathology in our cohort (Figure 2, Table S10). ChP volume vs. ChP tracer distribution and cognition: A larger ChP volume was linked to diminished distribution of PET tracer within the ChP itself (β = −0.17 per unit for ChP amyloid SUVR, p < 0.001; and β = −1.10 for ChP tau SUVR, p < 0.001). In parallel, ChP volume enlargement was associated with worse cognitive performance: for each unit increase in ChP volume, the MMSE score dropped by roughly 1.24 points on average (β = −1.24, p < 0.001). Thus, patients with an abnormally large ChP tended to have greater cognitive impairment.

ChP volume vs. ventricular structure and tracer distribution: ChP volume showed strong positive associations with lateral ventricle size (β = 22.78 cm3 per unit, p < 0.001), third ventricle size (β = 0.56 cm3, p < 0.001), and fourth ventricle size (β = 0.26 cm3, p < 0.001). In other words, patients with an enlarged ChP tended to have enlarged ventricles, suggesting that ventricular changes may be related to ChP remodeling. At the same time, larger ChP volume was associated with significantly lower PET tracer levels in the ventricular CSF. Amyloid SUVR in the lateral ventricle declined with increasing ChP size (β = −0.16, p < 0.001), and similar negative associations were seen for amyloid SUVR in the third (β = −0.07) and fourth ventricles (β = −0.03, both p < 0.001). A higher ChP volume also corresponded to lower tau PET signal in the lateral ventricle (β = −0.07, p < 0.001), although its relationship with tau in the third/fourth ventricles was not significant. These findings suggest that ChP remodeling may be linked to ventricular tracer distribution in the ventricles.

ChP volume vs. cortical pathology and brain volume: Importantly, ChP changes were tied to the burden of pathological proteins in the brain. A larger ChP volume was significantly associated with greater cortical amyloid deposition (β = 0.08 for total cortical amyloid SUVR, p < 0.001) and higher cortical tau burden (β = 0.15 for cortical tau SUVR, p = 0.015). Thus, patients with enlarge ChP tended to have more severe amyloid plaques and tau tangles in the cortex, linking ChP pathology to parenchymal AD pathology. Notably, ChP volume was not directly related to overall cortical volume (p = 0.631), indicating that the association between ChP and cognitive decline or pathology is not simply through gross brain atrophy. In white matter, ChP volume showed a mild positive correlation with white matter volume (β = 8.39, p = 0.039) but no significant association with white matter amyloid or tau uptake. Overall, these regression results suggest that ChP enlargement is intricately connected to key features of AD: it accompanies ventricular dilation and reduced PET tracer distribution, and it parallels both cognitive decline and increasing cortical protein aggregates.

Glymphatic function across groups and relation to ChP: Consistent with an overall clearance deficit in AD, the ALPS index showed a stepwise decline with disease progression. NC had the highest ALPS index (1.52), which dropped to 1.41 in MCI and further to 1.20 in AD (p < 0.001 for trend) (Figure 3B). Thus, glymphatic efficiency was significantly reduced even at the MCI stage and worst in AD dementia. Importantly, individuals with larger ChP volumes tended to have lower ALPS indices. In a regression model adjusting for age and sex, ChP volume was inversely associated with glymphatic function (β = −0.31, 95% CI: −0.44 to −0.18, p < 0.001) (Figure 3C). In practical terms, a enlarge ChP was linked to poorer perivascular CSF flow, suggesting that ChP pathology may hinder glymphatic clearance.

Mediation by glymphatic dysfunction: We next asked if the impaired glymphatic system could explain (mediate) some of the impact of ChP changes on the brain. Mediation analysis (adjusted for age and sex) was applied to outcomes including cognition (MMSE score), cortical pathology, brain volume, and ventricle size (Figure 3D). The ALPS index had significant ACME in several of these relationships. For instance, part of the effect of ChP enlargement on cognitive performance was mediated by glymphatic dysfunction: ChP volume indirectly contributed to lower MMSE scores via reducing the ALPS index (ACME β = −1.01, p = 0.044). Similarly, glymphatic impairment mediated a portion of the association between ChP volume and cortical amyloid burden (ACME β = 0.11 for cortical amyloid SUVR, p = 0.014), as well as ChP's links to total cortical volume (ACME β = −8.69 cm3, p = 0.020) and lateral ventricle volume (ACME β = 5.15 cm3, p < 0.001). In all these cases, the direction of mediation was consistent with expectations (e.g., ChP enlargement leads to glymphatic failure, which in turn leads to greater amyloid accumulation, more brain atrophy, and ventricle expansion). Even after accounting for glymphatic mediation, ChP volume still had significant direct effects on certain outcomes, highlighting that multiple pathways are at play. For example, ChP volume exerted a direct influence on cortical amyloid levels independent of ALPS (ADE β = −2.83, p = 0.010) and on ventricular metrics (e.g., direct effect on lateral ventricle volume β = +16.40 cm3, p = 0.008, and on ventricular amyloid SUVR β = −0.16, p = 0.002). These direct paths may reflect other clearance routes or processes not captured by the ALPS measure. In summary, the mediation analysis supports that glymphatic dysfunction is a significant intermediary through which ChP pathology can aggravate amyloid deposition, brain atrophy, and cognitive decline, although ChP abnormalities also directly contribute to clearance failure and ventricular changes.

CSF Aβ42 dynamics: In our cohort, CSF Aβ42 levels were lowest in AD, reflecting sequestration of Aβ in plaques. Mediation analysis indicated that the effect of ChP volume on CSF Aβ42 is primarily driven by cortical amyloid accumulation rather than ChP's own clearance function (Figure 4A). A larger ChP (as seen in AD) was associated with more amyloid deposited in the cortex, which in turn corresponded to lower Aβ42 in CSF (significant indirect effect via cortical amyloid SUVR, ACME = −76.10, p < 0.001). By contrast, although the mediation path through ChP amyloid tracer distribution indicated a positive relationship, it was not significant (ACME = 18.19, p = 0.61). The total effect of ChP volume on CSF Aβ42 was significant and negative (total effect β = −252.22, p < 0.001), consistent with the notion that patients with enlarge ChP have substantially lower CSF Aβ42.

CSF tau (t‐tau and p‐tau) dynamics: Unlike Aβ, CSF tau markers increase with disease progression. Our analyses revealed that ChP remodeling influences CSF tau levels via both reduced ChP clearance and increased cortical tau burden (Figure 4B–C). For CSF total tau, a larger ChP volume was linked to elevated t‐tau in CSF by a significant mediation effect through ChP tau PET tracer distribution (ACME = 12.08, p = 0.036). Concurrently, ChP enlargement led to greater tau accumulation in the brain, which also drove up CSF tau (mediation through cortical tau SUVR: ACME = 20.57, p = 0.008). Both indirect paths were significant, indicating dual contributions. A very similar pattern was observed for CSF p‐tau: the association between ChP volume and higher p‐tau was mediated by both decreased ChP tau PET tracer distribution (ACME = 1.37, p = 0.040) and increased cortical tau pathology (ACME = 2.61, p = 0.014). In all cases, the total effects of ChP volume on CSF tau were significant (e.g., total effect β = −32.79 for t‐tau, p = 0.018, and β = −3.56 for p‐tau, p = 0.038). Notably, the direct effects (ADE) of ChP volume on tau remained significant even after accounting for mediation, implying there may be additional factors linking ChP changes to tau levels (e.g., ChP‐driven clearance pathways via the meningeal lymphatic vessels and olfactory bulb). In summary, ChP remodeling appears to influence the levels of pathological proteins in CSF by affecting its own state and the pathological protein burden in the brain parenchyma.

Association of key in vivo MRI phenotypes

We investigated the in vivo MRI phenotypes of the ChP, ventricular system, and glymphatic function in APP/PS1 mice using high‐field MRI (Figure 5B–C; Figure S1A,C,D,F). At both 6 and 12 months of age, APP/PS1 mice exhibited larger ChP volumes compared to age‐matched WT mice (p < 0.05). Aging itself also led to ChP enlargement: 12‐month‐old mice showed increased ChP volume relative to 6‐month‐old mice in both genotypes (p < 0.05). Ventricular volume changes in mice mirrored the age‐related expansion pattern seen in humans: 12‐month‐old mice had significantly larger ventricles than 6‐month‐old mice (p < 0.05), whereas APP/PS1 mice tended to have slightly larger ventricles than WT mice at each age point. Meanwhile, the mALPS index was significantly reduced by both AD pathology and aging. At 6 months, APP/PS1 mice already displayed a lower mALPS index than WT controls (p < 0.05). By 12 months, the mALPS index declined further in both APP/PS1 and WT mice (p < 0.05). Notably, although the decline in the mALPS index tended to progress more rapidly in females, male and female mice exhibited similar trends. Crucially, the mouse data recapitulated the correlations between ChP volume, ventricular volume, and glymphatic function observed in human studies (Figure 5D; Figure S1B,E). ChP volume was positively correlated with total ventricular volume (p < 0.001). Conversely, ChP volume was negatively correlated with the mean mALPS index (p < 0.001).

Key molecular alterations

After confirming the in vivo MRI associations between ChP and the CSF‐mediated clearance structure, we turned our focus to key molecular changes underlying these phenotypes. Using immunofluorescence, we examined key astrocyte proteins involved in glymphatic function (Figure 5E–F). AQP4, a water channel on astrocyte endfeet, and β‐DG, which helps anchor AQP4 at the perivascular membrane, are critical for glymphatic flow. Immunofluorescence revealed that expression levels of AQP4 and β‐DG were significantly lower in 12‐month‐old mice compared to 6‐month‐old mice (p < 0.05), and APP/PS1 mice showed reduced expression relative to WT littermates at both time points (p < 0.05).

Beyond expression changes, the localization of AQP4 to perivascular astrocytic endfeet was also altered. In 6‐month‐old mice, AQP4 was highly polarized at vascular interfaces (co‐localizing with vascular markers such as lectin and with β‐DG in the endfeet). In contrast, 12‐month‐old and APP/PS1 mice exhibited redistribution of AQP4 away from the vasculature (Figure 5E). The degree of co‐localization between AQP4 and vascular markers was significantly reduced in older mice compared to younger mice and in APP/PS1 mice compared to WT mice (Pearson's correlations of AQP4 with β‐DG and with lectin were decreased, p < 0.05). Furthermore, reactive astrogliosis was evident: GFAP intensity (a marker of astrocyte activation) was significantly elevated in 12‐month‐old mice compared to 6‐month‐old mice and in APP/PS1 mice compared to WT mice (p < 0.05 for both) (Figure S2).

Subsequently, we used immunofluorescence to examine the expression of major ion and water transport proteins in the ChP epithelium, as well as the key Aβ transport receptor LRP1 (Figure 6). The results showed significant downregulation of several transporters with both aging and AD pathology (Figure 6A–C, F). Specifically, APP/PS1 mice exhibited reduced expression of TRPV4, KCC3, and AQP1 compared to age‐matched WT controls at both 6 and 12 months (p < 0.05). An age effect was also observed: 12‐month‐old mice displayed significantly lower expression of TRPV4, KCC3, and AQP1 in the ChP compared to 6‐month‐old mice (p < 0.05). Regarding LRP1, overall LRP1 expression in the ChP declined with age. Interestingly, 6‐month‐old APP/PS1 mice showed higher LRP1 levels in the ChP than age‐matched WT mice. However, by 12 months, APP/PS1 mice exhibited a decline in LRP1, falling significantly below the levels in age‐matched WT mice.

PCR quantification of mRNA levels in brain parenchyma and ChP for the above proteins further validated these trends (Figure S3). Regarding Aβ distribution, Aβ burden in the brain parenchyma increased significantly from 6 to 12 months of age, while its distribution within the ChP decreased, and Aβ in the CSF also declined markedly (Figure 6E,G; Figure S4). Notably, although female mice tended to exhibit faster progression, male and female mice exhibited similar trends. Changes in Aβ distribution coincided with alterations in LRP1 expression. Within the ChP, regions with high LRP1 immunoreactivity exhibited strong colocalization with Aβ, and a positive correlation was found between the total fluorescence intensity of Aβ and LRP1 in the ChP (Figure 6G). Moreover, the MD of water molecules in the ChP decreased significantly with AD progression and showed a positive correlation with the MD of water molecules in the ventricular system (Figure S5).

DISCUSSION

This work reveals extensive associations of the ChP with the CSF‐mediated clearance system, thereby defining it as a primary driver of AD progression. By integrating large‐scale human multimodal imaging data with experimental mouse data, we systematically characterize the remodeling features of the ChP across the AD continuum and explore their links with CSF clearance‐related markers, CSF pathological protein levels, parenchymal AD pathology, and cognitive function. Our findings demonstrate that ChP remodeling emerges early in the disease course, associates with ventricular enlargement, a reduced ALPS index, higher cortical amyloid/tau burden, and worse cognitive performance. Converging multimodal and cross‐species evidence supports a working model in which ChP remodeling is closely tied to broad alterations in the CSF‐mediated clearance system in AD, potentially serving as a critical nexus in these clearance processes.

Across the clinical continuum, ChP volume increases in step with disease stage, while ChP amyloid and tau tracer distribution decline; both patterns are evident in the cross‐sectional comparisons and directly visualized in the stage‐stratified panels (Figure 1D, Figure 2A; Table 1). Multivariable regression and risk modeling indicate that these ChP changes are not epiphenomena of aging: ChP enlargement behaves as an independent indicator of clinical progression (Table S8), and larger ChP volume associates with worse cognition and more advanced parenchymal pathology (Figure 2B,G–H). These in vivo signatures align with prior histopathology describing amyloid and tau inclusions, epithelial degeneration, and reduced CSF production in the ChP, 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 , 46 supporting the interpretation that reduced PET signal in the ChP may not merely represent incidental “off‑target binding” but rather could serve as a potential indicator of its functional status. 46

Ventricular readouts reflect CSF‐compartment signature adjacent to the ChP. Larger ChP volume tracks with expansion of the ventricular system and, importantly, with reduced tracer influx to the ventricles (Figures 1A–B, Figure 2C–E; Table 1). This macroscopic coupling suggests a close relationship between ChP remodeling and alterations in ventricular structure, accompanied by deficits in tracer redistribution. While prior dynamic PET studies connected ventricular tracer kinetics to cortical amyloid burden in AD, 24 , 25 our data support positioning the ChP as a key link in this association and raise a plausible hypothesis: the organ that both produces CSF and hosts the BCSFB shows structural hypertrophy and functional deficits precisely when ventricular inflow measurements deteriorate. The resulting picture is of a clearance engine losing both its pump (ChP) and its plumbing (ventricular system).

The glymphatic system, which reflects the transport pathways adjacent to the brain parenchyma, serves as a conduit between CSF spaces and the ISF compartments of brain tissue. Using the ALPS index as a non‐invasive proxy of glymphatic function, we observe a graded decline across diagnostic groups and a robust inverse relationship to ChP volume (Figure 3B–C; Table S11). Mediation analysis formalizes this link: decrements in ALPS significantly account for the way ChP enlargement relates to cognitive performance, cortical amyloid status and volume, and ventricular enlargement (Figure 3D). This statistical pathway dovetails with reports that ChP abnormalities and reduced lymphatic/glymphatic clearance co‐occur in AD, 27 , 47 , 48 and it suggests a plausible biophysical driver, impaired glymphatic flow leading to compromised CSF‐ISF exchange, between ChP remodeling and brain parenchymal pathology.

The statistical results indicate a partial mediation by the glymphatic system, implying that the influence of the ChP on disease progression also involves other pathways, such as meningeal lymphatics and arachnoid granulations/villi‐related clearance processes, which are closely related to CSF dynamics and may thus be affected by ChP remodeling. 28 , 49 Beyond AD, 50 the association between enlarged ChP and reduced glymphatic function has been widely reported in other neurological disorders 51 and psychiatric disorders. 52 Our study further demonstrates that this in‐vivo MRI‐derived phenotypic correlation is also observed in AD‐model mice. This indicates that the association is robust and generalizable, thereby representing a key component of CSF‐mediated clearance processes.

It is worth noting that both ChP alterations and the ALPS decline are already apparent at the transition from normal cognition to MCI (Figures 1, 2, 3), consistent with suggestions that glymphatic impairment can precede overt parenchymal atrophy and heavy plaque/tangle burden. 53 , 54

CSF biomarker analyses further explored how ChP pathology perturbs protein homeostasis. This hypothetical mediation model incorporates two classes of mediating variables: ChP Aβ/tau PET SUVR and total cortical Aβ/tau PET SUVR. Interesting observations emerge when examining the mediation effect sizes (Figure 4A–C): enlarged ChP mediates positive effects on CSF Aβ42, t‑tau, and p‑tau via its own reduced PET SUVR, suggesting that this pathway elevates CSF levels. In this hypothetical framework, diminished tracer signal at the ChP is interpreted as reflecting impaired clearance, an interpretation which aligns with the statistical finding of increased pathological protein levels in CSF. Conversely, enlarged ChP mediates negative effects on Aβ but positive effects on tau via increased total cortical PET SUVR.

Moreover, a temporal dissociation is evident, with earlier shifts in Aβ42 and later shifts in t‑tau/p‑tau (Figure 4A–C). We speculate that this aligns with the pathology of extracellular Aβ aggregation versus largely intracellular tau that is released upon neuronal injury, where the late surge in tau released from extensive neuronal injury overwhelms the diminished clearance via impaired ISF–CSF exchange. 55 , 56 It should be noted that the above interpretation of ChP signals is based on a hypothetical statistical model; until further human pathological evidence and causal support become available, caution is warranted in interpreting ChP tracer signal.

Mouse experiments provide cross‐species validation of key MRI phenotypes and explore associated molecular changes. In APP/PS1 animals, ChP enlargement is present at both earlier and later stages, while the mALPS index is reduced in parallel (Figure 5B–C). Correlation analyses recapitulate the human links between larger ChP, altered ventricular morphology, and poorer glymphatic function. At the molecular level, ChP expression of ion‐water transporters (TRPV4, KCC3, AQP1) and the Aβ transporter LRP1 is downregulated in AD mice, consistent with impaired CSF secretion and reduced Aβ translocation across the BCSFB (Figure 6A–E).

Previous contradictory reports 57 , 58 of either upregulated or downregulated LRP1 in the ChP may reflect disparities in experimental models and conditions, distinct stages of compensatory and decompensatory responses, or more intricate regulatory mechanisms. A recent single‑cell sequencing study of APP/PS1 mice corroborates our findings, 59 reporting early downregulation of barrier integrity and secretory genes in ChP epithelial cells. Moreover, the alterations in the ChP and glymphatic system tended to progress more rapidly in females, suggesting that both may be influenced by sex‐specific factors. These changes offer important molecular clues for investigating whether BCSFB dysfunction precedes overt ventricular enlargement, 17 , 60 and for explaining how ionic imbalance and diminished convective flow would suppress perivascular exchange and slow solute clearance. 61 , 62 , 63 , 64 , 65

Taken together, these results appear to support a unifying working model in which ChP remodeling serves as a key node along the CSF‐mediated clearance pathway and is closely linked to other nodes in this process (Figure 7). The sequence is internally consistent across modalities: (i) the ChP enlarges and undergoes functional decline (Figure 1D, Figure 2A); (ii) ventricular flow signatures deteriorate and ventricles expand under the influence of ChP pathology (Figures 1D, 2C–E); (iii) glymphatic transport weakens (Figure 3B–C), mediating the impact of ChP pathology on cognition, cortical pathology, and ventricular structure (Figure 3D); (iv) CSF biomarker homeostasis shifts through both impaired ChP function and deteriorated parenchymal‐to‐CSF exchange balance (Figure 4); and (v) key MRI phenotypes are reproduced (Figure 5A–D) with molecular specificity (Figure 5, Figure 6, Figure S1; immunofluorescence figure) in mice. This multi‐tiered agreement argues that the ChP may be a key factor shaping disease progression. It is important to note, however, that this working model requires further validation through human pathological evidence and causal animal experimental data.

Working model of CSF‐mediated clearance. CSF‐mediated clearance relies on intact CP, ventricular system, and glymphatic functions. The CP forms the blood‐CSF barrier, serving as a critical interface for extensive solute exchange between the brain and vasculature, and is the primary site of CSF production. Generated CSF establishes positive pressure and modulates solute concentration, driving its flow through the ventricular system and intracranial spaces. The glymphatic system facilitates exchange between CSF and brain interstitial fluid, enabling pathological proteins within the ISF to enter the CSF. Subsequently, these pathological proteins are cleared across the blood‐CSF barrier. Furthermore, elevated expression of the LRP1 transporter at the CP mediates efficient trans‐barrier clearance of CSF Aβ and tau into the bloodstream. These insights carry practical implications. First, they motivate biomarkers that center the ChP: high‐resolution volumetry and region‐specific PET readouts can detect early structural‐functional compromise. At the same time, ALPS can monitor glymphatic function. Second, they suggest therapeutic angles upstream of plaque/tangle formation: restoring ChP transport capacity, stabilizing BCSFB integrity, and augmenting CSF turnover could re‐establish the pressure gradients and convective forces the glymphatic system needs to clear toxic proteins.

We note limitations that contextualize these conclusions. Imaging was multi‐site and multi‐scanner, although harmonization and covariate control were applied; the ALPS index is a surrogate of glymphatic function rather than a direct flow measurement; and ChP PET signals remain debated as potential off‐target phenomena 43 despite mounting histological support. 41 , 44 , 45 , 46 Additionally, the APP/PS1 model used here does not recapitulate the tau pathology characteristic. Even so, the triangulation across independent modalities, endpoints, and species makes a compelling case for the centrality of the ChP in AD pathogenesis. Longitudinal and interventional studies, particularly those combining ChP‐targeted modulation and glymphatically impaired models with in vivo multimodal imaging, will be decisive next steps.

In summary, ChP remodeling is associated with ventricular system characteristics, glymphatic function, pathological protein levels in CSF, brain pathological burden, and cognitive impairment across the AD continuum. This study positions the ChP as a critical nexus in AD. Recognizing and targeting this nexus, diagnostically and therapeutically, offers a path to alter disease trajectories by repairing the brain's clearance machinery itself.
