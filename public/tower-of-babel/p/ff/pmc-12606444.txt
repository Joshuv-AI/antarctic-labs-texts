Efficacy of a multidomain intervention in older adults with vascular risks: The Japan‐Multimodal Intervention Trial for the Prevention of Dementia (J‐MINT)
Authors: Taiki Sugimoto, Kazuaki Uchida, Yoko Yokoyama, Ayaka Onoyama, Hisashi Noma, Hidenori Arai, Takashi Sakurai, J‐MINT study group
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONWhether untreated or uncontrolled vascular risk factors (VRFs), such as blood pressure, glycated hemoglobin, and high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) and non‐HDL cholesterol levels, modify the effects of multidomain interventions on cognitive decline in mild cognitive impairment (MCI) was examined.METHODSParticipants aged 65 to 85 years with MCI were randomized into intervention or control groups. BACKGROUND

As the global population ages, the increasing prevalence of dementia has become a major societal concern. 1 Despite considerable progress in the development of Alzheimer's disease (AD) therapies, 2 challenges like drug‐related adverse effects and limited applicability across diverse patient populations remain, 3 highlighting the need for non‐pharmacological interventions to prevent dementia. Dementia is influenced by a complex interplay of risk factors and mechanisms. To date, 14 modifiable risk factors have been identified, and interventions targeting these factors may prevent or delay dementia onset by as much as 45%. 4 Consequently, to prevent cognitive decline or dementia, multidomain intervention studies that simultaneously focus on addressing mechanisms and risk factors are ongoing globally. 5 Although some studies have demonstrated significant effects on primary outcomes, 6 − 10 others have not, 11 − 15 resulting in inconsistent findings.

Current evidence suggests that certain subgroups may derive greater benefits from multidomain interventions. For example, individuals at high risk for dementia, that is, those with the apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 allele 12 , 14 , 16 or those who test positive for dementia‐related biomarkers, 12 , 14 appear to benefit more from these interventions. From a societal implementation perspective, it is crucial to target these high‐risk individuals. However, the widespread implementation of genetic testing and biomarker analysis faces significant challenges, including high costs, insufficient evidence on biomarker performance across diverse populations, and ethical concerns. 17 , 18 Conversely, several studies have demonstrated the benefits of multidomain interventions among individuals with poorly controlled vascular risk factors (VRFs), such as untreated or uncontrolled hypertension or diabetes. 7 , 11 Subgroup analyses from the Japan‐Multidomain Intervention Trial for Prevention of Dementia in Older Adults with Diabetes study, which focused on outpatients with type 2 diabetes, also observed cognitive improvements in the intervention group with poor glycemic control at baseline. 15 These findings indicate that objectively assessing untreated or uncontrolled VRFs, such as hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia, rather than relying solely on medical history or treatment records, may more effectively identify populations that are likely to benefit from multidomain interventions in the phase of societal implementation.

In this study, a secondary analysis of the Japan‐Multimodal Intervention Trial for the Prevention of Dementia (J‐MINT) 14 was conducted to determine whether the efficacy of multidomain intervention differs according to the presence of untreated or uncontrolled VRFs, which were assessed objectively. Accordingly, this study aimed to clarify the effect of such interventions on VRFs in individuals with these factors. In Japan, screening for VRFs is routinely performed from midlife through old age to promote health and prevent the need for long‐term care. Thus, identifying targets that are more likely to benefit from these VRF‐based interventions could not only enhance the quality of life of the participants and their families but also provide valuable insights for health‐care professionals and policy makers.

METHODS

Study design

This secondary analysis was derived from the J‐MINT study, an 18 month, multicenter, randomized controlled trial conducted across five independent institutions. The study protocol and primary findings have been detailed in previous reports. 14 , 19 All participants were thoroughly informed about the study's objectives and associated risks before enrollment, and each provided written informed consent. The study procedures received approval from the institutional review boards of all participating institutions.

Participants

The J‐MINT study enrolled 531 individuals aged 65 to 85 years, who were diagnosed with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) based on criteria incorporating age, education, and neuropsychological test performance. Participants with a Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) score < 24, 20 or those who were unable to safely engage in physical exercise, were excluded. 19

This secondary analysis included participants who attended at least one session of the intervention program or received general health guidance, completed one or more post‐baseline neuropsychological assessments, and had available baseline data on VRFs, including systolic and diastolic blood pressure, glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), and non–HDL‐C.

Intervention

The intervention and control groups received treatment for diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia from their primary care physicians, in accordance with relevant clinical practice guidelines in Japan. Both groups were provided with the results of their blood tests and brain imaging (magnetic resonance imaging and computed tomography), and were advised to consult a physician if further evaluation was deemed necessary. 19 In addition, the control group received written general health‐related information every 2 months. 19

The intervention group also participated in a multidomain program that included group‐based physical exercise, nutritional counseling, and cognitive training. The physical exercise sessions were conducted once weekly for 90 minutes and encompassed activities such as stretching, strength training, aerobic exercise, dual‐task challenges, and group discussions. In total, participants attended 78 sessions during the study period. Nutritional counseling was delivered individually by certified health consultants through three face‐to‐face sessions of 60 minutes each and 12 telephone sessions over the intervention period. Moreover, participants were provided with customized cognitive training using the Brain HQ (Posit Science Corporation), specifically adapted for the J‐MINT trial. This cognitive training was organized into three intensive 3 ‐month phases, and participants were advised to complete at least 30 minutes per day on a minimum of 4 days each week. 19

Untreated or uncontrolled VRFs

To identify participants with untreated or uncontrolled vascular risk, the presence of hypertension, hyperglycemia, and dyslipidemia was defined based on objective markers regardless of medical history or medication use. Hypertension was defined as a resting systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mm Hg or a diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mm Hg. 21 Hyperglycemia was determined by an HbA1c level of ≥ 6.5%. 22 Dyslipidemia was defined as either a non–HDL‐C level of ≥ 170 mg/dL or an HDL‐C level < 40 mg/dL. 23 For analysis, individuals with any one of these conditions were considered to have untreated or uncontrolled VRFs, irrespective of medication use.

Measurements

Cognitive function

The main outcome of this analysis was the change in the average Z scores from baseline to the 18 month follow‐up. These scores were derived from a battery of neuropsychological tests, including the MMSE, 20 Logical Memory I and II subsets of the Wechsler Memory Scale‐Revised, 24 free and cued selective reminding test, 25 digit span from the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS)‐III, 26 Trail Making Test (TMT), 27 Digit Symbol Substitution Test subset of WAIS‐III, 26 and a letter fluency test. 27 The Z scores for each test were calculated using baseline means and standard deviations from all participants included in the secondary analysis. For the TMT, inverse Z scores were used, as lower scores on this test indicate better cognitive function.

Blood pressure and laboratory markers

Systolic and diastolic blood pressures and pulse pressure, along with several laboratory markers, were measured at baseline and the 18 month follow‐up. These markers included HbA1c, fasting glucose, insulin, homeostasis model assessment insulin resistance (HOMA‐IR), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), HDL‐C, triglycerides (TGs), and non–HDL‐C. HOMA‐IR was calculated using the following formula: HOMA‐IR = fasting insulin (µU/mL) × fasting glucose (mg/dL)/405. TG to HDL‐C ratio (TG/HDL‐C) was calculated by dividing the TG level by the HDL‐C level. Additionally, we assessed dementia‐related plasma biomarkers, including amyloid beta (Aβ)1‐40/Aβ1‐42, tau phosphorylated at threonine 181 (p‐tau181), neurofilament light chain (NfL), and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP). 19

Other measurements

At baseline, participant characteristics such as age, sex, education, comorbidities, medications, and smoking status were assessed by a self‐reported questionnaire. 19 Height and weight were measured and used to calculate the body mass index.

Randomization and masking

Participants were centrally randomized in a 1:1 ratio to either the multidomain intervention or control group using the dynamic allocation system developed by the trial statistician. 19 Randomization was stratified by four variables: (1) age at enrollment (65−74 vs. 75−85 years), (2) sex (female vs. male), (3) MMSE score (24−27 vs. 28−30), and (4) memory impairment (amnestic vs. non‐amnestic). The research staff responsible for the primary outcome assessments was blinded to the group assignments and was not involved in the intervention. However, intervention providers and participants were aware of the assigned groups. 19 ,

Statistical methods

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables were reported as means and standard deviations. Baseline characteristics were compared between participants with and without VRFs using the Kruskal−Wallis test or χ2 test.

To evaluate the efficacy of the multidomain interventions in preventing cognitive decline and to assess whether untreated or uncontrolled VRFs influenced its efficacy, a mixed‐effects model for repeated measures (MMRM) was used. 28 The main outcome was the change in the average Z scores. The model included the following covariates as fixed effects: age at randomization (65−74 vs. 75−85 years), sex (male vs female), years of education, memory impairment status (amnestic vs. non‐amnestic), APOE genotype (0 vs. ≥ 1 ε4 allele), and baseline cognitive score. All two‐way and three‐way interaction terms between intervention allocation, untreated or uncontrolled VRFs, and time visits were also included. An unstructured covariance structure was used, and the Kenward−Roger correction method was applied to adjust confidence intervals (CIs) and p values. 29

As subgroup analyses, the intervention effect within subgroups with each untreated or uncontrolled VRF was examined by incorporating a two‐way interaction term between intervention allocation and time visit, adjusting for the same covariates as in the primary model.

Furthermore, whether the intervention effect varied by the presence or absence of VRFs determined based on medical history, medication use, and objective markers was investigated by incorporating both two‐way and three‐way interaction terms between intervention allocations, VRFs, and time visits. The intervention effects within the subgroups with each VRF determined based on medical history, medication use, and objective markers were also examined.

To examine the effects of multidomain interventions on blood pressure and laboratory markers among participants with untreated or uncontrolled VRFs, multiple linear regression analyses were performed. Outcome variables were changes in blood pressure and laboratory markers from baseline to the 18 month follow‐up. The covariates were the age at enrollment, sex, memory impairment status, baseline cognitive composite score, APOE genotype, and baseline outcome values.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). All p values were two sided, and the significance levels were set at p < 0.05.

RESULTS

Characteristics of the participants

Of the 531 participants enrolled in the J‐MINT study, 81 withdrew after randomization, 17 had no evaluations after baseline, and 1 had missing blood pressure values at baseline, resulting in the inclusion of 432 participants (Figure 1). Of these participants, 298 had at least one untreated or uncontrolled VRF. Table 1 presents the characteristics of the participants with and without untreated or uncontrolled VRFs. The prevalence of untreated or uncontrolled VRFs was the highest for hypertension (n = 226, 52.3%), followed by dyslipidemia (n = 125, 28.9%) and hyperglycemia (n = 53, 12.3%). Significant differences were observed in body mass index and all VRF‐related markers among participants with and without VRFs (p < 0.05).

DISCUSSION

This secondary analysis of the J‐MINT trial demonstrated that multidomain interventions led to cognitive benefits in participants with untreated or uncontrolled VRFs, whereas no significant effects were observed in those without VRFs. Notably, the interventions showed pronounced effects in individuals with hypertension or hyperglycemia, but not among those with dyslipidemia. Additionally, the intervention group exhibited improvements in vascular risk markers, including reductions in systolic blood pressure and pulse pressure, increases in HDL‐C, and decreases in TG levels and TG/HDL‐C ratio.

Evidence suggests that specific subgroups at high risk of dementia benefit from multidomain interventions. 11 , 12 , 14 , 16 The current analysis highlighted the enhanced efficacy of the multidomain intervention among older adults with untreated or uncontrolled VRFs, and corroborates with the results of the Prevention of Dementia by Intensive Vascular Care (PreDIVA) trial, which reported that individuals with untreated hypertension who were adherent to the intervention were more likely to benefit from it. 11 Similarly, the Systematic Multi‐domain Alzheimer's Risk Reduction Trial (SMARRT) trial revealed a significant intervention effect in older adults with two or more dementia risk factors, which included poorly controlled hypertension (systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mm Hg) and diabetes (hyperglycemia with HbA1c levels ≥ 8.0% or potential hypoglycemia). 7 In the J‐MINT PRIME Tamba study, 8 a sister study of J‐MINT, a significant intervention effect on the primary endpoint was observed among participants with at least one of the following VRFs: being under treatment for hypertension, systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mm Hg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 85 mm Hg, being under treatment for diabetes, or HbA1c ≥ 6.0%. Notably, when VRFs were identified by incorporating self‐reported comorbidities or medication use, no significant intervention effect was observed in the present study. Among participants classified as not having untreated or uncontrolled VRFs, the medication usage rates for hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia were 35%, 5%, and 31%, respectively. These participants appeared to have relatively well‐managed conditions. Furthermore, the findings indicate that relying solely on self‐reported medical history or prescription records may not accurately identify populations at high risk for dementia who are more likely to benefit from interventions. Instead, targeting individuals with untreated or uncontrolled VRFs, regardless of their medical history or medication use, may improve the identification of those who may benefit from multidomain intervention.

In this study, although a significant intervention effect was observed in participants with poorly controlled VRFs, the effect size was small (0.11 standard deviation [SD] over 18 months). This finding is consistent with the results of previous multidomain intervention trials: Finnish Geriatric Intervention Study to Prevent Cognitive Impairment and Disability (FINGER; 0.04 SD over 2 years), 6 SMARRT (0.14 SD over 2 years), 7 Maintain Your Brain (0.18 SD over 3 years), 9 and US Study to Protect Brain Health Through Lifestyle Intervention to Reduce Risk (0.029 SD per year). 30 Although such effect sizes may appear modest at the individual and population levels over relatively short periods, their potential long‐term sustainability, particularly when implemented at scale, could yield meaningful societal benefits. 31 Accordingly, further studies with longer follow‐up periods are essential to better understand the broader societal implications of multidomain interventions. At the same time, cost effectiveness is a critical consideration for the successful societal implementation of multidomain interventions. 32 A previous scoping review indicates that when target populations are clearly defined according to dementia risk, multidomain interventions are more likely to be cost effective and cost saving. 33 Thus, prioritizing high‐risk groups, such as individuals with poorly controlled VRFs, may provide the greatest benefit within limited health‐care resources.

The mechanisms underlying the cognitive benefits of multidomain interventions among individuals with VRFs remain unclear. Herein, we investigated changes in dementia‐related plasma biomarkers; however, no significant intervention effects were observed for any of the biomarkers (Table 4). Notably, cognitive improvements were observed despite the absence of substantial changes in AD pathology‐ or neurodegeneration‐associated biomarkers, indicating a potential enhancement of cognitive reserve. Although the FINGER and PreDIVA trials did not report considerable intervention effects on neuroimaging biomarkers, 34 , 35 the Look AHEAD (Action for Health in Diabetes) trial demonstrated that a multidomain intensive lifestyle intervention showed beneficial effects on white matter hyperintensity and cerebral blood flow in individuals with obesity and diabetes. 36 , 37 These findings suggest that cerebrovascular pathways may contribute to cognitive benefits in populations with VRFs. Future studies incorporating advanced neuroimaging techniques are warranted to further elucidate the mechanisms underlying these effects.

The multidomain intervention also demonstrated beneficial effects on vascular risk markers, such as reductions in systolic blood pressure and pulse pressure, increases in HDL‐C, and decreases in TG levels and TG/HDL‐C, among individuals with untreated or uncontrolled VRFs (Table 4). In older adults, a widened pulse pressure and isolated systolic hypertension due to high systolic blood pressure and low diastolic blood pressure are common and associated with an increased risk of cerebrovascular and cardiovascular diseases as well as dementia. 38 − 40 Furthermore, a recent meta‐analysis demonstrated that a higher TG/HDL‐C ratio, which is closely related to insulin resistance, was significantly associated with a higher risk of cardiovascular events. 41 Notably, the findings of the present study indicate that even a relatively short‐term intervention can lead to improvements in these vascular markers, highlighting the potential for benefits in cerebrovascular and cardiovascular health. The FINGER study targeted individuals with a Cardiovascular Risk Factors, Aging, and Incidence of Dementia risk score ≥ 6, representing a high‐risk population; however, it did not observe significant effects on cholesterol or blood pressure during the intervention period. 6 Moreover, the long‐term follow‐up (over 7 years) of the FINGER trial suggested that multidomain interventions reduce the risk of cerebrovascular events. 42 Additionally, this intervention effect was pronounced among those who had a history of cardiovascular diseases. 42 The findings of the present study are consistent with the results of this prior study and suggest that multidomain interventions may contribute to the reduction of cerebrovascular and cardiovascular risks in older adults with untreated or uncontrolled VRFs, even in the short term.

This study has several limitations. First, it is a secondary analysis of the J‐MINT trial and does not rigorously control for statistical errors. In the subgroup analysis based on each VRF, the hyperglycemia subgroup included a relatively small sample size, resulting in wide 95% CIs and limiting the precision of the estimates. Future studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate these findings. In addition, the J‐MINT trial participants may represent a relatively health‐conscious older population, raising concerns about the generalizability of the results to the broader population of older adults with MCI.

Despite these limitations, the findings suggest that multidomain interventions may be more effective in preventing cognitive decline among older adults with untreated or uncontrolled VRFs. Furthermore, the intervention was associated with improvements in blood pressure and lipid profiles, suggesting a potential role in reducing the risk of cerebrovascular and cardiovascular events. These findings underscore the importance of targeting older adults with untreated or uncontrolled VRFs in future dementia prevention strategies. To confirm these effects, further validations through prespecified randomized controlled trials are needed.
