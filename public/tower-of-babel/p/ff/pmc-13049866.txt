Experimental quantification of resistance to subsidence conferred by endplate-specific expandable implants in anterior lumbar interbody fusion
Authors: Landyn Froberg, Andrew Zhang, James Robinson, Mason Favre, Milan Mody, Giovanni F Solitro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn anterior lumbar interbody fusion, implant subsidence remains a major complication, influenced by both patient- and implant-related factors. Cage sizing has been shown to influence the subsidence load and is dependent on both the surgical approach and the patient’s anatomic characteristics. Larger cage footprints have been shown to increase axial stability by allowing for a greater area of contact, but their limited sizes and footprints are not always able to accommodate the significant variance in vertebral body shape, endplate dimensions, and epiphysial rim depth. Therefore, the current study aims to quantify the incremental resistance to subsidence conferred by the use of novel endplate-specific expandable implants over conventional, static implants.MethodsFifteen cadaveric spines were reconstructed via computed tomography (CT) scans of the L5 vertebra, and the endplates and cortical shell were isolated from the inner trabecular core to recreate bone surrogates. The novel implant was positioned on each bone surrogate in the unexpanded (control) or expanded (endplate-specific expandable) configuration and compressed at a rate of 5 mm/min. The subsidence load and construct stiffness were evaluated for each group.ResultsThe subsidence load of the expandable implants quantified in 3478 N ± 588 was 20% greater than that of the control group (p < 0.05). No difference was found in construct stiffness (p > 0.05). There was no correlation (r = 0.260, p = 0.350) between the stiffness and epiphyseal rim contact, whereas a positive correlation (r = 0.796, p < 0.001) was found between the subsidence load and increased epiphyseal rim contact in the expandable implant group only.ConclusionsIn our study, endplate-specific expandable ALIF implants have shown higher resistance to subsidence than static implants. While, these results must be corroborated by clinical data, the superiority here identified indicates the potential for these implants to be advantageous over static implants. The linear correlation between the epiphyseal rim cross-sectional area of the endplate and the subsidence load, as found in the current study, highlights the potential for estimating the subsidence load during preoperative planning.Keywords: Lumbar spine, Degenerative disc disease, Anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF), Subsidence, Endplate-specific expandable implants

Background

Disc degeneration is caused by a series of biochemical processes that occur with aging and is one of the most common causes of disability worldwide [1]. Lumbar disc degeneration is associated with a range of clinical symptoms that most commonly include axial lower back pain that worsens with flexion and sitting, a decrease in mobility, and an overall reduction in quality of life [2]. In this context. anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF) is used to relieve pain that is not amenable to conservative nonoperative treatment [3, 4]. Implant subsidence is a known complication and while is not always symptomatic, it may present as imbalance of the spine, loss of disc space [5], severe pain, malalignment, neural compression, instability, and adjacent segment disease [6]. There is no consensus in the literature on the amount of subsidence that should be considered clinically relevant [7]; however, the rates of subsequent revision surgeries and adjacent level surgeries have been found to be significantly greater in patients who experienced subsidence [5, 6]. Patient factors such as age, sex, and bone mineral density, as well as implant factors such as implant material, dimension, and placement have been associated to subsidence [8, 9] that vary between 6 and 24% for ALIF [5, 7].

Sizing and placement of the interbody fusion device is critical for restoring intervertebral height and correcting lumbar lordosis without subsequent loss of deformity correction [10]. It has been shown to influence subsidence load [11] and is dependent on both the surgical approach and the patient’s anatomic characteristics, such as lumbar lordosis and vertebral body dimensions [7, 9, 12, 13].

Increased load distribution has been previously hypothesized to be essential in reducing subsidence [14], and implant placement is aimed at widest cage possible without posing a risk to neurovascular structures [2, 12, 15]. The contact area location between the interbody cage and the endplate is an important consideration in lumbar interbody fusion, as the central portion of the lumbar endplate is thinner and weaker than the peripheral region of the endplate [16, 17]. In fact, previous biomechanical experiments demonstrated that implants placed with increased epiphyseal rim contact were associated with increased resistance to subsidence [11, 18]. Current ALIF implants are available in various shapes, but their limited sizes and footprints [8, 9, 19] are not always able to accommodate the significant variance in vertebral body shape [18], endplate dimensions [20], and epiphysial rim depth [17].

In lumbar interbody fusion, sagittally expandable ALIF cages have been introduced to facilitate insertion and minimize the iatrogenic endplate damage caused by the impaction [21]. The expandability in other dimensions has later been used for minimally invasive transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF) in which the size of the interbody is restricted by the operative corridor [22]. Further studies have shown that the transverse expandability of the TLIF cages resulted in the equivalence to static ALIF cages in reducing segmental motion [23]. Expandable cages used for minimally invasive surgeries have been shown to reduce intraoperative retraction and decrease the rate of subsidence [24]. However, due to their small size, several drawbacks have been identified, including higher costs compared to static cages, difficulty with bone grafting, stress shielding [25], and improper stress distribution on the endplate, which can result in subsidence [26].

Given the advantages demonstrated by the expandability of TLIF implants, a novel endplate-specific expandable ALIF implant has been developed to address the limitations of static designs and better conform to the patient’s individual endplate anatomy (see Fig. 1).

Therefore, the current study aims to quantify the increment in subsidence load conferred by endplate-specific expandable ALIF implants compared with that conferred by conventional, static implants. We hypothesize that the use of expandable implants that allow maximization of epiphysial rim coverage corresponds to higher subsidence loads than do static implants. Furthermore, we also hypothesized that the resistance to subsidence is directly correlated with the epiphyseal rim dimensions in contact with the implant.

Methods

Following obtainment of IRB approval, CT scans of L5 vertebrae harvested from a total of 15 cadaveric spines aged 80yo±7 years (40% males, 60% females) were obtained via a GE LightSpeed VCT scanner with a slice thickness of 0.625mm. The vertebrae were reconstructed via 3D Slicer software [27], which isolated the endplates and cortical shell from the inner trabecular core. The obtained surfaces were imported into Fusion 360 (Autodesk, Mill Valley, California) and sectioned in correspondence with the superior endplate to extract profiles of the outer cortex and the inner trabecular core [28]. For data analysis purposes, the total cortical area and the anterior cortical thickness for each bone surrogate were extracted. The profiles of the surrogates were obtained through a linear extrusion of 20 mm in length and CNC machined using bone surrogate foam in accordance with the ASTM F1839 standard. As adopted in previous studies, the foam blocks were machined to imitate vertebral bodies and consisted of an outer layer of 40 pounds per cubic foot (PCF) foam bonded to a softer inner layer of 15 PCF foam, representing the cortical and cancellous bone, respectively [29–31]. Following a previously used methodology [30, 32] the endplate-specific expandable implants were positioned on the bone surrogates and compressed at a rate of 5 mm/min using an Instron 8874 (Instron, Norwood, MA) (Fig. 2). The implants used in the current study are prefabricated and made of a unique structure configurable to resemble a traditional ALIF implant but can expand transversally up to 10 mm and are locked in shape through a set screw (See Fig. 3). The expansion is controlled by the surgeon at the time of instrumentation, and for the purpose of this study, was executed with the aim of maximizing the contact between the implant and the epiphyseal rim. Each of the bone surrogates was tested with the implant in two configurations: unexpanded, conventionally sized controls with a width of 37 mm and then expanded (endplate-specific expandable). Data was acquired at 100 Hz, and the compression load was increased in 1 N increments from a reference preload of 5 N. The peak load was identified at a displacement of 2 mm, which is the reference position of subsidence as proposed for the clinical classification of moderate subsidence in a previous study [7, 33]. The stiffness was evaluated as the angular coefficient of the linear regression of the obtained load-displacement curve. Normality was evaluated on the data obtained for the control group via the Shapiro–Wilk test, and differences between the two implant configurations were evaluated via paired T-test for means or Wilcoxon signed-rank test for nonparametric data, both at a significance level of 0.05. The proportionality of the subsidence load and stiffness in relation to the epiphyseal rim surface area of the surrogates was analyzed via the Pearson correlation Coefficient for normally distributed data, whereas the Spearman Correlation Coefficient was used otherwise. All analyses were performed in R [34]. Sample sizing was executed considering two independent groups of continuous variables with an enrollment ratio of 1, an alpha of 0.05, and a power of 80%. Considering the experimental values of force at subsidence reported by Yuan at al [11]. For the interbody fusion cages with cross-sectional areas of 628mm^2 and 697mm^2 corresponding to a width increments of 5 mm, we determined the minimal sample size to be 4. A sample size of 15 was chosen for the current study, resulting in 30 total paired experiments.

Results

The subsidence load and construct stiffness of the static implant group (control) were 2898 N±243 (p=0.428) and 2657 N/mm±749, respectively (see Fig. 4a and b), both of which followed a normal distribution curve. For the endplate-specific expandable implants, however, only construct stiffness was normally distributed (p= 0.179), whereas the subsidence load did not follow a normal distribution curve. Therefore, a T-test was used for the stiffness value, whereas a Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for the peak loads. The coefficient of determination of the linear regressions used to calculate the construct stiffness was not significantly different between the two groups (see Table 1, p = 0.489). Endplate-specific expandable implants did not result in a significant improvement in stiffness (3165 N/mm±572, p = 0.056); however, the subsidence load of 3478 N±588 measured for the expandable implants was significantly greater (p = 0.002).

The subsidence load was not correlated with the surface area of the epiphyseal rim in the control group (r=0.260, p = 0.350, y=0.034x+114.9), but was correlated in the endplate-specific expandable implant group, with a Spearman correlation coefficient of r=0.589 (p = 0.021, y=0.043x+64.6) (Fig. 4a). Discussion

The mechanical goals of ALIF are to restore disc height and lordosis and to indirectly increase neuroforaminal diameter [3, 35]. Subsidence directly counteracts these goals and can dampen or negate the clinical benefits the procedure seeks to provide [3, 7]. Two factors affecting subsidence are vertebral body heterogeneity [36] and epiphyseal rim width variability, which has been documented to range between 3 and 7 mm [17]. (Fig. 5)

Previous studies have suggested that a wide interbody-rim contact area and endplate coverage of 30% or above are optimal for proper interbody fusion and improved load capacity [37]. Maximizing this requires the retraction of vital neurovascular structures, which can be particularly challenging in patients with obesity [38, 39]. A complication of ALIF is vascular injury, most commonly venous laceration during retraction of the great vessels [39]. Using an expandable implant may require less retraction of vital vascular structures, therefore mitigating this complication [39]. Additionally, the use of a wide cage in lumbar interbody fusion has been associated with increased intraoperative endplate damage [40], most commonly resulting from mechanical manipulation during sizing and forceful insertion of static devices [41]. [41] While these recommendations have been previously proposed in the literature, a specific quantification of the biomechanical advantages of expandable implants over static implants has not been proposed before. For the implant design considered in the current study, such advantage has been quantified to be of 20% on average. Furthermore, the customizability of the implants may lead to preservation of patient anatomy and reduced operating time, as previously documented for patient-specific implants [42–45].

To address our research question, the main limitation was the use of bone surrogates in place of cadaveric specimens. However, previous studies (see Table 2) have shown that the properties of bone surrogates are highly comparable to those of cadaver bone and produce results that follow the same general trend [46, 47]. This choice does not allow evaluation of the responses to the implant such as bone resorption [6, 48]; however it is common practice in preclinical studies, to reduce experimental costs while improving the reproducibility of the experiments [49]. To mitigate the limitations of previous studies in which a single foam density mimicking trabecular bone properties was used [32], we opted for a surrogate composed of two densities. While this approach allows differentiation of the configurations in terms of epiphyseal rim coverage, it did not fully represent cancellous bone or its complex lattice and mechanical properties [47]. Moreover, the use of surrogates did not allow an evaluation of the endplate injury that is widely documented for other spine fusions procedures such as lateral interbody fusion [50] and bone fragmentation at implant insertion [51]. Furthermore, we limited our study to L5 vertebral bone surrogate models only. This was motivated by the fact that the spine specimens we used were all harvested at the sacrum, and L4/L5 is one of the most common levels at which ALIF is performed [2]. While data from previous studies on endplate geometry show similar measurements for endplate depth and diameter between the L5 and S1 vertebral endplates [52], differences in segmental motion between these segments [53] must be noted, limiting the extension of our results to vertebrae other than L5.

Another limitation is that the compressive loading conditions used in this study do not fully represent the multitude of physiological loads encountered by the human spine [30], which are multidirectional and influenced by factors such as vertebral geometry, paraspinal muscle volume, and bone mineral density [30, 58, 59]. While the testing of the device with physiological loads on cadaveric specimens would provide more clinical value, it must be noted that the currently adopted testing protocol is in line with the ASTM F2077-22 standard (Standard Test Methods for Intervertebral Body Fusion Devices) for subsidence testing of interbody fusion cages that has widely adopted in previous research [30, 36, 60].

Furthermore, in spine interbody fusion, there are many implant variations in terms of size, material, and fixation [9] and results would have been different using different cages as a control (See Table 2) [11, 30, 31, 56, 57, 61]. Although we did not evaluate multiple implant sizes, the 37 mm-wide implant used for the control group was considered appropriate for the dimensions of the surrogates by a spine surgery–trained surgeon and has been previously used in a previously published human cadaveric study [54].

The current study focused on the shape variability that can be conferred to the implants independently of the material or the porosity conferred to host osteobiologics (BMP-2, DMP) [19, 62].

In spine surgery, limited implant footprints are available for surgeons to match the different endplate geometries [42, 44]. The expandable implant used in the current study achieves contact with regions of the vertebral endplate that have higher structural strength, without increasing the overall contact area compared with the static implant.

Therefore, the novelty of this study is given by the fact that we have firstly quantified the advantage of an implant intraoperatively sized to match the endplate over static implants, and even if we have only considered a customization along one degree of freedom, we have found an increase in the subsidence load.

It is common practice to bring multiple implant sizes to the operating room due to variability in bone morphology [9, 63], significantly increasing procedural costs. The use of the endplate-specific expandable implants adopted in the current study would allow the surgeon to customize the cage to meet the specific anatomical needs of the patient without the extensive equipment or preoperative planning required for static and 3D-printed implants [4, 39]. The current study only focused on the biomechanical advantages and these considerations require dedicated cost-analysis studies.

A decreased contact area between endplate and implants as well as endplate weakness have been indicated as causes of adverse events [16]. Cage subsidence has been observed to be much greater in patients with concave endplates [64], therefore, patients with irregular or convex endplate morphology would particularly benefit from implants that can be custom fit.

An additional finding of the current study is the correlation between the epiphyseal rim surface area and subsidence load [11, 17, 18] for the endplate-specific expandable implants (r=0.796, p < 0.001). The results produced in this study must be corroborated by cadaveric experiments and clinical trials. However, the improved subsidence load is result of an improved load distribution [42, 43] and therefore has the potential to reduce the rate of subsidence in ALIF.

Conclusions

The current study provides evidence of the mechanical advantages of endplate-specific expandable implants over static implants for compressive loading. While the current study was limited to bone surrogate models of the L5 vertebra, the 20% increase in the subsidence load over static implants provides the first insight into their potential for improving fusion outcomes. The recommendation of their adoption demands future studies aimed at corroborating the results proposed here with clinical data. However, the linear correlation between the epiphyseal rim cross-sectional area of the endplate and subsidence load could allow for patient-specific preoperative planning via CT data.
