TTPAL inhibits the progression of breast cancer and reprograms tumor-associated macrophages by inhibiting JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway
Authors: Lingfang Du, Lingyun Zhu, Yang Yang, Lili Pan, Ting Lei, Jun Zhang, Shuang Yao, Guanghua Luo, Xiaoying Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBreast cancer remains a major disease that poses a significant threat to the health of women worldwide. Identifying new targets and formulating effective treatment strategies is particularly crucial for breast cancer patients. Tocopherol alpha transfer protein like (TTPAL) promotes tumor development, but its precise role and underlying mechanisms in breast cancer progression remain unclear. This study aims to investigate the role of TTPAL in breast cancer progression and explore its potential mechanisms.MethodsWe analyzed TTPAL expression using data from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database. TTPAL expression levels were examined in breast cancer tissue and normal breast tissue by immunohistochemistry staining (IHC), and western blotting assay. Further CCK‑8 assay, colony formation assay, wound healing, transwell assay and tumor xenograft experiments were used to detect the effect of TTPAL on breast cancer progression. RNA sequencing was performed to explore the underlying mechanisms. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and rescue experiments were conducted to elucidate the interaction between TTPAL and the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway. Additionally, the effects of TTPAL on M2 polarization of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) were explored through co-culture experiments and analysis of tumor tissue.ResultsTTPAL mRNA and protein levels were significantly downregulated in breast cancer tissue and breast cancer cell lines. Ectopic TTPAL expression potently suppressed breast cancer cell proliferation, colony formation, migration in vitro, and tumor growth in vivo. Mechanistically, TTPAL interacts with JAK2, inhibiting JAK2 phosphorylation (Tyr1007/1008) without altering the total JAK2 protein level; subsequently reduces STAT3 phosphorylation (Tyr705), thereby attenuating the oncogenic JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway. Rescue experiments with Colivelin reversed the effect of TTPAL on tumor suppressive. Crucially, TTPAL-overexpressing breast cancer cells attenuated M2 polarization of TAMs in vitro and suppressed infiltration of CD206+ TAMs in vivo. Clinically, low TTPAL expression strongly correlated with high CD163⁺ TAMs infiltration in breast cancer patient tissues.ConclusionsTTPAL acts as a potent tumor suppressor in breast cancer by inhibiting JAK2/STAT3 signaling and impeding M2 polarization of TAMs within the tumor microenvironment (TME). Restoration of TTPAL function presents a promising dual-targeting therapeutic strategy to inhibit tumor cell growth and remodel the immunosuppressive TME.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13058-026-02246-3.Keywords: Breast cancer, TTPAL, JAK2/STAT3, Tumor-associated macrophages, M2 polarization

Introduction

Breast cancer is one of the most frequently occurring malignant tumors among women [1], posing a serious threat to their health. It exhibits high heterogeneity [2–4], with significant variations in tumor morphology, structure, and immunophenotype among different patients [5]. This diversity leads to markedly distinct clinical responses and treatment outcomes across individuals. The pathogenesis of breast cancer is highly complex [6, 7], and conventional treatments and biological therapies remain insufficient to fully address clinical challenges like metastasis [8], recurrence [9], and drug resistance [10, 11]. Therefore, in-depth exploration of the pathological and molecular mechanisms driving malignant progression, along with the identification of novel therapeutic targets, has become both a critical clinical challenge and an urgent research priority.

Tocopherol alpha transfer protein-like (TTPAL), located on chromosome 20, specifically binds lipophilic molecules (such as tocopherol and retinal). It mediates the directional transport of tocopherol from the endoplasmic reticulum to the plasma membrane, scavenges free radicals, and maintains the integrity of cellular membranes. Recently, researchers identified TTPAL among genes with hotspot mutations in colorectal cancer (CRC) samples, suggesting its potential involvement in CRC invasion and metastasis [12]. Gou et al. [13] performed whole-genome sequencing analysis of primary CRC tissue, revealing TTPAL as a significantly amplified gene in CRC. Through in vitro and animal model experiments, they demonstrated that TTPAL functions as an oncogene promoting CRC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion by activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. Liu et al. [14] identified TTPAL as a novel oncogenic driver in gastric cancer, facilitating tumorigenesis and metastasis. Mechanistically, TTPAL activates the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway through direct interaction with NNMT (Nicotinamide N-methyltransferase). These studies collectively indicate that TTPAL participates in tumor progression and metastasis. Nevertheless, its biological functions and mechanisms in other cancer types require further investigation.

Tumor initiation and progression are determined not only by the intrinsic properties of cancer cells but are also intimately linked to the TME [15]. Within this complex milieu, diverse stimuli and molecular interactions promote the infiltration of immune cells. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) [16], as the most abundant immune cell population in the TME, play a complex yet critical role during tumor development [17]. Consequently, elucidating how tumor cells influence macrophage differentiation [18–20] in the surrounding microenvironment has emerged as a key research focus, offering novel therapeutic targets and research strategies for cancer treatment [21–23].

In this study, we investigated the role of TTPAL in the progression of breast cancer. Our study demonstrated that overexpression of TTPAL suppressed the pression of breast cancer by inhibiting JAK2/STAT3 signaling. Specifically, we revealed that TTPAL interacts with JAK2, thereby reducing the phosphorylation level of STAT3. In addition, overexpression of TTPAL inhibited the M2 polarization of tumor-associated macrophages, which reshaped the tumor immune microenvironment. These findings highlight the pivotal role of TTPAL in breast cancer and suggest that it may serve as a promising therapeutic target for breast cancer treatment.

Materials and methods

Patient samples

Breast cancer tissue samples, comprising 148 pairs of primary breast cancer tissue and adjacent normal tissue samples from breast cancer patients who underwent surgery treatment were obtained from the Third Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University, China. There were no biases in selecting patients, and none of the patients had received any prior treatment, such as radiotherapy or chemotherapy. Ethical approval for all procedures was obtained from the Third Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University Committee.

All reagents and consumables were pre-chilled on ice in a biosafety cabinet. Oxidized-zirconium beads (G0203-150G, Servicebio) and dissection tools were disinfected with 75% ethanol, air-dried, and kept cold. Fresh tissues were rinsed three times with ice-cold PBS, blotted dry, weighed, and minced into 2–3 mm3 fragments. The fragments were transferred into pre-chilled screw grinding tubes (OSE-TH-D01, Tiangen) and lysed with 100–200 µL ice-cold RIPA buffer (BB-3122, Bestbio) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors per 10 mg tissue. Five to six sterilized grinding beads were added to each tube. Samples were homogenized at 4 °C in a bead-beater (3,480 rpm, 2 min × 2–3 cycles) with intermittent cooling on ice. Lysates were centrifuged at 4 ℃ and 12,000 × g for 15 min and the supernatants were quantified by BCA assay (23228, Thermo Fisher). All samples were adjusted to the same concentration. Add 5 × SDS-PAGE protein loading buffer (WB2001, New Cell & Molecular) and denature at 95 °C for 10 min.

Cell culture and reagents

The human breast carcinoma cell lines MDA-MB-231, SUM159, T47D, ZR-75–1, BT474, MCF-7 and MDA-MB-468 were obtained from the Cell Bank of Chinese Academy of Sciences and cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; VivaCell Biosciences, China) containing 10% FBS (VivaCell Biosciences). The murine breast carcinoma cell line 4T1 and the human monocyte THP-1 cell line were cultured in RPMI-1640 (VivaCell Biosciences) containing 10% FBS. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. For the co-culture assay, MDA-MB-231 or T47D cells (5 × 105) were added to a 6-well plate. THP-1 cells (5 × 105) were added 24 h later to 0.4 μm-diameter culture inserts (Millipore Corp, Billerica, MA) above the cancer cells for 48 h.

Construction of overexpression stable cell lines

Full-length complementary DNAs (cDNAs) of human TTPAL and mouse Ttpal were amplified from DNA templates using a standard PCR-based cloning strategy. PCR products were then ligated into the pGWLV01-new vector. Stable cell lines were generated as described previously. Briefly, plasmids used for overexpression of the target genes were co-transfected with lentivirus packaging vector mix into HEK293T cells. After 48 h, cultured medium from transfected cells was collected and filtered with a 0.45-μm filter. Cells were incubated with the filtered medium in the presence of 8 μg/ml polybrene (Sigma-Aldrich) overnight and selected using 5 μg/mL puromycin (ST551, Beyotime Biotechnology) or 10 μg/mL blasticidin (B861447, Macklin) for 1 week.

Cell proliferation and Colony-forming assay

MDA-MB-231or SUM159 cells were seeded into 96-well plate overnight at 10,000 cells per well or 5000 cells per well respectively. The culture medium was replaced with medium containing 18 μΜ concentrations of Colivelin (HY-P1061, MCE) for 6 h. Cell growth was monitored by CCK-8 assay according to the manufacturer’s instruction (CK04, Dojindo Molecular Technologies). For colony formation assays, cells in the logarithmic growth phase were dissociated into a single-cell suspension and subsequently centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min. Following resuspension in 1 mL of DMEM, the cells were enumerated, and 2000 cells were seeded per well in a 6-well plate. After incubation, the cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde and then stained with crystal violet (C0121, Beyotime Biotechnology). Clone formation efficiency was assessed using ImageJ software for quantification.

Wound healing assay

Cells were seeded on 6-well plates with medium containing 10% FBS and grown to confluence. The cells were scratched with a sterile 10-μL pipette tip to create artificial wounds. At 0 h, 6 h, 12 h and 24 h after wounding, phase-contrast images of the wound healing process were photographed with a 10× objective lens (Olympus, CKX41). Eight images per treatment were analyzed to determine averaging position of the migrating cells at the wound edges.

Transwell assays

Migration assays of the cells were conducted using transwell chambers (Corning, USA). Cells resuspended at a concentration of 5 × 104 cells/mL in 200 μL of serum-free medium supplemented with 1% BSA were seeded onto the apical side of the chamber. The basolateral side of the chamber was filled with 500 μL of medium containing 10% FBS. After 48 h, cells that had migrated through the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution for 30 min. The migrated cells were subsequently quantified using a phase-contrast microscope (CKX41, Olympus, Japan).

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the indicated cells using the Trizol (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) reagent according to the manufacturer’s instruction. The extracted RNAs were reverse-transcribed using a reverse transcription kit (Yeasen, China). Quantitative real-time PCR was conducted using SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen, China) on a real-time quantitative PCR system (ABI 7500, Thermo Fisher, MA, USA). Expression data were normalized to GAPDH to control the variability in expression levels. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1 in Supplementary Material.

Western blotting (WB)

Western blotting analysis was performed according to published protocols [24]. Primary antibodies were as follows: TTPAL (NBP1-92544, Novus; PA5-61381, Invitrogen), GAPDH (ab8245, Abcam), JAK2 (ab108596, Abcam), STAT3 (39139S, CST), Flag-Tag (8146S, CST), His-Tag (12698S, CST), Rabbit IgG (G3900S, CST), Mouse IgG(5415S, CST), p-JAK(Tyr1007/1008) (23776T, CST), p-STAT3(Ser727) (94994T, CST), p-STAT3(Tyr705)(9145T, CST). HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (SA00001-1, Proteintech) and HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (SA00001-2, Proteintech) secondary antibodies were conjugated to horseradish peroxidase. Immunocomplexes were detected using an ECL chemiluminescence kit (Thermo Fisher, MA, USA) by a Chemiluminescence detection equipment (4600, Tanon).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded specimens were collected and sectioned at 5 μm. For immunohistochemistry staining, after being treated with antigen retrieval solution (Tris–EDTA buffer, pH 9.0), tissue sections were incubated with TTPAL primary antibody (PA5-61381, Invitrogen), biotin-conjugated secondary antibody, and streptavidin-HRP. Sections were visualized with DAB substrate (K3468, Dako). Images were obtained using an Olympus CKX41 microscope. Primary antibodies used in this study are as follows: p-STAT3 (9145T, CST), CD206 (ab300621, Abcam), and Flag-tag (8146S, CST). For IF staining, fresh frozen sections were stained with primary antibodies targeting Flag-TTPAL, p-STAT3, and CD206. Secondary antibodies conjugated with fluorophores (A11012, A32723, Thermo fisher) were used, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (D523, Dojindo Molecular Technologies). Staining results were analyzed using fluorescence microscopy (IX73, Olympus) to assess marker expression and their correlations.

Immunoprecipitation (IP) assay

Breast cancer cells (5 × 106/reaction) stably transfected with TTPAL (Flag-tagged) expression vector or empty vector were extracted in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (R0100, Solarbio Life Science; Composition: 50 Mm Tris–HCl (PH7.4), 150 Mm NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.1% SDS) supplemented with protease inhibitor. IP was performed using anti-Flag M2 antibody (A2220, Sigma-Aldrich, German). The immune complexes were precipitated by PureProteome™ Protein A/G Magnetic Beads (LSKMAGAG10, Millipore, Billerica, MA) overnight at 4℃. The beads with extracted proteins were washed three times with PBS, and then 40 μL of 1 × SDS-PAGE Loading Buffer was added to the washed beads. Heat-denature the samples at 95℃ for 10 min to dissociate immunocomplexes. The sample components were electrophoretically separated on SDS polyacrylamide gels for western blotting analysis.

Flow cytometry

After co-culture with breast cancer cells, THP-1 cells were collected and washed with 1 × PBS containing 1% FBS and incubated with APC-conjugated anti-human CD163 (17-1639-42, Invitrogen) antibody for 30 min in the dark at 4 °C. Cells were analyzed using a FACS flow cytometer (NL-CLC, Cytek), and data analysis was performed using Flowjo software. The TAMs were harvested according to the method described in previous study [25]. In brief, the tumor masses were removed, minced, and digested with collagenase and hyaluronidase solution. The cell suspension was filtered through a cell mesh and resuspended in Hank's media plus 1% FCS for further analysis. Antibodies to CD45 (45-045-82), CD11b (11-0112-82), F4/80 (63-4801-82), CD86 (48-0862-80) were purchased from eBioscience. CD206 (clone C068C2) antibodies were purchased from Biolegend. Flow cytometric analysis was performed using a FACS flow cytometer. The gating strategy is detailed in the supplementary data.

Animal models

All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Jiangsu Agrochem Laboratory, and complied with the Guidelines for the Welfare and Use of Animals. To establish an orthotopic breast cancer model, 2 × 106 4T1 cells were inoculated into the mammary fat pad of female BALB/c mice (n = 5). To establish a subcutaneous xenograft tumor model, 2 × 106 MDA-MB-231 cells were subcutaneously inoculated into mammary fat pad of BALB/c nude mice. Starting from day 9 post-injection, tumor growth was carefully monitored every three days. Tumor volume was calculated using the equation (L × W2)/2. Three weeks later, tumors were excised and weighed. After 33 days, the mice were euthanized, and the subcutaneous tumors were carefully excised, weighed, photographed for documentation, and further analyzed as required.

RNA sequencing and identification of differentially expressed genes

Total RNA was extracted from MDA-231-NC and MDA-MB-231-TTPAL stable cell lines using Trizol reagent. RNA sequencing was performed and analyzed on the DNBSEQ T7 (Qiantang Biotechnology (Suzhou), China). Gene expression was standardized to 1 million reads and normalized for gene length. The mRNAs with a fold change of > 2 or < 0.5 were considered upregulated and downregulated, respectively. The raw sequence data reported in this paper have been deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive in National Genomics Data Center (ID: HRA014263) that are publicly accessible at https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa.

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as mean ± S.E.M. for experiments and numbers of mice used as indicated. The two-tailed Student’s t-test was used to evaluate statistical differences between the two groups. For multiple group comparisons, ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post-hoc correction was used. P values ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis of bar graphs and scatter plots in this manuscript was performed using Prism 9.0 software.

Results

TTPAL protein expression is frequently downregulated in breast cancer

To investigate the role of TTPAL in breast cancer, we analyzed its expression using data from the GEO dataset, which includes GSE15852 [26], GSE109169 [27], GSE33447 [28], and GSE62931 [29], as well as The TCGA database, and found that TTPAL protein expression was found to be significantly lower in breast cancer samples compared with normal breast samples (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, analysis of paired samples (primary breast cancer tissue and adjacent normal tissue) within these datasets revealed significantly higher TTPAL expression levels in adjacent normal tissue compared to primary breast cancer tissue (Fig. 1B). To explore the expression of TTPAL in different subtypes of breast cancer (i.e., TNBC, Luminal A, Luminal B, HER2 positive and HER2 negative) from METABRIC database [30]. As shown in Figure S1A, TNBC, Luminal A, Luminal B, and HER2 positive breast cancers all show no significant difference of TTPAL expression compared to TNBC tissue samples. Then, our analysis using the 3-gene classifier subtype revealed that TTPAL expression was lower in the ER+HER−Prolif low group compared to the other groups (Figure S1B). Additionally, survival analysis revealed that low TTPAL expression was related to poor overall survival in breast patients [31], as shown in Figure S1C, patients with low TTPAL expression had a shorter median overall survival than the high TTPAL expression.

We then examined TTPAL expression in primary breast cancer tissue. Our results revealed that both mRNA and protein levels of TTPAL were significantly decreased in primary breast cancer tissue compared to adjacent normal tissue (Fig. 1C–E). Immunohistochemical (IHC) assays further confirmed the downregulated expression of TTPAL in breast cancer tissue compared to adjacent non-cancerous tissue (n = 150) (Fig. 1F). Furthermore, TTPAL expression was reduced in all 7 breast cancer cells tested (MCF-7, BT474, ZR-75–1, T47D, MDA-MB-468, SUM159, and MDA-MB-231) compared with normal mammary epithelial cell line MCF-10A at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 1G, H). Taken together, these findings indicated that TTPAL expression was frequently downregulated in breast cancer, which might be related to the progression of breast cancer.

Overexpression of TTPAL inhibits breast cancer progression both in vitro and in vivo

To further confirm the role of TTPAL in breast cancer progression, we established breast cancer cell models with TTPAL overexpression (Fig. S2A, B). Specifically, TTPAL overexpression in breast cancer cells led to a significant slowdown in cell growth rate (Fig. 2A), a decrease in colony formation (Fig. 2B), a reduction in migration ability (Fig. 2C, D and Fig. S2C). To further determine the role of TTPAL on breast cancer progression in vivo, MDA-MB-231 cells with TTPAL overexpression were administrated subcutaneously to nude mice to establish xenograft tumors (Fig. 2E). The results showed that TTPAL overexpression markedly inhibited tumor growth rate (Fig. 2F), tumor volume (Fig. 2G) and tumor weight (Fig. 2H) compared to the control group. We then examined TTPAL expression in breast cancer tissue, the results revealed that protein levels of TTPAL were significantly increased in TTPAL-OE group compared to NC group (Fig. 2I). Collectively, these findings indicated that TTPAL played a key role in breast cancer progression and overexpression of TTPAL was an effective strategy to curb breast cancer progression.

TTPAL inhibits JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway in breast cancer

To further investigate the underlying mechanisms involved in the regulation of TTPAL in breast cancer cells, we performed RNA sequencing on MDA-MB-231 cells and SUM159 with TTPAL overexpression and the negative control (NC) cells. The detected differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were visualized through: volcano plot (Fig. 3A) and hierarchical clustering heatmap for statistical significance (Fig. 3B). We found that TTPAL overexpression results in altered expression of 1706 genes (MDA-MB-231) and 254 genes (SUM159). We identified an intersection of differentially expressed genes between the two cell types through a Venn diagram, revealing 44 genes that showed co-occurring changes in both (Fig. 3C). Further functional enrichment analysis of KEGG, we observed suppression of JAK-STAT signaling (Fig. 3D and Fig. S3A), which suggests its potential role as a key regulatory mechanism underlying the differential gene expression patterns between the two cell types. Consistent with the KEGG pathway inhibition pattern, forced expression of TTPAL attenuated JAK2/STAT3 activation as evidenced by decreased phosphorylation levels of p-JAK2 (Tyr1007/1008) and p-STAT3 (Tyr705) instead of p-STAT3 (Ser727) in MDA-MB-231 cells and SUM159 cells (Fig. 3E and Fig. S3B). Likely, overexpression of TTPAL also suppressed p-JAK2 and p-STAT3 levels in mouse tumor tissue (Fig. 3F). Furthermore, through IF staining, it was found that overexpression of TTPAL inhibits the level of p-STAT3(Tyr705) (Fig. 3G). Overall, these findings demonstrate that the interaction between TTPAL and JAK2, primarily reduces JAK2 kinase activity through decreased phosphorylation, rather than by stabilizing the JAK2 protein or affecting its transcription, TTPAL overexpression impedes breast cancer progression by suppressing the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway.

TTPAL interacts with JAK2 and reduces the phosphorylation of STAT3

To investigate the mechanism by which TTPAL regulates JAK2/STAT3 signaling in breast cancer, we conducted co-IP in MDA-MB-231 and SUM159 cells. The results demonstrate that TTPAL binds to JAK2 and interferes with JAK2-mediated STAT3 phosphorylation (Tyr705), which ultimately suppresses downstream signaling activation. Notably, no direct interaction was observed between TTPAL and STAT3 (Fig. 4A). Subsequently, molecular docking analysis [32, 33] using alphafod3 and ChimeraX was performed to predict the potential binding site between TTPAL and JAK2. As shown in Fig. 4B, two hydrogen bonds were formed between Glu68 and Lys206 of TTPAL and Lys350 and Gly281 of JAK2, respectively. Two key inter-chain polar interactions were identified within the complex. The ammonium group of Lys350 and the carboxylate oxygen Glu68 interaction (3.02 Å) constitutes a canonical salt bridge that anchors the αC-helix of JAK2 to the acidic loop of TTPAL. A second, shorter hydrogen bond (2.50 Å) between the side chain ammonium of Lys206 and the main-chain carbonyl oxygen of Gly281 provides an additional high-affinity tether, further stabilizing the heterodimer. To verify whether the interaction relies on both sites, truncated fragments of TTPAL were constructed (Fig. 4C). Co-IP assay results indicated that only the fragment containing both sites could bind to JAK2, while fragments containing either single site failed to interact (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, when both key residues in TTPAL were mutated to alanine, molecular docking revealed an increased spatial separation between the proteins and a loss of intermolecular bonding interactions (Fig. 4E).

To further confirm that the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway mediates the regulatory role of TTPAL in breast cancer, we performed a rescue experiment using the STAT3 agonist Colivelin (Fig. S4A). We found that overexpression of TTPAL inhibits the proliferation (Fig. 4F) and migration capabilities of breast cancer cells (Fig. 4G, H, Fig. S4B), while treatment with Colivelin can attenuate the tumor-suppressing effects of TTPAL. Consistently, in vivo experiments (Fig. 4I) similarly revealed that breast cancer tumors overexpressing TTPAL exhibit slower growth rates (Fig. 4J). However, treatment with Colivelin significantly increased tumor growth speed, resulting in larger tumor volumes (Fig. 4K) and tumor weights (Fig. 4L) compared to the TTPAL overexpression group.

Overexpression of TTPAL inhibits M2 polarization of TAMs in breast cancer

Integrated analysis of RNA sequencing through Gene Ontology (GO) revealed that TTPAL overexpression modulates signal transduction pathways driving inflammatory response and immune activation (Fig. S5A). Given the pivotal immunomodulatory functions of macrophages in tumor progression, we propose that TTPAL expression in breast cancer may regulate macrophage functions within the TME. In this investigation, we explored the potential regulatory roles of TTPAL in M2 polarization of TAMs in breast cancer. We performed a 48 h non-contact co-culture of 231-TTPAL/231-NC cells with THP-1 using a transwell system (0.4 μm pore size; THP-1 monocytes in the upper chamber, breast cancer cells in the lower chamber) (Fig. 5A). Subsequently, the mRNA expression levels of M2-type macrophage markers (CD163, IL-10 and Arg-1) and M1-type markers (IL-1α, IL-6 and TNF-α) were determined by qPCR. As shown in Fig. 5B and Fig. S5B, co-culture with NC group tumor cells significantly up-regulated the expression of M2 markers and down-regulated M1 markers, whereas co-culture with TTPAL-OE breast cancer cells markedly suppressed the expression of these M2 markers and increased the expression of M1 markers. Subsequently, flow cytometry analysis revealed that co-culture with NC group cells significantly increased the proportion of CD163-positive cells (approximately 25%), whereas co-culture with TTPAL-overexpressing breast cancer cells markedly reduced this proportion to approximately 4% (Fig. 5C, D).

To further determine whether breast cancer cells influence M2 polarization of TAMs through paracrine mechanisms, we collected conditioned medium (CM) from breast cancer cells and treated THP-1 cells for 48 h (Fig. 5E). Consistently, CM from NC group cells significantly promoted the mRNA expression levels of genes related to M2 TAMs (Fig. 5F), suppressed the expression of M1 markers (Fig. S5C), and increased the CD163⁺ cell population (Fig. 5G, H). In contrast, CM from TTPAL-overexpressing cells effectively suppressed NC-induced M2 polarization. Furthermore, to determine whether the inhibitory effect on M2 polarization was mediated by the inhibition of the JAK/STAT3 signaling pathway, conditioned medium treated with Colivelin was applied. The results demonstrated that Colivelin treatment restored the M2 polarization that had been suppressed by TTPAL (Fig. 5I, L, Fig. S5D). Collectively, these findings highlight the critical role of TTPAL in orchestrating an anti-tumor immune microenvironment by suppressing M2 macrophage polarization.

TTPAL attenuates infiltration of M2-polarized macrophages in breast cancer

In this study, we collected 150 breast cancer clinical tissue specimens, IHC staining was utilized to examine the correlation of TTPAL and CD163 expression levels (Fig. 6A). As Fig. 6B, among 104 patients with high CD163 expression, 99 (95.2%) exhibited low TTPAL levels. Conversely, high TTPAL expression was predominantly observed in CD163-low tumors (28/46, 60.9%). Subsequently, we established orthotopic 4T1 breast cancer transplantation models in mice (Fig. 6C). Our findings demonstrated that Ttpal overexpression inhibited tumor growth in vitro (Fig. S6A-E) and in vivo (Fig. 6D–F). IF staining analysis revealed a significant reduction in CD206⁺ cell infiltration within Ttpal-overexpressing tumors (Fig. 6G). Flow cytometry further confirmed a marked decrease in the proportion of CD206⁺ cells in tumor tissue upon Ttpal overexpression (Fig. 6H). Collectively, these results indicate that TTPAL suppresses the infiltration of M2-polarized macrophages into tumor microenvironments, thereby inhibiting breast cancer progression.

Discussion

Breast cancer is the leading cause of cancer morbidity and mortality in women around the world [34]. Although modern treatments are multimodal and there have been recent advancements in therapeutics, intricate pathogenesis remains a major clinical challenge in breast cancer treatment. The rational development of new diagnostic or prognostic tumor markers [35] and the identification of novel cellular targets [36, 37] for anticancer chemotherapy relies on a more definitive understanding of tumor biology, identifying effective therapeutic targets is an important issue in clinical practice.

TTPAL was located on chromosome 20q13.12, which is one of the most frequently amplified regions in solid tumors, such as colorectal, breast, ovarian, and gastric cancers. Recent studies have indicated that TTPAL activated Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway through binding to and stabilizing TRIP6 in colorectal carcinogenesis [13]. TTPAL also plays a significant role in promoting gastric carcinogenesis through promoting PI3K/AKT pathway via cooperating with NNMT [14]. However, our study revealed that TTPAL expression is downregulated in breast cancer tissue and cells. To validate these findings, we further analyzed clinical samples from cervical and ovarian cancers, and observed that TTPAL expression was also reduced in these malignancies (The results are not shown). These findings suggest that TTPAL may function through distinct regulatory mechanisms in female-specific cancers.

While our study and external datasets confirm reduced TTPAL mRNA expression in breast cancer, the upstream regulatory mechanisms remain elusive. We hypothesize that TTPAL downregulation may arise from transcriptional or post-transcriptional modification. Epigenetic regulation via promoter hypermethylation could suppress TTPAL transcription, as observed for other tumor suppressors. Additionally, analysis of the TTPAL promoter region revealed binding sites for multiple transcription factors, including FOXA1 [38], FOXA2 [39], HOXB13 [40], HNF4a [41], CEBPA [42], CEBPB [43] and CTCF [44], suggesting their potential role in regulating TTPAL transcription. Alternatively, post-transcriptional mechanisms—such as miRNA-mediated degradation or RNA-binding protein (RBP)-driven instability—may contribute. In future studies, investigating the mechanisms that regulate TTPAL expression could provide new therapeutic avenues for breast cancer lacking TTPAL.

In this study, we found that TTPAL was able to modulate the breast cancer progression by significantly suppressing JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway. It is known that activation of JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway plays a critical role in promoting tumorigenesis [45, 46], epithelial and mesenchymal transition (EMT) [47–49], chemo-resistance [50–52], and cancer cell stemness [53, 54]. Specifically in breast cancer, JAK2/STAT3 promotes the proliferation and migration abilities of breast cancer cells, while knocking down either JAK2 or STAT3 inhibits breast cancer progression both in vivo and in vitro [55]. In this study, we identified an interaction between TTPAL and JAK2. Molecular docking simulations suggested that TTPAL binds to JAK2 via the formation of two hydrogen bonds, involving residues Glu68 and Lys206 in TTPAL and Lys350 and Gly281 in JAK2. Further validation using truncated variants of TTPAL indicated that the binding likely depends on Glu68 and Lys206. Future work will involve generating single or double-point mutations to confirm the role of these residues, along with in vitro and in vivo experiments to assess the impact of truncated and mutated TTPAL on the biological behavior of breast cancer cells.

Additionally, we demonstrate that TTPAL reduces STAT3 phosphorylation levels and ultimately inhibits breast cancer progression. Regarding how TTPAL affects this process, we propose three potential mechanisms: (1) TTPAL binding to JAK2 may impair JAK2 association with the receptor, thereby disrupting the receptor-mediated phosphorylation of STAT3; (2) TTPAL binding to JAK2 may induce conformational changes in JAK2 that perturb its kinase activity; (3) TTPAL binding to JAK2 may create steric hindrance, preventing STAT3 from accessing the receptor and consequently preventing its phosphorylation. We will elucidate the precise molecular mechanism(s) in future work.

Tumor initiation and progression are determined not only by the intrinsic properties of the tumor cells themselves but also by their close interaction with the TME [56, 57]. Within the complex TME, interactions among various stimuli and molecules promote the infiltration of immune cells [58, 59]. TAMs being the most abundant immune cell subpopulation, play a significant role in the development and progression of breast cancer [60, 61]. To investigate whether TTPAL is involved in regulating TAM infiltration and polarization in breast cancer, this study examined the recruitment of M2-polarized macrophages in breast cancer patients using IHC. The results revealed TTPAL was associated with a decrease in the number of M2 macrophages, suggesting that TTPAL may play a role in modulating the peritumoral microenvironment by influencing the abundance of M2 TAMs in breast cancer. In vitro co-culture experiments demonstrated that TTPAL overexpression suppressed tumor-induced M2 polarization of macrophages.

Tumor cells secrete various cytokines and distinct matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) [62–64]. These cytokines activate receptor cells, while the MMPs degrade the surrounding extracellular matrix (ECM), facilitating tumor cell motility and thereby promoting metastasis. In this study, through non-contact co-culture of breast cancer cells and macrophages, we found that breast cancer cells overexpressing TTPAL inhibited the M2 polarization of TAMs. Notably, treating macrophages with conditioned medium from TTPAL-overexpressing breast cancer cells produced the same effect. This indicates that TTPAL likely inhibits the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway, thereby regulating the transcriptional production of downstream cytokines and altering the secretome of breast cancer cells. This modified cytokine profile acts in a paracrine manner on macrophages to reprogram the tumor immune microenvironment. And in future research work, we will identify the key cytokine involved and validate their roles through additional experiments.

Conclusions

In this study, we showed that TTPAL protein was lowly expressed in breast cancer tissue and cells, which was associated with breast cancer progression and poor prognosis. In addition, using a series of experiments, we demonstrated that TTPAL overexpression significantly inhibited the breast cancer progression in vitro and in vivo. Importantly, we identified that TTPAL interacts with JAK2, thereby reducing STAT3 phosphorylation, which ultimately suppresses downstream signaling activation (Fig. 7). Thus, our study established the TTPAL/JAK2/STAT3 axis as an important regulatory mechanism of breast cancer progression and provided a rationale for future therapeutic interventions in the treatment of breast cancer.
