Biomechanical evaluation of structural integrity after tibial plateau leveling osteotomy in dogs using finite element analysis
Authors: Hyun-Seok Joh, Kihoon Kim, Heung-Myong Woo, Junhyung Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCranial cruciate ligament rupture is one of the most common orthopedic problems in dogs, and tibial plateau leveling osteotomy (TPLO) is the preferred surgical treatment. This study aimed to evaluate biomechanical interactions between bone and implant under various loading conditions (standing, walking, trotting, and galloping) after TPLO using finite element analysis, and to assess the risk of fatigue failure. Full-length computed tomography scans of the tibia and fibula were used to reconstruct three-dimensional models. Osteotomy was performed according to the TPLO procedure, and appropriate plates and screws were applied. Von Mises stress was concentrated near the third and sixth tibial screws and the proximal fibula, while displacement increased proximally, with the greatest displacement observed in the caudolateral tibia. Both stress and displacement increased with load. Fatigue analysis showed no failure under standing and walking conditions, but fatigue damage occurred in the tibia and fibula during trotting and galloping. These findings suggest that limited daily activity and partial weight-bearing may be feasible in the early postoperative period under controlled rehabilitation conditions. However, activities involving trotting and galloping may increase fatigue risk. This study provides biomechanical evidence to support safer and more effective postoperative rehabilitation strategies in dogs.Keywords: Tibial plateau leveling osteotomy (TPLO), Cranial cruciate ligament rupture, Structural integrity, Fatigue failure, Finite element analysisSubject terms: Anatomy, Engineering, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Cranial cruciate ligament (CrCL) rupture is one of the most common causes of hind limb lameness in dogs1. The CrCL is an important stabilizing structure in the stifle joint, preventing cranial tibial thrust, tibial internal rotation, and stifle hyperflexion2,3. Damage to this ligament can result in pain, lameness, muscle atrophy, osteoarthritis, and secondary meniscal damage4.

Surgical treatments for CrCL injuries are diverse; however, tibial plateau leveling osteotomy (TPLO) has been used for 30 years and is currently the most widely preferred method5–7. The tibial plateau in dogs is inclined caudally, unlike in humans. This angle, referred to as the tibial plateau angle (TPA), varies among dog breeds but averages approximately 24°, which is greater than in humans, and tends to be larger in small dogs7,8. TPLO surgery eliminates cranial tibial thrust and restores stifle stability by cutting the tibia in a circular fashion with a radial saw and then rotating the proximal bone fragment to reduce the TPA. This procedure was initially performed primarily in large dogs, but more recently it has been increasingly applied to small dogs as well, with favorable prognoses9.

TPLO surgery effectively eliminates cranial tibial thrust, but it does not completely control tibial internal rotation; therefore, surrounding muscles such as the hamstrings and biceps femoris must additionally stabilize the joint after surgery6. Muscle atrophy is a common complication after TPLO or CrCL surgery, and one study reported that thigh muscle mass continued to decrease for five weeks after CrCL transection and began to increase only after 10 weeks10. These findings suggest that without proper rehabilitation immediately after surgery, functional recovery may be delayed, and joint mobility and weight-bearing capacity may be negatively affected. Recent studies have demonstrated that initiating early rehabilitation exercises, such as leash walking, immediately after surgery facilitates faster recovery11,12.

However, uncontrolled exercise may increase the risk of complications after TPLO surgery. Appropriate exercise restrictions are crucial after orthopedic surgery, and failure to adhere to them may result in delayed recovery or surgical failure and, in some cases, necessitate additional surgical intervention13. The incidence of complications after TPLO surgery has been reported to range from approximately 9.7% to 28%14,15. The most common complications include surgical site infection and hematoma, whereas major complications involve tibial fracture, tibial tuberosity fracture, fibular fracture, and screw loosening. According to a study by Colletti et al., the risk of complications increases approximately 1.1-fold for every 4.5 kg increase in body weight14. These findings suggest that factors causing excessive load after surgery may further increase the incidence of complications. In small dogs, the bones are relatively thin, and the cross-sectional area is small; consequently, the moment of inertia is low, and bone density also tends to be reduced, thereby increasing the risk of fracture16,17. Therefore, it is essential to avoid excessive load immediately after TPLO surgery and to promote recovery by gradually applying an appropriate level of load.

Previous biomechanical studies on TPLO have mainly focused on comparing the shapes of plates and screws, the alignment accuracy of the osteotomy fragment, or analyzing changes in intra-articular pressure following meniscectomy13–15. To date, no studies have quantitatively assessed stress distribution and fracture potential in bone and implants under different gait conditions (standing, walking, trotting, and galloping). The circular osteotomy performed in TPLO results in complex biomechanical characteristics, making quantitative numerical analysis essential for accurately assessing stress and displacement in the bone, screws, and plate under different loading conditions. Accordingly, this study aimed to analyze the mechanical response to load under various gait conditions immediately after TPLO surgery and to evaluate risk factors that may lead to fracture.

The purpose of this study was to quantify mechanical stress and displacement in the implant, tibia, and fibula under early gait conditions after TPLO surgery using finite element analysis (FEA), and to evaluate fatigue failure and mechanical stability under different loading conditions. The ultimate aim was to provide a biomechanical basis for the feasibility of early rehabilitation and the range of load tolerance in patients immediately after TPLO surgery, thereby supplying fundamental data for developing clinical veterinary rehabilitation plans.

Methods

Ethics approval

This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Kangwon National University (Approval No. KW-250626-2). All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines of Kangwon National University and applicable national regulations. Written informed consent was obtained from the dog guardians before the inclusion of clinical imaging data in this study.

Finite element modeling

Multi-slice computed tomography (CT) of the hind limb of a small dog (age: 8 years, weight: 6.8 kg, breed: Miniature Poodle, BCS: 5/9) with no stifle joint abnormalities was obtained using a 16-slice helical CT scanner (Alexion, Canon, Japan) at a slice thickness of 0.5 mm. The images were saved in DICOM (Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine) format and reconstructed into three-dimensional models using 3D Slicer (version 5.6.2, https://www.slicer.org). Pre-processed STL files underwent noise removal, hole filling, and surface smoothing using Meshmixer (version 3.5.474, Autodesk Inc., USA). Cortical and cancellous bone were distinguished through multiplanar reconstruction (MPR) of CT data, and cortical bone thickness was set to approximately 1 mm based on measured values.

The STL file was then imported into Ansys SpaceClaim 2024 R2 (ANSYS Inc., USA), where cortical and cancellous bone were separated to create a solid model. Virtual TPLO surgery was performed according to the Slocum method6. The tibial long axis was defined by connecting the center of the intercondylar eminence and the talus in the lateral view, and the TPA was measured as the angle formed with a line connecting the cranial and caudal margins of the tibial plateau18. A cylinder with a radius of 12 mm was created, and the tibia was osteotomized such that the central axis of the cylinder was positioned on the intercondylar eminence. The proximal bone fragment was then rotated to adjust the TPA to 6°, as shown in Fig. 115,19.

Three-dimensional tibia–fibula models before and after TPLO. (A) Intact tibia–fibula model before osteotomy. (B) Tibia–fibula model after TPLO.

After surgery, a 2.0 mm TPLO plate was applied, and the STL file of the plate was provided by the manufacturer (Arix Vet, Jeil Medical, Republic of Korea). The screw was modeled as a cylinder with a diameter of 2.0 mm and was configured for insertion along the trajectory defined by the screw hole of the plate20. The screws were numbered to facilitate systematic and comparative analysis (Fig. 1).

The final mesh was determined by adjusting the mesh size so that variations in von Mises stress or displacement did not exceed 5%. The global element size was set to 0.40 mm. A finer mesh than the minimum number of elements required to satisfy the convergence criteria was then applied to achieve more stable and reliable results. In this study, four-node tetrahedral elements were used. The mesh was refined at the screw–bone interfaces, plate holes, and osteotomy lines to better resolve stress gradients. This mesh strategy was selected to provide a reproducible and numerically stable basis for evaluating structural integrity, stress/displacement distributions, and stress–life fatigue behavior of the TPLO construct21. Mesh verification analysis confirmed that the model quality was suitable for analysis.

Material properties

The model was imported into Ansys Workbench 2024 R2 (ANSYS Inc., Canonsburg, PA, USA). The implants, including the plate and screws, were assigned the Ti6Al4V alloy. Both implants and bone were assumed to be elastic, linear, and isotropic. Cortical bone was assigned a Young’s modulus of 15 GPa and a Poisson’s ratio of 0.3; cancellous bone was assigned a Young’s modulus of 600 MPa and a Poisson’s ratio of 0.3; and the titanium alloy (plate and screws) was assigned a Young’s modulus of 110 GPa and a Poisson’s ratio of 0.322–25.

Load and boundary conditions

The load-applied area was defined as the concave region formed by the line connecting the cranial and caudal horns of the medial and lateral menisci26,27. The load was applied as static compressive loading and was gradually ramped to the assigned load magnitude. Four gait conditions were simulated—standing (static weight-bearing), walking (slow and stable gait), trotting (symmetric diagonal-limb gait), and galloping (fast asymmetric gait)—corresponding to 30%, 70%, 85%, and 155% of body weight, respectively (Fig. 2)26,28–30. applied as a uniformly distributed surface force over the medial and lateral meniscal regions, with the applied value multiplied by factors of 2.3 and 2.1 for the medial and lateral menisci, respectively26. The direction of the load was aligned with the mechanical axis, representing the weight-bearing axis (Fig. 2)31,32.

The contact conditions were set to reflect mechanical interactions between bone and implant. Cortical and cancellous bone within each bone segment was modeled with bonded contact, and the fibula was bonded to the tibia using a multi-point constraint method at the proximal tibia27,33–35. The distal portions of the tibia and fibula were constrained as fixed supports without displacement. A friction coefficient of 0.4 was applied between the proximal and distal tibial segments at the osteotomy interface27,36. The plate and screws were defined as bonded contact to simulate the locking compression plate (LCP) method37. Screws and cancellous bone were assigned a friction coefficient of 0.8, while screws and cortical bone were constrained as fixed supports27,38,39. The plate–screw interface was defined as bonded contact, simulating the locking compression plate method, and no friction was assumed between the plate and the bone. These boundary and contact conditions were selected based on prior finite element protocols and published experimental data to represent postoperative fixation.

Fatigue properties

Fatigue analysis was performed using a stress–life method for stress amplitudes obtained from the von Mises stresses derived from static finite element analyses. Life predictions were made for cortical bone and titanium implants, as both can significantly influence postoperative failure. The S–N relationships were defined using literature-reported empirical equations and corrected for mean-stress effects using the Goodman relation40,41. Ultimate strength values of 960 MPa for Grade V titanium alloy and 142.1 MPa for cortical bone were used in the mean-stress correction42,43. A fatigue-life threshold of 10⁶ cycles was applied, and failure was considered to have occurred when the predicted life was below this threshold44. Cyclic loading was applied as a zero-based (compression–zero–compression) cycle (R = 0) to represent weight-bearing in stance and unloading during the swing phase in gait.

Results

Von Mises stress

Von Mises stress under four gait conditions (standing, walking, trotting, and galloping) after TPLO surgery was quantitatively analyzed for each component (plate, screws, tibia, and fibula). As the applied load increased with gait intensity, the stress acting on both the implant and the bone also increased. The plate experienced the highest stress among all model components, with maximum stress concentration observed at the caudal curvature of the plate (Fig. 3). The maximum stresses in the plate for each gait condition were 38.52 MPa in standing, 88.90 MPa in walking, 107.30 MPa in trotting, and 169.61 MPa in galloping (Fig. 4). For the screws, high stress was concentrated in the third and sixth screws. Stress increased progressively from the first to the third screw, decreased after the fourth screw, and then increased again at the sixth screw. The lowest stress was observed at screw 2 during standing and at screw 1 during walking, trotting, and galloping. Stress was primarily concentrated around the screw head. In the bone, the maximum stress of 111.2 MPa occurred at the tibiofibular junction during galloping, but this level did not reach the material’s fracture limit, suggesting that the structure remained within the safe stress range under all loading conditions. Additionally, high stresses were observed in the tibial condyle, the mid-distal fibula, and around the sixth screw of the distal fragment across all gait conditions. In the proximal fragment, stresses were widely distributed around the first and third screws, while in the distal fragment, stresses tended to concentrate in the caudal region.

Displacement

In this study, displacement was defined as the resultant displacement. As the applied load increased, overall displacement also increased. The maximum displacement values under the four gait conditions (standing, walking, trotting, and galloping) were 0.098 mm, 0.217 mm, 0.261 mm, and 0.420 mm, respectively. During galloping, the maximum displacement (0.30 mm) occurred at point F along the osteotomy line (A–F), the second highest displacement (0.27 mm) was observed at point A, and the minimum displacement (0.20 mm) was recorded at point D. When measured at the tibial plateau condyle, similar maximum displacement values were observed at points I (0.416 mm) and J (0.415 mm), while the lowest displacement (0.29 mm) occurred at point K. Displacement under all gait conditions remained below the clinically acceptable threshold of 2 mm (Fig. 5). Displacement was greatest in the proximal region and showed an increasing trend toward the lateral and caudal directions of the tibial plateau. Quantitative analysis indicated that lateral measurement points (G–J) exhibited greater displacement than medial points (K-N), with the largest displacement observed in the caudolateral region of the tibial plateau (Fig. 6).

Fatigue analysis

The fatigue analysis results based on 106 cycles showed that no fatigue failure was predicted in the bone under standing and walking conditions, and no fatigue failure was predicted for the implant under any of the gait conditions. However, as shown in Fig. 7, during trotting the first fatigue failure in the bone was predicted at the tibiofibular junction at 91,807 cycles. During galloping, fatigue failure was observed at the same site at 437 cycles, which was markedly earlier. In addition, during trotting, failure occurred at 404,340 cycles in the mid-distal portion of the fibula, whereas during galloping, failure occurred at 5,344 cycles in the same region. Furthermore, under galloping conditions, fatigue failure occurred at 8,219 cycles in the sixth screw hole of the distal bone fragment and at 425,180 cycles in the fibular neck (Figure 8).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to quantitatively evaluate biomechanical behavior during the early rehabilitation phase following TPLO surgery using FEA. In particular, the distribution of mechanical stress and displacement patterns acting on the canine tibial structure was visualized, and the potential for fatigue fracture in the bone was analyzed. Appropriate weight-bearing in early rehabilitation is crucial because it promotes bone healing and positively influences functional recovery. However, excessive exercise may hinder recovery or cause damage to the surgical site. Unlike humans, dogs often have difficulty adhering to exercise restrictions immediately after surgery and may suddenly jump or move excessively when excited or upon seeing their owner. In such cases, unexpected loads may be applied to the bone and implant, making it clinically important to quantitatively evaluate these mechanical effects. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the biomechanical behavior of the TPLO construct during the early postoperative period by analyzing stress distribution, displacement, and fatigue behavior under various gait conditions (standing, walking, trotting, and galloping).

The von Mises stresses analyzed in this study were concentrated at the tibiofibular junction. This distribution can be influenced by the interface definitions in our model, including multi-point constraints between the tibia and fibula and bonded contact between cortical and cancellous bone27,33–35. These constraints increase local interfacial stiffness, which can elevate stresses in adjacent regions. Given the clinical relevance of the tibiofibular region in TPLO, the proximal tibia–fibula interaction was represented using an MPC coupling to approximate ligamentous constraint, consistent with previously reported finite element approaches27,35. Under these conditions, the stress concentration observed in the tibiofibular junction was consistent with previously reported TPLO failure patterns, including fibular disarticulation, valgus deformity, and lateral collapse, as described in prior biomechanical and clinical studies45–47. This stress concentration appears to be closely related to the increased displacement in the lateral and caudal directions. Because the high-stiffness plate is positioned on the medial side, greater displacement may occur in the lateral bone—which is relatively more flexible—when load is applied. In addition, the circular osteotomy accentuates caudal displacement, which contributes to stress concentration in the tibiofibular junction located on the caudolateral side. If stress in this region is repeated during gait, failure may occur at the tibiofibular junction. This mechanism is consistent with the pattern reported by Tuttle et al. and Taylor et al., in which most fibular fractures occur during the recovery period rather than during surgery, thereby providing a mechanistic basis for explaining the cause of this injury46,48.

Previous biomechanical analyses of TPLO have reported fractures occurring in screw holes of the proximal or distal bone fragment, or in the bone itself45,47. In this study, stress concentration areas related to the implant were observed at the third and sixth screws and at the caudal curvature of the plate. Elevated stress at S3, adjacent to the osteotomy in the proximal fragment, may be related to the rock-back phenomenon, as this screw may contribute to resisting proximal fragment rotation49. Elevated stress at S6, located in the distal plate segment, may reflect plating biomechanics in which screw loading is influenced by the plate working length and screw position50. This finding suggests that the plate effectively transferred load to the distal portion, preventing excessive stress concentration at the osteotomy line, although localized stress concentrations may occur at specific screws and screw holes. Such concentrations may lead to fatigue failure under repeated loading. In this study, the maximum stresses applied to the plate and screws were significantly lower than the ultimate strength of the titanium alloy, and no implant failure was observed, which is consistent with findings reported in previous studies45,47. The stress concentration areas can be explained mechanically by their proximity to the mechanical axis and their caudal location relative to the plate. In addition, stress distribution may be influenced by factors such as the anatomical configuration of the bone, osteotomy site, and plate design. Therefore, the observation of relatively high stress in screws inserted into the proximal bone fragment should be interpreted cautiously in light of these complex factors.

To evaluate the biomechanical load acting on the canine tibia, axial loads were applied according to four representative gait patterns: standing, walking, trotting, and galloping. Standing is a static posture in which the dog supports its body weight on all four limbs with minimal joint movement and external impact. Walking is a slow gait in which each limb alternately contacts the ground, characterized by high stability because more than two limbs are always in contact with the ground during the gait cycle. Trotting is a symmetrical, medium-speed gait in which the forelimbs and hindlimbs on a diagonal line contact the ground simultaneously. In this gait, propulsion and joint range of motion are increased, and peak loads are greater compared to walking. Galloping is the fastest asymmetrical gait, in which momentary impact and load are maximized owing to the short contact time and high propulsion force. The applied axial load may be further amplified by anatomical factors in the stifle joint. The proximal tibia has a caudally inclined tibial plateau, and owing to the influence of the quadriceps muscle and surrounding ligamentous structures, the force transmitted to the stifle joint is greater than that of a simple axial load. Indeed, previous studies have reported that the load applied to the meniscus can reach approximately three to four times body weight26,30. Reflecting these biomechanical characteristics, coefficients of 2.3 and 2.1 were applied to the medial and lateral menisci, respectively26.

Displacement measurement points A to F were placed along the proximal surface of the osteotomy line to evaluate the potential for bone union, whereas points G to N were positioned at the most medial and lateral aspects of the tibial condyles to allow comparison of displacement differences. Displacement increased progressively with greater load. Among the osteotomy sites, point F showed the greatest displacement (0.30 mm), followed by point A (0.27 mm). Previous studies have reported that displacement of less than 2 mm at the osteotomy line is considered clinically stable51,52. In this study, even under galloping conditions, displacement at the osteotomy site remained within clinically acceptable limits, suggesting mechanical stability. As shown in Fig. 5, displacement was visualized in a band-like pattern, with the greatest displacement in the proximal region, gradually increasing toward the lateral and caudal directions. When comparing specific points, the greatest displacement occurred at point I, while the smallest displacement was observed at point D. This pattern suggests that a subtle rotational force may be applied to the proximal bone segment in the direction of increasing the TPA. Notably, this distribution appears to correlate with the “rock-back” phenomenon occasionally observed after TPLO surgery. The “rock-back” phenomenon, defined as an increase in the TPA following TPLO, has been reported in several studies; however, its underlying cause remains unclear. An increase in TPA was observed by Kergosien et al. and Bergh et al., while McLean et al. attempted to investigate potential mechanisms, although a definitive explanation has not yet been established49. The displacement pattern observed in this study suggests that increased loading may induce a rotational force on the proximal bone fragment, potentially contributing to one of the mechanical factors associated with the rock-back phenomenon. These findings may provide foundational data for elucidating the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying rock-back after TPLO surgery.

This stress-based workflow was selected because the state of stress can directly reflect how weight-bearing capabilities are transmitted within the bone–implant construct and has been used for comparative evaluation of bone in recent orthopedic finite element studies27,53. In addition, prior FE fatigue studies have similarly used static-analysis stresses as inputs for fatigue calculations54,55. Accordingly, the fatigue-life results reported here should be interpreted as model-based predictions for relative comparison of fatigue-risk trends across loading conditions rather than as absolute predictions of clinical failure. Because the model does not incorporate biological recovery processes such as bone healing and microdamage repair, the predicted fatigue lives may be conservative. Nevertheless, because postoperative activity restriction may not be strictly followed in canine patients, a conservative worst-case assessment based on peak stress may be appropriate for identifying loading conditions that increase the risk of catastrophic complications.

In this study, the occurrence of fatigue failure was evaluated by applying repeated loads under various gait conditions after TPLO surgery. No fatigue failure was predicted in the cortical bone under standing and walking conditions; however, fatigue failure was predicted at the tibiofibular junction after 91,807 cycles during trotting and after only 437 cycles during galloping. Additionally, failure was predicted at 425,180 cycles in the fibular neck, at 5,344 cycles in the mid-distal region of the fibula, and at 8,219 cycles adjacent to the sixth screw hole. These regions correspond to areas where von Mises stress was previously high in the corresponding static analyses, suggesting that these structures are relatively vulnerable to repetitive loading. These results indicate that limited exercise—particularly controlled rehabilitation activities such as short leash walking that prevents trotting—may be feasible immediately after TPLO surgery. However, this study did not account for the physiological processes of bone healing and remodeling; therefore, in a biological environment, failure may occur at a later time point. Nevertheless, when repetitive loads equivalent to several times body weight are concentrated on bone, the risk of structural failure can increase substantially owing to the accumulation of fatigue damage. Based on the reported daily step counts, if this activity is repeated under trotting conditions, fatigue failure could occur within about 5 days in the absence of exercise restriction56. Therefore, during the early rehabilitation period, strenuous activities such as excitement, jumping, and running need to be restricted, whereas controlled low-intensity activities such as short-leash walking may be applied. In clinical practice, in patients for whom exercise restriction is difficult, excessive movement may be controlled through pharmacological measures57. In conclusion, this study demonstrates that rehabilitation therapy immediately after TPLO surgery can play an important role in functional recovery, while emphasizing that load management and activity restriction during the early rehabilitation phase are important for maintaining stability at the surgical site.

The effectiveness and necessity of rehabilitation therapy after TPLO surgery have been repeatedly demonstrated in various studies. Marsolais et al. reported that dogs receiving postoperative rehabilitation showed significant improvements in ground reaction forces (peak vertical force, vertical impulse) compared with those managed using traditional activity restriction58. Monk et al. also observed increased thigh circumference and improved range of motion in dogs that underwent intensive rehabilitation immediately after surgery10. Although TPLO improves the mechanical stability of the stifle joint by reducing the TPA, it does not completely eliminate cranial tibial thrust6. This residual thrust must be controlled by active stabilizers, such as the biceps femoris and semimembranosus muscles. Therefore, early rehabilitation aimed at restoring muscle function is essential for maintaining joint stability and accelerating overall functional recovery12. Regarding the safety of early postoperative rehabilitation, a large retrospective study by Romano et al. found no significant difference in perioperative complication rates between dogs receiving certified rehabilitation therapy and those managed using traditional restriction59. Moreover, dogs in the rehabilitation group were 1.9 times more likely to achieve functional recovery within 8 weeks and had a 2.9-fold lower incidence of unacceptable outcomes59. These findings support rehabilitation intervention, even immediately after TPLO surgery, as a safe and effective strategy for promoting recovery.

This study was based on FEA and, like similar simulations, has several limitations. First, bone was modeled as an isotropic, linear elastic material, and soft tissues (e.g., menisci, cartilage, ligaments, muscles) were excluded. A homogeneous bone material model was used to improve computational efficiency and feasibility while maintaining reliable comparative predictions across various loading conditions. This approach limits the ability to fully replicate physiological conditions, and incorporating heterogeneous bone properties and soft tissues in future work may improve physiological fidelity and refine model-based predictions. Second, only static axial loads were applied; dynamic forces such as flexion, torsion, and shear that occur during actual gait or rehabilitation were not considered. Future studies should incorporate time-dependent and cyclic loading. Third, in the virtual surgery conducted during 3D modeling, the osteotomy was assumed to be perfectly reduced. In addition, screws were modeled as smooth cylinders without thread geometry to simplify the computational process. In clinical practice, however, achieving precise osteotomy reduction is technically challenging, and omission of thread representation may influence stress distribution at the screw–bone interface. These modeling assumptions, while necessary for simplification, may limit the accuracy of simulated implant–bone interactions. Fourth, the material properties and boundary conditions applied in this study were derived from values reported in previous literature, and the bone model itself was generated from CT data of a single individual.

As such, the model does not reflect inter-individual variability in anatomical structure, age, or bone quality, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future studies incorporating subject-specific cadaveric data, multi-subject models, and sensitivity analyses varying body weight, bone geometry, and material properties would improve the fidelity and clinical applicability of the simulation results.

In the future, large-scale clinical studies and experimental validation using cadaveric specimens will be necessary to encompass a wider range of anatomical structures and biomechanical responses.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that FEA can effectively predict mechanical stress, displacement, and fatigue life under different gait conditions, offering model-based insights into postoperative loading during early rehabilitation. These findings provide a biomechanical data relevant to the timing and intensity of rehabilitation. Future studies incorporating complex loading scenarios, breed-specific anatomical variations, biological responses, and implant design refinements are expected to improve model accuracy and clinical relevance.
