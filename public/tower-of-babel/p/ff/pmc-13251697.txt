ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundRespiratory infections cause major morbidity and mortality globally, highlighting the need for robust early-warning systems (EWS). The Alert-Early System of Outbreaks with Pandemic Potential (ÆSOP) was co-developed with surveillance stakeholders to detect outbreaks using administrative primary health care (PHC) data. We aimed to report ÆSOP's feasibility and performance for detecting influenza-like illness (ILI) outbreaks in a real-world context and to document lessons learned from translating research into routine health services.MethodsThe pilot was conducted in Amazonas, Brazil, from April to July 2024, covering 62 municipalities. ÆSOP relies on anomaly detection models applied to weekly counts of ILI-related PHC encounters, and municipal-level warnings are issued when thresholds are exceeded. Local health authorities validated warnings through a structured questionnaire. Performance was evaluated by sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive value (PPV and NPV).FindingsDuring the study period, 1.8 million PHC encounters were reported, of which 6.6% were ILI-related. ÆSOP issued 104 warnings across 41 municipalities. Sensitivity was 62.8% (95% CI 54.4%–70.5%), specificity 95.5% (95% CI 93.1%–97.1%), PPV 82.7%, (95% CI 74.3%–88.8%), and NPV 88.1% (95% CI 84.7%–90.9%). In 72% of confirmed outbreaks, ÆSOP provided the first signal to authorities, and in 56%, warnings supported responses including enhanced risk communication, equipment/test deployment, and strengthening of intergovernmental coordination.InterpretationÆSOP anticipated outbreaks in real-world usage and was validated by authorities as a decision-support tool. Its reliance on administrative data and open-source technologies enhances scalability and adaptability across health systems globally.FundingRockefeller Foundation (award 2023 PPI 007).

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched articles indexed in PubMed on August 1, 2025, without language or date restrictions, using the following search strategy: ((“early warning system” OR “syndromic surveillance” OR “outbreak detection” OR “epidemic intelligence”) AND (“respiratory infection” OR “respiratory disease” OR “influenza-like illness” OR “ILI” OR “COVID-19″ OR “influenza”) AND (“primary health care” OR “administrative data” OR “health information system” OR “routine data")). The search returned 44 records, all of which were screened and systematically assessed. Of these, 13 original research studies described the implementation (at varying levels of maturity) of surveillance systems for respiratory diseases using routinely collected data. The geographies covered in these studies included the United Kingdom (four studies), Brazil (two studies describing contributions of our research group in validating and describing of the ÆSOP EWS evaluated herein), Spain (two studies), as well as Australia, Canada, China, Germany, and Micronesia (one study each). Overall, although numerous initiatives have leveraged alternative or routinely collected data sources to anticipate increases in respiratory diseases, and some have been formalized as EWSs operating at national or sub-national scales, most studies lack formal assessments of real-world performance. In particular, independent validation of alerts generated by these systems using decisions or actions taken by health authorities was largely absent, limiting evaluation of the practical utility and public health impact of these systems.

Added value of this study

Despite the urgent need to strengthen the anticipatory capacity of disease surveillance through the deployment of EWSs that are tested and validated under real-world conditions (i.e. outside simulated scenarios), few studies have measured the performance of many literature-reported systems. This study addresses this gap by conducting a pilot implementation of the ÆSOP EWS, which uses routinely collected administrative data from primary health care attendances to detect outbreaks of respiratory diseases, in the state of Amazonas, Brazil, over an eleven epidemiological week period. Alerts generated by the system were independently reviewed and assessed by health authorities using external data sources including field data, allowing the calculation of standard performance metrics for the EWS by flagging each alert as true or false. Furthermore, the practical value of each alert was evaluated based on its contribution to public health decision-making, enabling an assessment of the system's real-world impact. Our findings support the use of the ÆSOP EWS as a practical, operationally useful tool and highlight the importance of field-based performance evaluation for EWSs.

Implications of all the available evidence

The evidence shows that, despite the need to improve the anticipatory capacity of surveillance systems, few EWSs are rigorously evaluated under real-world conditions, limiting their policy relevance. This study demonstrates that a validated EWS embedded in routine primary health care data can generate actionable alerts with measurable performance when assessed by health authorities. Systematic field evaluation and intersectoral collaboration (particularly among academia and public health authorities) in the design and deployment of EWSs are essential components of effective surveillance policy and pandemic preparedness strategies.

Introduction

Over the past century, humankind has experienced five pandemics caused by respiratory viruses: 1918 (influenza H1N1), 1957 (influenza H2N2), 1968 (influenza H3N2), 2009 (influenza H1N1), and 2020 (SARS-CoV-2), claiming millions of lives globally.1 Even during inter-pandemic periods, pandemic-prone pathogens and localized epidemics pose a significant burden of disease, inflicting increased morbidity and excess mortality,2 and several studies have suggested that a future pandemic is most likely to be caused by respiratory viruses.3 Improvements to early-warning systems (EWS), such as technological platforms for detecting outbreaks before they turn into large-scale emergencies, are crucially needed.4 Next-generation EWS rely on data streams with greater potential for early detection than traditional surveillance systems, which are typically based on mandatory notification of cases from a pre-specified list of diseases and do not prioritize early warning.5,6 Examples of data streams that have been used to inform EWS include work and school absenteeism, over-the-counter drug sales, internet-based signals, and visits to emergency departments or primary health care units.7,8 However, the availability and quality of these data streams vary considerably across countries, reflecting differences in the breadth, scope, and governance of health systems worldwide.

The Brazilian Unified Health System (SUS, in its Portuguese abbreviation) provides comprehensive health care, free at the point of delivery, to the entire population.9 The effective management of the SUS relies on diverse information systems, resulting in a wealth of administrative registries. These readily available data represent a valuable resource for syndromic surveillance, enabling the identification of disease clusters before diagnoses are confirmed.10,11 Building on this, we developed the Alert-Early System of Outbreaks with Pandemic Potential (ÆSOP), a data-driven, next-generation EWS that uses syndromic surveillance for the early detection of infectious disease outbreaks.12

ÆSOP's primary data source is the Brazilian national Primary Health Care (PHC) database. Managed by the Ministry of Health (MoH), this hierarchical and decentralized information system compiles data on all PHC encounters to inform resource allocation from the federal to the municipal level. PHC services operate with high geographic granularity13 and are a common entry point to the health system for individuals presenting with mild-to-moderate symptoms. Therefore, surges in PHC encounters related to respiratory complaints are expected to precede increases in hospitalizations, where a smaller fraction of severe cases require specialized care.

The ÆSOP system development (see section ÆSOP's ideation) started in 2023, following an agreement with the MoH for the regular transfer of national data on PHC encounters to be integrated into the EWS. Initial steps included data curation and technical alignment to ensure the swift and secure transfer of weekly updated data. A Data Quality Index (DQI) assessing whether the PHC administrative database met quality requirements for syndromic surveillance was developed and integrated into the EWS.14 In parallel, anomaly detection methods to identify unusual deviations from baseline in these time series, yielding alerts of potential outbreaks, were explored through proof-of-concept studies. Findings from these studies demonstrate that the PHC-based EWS provides up to a three-week lead time relative to hospitalizations for severe respiratory disease.15 A comparable early detection capacity was observed for dengue when contrasted with SINAN, the Brazilian Information System for Notifiable Diseases, traditionally used for surveillance of this disease.16,17

To improve preparedness and response to future outbreaks, the feasibility of an EWS must be evaluated as a pragmatic, context-adapted tool. Here we report a pilot study to assess the real-world effectiveness of the ÆSOP EWS in detecting outbreaks of influenza-like illness (ILI) in the state of Amazonas, Brazil, over eleven epidemiological weeks of 2024. We present the system's performance results and share lessons learned from our engagement with end-users. Our findings demonstrate the potential of routinely collected administrative data to support timely and evidence-based decision-making, offering practical insights for scaling and adapting EWS in comparable settings.

Methods

Ethics statement

The study protocol leading to the ÆSOP initiative, based on secondary, aggregated, non-identified data, was approved by the Ethical Review Board of Oswaldo Cruz Foundation - Fiocruz Bahia, protocol CAAE: 61444122.0.0000.0040.

ÆSOP's ideation

From its conception, ÆSOP was designed to be adopted by Brazil's health authorities, with development centered on addressing public health needs. Ensuring the participation of diverse stakeholders across the public health sector and establishing an open channel for communication were key to the system's ideation. In 2022, a co-creation workshop (reported in Supplementary File S1) brought together infectious disease specialists, epidemiologists, data scientists, and surveillance authorities to collectively define key principles, implementation requirements, and priority diseases for monitoring, among which respiratory outbreaks emerged as a critical focus.12 Regular check-ins and iterative improvements followed, informed by feedback from surveillance authorities at the municipal, state and federal levels, enabling a fit-for-purpose implementation. These authorities were identified as the main potential users of this EWS. In 2023, stakeholders were reconvened in a workshop to present results and discuss progress. At that meeting, the MoH recommended conducting a pilot study, described herein, to evaluate the system's performance prior to its integration into the national surveillance framework.

ÆSOP EWS operationalization

At the time the pilot was conducted, the system relied on data from the National Information System on Primary Care (SISAB) for syndromic surveillance and outbreak detection. The SISAB database collects data of all publicly funded PHC encounters in Brazil, which covers at least 75% of the Brazilian population (approximately 160 million people), extending its reach to regions lacking more advanced healthcare facilities, including underserved rural and remote regions. All encounters in the database are coded by either the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10) or the International Classification of Primary Care, 2nd edition (ICPC-2). For the ÆSOP development, we reached an agreement with the MoH for the regular, weekly transfer of the national PHC database aggregated by municipality, date and reason for encounter. Supplementary Figure S1 illustrates the overall data pipeline from a PHC encounter registry in a health facility to data transferring to ÆSOP.

We curated a list of 50 ICD-10 and ICPC-2 codes based on clinical expert review to define ILI-related encounters, as detailed in our previous work15 (Supplementary Table S1).

The data analysis workflow of the ÆSOP EWS consists of a two-step automated pipeline: An initial data quality assessment followed by the application of anomaly detection algorithms. Because SISAB is an administrative database primarily designed for operational purposes, a real-time data quality index (DQI) was developed to ensure its suitability for epidemiological surveillance.14 The DQI is a composite indicator assessing the timeliness, completeness and consistency of weekly data updates. Using an 8-week moving window, timeliness is defined as at least 80% of reported data referring to encounters in the same or previous week. Completeness is defined as the presence of at least one PHC encounter record in each week of the rolling window, irrespective of volume. Consistency is defined as the number of PHC encounters reported in a given week not falling below the threshold of the mean weekly volume over the previous three months minus 2 standard deviations, thereby capturing unusually low reporting volumes. The DQI is expressed as “suitable” when all criteria are met, and each week the EWS generates warnings only for municipalities with a suitable DQI (Supplementary Figure S1).14 Subsequently, previously described anomaly detection algorithms are applied to the PHC time series to identify early warning signals of potential ILI outbreaks: the Early Aberration Reporting System (EARS, variation C2), a widely used statistical method for detecting early signals of health events based on the cumulative sum (CUSUM) method,18 the Epidemic Volatility Index (EVI) developed by Koustolas et al. based on calculating the rolling standard deviation of case counts in a time series,19 and the Mixed Model of Artificial Intelligence and Next-Generation method (MMAING), an unsupervised machine learning method developed by our team.20

To assess the anticipatory capacity of these methods for early anomaly detection using PHC data, we conducted proof-of-concept analyses testing different algorithms, parametrizations, and baseline lengths. Performance metrics varied minimally across configurations.15,20 Importantly, these findings reflect averages across all 5570 municipalities in the country, and the best-performing configuration at the national level may not generalize to each local context. In this context, the optimal balance between sensitivity, PPV, and related metrics depends on the public health strategy adopted by local authorities. Given our aim to prioritize system usability, and informed by stakeholder input, we optimized the ÆSOP EWS to balance sensitivity with the operational constraints faced by municipalities. The summarized documentation of anomaly detection methods implemented in the dashboard, and performance metrics results from proof-of-concept analyses are available in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3.

Finally, an ensemble approach combining the three methods through a hard voting scheme was implemented. This enables the system to issue municipal-level warnings of potential ILI outbreaks, which are displayed on a dashboard. We opted for a hard voting ensemble scheme because it provides a robust, unsupervised approach to signal aggregation. It preserves model independence while minimizing variability without relying on a shared, pre-defined outbreak threshold definition.

An open-source technological stack was used to create the user interface within the dashboard, ensuring transparency, flexibility, and reproducibility. For the frontend, the React.js javascript library was used to implement the application, combined with bootstrap for responsive design. Features include interactive graphs generated with Chart.js and dynamic maps and other geographical visualizations created with Leaflet showing municipal-level warnings, as well as tables that enable data export. The backend relies on MySQL for efficient data storage and a Laravel API to enable automated data access and updates. Screen captures and available functionalities in the platform are detailed in Supplementary File S2. Data quality assessment and anomaly detection algorithms were implemented in Python (version 3.9) and R (version 4.3.1).

Pilot description and communication flow

The pilot study assessing the ÆSOP EWS was conducted in the state of Amazonas, Brazil, following a proposal by the MoH. The state of Amazonas, with 4.28 million inhabitants distributed across 62 municipalities, represents a strategic region of the country, sharing borders with five other states (Roraima, Pará, Mato Grosso, Rondônia, and Acre) and three countries (Colombia, Peru, and Venezuela). The state is also home to Brazil's largest Indigenous population (490,935 people or 29% of the national total).21 Notably, influenza seasonality in the Amazon region coincides with the months during which the pilot was launched, preceding the seasonality observed in the rest of the country.

The pilot was conducted from epidemiological week (EW) 18 (starting April 28) to EW 28 (starting July 7) of 2024. During this period, weekly ÆSOP warnings were issued to local health authorities, who then verified the actual epidemiological situation. Information followed a consensually defined chain of communication that included federal, state and municipal levels, as well as the National Center for Strategic Information in Health Surveillance (CIEVS) network (Fig. 1). Verification and risk assessment of ÆSOP warnings were based on local confirmation by health authorities, following official guidelines for early warning alert and response, as well as established local protocols.6,22 This process includes consultations with health facilities and surveillance officials, along with combining contextual information, such as signals from Media-Based Event Surveillance conducted by the National CIEVS. The outcome of local verification was recorded as a binary variable (confirmed/not confirmed) and was regarded as the ground truth, as the pilot was meant to simulate the real-world incorporation of ÆSOP EWS into routine surveillance. Initially, information and alerts were exchanged among all parties (the research team; municipal and state health authorities in Amazonas; and federal health authorities) via Excel spreadsheets shared by e-mail. Midway through the pilot, this mechanism was replaced by an online dashboard containing the same information but allowing real-time edition and data access to all engaged.

EWS performance evaluation

We evaluated the EWS performance by assessing its sensitivity, specificity, and positive and negative predictive values (PPV and NPV, respectively). Since the EWS was designed for early outbreak detection, we hypothesized that true outbreaks might only be confirmed by health authorities after the system had issued a warning. Accordingly, an ÆSOP warning was considered a true positive (TP) when an outbreak was confirmed by local health authorities in the same week or the following week after issuance. Conversely, a true negative (TN) was defined as the absence of an outbreak report by health authorities in the same or the following week, in the absence of an ÆSOP warning.

Sensitivity was defined as the proportion of all outbreaks identified by health authorities that were timely detected by the EWS, i.e. in the same or the preceding week. It was calculated by dividing the number of TP warnings by the total number of outbreaks identified by health authorities. Specificity was defined as the capacity of the system to identify weeks with no outbreaks, and it was calculated by dividing the number of TN by the total number of weeks with no confirmed outbreaks. PPV was defined as the probability that an ÆSOP warning corresponded to a confirmed outbreak, estimated by dividing the number of TP warnings by the total number of ÆSOP warnings, while NPV was defined by the probability that the non-issuance of an ÆSOP warning corresponded to a non-outbreak week. NPV was estimated by dividing the number of TN by the number of weeks with no ÆSOP warnings (Supplementary Table S4).

The funders did not play any role in study design; in the collection, analysis, and interpretation of data; in the writing of the report; nor in the decision to submit the paper for publication.

Importantly, of the 86 ÆSOP warnings confirmed by local authorities, in 62 instances (71.9%) health authorities reported that the EWS was their first source of awareness of the outbreak. Furthermore, in 48 (55.8%) events, health authorities indicated that the system contributed to decision-making.

In situations where a warning was followed by the confirmation of an outbreak, the most frequently reported response measures included strengthening risk communication, consulting experts within health departments, enhancing communication between state and municipal levels, and providing protective health equipment (such as facial masks) and pharmaceutical supplies, including increasing the availability of COVID-19 test kits.

Discussion

This study underscores the urgent need to strengthen surveillance tools for monitoring (re)emerging infectious diseases of epidemic and pandemic potential. For instance, during the pilot, ÆSOP provided the first source of outbreak awareness for health authorities in 71.9% of confirmed warnings, and supported decision-making in 55.8% of those events. Overall, ÆSOP warnings showed sensitivity of 62.8% (95% CI 54.4%–70.5%), specificity of 95.5% (95% CI 93.1%–97.1%), PPV of 82.7% (95% CI 74.3%–88.8%) and NPV of 88.1% (95% CI 84.7%–90.9%).

Performance metrics were significantly lower when considering outbreak confirmation within the same EW compared to confirmations occurring in the same or subsequent week. This finding aligns well with our initial assumption that signals generated by an early warning system may have preceded the formal recognition of outbreaks by health authorities. The improved performance observed when allowing a one-week lag is therefore consistent with the expected temporal dynamics of surveillance and response. Nevertheless, these findings might possibly be affected by the verification strategy adopted in our study, given the dependence between signal generation and outcome verification, as further discussed below.

In Brazil, mandatory notification of selected priority diseases has been regulated since 1998, with the SINAN database serving as the national repository for this information.17 Nonetheless, underreporting, notification delays, and detection gaps persist, representing challenges that are particularly pronounced in territorially large countries. These challenges have been illustrated by the cryptic transmission of Zika virus in Brazil and other Latin American countries for nearly 12 months before official reports,23 and more recently by the undetected circulation of SARS-CoV-2 lineages even in a scenario of heightened surveillance.24 Consequently, indicator-based approaches to surveillance, while historically important, must be complemented by additional strategies, including event- and community-based surveillance, the triangulation of which form the foundation of next-generation EWS.

These next-generation EWS must leverage the wealth of routinely collected data available in each country to support faster decision-making by health authorities. In the Americas, many countries (with the USA as a notable exception) maintain universal or near-universal health systems, several of which compile data on citizens' health encounters in rich, centralized registries for administrative purposes. Examples of these data sources include records from primary health care or emergency departments visits, ambulance dispatches, and laboratory results. Utilizing these readily-available, automatically collected data for surveillance has a dual advantage: it imposes no additional burden on health services personnel while providing critical anticipatory capacity for outbreak detection.5,10 During this pilot, the ÆSOP EWS relied on PHC data to perform syndromic surveillance. Ongoing work includes incorporating additional data streams, such as non-prescription drug sales and web searches, to enhance early warning and situational awareness.12 By integrating multimodal data with complementary roles into the EWS, we seek to ensure sufficient and reliable information is available to decision-makers, introducing redundancy and enhancing sensitivity.

The results from this pilot provided important insights to guide future work. Initially, EWS models were implemented and parametrized based on proof-of-concept studies using either existing surveillance data15,16 or synthetic datasets.20 Within this framework, the best-performing configuration reflects average results across all 5570 municipalities and might not be the optimal choice for each location. As a first comparison of model outputs with real-world conditions, we lacked a well-defined baseline threshold necessary to train a meta-classifier without introducing significant circular bias, which precluded differential weighting of models. In this context, we adopted a hard voting ensemble scheme to enable the straightforward combination of independent models while preserving interpretability. Additionally, hard voting helps reduce variability across model outputs and provides balanced, robust predictions. This approach aligned well with the scope of the pilot, given the need for clear and actionable outputs when comparing the EWS signals with outbreaks confirmed by health authorities. Building on these findings, future work will explore more advanced ensemble strategies, such as stacking approaches, to improve performance and robustness of the EWS. Additionally, we will refine model parametrization to account for local heterogeneity, including differences in municipal population size and health system structure, to establish context-specific thresholds.

Likewise, following the ongoing integration of ÆSOP into the national surveillance framework conducted by the MoH, recurrent engagement with stakeholders will be used to inform improvements of the system. Periodic revision of modeling strategies to attend public health needs is of great importance. For instance, Noufaily et al.25 developed the improved (flexible) Farrington, building on limitations identified through the practical application of the original method26 in routine public health surveillance. In particular, the algorithm was refined to reduce the rate of false alarms.

Our study has important limitations, one of which is the relatively short period of the pilot. As a pragmatic implementation study, we aimed to minimize disruptions to the workflow within surveillance services. The duration of the pilot was therefore defined in collaboration with local health professionals, who volunteered for testing the ÆSOP system while carrying out their regular duties. This relatively short period, which coincided with the typical influenza seasonality in the region, limits the evaluation of long-term variability and seasonal effects, hindering a more comprehensive assessment of the EWS performance. Additionally, since higher engagement is often expected during pilot phases than under routine conditions, the short study period precluded assessment of the sustainability and usability of the system during typical operational constraints.

Additionally, we followed a pragmatic approach to simulate the real-world integration of ÆSOP into routine surveillance. This framework, however policy-oriented, introduces important limitations related to incorporation bias. Confirmation of warnings was based on verification and risk assessment by local health authorities, following official guidelines22,27 and established local protocols. Because warnings may influence awareness and trigger investigations, confirmed events are not fully independent of the detection mechanism, potentially overestimating performance metrics. Conversely, events occurring in the absence of a warning might go under-ascertained. Finally, verification outcomes may also depend on local operational capacity, introducing variability unrelated to the intrinsic performance of the EWS.

A key lesson was the critical role of institutional support from stakeholders. Commitment from federal, state and local authorities was essential not only for operationalizing the integration of the EWS into routine surveillance, but also for ensuring the continuous feedback and assessment of the system. Of note, Brazil's health space governance is decentralized, with federal, state, and municipal governments sharing administrative responsibilities. Convening public health authorities and scientific experts from the earliest stages of planning was crucial to fostering a collaborative, trust-based environment. Shared ownership and active participation were evident throughout the process, exemplified by the spontaneous decision of a MoH representative to pilot the EWS in Amazonas in early 2024, alongside the full support of local authorities to adopt the technology. Their involvement was also key in promoting the EWS and coordinating communication among all participating institutions. Likewise, strong researcher–policymaker relationships and co-creation have been identified as key factors for enhancing the routine use of modeling evidence in public health policymaking.29

Given the focus on the public health utility of the EWS, regular check-ins during both the planning and deployment phases of the pilot were an important success factor. Close engagement with local, state and federal levels of the public health system ensured alignment with local priorities. For example, respiratory outbreaks were included as a priority for the EWS following a discussion with stakeholders in a co-creation workshop (Supplementary File S1). Regular meetings also provided a forum for clarifying roles, addressing technical and logistical issues, and adapting the system to user needs. At the request of participants, an online dashboard was developed to replace the original Excel-based mechanism for information exchange. In line with these findings, Smith et al.10 highlighted the importance of involving public health professionals to tailor syndromic surveillance systems to public health needs, while Phung et al.30 pointed to the need for the co-creation of practical and user-friendly tools.

The implementation process underscored the need for a shared understanding of core operational definitions. Particularly, the ÆSOP development team identified the absence of clear guidelines defining what constitutes an outbreak and specifying the intensity level that should trigger a warning. Ambiguity in these definitions can undermine the interpretation of results and limit their comparability. On the other hand, feedback from health authorities in workshops emphasized the need for clear communication of the system's model outputs. Co-creation workshops and regular check-ins facilitated knowledge translation in both directions: strengthening the capacity of health authorities to use data-driven warnings to inform public health decisions, while enhancing scientists' ability to communicate evidence effectively to policymakers.29

The integration of event-based surveillance (EBS) and traditional surveillance is crucial for enhancing early detection and rapid response to respiratory disease outbreaks. EBS allows for real-time identification of warning signals, while traditional surveillance provides consolidated epidemiological and historical data. The implementation of the AESOP EWS in Brazil exemplifies this integration, aligning with the WHO's Mosaic Respiratory Surveillance Framework.27 This framework advocates for a coordinated and collaborative approach, integrating multiple complementary surveillance systems to provide a comprehensive view of respiratory viruses with epidemic and pandemic potential. By combining different surveillance strategies, the AESOP EWS contributes to a more effective response tailored to local needs, in line with WHO recommendations for strengthening respiratory disease surveillance.

Conclusions

This pilot study suggests that the ÆSOP EWS can anticipate the detection of respiratory outbreaks when applied to a real-case setting in the state of Amazonas, Brazil. Feedback from local and federal surveillance authorities indicates its potential to support decision-making and guide response measures. By relying on administrative data and open-source tools and codes, the EWS offers a flexible framework that can be adapted and extended to diverse contexts, leveraging the data streams available in each country. In doing so, it has the potential to strengthen collective health surveillance capacities globally, while aligning regionally with the strategic priorities outlined in PAHO's recently approved Strategy on Epidemic Intelligence for Strengthening Early Warning of Health Emergencies.6
