In vitro antifungal and antioxidant activity of Cassia singueana stem bark ethanolic extracts and its fractions obtained from Gashua Town, Nigeria
Authors: Abubakar Ali, Aminu Muhammad, Kadai Alhaji Lawan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing resistance of fungal pathogens to conventional antifungal drugs and the growing interest in plant-based therapeutics have intensified the search for alternative bioactive agents. This study investigates the in vitro antifungal and antioxidant properties of ethanolic extracts obtained from the stem bark of Cassia singueana, collected in Gashua Town, Yobe State, Nigeria. The stem bark was air-dried, pulverized, and extracted using ethanol, followed by fractionation into different solvent fractions based on polarity. The antifungal activity of the extract was evaluated against Candida albicans and Aspergillus niger, using agar well diffusion and microdilution. At the same time, its antioxidant potential was assessed through established methods for free-radical scavenging. The evaluation of the extract’s antioxidant activity showed that the ethanol extract (CSET) had the strongest ability to neutralize DPPH free radicals, with an IC₅₀ value of 0.37 µg/ml. The plant also demonstrated strong antifungal effects: the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of the sample ranged from 1.25 to 2.5 mg/ml, while the minimum fungicidal concentration (MFC) fell between 2.5 and 5.0 mg/ml. These findings suggest that Cassia singueana stem bark possesses valuable pharmacological properties and may serve as a potential source of natural antifungal and antioxidant agents. Further studies are recommended to isolate and characterize the specific phytochemicals responsible for these activities.Keywords: Cassia singueana, Extracts, Partition, Fractions, Antifungal, AntioxidantSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Drug discovery, Microbiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Medicinal plants remain an invaluable resource for the discovery of new bioactive compounds with therapeutic potential1. For centuries, plant-derived remedies have been used in traditional medicine for the prevention and treatment of various diseases1. In many parts of northern Nigeria, including Gashua town in Yobe State, plant-based remedies are commonly used for the management of infectious and inflammatory conditions. Sharma2. With the increasing global challenge of antimicrobial resistance and the limitations of existing synthetic drugs, there is renewed scientific interest in identifying and validating natural products with pharmacological potential3.

Cassia singueana Delile, commonly known as African cassia or Winter cassia, belongs to the family Fabaceae (subfamily Caesalpinioideae)4. It is a small tree or shrub widely distributed in tropical and subtropical regions of Africa, including Nigeria, Sudan, and Ethiopia (Plate 1)4. The plant is well recognized in African traditional medicine, where different parts such as the leaves, roots, and stem bark are used for managing various ailments, including fever, malaria, diarrhea, cough, chest pain, and skin infections4. The bark and root decoctions are also used as tonics and for the treatment of wounds and parasitic diseases5.

Cassia singueana plant.

Phytochemical investigations of C. singueana have revealed a wide range of secondary metabolites, including flavonoids, tannins, saponins, phenolic compounds, anthraquinones, terpenoids, and alkaloids6. These constituents are known for their diverse biological activities, such as antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimalarial effects6. Several studies have reported antibacterial and cytotoxic properties of extracts from C. singueana, suggesting that the plant may serve as a promising source of natural bioactive agents7. Different parts of the plant, including the leaves, roots, and stem bark, are traditionally employed for the treatment of fever, gastrointestinal disorders, skin infections, wounds, and inflammatory conditions. The use of the plant in managing skin-related ailments suggests possible antimicrobial, including antifungal, potential. However, there is limited scientific information on its antifungal potential, especially with reference to isolates from different geographical locations, which may influence phytochemical composition and biological activity.

Fungal infections remain a major public health concern worldwide, particularly among immunocompromised individuals8. The emergence of resistant strains of pathogenic fungi such as Candida albicans, Aspergillus niger, and Cryptococcus neoformans has made the treatment of fungal diseases increasingly difficult9. Synthetic antifungal agents, though effective, are often associated with adverse effects, high cost, and reduced efficacy due to resistance. Misol et al.10, Therefore, the search for new, safe, and affordable antifungal compounds from natural sources, especially medicinal plants, is of great importance.

In addition, oxidative stress caused by an imbalance between free radicals and antioxidant defenses has been implicated in the pathogenesis of many chronic diseases, including cancer, cardiovascular disorders, and neurodegenerative conditions11. Natural antioxidants from plant materials can scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby mitigating oxidative damage and contributing to overall health12. The antioxidant potential of plant extracts is often attributed to phenolic and flavonoid compounds, which may also play a role in antimicrobial defense mechanisms13.

A study conducted in South Africa investigated the antioxidant activity of different parts of Cassia singueana using ethyl acetate and ethanolic extracts. The findings revealed that the ethanolic stem extract exhibited stronger antioxidant activity compared to the other extracts tested. However, that study focused primarily on antioxidant activity and did not extensively evaluate the antifungal potential of the plant14.

In our community, particularly in Gashua town, the plant is widely used in traditional medicine. Local practitioners commonly prepare remedies by boiling the leaves, stem bark, seeds, and sometimes the flowers, and the decoction is taken for the treatment of various fungal-related infections and other ailments. Despite this widespread traditional use, there is currently no documented scientific research conducted on plant materials sourced from our locality to validate these claims.

Therefore, there is a clear need for this study to investigate both the antifungal and antioxidant activities of Cassia singueana, especially using samples collected from our community. This research will not only complement previous findings from South Africa but also provide scientific evidence to support its traditional use in Gashua and contribute to the existing body of knowledge on the medicinal potential of the plant.

Methodology

Collection and identification of plant material

The stems of Cassia singueana were collected on 26 January 2017 from Out skirt of the town along Gashua Dumburi road Gashua with permission of the Forestry officer of Bade Local Government Yobe State. Gashua is located at approximately 12.874° N, 11.041° E, and the nearest major city, Nguru, lies about 65 km away. Plate 2 shows the Map of the area. The plant was identified by a Botonists (Baha’uddeen Said Adam) at the Department of Plant Biology Herbarium Bayero University, Kano with voucher number BUKHAN 0316. The stembark was immediately air-dried at room temperature, the dried samples were ground to a fine powder using a local mortar and pestle, and stored.

Extraction of the stembark powder of Cassia singueana

The air-dried powdered plant material (500 g) was defatted using n-hexane (2 L). The residue was percolated with ethanol (2 L) for 10 days. The extract was filtered, concentrated using a rotary evaporator (Rotavapor R-210/21J) to yield ethanol extract (CSET).

Partitioning of ethanol extract

Ethanol extract (10 g) was partitioned using n-hexane, chloroform, and a 60% aqueous methanol gradient. The fractions were concentrated using a rotary evaporator to obtain the n-hexane fraction (CSHX) and the chloroform fraction (CSCH). The 60% aqueous methanol was dried by a freeze-drying process (freeze dryer RV5) to obtain the aqueous methanol fraction (CSAM). The weight and appearance of the respective fractions were recorded15.

Determination of antifungal activity

The extracts were dissolved in 1 ml of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). 0.2 g of each extract was dissolved in 2 ml of the solvent, which corresponds to 10 mg/mL (10,000 µg/mL). The working concentrations of 10 mg/ml, 5 mg/ml, 2.5 mg/ml, and 1.25 mg/ml, prepared by the serial dilution method. Candida albicans and Aspergillus niger were the microbes used. The test microorganisms are clinical isolates from the Department of Microbiology, Aminu Kano Teaching Hospital, Kano. The cultures are sub-cultured in nutrient broth, incubated for 24 h to be used as inoculum. Candida was grown on PDA slant and PDA broth. The prepared media were sterilized at 121 °C for 30 min, dispensed into Petri dishes. The in vitro antifungal studies were carried out using different solvent extracts of the stem bark of Cassia singueana. In brief, sterile PDA was poured into sterile Petri dishes and allowed to solidify for 30 min. Then 50 µl of fresh culture of fungi was spread on Petri dishes with the help of a sterile spreader. For the agar well diffusion method, wells (0.6 cm) were made in the agar plates with the help of a cork-borer. 100 µl (10 mg/1 ml) of the extract in DMSO was introduced into the well, and the plates were incubated overnight at 37 °C. For every strain of fungi, DMSO was used as a control. Ketoconazole (20 mg/ml) was used as a standard antifungal agent. The inhibition of fungal growth was determined by measuring the diameter of the clear zone around the well. The experiment was conducted in triplicate, and the results are presented as mean ± SD in Plate 3.

Antifungal assay.

Determination of minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)

MIC of crude extracts of Cassia singueana with n-Hexane, chloroform, ethanol, and aqueous methanol were tested using the broth dilution method. Varying concentrations of the extract (10 mg/ml, 5 mg/ml, 2.5 mg/ml, 1.25 mg/ml) were prepared in DMSO. 0.1 ml of each concentration was added to each 9 ml of nutrient broth containing 0.1 ml of the standardized test fungal strain. All the tubes were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. A positive control was set up by using DMSO and fungal culture.

Determination of minimum fungicidal concentration (MFC)

MFC value was determined by subculturing the dilution (which showed no visible turbidity) onto freshly prepared potato dextrose agar (PDA) media. The plates were incubated further for 18–48 h at 37 °C. the highest dilution that yielded no single fungal colony on the PDA plates was taken as MFC.

Determination of free radical scavenging activity

Each test extract was made into a stock solution (5 mg/ml), further diluted to final concentrations of 1000, 500, 250, 125, 62.5, 31.25, 15.62, and 7.81 µg/ml in methanol using a dilution formula. The free radical scavenging activity of the stem bark extracts of Cassia singueana was measured by a decrease in absorbance of methanolic DPPH solution at 517 nm in the presence of the extract. Then, a total of 50 µM DPPH methanolic solution (160 µL) was added to the sample solution (40 µL). The mixture was vortexed for 1 min and then left to stand at room temperature for 30 min in the dark, and its absorbance was read at 517 nm. Determination of total antioxidant activity

The total antioxidant activity of crude extracts was determined according to the method of Prieto et al.17, Using Phosphomolybdenum Assay with with slight modification. An aliquot of 20 µL of sample solution (0.1 mg/mL) was mixed with 180 µL reagent solution (0.6 M sulphuric acid, 28 mM sodium phosphate, and 4 mM ammonium molybdate). Reaction mixture was incubated at 95 °C for 90 min in a water bath. Absorbance of all the sample mixtures was measured at 695 nm. Total antioxidant activity is expressed as the number of equivalents of ascorbic acid. A calibration curve of ascorbic acid was prepared, and the total antioxidant activity was standardized against ascorbic acid and was expressed as mg ascorbic acid equivalents per gram of sample on a dry weight (DW) basis.

The CSET extract (10 g) was fractionated using solvents of varying polarity: n-Hexane, chloroform, and 60% aqueous methanol. MIC and MFC determination

The determination of the Minimal Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) indicates the ability of a sample to inhibit microbial replication. MIC refers to the lowest concentration of an antimicrobial that will inhibit visible growth of a microorganism after overnight incubation, while minimum fungicidal concentration (MFC) refers to the lowest concentration of an antimicrobial that will prevent the growth of a fungus after subculture onto antibiotic-free media (i.e., concentration that will kill the microorganism). MIC and MFC of the samples from the stembark of Cassia singueana against fungal strains, Candida albicans and Aspergillus niger were evaluated (Table 3). The extracts, CSET, CSAM, and CSHX, showed a significant microbial inhibition of 1.25 mg/ml against both Candida albicans and Aspergillus niger. CSCH and the isolated compound inhibited the growth of the organism at 2.5 mg/ml. The minimum fungicidal concentration (MFC) of the test samples indicated that CSET and CSAM had an MFC value of 2.5 mg/ml, twice the inhibition activities of CSCH, CSHX, and CSAM253-114 with an MFC of 5 mg/ml. Djeussi18 reported that a sample is fungicidal when the ratio of MFC and MIC is ≤ 4, while fungi static when the ratio is > 4. Thus, the values obtained for MFC/MIC ≤ 4 indicate that the tested samples might be acting as fungicidal.

Discussion

The extraction of the stem bark of Cassia singueana using ethanol produced a crude extract yield of 11.80%. This relatively good yield suggests that ethanol was effective in extracting a wide range of phytochemicals from the plant material. Ethanol is widely used in phytochemical studies because it can dissolve both polar and moderately non-polar compounds such as flavonoids, tannins, phenolic acids, and alkaloids19,20. The yield obtained in this study is consistent with previous reports on Cassia singueana and other Cassia species, where ethanol extraction has been shown to recover a significant number of bioactive constituents7,21.

Further fractionation of the crude extract using solvents of different polarity revealed that the aqueous methanol fraction (CSAM) had the highest recovery (68%). This indicates that most of the phytochemicals present in the crude extract are polar in nature. Polar fractions of medicinal plants are often rich in phenolic compounds and flavonoids, which are well known for their antimicrobial and antioxidant activities22. Similar observations have been reported in previous studies on Cassia singueana, where polar solvent fractions showed higher biological activity than non-polar fractions23.

The antifungal results showed that the ethanol extract (CSET) exhibited noticeable inhibitory activity against the fungal pathogens Candida albicans and Aspergillus niger. At a concentration of 10 mg/mL, the extract produced inhibition zones of 14 mm and 16 mm, respectively. These findings suggest that the crude extract contains compounds capable of suppressing fungal growth. Similar antifungal effects have been reported for other species in the Cassia genus, such as Cassia fistula and Cassia alata, which have shown inhibitory activity against several pathogenic fungi24. The observed activity may therefore be linked to the presence of phytochemicals such as anthraquinones, flavonoids, and phenolic compounds commonly found in Cassia species25.

The aqueous methanol fraction (CSAM) also demonstrated considerable antifungal activity against both fungal strains. This activity may be attributed to polar bioactive compounds that can disrupt fungal cell membranes, inhibit essential enzymes, or interfere with microbial metabolic processes26. Previous studies have similarly shown that methanol or aqueous methanol extracts of medicinal plants often exhibit strong antimicrobial activity due to their high content of phenolic compounds27. This supports the idea that the antifungal activity observed in this study may be associated with phenolic constituents present in the polar fraction.

On the other hand, the chloroform fraction (CSCH) did not show any antifungal activity against the tested fungi. This may indicate that the compounds present in this fraction are either inactive against the organisms tested or present in concentrations too low to produce measurable inhibition. Comparable findings have been reported in other phytochemical studies where certain intermediate-polarity fractions showed lower antimicrobial activity compared with highly polar extracts22.

The n-hexane fraction (CSHX) showed selective activity against Aspergillus niger but not against Candida albicans. This difference in activity could be related to variations in the cell wall structure of the fungi, which may influence their susceptibility to non-polar compounds extracted by hexane. As expected, the standard antifungal drug Ketoconazole produced a much larger inhibition zone (33 mm) compared with the plant extracts, reflecting its high potency as a clinically used antifungal agent. Nevertheless, the activity observed in the plant extracts suggests that Cassia singueana may contain compounds with potential antifungal properties.

The determination of minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum fungicidal concentration (MFC) provided further insight into the antifungal effectiveness of the extracts. Most of the samples showed MIC values between 1.25 and 2.5 mg/mL, indicating good inhibitory activity against the tested fungi. The MFC values also suggested that the extracts may exert fungicidal effects. According to the criteria proposed by Brice Sorel Djeussi and colleagues, a compound is considered fungicidal when the ratio of MFC to MIC is less than or equal to four18. The ratios obtained in this study fall within this range, suggesting that the extracts may not only inhibit fungal growth but may also lead to fungal cell death.

The antioxidant potential of the extracts was evaluated using the DPPH radical scavenging assay, which is widely used to assess the ability of plant extracts to neutralize free radicals28. Among the tested samples, the aqueous methanol fraction (CSAM) showed strong radical scavenging activity with 95.68% inhibition at 1000 µg/mL. The isolated compound also demonstrated very high activity, reaching almost complete inhibition (99.66%). The ethanol extract (CSET) showed an IC₅₀ value of 0.37 µg/mL, which is comparable to the standard antioxidant Ascorbic acid (0.33 µg/mL). These results suggest that the plant contains compounds capable of effectively neutralizing free radicals.

The total antioxidant capacity determined using the phosphomolybdenum assay further supported the results obtained from the DPPH assay. The aqueous methanol fraction showed the highest antioxidant value, followed by the crude ethanol extract. This pattern suggests that polar compounds present in the plant are largely responsible for the antioxidant activity observed. Phenolic compounds are known to act as reducing agents that donate electrons or hydrogen atoms to stabilize reactive oxygen species, thereby helping to prevent oxidative damage in biological systems17.

Oxidative stress has been linked to the development of many diseases, including inflammation, cardiovascular disorders, and cancer. Therefore, the strong antioxidant activity observed in this study may partly explain the traditional medicinal use of Cassia singueana in African herbal medicine22.

Overall, the results of this study suggest that the stem bark of Cassia singueana contains biologically active compounds with notable antifungal and antioxidant properties. The ethanol extract and the aqueous methanol fraction, in particular, showed promising activity. These findings highlight the potential of this plant as a source of natural bioactive compounds and support the need for further studies aimed at isolating and characterizing the active constituents responsible for these activities.

Conclusion

This study explored the biological potential of the stem bark of Cassia singueana by examining its extraction yield, antifungal activity, and antioxidant properties. The ethanol extraction produced a moderate yield, indicating that the solvent was effective in extracting a variety of phytochemicals from the plant material. Further fractionation showed that the aqueous methanol fraction had the highest recovery, suggesting that many of the bioactive compounds present in the stem bark are polar in nature.

The antifungal results showed that the crude ethanol extract and some of its fractions were able to inhibit the growth of the fungal pathogens Candida albicans and Aspergillus niger. In particular, the ethanol extract and the aqueous methanol fraction demonstrated notable antifungal activity, while the n-hexane fraction showed limited activity and the chloroform fraction showed no observable inhibition. Although the standard antifungal drug Ketoconazole produced stronger activity, the results indicate that the plant extracts still possess promising antifungal properties.

The study also revealed strong antioxidant activity in the extracts, especially in the ethanol extract and aqueous methanol fraction, as demonstrated by the DPPH radical scavenging assay and the phosphomolybdenum assay. These findings suggest that the plant contains compounds capable of neutralizing free radicals and may therefore, help reduce oxidative stress.

Overall, the results support the traditional medicinal use of Cassia singueana and highlight its potential as a natural source of compounds with antifungal and antioxidant properties. These findings provide a scientific basis for further investigation of this plant in the search for new natural therapeutic agents.

Recommendations

Based on the findings of this study, further research is recommended to isolate and identify the specific bioactive compounds responsible for the antifungal and antioxidant activities observed in Cassia singueana. Advanced analytical techniques such as nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), infrared spectroscopy (IR), and mass spectrometry should be employed to determine the chemical structures of the active constituents. In addition, further investigations, including in vivo studies and toxicity evaluations, are necessary to assess the safety and potential therapeutic applications of the extracts. Future studies should also explore the effectiveness of the plant extracts against a wider range of microbial pathogens in order to better understand their antimicrobial spectrum. Moreover, investigating the mechanisms through which the extracts exert their antifungal and antioxidant effects would provide deeper insight into their biological activities. Ultimately, these studies could support the development of pharmaceutical or herbal formulations derived from the active fractions of this plant.
