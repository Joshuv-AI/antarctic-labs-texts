Prescribing Cascades: An Umbrella Review and Updated Systematic Review
Authors: Massimo Carollo, Salvatore Crisafulli, Marta Zerio, Francesco Maccarrone, Anna Paladin, Margherita Selleri, Anna Forti, Francesco U S Mattace-Raso, Gianluca Trifirò
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrescribing cascades (PCs) often remain underrecognized, contributing to inappropriate prescribing and increased healthcare burden.ObjectiveThis study aimed to synthesize existing knowledge on reported PCs and identify the medications and adverse drug reactions (ADRs) involved.MethodsAn umbrella review was conducted using PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and Scopus (inception–16 July 2025). Systematic and scoping reviews on potential PCs were included. An updated systematic review, using the same databases, was conducted to cover the period from 16 February 2022 (the end date of the search period in the most recent included review) to 16 July 2025 to identify more recent studies.ResultsTwo systematic and two scoping reviews were included in the umbrella review, for a total of 156 primary studies, and 34 more studies met the inclusion criteria of the updated systematic review. Overall, 84 different ADRs potentially leading to PCs were described. Medications mostly involved in the onset of ADRs leading to PCs were antidepressants, antidementia drugs, antipsychotics, anxiolytics, hypnotics/sedatives, lipid-modifying agents, and antihypertensives. The most frequently reported ADRs belonged to the “psychiatric disorders,” “nervous system,” and “gastrointestinal disorders” system organ classes. Prescription sequence symmetry analysis (PSSA) emerged as the most used method for PC identification.ConclusionsPCs are an underrecognized driver of inappropriate polypharmacy, especially in older adults, yet current evidence is largely retrospective and heterogeneous. By consolidating available data, our work provides a valuable reference tool for clinicians and researchers. Future efforts should focus on establishing standardized definitions and developing clinical strategies to prevent inappropriate PC use.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40266-026-01295-9.

Introduction

The complexity of pharmacological therapies has been significantly increasing owing to the growing prevalence of multimorbidity and the related use of polypharmacy, particularly among older adults [1]. While the appropriate use of multiple medications can improve disease management, it may lead to increased risk of adverse drug reactions (ADRs), drug–drug interactions (DDIs), and medication errors [2, 3]. One of the contributing factors to inappropriate prescribing is the phenomenon of prescribing cascades (PCs), i.e., an ADR is misinterpreted as a (non-drug-related) new medical condition, prompting the prescription of an additional medication to manage the symptoms caused by the initial drug [4]. However, definitions reported in scientific literature can vary with respect to the root cause (misinterpretation versus recognition of the side effect), the focus on intentionality and clinical appropriateness, and the scope of included interventions (prescription-only versus over-the-counter or diagnostic procedures) [5].

PCs can be problematic when the potential risk of adding a new medication outweighs its potential benefits [6]. Concerns arise when ADRs go unrecognized, leading to unnecessary treatments that could otherwise be avoided by simply lowering the dose of or withdrawing a non-life-saving medication. This may be largely due to cognitive biases such as diagnostic overshadowing, in which the focus remains on treating symptoms rather than identifying potential iatrogenic causes [7]. Furthermore, the identification of PCs is hindered by multimorbidity and polypharmacy, which can complicate the detection of ADRs. This is particularly true for older adults, who are more susceptible to ADRs due to age-related changes in pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics that heighten their sensitivity [8]. The exact prevalence of PCs remains uncertain, as it varies widely depending on factors such as the involved drug classes, the number of prescribed medications, and the specific healthcare setting [9, 10]. Detecting PCs is crucial as undetected cascades can increase the risk of ADR-related hospitalizations and negatively impact patients' quality of life [11].

Addressing PCs requires a multifaceted approach, including the development of specific educational programs for healthcare professionals and, where appropriate, the implementation of medication review and deprescribing strategies [3]. To our knowledge, no tool is currently available specifically for the early detection and prevention of PCs. The development of such a tool first requires identifying all potential PCs and, subsequently, evaluating their clinical relevance.

Considering the heterogeneity of data sources, the broad spectrum of clinical scenarios in which PCs may occur, and the availability of systematic and scoping reviews on this topic, we conducted an umbrella review and an updated systematic review of literature to synthesize existing evidence on reported PCs, with the specific aim of identifying the medications involved and the associated ADRs.

Methods

Umbrella Review

Search Strategy and Selection Criteria

This umbrella review was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Overviews of Reviews (PRIOR) statement [12], and the study protocol was registered on PROSPERO (CRD42024559957), with the following amendments. First, the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Systematic Reviews was used to assess the methodological quality of the reviews included in the umbrella review, instead of A Measurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews (AMSTAR) or AMSTAR-2. This choice was made because the JBI tool is suitable for the appraisal of systematic reviews of observational studies and scoping reviews, whereas AMSTAR and AMSTAR-2 were primarily developed for reviews of interventional studies [13–16]. Second, on the basis of the characteristics and heterogeneity of the retrieved evidence, it was not possible to synthesize or extract information concerning the clinical relevance of PCs or potential management strategies.

Relevant studies were searched in the bibliographic databases PubMed (including PubMed Central and Medline), Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science (all databases), and Scopus, from their inception until 16 July 2025. A preliminary search strategy was developed for PubMed and subsequently adapted for the other databases. No restrictions were applied regarding publication date, and only studies written in English were considered. The search terms included keywords related to systematic or scoping reviews and PCs. The detailed search strategy developed for each database is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

This umbrella review included systematic and scoping reviews investigating potential PCs, while other types of studies (e.g., experimental trials, observational studies, and narrative reviews), conference abstracts, book chapters, and editorials were excluded. A manual search of reference lists from included studies, as well as from narrative reviews on this topic (used solely for citation searching and not for inclusion), was also conducted to identify additional relevant literature. Four reviewers (MZ, FM, AP, and MS) independently reviewed titles and abstracts to exclude clearly irrelevant studies. The full texts of the selected articles were then assessed by two reviewers (MC and MZ) for inclusion. All discrepancies were resolved through discussion or, if needed, intervention from a senior author (GT) until consensus was reached.

Data Extraction

Data from each included study were independently extracted by four reviewers (MZ, FM, AP, and MS). Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or by consulting a senior author (GT) until consensus was reached. Data extracted from each systematic or scoping review included the bibliographic identifiers of the publication (i.e., first author and year of publication), type of review (i.e., systematic or scoping), searched databases, search period, study objectives, number and designs of included studies, PCs identified, and relative primary literature sources. Individual PCs reported in the included systematic and scoping reviews were presented in tabular form. Specifically, the ADRs leading to PCs were coded using the preferred terms (PTs) and grouped according to the system organ classes (SOCs) of the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA). PCs were extracted from the included reviews as reported, without reclassification or reinterpretation by the reviewers. However, in a limited number of cases, PCs were not extracted when the corresponding primary prescription sequence symmetry analysis (PSSA) studies did not report a statistically significant association.

Risk-of-Bias Assessment

The quality of the included systematic and scoping reviews was independently assessed by two authors (MC and SC) using the JBI Qualitative Assessment and Review Instrument for Systematic Reviews [16]. Any discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion with a senior author (GT) to reach a consensus. In accordance with the JBI Manual [17], question 9 (“Was the likelihood of publication bias assessed?”) was considered not applicable, as the included studies were qualitative systematic reviews.

The methodological quality of each study was rated on the basis of the percentage of affirmative responses to the JBI checklist items. Studies with ≤ 49% positive responses were considered to have a high risk of bias, those with 50–69% moderate risk, and those with ≥ 75% low risk of bias. No studies were excluded on the basis of the results of the quality appraisal.

Updated Systematic Literature Review

Search Strategy and Selection Criteria

To update the evidence summarized in the umbrella review, we conducted a systematic review of the literature searching for relevant studies published from 16 February 2022 (i.e., the end date of the search period of the most recent systematic review included in the umbrella review) to 16 July 2025, using the same bibliographic databases. The search strategy included the same keywords used for the umbrella review, with no restriction for the study type (i.e., systematic and scoping reviews). The detailed search strategy developed for each database is provided in Supplementary Table 2. This systematic search included all experimental, observational, and descriptive studies investigating potential PCs, while other types of studies (e.g., systematic, scoping, and narrative reviews), conference abstracts, book chapters, and editorials were excluded. A manual search of reference lists from included studies was also conducted to identify additional relevant literature. Four reviewers (MC, SC, MZ, and FM) independently reviewed titles and abstracts of identified records to exclude clearly irrelevant studies. The full texts of the selected articles were then assessed by two reviewers (MC and SC) for inclusion. All discrepancies were resolved through discussion or, if needed, intervention from a senior author (GT) until consensus was reached. Data from each included study were then independently extracted by two reviewers (MS and SC). Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or through the intervention of a senior author (GT).

Data Extraction

For each study included in the systematic review, we extracted the following information: bibliographic identifiers of the publication (i.e., first author and year of publication), study type, and PCs identified. Individual PCs reported in the included studies were presented in tabular form, and the ADRs leading to PCs were coded using the PTs and grouped according to the SOCs of the MedDRA. PCs were extracted from the included primary studies as reported, without reclassification or reinterpretation by the reviewers.

Risk-of-Bias Assessment

The quality of the included studies was independently assessed by two authors (MC and SC) using the JBI Qualitative Assessment and Review Instrument for Cohort Studies, Case Reports, Prevalence Studies, Case Control Studies, and Qualitative Research for PSSA and retrospective cohort studies, case reports, cross-sectional, case–control, and descriptive qualitative studies, respectively [15]. Any discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion with a senior author (GT) to reach a consensus. The methodological quality of each study was rated on the basis of the percentage of affirmative responses to the JBI checklist items. Studies with ≤ 49% positive responses were considered to have a high risk of bias, those with 50–69% moderate risk, and those with ≥ 75% low risk of bias. No studies were excluded on the basis of the results of the quality appraisal.

Results

Umbrella Review

Process of Literature Search

The flow chart for study selection is shown in Fig. 1. The initial literature search identified a total of 436 studies. After the removal of duplicates (N = 52), 384 titles and abstracts were independently screened, and only 10 (2.6%) full-text articles were selected for further evaluation. Six publications related to a total of four individual studies (i.e., two systematic reviews and two scoping reviews) met the inclusion criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis (Fig. 1; Table 1). The list of studies excluded after full-text assessment, with reasons for exclusion, is reported in Supplementary Table 3.

Study Characteristics

Overall, two systematic reviews and two scoping reviews were included in this umbrella review. These studies were published between 2021 [18] and 2023 [19, 20] (Table 1).

In particular, the systematic review conducted by Adrien et al. was aimed at providing an overview of PCs and included 95 observational studies (80 cohort and/or PSSA studies and 15 case–control studies) published until 2 September 2021 [20]. The systematic review conducted by Doherty et al. was aimed at identifying published PCs among community-dwelling adults and included 101 studies (85 cohort and/or PSSA studies, 3 nested case–control studies, 5 case–control studies, 5 cross-sectional studies, and 3 case-crossover studies) published until February 2022 [10, 21, 22]. As for the scoping reviews, Chen et al. summarized the evidence from 32 observational studies (7 cohort and/or PSSA studies, 1 case–control study, 13 cross-sectional studies, and 11 case reports) to evaluate the methodology and results of relevant articles assessing PCs [19]. Lastly, the scoping review conducted by Morris et al. included 53 studies, and it was aimed at examining the utility and methodological advancements of PSSA to identify PCs [18].

Overall, 156 distinct primary studies were retrieved from the systematic and scoping reviews. In total, 25 [23–47] of them were excluded as they did not identify PCs, resulting in 131 studies included in our synthesis (Table 2; Fig. 2). Of these, 60 (45.8%) were retrospective studies using PSSA, 30 (22.9%) were retrospective cohort studies, 13 (9.9%) were case–control studies, 11 (8.4%) were case reports, 10 (7.6%) were cross-sectional studies, 2 (1.5%) were case-crossover studies, 2 (1.5%) were qualitative descriptive studies, 1 (0.8%) was a prospective cohort study, 1 (0.8%) was a case series, and 1 (0.8%) was a computational study. Study Characteristics

Among the 34 studies included, 16 (47.1%) were PSSA [48–63], 5 (14.7%) retrospective cohort studies [64–68], 5 (14.7%) case reports [69–73], 4 (11.8%) cross-sectional studies [74–77], 3 (8.8%) descriptive qualitative/consensus studies [78–80], and 1 (2.9%) nested case–control study [81] (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 5). The risk of bias was considered low in 19 (55.9%) of the included studies [54–56, 59, 64, 66, 67, 69, 71–81], moderate in 12 (35.3%) studies [48–53, 57, 60–63, 70], and high in 3 (8.8%) studies [58, 65, 68] (Supplementary Table 7).

Prescribing Cascades

The four systematic/scoping reviews included in the umbrella review identified a total of 58 distinct ADRs potentially leading to PCs, distributed across 16 SOC categories; 26 additional ADRs were identified through the 34 studies included in the updated systematic review, bringing the number of SOC categories to 17. PCs documented in at least one PSSA, cohort study, case–control study, or cross-sectional study are reported in Table 2, while those described only in case reports, case series, or other descriptive investigations (e.g., qualitative or computational studies) are reported in Supplementary Table 8. Supplementary Table 9 lists PCs in which the ADRs or the drugs involved were not specified.

Specifically, the most frequently involved SOCs were “psychiatric disorders” (15 ADRs; 17.9%), “nervous system disorders” (11 ADRs; 13.1%), “gastrointestinal disorders” (11 ADRs; 13.1%), and “metabolism and nutrition disorders” (9 ADRs; 10.7%).

Medications involved in the onset of ADRs leading to PCs were mainly those used for the treatment of chronic conditions, such as psychoanaleptics (mostly antidepressants and antidementia drugs), psycholeptics (i.e., antipsychotics, anxiolytics, and hypnotics/sedatives), lipid-modifying agents, and agents acting on the renin–angiotensin system (Table 2, Supplementary Table 10, and Supplementary Fig. 2).

Discussion

In this study, we included evidence from a total of 165 (131 from the umbrella review and 34 from the updated systematic review) individual articles. Overall, 84 distinct ADRs with the potential to trigger PCs were identified. Medications mostly involved in the onset of ADRs leading to PCs were those used to treat chronic conditions in older patients, such as antidepressants, antidementia drugs, antipsychotics, anxiolytics, hypnotics/sedatives, lipid-modifying agents, and antihypertensives, underscoring the importance of conducting comprehensive medication reviews to identify potentially inappropriate medications. PCs represent a frequently underrecognized phenomenon that may substantially contribute to inappropriate polypharmacy, with important implications for both patient outcomes and healthcare system sustainability [82]. Although certain intentional PCs can be appropriate or clinically justified, many are considered problematic, as the risks associated with introducing a new medication may outweigh its expected benefits [6]. Indeed, many PCs reported in this study may be either appropriate (e.g., when discontinuation of the causative therapy is not feasible owing to its therapeutic importance) or inappropriate, depending on several factors. These include the severity of the ADR, the strength of the suspected causal relationship, the overall contribution to the patient’s medication burden, patient-related factors, therapeutic goals, available alternatives, and potential consequences for quality of life [83]. A clear example is represented by calcium channel blockers, widely prescribed for hypertension, which may induce peripheral edema. Rather than prompting a reassessment of the antihypertensive regimen or a dose adjustment (the edema could be mild and dose-related), this ADR may lead to the addition of diuretics, potentially exposing patients to dehydration and electrolyte imbalances [84, 85]. This sequence exemplifies how a clinically reasonable response to an ADR may evolve into a problematic PC if alternative management strategies are not taken into account. Other well-documented examples include the initiation of allopurinol following furosemide-induced hyperuricemia [86], the use of anti-Parkinsonian medications to counteract extrapyramidal symptoms caused by antipsychotics [87], and anticholinergic treatment for urinary incontinence induced by cholinesterase inhibitors in patients with dementia, paradoxically counteracting the cognitive benefits of the initial therapy [88–90]. Interestingly, a recent consensus panel of 38 experts in geriatric pharmacotherapy identified this cascade but did not deem it clinically relevant [79]. Instead, they considered the reverse scenario, i.e., anticholinergic agents prescribed for urinary incontinence leading to cognitive impairment and subsequently prompting the initiation of cholinesterase inhibitors or memantine, as clinically relevant [79].

The evidence summarized in our review was derived from studies with diverse designs, with a notable increasing trend in publications on PCs over the last decade. The majority of data originated from retrospective studies employing PSSA, cohort studies, and case–control studies. However, a substantial portion of the available data also came from case reports [91–99]. While case reports are a valuable source for identifying rare events and generating hypotheses, they generally represent the lowest level of clinical evidence, particularly when compared with larger observational studies and experimental research. Consequently, they are insufficient to establish causal relationships or to estimate the frequency and clinical relevance of PCs. By contrast, larger observational studies, particularly those with longitudinal follow-up and adequate adjustment for confounding, provide more robust insights into prescribing patterns and PC dynamics, although they may still yield spurious associations [100]. As expected, no experimental studies specifically designed to investigate PCs were found. Nevertheless, randomized controlled trials often report ADRs, which can allow for indirect inferences regarding the potential occurrence of PCs, particularly when ADR-related symptoms lead to subsequent prescriptions [101]. This represents a promising area for hypothesis generation and secondary data analyses. Among available methods, PSSA has been shown to be particularly relevant for identifying potential PCs. This case-only study design detects asymmetry in the initiation sequence of two medications, assuming that temporal association may suggest causality [18]. Its main advantages include the relatively easy implementation in large healthcare databases, independence from detailed clinical data, suitability for population-level analyses, and computational efficiency [102]. Furthermore, PSSA can identify potential signals for rare events [103, 104]. However, several limitations of this method should be acknowledged. PSSA relies on assumptions about prescribing behavior that may not be generalizable across different clinical settings, as prescribing patterns can vary by institution, region, and clinician preference. Additionally, this method cannot distinguish between appropriate and inappropriate PCs and is sensitive to temporal prescribing trends [102]. The possibility of false-positive or false-negative findings also exists, as PSSA is influenced by factors that can lead to the asymmetry of prescriptions, such as protopathic bias, reverse causation, and confounding by indication [102]. Likewise, observational cohort or case–control studies provide associations that remain susceptible to residual confounding and biases. Even in studies reporting robust statistical associations, the presence of a PC cannot be confirmed without detailed clinical plausibility. Case reports, on this point, can offer more narrative evidence but lack generalizability. Altogether, the current body of literature describes potential PCs with varying levels of plausibility, and, as such, the PCs identified must be regarded as potential rather than definitive.

From a clinical perspective, it is essential to raise clinicians’ awareness of PCs, particularly those that often remain underrecognized. In this regard, establishing the biological plausibility and clinical relevance of potential PCs is important to prevent fatigue alerts during medication reviews. One of the approaches to identify potential PCs is using the Summary of Product Characteristics (SmPCs) to ascertain whether the ADR (e.g., hyperuricemia) caused by the first drug (e.g., furosemide) is one of the indications for use of the second drug (e.g., allopurinol). As for PCs’ clinical relevance, one strategy is to rely on the consensus of expert panels, as previously done in two of the studies included in our updated systematic review [79, 80]. Our study may contribute in this regard, serving as an updated starting point for a new consensus based on all available evidence to date. However, in routine clinical practice, performing these evaluations manually is often burdensome, clinicians may face significant time constraints or may lack the specific pharmacological expertise required to revise polytherapy, and dedicated clinical pharmacology teams are not always available to provide bedside consultation. To bridge this gap, integrating digital solutions into clinical decision-support systems or electronic health records that can flag potential PCs, or more broadly, medications likely to worsen existing conditions and thereby prompt treatment intensification (e.g., hyperglycemia-inducing drugs leading to escalation of antidiabetic therapy), could enhance real-time decision-making. Increasing clinician awareness, along with providing decision support for therapeutic alternatives or dose adjustments, may help prevent or mitigate PCs and prioritize targeted deprescribing interventions, ultimately contributing to polypharmacy reduction. To date, to the best of our knowledge, no dedicated clinical tool exists for systematic PC detection and management. Until such a resource becomes available, ongoing professional education, structured medication reviews, and evidence-based deprescribing protocols remain crucial strategies, particularly in high-risk patient populations [3].

Limitations

Although this study provides a comprehensive overview of PCs, the findings should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, as expected, data reporting across studies was highly heterogeneous, limiting comparability and precluding quantitative synthesis (e.g., meta-analysis of PC incidence or risk estimates). In addition, the (anticipated [5]) heterogeneity in PC definitions across studies and the variability in ADR coding and prescription recording (e.g., use of MedDRA PTs versus narrative descriptions) may have led to misclassification or to several events being missed. Second, only studies published in English were included. While this approach may have resulted in the exclusion of relevant evidence published in other languages, it allowed for more consistent data extraction and appraisal and ensured the inclusion of studies published in journals adhering to internationally recognized reporting and peer-review standards, which may be associated with higher methodological transparency. Third, it was not possible to summarize or extrapolate information on the clinical relevance of PCs or on potential management strategies. This limitation reflects the characteristics of the available evidence, as most studies focused on PC identification rather than on standardized clinical outcomes, severity assessment, or patient-centered endpoints. When reported, management strategies were inconsistently described and highly context-specific, precluding meaningful synthesis or generalization. Moreover, existing literature reveals substantial gaps in both methodological rigor and clinical applicability. Prospective studies are therefore needed to validate PC detection methods, assess the clinical impact of PCs, and evaluate the effectiveness of clinical and educational strategies for PC prevention and management. Finally, the overall quality and completeness of our findings are influenced by the limitations of each included review/study. It is possible that other relevant studies published prior to February 2022, which were not captured by the systematic and scoping reviews included in the umbrella review, might have been missed.

Conclusions

PCs are an underrecognized driver of inappropriate polypharmacy, especially in older adults, yet current evidence is largely retrospective and heterogeneous. This umbrella review and updated systematic review provides a comprehensive synthesis of the studies exploring PCs to date, identifying 84 ADRs with the potential to trigger them. By integrating available data, this work provides a reference for clinicians managing complex therapeutic regimens and a solid foundation for researchers aiming to further explore the clinical relevance of PCs.
