CALR-dependent endoplasmic reticulum N-glycoprotein maturation promotes Japanese encephalitis virus entry and replication
Authors: Yonghui Zhang, Jinyan Zhang, Rui Jiang, Jingpei Han, Hailong Liu, Yuan Wang, Liuxing Qin, Zishi Guo, Yuqing Yang, Jinxue Ruan, Xinyun Li, Xiangmin Li, Kun Han, Ping Qian, Shengsong Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe endoplasmic reticulum (ER) chaperone machinery participates in multiple stages of viral infection, from entry through assembly and release. However, how ER-resident quality-control mechanisms shape cellular permissiveness to Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV) remains unclear. Here, we identify the ER-resident chaperone calreticulin (CALR) as a key host factor that promotes cellular permissiveness to JEV infection. CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout of CALR markedly reduces JEV attachment, internalization, and productive replication. Mechanistically, CALR depletion disrupts N-linked glycosylation homeostasis and selectively reduces the abundance of the integrin subunit ITGA3 through an ER glycan-dependent maturation checkpoint. Glycomic analysis revealed accumulation of immature N-glycan precursors, indicating compromised N-glycoprotein maturation. In parallel, CALR deficiency markedly reduced the abundance of the viral envelope (E) glycoprotein, suggesting that CALR plays a dual role in shaping host permissiveness and supporting viral glycoprotein biogenesis. Together, these results identify CALR as a central regulator of a replication-permissive ER glycoprotein maturation state that supports both ITGA3-associated cellular adhesion and efficient viral E protein biogenesis, thereby supporting JEV replication. Our findings highlight the calnexin/calreticulin (CANX/CALR)-dependent N-glycosylation axis as a potential host-directed target for antiviral strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13578-026-01607-x.Keywords: Japanese encephalitis virus, CANX/CALR cycle, N-linked glycosylation, ITGA3, Envelope (E) glycoprotein

Introduction

Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV), a mosquito-borne neurotropic flavivirus, remains a leading cause of viral encephalitis across Asia and the Western Pacific regions despite decades of vaccination [1, 2]. An estimated 67,900 severe cases and 13,600–20,400 deaths occur annually, with peak incidence in South, East, and Southeast Asia [3]. Ecologically, JEV is maintained in an enzootic transmission cycle involving avian reservoir hosts, swine amplifying hosts, and mosquito vectors, with Culex tritaeniorhynchus serving as a primary vector [4]. In swine, infection during pregnancy can cause abortion, stillbirth, and congenital malformations, resulting in significant economic losses to the swine industry [5, 6]. These dual threats to public health and animal agriculture underscore the need to elucidate host pathways that determine cellular susceptibility to JEV infection.

Efficient flaviviral infection requires coordinated virus-host interactions across multiple stages of the viral life cycle, including viral entry, translation and genome replication, progeny virion assembly, and release [7, 8]. While previous studies have identified host factors and pathways relevant to JEV infection, how these determinants are integrated into a broader replication-permissive cellular state remains incompletely defined [9, 10]. A porcine genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 knockout screen (PigGeCKO v1.0) previously identified calreticulin (CALR), an ER-resident soluble chaperone, as a critical host factor for JEV replication [10]. A complementary cytosine base-editing screen further showed that a noncoding intronic CALR variant substantially modulates JEV replication, implicating regulatory mechanisms beyond the coding sequence [11]. Given CALR’s well-established role in ER glycoprotein folding and quality control [12, 13], these observations raised the possibility that JEV permissiveness depends on CALR-orchestrated ER glycoprotein maturation broadly rather than on CALR alone. Yet, the specific mechanisms by which CALR supports infection, whether through early infection susceptibility, viral glycoprotein biogenesis, or both, remain undefined.

CALR functions as a core component of the calnexin/calreticulin (CANX/CALR) lectin chaperone cycle, a conserved ER quality-control (ERQC) system that promotes the folding of nascent N-glycoproteins [14]. This cycle recognizes monoglucosylated N-linked glycans (Glc1Man9GlcNAc2) on newly synthesized proteins, facilitating chaperone-assisted folding and preventing misfolded protein accumulation [15, 16]. The importance of ER proteostasis in viral life cycles is evident in CANX/CALR dependencies documented across diverse virus families, including influenza virus hemagglutinin and neuraminidase [17, 18], hepatitis B virus M protein [19], rabies virus G protein [20], herpes simplex virus gB/gC/gD [21], human cytomegalovirus gB [22], Hantaan virus Gn/Gc [23], Ebola virus GP [24], and HIV-1 gp160 [25, 26]. SARS-CoV-2 similarly requires host CANX/CALR and Spike N-glycans for efficient virion incorporation and infectivity [27]. In flaviviruses, including JEV, prior studies have likewise established that envelope protein N-glycosylation contributes to glycoprotein maturation, particle formation, and secretion [28–30]. However, prior studies have mainly considered CANX/CALR as chaperones for individual viral glycoproteins [17–27]. Whether lectin chaperone-dependent N-glycoprotein maturation contributes to a broader host state permissive for flaviviral infection by simultaneously supporting both viral entry determinants and glycoprotein biogenesis remains unclear, particularly for JEV.

We employ an integrated strategy combining CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout, multi-omics analysis, and targeted functional assays to dissect how CALR supports a replication-permissive host state for JEV. We show that CALR deficiency severely impairs early infection, including viral attachment and internalization, which decline sharply, accompanied by remodeling of cell-surface adhesion molecules. CALR supports a selective N-glycoprotein maturation state required for the steady-state abundance of host ITGA3, while also supporting the biogenesis of the viral envelope (E) glycoprotein. A knockout panel of N-glycosylation components with pharmacological inhibition reveals a functional hierarchy of ER glycoprotein maturation dependencies for JEV replication. These findings support the CANX/CALR cycle as a critical ER-localized control point creating a replication-permissive folding environment. Collectively, we show that CALR integrates ITGA3-associated cellular entry with ER-dependent viral glycoprotein production to enable productive JEV infection.

Results

CALR knockout reduces JEV attachment and internalization during early infection

To determine whether CALR expression is altered during JEV infection, we infected PK-15 cells with the genotype 3 JEV strain RP9 (JEV-RP9) at MOIs of 0.03, 0.1, and 1 and quantified CALR mRNA levels at 24 h post-infection (hpi) (Fig. 1A). In parallel, CALR protein expression was detected over a time course (0, 12, 24, and 36 hpi) by immunoblotting in WT PK-15 cells infected at an MOI of 1 (Fig. 1B). Neither CALR transcript nor protein levels changed substantially following infection, indicating that JEV does not significantly modulate CALR expression in PK-15 cells under these conditions.

Despite the absence of virus-induced CALR regulation, CALR ablation markedly reduced viral replication outputs, as demonstrated by decreased NS3 protein abundance and lower intracellular and extracellular viral RNA levels in CALR-KO cells relative to WT controls (Figs. 1C-G). To define the temporal window of CALR dependence, we performed a time-course infection in CALR-KO and wild-type (WT) PK-15 cells. NS3 levels were already substantially reduced in CALR-KO cells by 12 hpi (Fig. 1H), suggesting that CALR supports events occurring at, or immediately following, the onset of infection and prompting us to examine early entry steps.

We next quantified JEV entry by separating viral attachment from subsequent internalization. For attachment, cells were incubated with JEV at 4 °C to restrict virion engagement to the cell surface, followed by extensive washing to remove unbound particles. Cell-associated viral RNA measurements showed that CALR-KO reduced JEV attachment by ~ 49% relative to WT cells (Fig. 1I). For internalization, virion binding was initiated at 4 °C and then shifted to 37 °C to allow endocytic uptake. Extracellular virions were subsequently removed by Proteinase K treatment before RT-qPCR quantification. Under these conditions, CALR-KO decreased internalized JEV RNA by ~ 32% (Fig. 1J). Notably, the reduction in internalized viral RNA was less pronounced than the reduction in viral attachment. This may suggest that CALR deficiency has a stronger impact on cell-surface factors involved in JEV attachment than on the downstream internalization step.

Collectively, these findings identify CALR as a critical host factor facilitating the early phase of JEV infection by promoting both viral attachment and internalization. The rapid emergence of the defect at 12 hpi further supports a role for CALR in establishing initial cellular permissiveness. We therefore next investigated whether CALR regulates the cell-surface environment required for efficient virion engagement.

CALR deficiency remodels the cell-surface adhesion network and reduces cellular permissiveness to JEV infection

Although CALR is primarily an ER-resident lectin chaperone, it can translocate to the plasma membrane under specific conditions [31–33]. To determine whether CALR functions as a receptor-like entry factor for JEV, we examined its interaction with full-length JEV E and a panel of domain-truncated Flag-tagged E constructs. JEV E contains a central domain I (DI), a fusion-associated domain II (DII), and a receptor interaction-associated domain III (DIII). In particular, DIII and its exposed DE/FG loops have been implicated in flaviviral receptor binding [34]. We therefore used E-Flag, DI + DII-Flag, DIII + TMD-Flag, E-ΔDII-Flag, and E-ΔDIII-Flag to assess whether CALR association depended on the DIII region (Fig. 2A). CALR was efficiently co-precipitated by full-length E as well as DI + DII-Flag, E-ΔDII-Flag, and notably E-ΔDIII-Flag, whereas DIII + TMD-Flag failed to pull down CALR. Given the established role of DIII in flaviviral receptor binding [35, 36], the retained interaction with E-ΔDIII argues against a simple model in which CALR functions as a direct viral attachment receptor. Consistently, antibody-blocking assays using increasing concentrations of anti-CALR antibody (0–100 µg/mL) did not reduce NS3 levels in infected cells (Fig. 2B), suggesting that CALR does not play a dominant role as an attachment receptor under our experimental conditions. These observations prompted us to explore whether CALR instead supports JEV entry indirectly by remodeling host cell-surface determinants required for virion engagement.

To define how CALR influences the host entry interface, we performed quantitative proteomic profiling of CALR-KO and WT PK-15 cells. A total of 342 proteins were differentially expressed, including 173 downregulated and 169 upregulated proteins in CALR-KO cells (Fig. 2C). Pathway analysis revealed that CALR ablation primarily remodeled processes related to cell-surface adhesion, extracellular matrix (ECM) organization, glycosaminoglycan/heparan sulfate (HS) biology, and membrane microdomain organization (Figures S1A-D). This suggests a coordinated restructuring of the host cell surface and entry-related architecture, rather than isolated changes in individual proteins. Given the reduced viral attachment and internalization observed upon CALR-KO cells, we prioritized differentially expressed candidates most relevant to the host entry interface, especially those involved in cell-surface adhesion and extracellular matrix interactions. We therefore focused on adhesion-associated candidates with known or plausible roles in virus-host interactions.

Proteomic profiling revealed a coordinated downregulation of adhesion-related and ECM-associated proteins in CALR-KO cells (Figure S1C). These included integrin subunits (ITGA2, ITGA3, ITGA5, and ITGB1) as well as key ECM components such as LAMB1, LAMC1, and FN1 (Fig. 2C). Given previous studies linking integrins to flavivirus entry and highlighting the broader roles of ECM components in pathogen adhesion and invasion [37–39], these alterations suggest a compromised cell-surface interface for viral attachment and entry. Concurrently, our analysis identified reduced levels of HS-associated factors, including HSPG2, AGRN, and NAGLU, indicating possible decreases in HS abundance or changes in its surface organization (Fig. 2C). We also observed a decline in proteins linked to cholesterol-rich membrane microdomains and endocytic platforms, most notably CAV1 and CAVIN1 (Fig. 2C). In contrast, proteins upregulated in CALR-KO cells were predominantly associated with metabolic reprogramming and redox adaptation (Figure S1A). This group included ALDOB, CPS1, FBP1, GCLC, GSTA1, NQO1, and LDHA, along with stress-responsive factors such as NDRG1 and P4HA1 (Fig. 2C). This overall expression profile reflects a shift away from a cell-surface state that supports efficient viral adhesion and internalization. Collectively, these proteomic signatures indicate that loss of CALR systematically remodels the cell-surface adhesion landscape and membrane architecture, thereby altering cellular susceptibility to JEV infection.

We next validated representative downregulated candidates by immunoblotting and confirmed reduced protein abundance of ITGA2, ITGA3, ITGB1, LAMB1, and LAMC1 in CALR-KO cells (Fig. 2D), corroborating the proteomic signature at the protein level. Given the established participation of integrins in flaviviral entry, we functionally tested ITGA3 and ITGB1 by generating ITGA3-KO and ITGB1-KO PK-15 cells (Fig. 2E). Upon infection with JEV at an MOI of 0.1, both knockouts displayed markedly decreased NS3 levels at 24 hpi (Fig. 2F-G) and reduced viral genome copy numbers in supernatants (Fig. 2H) compared with WT cells, indicating that ITGA3 and ITGB1 are important for supporting efficient JEV infection. Consistently, immunofluorescence and confocal microscopy revealed co-localization of viral E protein with ITGA3 and ITGB1 in PK-15 cells (Figs. 2I and S2A). This signal likely reflects overlap during biosynthesis and trafficking, in addition to any co-localization at the cell surface. Moreover, Co-IP assays in HEK293T cells co-expressing ITGA3, ITGB1, and JEV E showed that E specifically interacted with ITGA3, but not ITGB1 (Fig. 2J). These results, together with the canonical α/β heterodimeric structure of integrins, support an infection-permissive ITGA3/ITGB1 module in which ITGA3 serves as the principal viral interaction subunit.

In parallel, we tested heparan sulfate (HS)-associated components, as HS is known to promote viral binding and subsequent entry into host cells non-specifically [40, 41]. Immunofluorescence staining confirmed decreased HSPG2 abundance in CALR-KO cells (Figures S2B-C), and genetic disruption of HSPG2 reduced JEV infection, as indicated by diminished NS3 expression (Figures S2D-E). In addition, staining with an anti-heparan sulfate antibody showed reduced surface HS signal in CALR-KO cells (Figures S2F-H), indicating a global attenuation of HS-dependent viral attachment capacity. Together, these findings demonstrate that CALR deficiency induces coordinated remodeling of adhesion-related cell-surface architecture. The combined reduction in an ITGA3-centered integrin module and HS abundance likely underlies the diminished permissiveness of CALR-deficient cells to productive JEV infection.

CALR selectively sustains ITGA3 abundance through N-linked glycoprotein maturation, independently of overt UPR, autophagy, or ERAD activation

To clarify how CALR regulates integrin expression, we first investigated whether CALR deficiency reduces ITGA3 abundance through activation of stress-associated degradative pathways (Fig. 3A). Given CALR’s well-established roles in ER-based protein folding quality control and calcium homeostasis [42, 43], we initially examined whether CALR-KO triggers an ER stress response that could secondarily influence ITGA3 abundance through activation of the unfolded protein response (UPR) pathway. The UPR pathway is orchestrated by three core signal transducers: protein kinase R-like ER kinase (PERK), inositol-requiring enzyme 1 (IRE1), and activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6) [44, 45]. However, Western blot analysis showed no substantial changes in the ER stress marker binding immunoglobulin protein (BiP) or in the activation status of UPR sensors PERK, phosphorylated IRE1 (p-IRE1), and ATF6 in CALR-KO PK-15 cells compared with WT cells (Figure S3A). These data indicate that UPR activation is not evident in CALR-deficient cells and therefore unlikely to account for ITGA3 downregulation.

We next examined whether lysosome-dependent autophagic degradation contributes to reduced ITGA3 abundance in CALR-KO cells. Under basal conditions, CALR-KO cells exhibited markedly reduced LC3-II levels together with decreased p62 abundance compared with WT cells, indicating a low baseline autophagosome formation state. Upon bafilomycin A1 (Baf-A1; 100 nM, 6 h) treatment, both CALR-KO and WT cells showed increased LC3-II and p62 accumulation relative to untreated controls. However, the magnitude of accumulation was substantially lower in CALR-KO cells than in WT cells (Figure S3B). These data do not support enhanced lysosome-dependent autophagic degradation as the major cause of reduced ITGA3 abundance in CALR-KO cells. Instead, the observed LC3-II and p62 patterns are more consistent with impaired autophagosome biogenesis under our experimental conditions.

We further examined whether CALR deficiency places cells into a hyper-degradative ER quality control state that could secondarily contribute to reduced ITGA3 abundance. The ER-associated degradation (ERAD) pathway is a central ER quality control module responsible for eliminating misfolded glycoproteins. Western blot analysis revealed that the abundance of the ERAD lectin adaptors EDEM1 and EDEM3, two representative components involved in targeting misfolded glycoproteins for degradation, displayed a consistent downward trend in CALR-KO cells compared with WT cells (Figure S3C). Thus, CALR deficiency does not appear to induce compensatory ERAD activation, nor do the data support accelerated ERAD as the principal mechanism underlying reduced ITGA3 abundance.

Given CALR’s established role in N-linked glycoprotein maturation, we next examined whether CALR deficiency is associated with altered post-translational N-glycosylation. Because integrins function as α/β heterodimers, we included ITGB1 as a comparative control in subsequent assays to test whether CALR-dependent glycosylation selectively affects ITGA3 or more broadly impacts integrin components. Glycomics profiling revealed pronounced accumulation of immature high-mannose N-glycan structures (e.g., Man9GlcNAc2, Glc1Man9GlcNAc2, Glc2Man9GlcNAc2) accompanied by a marked reduction in complex and hybrid N-glycans in CALR-KO cells (Fig. 3B). This profile indicates impaired conversion of ER-resident high-mannose intermediates into mature Golgi-processed complex glycans and supports a CALR-dependent defect in N-glycoprotein maturation that may contribute to reduced ITGA3 abundance.

To validate these glycomic alterations at the cellular level, we performed lectin staining using a panel of N-glycan-specific lectins. Consistent with the accumulation of glycan precursors, Concanavalin A (ConA) and Aleuria aurantia lectin (AAL), which recognize high-mannose and fucosylated mannose structures, showed little difference between CALR-KO and WT cells. In contrast, Phaseolus vulgaris erythroagglutinin (PHA-E), a lectin that selectively binds bisected and multi-antennary complex N-glycans generated downstream of MGAT1/MGAT3, was markedly reduced in CALR-KO cells, indicating a pronounced loss of mature glycan structures (Fig. 3C). Lectin staining for terminal sialic acids (Sambucus nigra lectin, SNA; Maackia amurensis lectin II, MAL-II) was minimal in both CALR-KO and WT PK-15 cells, indicating low endogenous levels of α2,6- and α2,3-linked sialylation in this cell line (Figures S3D-E). Collectively, this lectin-binding profile provides orthogonal, cell-based evidence that CALR deficiency is associated with impaired formation of mature complex N-glycans, in agreement with the glycomics results.

To functionally probe whether N-glycosylation contributes to CALR-dependent integrin expression, we next examined whether pharmacological disruption of N-glycosylation phenocopies CALR deficiency and whether this effect is selective for ITGA3. WT and CALR-KO PK-15 cells were treated with 1-deoxynojirimycin (1-DNJ), an α-glucosidase inhibitor, which prevents glucose trimming and thereby impairs entry of nascent N-glycoproteins into the CANX/CALR cycle, and ITGA3 and ITGB1 protein abundance were assessed. Under basal conditions, ITGA3 and ITGB1 protein abundance were reduced in CALR-KO cells compared with WT cells (Fig. 3D-E). Upon 1-DNJ treatment, ITGA3 levels were further decreased in both WT and CALR-KO cells, with a more pronounced reduction observed in WT cells. This smaller dynamic range in CALR-KO cells is consistent with their reduced basal ITGA3 abundance. By contrast, ITGB1 showed little additional change upon 1-DNJ treatment under the conditions tested (Fig. 3D-E). Consistent with this observation, treatment of WT PK-15 cells with increasing concentrations of 1-DNJ resulted in a clear dose-dependent reduction of ITGA3 protein abundance, whereas ITGB1 levels remained relatively stable across all concentrations tested (Fig. 3F-G). Together, these results indicate that ITGA3 is substantially more sensitive than ITGB1 to disruption of N-glycosylation under our experimental conditions.

To further examine whether CALR directly promotes ITGA3 expression, we performed ectopic co-expression assays in HEK293T cells. FLAG-tagged ITGA3 or ITGB1 constructs were co-transfected with increasing doses of HA-tagged CALR expression plasmids (0, 0.5, 1, and 2 µg). Western blot analysis revealed a dose-dependent increase in ITGA3 protein abundance upon CALR co-expression, accompanied by a modest increase in ITGB1 expression (Fig. 3H-I), consistent with a chaperone-like role of CALR in supporting ITGA3 biogenesis within the ER folding environment. Fluorescence and confocal microscopy further showed that CALR and ITGA3 co-localized predominantly in the ER (Figs. 3J and S3F), and co-immunoprecipitation assays demonstrated an association between CALR and ITGA3 (Fig. 3K). Collectively, these findings support a model in which CALR associates with ITGA3 in the ER and promotes its glycan-dependent maturation and abundance. By sustaining ITGA3 surface expression, CALR supports a permissive cellular environment that facilitates productive JEV infection.

The CANX/CALR cycle supports JEV E glycoprotein maturation and viral replication through host N-linked glycosylation

Because CALR deficiency profoundly altered host N-glycoprotein maturation, we next investigated whether disruption of the ER glycoprotein folding environment also impacts viral glycoprotein biogenesis, focusing on the JEV E protein, a typical N-glycosylated viral glycoprotein whose folding depends on ER quality control machinery. Co-immunoprecipitation assays using individually expressed Flag-tagged JEV proteins revealed that CALR interacts with multiple viral proteins, including the E protein (Fig. 4A). We therefore focused subsequent analyses on the E glycoprotein. Ectopic expression of E-Flag in PK-15 cells revealed markedly reduced E protein abundance in CALR-KO cells compared with WT controls (Fig. 4B). Consistently, during viral infection, E protein was readily detectable in WT cells but was barely detectable in CALR-KO cells across multiple MOIs at 24 hpi (Figure S4A). These data indicate that CALR contributes to the establishment of an ER folding environment that supports the steady-state abundance of the viral E glycoprotein.

To place this CALR-dependent phenotype within the spatial context of ER glycoprotein folding, we next investigated the subcellular relationship between CALR, its homolog CANX, and the JEV E protein. Immunofluorescence staining of PK-15 cells co-expressing HA-tagged CALR or CANX and Flag-tagged JEV E revealed clear co-localization of both chaperones with the E protein, predominantly in the ER (Fig. 4C, S4C), consistent with the ER-resident localization of the CANX/CALR chaperone system and the site of E protein biogenesis. Co-immunoprecipitation assays in the same system further confirmed that both CALR and CANX associate with the JEV E protein (Fig. 4D). Notably, the CALR-E association was retained when using the N154Q mutant of JEV E, in which the conserved N-glycosylation site (Asn154) is substituted with Gln (Fig. 4D). Our Co-IP result indicates that the CALR-E association is not abolished by the N154Q mutation. However, our current data do not distinguish whether this residual association reflects productive glycan-independent chaperoning, recognition of another glycan-dependent state, or retention of a non-native E conformer.

Because CALR and CANX cooperate in ER quality control of N-glycoproteins [13, 46], we next examined whether CANX similarly contributes to JEV replication. We generated CANX-KO PK-15 cells via CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing and compared virus-induced cytopathicity across genotypes. Following 60 h of JEV infection at an MOI of 1, WT cells exhibited extensive cytopathic effects (CPE), whereas both CALR-KO and CANX-KO cells showed substantial protection from JEV-induced CPE (Figure S4B). Notably, CALR-KO cells displayed the strongest resistance, supporting a major role for CALR in maintaining a replication-permissive ER folding environment.

To further dissect the functional contribution of the CANX/CALR module, we compared viral protein abundance and genome replication in CALR-KO, CANX-KO, and CANX/CALR double-KO (DKO) cells. Western blotting and indirect immunofluorescence staining showed reduced NS3 and E protein levels in all KO cell lines relative to WT cells, with the strongest suppression observed in DKO cells (Fig. 4E-H, S4D). RT-qPCR analyses of both relative intracellular viral RNA and absolute extracellular viral RNA copy numbers confirmed that JEV replication was inhibited in CALR-KO and DKO cells, whereas CANX-KO cells showed a more modest reduction (Fig. 4I-J). Together, these findings support a cooperative role for the CANX/CALR lectin chaperone system in sustaining viral gene expression and replication, with CALR serving as a primary contributor and CANX providing complementary support.

Because the CANX/CALR system is functionally coupled to N-glycan processing, we next tested whether pharmacological disruption of N-glycosylation phenocopies the genetic perturbations. WT PK-15 cells were treated with increasing concentrations of 1-DNJ (0–2 mM) and then infected with JEV at an MOI of 1 for 24 h. Immunoblotting revealed a dose-dependent decrease in NS3 and E protein abundance with increasing 1-DNJ concentration (Fig. 4K). To determine whether broader disruption of N-glycosylation similarly impairs viral replication, we pharmacologically perturbed additional stages of the host N-glycosylation pathway upstream of this checkpoint (schematized in Fig. 5A). Specifically, we used tunicamycin to block early N-glycan initiation [47], NGI-1 to inhibit oligosaccharyltransferase-mediated glycan transfer [48], and castanospermine and miglitol to inhibit ER α-glucosidase-mediated glucose trimming steps required for entry into the CANX/CALR cycle [49, 50]. All compounds reduced NS3 and E protein levels (Fig. 4L), with tunicamycin and NGI-1 showing the most pronounced suppression under the conditions tested.

Finally, to assess the intrinsic chaperone capacity of CALR and CANX toward JEV E in a heterologous context with reduced cell-type-specific confounders, we performed ectopic co-expression assays in HEK293T cells. Overexpression of either chaperone enhanced E protein abundance in a dose-dependent manner, with CALR showing a more pronounced effect (Fig. 4M-N). These results further support a model in which the CANX/CALR chaperone system sustains the abundance of the JEV E glycoprotein by maintaining an ER folding environment permissive for viral glycoprotein biogenesis.

Collectively, these results identify the CANX/CALR chaperone module and the coupled host N-glycosylation pathway as critical determinants of JEV E protein maturation and productive viral replication. Both genetic disruption of lectin chaperones and pharmacological inhibition of N-glycosylation profoundly impair JEV replication, supporting the CANX/CALR-dependent ER folding network as a key host dependency pathway for JEV infection.

Systematic perturbation of host N-glycosylation identifies the CANX/CALR lectin chaperone cycle as a major control node for JEV replication

To extend our mechanistic framework beyond CALR and define its role within the broader host N-glycosylation network, we generated a panel of PK-15 knockout cell lines targeting key steps in the N-glycan processing pathway (Figs. 5A and S5). These included the catalytic subunits of the oligosaccharyltransferase (OST) complex (STT3A-KO, STT3B-KO), the ER-localized α-glucosidases that supply substrates to the lectin chaperone cycle (MOGS-KO, GANAB-KO), a downstream Golgi N-glycan branching enzyme (MGAT1-KO), and the ER lectin chaperones (CALR-KO, CANX-KO, and CANX/CALR double-knockout). Together, these perturbations span the major stages of the host N-glycosylation pathway illustrated in Fig. 5A.

To systematically determine how disruption of individual glycosylation checkpoints affects the replication-permissive ER folding environment, all knockout cell lines were infected with JEV at an MOI of 1 and analyzed at 24 hpi using three complementary readouts: intracellular NS3 protein abundance by immunoblotting, relative viral RNA levels by quantitative RT-PCR, and the fraction of NS3-positive cells by immunofluorescence. Here, NS3 was used as a standardized indicator of productive infection and replication output, whereas E was examined in other experiments as a more direct indicator of viral glycoprotein maturation.

Immunoblotting revealed differential suppression of NS3 levels across the KO panel, with the strongest restriction observed upon perturbation of the lectin chaperone module (CANX/CALR) and the upstream glucose trimming steps (Fig. 5B). From lowest to highest NS3 abundance, the hierarchy was: CANX/CALR-DKO < GANAB-KO < MOGS-KO < CALR-KO < CANX-KO < STT3A-KO < MGAT1-KO < STT3B-KO < WT. RT-qPCR quantification of viral RNA mirrored this trend (Fig. 5C), and immunofluorescence imaging further supported a differential decrease in NS3-positive cells across the panel (Fig. 5D-E), indicating that disruption of host glycoprotein folding/glycosylation constrains productive infection.

Collectively, these data demonstrate that JEV replication is highly dependent on an intact host N-glycosylation network and identify the CANX/CALR lectin chaperone cycle as a major regulatory node within this pathway. The particularly strong antiviral effect observed upon combined CANX/CALR ablation further supports a major role for the CANX/CALR cycle in maintaining the replication-permissive ER glycoprotein-folding environment required for efficient JEV replication.

Discussion

Our previous porcine genome-wide CRISPR knockout screen identified CALR as a critical host factor required for JEV replication [10]. As an ER-resident chaperone, CALR plays a critical role in glycoprotein folding and N-linked glycosylation [12, 13]. Here, through integrated genetic perturbation, multi-omics profiling, and functional analysis, we define a unified model in which CALR supports a host cellular state permissive for JEV infection through two interconnected mechanisms: (i) maintenance of a glycan-dependent cell-surface adhesion network centered on ITGA3, and (ii) preservation of an ER glycoprotein-folding environment that supports viral glycoprotein biogenesis. In WT cells, an adhesion landscape enriched in ITGA3/ITGB1 and HS-associated components likely promotes efficient virion engagement, while the ER glycoprotein quality-control machinery supports maturation of newly synthesized viral structural proteins, including E. In CALR-deficient cells, both layers of permissiveness are disrupted: viral early infection is impaired, E protein abundance is markedly reduced, and downstream replication is strongly restricted (Fig. 6). These findings therefore position CALR not simply as a host dependency factor, but as a central regulator of the infection-permissive state required for efficient JEV replication.

Proposed model illustrating how CALR regulates cellular permissiveness to JEV infection through dual glycoprotein maturation-associated mechanisms. In WT cells (left), the CANX/CALR lectin chaperone cycle maintains an ER folding environment that supports efficient maturation and abundance of the viral E glycoprotein. Simultaneously, CALR-dependent N-glycosylation selectively supports ITGA3 maturation and steady-state abundance, establishing a key CALR-dependent node within a broader adhesion- and glycan-associated cell-surface network. Together, these processes facilitate early viral entry and productive replication. In CALR-KO cells (right), disruption of the CANX/CALR cycle impairs ER glycoprotein maturation, leading to reduced ITGA3 and viral E protein abundance and consequently restricting both early infection and downstream viral replication. Broader glycosylation remodeling may additionally alter other adhesion- and glycan-associated surface components (e.g., CAV1, HSPG2, and HS chains)

A major mechanistic insight from this study is that CALR deficiency disrupts N-glycosylation homeostasis in a manner that links host-cell permissiveness with viral glycoprotein maturation. Glycomics profiling revealed accumulation of immature high-mannose N-glycan precursors, including Glc1Man9GlcNAc2 and Glc2Man9GlcNAc2, together with depletion of complex and hybrid glycans, indicating impaired progression from ER-resident intermediates to mature Golgi-processed structures. Lectin staining independently confirmed a substantial loss of mature complex N-glycan features at the cellular level. Functionally, inhibition of glucose trimming with 1-DNJ, which disrupts productive engagement of nascent glycoproteins with the CANX/CALR cycle, caused a dose-dependent reduction of ITGA3 abundance. By contrast, ITGB1 remained comparatively stable under the same conditions, indicating selective sensitivity of the ITGA3 subunit to CALR-dependent glycoprotein maturation. Because ITGB1 pairs with multiple α-integrin subunits, reduced ITGA3 abundance would be expected to preferentially impair the abundance of the ITGA3/ITGB1 heterodimer without equivalently reducing total ITGB1 levels. This provides a mechanistic explanation for why ITGA3 emerged as the most prominent CALR-dependent adhesion node in our system. More broadly, the data support a model in which CALR maintains a glycoprotein maturation state that selectively sustains ITGA3 maturation while simultaneously remodeling additional adhesion- and glycan-associated surface components through broader alterations in N-glycosylation.

Our findings further indicate that CALR regulates this permissive state independently of overt activation of canonical stress or degradative pathways. CALR deficiency did not induce detectable UPR activation, and autophagy marker profiles were more consistent with reduced autophagosome formation than enhanced lysosome-dependent degradation. Previous studies in calreticulin-deficient fibroblasts reported accumulation of unfolded proteins and activation of UPR-associated pathways, including PERK, IRE1α, and eIF2α phosphorylation [52]. In contrast, under our conditions, we did not observe overt activation of canonical UPR markers in CALR-KO PK-15 cells, and our proteomic analyses similarly failed to identify broad UPR induction. This discrepancy may reflect cell-type-specific differences, long-term adaptation in clonally selected knockout cells, or distinct client-specific consequences of CALR loss. Likewise, reduced EDEM1 and EDEM3 abundance argues against hyper-activation of ER-associated degradation (ERAD) as the principal cause of ITGA3 loss. Although additional ER quality-control pathways not directly examined here may contribute, the overall data indicate that CALR deficiency primarily impairs glycoprotein maturation capacity rather than globally shifting cells toward stress-induced degradation. Interestingly, we also observed reduced abundance of proteins associated with cholesterol-rich membrane microdomains and endocytic platforms, particularly CAV1 and CAVIN1, consistent with previous reports linking caveolin-associated membrane organization and actin remodeling to JEV entry [53]. Together, these observations highlight the multifaceted role of CALR in coordinating the cellular environment required for productive infection.

A second layer of CALR-dependent permissiveness operates at the level of viral glycoprotein biogenesis. Previous studies in flaviviruses, including JEV, have already established that envelope protein N-glycosylation contributes to viral glycoprotein maturation, virion assembly, and secretion [28–30]. Our findings extend this framework by positioning CALR within a broader host-permissiveness network that links host-cell entry determinants with viral glycoprotein maturation. CALR is associated with multiple JEV proteins, including E, and E protein abundance was profoundly reduced in CALR-KO cells during both ectopic expression and authentic infection. These results indicate that CALR is required to maintain E glycoprotein abundance. CANX likewise associated and co-localized with E in the ER, and comparative knockout analyses revealed graded defects in viral replication (CANX/CALR-DKO > CALR-KO > CANX-KO), supporting complementary roles for both lectin chaperones in maintaining a replication-permissive ER folding environment. Importantly, the stronger phenotype associated with CALR deficiency suggests that CALR and CANX are not functionally equivalent during JEV infection. This may reflect differential client specificity, unequal dependence of viral and host glycoproteins on the CALR branch of the ER lectin chaperone system, or additional non-redundant functions that become apparent during infection [54], although the relative contribution of these possibilities remains unresolved. Notably, the CALR-E association persisted when the conserved E N-glycosylation site was mutated (N154Q), indicating that the association is not completely abolished by loss of the N154-linked glycan. However, the biological significance of this residual association remains unclear and may reflect productive glycan-independent chaperoning or instead non-productive retention of non-native E conformers.

More broadly, our study highlights the pronounced dependence of JEV on ER-centered N-glycan processing and lectin chaperone-mediated folding checkpoints. Although the knockout panel generally revealed stronger antiviral effects following disruption of ER glucose-trimming and CANX/CALR-associated steps than after perturbation of distal Golgi processing (MGAT1), the phenotypic strength did not strictly follow a linear upstream-to-downstream pathway order. Instead, the magnitude of viral restriction likely reflects a combination of pathway position, functional redundancy, checkpoint-specific importance for entry into the CANX/CALR cycle, and the differential sensitivity of JEV-relevant host and viral glycoproteins to perturbation at distinct stages. Within this framework, the particularly strong antiviral effects caused by disrupting glucose-trimming enzymes and lectin chaperone-associated checkpoints indicate that ER folding control represents a critical vulnerability for JEV, whereas distal Golgi remodeling appears less restrictive under our experimental conditions. This state-centered perspective may also help explain virus-specific differences in CALR/CANX function. For example, during Ebola virus infection, CANX and CALR promote misfolding and ERAD/reticulophagy-dependent degradation through RNF185-mediated ubiquitination, thereby modulating fitness rather than supporting productive glycoprotein maturation [55]. Thus, ER quality-control states regulated by CALR may be exploited differently depending on the folding requirements and replication strategies of individual viruses.

Our findings may also have broader implications for flavivirus biology. Many flaviviruses share conserved ER-dependent replication and maturation programs and rely on glycosylated envelope proteins for efficient virion biogenesis and infectivity [30, 56–58]. Prior studies on dengue and Zika viruses therefore provide a rationale for investigating whether CALR similarly contributes to permissive glycoprotein maturation states across related flaviviruses [56, 57]. At the same time, CALR is not universally pro-viral. In SARS-CoV-2 infection, CALR has been reported to restrict infectivity by promoting lysosome-dependent degradation of the Spike protein [59]. Together, these observations support a context-dependent model in which CALR regulates viral permissiveness according to the specific ER folding and trafficking requirements of individual pathogens. Importantly, the mechanistic conclusions of the present study derive primarily from the PK-15 model systems using the JEV-RP9 strain and should therefore be validated in additional cell types and related flaviviral systems to assess their broader generalizability.

Finally, our findings have implications for host-directed antiviral strategies. Previous studies have already demonstrated that flaviviral infection can be suppressed by targeting host glycosylation-associated pathways, including ER glucosidase-dependent trimming steps [49, 60, 61]. Our data do not introduce glycosylation-targeting antiviral strategies de novo, but instead place CALR/CANX-linked glycoprotein maturation within a broader host-directed antiviral framework. More specifically, the present study refines this framework by identifying the CALR/CANX-linked ER glycoprotein maturation network as a mechanistically defined vulnerability in JEV infection. Genetic ablation of CALR, pharmacologic inhibition of the CANX/CALR cycle (1-DNJ), or blockade of N-glycosylation steps (tunicamycin, NGI-1) each potently restricted JEV replication, underscoring the strong dependence of JEV on ER glycoprotein maturation. However, because CALR is a multifunctional host protein involved in ER quality control and calcium homeostasis, direct therapeutic targeting may carry substantial toxicity risks. Future strategies may therefore benefit from selectively disrupting virus-specific CALR-dependent interactions or targeting discrete glycosylation checkpoints required for viral replication while preserving essential host functions.

In summary, this study identifies CALR as a central regulator of cellular permissiveness to JEV infection by coordinating both an ITGA3-centered glycan-dependent adhesion network and an ER folding environment required for efficient viral E glycoprotein maturation. By defining permissiveness as a regulated cellular state, our findings establish a mechanistic framework linking ER chaperone function, N-glycosylation, and flaviviral replication. These results further highlight the CANX/CALR-dependent glycoprotein maturation axis as a promising target for host-directed antiviral intervention and provide a foundation for future studies examining how ER lectin chaperone networks shape viral tropism and pathogenicity across flaviviruses.

Materials and methods

Plasmid construction

Single-guide RNA (sgRNA) sequences targeting porcine CALR, CANX, STT3A, STT3B, MOGS, GANAB, MGAT1, ITGA3, ITGB1, and HSPG2 were designed using the sgRNAcas9 software [62]. Individual paired oligonucleotides of sgRNA (50 µM per oligo) were annealed and cloned into pKLV2-U6gRNA5(BbsI)-PGKpuro2AZsG-W (Addgene #67975), which was linearized with BpiI (Thermo Fisher). All primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Data 1.

Cell culture and virus infection

PK-15 and HEK293T cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Cell-Box, AUS-01 S-02), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, 15140), and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. For infection experiments, cells were infected with JEV-RP9 at an MOI of 0.03, 0.1, or 1, as specified, and harvested at designated time points post-infection (typically 24 or 48 hpi) for downstream analyses.

sgRNA library lentivirus production and transduction

Lentivirus production was carried out by co-transfecting HEK293T cells in 100 mm dishes with 12 µg of sgRNA library plasmid, 4 µg of the envelope plasmid pMD2.G (Addgene, #12259), and 8 µg of the packaging plasmid psPAX2 (Addgene, #12260) using JetPRIME transfection reagent (PolyPlus), following the manufacturer’s protocol. At 60 h post-transfection, the culture supernatants were harvested, filtered through a 0.45 μm low protein-binding membrane (Millipore), and ultracentrifuged at 100,000×g for 3 h at 4 °C. The resulting viral pellets were resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4), aliquoted, and stored at -80 °C until use.

For transduction, target cells were incubated with lentiviral supernatants in the presence of 8 µg/mL polybrene (Millipore, 1003-G). After 24 h, the medium was replaced with fresh complete culture medium to remove residual virus.

CRISPR/Cas9-mediated generation of knockout cell lines

SgRNAs targeting CALR, CANX, STT3A, STT3B, MOGS, GANAB, MGAT1, ITGA3, ITGB1, and HSPG2 were synthesized and cloned into the pKLV2-U6gRNA5(BbsI)-PGKpuro2AZsG-W vector (Addgene, #67975). Lentivirus production and transduction were performed as previously described. Single-cell clones were isolated by limiting dilution and screened for gene disruption by PCR and Sanger sequencing. Protein depletion was confirmed by Western blotting.

RNA extraction and quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Reverse transcription was performed using NovoScript® Plus All-in-one 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Novoprotein, E047-01B). Relative gene expression was measured by SYBR Green-based qPCR (Novoprotein, E099-01 A) using GAPDH as an internal control. For absolute quantification of viral RNA, standard curves were generated from plasmids containing JEV-C gene fragments.

Western blot and antibodies

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked in 5% milk and incubated with primary antibodies against Flag (Abbkine, ABT2011), HA (Abbkine, ABT2041), JEV-NS3 (GeneTex, GTX125868), JEV-E (GeneTex, GTX638878), ITGA2 (Abcam, ab133557), ITGA3 (Abclonal, A17502), ITGB1 (Abcam, EPR16895), LAMB1 (Abclonal, A19970), LAMC1 (Abclonal, A16020), CALR (Abclonal, A1066), CANX (Proteintech, 10427-2-AP), BiP (Proteintech, 11587-1-AP), PERK (Abcam, EPR19876-294), EIF2α (Proteintech, 11170-1-AP), p-EIF2α (Affinity, #AF3087), ATF6 (Abcam, EPR22690-84), LC3 (Abcam, ab192890), p62 (Abclonal, A19700), EDEM1 (Proteintech, 26226-1-AP), EDEM3 (Proteintech, 27310-1-AP), HSPG2 (Abcam, ab2501), β-actin (Abbkine, A01010-SK) and GAPDH (Abclonal, AC033). HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were detected with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Beyotime, A0208) or goat anti-mouse IgG (Beyotime, A0216), and signals were detected with ECL reagent (Millipore, WBKLS0500).

Immunofluorescence, lectin staining, and confocal microscopy

Cells grown on confocal dishes (Nest, BS-15-GJM) were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100. After blocking in 5% BSA (VETEC, V900933), cells were incubated with primary antibodies against Flag (Abcam, ab205606), HA (MBL, M180-3), MYC (Abbkine, ABT2060), or anti-heparan sulfate (US Biological, H1890), followed by fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor™ 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-11008), Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor™ 568 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-11004). Nuclei were stained with DAPI. Images were captured using a Nikon AXR NSPARC confocal microscope. Co-localization analyses were quantified using Pearson’s correlation coefficient (PCC) in selected regions of interest (ROIs) from multiple fields using ImageJ with the Coloc2 plugin. Biotinylated lectins used in this study included Concanavalin A (Vector Labs, B-1005), Aleuria aurantia lectin (Vector Labs, B-1395), Phaseolus vulgaris erythroagglutinin (Vector Labs, B-1125), Sambucus nigra lectin (Vector Labs, FL-1301), and Maackia amurensis lectin II (Vector Labs, B-1265). For lectin staining, fixed cells were blocked with 5% BSA and incubated with the indicated biotinylated lectins according to the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by detection with Streptavidin-CoraLite® Plus 555 Conjugate (Proteintech, CL555-PF00030), and fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

HEK293T cells were transfected with combinations of plasmids encoding FLAG-tagged JEV-E, HA-tagged ITGA3, ITGB1, CALR, or CANX. At 36 h post-transfection, cells were lysed in NP-40 Lysis Buffer (Beyotime, P0013F) containing Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Abmole, M5293). The cell lysates were incubated with Flag-tag mAb (MBL, M185-3 S) at 4 °C overnight and then with Protein A/G magnetic beads (Thermo, 88802) for 6 h. After incubation, the magnetic beads were rinsed five times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then resuspended in 1× Protein Sample Loading Buffer (Epizyme, LT101) for protein elution. The eluted co-immunoprecipitated proteins were analyzed by Western blot (immunoblotting) using the indicated antibodies.

Proteomics and glycomics analysis

Label-free quantitative proteomics was performed on whole-cell lysates from CALR-KO and WT PK-15 cells. Protein digestion, LC-MS/MS analysis, and data processing were performed by Wuhan Gene Create Biological Engineering Co., Ltd. Differentially expressed proteins were defined by |log₂FC| ≥ 0.58 and adjusted P-value < 0.05. Glycomics profiling was conducted using HILIC-UPLC following PNGase F digestion and fluorescent labeling with procainamide, focusing on the detection of high-mannose and hybrid/complex-type N-glycans.

Chemical inhibitor treatments

To inhibit the N-glycosylation pathway, cells were treated with 1-DNJ (Abmole, M3923), tunicamycin (MCE, HY-A0098), NGI-1 (MCE, HY-117383), castanospermine (MCE, HY-N2022), or miglitol (MCE, HY-B0481) at indicated concentrations for 24 h. For autophagy pathway analysis, bafilomycin A1 (MCE, HY-100558) was used for 6 h before sample collection.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the R programming language. The means ± SD were determined for each treatment group in the separate experiments. Two-tailed Student’s t-test was used to determine significant differences between treatment and control groups (*P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001, ns, not significant).
