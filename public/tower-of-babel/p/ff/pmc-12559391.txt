Molecular and agronomic assessment of faba bean genotypes identifies resistance to Orobanche crenata infestation
Authors: Yu Wang, Yossry E Salama, Khaled E Amer, Waly F Elsayed, Salwa A Orabi, Ahmed H El-Sappah, Ehab M B Mahdy, Ehab M Zayed, Mohamed M A Elashtokhy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFaba bean (Vicia faba L.)(V. faba), an essential legume in Egypt, is severely impacted by broomrape (Orobanche crenata) (O. crenata), a parasitic weed that feeds on roots, making chemical control difficult without harming the crop. Three faba bean genotypes (Giza-843, Misr-3, and Sakha-3) along with nine pure lines were evaluated for resistance to broomrape in field trials (2021–2023) using gene barcoding (rbcL, rpoC1), ten Start Codon Targeted (SCoT) markers, and phonological and agronomic traits to identify resistance sources. Five faba bean genotypes—Giza 843, Lines 2, 7, 8, and 9—significantly reduced all broomrape growth and productivity traits. Line 5 had the lowest spike length, fresh and dry spike weight, number of spikes/m2, and number of capsules/spike across both seasons. Genotypes Line 5, Line 6, and Misr 3 exhibited the highest seed yield per plot and hectare under normal and broomrape-infested conditions. Line 4 showed the lowest yield reductions, followed by Lines 9 and 8. The study generated 103 amplicons with a polymorphism rate of 51.46%, with SCoT-10 being the most informative marker, revealing 69% polymorphism and affecting 13 amplicons. Three faba bean genotypes showed significant genetic diversity, high seed output, and effectively reduced broomrape growth, highlighting their potential for genetic improvement and sustainable use in future research. Introduction

Genetic improvement has offered tremendous scope and potential to conventional crop breeding methods, crop tolerance, and crop management1. Breeding against biotic stress, such as weeds, is vital in reducing crop losses. Local germplasm of a given crop plays a crucial role in the characterization and evaluation, germplasm identification, and genetic improvement. These could discriminate promising genotypes and set a proper key for the sustainable utilization2. Genetic improvement against biotic stresses, including weeds, pathogens, and herbivores, is based on evaluating local germplasm availability and identifying new genes1. Weeds like Broorape (Orobanche spp.) which compete with strategic crops ultimately affect the growth and yield of a crop3.

V. faba is one of Egypt’s most important legume crops, as its seed is used as a protein source (21 to 34%) for human and animal feeding and has valuable agronomic function because of its high capacity for N2 fixation, and a rich source of carbohydrates, dietary fiber, choline, lecithin, minerals4,5. Faba bean seeds are a rich source of carbohydrates, dietary fiber, choline, lecithin, and minerals6 and a low-fat, sodium- and cholesterol-free source7. The total harvested area of Faba bean in Egypt was 20,729 hectares, which produced about 190937.01 tons of dry seeds8. Broorape (Orobanche spp.) is one of the most root-holoparasitic faba bean species and causes lots of problems in soil and plant growth and development through depleting the chlorophyll and entirely depending on the host for nutritional requirements9 (http://ephytia.inra.fr/en/C/5193/Tomato-Orobanche-spp-broomrape). It causes ~ 5-100% yield losses under the dried and warmer conditions10. The long-term impact of the broomrapes becomes more serious, where broomrape seeds quickly spread to a wide area, as they are very light and can persist in soil for up to 20 years, causing a severe problem in faba bean cultivated areas10. The Orobanche seeds are spread by humans, agricultural tools, crop seeds, propagules, and animal manure11. Crenate broomrape (O. crenata) is the most damaging and widespread species infecting the Faba bean12. Seeds of these parasitic weeds may remain dormant in the soil for many years until the release of a chemical signal from a host plant stimulates germination. This provides the parasite with great genetic adaptability to environmental changes, including host resistance, agronomical practices, and herbicide treatments13.

Controlling broomrapes is a multi-faceted response in legumes, especially faba bean, as a strategic crop. Many defense mechanisms have been detected in plants resistant to broomrape attack, including cell wall reinforcement, production of toxic compounds14, and sealing vascular tissues15. There is wide diversity in the Egyptian Faba bean population’s resistance to broomrape, and both Giza-843 and Misr-1 cultivars are significant sources of broomrape resistance in the Egyptian faba bean population16–18.

Genetic improvement of faba beans against broomrape infestation must examine diversity in local genotypes. The approach of characterization and evaluation is a priority for genetic improvement. Characterizing the agronomic attributes is the first step in selecting promising genotypes. This approach is easy and cheap, but it consumes time. Molecular profiling determines the genetic variation and relationships among genotypes towards genetic improvement. Genetic improvement is based on the magnitude and nature of genetic diversity to get a wide range of gene recombination for quantitatively inherited traits. Complementary information between morphological characterization and molecular marker analysis plays a significant role in the genetic screening of germplasm, providing more resolving power of genetic diversity19,20. Recently, more sensitive DNA-based techniques, such as SCot markers, are most suitable due to their ease of handling, reproducibility, multi-allelic nature, codominant inheritance, relative abundance, and genome-wide coverage21. Polymorphism derived from the SCoT technique has recently arisen as a new and promising marker for assessing genetic diversity22 due to its extended primer sequences and improved reproducibility. The SCoT markers rely on the conserved region surrounding the translation initiation codon, ATG23, and do not require prior genomic sequence information. These markers can be used directly in marker-assisted breeding programs, unlike random markers, and can also be used to evaluate fruitfully genetic diversity, quantitative trait loci (QTL) mapping, and DNA fingerprinting in various crops. DNA barcoding has recently been traditionally preferred to sequencing plastid and chloroplast genes for plant identification or accurate verification24 due to its lower intramolecular recombination25. This can be utilized for plant classification in orthologous databases26. Many genes, such as rbcL, matK, rpoC1, psbA-trnH, and ITS, have been used for identifying and documenting plant diversity and have long been considered the standard for plant barcoding27.

This study evaluated some new Faba bean genotypes under broomrape infestation to determine the most tolerant genotypes that could improve the Faba bean genetically and sustain its future utilization.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Twelve Faba bean genotypes, including three local genotypes (Giza 843, Misr 3, and Sakha 3), as well as nine pure lines, were collected from the Food Legumes Research Department hybridization program at the Agricultural Research Center, Field Crops Research Institute - Food Legumes Research Department in Cairo, Egypt. The tested genotypes in Table S1 were evaluated under broomrape infestation in the experimental farm at the Faculty of Agriculture, Damanhur University, Egypt, and Etay Elbaroad Research Station, Food Legume Research Department, Field Crops Research Institute, Agricultural Research Center, Egypt, over two years, during 2021–2022 and 2022–2023 growing seasons. The field was known to be naturally heavily and uniformly infested with Orobanche spp. seeds. Names and pedigree of all tested genotypes are presented in Table S1. The experimental soil physical and chemical properties, as given in Table S2, were determined according to Jackson28.

Field experiment: The recent evidence regarding the transfer of host-derived non-nutrient solutes to plant parasites. We explored these implications for parasite biology and ecology. Faba bean seeds of all tested genotypes were sown on 28 October 2021 and 2022 in two experiments, each designed as a Randomized complete block with three replicates. The plot size was 12 m2. Each plot included five ridges, 60 cm apart, and 4 m long. Seeds of faba bean cultivars were sown on the two sides of ridges in hills at the rate of one seed per hill with 20 cm between hills. Seeds were planted in the infected field to evaluate faba bean genotypes that are intolerant to O. crenata infestation, as well as in clean soil free from broomrape infestation. This study’s broomrape-infested field had a lengthy history of O. crenata infestation and was not purposefully prepared or inoculated; instead, it was a naturally “sick” field. The soil is a major vector for this infestation because of the long-term persistence of broomrape seeds, which are a byproduct of the production of vulnerable host crops. When exposed to chemical stimulants (strigolactones and similar substances) released by the roots of a host plant, broomrape seeds can stay dormant in the soil for almost ten years before sprouting. Although infected seeds may also spread broomrape, our study found that the infection started in the soil seed bank. As a result, the field is a living example of the parasitic weed pressure that occurs in soils.

Broomrape measurements

After 125 days of faba bean sowing, 1 m2 from each plot was randomly chosen to estimate the number of broomrape spikes/m2, fresh spikes weight/m2 (g), and dry spikes weight/m2 (g). Also, ten random broomrape spikes from each plot at harvest were used to determine the number of capsules/broomrape spike and the length of the broomrape spike (cm). The average fresh weight of broomrape spikes in 1 m2 was measured directly after harvest. These fresh spikes were then air-dried for one week at room temperature and weighed again to determine the average dry weight/m2.

Faba bean measurements

Ten plants from each plot were measured for growth habits, including plant height (cm), number of branches/plant, and number of leaves/plant. Fresh leaves were used to estimate the total chlorophyll content (mg/100 g fresh weight) after 105 days of seed sowing, according to Moran and Porath29. The number of pods/plant, seeds/pod, and 100-seed weight (g) were measured at harvest. Seed yield/plot (kg) was recorded as the total seed weight of plants per plot. Seed yield/hectare (ton) was estimated by converting the yield in plot (kg) to hectares.

DNA extraction

DNA was extracted from 100 g of fresh leaves using the manufacturer’s protocol for the ZR Plant/Seed DNA DNeasy Plant MiniPrep™ Kit (www.zymoresearch.com) and then quantified with a NanoDrop Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.)30. All samples were adjusted to a concentration of 10 ng/µl for subsequent analyses31,32.

Gel electrophoresis and polymerase chain reaction (PCR)

Template DNA, primers, deoxyribonucleotide triphosphates (dNTPs), MgCl₂, PCR buffer, and GoTaq DNA polymerase (Promega) were used in reaction quantities ranging from 25 to 50 µL, as directed by the manufacturer. In a Perkin-Elmer/GeneAmp® PCR System 9700, the thermal cycling procedure began with denaturation at 94 °C, continued with 35–40 cycles of denaturation, primer annealing, and extension, and ended with an elongation step at 72 °C.

1.5% agarose gels, prepared in 1X TBE buffer with 0.5 µg/mL ethidium bromide, were used for separating PCR products. A 100 bp DNA ladder was utilised to determine the molecular size. A Gel Documentation System (Bio-Rad®) was used to photograph and observe the gels under ultraviolet light33.

Scot markers for start codons

Ten SCoT primers produced by Operon Biotechnologies of Cologne, Germany, were used to amplify twelve different faba bean genotypes (Table S3), as outlined by Collard and Mackill22. The electrophoresis and PCR reaction components followed the guidelines outlined before.

DNA barcoding technology

As per the protocol outlined by Ateto, et al.34, two chloroplast areas, rbcL and rpoC1 (Table S4), were amplified and sequenced. With an annealing step at 50 °C, the PCR reaction followed the basic protocol mentioned before regarding composition and cycling. I used the EZ-10 Spin Column Purification Kit to purify the amplified products. Then, I sequenced them using an ABI PRISM 3730XL Analyser (Applied Biosystems) with BigDyeTM Terminator Cycle Sequencing Kits. MORSI, et al.35 was followed for data analysis and sequencing.

Data analysis

The obtained data were subjected to analysis of variance according to Gomez36. LSD was used to compare treatment means at a 5% level of probability. Clear amplicons were selected for statistical analysis. Only polymorphic amplicons were scored as present (1) in a data matrix. Bands without apparent polymorphisms or only polymorphic in one replicate were scored as absent (0). The number of total bands, unique bands, polymorphic bands, and the percent of the total bands that were polymorphic or specific were calculated. Some genetic parameters were estimated such as Shannon’s information index (I) = -1 × (p × Ln (p) + q × Ln(q)), effective number of alleles (Ne) = 1 / (p2 + q2), diversity (h) = 1 ÷ (p2 + q2), and = Unbiased Diversity (uh) = (N ÷ (N − 1)) × h); Where p = Band Freq. and q = 1 - p. The cluster analysis was constructed using the unweighted pair-group method with arithmetic averages (UPGMA) to generate a dendrogram for studying the relationships among germplasm populations, as described by Nei and Li37. A binary matrix was then transformed into a genetic similarity (GS) matrix using Jaccard’s coefficient. Jaccard38. The sequences were analyzed using the BLAST program (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/BLAST). Sequences were aligned using Align Sequences Nucleotide BLAST. Data sets were fed into GenAlEx (version 6.5) – Genetic Analysis in Excel39 Program and SPSS (version 14.0).

Results

Effect of Faba bean genotypes on Broomrape growth and productivity during the 2021/2022 and 2022/2023 seasons

The presented data in (Fig. 1; Table S5) confirmed that broomrape growth and productivity were significantly affected by faba bean genotypes in both seasons. The five faba bean genotypes Giza 843, Misr 3, Line 4, Line 5, and Line 6 significantly reduced all broomrape growth and productivity traits. The parasitic broomrape on faba bean genotypes Line 5 had the lowest spike length (31.97 and 32.61 cm), fresh spike weight (37.18 and 36.06 g), dry spike weight (22.53 and 21.41 g), number of spike/m2 (5.16 and 5.11) and number of capsules/ spike (16.19 and 16.51) in both seasons of the study, respectively. The two faba bean genotypes, Line 6 and Misr 3, ranked second and third among all evaluated faba bean genotypes in this study in reducing the growth and productivity of broomrape in both seasons. In contrast, the three faba bean genotypes, Misr 3, Giza 843, and Sakha 3, were the most sensitive to broomrape infestation, resulting in the lowest reduction in broomrape growth and productivity in both seasons. Broomrape that parasitoid on faba bean genotypes Misr 3 recorded the highest spike length (91.81 and 93.65 cm), fresh spike weight (194.70 and 188.86 g), dry spike weight (118.01 and 112.11 g), number of spike/m2 (27.00 and 27.28) and number of capsules/ spike (84.79 and 86.49) in the first and second seasons, respectively. Conversely, the four faba bean genotypes, Lines 1, 4, and 7, showed a moderate reduction in all broomrape growth and productivity in both seasons.

The reduction in broomrape growth and yield under different faba bean genotypes may be due to the resistance of faba bean genotypes to O. crenata can occur at various levels of the parasite life cycle as (1) developing lower host root density, (2) reduced attachment of O. crenata on host roots, and (3) delayed development of infection events on host roots. Furthermore, the low induction of O. crenata seed germination could not be taken as an indicator of resistance40.

Growth traits

Data shown in Figure S1 and Table 1 revealed a significant difference among all tested faba bean genotypes in their growth traits in both seasons. The six faba genotypes, Giza 843, Misr 3, Line 1, Line 4, Line 5, and Line 6, were the most tolerant to broomrape infestation, showing the lowest reduction percentages in all growth traits. The lowest reduction in plant height was recorded in Line 6 (6.62% and 6.16%), followed by Line 4 (6.63% and 6.23%), then Line 5 (6.63% and 6.28%), and Giza 843 (6.62% and 6.30%) in both seasons, respectively. The undesirable reductions in plant height were observed in the tallest plants of lines 10, 11, and 12 under both infected and uninfected conditions in both seasons. On the other hand, the highest reduction in plant height was observed in Sakha 3 (56.27 and 56.62%) in the first and second seasons, respectively. Concerning the branch number/plant, Line 5 had the highest number of branches (4.33 and 3.23) under normal conditions. These values decreased to 2.50 and 2.33 under broomrape infestation conditions, with 42.26% and 27.86% reduction percentages in the two seasons, respectively.

Yield and yield component traits

The results in Figure S2 and Table 2 showed significant variation among all genotypes in all yield traits and their components in both seasons. The genotypes of Lines 3, Lines 4, Lines 7, Lines 10, Lines 11, and Lines 12 showed excellent tolerance to broomrape, as they gave the highest number of pods, number of seeds/pod, 100-seeds weight, and seed yield per plot and hectare in both seasons under the normal and broomrape conditions. Line 1 showed the lowest desirable reduction ratios resulting from broomrape infection for the number of pods/plant (− 39.47% and 8.78%), followed by Line 4 (1.64% and 23.53%), and then Line 6 (1.84% and 23.64% in both seasons, respectively). The increased number of pods/plant for Line 1 under broomrape infection, as indicated by the negative reduction ratio (-39.47%), suggests a possible compensatory mechanism. This response may involve redirecting plant resources towards reproductive growth as a survival strategy, an essential trait for maintaining yield under stress. This behavior highlights a potential escape or tolerance mechanism rather than true resistance41. On the other hand, Sakha 3 showed the highest reduction percentage in the number of pods/plants caused by broomrape (59.72% and 64.85%) compared to all other genotypes in both seasons. The results showed that while some genotypes experienced a general decline in growth and yield parameters, specific lines, such as Misr 3, exhibited superior performance by effectively mitigating the adverse effects of broomrape infestation. This suggests that the genetic makeup of these lines provides a basis for resistance, resulting in a more stable yield even under stressful conditions. This observation is supported by the data in Table S2, which demonstrates that genotypes with specific genetic traits could maintain higher biomass and yield components despite the presence of the parasite.

Regarding the number of seeds/pods, the obtained data revealed that Line 4 recorded the lowest desirable reduction ratios for the number of seeds/plant (1.97 and − 25.89%), followed by Line 1 (1.99 and − 13.11%), then Line 5 (2.14 and − 15.79%) and Line 6 (1.97 and − 7.23%) in both seasons, respectively. On the other hand, Line 2 showed the highest undesirable reduction percentage for the number of seeds/pods caused by broomrape (37.32% and 70.18%) compared to other genotypes in both seasons. The superior performance of genotypes such as Line 7, 4, 8, and 9 in terms of seeds/pods under infected conditions can be attributed to specific genetic characteristics that confer higher tolerance or resistance to broomrape. This is further supported by our molecular analysis (as detailed in Tables S6 and S7), where these genotypes exhibited unique genetic markers potentially linked to mechanisms that either inhibit broomrape attachment or reduce its negative impact on plant vigor and yield components42.

The 100-seed weight data presented in Figure S2 indicated that Line 5 had the lowest desirable reduction ratios for the 100-seed weight (1.96 and 1.95%), followed by Giza 843 and Line 6 with the exact reduction percentages (1.96 and 1.96%), then Line 4 (1.97 and 1.97%) in both seasons, respectively. Discussion

Parasitic plants are essential to many terrestrial ecosystems and majorly impact human agriculture. The harmful species, primarily belonging to four genera of parasitic plants (Striga, Orobanche, Philobanche, and Cascuta), infest farmers in many parts of the world, pose severe threats to subsistence agriculture in developing regions, and cause annual economic losses estimated at several billion dollars for cereal and vegetable production. Reduced crop yields - and, in some cases, complete crop loss - result directly from the extraction of water and nutrients by parasites from cultivated host plants. Consequently, the mechanisms of parasite resource extraction and their effects on host plants have been the subject of considerable research.

However, the biology and ecology of parasitic plants remain poorly studied compared to other plant groups. In contrast, relatively little work has explored the bottom-up influence of host-derived factors on plant parasites and their subsequent interactions with other organisms. Most previous research in this area has focused on nutrient transfer, which, in addition to supporting parasite growth, can affect access to resources (e.g., carbon and nitrogen) for other community members – including herbivores, pollinators, decomposers, and other plants. Thus, plant parasites can significantly influence ecosystem dynamics through effects on nutrient cycling. However, a variety of biologically active non-nutrient solutes are also likely to be transferred from host plants to parasites, including plant hormones, proteins, and RNAs that can have broad effects on plant growth and small molecules and proteins involved in host plant defence that may also influence subsequent interactions of the parasite with other organisms. Thus, diverse aspects of host plant biochemistry are likely to affect the physiology and ecology of plant parasites, with implications for broader community dynamics and potentially for managing parasitic weeds – although our current understanding of such effects is limited. Our findings suggest that parasitic plants receive non-nutrient solutes from their hosts, which could influence parasite physiology, alter the interaction between hosts and parasites, and decrease production when infestation occurs.

Based on the low infection level and minimal impact on yield and vegetative growth, some faba bean genotypes were identified as promising sources of resistance to O. crenata, which could be introduced into the faba bean breeding program43. In the Egyptian faba bean population, Gadalla, et al.44 found that control plants (without Orobanche infection) and infected plants (with or without Orobanche attachment) demonstrated that Giza 3 was the susceptible cultivar and Giza 843 was tolerant, with the lowest number of Orobanche tubercles per plant. Also, Eid, et al.45,46 found wide diversity among the Egyptian faba bean genotypes in reducing broomrape growth and yield. They also indicated that Giza 843 and Misr 3 recorded the lowest values of O. crenata m- 2 and the highest values of yield and its attributes compared to Giza-2 and Sakha 3 varieties.

Previous findings align with those of45,46, Soliman, et al.47, who found wide diversity among Egyptian faba bean genotypes in terms of growth and yield under normal and broomrape infestation conditions. They also indicated that Giza 843 and Giza 716 had the highest yields and attributes compared to the Giza-2 and Sakha 3 varieties. In contrast, Abu-Amer, et al.48 observed that a considerable variation among faba bean cultivars was recorded for all the studied characters. Misr 1 cultivar showed the highest values for all the studied characters; however, the differences between Misr 1 and Giza 843 were insignificant for seed yield (ard. /fed) in the second season and for biological yield in both seasons.

Yield is the most essential and complex trait in crops. It reflects the interaction of the environment with all the growth and development processes that occur throughout the life cycle. Crop yield is directly and multiply determined by the traits of the yield component (such as seed weight and seed number). Our results are similar to those reported by45,46,], Soliman, et al.47, who found wide diversity among Egyptian faba bean genotypes in terms of growth and yield under normal and broomrape-infested conditions. They also indicated that Giza 843 and Giza 716 had the highest yields and attributes compared to the Giza-2 and Sakha 3 varieties. In contrast, Abu-Amer, et al.48 observed that a considerable variation among faba bean cultivars was recorded for all the studied characters. Misr 1 cultivar detected the highest values for all the studied characters; however, the differences between Misr 1 and Giza 843 were insignificant for seed yield (ard./fed) in the second season and for biological yield in both seasons.

The statistical strength of the relationship between number of spike/m2 of broomrape and the reduction in various faba bean yield and yield components traits (R²>0.5 in all cases, most exceeding 0.85), extremely significant p-values (p ≤ 0.008), and biological plausibility combine to prove that broomrape spike density is a primary determinant of yield reduction in faba bean, primarily through its impact on pod number. This robust cause-and-effect link provides a solid foundation for targeting broomrape management to protect yield components critical for crop productivity. Broomrape is an obligate parasitic weed that attaches to host plant roots, extracting water, nutrients, and photosynthates. Its presence severely impairs the physiological processes of the host, notably reducing nutrient acquisition, photosynthetic efficiency, and the allocation of assimilates to reproductive organs. This leads directly to reductions in pod set, seed formation, and seed filling, as observed in the current study. A similar physiological chain of events has been previously documented: Ridwan, et al.49 reported that broomrape infection resulted in significant reductions in biomass accumulation and seed yield in faba beans due to decreased nutrient and carbohydrate flow to the pods. Similarly, Fernández-Aparicio, et al.50 explained that the parasitic action of broomrape limits host growth and reproductive success by depleting resources critical for grain development. Previous research corroborates this direct physiological link. Pérez-de-Luque, et al.51 confirmed that broomrape competition interferes with nitrogen and mineral uptake in legumes, resulting in significant yield penalties. Additionally, Chachalis, et al.3 found that broodmapre infestation leads to yield reductions that closely track the severity of parasite establishment, supporting the use of broomrape incidence as an early-stage predictor of final yield.

High genetic diversity positively affects an agroecosystem’s resilience and function, while also facilitating the selection of promising genotypes and the genetic improvement of the crop. The adequate number of alleles (Ne) is the reciprocal of gene homozygosity. When h is high, Ne will increase52. Unique attributes of ‘markers’ discriminatory power have been used in various studies using the primer combination in genetic diversity studies19. Different methodologies, including DNA-based markers and DNA barcoding, remain the first step in describing and evaluating germplasm, and are helpful tools for revealing their genetic makeup2. Description-based markers have proven to be valuable tools in assessing genetic variation19,53. Genetic distance refers to the genetic divergence between species or populations within a species. Smaller genetic distances indicate that populations have more similar genes, suggesting they are more closely related, have a recent common ancestor, or have had a recent mating event; these data (Table 3) could help determine the desirable genotypes for genetic improvement. Our results correspond to those of Mahdy and Rizk20 and El-Taher54.

This allows for effective sequencing, even though many authors disagree with sequences longer than 1000 bp due to difficulties in bi-directional sequencing55. Mohamed, et al.56 concluded that the rbcL (600 bp) is less flexible than matK (900 bp) on local barley cultivars. Many studies have revealed that changes in the plastid genome occur during the evolutionary process57,58. Those studies have supported the entire rbcL gene with other barcodes59. The rbcL gene has been utilized in multiple disciplines, including phylogenetics and evolution60,61.

The Consortium for the Barcode of Life (CBOL) has established that the rbcL gene, a standard plant DNA barcoding marker, is essential for species identification and optimal sequential efficiency57,62. These gene pools enrich genetic diversity due to their adaptability and genetic structure. The background on genetic structure and parameters provides a basis for understanding genetic improvement, diversity, relationships, and sustainable utilisation. Genetic variation helps determine the relationship between germplasm and the possibility of evolutionary change over time in the area. The molecular marker used when it was first selected must be considered critically. Both markers have great potential for screening between genotypes and assessing the polymorphism level. The results indicated that both DNA-based techniques could be used to assay genetic diversity. Consequently, we could construct unique DNA profiles of each genotype for future use. Besides the ability of DNA barcoding to discriminate among germplasm, both markers amplified four promising genotypes initially identified based on morphological and molecular profiling. The profiling of DNA barcoding provides precise authentication of given germplasm due to its greater constancy and non-influence on environmental changes2,55. Therefore, we registered and banked four promising genotypes at NCBI as reference data. DNA barcoding provides proper authentication, which is particularly useful for breeding purposes2. Genetic distance to the closest genotypes decreases when evaluation occurs in different geographical sites and germplasm63.

The present study has shown a broad genetic base in local Faba bean germplasm and recommends the review of genotypes through molecular markers. This study would support the application of molecular fingerprinting for sustainable utilisation, rectification, purification, and germplasm identification. This indicates that the Vicia species exhibits a broad genetic diversity and is distributed across various agroclimatic regions.

Conclusion

Noteworthy variances in phenological attributes showed a high level of genetic diversity in Faba Bean genotypes, which could be sustainably utilized in future genetic improvement. Moreover, the high polymorphism obtained by PCR-based techniques proves the discriminatory power of those markers in identification and diversity analyses. The rbcL gene barcode plays a significant role in differentiating between a broad sense of closely related germplasm. Combining phenological and genetic parameters would be an inclusive approach for screening Egyptian germplasm. Our findings suggest genetic improvement should focus on these potential traits and genotypes. Also, consider sowing three faba beans. Four faba bean lines, viz., Sakha 3, Line 2, Line 4, and Line 9, are promising genotypes against broomrape infestation. They should be included in the production map of broomrape-tolerant genotypes in research initiatives to boost seed output in Egypt.
