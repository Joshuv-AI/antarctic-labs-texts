ABSTRACT
Key Points•METTL1, a tRNA m7G writer, promotes LSC homeostasis and leukemogenesis by activating tRNAPheGAA/HCK/CXCR4 signaling.•Pharmacologically targeting METTL1/m7G axis with a small-molecule inhibitor eliminates LSCs and myeloid leukemia.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a fatal form of hematopoietic malignancy.1, 2, 3 Leukemia stem cells (LSCs) are a subpopulation of AML cells with unlimited self-renewal ability and are responsible for AML development, relapse, and chemoresistance.4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 Thus, eradicating LSCs is a critically important strategy to cure AML.11 LSCs reside within bone marrow (BM) niches that play a pivotal role in sustaining LSC homeostasis and self-renewal.10, 11, 12 These BM niches are characterized by high expression of C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 12 (CXCL12).13 Interaction between CXCL12 produced by BM stromal cells and the CXCR4 receptor on AML blasts anchors LSCs within the BM microenvironment.14, 15, 16 Hematopoietic cell kinase (HCK), an Src family tyrosine kinase, serves as a direct effector of G proteins.17, 18, 19 It is predominantly expressed within hematopoietic cells and has been strongly implicated in AML.20, 21, 22, 23, 24 As a G protein–coupled receptor, CXCR4 activates Src tyrosine kinases including HCK, via the G protein α subunit,25, 26, 27 thereby controlling LSC homing to and retention within the BM niche.14,15

RNA modifications constitute a fundamental layer of posttranscriptional gene regulation. Dysregulation of RNA modifications is increasingly recognized as critical driver of leukemogenesis. Among these, RNA N6-methyladenosine (m6A) has been extensively involved in AML pathogenesis, LSC maintenance, and microenvironment remodeling.28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33 Importantly, pharmacological targeting m6A regulators has demonstrated promising antileukemia efficacy.34, 35, 36, 37 Despite these advances, >170 different RNA chemical modifications have been identified,38, 39, 40, 41, 42 yet the biological significance of most remains largely unexplored in leukemia. N7-methylguanosine (m7G) is one of the most abundant modifications on transfer RNAs (tRNAs),43, 44, 45 but its function in leukemia, especially LSC biology, remains poorly understood. Methyltransferase-like protein 1 (METTL1), in complex with the scaffolding protein WD repeat domain 4 (WDR4), catalyzes m7G formation on certain tRNAs.46,47 METTL1 has been broadly implicated in the pathogenesis of multiple cancer types43,48, 49, 50, 51, 52, yet its role in LSC homeostasis and leukemia–microenvironment has not been systematically investigated. Although we and others identified candidate small-molecule METTL1 inhibitors,53, 54, 55 their in vivo antitumor efficacy or bioavailability remain largely uncharacterized. This underscores a critical gap in developing effective METTL1-targeted therapies.

In this study, we systematically interrogated all currently known RNA modification regulators and identified METTL1 as the most clinically relevant regulator in AML and LSCs. Mechanistically, METTL1 loss significantly reduces the m7G abundance and overall levels of tRNAPheGAA, thereby suppressing translation of transcripts enriched in tRNAPheGAA-dependent codons, including HCK. Reduced HCK expression disrupts CXCR4 signaling, thereby impairing LSC homing to the BM niche and self-renewal. Furthermore, we identified a selective and potent METTL1 inhibitor that effectively eliminates LSCs and AML. Together, our findings uncover a previously unappreciated epitranscriptomic mechanism mediated by the METTL1/tRNAPheGAA/HCK pathway governing LSC–BM niche interactions and provide compelling proof-of-concept evidence that METTL1 is a therapeutically actionable and druggable target in cancer.

Materials and methods

Primary patients with AML and healthy donor specimens

Samples from patients with AML (supplemental Table 1, available on the Blood website) were obtained through BM aspiration at the time of diagnosis or relapse, following written informed consent, from City of Hope and Cincinnati Children’s Hospital under institutional review board–approved protocols. Erythrocyte lysis was performed on all samples, and mononuclear cells were cryopreserved at −150°C for subsequent studies.

Animals

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (numbers 22066 and 22068) at City of Hope. The animal strains used were NSG-SGM3 (NSGS; The Jackson Laboratory; RRID: IMSR_JAX:013062), NOD-scid IL2Rgammanull (NSG; The Jackson Laboratory; RRID: IMSR_JAX:005557), Mettl1 conditional knockout (cKO) (Mettl1fl/+ and Mettl1fl/fl; genetic background: C57BL/6J [CD45.2]; Cyagen), Mx1-cre (The Jackson Laboratory; RRID: IMSR_JAX:003556), and NCI B6-Ly5.1 (Charles River Laboratories; RRID: IMSR_CRL:564).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10. Data normality was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data were analyzed using Student t tests, and log-rank tests were performed for Kaplan-Meier survival curves. P value <.05 represented a significant difference.

Results

METTL1 is an essential oncogene for AML development and LSC maintenance

Thus far, 155 proteins responsible for 39 RNA modifications, acting as writers, erasers, or readers, have been characterized38, 39, 40, 41, 42,56 (supplemental Table 2). To investigate whether these RNA modifications are implicated in AML, we analyzed their CRISPR scores (supplemental Table 3; data are available for 152 of the 155 genes) in a genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 screen data set57 and compared their dependencies in AML cells with non-AML cancer cells. Of the 19 genes with significant differences (adj P < .05), 8 were critical for AML cell survival (CRISPR score < −0.557) (Figure 1A), and 13 had lower CRISPR scores in AML cell lines than in non-AML cancer cells (Figure 1B). In addition, 74 of the 155 genes are expressed at significantly higher levels in LSC+ populations than in LSC– populations in patients with AML58 (Figure 1C). Among the 3 overlapping genes (Figure 1D) that are selectively essential for AML survival (Figure 1E) and highly expressed in LSCs (Figure 1F), METTL1 demonstrated the highest clinical relevance in AML. Specifically, METTL1 is highly expressed in patients with AML (Figure 1G), with elevated expression significantly correlated with poor prognosis (supplemental Figure 1A-B). To evaluate the in vivo role of METTL1 in leukemogenesis, we established primary and secondary BM transplantation (BMT), human-in-mouse xenograft, and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) AML models. For primary BMT model, we generated Mettl1 cKO model by crossing Mettl1fl/fl with Mx1-Cre mice (supplemental Figure 2A-B). Lineage negative (Lin–) progenitors from Mettl1 cKO mice were virally transduced with MLL-AF9 and transplanted into immunocompetent mice. Poly (I:C)-induced Mettl1 knockout (KO) dramatically suppressed AML initiation, reduced AML infiltration in recipients (supplemental Figure 2C-D), and extended survival (Figure 1H). We next isolated leukemic blasts from phosphate-buffered saline-treated Mettl1fl/fl mice that developed full-blown AML for secondary BMT, revealing that Mettl1 KO significantly delayed AML progression and prolonged survival (Figure 1I). Importantly, secondary BMT-based in vivo limiting dilution assay demonstrated that Mettl1 loss significantly reduced LSC frequency (Figure 1J).

Regarding xenograft model, firefly luciferase-labeled MA9.3RAS cells (MLL-AF9 and NRASG12D 59) were transplanted into immunodeficient NSGS mice. METTL1 KO markedly reduced AML burden and prolonged survival (Figure 1K). Flow cytometry and complete blood count analysis confirmed the reduced leukemia burden in METTL1 KO group (supplemental Figure 2E,G). Wright-Giemsa staining revealed a notable reduction of AML cells in peripheral blood following METTL1 KO; hematoxylin and eosin staining demonstrated that METTL1 KO reduced infiltration of AML into spleen and liver (supplemental Figure 2F). In addition, METTL1 KO significantly mitigated AML-induced splenomegaly (supplemental Figure 2H). Finally, using an AML PDX model, we consolidated that METTL1 KO significantly suppresses AML proliferation in vivo (supplemental Figure 2I), reduces leukemia burden, and prolongs overall survival (Figure 1L). Altogether, these data indicate that METTL1 represents a unique vulnerability in AML, and plays a crucial role in driving leukemogenesis and maintaining LSC frequency in vivo.

The enzymatic activity of METTL1 is needed for LSC homing and self-renewal

Given that AML development is closely linked to LSC persistence,11 and that METTL1 is highly expressed in LSCs, we next investigated the roles of METTL1 in LSC homeostasis. Using our Mettl1 cKO mice, we demonstrated that Mettl1 KO dramatically inhibited MLL-AF9-, AE9a (AML1-ETO9a)–, and FLT3ITD-driven leukemic transformation and LSC colony-forming ability (Figure 2A-B). CRISPR/Cas9-mediated Mettl1 KO (supplemental Figure 3A-B) confirmed that Mettl1 loss reduced MLL-AF9 LSC colony-forming potential (supplemental Figure 3C). Consistently, Mettl1 knockdown (KD) in FLT3ITD- and AE9a-driven AML blasts (supplemental Figure 3D) led to substantial reductions in LSC colony-forming capacity (supplemental Figure 3E-F). We then knocked out endogenous METTL1 in an AML PDX sample (no. 0148 with DNMT3 and IDH2 mutations), and overexpressed wild-type (WT) METTL1 and a catalytically inactive (CI) mutant (amino acids 107-109 EIR/AAA; reported by us60 and others61) (Figure 2C-D). METTL1 KO significantly decreased LSC frequency, which could be rescued by WT but not CI METTL1 (Figure 2E-F), indicating METTL1’s methyltransferase activity is important to its function in maintaining LSC frequency. Such function were further confirmed in MA9.3RAS AML cells (supplemental Figure 3G). Considering that LSCs reside within the BM niche and their homing and retention in this microenvironment is critical for maintaining their self-renewal capacity in vivo,10 we next explored whether METTL1 contributes to LSC homing. Using an in vitro coculture system (Figure 2G), we found that Mettl1 loss significantly impaired the migration of murine AML blasts toward BM stroma (Figure 2H). This finding was validated in 2 PDX models (Figure 2I). To further assess whether METTL1-mediated LSC self-renewal is driven by enhanced homing in vivo, we employed flow cytometry and in situ confocal immunofluorescence imaging (Figure 2J). Flow cytometry showed that Mettl1/METTL1 loss significantly inhibited the homing of AML blasts in vivo (Figure 2K). In situ bone imaging revealed substantially fewer METTL1-deficient AML blasts migrating into the osteopontin+ (OPN+) BM stromal cells compared with controls (Figure 2L).

Other in vitro functional studies in different AML cells underscore the crucial role of METTL1 in leukemia. Specifically, in MA9.3RAS AML cells (supplemental Figure 4A-B), METTL1 KO significantly suppressed cell growth (supplemental Figure 4C) and induced cell cycle arrest (supplemental Figure 4D), which could be fully rescued by METTL1-WT, but not METTL1-CI. The role of METTL1 in cell cycle arrest was further validated in AML PDX cells (supplemental Figure 4E). Consistently, in Mono-mac-6 (supplemental Figure 4F-G) and U-937 (supplemental Figure 4J-K), METTL1 promoted AML growth (supplemental Figure 4H,L) and suppressed apoptosis (supplemental Figure 4I,M) depending on its enzymatic activity.

Finally, to understand the mechanism underlying high METTL1 expression in AML (supplemental Figure 5A), we examined its upstream regulation, and observed high levels of the active histone mark H3K4me3 at its promoter region (supplemental Figure 5B). Further unbiased analyses revealed that, among all H3K4 methyltransferases, KMT2B and KMT2F exhibit a significant and positive correlation with METTL1 across samples from patients with AML (supplemental Figure 5C). In addition, similar to METTL1, KMT2B but not KMT2F was notably upregulated at both mRNA and protein levels in AML (supplemental Figure 5D-E). KMT2B KD decreased METTL1 expression at both mRNA and protein levels (supplemental Figure 5F-G), accompanied by a reduction in H3K4me3 at the METTL1 promoter region (supplemental Figure 5H) and a decrease in tRNA m7G modification (supplemental Figure 5I). Therefore, high METTL1 expression in AML is at least partially driven by KMT2B-mediated H3K4me3 modification (supplemental Figure 5J).

In summary, our data show that METTL1 facilitates LSC homing into BM niche and enhances LSC self-renewal. Moreover, METTL1 enzymatic activity is essential for maintaining LSC frequency and AML cell survival.

Mettl1 loss does not functionally impair normal hematopoiesis

To assess whether METTL1 could serve as a specific vulnerability in AML, we examined its role in normal hematopoiesis using Mettl1 cKO mice (Figure 3A-B). Heterozygous or homozygous Mettl1 KO did not significantly affect body weight (supplemental Figure 6A). Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) indicated that homozygous Mettl1 KO somewhat reduced hematopoietic stem cell frequency, but this did not result marked changes in representation of downstream hematopoietic cell populations (Figure 3C-D). Complete blood count analysis revealed that Mettl1 KO did not significantly affect white blood cells, lymphocytes, red blood cells, and hemoglobin levels, but platelet numbers were reduced (Figure 3E). Flow cytometry analysis of major hematopoietic lineages, including T lymphoid, B lymphoid, myeloid, and erythroid cells, revealed no significant changes in BM populations (Figure 3F; supplemental Figure 6B). In the spleen, homozygous Mettl1 KO led to a moderate but significant decrease in the myeloid population and a slight increase in the erythroid population (Figure 3G). We further evaluated BM progenitors, including Lin–, LK (Lin–c-Kit+Sca-1–), and LSK (Lin–c-Kit+Sca-1+) cells (supplemental Figure 6C). Homozygous Mettl1 KO loss caused a slight but significant decrease in the Lin– population, whereas neither heterozygous nor homozygous KO significantly influenced LK or LSK populations (Figure 3H). Colony-forming assays indicated that, unlike the significant reduction seen in MLL-AF9 LSCs, homozygous Mettl1 KO showed little impact on normal hematopoietic stem cell self-renewal (Figure 3I-J). Hematoxylin and eosin staining showed that homozygous Mettl1 KO had no discernible impact on organ structure (supplemental Figure 6D). The METTL1/m7G-tRNAPheGAA axis promotes HCK translation and expression

To elucidate critical mechanisms by which METTL1 promotes LSC self-renewal, we first compared m7G-modified tRNA levels between CD34+ LSCs and CD34– bulk AML cells, as well as their steady-state abundance following METTL1 depletion. Among the m7G-modified tRNAs significantly reduced upon METTL1 KO, tRNAPheGAA, tRNAAlaTGC, tRNALysCTT, tRNALysTTT, and tRNAValTAC were markedly upregulated in CD34+ LSCs (Figure 4A-B). Transcriptome-wide RNA-seq showed that METTL1 KO led to substantially more downregulated than upregulated transcripts in both Mono-mac-6 and U-937 cells (Figure 4C; supplemental Figure 7A). Consistent with a reduction in the majority of m7G-modified tRNAs upon METTL1 loss (Figure 4B), surface sensing of translation (SUnSET) assay63 and polysome profiling revealed that METTL1 KO markedly suppressed mRNA translation efficiency (supplemental Figure 7B-D). Because tRNA depletion is known to induce ribosome stalling and collisions, key triggers in the no-go decay (NGD) mRNA surveillance pathway,43,64, 65, 66, 67 we hypothesized that the METTL1 KO-induced reduction of m7G-modified tRNA leads to ribosome collision and mRNA NGD, thereby inducing global mRNA downregulation observed by RNA-seq. To test this hypothesis, we monitored ribosome collisions by RNase-digested ribosome profiling68 and observed a significant increase in disome accumulation upon METTL1 loss, indicating enhanced ribosome collisions (Figure 4E; supplemental Figure 7E-F). Furthermore, to examine the impact of METTL1-mediated tRNA m7G modification on mRNA fate, we engineered firefly luciferase (F-Luc) reporter mRNAs containing either 10× m7G-tRNA-dependent codons (TTC, decoded by tRNAPheGAA) or 10× non–m7G-dependent codons (ACT or GGA). tRNAPheGAA was selected because, among the m7G-tRNAs significantly upregulated in LSCs, it is the most markedly reduced upon METTL1 loss in AML. METTL1 loss selectively decreased both steady-state levels (supplemental Figure 7G) and stability (Figure 4F) of F-Luc mRNAs enriched in m7G-tRNA-dependent codons. These results suggest that METTL1 loss reduces m7G-modified tRNAs, such as tRNAPheGAA, which not only suppresses the translation of those mRNAs with a high frequency of these codons, but also triggers the NGD pathway.

Gene set enrichment analysis revealed that METTL1 KO consistently suppressed pathways associated with cytokine signaling, inflammatory responses, and mTORC1 signaling (supplemental Figure 8A-B), with cytokine mediated signaling standing out as the most significantly affected pathway in both Mono-mac-6 and U-937 cells (Figure 4G; supplemental Figure 8C-E). Among the core-enriched genes within cytokine-mediated signaling (supplemental Figure 8F), HCK emerged as the most promising candidate because (1) its mRNA and protein exhibited the most pronounced reduction following METTL1 KO (supplemental Figure 8G-H), (2) it is highly expressed in patients with AML (supplemental Figure 8I), and (3) its high expression is significantly associated with poor prognosis in patients with AML (supplemental Figure 8J). Polysome profiling analysis demonstrated that METTL1 KO significantly attenuated HCK mRNA translation in AML (Figure 4H; supplemental Figure 9A). In addition, METTL1 KO induced HCK mRNA decay (Figure 4I), resulting in decreased steady-state levels (supplemental Figure 9B). Western blotting confirmed that METTL1 KO substantially reduced HCK protein levels in an enzymatic activity-dependent manner (Figure 4J).

We next explored how METTL1 promotes HCK expression through an m7G-dependent mechanism. Cross-linking immunoprecipitation followed by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (CLIP-qPCR) did not identify a robust interaction between METTL1 and HCK mRNA (supplemental Figure 9C) and our prior study60 did not recognize HCK as a high-confidence m7G-modified mRNA, indicating that METTL1 does not directly catalyze internal m7G formation in HCK mRNA. We then evaluated whether METTL1 indirectly regulates HCK expression through m7G-modified tRNAs and the NGD mechanism. The translation of 11 amino acids encoded by HCK mRNA completely depends on m7G-modified tRNAs (supplemental Figure 9D). For most of these m7G-modified tRNAs, their levels were decreased following METTL1 KO in AML (Figure 4B), with tRNAiMetCAT, tRNAMetCAT, and tRNAPheGAA showing the most significant decrease (Figure 4K; supplemental Figure 9E). m7G methylated RNA immunoprecipitation (MeRIP) followed by northern blotting (Figure 4L) and qPCR (supplemental Figure 9F-G) demonstrated that METTL1 directly deposits m7G modification in tRNAiMetCAT, tRNAMetCAT, and tRNAPheGAA. Compared with tRNAiMetCAT and tRNAMetCAT, METTL1 showed significantly higher enzymatic activity toward tRNAPheGAA (Figure 4M). Using NaBH4/aniline cleavage assays (Figure 4N), we confirmed the presence of m7G modification in tRNAPheGAA and showed that this is METTL1-dependent. To further investigate whether tRNAPheGAA is involved in regulating HCK expression, we conducted both loss- and gain-of-function studies. KD of tRNAPheGAA markedly inhibited mRNA translation (supplemental Figure 9H) and significantly reduced HCK mRNA and protein levels (Figure 4O; supplemental Figure 9I). Importantly, overexpression of tRNAPheGAA could largely rescue METTL1 KO-mediated loss of HCK expression (Figure 4P; supplemental Figure 9J), demonstrating that the METTL1/tRNAPheGAA axis contributes to HCK expression in AML.

Collectively, METTL1 loss-mediated reduction of m7G-modified tRNAs, notably tRNAPheGAA, promotes ribosome collision, thereby triggering the decay of transcripts that heavily rely on these tRNAs, such as HCK.

The Src tyrosine kinase HCK acts as a direct effector of G proteins17, 18, 19 and is highly expressed in hematopoietic system.20, 21, 22, 23, 24 CXCR4 activates Src tyrosine kinases, including HCK,25, 26, 27 and plays a central role in controlling LSC homing to the BM niche.14,15 In vitro coculture of CXCR4-expressing AML cells with CXCL12-expressing BM stromal cells revealed that KD of tRNAPheGAA or HCK significantly attenuated the migration of AML blasts toward BM stroma (Figure 5D; supplemental Figure 10E). In vivo homing assays via in situ bone imaging (Figure 5E) and flow cytometry (Figure 5F) independently confirmed the crucial role of the tRNAPheGAA/HCK pathway in LSC homing. Notably, overexpression of tRNAPheGAA substantially restored the LSC homing impaired by METTL1 KO, both in vitro and in vivo (Figure 5D,F, right). HCK KD attenuated AML migration into BM stroma to a degree comparable to that observed with the CXCR4 inhibitor AMD310069 (Figure 5G; supplemental Figure 10F-G); moreover, HCK KD could not further significantly reduce migration in AMD3100-treated cells. Those results suggest HCK and CXCR4 are in the same pathway to mediate LSC migration.

Flow cytometry demonstrated that depletion of METTL1, tRNAPheGAA, or HCK significantly reduced surface CXCR4/pCXCR4 levels in AML PDX cells (Figure 5H-I) and cell lines (supplemental Figure 10H-10J). Consistently, western blotting confirmed that METTL1 or HCK loss suppressed HCK/CXCR4 signaling in vitro (supplemental Figure 10K-L). Importantly, METTL1/Mettl1 or Hck loss similarly impaired HCK/CXCR4 signaling in vivo (Figure 5J). Finally, phenocopying METTL1 loss, depletion of tRNAPheGAA or Hck, as well as phenylalanine deprivation, significantly delayed leukemogenesis and prolonged survival (Figure 5K).

These findings establish tRNAPheGAA/HCK as an important downstream signaling of METTL1, directly mediating its roles in LSC homeostasis. METTL1 loss disrupts tRNAPheGAA/HCK/CXCR4 signaling, impairing LSC homing and self-renewal and suppressing leukemogenesis.

Pharmacological inhibition of METTL1 demonstrates potent antileukemia efficacy

We next conducted a high-throughput screening with 264 086 compounds to identify potential small-molecule inhibitors targeting METTL1 (Figure 6A). Top-ranked hits preferably docked within METTL1’s enzymatic pocket (Figure 6B; supplemental Table 5). By assessing the antileukemia efficacy of the top 400 hits in Mono-mac-6 cells, followed by validation in MA9.3RAS and PDX cells, we identified the top 25 candidates with robust antileukemia activity (supplemental Figure 11A; supplemental Tables 6-8). We then evaluated their impact on METTL1 methyltransferase activity both in vitro (cell-free; Figure 6C; supplemental Figure 11B) and in cellulo (supplemental Figure 11C). Of the top-ranking compounds, NSC137443 demonstrated potent inhibition on METTL1 methyltransferase activity in vitro and in cellulo. Docking studies of M1i revealed structural clashes with SAM (S-adenosylmethionine)/SAH at the adenosyl group-binding site of METTL1 (Figure 6H), suggesting that M1i may act as a competitive inhibitor. Enzyme kinetics assay showed that 50% infective dose values of M1i increased linearly with SAM concentrations (supplemental Figure 11F), supporting its competition with SAM to bind to the enzymatic pocket to inhibit METTL1 methyltransferase activity. Moreover, saturation transfer difference nuclear magnetic resonance (STD-NMR)70 demonstrated a direct interaction between M1i and METTL1 proteins (Figure 6I; supplemental Figure 11G). Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)71 and drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS)72 assays further validated this interaction in AML cells (Figure 6J-K). Mutation of E107 and I108, 2 amino acids predicted to interact with M1i (see Figure 6H), substantially impaired the direct interaction between METTL1 and M1i (Figure 6K, bottom). M1i treatment notably reduced tRNA m7G levels in METTL1-deficient AML cells that had overexpression of the METTL1-WT but not the E107/I108 mutant (Figure 6L). In addition, we showed that E107/I108 are essential for METTL1’s function in promoting AML survival (Figure 6M). Consistent with the effects of METTL1 loss, M1i treatment dramatically reduced tRNAPheGAA m7G levels, tRNAPheGAA and HCK steady-state (Figure 6N), and protein (Figure 6O) levels. Importantly, M1i had minimal impacts on the enzymatic activities of other SAM-dependent methyltransferases (supplemental Figure 11H), nor did M1i treatment significantly affect global mRNA m6A levels (supplemental Figure 11I), supporting its high specificity in inhibiting METTL1.

Subsequent in vitro functional studies revealed that M1i robustly and selectively suppressed the growth of various AML cells compared with that of normal hematopoietic cells (Figure 7A). Consistent with the specificity of M1i for METTL1, METTL1 KO in AML cells reduced their sensitivity to M1i, an effect dependent on METTL1 methyltransferase activity (supplemental Figure 12A). Furthermore, M1i significantly increased apoptosis (supplemental Figure 12B), and reduced LSC self-renewal ability of both human and murine AML blasts (Figure 7B; supplemental Figure 12C-D). We then assessed the antileukemia efficacy of M1i in vivo. In the xenograft model using Mono-mac-6 cells, M1i (0.2 mg/kg iv, every other day) significantly reduced the leukemia burden (Figure 7E-F), suppressed AML infiltration in recipient mice (supplemental Figure 12F-G), and prolonged overall survival (Figure 7G). In another xenograft model, M1i similarly reduced leukemia burden (Figure 7H-I; supplemental Figure 12H-I) and extended overall survival (Figure 7J). In AML PDX models, we confirmed the robust antileukemia effect of M1i on suppressing leukemia initiation and progression (Figure 7K); M1i treatment also significantly reduced LSC frequency in vivo (Figure 7L).

M1i treatment in xenograft models did not cause significant changes in body weight (supplemental Figure 12J), indicating low toxicity. To further evaluate toxicity, healthy C57BL/6 mice were treated with M1i at 0.2 and 0.5 mg/kg every other day for 4 weeks (supplemental Figure 13A). Neither dose significantly affected body weight, organ weight, or organ structure (supplemental Figure 13B-E). To assess its impact on normal hematopoiesis, we analyzed PB, BM, and spleen samples. Both doses had minimal effects on the frequency or population of mature hematopoietic lineages or progenitors (supplemental Figure 13F-I). Notably, consistent with findings in homozygous Mettl1 KO models, 0.5 mg/kg M1i led to a reduction in platelet count in PB (supplemental Figure 13F).

In conclusion, our study uncovers a previously unrecognized role of METTL1, particularly its methyltransferase activity, in promoting LSC homing and self-renewal. Mechanistically, METTL1 modifies tRNAPheGAA, selectively regulating the translation and stability of mRNAs enriched in its cognate codons, such as HCK. These findings establish the functional importance of the METTL1/tRNAPheGAA/HCK signaling in LSC homing within the BM microenvironment. Importantly, we identified a selective and effective METTL1 inhibitor and provided proof-of-concept evidence that METTL1 is an actionable therapeutic target for leukemia.

Discussion

Distinct from previously reported roles of METTL1 in promoting proliferation of AML cell lines,43,52 our study provides unique insights into METTL1 in regulating LSC homing and self-renewal and characterizes an entirely new target tRNAPheGAA. Thus far, over 20 distinct tRNAs carry m7G modification at position G4643, 44, 45 to stabilize tRNAs.73, 74, 75 This diversity of m7G-modified tRNAs suggests that the functionally essential tRNA targets or downstream pathways of METTL1 are inherently context dependent, a concept well recognized in RNA epigenetics.76, 77, 78, 79, 80 Zhao et al52 and Orellana et al43 reported that METTL1 loss decreases tRNA m7G levels and destabilizes certain tRNAs such as tRNAAlaAGC and tRNAArgTCT, thereby suppressing mRNA translation and AML cell growth. In contrast, through an unbiased evaluation of all m7G-modified tRNAs in LSCs and in AML following METTL1 KO, we identified tRNAPheGAA as an important target of METTL1 in modulating LSC homing and self-renewal. Importantly, our work uniquely establishes a previously unrecognized link between METTL1, HCK, and CXCR4 in controlling LSC homing. Notably, consistent with tRNAPheGAA loss, phenylalanine deprivation81, 82, 83, 84 reduced LSC self-renewal and suppressed leukemogenesis (Figure 5B,K), confirming the critical role of METTL1/tRNAPheGAA axis in LSC homeostasis. Despite these insights, we anticipate that additional m7G-modified tRNAs that are increased in LSCs and decreased following METTL1 loss, such as tRNAAlaTGC, tRNAValTAC, and tRNALycCTT, may also act as downstream effectors of METTL1 in a context-dependent manner, warranting further investigation.

Ribosome dysregulation is closely coupled with the activation of the NGD mRNA surveillance pathway.85, 86, 87 In our study, ribosome collisions induced by METTL1 KO serve as a critical signal triggering mRNA NGD. The mRNA decay is likely secondary to tRNA loss-mediated translation suppression, with both mechanisms contributing to the dysregulation of METTL1 downstream targets. A more detailed dissection of the relative contributions of METTL1-mediated translation regulation versus mRNA stability would provide valuable mechanistic insight. It is also noteworthy that METTL1/tRNAPheGAA regulates multiple pathways and genes. Beyond HCK, we identified additional candidate targets in the pathways, such as VEGFA, CXCL8, and CD70 (Figure 4C), which share a high frequency of tRNAPheGAA-dependent codons. Those potential targets, like HCK, could contribute to METTL1-mediated leukemogenesis, LSC self-renewal88, 89, 90 and modulation of the tumor microenvironment.91,92

Finally, M1i, a potent and selective METTL1 inhibitor, effectively eliminates LSCs and suppresses leukemogenesis by targeting the METTL1/m7G-tRNAPheGAA axis across multiple leukemia models, including PDX models. This proof-of-concept evidence establishes METTL1 as a druggable epitranscriptomic vulnerability and positions M1i as a promising lead for developing therapies against METTL1-driven cancers. Nonetheless, further optimization of M1i will be necessary to advance it toward clinical applications. Notably, METTL1/Mettl1 loss exerts a stronger antileukemic effect in immunocompetent versus immunodeficient models (Figure 1H-I vs 1K-L), likely because of increased dendritic cell populations in leukemia microenvironment following Mettl1 loss (supplemental Figure 14). These findings suggest that combining METTL1 inhibitors with immunotherapy93 could be a compelling strategy to eliminate LSCs and AML. In addition, because METTL1 loss reduces steady-state levels of m7G-modified tRNAs, exploring potential synergy between METTL1 inhibitors and amino acid deprivation represents an attractive approach for LSC eradication.94, 95, 96
