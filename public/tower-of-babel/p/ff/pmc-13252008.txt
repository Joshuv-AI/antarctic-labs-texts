Integrative Multi‐Omics Analysis Elucidates the Progressive Disease Landscape and Reveals Dynamic Protein Biomarkers for MASLD Surveillance
Authors: Kejun Liu, Bozhi Zhang, Yezhen Tang, Zhenyao Tan, Yang Bu, Yongxue Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMetabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is the most prevalent chronic liver disease worldwide, encompassing a continuum ranging from simple steatosis to steatohepatitis, hepatic fibrosis, and cirrhosis. Despite the complex and heterogeneous pathogenesis, effective therapeutic targets remain elusive. In this study, we sought to identify and validate critical genes implicated in MASLD progression through multi‐omics integration and machine learning algorithms. Analysis revealed considerable activation of lipid metabolism, oxidative stress, and inflammation‐related pathways throughout disease progression, with notable upregulation of AKR1B10, COL1A2, and SPP1 and downregulation of CYP2C19. These expression patterns were consistently verified across in vivo and in vitro models. Functional assays indicated that AKR1B10 knockdown or CYP2C19 overexpression substantially attenuated hepatocellular lipid accumulation, alleviated oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, and suppressed key lipogenic gene expression. Collectively, these findings elucidate key molecular axes in MASLD progression and provide mechanistic insights and theoretical foundations for the development of targeted therapies.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is a leading cause of chronic liver disease globally. Epidemiological data indicate that approximately 25% of the adult population worldwide is affected by MASLD. Among these individuals, nearly 20% with metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatohepatitis (MASH) progress to metabolic dysfunction‐associated hepatic fibrosis (MAHF), while an estimated 5%–10% may develop hepatocirrhosis (HC) or hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [1, 2, 3]. Patients diagnosed with MASLD exhibit metabolic dysfunction; as the condition advances to MASH, it is characterized by the activation of inflammatory pathways and excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition. This progression substantially increases the risk of developing MAHF, HC, and HCC, presenting a considerable public health concern and resulting in significant socioeconomic impact [4].

The MASLD disease spectrum begins with MASL, characterized by excess triglyceride (TG) deposition in hepatocytes. This process is primarily driven by insulin resistance, resulting in excessive delivery of free fatty acids (FFAs) from adipose tissue to the liver, combined with increased hepatic de novo lipogenesis and decreased β‐oxidation [5]. The key factors driving MASL progression to MASH include reduced oxidative phosphorylation efficiency and reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation triggered by mitochondrial dysfunction, causing hepatocyte injury and inflammatory responses [6]. In the MASH microenvironment, growth factors such as transforming growth factor‐β (TGF‐β) and platelet‐derived growth factor (PDGF) released by damaged hepatocytes drive the transformation of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) into myofibroblasts via the SMAD and MAPK pathways, leading to excessive ECM deposition [7, 8]. Additionally, hormone disorders associated with metabolic syndrome regulate hepatic lipid metabolism, further exacerbating the hepatic inflammatory microenvironment and triggering a “second hit” effect that promotes MASLD progression [9, 10]. MASLD pathogenesis is complex and highly heterogeneous, with metabolic disorders—such as obesity, insulin resistance, and dyslipidemia—serving as primary contributions. This underscores MASLD as a critical hepatic manifestation of multi‐system metabolic dysfunction [5, 11]. Currently, effective targeted therapies capable of arresting disease progression are lacking, largely owing to the incomplete understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying the transition from early steatosis to advanced fibrosis and the absence of high‐sensitivity biomarkers for early diagnosis, risk stratification, and assessment of therapeutic efficacy [12, 13]. Additionally, the invasive nature of existing diagnostic procedures and insufficient sensitivity of imaging modalities further complicate the clinical management of MASLD [14]. Early‐detection biomarkers can help screen high‐risk individuals to facilitate treatment. When combined with lifestyle and pharmacological interventions, these markers may reduce the incidence of end‐stage liver disease.

To address these limitations, the current study aimed to delineate the continuous molecular landscape of MASLD progression across the MASL‐MASH‐MAHF‐HC spectrum by integrating multi‐cohort transcriptomic datasets with machine‐learning‐based feature selection. We further combined single‐cell transcriptomics, immune‐infiltration analysis, and intercellular communication inference to define the cellular sources and stage‐associated signaling context of the identified candidates. Expression validation and mechanistic interrogation of core genes were performed using clinical tissue microarrays, staged mouse models, and hepatocyte functional assays. By linking stage‐spanning molecular changes to cell‐type specificity and hepatocyte phenotypes, this study provides an integrated framework for biomarker development and hypothesis generation for targeted intervention in MASLD.

Materials and Methods

Differentially Expressed Genes Associated With MASLD Progression

The R package limma (version 3.50.0) was used to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with MASLD progression. Raw expression matrices underwent background correction, quantile normalization, and log2 transformation before linear‐model fitting. Moderated t statistics were generated using the empirical Bayes (eBayes) framework. Genes with |log2FC|> 0.5 and p < 0.05 were retained as candidate DEGs, and Benjamini‐Hochberg correction was applied for multiple testing control. Volcano plots were generated using ggplot2 (version 3.3.6), and heatmaps with hierarchical clustering based on Euclidean distance were generated using pheatmap (version 1.0.12).

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) Enrichment Analysis

GO enrichment analyses were performed for biological processes (BP), molecular functions (MF), and cellular components (CC). The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) was used to identify significantly altered metabolic pathways enriched within the DEG lists. The R package clusterProfiler (version 4.2.2) was employed to perform GO and KEGG enrichment analyses on MASLD progression‐related DEGs (p < 0.05).

Machine Learning Identification of Key Genes

Key genes implicated in MASLD progression were identified using support vector machine recursive feature elimination (SVM‐RFE), least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, and random forest. For SVM‐RFE, the e1071 package was used with a radial basis function kernel and 10‐fold cross‐validation to iteratively remove the least informative features and identify the feature subset with the lowest classification error. Random forest analysis was performed using 500 trees, and gene importance was ranked by mean decrease in accuracy and mean decrease in Gini. LASSO regression was implemented using glmnet with a binomial family, and the penalty parameter λ was selected through 10‐fold cross‐validation using the 1‐SE criterion. Genes retained by the intersection of the three algorithms were defined as robust key genes for downstream analysis.

Protein–Protein Interaction (PPI) Network Construction

PPI networks for key genes were constructed with the GeneMANIA platform. Subsequent GO and KEGG analyses were performed on key genes and their interactors using the clusterProfiler package.

Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA)

GSVA, an unsupervised non‐parametric enrichment method, was used to estimate pathway activity variation across samples. GSVA was performed with the GSVA package (version 1.42.0) using the MSigDB Hallmark gene set collection (h.all.v7.5.1.symbols.gmt) as the reference. Enrichment scores were calculated for each gene set in each sample and used for between‐group pathway comparisons and correlation analyses with key genes.

Immune Infiltration Analysis

Single‐sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA), an extension of gene set enrichment analysis, computes separate enrichment scores for each sample‐gene set pair. Immune cell marker gene data were downloaded from TISIDB, and relative enrichment scores for various immune cell types were calculated based on the gene expression profile of each sample. Changes in immune cell infiltration levels in MASL, MASH, and MAHF samples were visualized using the R package ggplot2.

Potential Drug Prediction and Molecular Docking

To gain deeper insight into the therapeutic potential of key genes in MASLD and to identify effective drugs targeting these genes, we queried the DGIdb v5.0.8 database (https://beta.dgidb.org/) to retrieve detailed drug‐gene interaction data. Molecular docking of candidate drugs with their target proteins was performed using AutoDockTools 1.5.7. The binding energy reflects the likelihood of binding between a drug and its target protein: lower binding energy indicates higher binding affinity and a more stable conformation. Docking results were visualized with PyMOL 2.5.0.

GWAS

The MASLD dataset used in this study was obtained from the UK Biobank and comprised 2558 MASLD cases and 395241 controls. MASLD diagnoses were established using hospital records (ICD‐10: K74.0, K74.2, K75.8, K76.0, and K76.9). Exclusion criteria included alcohol dependence, alcoholic liver disease, alpha‐1 antitrypsin deficiency, Alagille syndrome, liver transplantation, cystic fibrosis, viral hepatitis, abetalipoproteinemia, LCAT deficiency, lipodystrophy, copper metabolism disorders, Reye's syndrome, congenital metabolic defects, HELLP syndrome, starvation, and acute fatty liver.

Summary‐Data‐Based Mendelian Randomization (SMR) Analysis

SMR analysis was employed to evaluate the causal relationships between protein levels and MASLD. The heterogeneity in dependent instruments (HEIDI) test, which uses multiple SNPs in the same genomic region, was applied to distinguish whether the association between a protein and MASLD was driven by a shared causal variant (pleiotropy) or by genetic linkage. Both SMR and HEIDI analyses were conducted using SMR software (v1.3.1).

Single‐Cell Transcriptomic Data Collection

The MASLD single‐cell sequencing dataset GSE202379 was obtained from the GEO database and comprised liver tissues from 53 patient samples: 7 MASL, 27 MASH, 4 MAHF, and 15 HC. Single‐cell analyses were performed using Seurat (version 4.1.1). Cells with fewer than 200 or more than 5000 detected genes, or with > 20% mitochondrial transcripts, were excluded. Data were normalized and variance‐stabilized using SCTransform, followed by principal component analysis (PCA). The first 20 principal components were retained for downstream analysis. Batch effects across samples were corrected using Harmony (version 0.1.0). Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) embedding was generated with RunUMAP (n.neighbors = 30, min.dist = 0.3). Cluster marker genes were identified using FindAllMarkers (min.pct = 0.25, logFc.threshold = 0.25), and cell types were assigned according to established lineage markers. Cell‐type proportions were then summarized across disease stages.

Cell Trajectory Construction via Pseudotime Analysis

Pseudotime trajectory analysis was conducted in hepatocytes using Monocle 2 (version 3.3.5). The hepatocyte subset identified in Seurat was imported into Monocle as a CellDataSet object based on raw UMI count data, corresponding cell metadata, and gene annotation files. After estimating size factors and dispersion parameters, ordering genes were selected using the differentialGeneTest function, and genes with q‐values < 1 × 10−10 were retained for downstream trajectory construction. The selected ordering genes were specified with the setOrderingFilter function. Dimensionality reduction was performed using the DDRTree algorithm, and cells were arranged along the inferred developmental trajectory with the orderCells function. Cell states, branch points, and pseudotime distributions were visualized using Monocle built‐in plotting functions. Branch‐dependent gene expression dynamics were further analyzed using branch expression analysis modeling, and significantly altered genes across branches were subjected to GO and KEGG enrichment analyses.

Cell–Cell Communication and Ligand–Receptor Interaction Analysis

Intercellular communication was analyzed using CellChat (version 1.1.3) with the CellChatDB human ligand–receptor interaction database. For each cell group, average ligand and receptor expression levels were computed, and interaction probabilities were inferred based on the law of mass action. Statistical significance was assessed using permutation testing (n = 100). CellChat objects from different disease stages were merged for comparative analysis, and differences in interaction number, interaction strength, and pathway‐specific signaling patterns were visualized using netVisual_diffInteraction, netVisual_bubble, and netVisual_aggregate.

Immunofluorescence Detection of Human Liver Tissue Microarrays

Commercial human liver tissue microarrays (SinoSeq, D160Lv03; comprising 10 normal liver, 30 MASL, 30 MASH, 30 MAHF, 30 HC, and 30 HCC samples) were immunofluorescently stained. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, antigen‐retrieved with citrate buffer (pH 6.0, 95°C, 15 min), and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at room temperature for 60 min. Primary antibodies specific for AKR1B10 (Thermo Fisher, PA5‐22036, 1:1000), secreted phosphoprotein 1 (SPP1; Affinity, AF0227, 1:1000), COL1A2 (Affinity, AF7001, 1:1000), and CYP2C19 (Proteintech, 55 453–1‐AP, 1:3000) were incubated overnight at 4°C. Four‐color fluorescence staining was conducted using the Multiple Fluorescence Staining Kit Plus (RuChuang Biotech, RC0086Plus‐67RM). Nuclear counterstaining was performed with DAPI, and samples were mounted with antifade medium. Images were acquired under identical exposure settings using a confocal microscope (Olympus, FV4000MPE), with at least three randomly selected high‐power fields (HPFs) analyzed per core. Relative fluorescence intensity (RFI) was quantified in ImageJ as the integrated density of the target signal normalized to the integrated density of DAPI in the same field.

Construction of Staged MASLD Mouse Models

All animal experiments adhered to the Institutional Animal Experimental Regulations and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee (approval number: KYLL‐2025‐1925). Staged MASLD models were generated using 7 week‐old male C57BL/6J mice, with six mice in each group (n = 6 per group). Induction protocols varied by group: the MASL group received 60% high‐fat diet (HFD) feeding for 8 weeks; the MASH group received 60% HFD feeding for 12 weeks; the MAHF group received HFD combined with intraperitoneal injection of 5% CCl4 solution (2 μL/g, equivalent to approximately 0.1 μL/g pure CCl₄ per injection) twice weekly for 4 weeks (total 12 weeks); the HC group received HFD combined with intraperitoneal injection of 5% CCl4 solution (2 μL/g) twice weekly for 8 weeks (total 16 weeks); the control group received equivalent volumes of corn oil intraperitoneal injection concurrently.

Mice were euthanized humanely by cervical dislocation under anesthesia. Liver tissues were excised, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, routinely dehydrated, paraffin‐embedded, and sectioned (5 μm thickness). The remaining tissues were snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen for storage. Paraffin sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to assess steatosis and inflammation and Masson's trichrome to evaluate collagen deposition (Solarbio, G1340) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Western Blotting

Total protein was extracted, and concentrations were quantified via the BCA method (GBCBIO, G3422). Equivalent amounts of protein (20 μg) were resolved using sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. After blocking with 5% skim milk at room temperature for 1 h, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. The antibodies used included: AKR1B10 (Thermo Fisher, PA5‐22036), SPP1 (Affinity, AF0227), COL1A2 (Affinity, AF7001), CYP2C19 (Abcam, ab137015), ACC1 (Affinity, AF6421), FASN (Affinity, DF6106), and SREBP1c (Affinity, AF6283); α‐tubulin (Proteintech, 66 031–1‐Ig) or GAPDH (Proteintech, 60004–1‐Ig) served as internal controls. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h, followed by enhanced chemiluminescence detection and signal acquisition using a gel imaging system. Western blot experiments were independently repeated three times, and densitometric analyses were performed using Image J.

Oil Red O Staining for Lipid Droplet Accumulation

HepG2 cells were seeded in 6‐well plates and cultured to approximately 80% confluence prior to fatty acid stimulation. Following treatment, cells were washed twice with PBS, fixed with 4% PFA for 30 min, and stained with Oil Red O. Images were acquired using a microscope at 400× magnification. Seven experimental groups were established: (1) HepG2 cells; (2) HepG2 cells + FFA; (3) HepG2 cells + FFA + siRNA‐NC; (4) HepG2 cells + FFA + siRNA‐AKR1B10; (5) HepG2 cells + FFA + OE‐NC; (6) HepG2 cells + FFA + OE‐CYP2C19; (7) HepG2 cells + FFA + OE‐CYP2C19 + siRNA‐AKR1B10. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) Detection of Intracellular TG, Tumor Necrosis Factor‐α ( TNF‐α), and Interleukin 6 ( IL‐6) Contents

HepG2 cells were cultured under different treatment conditions. Subsequently, TG, TNF‐α, and IL‐6 levels were measured with ELISA. Each experiment was performed in triplicate, and data were quantified using standard curves.

Biochemical Kit Detection of Intracellular MDA Content

HepG2 cells were cultured under various conditions, and intracellular oxidative stress was evaluated by quantifying malondialdehyde (MDA) levels using a commercial kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, A003‐1‐1). Absorbance readings were made using a microplate reader. All experiments were repeated three times.

Flow Cytometry Detection of Intracellular ROS

HepG2 cells were treated before being stained with a DCFH‐DA fluorescent probe (Invitrogen) for 30 min. After washing, intracellular ROS levels were measured with flow cytometry (BD FACSCalibur, USA), and data were analyzed using FlowJo/CellQuest. All experiments were repeated three times.

Quantitative Real‐Time Polymerase Chain Reaction (qPCR)

qPCR was performed to analyze the expression of AKR1B10, CYP2C19, ACC1, FASN, and SREBP1c. Total RNA was extracted using the RNA isolator Total RNA Extraction Reagent (VAZYME), and cDNA was synthesized after reverse transcription (VAZYME). Quantitative PCR was performed using ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (VAZYME), employing specific primers for each target gene, with GAPDH serving as the internal reference for normalization. All experiments were repeated three times using the following primer sequences (Table 1).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.2) and GraphPad Prism software (version 9.5.0). Spearman's rank correlation was used to assess correlations among parameters. The Wilcoxon test was used to assess differences between groups. Continuous variables were tested for normality (Shapiro–Wilk test) and homogeneity of variance (Levene's test) before analysis. Data meeting assumptions of normal distribution and equal variance were analyzed using one‐way analysis of variance for multigroup comparisons, with Tukey's post hoc test for pairwise comparisons across all groups and Dunnett's method for comparisons with a specified control group. For data that did not conform to normality or homogeneity of variance, the Kruskal‐Wallis test was applied, followed by Dunn's post hoc test. All statistical tests were two‐tailed, and a p < 0.05 was considered significant. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

Results

Transcriptomic Analysis Identifies Gene Expression Profiles Associated With MASLD Progression

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying MASLD progression, RNA‐seq analysis identified 69 DEGs between MASL and MASH, including 56 upregulated and 13 downregulated genes (Figure 1A,B). A comparison between MASH and MAHF identified 173 DEGs, comprising 114 upregulated and 59 downregulated genes (Figure 1C,D). Additionally, 14 genes intersected between the DEG sets: AKR1B10, ENPP2, COL1A2, GABRB3, FAT1, CD24, SPP1, EPCAM, CCL20, CHST9, CYP2C19, THOP1, PPP1R1A, and RAMP1 (Figure 1E,F). GO and KEGG analyses indicated that the intersecting genes were primarily enriched in metabolic processes, oxidative stress, and inflammatory signal transduction pathways (Figure 1G,H).

Machine Learning Identification of Key Genes Associated With MASLD Progression

The SVM‐RFE algorithm identified six genes associated with MASLD progression: AKR1B10, COL1A2, CYP2C19, GABRB3, RAMP1, and SPP1 (Figure 2A). Random forest analysis identified eight highly informative genes—AKR1B10, EPCAM, CCL20, CHST9, CD24, COL1A2, SPP1, and CYP2C19—based on mean decrease in accuracy and mean decrease in Gini (Figure 2B). LASSO regression retained eight genes in the parsimonious classifier (Figure 2C,D). Intersection of these three orthogonal algorithms yielded four robust candidates—AKR1B10, COL1A2, CYP2C19, and SPP1—thereby reducing stage‐associated DEGs to a concise set with higher stability across modeling strategies (Figure 2E). In the external validation set (GSE135251), AKR1B10, COL1A2, and SPP1 progressively increased whereas CYP2C19 progressively decreased across disease stages (p < 0.05; Figure 2F), supporting their stage‐spanning relevance in MASLD progression.

GeneMANIA‐based PPI analysis connected these genes to networks implicated in lipid metabolic dysregulation, fibrosis, and inflammation (Figure 2G). Subsequent GO and KEGG enrichment analyses highlighted integrin signaling, PI3K‐AKT signaling, and immune cell‐mediated inflammatory pathways (Figure 2H), indicating that the selected genes were not isolated markers but were embedded within biologically coherent programs relevant to MASLD progression.

Furthermore, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, glycolysis, IL‐6/JAK/STAT3 signaling, TGF‐β signaling, and inflammatory responses were significantly upregulated in MASH (Figure S1A); AKR1B10, COL1A2, and SPP1 correlated positively, and CYP2C19 correlated negatively with these pathways (Figure S1B). In MAHF, upregulated pathways included those related to angiogenesis, apoptosis, hypoxia, KRAS signaling, Notch signaling, P53, and Wnt/β‐catenin signaling (Figure S2A); AKR1B10, COL1A2, and SPP1 were positively correlated with these pathways, whereas CYP2C19 was negatively correlated (Figure S2B).

Immune infiltration analysis revealed positive correlations between AKR1B10, COL1A2, SPP1, and immune cells, whereas CYP2C19 exhibited strong associations with activated dendritic cells (DCs) and other key immune cell populations (Figure S3).

Protein‐drug molecular docking analysis revealed favorable scores and stable binding conformations for AKR1B10‐Tolrestat and SPP1‐Calcitonin (Figure S4). Mendelian randomization (MR) screening indicated no significant association between these drug target proteins and other diseases (FDR < 0.05; Figure S5).

Dimensionality Reduction, Clustering, and Annotation of scRNA‐Seq Data in MASLD Progression

To determine the cellular localization of the key genes, the single‐cell dataset GSE202379 underwent quality control, yielding 87978 cells. These cells were distributed into 17 clusters and annotated according to gene‐expression features and canonical cell markers (Figure 3A,B). Eight major cell types were identified: hepatocytes, endothelial cells, lymphocytes, cholangiocytes, HSCs, neutrophils, macrophages, and B cells. Although the overall proportions of major cell types did not show striking stage‐dependent shifts (Figure 3C), this atlas provided the basis for defining the cell‐type‐specific expression patterns of the four key genes and for subsequent focused analyses. DEGs across cell clusters and the expression patterns of representative marker genes were further characterized (Figure 3D,E).

Expression Distribution of Key Genes During MASLD Progression in scRNA‐Seq Data

The distribution of gene expression at single‐cell resolution showed clear cell‐type specificity for the four key genes. AKR1B10 and CYP2C19 were predominantly expressed in hepatocytes, SPP1 was mainly expressed in cholangiocytes, and COL1A2 was enriched in HSCs (Figure 4A). Because the two genes selected for downstream functional perturbation (AKR1B10 and CYP2C19) were both hepatocyte‐predominant, we performed focused pathway analyses in hepatocytes to link their expression to the major parenchymal programs driving steatosis progression. Compared with other liver cell types, hepatocyte DEGs were enriched in fatty acid metabolism, responses to xenobiotic stimuli, carboxylic acid biosynthesis, and organic acid biosynthesis; cellular components included blood microparticles, collagen‐containing ECM, platelet alpha granules, high‐density lipoprotein particles, and platelet alpha granule lumens; molecular functions included monooxygenase activity, aromatase activity, and steroid hydroxylase activity (Figure 4B). KEGG analysis further highlighted focal adhesion, actin cytoskeleton regulation, and peroxisome‐associated pathways (Figure 4C).

Expression Patterns of Critical Genes and Pathway Enrichment Analysis During Hepatocyte Pseudotime Differentiation

Given the predominant hepatocyte expression of AKR1B10 and CYP2C19, pseudotime analysis was performed to characterize hepatocyte state transitions across MASLD progression. The reconstructed trajectory contained two major branch points and five transcriptional states, indicating continuous remodeling rather than discrete, stage‐confined hepatocyte identities (Figure 5A–D).

DEGs across the branches were subjected to enrichment analysis to interpret the biological programs underlying differentiation node 1. Cluster 1 genes were enriched in nutrient‐response and positive regulation of kinase activity pathways, whereas Cluster 2 genes were primarily associated with immune‐related programs including antigen processing and peptide antigen presentation via MHC class I. Cluster 4 genes were associated with SMAD protein complex formation, type I transforming growth factor beta receptor binding, and I‐SMAD binding, consistent with a pro‐fibrogenic transcriptional state. Cluster 5 genes were enriched in metabolic and ion channel‐related pathways, including regulation of lipoprotein lipase activity, the NADP metabolic process, and the chloride channel complex (Figure 5E). AKR1B10 and CYP2C19 both exhibited dynamic, stage‐linked variation along pseudotime rather than static expression patterns (Figure 5F). Together, these branch‐enriched programs suggest a continuum from nutrient‐responsive and immune‐associated states to pro‐fibrogenic and metabolically remodeled hepatocyte states during MASLD progression, with AKR1B10 and CYP2C19 showing dynamic variation along this trajectory.

Differences in Cell Communication During MASLD Progression

CellChat analysis was used as a hypothesis‐generating approach to nominate stage‐associated changes in intercellular signaling during MASLD progression. Compared with MASL, hepatocyte interactions with other cell types increased in number but decreased in strength in MASH (Figure 6A). At the global level, MASH showed more communication events than MASL but lower aggregate interaction intensity (Figure 6B,C). Comparative signaling analysis identified the bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) pathway as a candidate axis showing stage‐dependent remodeling (Figure 6D). Chord diagrams suggested reduced B cell‐hepatocyte BMP signaling in MASH (Figure 6E), accompanied by decreased BMP8A expression (Figure 6F).

When HC was compared with MASH, the interaction strength among hepatocytes, endothelial cells, and macrophages was increased (Figure 7A). HC also showed more total communication events, whereas the overall interaction strength remained broadly comparable at the network level (Figure 7B,C). The Visfatin pathway emerged as a candidate signaling program preferentially enhanced in HC (Figure 7D). In MASH, Visfatin signals were predominantly transmitted from hepatocytes to endothelial cells, lymphocytes, cholangiocytes, HSCs, neutrophils, and hepatocytes, whereas in HC the sending populations expanded to hepatocytes, neutrophils, endothelial cells, and macrophages, with cholangiocytes, endothelial cells, and hepatocytes acting as major receiver populations (Figure 7E,F). Validating Key Gene Expression Changes in MASLD Progression Through Clinical and Animal Studies

Immunofluorescence analysis of human liver tissue microarrays, quantified as DAPI‐normalized relative fluorescence intensity, showed that the AKR1B10 and SPP1 protein levels progressively increased from MASL to MASH, MAHF, HC, and HCC. COL1A2 was markedly upregulated during MAHF and HC, whereas CYP2C19 progressively decreased with disease severity (Figure 8A). These results were consistent with transcriptomic data from the validation set (GSE135251), supporting the identification of these four proteins as critical molecules involved in MASLD progression.

H&E and Masson's trichrome staining verified the successful establishment of staged MASLD mouse models, with six mice per group, showing progressive steatosis, inflammatory injury, and collagen deposition across disease stages (Figure 8B). Western blot analysis was repeated in three independent experiments and demonstrated progressive increases in AKR1B10, COL1A2, and SPP1, together with a decrease in CYP2C19 across disease progression (Figure 8C).

AKR1B10 Knockdown or CYP2C19 Overexpression Improves Lipid Metabolism in Hepatocytes

Functional studies utilizing an FFA‐induced cell model were conducted to assess the impact of AKR1B10 and CYP2C19 on hepatocyte lipid homeostasis. Hepatocyte cell lines were constructed with AKR1B10 knockdown or CYP2C19 overexpression alone, and transfection efficiency was detected using qPCR and western blot analysis (Figure 9A–D). Oil Red O staining and intracellular TG assays demonstrated that FFA treatment induced severe hepatic steatosis (Figure 9E,F). AKR1B10 knockdown and CYP2C19 overexpression significantly reduced lipid droplet accumulation and TG levels, with the combined intervention (OE‐CYP2C19 + si‐AKR1B10) achieving the most notable improvement.

Mechanistically, qPCR and western blot analyses showed that AKR1B10 knockdown or CYP2C19 overexpression suppressed the lipogenic program, with reduced expression of SREBP1c and its downstream targets ACC1 and FASN (Figure 9G–Q). The combined intervention exerted the strongest inhibitory effect, linking these perturbations to attenuation of de novo lipogenesis rather than to a purely descriptive reduction in neutral lipid content.

AKR1B10 Knockdown or CYP2C19 Overexpression Reduces Hepatocyte Oxidative Stress and Inflammation

The effect of AKR1B10 knockdown or CYP2C19 overexpression in mitigating FFA‐induced oxidative stress and inflammatory damage in hepatocytes. Exposure to FFAs significantly exacerbated secondary damage, oxidative stress, and inflammation, as evidenced by increased levels of oxidative damage markers MDA and ROS. Both AKR1B10 knockdown and CYP2C19 overexpression decreased MDA content and ROS production (p < 0.05), with the combined intervention achieving the highest efficacy and effectively attenuating oxidative stress (Figure 10A,B). Additionally, FFA treatment induced the secretion of inflammatory cytokines TNF‐α and IL‐6. Both genetic interventions significantly suppressed the release of both cytokines (p < 0.05), while the combined intervention further reduced the inflammatory response (Figure 10C,D). The consistent effects observed between oxidative stress reduction and inhibition of inflammatory responses suggest a potential involvement of shared upstream signaling pathways, such as improved lipid metabolism or direct modulation of pathways including ROS/JNK/NF‐κB.

Discussion

By integrating bulk transcriptomics, machine‐learning‐based feature selection, single‐cell analysis, clinical tissue validation, staged mouse models, and cell‐based functional perturbation assays, this study delineated a continuous molecular landscape of MASLD progression from steatosis to fibrosis/cirrhosis. Rather than identifying completely unprecedented genes, our work refines the stage‐spanning behavior, cellular origin, and functional relevance of four recurrent candidates—AKR1B10, COL1A2, SPP1, and CYP2C19—within a unified disease‐continuum framework. The combined analyses linked these genes to lipid metabolic dysregulation, inflammation, fibrosis‐related signaling, and evolving intercellular communication programs, thereby providing a more integrated basis for biomarker prioritization and mechanistic hypothesis generation in MASLD. Among the four genes, AKR1B10 and CYP2C19 were of particular interest because both were predominantly localized to hepatocytes and were therefore taken forward for focused functional interrogation. AKR1B10 is minimally expressed in normal liver but is induced under hepatocellular stress and malignant transformation [15, 16, 17]. In our study, AKR1B10 progressively increased across the MASLD stages and positively correlated with inflammatory and lipogenic programs. Recent evidence suggests that AKR1B10 can regulate ACC1‐dependent de novo lipogenesis [18, 19], which is consistent with our finding that AKR1B10 knockdown reduced TG accumulation together with SREBP1c, ACC1, and FASN expression. These findings support the interpretation that AKR1B10 may participate in hepatocyte lipogenic remodeling rather than only serving as a passive disease marker.

COL1A2 encodes type I collagen, a key component of the ECM essential for maintaining tissue homeostasis. When HSCs are activated, pro‐fibrotic factors such as TGF‐β can increase COLIA2 transcription, resulting in greater collagen deposition [20, 21]. SPP1 is a multifunctional glycoprotein involved in bone metabolism, immune regulation, cell adhesion, and tissue repair. In patients with MASH and in mouse models, hepatic macrophages comprise subsets with high SPP1 expression, particularly in areas affected by steatosis and fibrosis; thus, enhancing SPP1‐OSM‐ARG2 crosstalk between hepatocytes and macrophages may benefit patients with MASH [22]. However, the roles of SPP1 may vary depending on its cellular origin: cholangiocyte‐derived SPP1 may exacerbate fibrosis by promoting TGF‐β secretion or activating HSC activation [23, 24], suggesting that SPP1 function requires comprehensive assessment in combination with specific cellular microenvironments. Using MR, this study confirmed that SPP1 is an independent risk factor for MASLD, thereby supporting its therapeutic targeting. The favorable docking affinity between calcitonin and SPP1 suggests that calcitonin may competitively bind SPP1's integrin‐binding domain (RGD motif), blocking SPP1‐integrin interactions, which could reduce inflammatory cell infiltration and HSC activation.

CYP2C19 is a key member of the cytochrome P450 superfamily and is predominantly expressed in hepatic tissue. Liver diseases—including hepatitis, cirrhosis, and liver failure—can suppress CYP2C19 activity through hepatocyte injury and the release of inflammatory mediators, with enzymatic activity inversely correlated with liver disease severity [25, 26]. CYP2C19 plays an essential role in the biotransformation of drugs and xenobiotics; diminished enzyme activity may increase susceptibility to drug‐induced liver injury, thereby complicating therapeutic management in patients with MASLD. Clinically, individuals with low CYP2C19 expression should be prescribed medications metabolized by this enzyme (such as proton pump inhibitors) with caution to mitigate the risk of compounded drug‐induced liver injury and MASLD progression. Recent transcriptomic and proteomic studies further indicate that CYP2C19 is progressively downregulated during MASLD progression, particularly in histologically severe disease [27, 28]. In addition, modeling studies suggest that MASLD‐associated metabolic changes may alter CYP2C19‐related handling of endogenous and exogenous substrates, further supporting a link between CYP2C19 dysregulation and disturbed hepatic metabolic homeostasis [29]. In the present study, CYP2C19 overexpression was accompanied by reduced TG accumulation and downregulation of SREBP1c, ACC1, and FASN, suggesting that CYP2C19 downregulation may be linked to both altered xenobiotic metabolism and dysregulated hepatocellular lipogenic remodeling.

Immune infiltration analysis revealed that increased abundance of adaptive immune cells, including activated CD4+ T cells and DCs, was positively associated with disease progression, suggesting an active role for adaptive immune responses in MASLD. As antigen‐presenting cells, DCs capture DAMPs and present them to CD4+ T cells via MHC‐II molecules, thereby inducing Th1/Th17‐type immune responses, such as secretion of IFN‐γ and IL‐17 [ 30]. The positive correlation between DC infiltration and SPP1/AKR1B10 expression may reflect coordinated inflammatory remodeling. Modulating excessive DC activation may therefore warrant further investigation in the context of adaptive immune dysregulation in MASLD. Furthermore, IL‐6 and IL‐17 secreted by activated CD4+ T cells directly promote HSC activation, while IFN‐γ secreted by Th1 cells exacerbates liver injury by promoting the pro‐inflammatory (M1) phenotype in macrophages [31].

This study identified a marked increase in interaction strength among hepatocytes, endothelial cells, and macrophages in cirrhosis. Because AKR1B10 and CYP2C19 were predominantly localized to hepatocytes, our pathway and pseudotime analyses were intentionally focused on hepatocyte states to maximize mechanistic resolution for the genes advanced to functional validation. At the tissue‐network level, the increased interaction strength among hepatocytes, endothelial cells, and macrophages points to progressive remodeling of the hepatic microenvironment during fibrotic progression. Dysfunctional liver sinusoidal endothelial cells can disrupt substance exchange between hepatocytes and the bloodstream and activate HSCs, thereby promoting fibrotic progression [32]. Enhanced interactions between macrophages—particularly Kupffer cells—and hepatocytes may reflect amplified chronic inflammation driven by sustained lipotoxic stimulation [33]. In cirrhosis, these network changes coincided with enhanced Visfatin signaling. As an insulin‐like factor secreted by adipose tissue and hepatocytes, active Visfatin signaling could serve as a central node linking metabolic stress, inflammation, and fibrosis [34, 35, 36]. During the MASH stage, Visfatin transmission was primarily unidirectional from hepatocytes to endothelial cells and lymphocytes, whereas in cirrhosis the sending populations expanded to include neutrophils, endothelial cells, and macrophages. This shift suggests that multicellular communication networks become progressively rewired as MASLD advances and highlights stage‐dependent signaling programs as a potentially important source of mechanistic insight and therapeutic targets.

Although this study has yielded significant findings, various limitations must be addressed in future research. First, there is insufficient functional validation. In vivo roles and the specific molecular mechanisms of key genes require further confirmation. Second, drug experimentation requires deeper exploration; animal models should be used to assess the efficacy and safety of the potential drugs identified. Third, clinical validation is required: large multicenter cohort studies are needed to determine whether the identified key genes serve as effective serum biomarkers for MASLD staging and to clarify their clinical utility. Finally, spatial heterogeneity remains a challenge; incorporating spatial transcriptomics will enable precise mapping of cellular communication, providing anatomical evidence for targeted drug delivery.

Conclusion

This study systematically analyzed and validated key genes (AKR1B10, COL1A2, SPP1, and CYP2C19) implicated in MASLD progression, as well as their regulatory networks. The results elucidated their roles in lipid metabolism, hepatic fibrosis, and inflammatory responses. Notably, AKR1B10 knockdown or CYP2C19 overexpression significantly alleviates lipid droplet accumulation and inflammatory responses, providing theoretical support for improved diagnosis and targeted treatment of MASLD. Future research is required to validate the efficacy of candidate drugs and to investigate multi‐target combination therapies that may address current clinical challenges in the management of MASLD.
