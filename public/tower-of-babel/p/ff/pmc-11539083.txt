Necrotising cellulitis occurring after cosmetic blepharoplasty: A case report
Authors: Rhyme Timoumi, Laura Moyal, Jean-Philippe Nordmann, Amin Bennedjai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryPeriorbital necrotising cellulitis is a rare localisation of fasciitis and an exceptional complication of cosmetic blepharoplasty.We discuss here the case of a 52-year-old woman who presented with orbital cellulitis 2 days after undergoing a bilateral cosmetic lower blepharoplasty. The diagnosis was confirmed on magnetic resonance imaging. Cellulitis was complicated by ocular hypertension resistant to local treatment and intravenous acetazolamide, leading to a decrease in visual acuity.Infection management consisted of intravenous antibiotics, corticosteroid bolus and drainage surgery. Bacteriological samples yielded Staphylococcus epidermidis resistant to all betalactamines. Anatomopathological analysis of the drained sample revealed an aspect compatible with necrotising cellulitis.To our knowledge, this is the seventh case of necrotising cellulitis occurring after blepharoplasty reported in the literature. The other cases were due to the virulent Streptococcus pyogenes and required surgical debridement and antibiotics. Our case is unique, as it is the first reported case of necrotising cellulitis due to another pathogen, Staphylococcus epidermidis, which appears less virulent.Keywords: Fasciitis, Necrotising cellulitis, Blepharoplasty, Aesthetic surgery, Nosocomial infection

Introduction

Periorbital necrotising fasciitis (PNF) is a rare localisation of fasciitis that occurs less frequently than fasciitis affecting the legs and perineal region. This is attributed to the important vascularization of the face, which protects against infections.

PNF corresponds to a necrotising cellulitis affecting the subcutaneous tissues surrounding the eyes. Contrary to preseptal cellulitis that can be treated with medication, PNF is much more severe, requiring surgical debridement along with antibiotics to be controlled.

Necrotising cellulitis is a rare complication of aesthetic surgery. In a literature review, only 15 out of 3782 articles on necrotising cellulitis were related to aesthetic surgery.1 Liposuction was the most common surgical procedure followed by blepharoplasty. Nevertheless, PNF remains an exceptional complication of blepharoplasty. Only six other cases have been reported to date.2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7

We discuss here the case of a patient presenting with PNF following cosmetic lower blepharoplasty.

Case description

We report the case of a 52-year-old woman who presented at the emergency department of Quinze-Vingts Institute, Paris, France, who had experienced swollen eyelids and painful red eyes for the past 2 days. Four days before, she had undergone a transconjunctival inferior blepharoplasty. She had no major comorbidity.

The initial examination made at the emergency department found a reduced best corrected visual acuity of 7/10 (80 on the Early Treatment of Diabetic Retinopathy Study [ETDRS] scale) on the right eye (OR) and 5/10 (70 on the ETDRS scale) on the left eye (OS). Intraocular pressure was high in both eyes: 26 mmHg OR and 47 mmHg OS.

From a clinical perspective, we noticed bilateral erythematous and indurated oedema of the eyelids (Figure 1). The patient had no proptosis and did not complain of diplopia. Nevertheless, we observed limited movement in the superior, inferior, and lateral rectus muscles, which was predominant in the left eye.

Photograph of the patient on the first day of medical care showing significant indurated oedema and erythema of the eyelids.

A slit lamp examination revealed bilateral conjunctival hyperaemia and bilateral chemosis. There was no anterior segment inflammation or papillary oedema.

In light of this clinical picture of orbital cellulitis as a complication of oculoplastic surgery, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed. MRI confirmed the diagnosis of bilateral cellulitis, which had started to spread into the orbit of the left eye (Figure 2).

Magnetic resonance imaging showing bilateral infiltration of the soft tissues, which had started to spread into the orbit of the left eye (arrow).

Intravenous antibiotics of cefuroxime 200 mg/kg/day and metrodinazole 1500 mg/day was administered. Ocular hypertension was treated with acetazolamide IV 1500 mg/day and local B-blockers.

Given the unfavourable evolution on day 2, fluid was drained by a subconjunctival incision on the right inferior eyelid and a subciliary incision on the left inferior eyelid. The collected sample confirmed the presence of Staphylococcus epidermidis resistant to all betalactamines. Anatomopathological analysis found an aspect compatible with necrotising cellulitis.

Despite drainage surgery and intravenous antibiotics, increased swelling and induration of the eyelids was observed. A new MRI confirmed the extension of the orbital cellulitis without meningeal involvement (Figure 3a), interpreted as an extension of the inflammatory process. Therefore, three boluses of 500 mg methylprednisolone were administered intravenously between days 4 and 7.

Magnetic resonance imaging performed on (a) day 2 showing the extension of the inflammation into the orbit (arrows) and on (b) day 8 of hospitalization showing a decrease in the infiltration of the soft tissues and orbit.

Significant clinical improvement occurred after steroid treatment with a reduction in eyelid swelling and chemosis and an improvement in upward gaze.

On day 8, visual acuity was measured at 8/10 OR (80 with ETDRS scale) and 7/10 OS (80 with ETDRS scale). Intraocular pressure was normal at 11 mmHg OR and 13 mmHg OS under oral administration of 750 mg acetazolamide/day. Eyelid opening returned to normal. A small periorbitary oedema and limited upward gaze persisted. A new MRI confirmed the clinical regression of the cellulitis (Figure 3b).

The patient was discharged from hospital with amoxicillin/acid clavulanic and prednisone. After 10 days, the infection was healed.

Discussion

PNF usually occurs secondary to a traumatism (35%) or surgical procedure (13%), although no trigger is identified in half of cases.8 The mortality rate is estimated at 14%, much less than the mortality rate of 30% for necrotising fasciitis affecting other parts of the body.9

Necrotising fasciitis (NF) is divided into two types according to its microbiological status.8 NF type 1 is polymicrobial and is associated with risk factors of immunodepression unlike NF type 2. By contrast, NF type 2 is caused by a single bacterium, mostly Streptococcus pyogenes or Pseudomonas aeruginosa. In NF type 2, toxins released by the bacteria stimulate platelet aggregation and are responsible for microthrombi reducing the tissue perfusion, thus resulting in necrosis. Our patient suffered from NF type 2, as only one pathogen was identified without immunodepression.

In our case, few elements were evocative of necrotising cellulitis. From a clinical perspective, necrosis of the skin was absent. The limited gaze movements pointed to an orbital extension but not to the necrotising character of the infection. Only the non-response to intravenous antibiotics at the start of treatment and the sudden progression of swelling provided clues for the final diagnosis. The inflammatory biological syndrome remained moderate. Overall, the clinical picture of the necrotising cellulitis in our case seemed less severe than what is described in the literature. This may be explained by the pathogen identified in our case, Staphylococcus epidermidis, probably less virulent than Streptococcus pyogenes.

Our case is unique, as it seems to be the first reported case of PNF not caused by Streptococcus pyogenes. To our knowledge, this is the seventh case of PNF occurring after blepharoplasty.2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7

Management of PNF should include both intravenous antibiotics with at least two broad-spectrum antibiotics and a large surgical debridement of the necrotising tissues, which may be repeated if necessary. Belonging to the lincosamide family, clindamycin was used in five of the six reported cases of necrotising cellulitis after blepharoplasty.2, 3, 4, 5, 6 Its anti-toxic properties may be interesting.

As PNF after blepharoplasty is a nosocomial infection mostly due to Streptococcus pyogenes but also to resistant Staphylococcus as in our case, the systematic use of vancomycin associated with clindamycin should be discussed as first-intention antibiotic therapy.

Drainage surgery seemed sufficient to treat the infection. No debridement was needed. This may be explained by the relatively young age of our patient and the lower virulence of the bacterium.

Our case also underlines the massive inflammatory component of the infection, as clinical improvement was only noticed after the administration of corticosteroid bolus. The use of anti-inflammatory treatment may thus be controversial in this context.

Finally, the role of hyperbaric oxygen therapy in the treatment of NF has been discussed in a few studies, as it has been shown to reduce mortality and to improve tissue viability.10 It is presumed that hyperbaric oxygen therapy inhibits the release of exotoxins, improves leucocyte function, and thus better controls the NF, thus reducing the number of surgical debridement. Hyperbaric oxygen therapy was used in three out of the six previous cases of NF occurring after blepharoplasty.2,5,7

As necrotising cellulitis after blepharoplasty is a rare condition, diagnosis should be made rapidly to avoid any treatment delays.
