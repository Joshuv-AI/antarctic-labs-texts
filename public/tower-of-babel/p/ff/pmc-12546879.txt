Antibiotic purchasing through online pharmacies: A mystery shopper study
Authors: Norah O Abanmy, Sarah A Alshehri, Renad A Alsudais
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe illegal sale of antibiotics through online pharmacies (OPs) can lead to misuse and poses major health risks, because drug quality and authenticity are not ensured. This study was aimed at investigating the extent of illegal online antibiotic sales from both local and international OPs.Material and MethodsA mystery shopper study was conducted on topical clindamycin, oral amoxicillin, and amoxicillin/clavulanic acid. A search term for each antibiotic in both English and Arabic was used to search Google, Bing, and Yahoo. The first 20 results were obtained, examined, and evaluated according to predetermined criteria. Purchasing was performed according to a systematic approach, to explore whether OPs would sell antibiotics without valid prescriptions, accept outdated prescription, or reject sales.Results and DiscussionAt the 60 OPs identified, 22 purchases were made, and ten products were successfully delivered, all from local OPs. Some international OPs requested Bitcoin payment and therefore appeared to be potential scam websites. A substantial number of OPs did not adhere to regulatory requirements, and 33.3 % (20 of 60) did not request prescriptions. Among those OPs, 45 % (9 of 20) were fake, and 45 % (9 of 20) sold topical clindamycin. Whereas 96.7 % of OPs provided contact details, only 13.3 % had quality certifications. Return policies were displayed by 85 % of OPs, and 88.3 % displayed drug information and related warnings, including adverse effects and interactions.ConclusionOur findings underscore the urgent need for stricter regulations and enhanced monitoring of OPs. However, searching of the dark web, which is known to have illegal marketplaces offering prescription drugs, particularly antibiotics, was not investigated.Keywords: Antibiotics, Mystery shopper, Online pharmacy, Prescription

Introduction

Online pharmacies (OPs) are platforms or websites that operate via the Internet and sell pharmaceutical products. The primary purpose of OPs is to enable people to conveniently purchase prescription medications, over-the-counter medications, and healthcare products without physically visiting a pharmacy. OPs have markedly gained popularity in recent years, because they provide prescription medications, particularly the antibiotics used most frequently by patients; offer convenient access to pharmaceutical medications at relatively lower cost than those at physical pharmacies; reach large numbers of customers at distant geographical locations; and offer delivery to customers' desired locations.1, 2, 3 Reputable OPs operate in accordance with specific regulations, to ensure adherence to safety standards and regulatory requirements, such as requiring customers to provide a valid prescription when ordering prescription medications, providing contact details for customer inquiries, declaring their geographical location, identifying the shipping origin, and obtaining at least one quality certification. According to the Saudi Food and Drug Authority's Guideline of Good Storage and Distribution Practices (GSDP), medicines must be sourced from licensed suppliers, stored and transported under controlled conditions, documented for full traceability, and dispensed only with a valid prescription.4

Examples of quality certification for OPs operating internationally include the Verified Internet Pharmacy Practice Site (VIPPS) from the National Association of Boards of Pharmacy (NABP) and the Health on the Net (HON) code. The Joint Commission on Accreditation of Healthcare Organizations (JCAHO) certifications for locally operated OPs are the Ministry of Health (MOH) license number for electronic facilities or the Ministry of Commerce (MOC) commercial license number.5 In addition to displaying privacy statements, information disclaimers, and return policies, as well as providing customers with an invoice after ordering the medication, OPs governed by a regulatory agency (e.g., the Food and Drug Administration [FDA], Therapeutic Goods Administration [TGA], European Medicines Agency [EMA], or National Association of Pharmacy Regulatory Authorities [NAPRA]) often display a logo to confirm their authority and are registered as authorized retailers with the relevant regulatory authorities.2 However, this is not always the case.

Despite offering convenience, the emergence of OPs has introduced multiple problems in healthcare that require critical attention and control. Many illicit OPs that do not follow these strict guidelines have found their way to the market, and sell counterfeit medications, low-quality medications, or even prescription drugs without prescription.1 Sales without prescriptions might be the most concerning aspect, because OPs providing easy access to prescription medications pose a substantial health threat, particularly for antibiotics. In a survey-based study conducted in the Far East Federal District of Russia, including 3789 respondents, a substantial 36.9 % of all prescription drugs ordered online were antibiotics.6 Although antibiotics are prescription-only in many countries, these drugs are frequently purchased without prescription worldwide. Illegal access to antibiotics through community pharmacies selling them without prescription has been described in previous studies.7, 8, 9, 10 OPs have been suggested to make the sale of antibiotics without prescription even easier.11, 12, 13 This illegitimate practice might be mitigated by providing appropriate education to pharmacists, and applying rigorous rules and policies to community pharmacies; however, such control measures are particularly difficult to implement for OPs.

Facilitating access to drugs without prescription contributes to antibiotic misuse and overuse, thus increasing the incidence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) worldwide. AMR is a major threat to global public health, and its progression is closely associated with inappropriate antibiotic consumption, including self-medication and access to drugs without prescription, particularly through online platforms.14,15 Petersen et al. have highlighted that such unregulated access and misuse substantially contribute to the global spread of resistant organisms, and low- and middle-income countries are disproportionately affected by relatively weak regulatory frameworks and enforcement mechanisms.16 This situation is worsened by the growing role of OPs in bypassing prescription requirements, thus hindering monitoring and control of antibiotic use.15 In November 2023, WHO experts warn about AMR, in this report they mentioned that without immediate intervention, AMR could result in up to 10 million deaths a year by 2050.14 The AMR burden is also increasing in KSA (SA). According to a recent review,16 both Gram-positive and Gram-negative resistant pathogens are prevalent in SA. Despite this information and the expanded use of OPs, no previous local studies, to our knowledge, have evaluated antibiotic access through OPs. This study was aimed at investigating and assessing the prevalence of illegal sale of antibiotics without valid prescriptions through OPs, by using the mystery shopper technique. An additional aim was examining the accessibility and extent of such sales at national and international levels, to provide valuable insights into the magnitude of this problem, and guide policymakers and healthcare authorities in combating the illegal online sale of antibiotics.

Materials and Methods

OP search and selection

For the purpose of the current work, two antibiotics (amoxicillin oral tablets and topical clindamycin solution) were chosen. Different dosage forms of the antibiotics were selected to widen the scope of the study and capture additional selling processes. For OP selection, we used three search engines, Google, Bing, and Yahoo, all accessed through a single web browser, Google Chrome. A convenience sampling approach was used to select OPs from the search results. The first 20 results from each search engine were included in this study. This process was repeated for each search term in Arabic and English until the desired sample size of 120 websites was obtained for each antibiotic. Because amoxicillin is also available in combination with clavulanic acid, a search term “amoxicillin/clavulanic acid and Augmentin” was also used, thus yielding another 120 results and a total of 240 results for amoxicillin alone. The following search terms were used across the search engines: “buy topical clindamycin, buy oral amoxicillin, buy oral amoxicillin/clavulanic acid” in English, and “شراء كليندامايسين موضعي, شراء أموكسيسيلين, شراء اموكسيسيلين وحمض الكلافونيك أو اوقمنتين” in Arabic, to ensure the relevance of the selection process (Figure 1).

Evaluation of selected OPs

Criteria were determined specifically for this research purpose, according to Saudi and international regulations, as well as the characteristics of OPs, and were assembled according to Orizio et al.2

Purchasing and payment

Ordering and purchasing were conducted by two pharmacy interns serving as mystery shoppers, according to the technique described by Gong et al.13 Each intern was assigned to one antibiotic and a language of either Arabic or English, and searched each of the three search engines. The first 20 results appearing on the results page were selected (Figure 2). For each selected OP, the interns first attempted to place orders directly or to contact customer service if necessary. If a prescription was required, an expired or unvalidated prescription was uploaded. If sale of an antibiotic occurred, an investigation was conducted.

Data analysis

Data were stored and managed in Microsoft Excel. Variables are reported as numbers and percentages, and the results are presented according to descriptive analysis.

Results

Search process

A total of 66 OPs were identified in the search process: 27 from searches for topical clindamycin, and 39 from searches for oral amoxicillin and amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (Figure 3, Figure 4). Six OPs appeared within the search results for both antibiotics; therefore, the total number of investigated OPs was 60. International OPs represented a substantial proportion (53 %, n = 32) of the total sample. The international OPs were from the USA, UK, Canada, Australia, Egypt, Qatar, Kuwait, Nibal, India, the Philippines, Singapore, and Malaysia. Local OPs constituted 31.6 % (n = 19) of the sample. The remaining 15 % (n = 9) of OPs were in unknown/unclear regions.

Purchasing process

Of the ten completed purchases for topical clindamycin, one was from a fake OP that did not deliver the product, and two OPs refused to deliver the medication. Seven products were ultimately received. Among the OPs that refused delivery, one contacted us, stating that they did not offer pharmaceutical temperature-controlled transport services in our region. The other declined to deliver without providing a reason. We attempted to contact both OPs, but they did not respond. Both OPs subsequently refunded the payment.

For amoxicillin and oral amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, purchases were attempted from 12 OPs, 10 local OPs, one international OP, and one fake OP. Among the local OPs, seven rejected the outdated prescriptions; two did not request a prescription, despite stating “Prescription required to order this medicine” on their websites; and one accepted the outdated prescription. The two OPs that did not request a prescription and the OP that accepted the outdated prescription successfully delivered the products. Purchasing amoxicillin/clavulanic acid from international OPs was feasible from only one OP, which eventually rejected delivery of the medication, because of its obligations under its local laws. Although a prescription was required, whether an outdated prescription would have been accepted is unclear, because the product was not delivered.

For some OPs, an erectile dysfunction drug was offered as a free bonus item when we attempted to order an antibiotic. Some OPs’ links displayed different names from those on the main website. One OP masqueraded as a real existing university website, but clicked links led to a different website for customers to order through.

Evaluation process

Of the 22 OPs from which we made purchases, two did not have a clear location or origin. When we attempted to purchase from them, neither requested a prescription. One OP offered oral amoxicillin/clavulanic acid at a higher price than local OPs and claimed to deliver worldwide. The purchase and payment were completed, but the product was never delivered, and the website link subsequently ceased to work. The second OP offered topical clindamycin and also claimed to deliver worldwide. However, it rejected payment in real currency and insisted on Bitcoin instead. Notably, another OP offered a 10 % discount for use of Bitcoin as payment. Bitcoin is a digital currency that is decentralized (not controlled by a specific agency or banks) and can be traded between individuals via the Internet. Although it is not a real monetary currency, it can be used to buy real objects.17 We eventually did not complete the transaction and determined that OPs with these characteristics were fake or scam websites that claimed to be OPs. Additionally, one international OP included in our study sells OTC and medications for long-term use, but not antibiotics; therefore, it was not included in the purchase count.

OPs were evaluated according to 15 criteria (Table 2). The evaluation was applied to all identified OPs, regardless of whether a purchase was made. The evaluation of OPs offering both topical and oral antibiotics led to different results for criteria 1 and 2. For the three local OPs that offered both antibiotics, separate evaluations were performed; because each of those OPs was counted twice, the actual number of local OPs was 22 rather than 19. Three international OPs were also counted twice, once for each antibiotic. However, because their evaluations were identical, the actual number of OPs was 32 rather than 35.

Clear contact details were provided by 96.7 % (58) of OPs; however, only 13.3 % of OPs had at least one quality certification. Of the contacted OPs, 33.3 % (20) did not request a prescription. Among those OPs, most were either fake (nine OPs, 45 %) or sold topical clindamycin (nine OPs, 45 %), whereas only two local OPs (10 %) offered amoxicillin and amoxicillin/clavulanic acid. OPs were considered to have a recent update if their homepage displayed an update date within the prior 3 months. Date updates were found in only eight OPs (13.3 %), whereas 18 OPs (30 %) showed current offers. The remaining 34 OPs (56.7 %) could not be definitively assessed according to this criterion, because of insufficient information. The provision of invoices could not be assessed for 43 OPs (71.7 %). The primary reason was the inability to complete the ordering process, which was observed with 31 OPs (72 %), mostly involving international orders. Other reasons included rejected outdated prescriptions (5 OPs, 11.6 %) and fake OPs (7 OPs, 16 %). Return policies were provided by 85 % of OPs (51). Moreover, 53 OPs (88.3 %) displayed drug names, indications, package sizes, prices, and related warnings, including adverse effects and interactions. Of the international OPs, 14 (43.75 %) displayed a logo, and 17 (53.1 %) were registered with a regulatory authority. Among local OPs, 18 (94.7 %) displayed a commercial license number issued by the MOC, whereas only one of those OPs also displayed a Saudi Food and Drug Authority (SFDA) license number on its homepage.

Discussion

This study assessed the prevalence of illegal sale of antibiotics without a valid prescription through OPs, and examined the accessibility and extent of such sales at national and international levels. A total of 22 purchases were made from 66 OPs, and 11 (eight of which were topical clindamycin) antibiotics were received. Antibiotics were purchased without a valid prescription, and all 22 purchases were from local OPs. Notably, a considerable number of OPs operated without adhering to established regulatory requirements, and many did not request a prescription. This finding prompts critical concerns regarding public health safety, although most of these OPs (70 %) sold topical antibiotics. We also identified fake OPs. Some of the evaluation criteria were clearly violated by several fake OPs, such as providing a clear geographical location or origin, updating website content, and providing quality certification. In addition, fake OPs did not ask for a prescription to sell antibiotics; instead, they claimed that they offered antibiotics without prescription requirements, to promote their sales.

Illegal prescription drug selling still occurs despite the establishment of restrictive regulations.6,11,13,18 In 2022, Soboleva et al. conducted an online survey investigating various aspects of purchasing pharmacy products online, including prescription drugs.6 Among 3789 respondents from the Far Eastern Federal District of Russia, 52.7 % stated that prescriptions were not required when ordering. Antibiotics were the most frequently ordered prescription drugs (36.9 %). However, the study did not measure the number of antibiotics ordered without prescription. Gong et al., in a study examining the prevalence of over-the-counter antibiotic sales, both online and at community pharmacies in China in 2020,13 found that 174 (79.1 %) OPs sold antibiotics, 118 (67.8 %) of which did not ask for a prescription and 44 (25.3 %) of which accepted outdated prescriptions. These percentages are much higher than those reported in the current study, given the larger scope of the search and larger number of OPs in those countries. A study in the United Kingdom by Boyd et al. has analyzed and evaluated OPs selling antibiotics. That study explored the quality and legality of OPs, analyzed the purchasing process, and assessed antibiotic stewardship and patient concerns, but did not attempt to order any antibiotics.11 In this study, after analyzing 20 websites from Google and Yahoo searches, we found that most of the OPs (75 %) were not registered with the Medicines and Healthcare Products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) or The General Pharmaceutical Council (GphC), and 50 % operated from unclear locations. Lack of authorization was also observed in our sample: most local OPs did not display SFDA license numbers on their homepages but instead provided only their MOC license numbers. According to Alfageh et al. (2024), online pharmacy services in SA are typically offered as add-ons to licensed community pharmacies, and multiple governmental authorities have legislative rules applying to online pharmacies. The authorities described were the SFDA, the MOC, the MOH, and the Customs Authority.19

Boyd et al. also examined whether OPs in their sample asked for a prescription. Whereas 45 % did not ask for a prescription, 80 % had a consumer driven choice, in which the initial choice of the specific antibiotic, dose, and quantity was made by the consumers themselves.11 In addition, the study categorized OPs according to their displayed locations. A total of 50 % operated from unknown locations, a lower percentage than observed in our sample. Although the authors examined these aspects of OPs in their sample, their conclusion that OPs actually sell antibiotics without prescription was not validated, because purchasing was beyond the scope of the study. A similar study, conducted by Mainous et al. in the United States, examined antibiotics available without prescription on the Internet, including type, quantity, and shipping time.18 The study sample consisted of 138 vendors, 36.2 % of which sold antibiotics without prescription. To provide further support, the investigators submitted an order to a single vendor, which arrived approximately 1 month later from Mexico.

Ordering from international OPs or OPs outside SA was challenging in this study. All international OPs in our sample exhibited at least one characteristic that constituted an obstacle to ordering. OPs, particularly those from the UK and the US, delivered only within those countries and did not ship internationally. A further complication was that some OPs required consumers to share their locations; any customers with locations outside the country's borders cannot place orders. Other OPs required use of mobile numbers with a country code matching the country in which they operate. Another barrier to antibiotic purchasing without prescription was the need for an online consultation or a video call with a physician to determine eligibility for a prescription. These characteristics prevented us from ordering and precluded our evaluation of those OPs according to several criteria, such as accepting outdated prescriptions or issuing receipts. Several criteria markedly differed between local and international OPs; for example, no local OPs displayed quality certification on their websites, whereas some international OPs met this criterion. Other criteria differing between local and international OPs included regulatory oversight by local authorities, the presence of a logo or other identifiers verifying OP legitimacy, registration with a relevant regulatory body, and item pricing.

Our findings have substantial implications. For healthcare professionals, the ease with which antibiotics can be purchased without prescription poses a direct risk to patient safety and public health. This finding reinforces the crucial roles of healthcare providers in educating patients about the dangers of self-medication and the importance of obtaining prescriptions through legitimate channels. Furthermore, policymakers should consider implementing stricter regulations on online pharmacies and enhancing monitoring of their activities. This monitoring could involve greater scrutiny of websites claiming to sell medications as well as the establishment of a centralized database of verified OPs to help consumers make informed choices.

This study has several strengths and limitations. The strengths include that this study is the first of its kind in SA, to our knowledge, and it lays a groundwork for further research to gain deeper insights into OP use in SA, an area where knowledge is currently lacking. In addition, the mystery shopper method effectively simulated real-world purchasing scenarios. Moreover, our use of search terms in two languages widened the scope of the results. Finally, we explored the ability to purchase two different antibiotics and dosage forms, to capture a wider scope of selling processes.

Several methodological aspects of this study could have been approached differently. Although we used a convenience sampling approach, our reliance on the first 20 search results from each search engine led to an overrepresentation of certain OPs because of search engine algorithms, thus potentially limiting the study's ability to accurately reflect the broader landscape of OPs. A more randomized method might have provided a broader range of OPs and potentially offered additional insights into their operations. Furthermore, the study's narrow focus on two antibiotics might limit the generalizability of the findings to other antibiotics. Another possible limitation is that we did not investigate the extent of illegal sales through social media platforms or the dark web. Finally, we used only one browser to access the desired search engine.

Future research could benefit from including a wider range of antibiotics and other prescription drugs to provide a more comprehensive analysis of OP practices. Additionally, expanding the scope of the study to include a larger number of social media platforms, the dark web, and delivery applications could identify additional OPs, thus enabling a more thorough assessment of the OP landscape.

Conclusion

This study revealed a trend toward local and international OPs dispensing antibiotics without prescription. Although the number of OPs that sold antibiotics without prescription might seem small, our findings indicated non-compliance with prescription regulations. Particularly concerningly, we found that antibiotics could easily be obtained without medical and regulatory supervision. The ease with which antibiotics can be sold without prescription poses substantial risks to patient safety and public health, and might further exacerbate the critical issue of antimicrobial resistance. As the popularity of OPs continues to grow, implementing stricter regulatory enforcement, regularly monitoring OP practices, and enhancing public education will be crucial to safeguard public health and ensure that patients receive safe and effective medication through legitimate channels.
