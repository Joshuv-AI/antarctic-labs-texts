Incidence, predictors and outcomes of delirium in acute aortic syndrome
Authors: Shuying Chen, Yanjun Luo, Xiaojia Lu, Xiansheng Huang, Long Lin, Shaobin Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study was undertaken to examine incidence and predictors of delirium in acute aortic syndrome (AAS) patients with or without surgery, and to evaluate its association with early and follow-up outcomes.MethodsA cross-sectional study was retrospectively performed at a single center using a database that was prospectively maintained between 2019 and 2021. Measured outcomes included postoperative results and both early and follow-up survival rates.ResultsFive hundred ninety-two AAS patients were recruited. Delirium was seen in 31.6% (187/592) of patients. Predictors of delirium included the use of sedatives (odds ratio (OR) = 16.490, 95%-confidence interval (CI) = 8.981–30.279, P < 0.001) and sodium nitroprusside (OR = 2.676, 95%CI = 1.729–4.142, P < 0.001). Delirium was revealed as a risk factor for length of hospital stay (LOS) (OR = 1.101, 95%CI = 1.023–1.185, P < 0.05) and prolonged intensive care unit (ICU) stay (OR = 3.073, 95% CI = 1.744–5.415, P < 0.001).ConclusionThe incidence of delirium in AAS patients is 31.6%, significantly increasing LOS and ICU stay duration. The use of sedatives and sodium nitroprusside were revealed as risk factors for delirium.

Introduction

Acute aortic syndrome (AAS) comprises life-threatening conditions marked by a compromise in aortic wall. Common forms of AAS include intramural hematoma, penetrating atherosclerotic ulcer and aortic dissection all of which have the potential to cause rupture [1]. Despite significant advancements in the preventing, diagnosing, and managing acute aortic dissection, it persists as a complicated cardiovascular issue with high immediate mortality and considerable morbidity among those who survive initial phase [2]. Additionally, of postoperative complications remains relatively elevated, reducing life quality and leading to poor prognosis [3]. Early detecting preventing risk factors for these complications are crucial.

Delirium is a neuropsychiatric condition marked by a sudden onset and a variable course of confusion and inattention. It is among most frequent complications in AAS, with an incidence rate between 10% and 50% [4–6]. Risk factors like multiple comorbidities, preexisting cognitive impairment, advancing age and substance abuse can increase the likelihood of patients developing postoperative delirium. Additionally, perioperative factors including blood transfusion, glucose management, medication exposure can significantly affect the incidence of delirium [6].

It is well-known that delirium can lead to extended length of hospital stay (LOS), greater resource utilization, and higher rates of mortality in AAS patients, regardless of whether they undergo cardiovascular surgery [4, 7]. Patients undergoing aortic surgery or amputation are often in a vulnerable state and more susceptible to delirium comparing to those undergoing other procedures [8]. Nevertheless, there is a scarcity of studies documenting the incidence and impact of delirium in AAS.

Gathering more information on delirium risk factors could help clinicians in early detecting and preventing such condition, thereby enhancing decision-making and reducing mortality and morbidity. Consequently, this study was to examine incidence, predictors, and outcomes of delirium in patients with AAS.

Patients and methods

Ethical statement

The Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Shantou University Medical College approved the study (No. B-2024-010). Throughout the study, researchers ensured data confidentiality and compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Therefore, patient consent to review their medical records was not required for this retrospective study.

Data collection

This retrospective observational study was conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Shantou University Medical College using a prospectively maintained institutional database. Between January 2019 and December 2021, 594 consecutive patients with AAS were enrolled. Exclusion criteria included: (1) previous aortic interventions, (2) malignant tumors, (3) blunt traumatic thoracic aortic injury, (4) connective tissue diseases, (5) cognitive impairment or preexisting dementia (Patients with dementia were excluded based on prior clinical diagnoses and medical history), and (6) operative and baseline information was missed (For missing data, cases where key variables (such as sedative use or delirium assessment) were unavailable were excluded). Clinical, laboratory, imaging, treatment, and outcome data were retrospectively extracted from electronic medical records, intensive care unit (ICU) databases, operative reports, nursing records, and imaging archives for all eligible patients diagnosed with AAS between January 2019 and December 2021. Data extraction was independently performed by two trained investigators using a standardized data collection form. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus and, when necessary, adjudicated by a senior clinician. Logical checks and cross-validation against source records were applied to ensure data accuracy and internal consistency.

AAS was defined as acute aortic dissection, intramural hematoma, or penetrating atherosclerotic ulcer confirmed by contrast-enhanced computed tomography angiography (CTA) within 14 days of symptom onset. Eligible patients were identified retrospectively from institutional records. All included patients underwent CTA of the entire aorta at admission. Imaging variables, including maximum aortic diameter (outer-to-outer), extent of dissection, and false lumen status, were extracted from radiology reports and reviewed using a dedicated imaging workstation (Centricity Universal Viewer, GE Healthcare). These imaging findings were used to define the study population and characterize anatomical features of AAS.

Delirium was ascertained retrospectively from routinely documented nursing and physician records during hospitalization. Delirium screening in both intensive care unit (ICU) and general ward settings was based on documentation of the Confusion Assessment Method–Intensive Care Unit (CAM-ICU) performed as part of standard clinical care. In the ICU, CAM-ICU assessments were commonly documented at regular nursing intervals (typically every 8 h), whereas in general ward settings, assessments were recorded less frequently, usually once per nursing shift or in response to observed changes in mental status. For postoperative patients, delirium assessments were documented beginning in the postoperative recovery unit and continued during the early postoperative period (up to postoperative day 5) when available. The number and timing of recorded assessments therefore varied across patients according to care setting and length of stay, reflecting routine clinical practice rather than a predefined study protocol.

CAM-ICU documentation included assessment of: (1) acute onset or fluctuating course of mental status, (2) inattention, (3) altered level of consciousness, and (4) disorganized thinking. Delirium was considered present when the medical record indicated fulfillment of established CAM-ICU diagnostic criteria (features 1 and 2, or features 3 and 4). When delirium was recorded by nursing staff, confirmation was documented in physician notes by two attending clinicians (most commonly intensivists or cardiologists) based on clinical evaluation and standardized diagnostic criteria. Only delirium episodes documented in the medical record were included in the analysis.

In this study, sedatives, including midazolam and dexmedetomidine, were administered per standardized hospital protocols, ensuring slow intravenous infusion at recommended doses. Propofol was used as an adjunct in cases where adequate sedation was not achieved. Diazepam and clonazepam were rarely utilized. Given the variability in sedation needs and patient responses, strict monitoring of infusion rates and cumulative dosages was maintained. In this study, sedation was operationally defined as any documented administration of intravenous sedative agents during hospitalization, regardless of whether given as a single bolus dose or continuous infusion. Analgesics were administered according to established protocols. The preferred analgesics were butorphanol and fentanyl, while tramadol or morphine were used as supplemental options. The use of tramadol or morphine alone was uncommon. Pain management strategies were tailored to patient needs, ensuring adequate analgesia while minimizing the risk of excessive sedation and associated delirium. Sodium nitroprusside was administered intravenously for hypertensive crisis in AAS, with an initial dose of 0.25–0.5 µg/kg/min, titrated based on patient response to maintain systolic blood pressure below 120 mmHg. To mitigate the risk of hemodynamic instability, blood pressure was continuously monitored, and adjustments were made to prevent excessive hypotension.

Study variables were predefined and grouped into the following categories in accordance with the study objectives: (1) Demographic characteristics and comorbidities, including age, sex, smoking and drinking history, and chronic medical conditions; (2) Preoperative medications and laboratory measurements, obtained from admission records; (3) Imaging characteristics, including aortic diameter, dissection extent, and false lumen status; (4) Sedative and analgesic exposure, defined as documented administration of any sedative or opioid analgesic during hospitalization; and (5) Delirium-related variables and outcomes, including delirium occurrence and associated clinical outcomes.

Follow-up data were obtained retrospectively as part of routine clinical care rather than through a predefined prospective protocol. Information on post-discharge outcomes, including survival status, re-intervention, stroke, and readmission, was extracted from outpatient clinic records and documented telephone follow-up notes when available. Follow-up assessments were typically recorded at approximately 1, 3, 6, and 12 months after discharge and annually thereafter, reflecting standard institutional practice.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes of this study were (1) the incidence of delirium during index hospitalization and (2) early clinical outcomes directly related to the index admission.

Early outcomes included in-hospital mortality, length of hospital stay (LOS), and prolonged ICU stay. Prolonged ICU stay was defined as an ICU length of stay exceeding 3 days following the index procedure or admission.

Secondary outcomes included short-term and long-term mortality and other follow-up events. Short-term mortality was defined as death occurring within 30 days of diagnosis, whereas long-term outcomes included all-cause mortality during follow-up, as well as re-intervention, stroke, and readmission.

All mortality outcomes were explicitly analyzed according to their respective timeframes (in-hospital, 30-day, and follow-up) to ensure temporal clarity and alignment with the retrospective study design.

Statistical analysis

Missing data were handled using a complete-case analysis approach. Patients with missing key baseline or operative data required for delirium assessment were excluded at the screening stage according to predefined exclusion criteria. For the included cohort, partial missingness in laboratory measurements and follow-up outcomes was limited and no imputation techniques were applied. Analyses for each variable were conducted using available data only.

Candidate variables for univariable logistic regression analysis were selected a priori based on clinical relevance, previous evidence regarding delirium in acute cardiovascular and aortic conditions, and data completeness. These variables included demographic characteristics, comorbidities, laboratory indices reflecting inflammatory and coagulation status, imaging features of AAS, treatment modality, and exposure to sedatives, analgesics, and antihypertensive agents.

Variables demonstrating an association with delirium at P < 0.10 in univariable analysis were subsequently entered into multivariable logistic regression models to identify independent predictors, using a forward stepwise selection method.

The cumulative follow-up mortality rate was depicted using Kaplan–Meier curves and compared using the log-rank test. Logistic and Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were used to explore the association between delirium and early and follow-up outcomes. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals, and no violations were detected. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0.

Results

Clinical characteristics

A total of 594 consecutive patients with suspected AAS were initially screened during the study period. Among these, 2 patients were diagnosed with thoracic aortic aneurysm and were excluded because they did not meet the diagnostic criteria for AAS. No patients were excluded due to pre-existing dementia or cognitive impairment, and no cases were excluded because of missing baseline or operative data. Consequently, 592 patients met all inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis. The study flow was shown in Fig. 1.The study included 592 patients with AAS (444 males, 75.0%) with mean age of 60.2 ± 12.0 years. Hypertension was identified in 389 (65.7%) of these patients, making it the most prevalent comorbidity. Among Stanford type A AAS patients (256 cases), 205 (80.1%) underwent surgery, with 1 undergoing TEVAR and another undergoing TEVAR concomitant with a chimney procedure. Surgical procedures included ascending aorta replacement, Bentall operation, and Sun’s operation. Among Stanford type B AAS patients (336 cases), 7 (2.1%) underwent surgery, 97 (28.9%) underwent TEVAR, and 42 (12.5%) underwent hybrid surgery (TEVAR + supra-arch bypass). Delirium was confirmed in 187 (31.6%) patients. Comprehensive baseline characteristics are outlined in Table 1.

Predictors of delirium

For univariable logistic analysis, type A aortic dissection (TAAD), D-dimer levels, white blood cell count, involvement of arch vessels, ICU stay, surgery, and the use of sedatives (including diazepam, clonazepam, midazolam, dexmedetomidine, propofol), analgesics (including tramadol, butorphanol, morphine, remifentanil, and fentanyl), or sodium nitroprusside were significant predictors of delirium (Tables 1 and 2). After adjusting for confounding factors, the use of sedatives (OR = 16.490, 95%CI = 8.981–30.279, P < 0.001) and sodium nitroprusside (OR = 2.676, 95%CI = 1.729–4.142, P < 0.001) remained risk factors for delirium (Table 3). ROC revealed that final model incorporating use of sedatives had moderate predictive value for delirium (AUC = 0.765, 95%CI = 0.726–0.805; Table 4; Fig. 2).

Delirium and early outcomes

After discharge, 34 patients were lost to follow-up, including 9 with delirium and 25 without delirium. As illustrated in Table 5., patients with delirium experienced significantly longer hospital stays, extended ICU stays, higher AKI rates, and increased hospital costs (P < 0.05). However, they had similar rates of 30-day mortality, spinal cord ischemia, stroke, limb ischemia, and re-intervention compared to those without delirium (P > 0.05 for each). Multivariable logistic regression analysis indicated that delirium was an independent predictor of LOS (OR = 1.101, 95%CI = 1.023–1.185, P < 0.05) and extended ICU stay (OR = 3.073, 95% CI = 1.744–5.415, P < 0.001; Table 6).

Delirium and follow-up survival

Median follow-up duration was 37.0 months (IQR, 21.0–57.0 months). During follow-up, 43 (7.7%) patients died post-procedure, including 17 (9.8%) from the delirium group and 26 (6.8%) from the non-delirium group (P < 0.001) (Table 5). Cumulative survival rates at 1 and 2 years were 93.7% and 92.5% for patients with delirium, compared to 97.9% and 96.1% for those without delirium. Cumulative follow-up mortality was similar between patients with and without delirium (log-rank = 1.585; P = 0.208) (Fig. 3). Likewise, the rates of re-intervention, stroke and readmission were comparable between patients with and without delirium.

Discussion

The study revealed a significant incidence of delirium among patients with AAS, with 31.6% (187 out of 592) patients developing delirium. This high incidence underscores the critical nature of delirium as a postoperative complication in AAS patients. The variation in delirium incidence (10% to 50%) reported in previous studies highlights the complex interplay of patient demographics, comorbidities, and perioperative factors [4–6, 9]. Our findings align with the higher end of this spectrum, emphasizing the necessity for vigilant monitoring and management strategies in this patient cohort.

Univariable logistic regression analysis identified several significant predictors of delirium in AAS patients. These included the presence of TAAD, elevated D-dimer levels, increased white blood cell count, involvement of arch vessels, prolonged ICU stay, surgical intervention, and the use of specific medications (sedatives and sodium nitroprusside). Each of these factors contributes uniquely to the risk profile, with surgical and medication-related variables standing out prominently. The increased risk of delirium associated with TAAD, elevated D-dimer and white blood cell counts, and arch vessel involvement likely reflects greater disease severity, heightened inflammatory and coagulopathic responses, and increased surgical complexity with potential effects on cerebral perfusion [10–12]. After adjusting for confounding factors, the multivariable logistic regression highlighted the use of sedatives (OR = 16.490) and sodium nitroprusside (OR = 2.676) as independent risk factors for delirium. The high odds ratio associated with sedative use underscores their profound impact on cognitive function, which is consistent with their pharmacological effects on the central nervous system [13]. Sedatives, essential for managing anxiety and agitation, significantly increase the risk of delirium [14]. The balance between their therapeutic benefits and potential adverse effects requires careful consideration. Sodium nitroprusside, often used for blood pressure control in the perioperative period, its association with delirium may reflect its effects on cerebral perfusion and overall hemodynamic stability [15]. In the final predictive model, primarily driven by sedative use, demonstrated a moderate predictive value for delirium (AUC = 0.765). This model offers a practical tool for clinicians to identify high-risk patients and implement preventive strategies. However, the moderate AUC value indicates room for improvement, suggesting the need for integrating additional predictors or employing more sophisticated modeling techniques to enhance predictive accuracy. In our study, delirium significantly impacted early postoperative outcomes, particularly hospital and ICU length of stay. Patients with delirium had prolonged hospital stays and extended ICU stays [16], with multivariable analysis confirming delirium as an independent predictor for both. However, the administration of sedatives and sodium nitroprusside follows complex clinical protocols, and it remains challenging to determine whether these agents directly induce delirium or serve as markers of underlying hemodynamic instability. Future studies should aim to stratify delirium risk based on sedative dosages, infusion durations, and patient-specific hemodynamic responses.

In the management of AAS, prompt and effective blood pressure reduction is essential to limit aortic wall stress and prevent disease progression. In our cohort, sodium nitroprusside was the primary intravenous antihypertensive agent used for hypertensive crisis, reflecting its rapid onset of action, short half-life, and ability to achieve precise blood pressure control with a relatively lower incidence of reflex tachycardia compared with other vasodilators. A small number of patients received alternative intravenous antihypertensive agents, including nitroglycerin or urapidil, administered via continuous infusion either as substitutes for or adjuncts to sodium nitroprusside; however, no apparent association between these agents and delirium was observed, and formal statistical analysis was not feasible due to the limited number of exposed patients. Although sodium nitroprusside emerged as an independent predictor of delirium in the multivariable analysis, its use must be interpreted within the broader clinical context of hypertensive crisis in acute aortic syndrome. Severe hypertension reflects heightened sympathetic activation, pain, anxiety, and systemic stress, all of which may independently predispose patients to delirium. Moreover, despite continuous monitoring and careful titration, the rapid pharmacodynamic profile of sodium nitroprusside may result in transient hypotension or rapid blood pressure fluctuations, potentially compromising cerebral perfusion and increasing delirium risk. Therefore, sodium nitroprusside may function not only as a direct pharmacological contributor but also as a surrogate marker of hemodynamic instability and disease severity. Importantly, during routine clinical practice, we consistently observed delirium-related manifestations, such as agitation, restlessness, and disorganized speech, occurring shortly after initiation of nitroprusside infusion, with symptom improvement following drug discontinuation, which prompted its specific emphasis in this study. Nevertheless, given the retrospective design and the absence of detailed blood pressure variability metrics, residual confounding related to hypertensive crisis severity and dynamic hemodynamic changes cannot be fully excluded. Future prospective studies incorporating continuous hemodynamic monitoring and standardized indices of hypertension severity are warranted to clarify the causal pathways linking blood pressure management strategies and delirium in AAS patients.

While sedative use and sodium nitroprusside administration were identified as independent risk factors, their inclusion alone does not fully capture the multifactorial nature of delirium development. Future research should explore additional variables, such as systemic inflammatory markers, intraoperative hemodynamic fluctuations, and pre-existing cognitive impairment, to refine delirium prediction models. Machine learning approaches integrating multimodal perioperative data may enhance predictive capabilities and assist in early risk stratification.

Delirium was associated with higher rates of postoperative AKI and increased hospital costs, highlighting its broader relationship with patient morbidity and healthcare resource utilization. However, this association should be interpreted with caution, as patients who develop delirium are often more critically ill at baseline, with greater physiological derangement and procedural complexity, which may independently predispose them to AKI and increased healthcare expenditures. In this context, factors such as hemodynamic instability, systemic inflammation, and exposure to potentially nephrotoxic or sedative medications may reflect underlying illness severity rather than direct causal pathways linking delirium to renal dysfunction [17]. Similarly, the increased hospital costs observed in delirious patients are likely driven by prolonged length of stay, higher complication burden, and the need for intensified monitoring and supportive care [18]. Future studies incorporating validated severity-of-illness or surgical risk scores (e.g., APACHE II, SOFA, or EuroSCORE II) would enable more robust adjustment for baseline risk and help clarify whether delirium represents an independent contributor to adverse outcomes or a marker of overall disease severity.

First, as a single-center retrospective observational study, the findings may be subject to selection bias and may not be fully generalizable to other institutions or healthcare systems. Second, although delirium assessment was routinely performed using the CAM-ICU by trained nursing staff, underrecognition of hypoactive delirium cannot be completely excluded. Third, detailed quantitative data on perioperative medication exposure, including the proportion of patients receiving individual sedatives or antihypertensive agents, cumulative doses, infusion duration, and temporal relationships with delirium onset, were not consistently available in this retrospective cohort, precluding dose–response or duration-dependent analyses and limiting further exploration of medication-specific effects. In addition, despite multivariable adjustment, residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled out, and the predictive model requires external validation. Future multicenter prospective studies with standardized delirium assessment and granular perioperative medication recording are warranted to confirm and extend our findings.

Conclusions

Our findings indicate that delirium in AAS patients is associated with increased ICU and hospital stays, underscoring the importance of identifying high-risk patients early. The use of sedatives and sodium nitroprusside were revealed as risk factors for delirium. Future studies should focus on developing and validating tailored risk stratification methods and preventive strategies to reduce delirium incidence.
