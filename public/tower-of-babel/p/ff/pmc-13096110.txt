Pilot degradation study of gamma irradiated ciprofloxacin in its primary packaging material for space pharmaceutical applications
Authors: Manali Patel, Priti Mehta, Saif Khan, Birendra Singh, Haidar Abbas Masi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ensuring the stability of pharmaceuticals is crucial for astronaut health and mission success during long-duration spaceflight. Space radiation can penetrate spacecraft shielding and compromise the stability of drugs, yet literature on such effects remains limited. Ground-based simulations thus offer critical insights about how pharmaceuticals behave in extreme space environment. The present preliminary comparative study investigated the impact of gamma and photolytic irradiation on the stability of ciprofloxacin, a key antibiotic in the ISS formulary. The study was carried out on ciprofloxacin API solutions, solid API, and both solid and liquid formulations, with and without primary packaging material. The results of physical evaluation showed dose-dependent color changes from colorless to brown in API solutions (100 ppm and 3,000 ppm) as well as liquid formulations without primary packaging material. The XRD analysis of API powder revealed loss of crystallinity at 400 Gy gamma dose. Chemical stability of each irradiated sample checked using high performance liquid chromatographic method. A comparative degradation profile of photo irradiated and gamma irradiated samples of ciprofloxacin revealed the significant difference. Degradation products, CIP1 and CIP2 were observed only in gamma irradiated samples while CIP6 was observed after photo irradiation. The identification of degradation products was carried out using LC–MS/MS and based on their fragmentation patterns, plausible degradation pathways were proposed. This is the first report to show that the primary packaging material used for ciprofloxacin eye/ear drops could not control the degradation on exposure to gamma rays.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-37307-2.

Introduction

Different space agencies and their global partners are preparing for extended human space exploration missions beyond low Earth orbit (LEO), such as round-trip voyages to Mars lasting 2–3 years1. However, these deep space missions pose increased health risks to crews due to prolonged exposure to space environmental hazards. Maintaining crew health during space travel is essential, and medicine plays a vital role in it2. Medicine has a defined shelf life on Earth but in the harsh environments of space, it degrades much faster3. Currently, medicines are resupplied for short-duration space missions. However, for long-duration space missions, such resupply won’t be feasible. Therefore, it’s crucial to evaluate the stability of medicines for use in space to ensure their effectiveness over extended periods4.

Du et al. reported increased instability in 35 drugs stored for 28 months aboard the International Space Station (ISS)5. Corey et al. assessed repackaged medications like amoxicillin and ibuprofen at the ISS, showing faster degradation than on Earth. Wotring and Khan retested Du et al.’s samples confirming increased degradation, especially in drugs sensitive to radiation and repackaging1,6. Choung et al. assessed the stability of vitamin B complex formulations after four months storage on the ISS, comparing flight samples and ground controls. One formulation remained stable while other exhibited discrepancies in vitamin B1 strength7. A study from Wotring et al. showed only one medicine among nine evaluated at ISS, did not met the United States Pharmacopeia (USP) guidelines for potency, with no unusual degradation products found. This indicates the potential extended shelf life of certain drugs under spaceflight conditions8. A study by Daniel et al. at National Aeronautics and Space Administration Space Radiation Laboratory (NASA-SRL) evaluated the effects of proton and iron radiation (0.1–50 Gy) on the stability of drugs such as promethazine and clavulanate. The results showed degradation observed at lower radiation dose of 10 Gy, indicating importance of evaluating drug stability across different radiation doses9,10. Collectively, the findings indicate that prolonged exposure to space radiation and repackaging of medication may alter the drug stability and reduce the potency of drugs.

Tran et al. published a report related to the stability of ibuprofen in its tablet formulations. Ibuprofen tablets were exposed to space radiation inside and outside of ISS. The results indicated that inside the ISS, tablets remained stable in the presence of iron oxide. However, significant degradation (30–50%) was observed after exposure of tablets to the space environment outside ISS. In simulated radiation studies, tablets with excipient resisted degradation, while pure ibuprofen degraded rapidly, highlighting the critical role of excipient in formulation to ensure space drug stability11.

Plante et al. exposed solid oral formulations of acetaminophen, amoxicillin, ibuprofen, and promethazine in their packaging material to simulated galactic cosmic radiation (0.5 Gy and 1.0 Gy) for three years at the NASA-SRL. The results showed that Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient (API) content in both irradiated and control drugs gradually declined over time, yet consistently remained within the USP accepted potency range. Impurities increased progressively with storage time (notably in acetaminophen, ibuprofen, and amoxicillin) in both control and exposed samples with similar degradation profile. Minor and time dependent dissolution changes were reported with slightly faster release in promethazine and slower release in amoxicillin, but irradiation had no significant effect12.

Both the study performed using solid oral formulations. However, ISS formulary consists of a considerable number of liquid formulations, which are inherently more susceptible to degradation compared to solid dosage forms due to the presence of water molecules. Therefore, it is important to perform a stability study of liquid formulations in its packaging material for long term missions to the Moon or Mars.

To assess the quality of pharmaceuticals before market authorization, regulatory bodies such as the ICH, WHO, and FDA provide well established guidelines13. However, there are no established guidelines and policies for the evaluation of space pharmaceuticals to be stored and used during space travel.

The stability of pharmaceuticals is a critical aspect directly impacting a drug’s shelf-life and its ability to deliver the intended efficacy. A stable drug must retain its physical and chemical integrity throughout its intended storage period7. Numerous factors influence pharmaceutical stability, including environmental conditions such as temperature, humidity, radiation, and oxygen, as well as product-related factors such as excipients, manufacturing processes, dosage forms, and packaging materials14.

Recently, NASA addressed this gap by publishing a framework outlining data requirements for drug stability classification in space. This framework emphasizes critical factors like curated data on drug chemistry, manufacturer reported shelf life, degradation pathways, radiolytic sensitivity, and packaging or repackaging conditions. The report states that the NASA-SRL facility is available for evaluating the radiolytic sensitivity of pharmaceuticals. According to the framework, radiolytic sensitive liquids/semi-solids, medications with short shelf lives and repackaged solid dosage required special attention6. To better understand drug degradation caused by ionizing radiation in space, it is essential to compare it with photo radiation conducted under controlled terrestrial conditions on Earth as per ICH Q1(B) guidelines15.

The space radiation environment is a complex and dynamic system comprising various types of non-ionizing and ionizing radiation, including solar rays, cosmic rays, and trapped radiation16. Solar rays primarily originate from the Sun and consist of electromagnetic radiation, such as UV light, X-rays, and gamma rays, as well as energetic particles, such as protons and electrons, emitted during solar flares and coronal mass ejections17. Cosmic rays, on the other hand, are high-energy particles originating from outside the solar system, these particles include protons and heavy ions. Trapped radiation refers to charged particles, primarily electrons and protons, trapped within Earth’s magnetic field in the Van Allen radiation belts. Space radiation interacts with the shielding material of spacecraft to create a variety of secondary particles, such as gamma rays, X-rays, heavy ion bremsstrahlung, neutrons, and proton recoils. That creates a complex environment inside the spacecraft which differs significantly from Earth18.

Due to its high penetrability, gamma radiation may interact with pharmaceuticals and generate free radicals so, it essential to assess the stability of medicines in its presence. Real-time stability studies in space face hurdles like time, cost, limited storage, and sample retrieval difficulties. Therefore, Simulated space radiation terrestrial studies are important to understand the impact of radiation on the stability of pharmaceuticals to predict the shelf life of space pharmaceuticals19,20.

Ciprofloxacin (CIP) is an antibacterial agent belonging to the fluoroquinolone class which is used for bacterial infections21. As per the NASA report of ISS formulary, it is reported that CIP is widely used space medicine in the form of tablets as well as liquid formulations such as eye/ear drops during space travel1. It is a photolabile drug and prone to degrade in the presence of UV-visible light22,23. Based on these considerations, the objective of this study was to evaluate the impact of different doses of gamma radiation on the stability of selected CIP API and its liquid formulation, both with and without primary packaging material. In addition, the study aimed to compare degradation profile generated by gamma radiation and UV-visible radiation.

After irradiation, physical evaluation of color and clarity of solutions was carried out, while X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was used to assess changes in crystallinity and lattice structure of the solid API. Chemical stability was evaluated using High Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC), and mass spectroscopy (LC–MS/MS) for detailed identification of degradation products. In addition, in silico toxicity prediction was performed to assess the safety relevance of the degradation products, ensuring a comprehensive evaluation of ciprofloxacin after photo and gamma irradiation.

Experimental work

The procedures for sample preparation, gamma irradiation, photo irradiation and HPLC were performed as previously reported in Patel et al.24.

Materials

The reference standard of CIP (purity ≥ 99.98%) was procured from Yarrow Chem, Mumbai, India. Four different marketed formulations were procured from local pharmacies, including two liquid preparations (FORM1 and FORM2; 0.3% w/v eye/ear drops of different brands) and two solid formulations (FORM3 and FORM4; 500 mg tablets of different brands).

HPLC-grade methanol, acetonitrile, orthophosphoric acid, trimethylamine, and Milli-Q water were purchased from Merck, Mumbai, India. PVDF membrane filters and PVDF syringe filters with a pore size of 0.45 μm were procured from Millipore Ltd, Bangalore, India.

Methods

Sample preparation

For control, all samples were stored in glass bottles wrapped in aluminum foil and kept in a stability chamber at 25 °C/45% relative humidity (RH).

Aqueous API solutions of CIP (100 ppm and 3,000 ppm), solid CIP (100 mg powder), were used for the radiation exposure study. All procured marketed formulations of CIP are exposed to radiation with and without primary packaging.

Gamma degradation study

All prepared samples were exposed to gamma radiation at doses 25, 50, 100, 400, and 800 Gy using a Cobalt-60 source at the Inter-University Accelerator Centre (IUAC), New Delhi with the irradiation rate of 1.576 kGy/hr, energy level of 1.2 MeV and at 18 °C temperature. Accordingly, the exposure durations were 1–30 min for 25–800 Gy gamma radiation doses.

The gamma irradiation doses (25, 50, 100, 400, and 800 Gy) were selected to check dose dependent degradation of CIP in various selected matrix according to feasibility of radiation exposure facility.

Photo degradation study

According to ICH Q1(B) guideline, a photostability study was conducted using a photostability chamber (Thermolab Scientific Equipment, Mumbai, India). All control samples were stored in glass bottles wrapped with aluminum foil. For the exposure, API solutions, API powder and all formulations without packaging material were placed in an uncovered quartz crucible kept inside the photostability chamber. The formulations with primary packaging material were kept as such in photo stability chamber for exposure. The samples underwent simultaneous exposure to 200 W*hr/m2 UV radiation and 1.2 million lux hours of visible radiation at 25 °C temperature.

Physical evaluation

The physical attributes of each sample were evaluated for its color and the clarity of the solution.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

XRD patterns were recorded for ciprofloxacin control (unexposed), photo irradiated, and gamma irradiated solid API samples at doses of 400 Gy and 800 Gy. The evaluation was carried out using a Bruker AXS D8 Focus P-XRD and a Bruker AXS D8 VENTURE SC-XRD system. A Ni-filtered CuK (α) radiation source was employed, operating at a voltage of 40 kV and current of 400 mA. Scans were performed at a rate of 0.5 s per step, over a 2θ range of 5° to 30°, with a step size of 0.049° and a time constant of 1.0 s.

High performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)

All samples were exposed in duplicate sets to both photolytic and gamma radiation. All Control and exposed samples were stored and analyzed within 2 weeks of radiation exposure.

The chemical stability of each sample was evaluated through percentage degradation analysis and impurity profiling utilizing the 1260 Infinity Quaternary HPLC system from Agilent Technologies. The system was equipped with a 100 µL capacity loop autosampler and is integrated with OpenLAB CDS EZChrom software (version A.01.03).

For the analysis of CIP samples, the official method outlined in the British Pharmacopoeia (BP, 2018) for related substances was employed. The optimized chromatographic conditions are detailed in Table 1. All control and exposed samples were diluted with water to get the appropriate concentration for HPLC analysis.

Mass spectroscopy (LC–MS/MS)

The chromatographic conditions used for separating the drug and its degradation products were adapted from Van Doorslaer et al. (Van Doorslaer et al., 2011). Sample separation was carried out using an Agilent 1290 Infinity UHPLC system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), comprising a degasser, thermostatic autosampler, gradient pump, column oven, and photodiode array (PDA) detector. Chromatographic separation was achieved on a Phenomenex C18 column (150 mm × 4.5 mm, 5 μm). The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water and in acetonitrile at isocratic ratio of 87.5:12.5%v/v with a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. UV detection was performed at 278 nm, with the column temperature of 30 °C and an injection volume of 10 µL.

The MS/MS analysis was performed using an AdvanceBio LC6545XT quadrupole time-of-flight (QTOF) mass spectrometer equipped with a Jet Stream electrospray ionization (ESI) source (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Detection was carried out in positive ESI mode under the following optimized conditions: capillary voltage 3.5 kV, drying gas temperature 325 °C, drying gas flow 13 L/min, sheath gas temperature 350 °C, sheath gas flow 11 L/min, nebulizer pressure 35 psi, nozzle voltage 1000 V, fragmentor voltage 150 V, skimmer voltage 65 V, and octopole RF peak voltage 750 V. Argon gas was used for collision-induced dissociation (CID), with collision energy set in the range of 20–40 eV. Data were acquired in centroid mode, scanning from m/z 100–500 for MS and m/z 50–500 for MS/MS at a rate of 4 spectra per second. The system was calibrated using Agilent TOF reference mass solutions, and data processing was performed using Agilent MassHunter software (version 10.0 service release 1).

Results

Physical evaluation

The samples were evaluated within two days of exposure for physical assessment.

The aqueous solutions of CIP API and its liquid formulations without packaging, showed a color change from colorless to light brown upon exposure to both photolytic and gamma radiation. At radiation doses of 100 Gy and below, the 3,000 ppm CIP API solutions exhibited a visible color change, whereas the 100 ppm CIP API solutions showed no observable change.Analysis of photo exposed samples revealed that both 100 ppm and 3,000 ppm CIP API solutions exhibited nearly similar color changes, whereas the liquid formulations of the same concentration showed greater color intensity (Fig. 1).

No color change was observed in solid CIP API and all CIP formulations i.e., eye/ear drops and tablets with packaging material when exposed to gamma and photolytic radiation. Additionally, FORM1 & FORM2, when exposed to 25 Gy and 50 Gy gamma radiation dose, samples exhibited turbidity which disappeared at higher radiation doses. The results of physical evaluation indicate a dose and concentration dependent changes in color intensity of CIP API solutions subjected to gamma radiation.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

XRD diffraction pattern illustrates significant changes in the crystallinity of CIP API upon exposure to photo and gamma irradiation (Fig. 2). The non-irradiated CIP API and photo irradiated sample exhibit sharp, intense peaks, indicating a well-defined crystalline structure.

400 Gy gamma irradiated CIP API samples showed absence of characteristic peaks at 2θ values, indicating a substantial loss of crystallinity (Table 2). In the 800 Gy irradiated samples, only two diffraction peaks were observed, with significantly altered intensities, suggesting extensive physical structure degradation. The strongest peak for all samples appears near 2θ ≈ 26.3°, but its intensity varies drastically across treatments, indicating the impact of different radiation on the physical structure of CIP.

A notable decrease in peak intensity and sharpness indicates a transition towards an amorphous state. The progressive decrease in diffraction intensity and peak resolution in these samples is indicative of radiation induced disruption of the crystalline lattice.

Chemical stability of CIP

All irradiated and control samples were analyzed by HPLC to evaluate the impact of radiation on the chemical stability of CIP API and its formulations. Furthermore, the chromatographic profiles of photodegraded CIP API and formulations were compared with gamma irradiated samples for the identification of degradation products generated by gamma irradiation.

As illustrated in Fig. 3, the chromatographic peak of CIP appeared at a retention time (Rt) 8.7 ± 0.5 min which is well separated from all generated degradation products after gamma irradiation and photo irradiation in samples. The control API samples and formulations, as well as the photo and gamma exposed solid API samples and formulations did not exhibit any impurities (Supplementary material Figs. 1 & 2).

Almost same percentage of degradation was observed for photo exposed and 400 Gy gamma exposed CIP samples. However, the generated degradation products shown differences in both samples at any given concentration (Fig. 3). Detailed information about degradation percentage, and generated degradation products (CIP1-CIP8) along with its relative retention time (RRT) is shown in Table 3. All degradation products below 0.05% were considered only for reporting and not for identification.

When the degradation profile of 400 Gy gamma irradiated 100 ppm CIP API sample was compared with a photo exposed sample, it was observed that CIP1(0.48%), and CIP2(3.39%) were present in gamma irradiated samples but not in photo exposed samples. CIP6(0.45%) was not present in gamma irradiated samples but observed in photo exposed samples. All other degradation products (CIP3-CIP5, CIP7, and CIP8) were present under both conditions, although their relative percentages varied between gamma irradiated and photo irradiated samples. CIP3 consistently appeared in all photo exposed API samples (100 ppm & 3,000 ppm) and FORM1 and FORM2 without packaging, and was also unexpectedly detected in 400 Gy & 800 Gy gamma irradiated FORM1 and FORM2 without packaging, however, in all cases its level remained below the identification limit of 0.05%. The common degradation products CIP5 and CIP8 were present at 10.6% and 6.18% respectively in photo irradiated samples, however, their levels were reduced to 0.04% and 0.06%, respectively in gamma irradiated samples. (Fig. 3A). As shown in Fig. 4, FORM1 and FORM2 with primary packaging material, also exhibited dose dependent degradation when exposed to different doses of gamma radiation.

When a degradation profile of 3,000 ppm CIP API sample was compared, it was observed that CIP2(0.05%) was present in 400 Gy gamma irradiated samples but not in photo exposed samples. CIP5(0.03%) was absent in the gamma irradiated sample but present in the photo exposed sample.

As shown in Fig. 3(B-D) a comparative degradation profile of gamma irradiated samples of 3,000 ppm CIP API sample and CIP formulations (FORM1 & FORM2), it was observed that CIP5 was absent in the API solution but appeared in the FORM1 &FORM2 when exposed without packaging. However, the percentage of CIP5 is very low (0.09%) in exposed CIP liquid formulations. A total of five degradation products (CIP2-CIP5, CIP7) were observed in both FORM1 and FORM2 on gamma irradiation and photolytic irradiation without packaging. CIP7 was identified as the prominent peak in all gamma and photolytically irradiated samples, at any given concentration.

As shown in Table 3, FORM1 and FORM2 without primary packaging showed a slightly higher percentage of degradation compared to the same concentration of API (3,000 ppm) when exposed to photo and gamma radiation. This might be due to the presence of excipients. Surprisingly, when FORM1 and FORM2 were exposed to photo and gamma radiation in primary packaging material, no degradation observed in photo exposed samples while five similar degradation products (CIP2, CIP5, CIP7) were observed in gamma irradiated samples (Fig. 3E&F).

Mass spectroscopy

The degradation products were obtained in very small amounts and could not be isolated in pure form therefore, degradation products were characterized using mass spectroscopy. The mass spectral analysis was carried out in a positive mode using electrospray ionization. Seven of the eight degradation products were identified using LC–MS-QTOF. CIP3 is observed in photo degraded samples, but due to its very low concentration (0.04%), it cannot be reliably identified and therefore excluded from mass reporting. The chemical structures of each degradation product were predicted by comparing its fragmentation patterns to those of the parent CIP. The mass spectra of CIP API and its degradation products are presented in Fig. 5 The overall degradation pathway of ciprofloxacin is illustrated in Fig. 6, while the detailed fragmentation pathways of individual degradation products are provided in supplementary Fig. 3. The Table 4. illustrates expected chemical structures of the CIP degradation products along with their RRT and elemental composition.

The mass spectrum of CIP showed a protonated molecule peak [M + H]1+ at a mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) 332.1402. The MS/MS spectrum of the precursor ion (m/z 332.1402) displayed characteristic product ions at m/z 314.1303, 288.1508, 268.1441, 245.1084, 231.0932, and 203.0612. The fragment at m/z 314.1303 was produced by loss of a water molecule (–H₂O, 18 Da) from the carboxylic acid. Subsequent loss of carbon monoxide (CO, 28 Da) along with the addition of molecular hydrogen (H₂, 2 Da) generates the fragment at m/z 288.1508. Further fragmentation involved the loss of an ethylamine group (C₂H₅N, 43 Da) from m/z 288.1508 to yield the fragment at m/z 245.1084. Loss of a methylene unit (CH₂, 14 Da) from this ion resulted in the formation of the fragment at m/z 231.093225,26.

Mass spectra of CIP1 showed a protonated molecule peak [M + H]1+ at m/z 348.1358, corresponding to a monohydroxylated derivative generated due to gamma radiation. This 16 Da mass increase is consistent with oxygen radical (O−) substitution on the electron-rich piperazine ring of CIP, a well-established mechanism under photocatalytic or radiolytic oxidative stress. The MS/MS spectrum of the precursor ion displayed characteristic product ions at m/z 330.1257 (loss of H₂O), 304.1094, 262.0751, 247.0881, and 219.0933 reflecting sequential dehydration, piperazine ring cleavage, and further cyclopropane degradation27–29.

Subsequent oxidation of CIP1 leads to the replacement of the fluorine atom (F, 19 Da) on the quinolone core with a hydroxyl group (OH, 17 Da), yielding a net mass decrease of 2 Da and resulting in the formation of CIP8 at m/z 346.1359. The MS/MS spectrum of the precursor ion displayed characteristic product ions at m/z 328.1291, 300.1347, 259.0714, and 245.0559 similar fragmentation pattern as CIP1. This transformation aligns with a nucleophilic aromatic substitution mechanism, enabled by the electron-deficient nature of the fluorinated quinolone ring29,30. Further, CIP4 was generated from CIP8 by the elimination of one hydroxyl group, most likely from the piperazine ring, via dehydration or further oxidation. This transformation, involving the loss of 16 Da, results in a mono-hydroxylated, defluorinated CIP derivative at m/z 330.1448. The MS/MS spectrum revealed prominent fragment ions at m/z 312.1342, 286.1551, 269.0916, 243.1130, generated due to progressive breakdown of the piperazine ring22,30–32. The fragmentation pattern aligns well with CIP1 and CIP8, particularly following dehydration, ring cleavage, and extensive fragmentation of the piperazine moiety.

The degradation pathways differed between photo and gamma irradiation. In the gamma-induced pathway, CIP1 was first formed, followed by the generation of CIP8 and subsequently CIP4. In contrast, during photodegradation, CIP8 was directly generated from CIP API, which then led to the formation of CIP4.

Mass spectra of CIP2 displayed a protonated molecule peak [M + H]1+ at m/z 304.1454, generated from CIP API through the conversion of the carboxylic acid group (–COOH) to a hydroxyl group (–OH) on the quinolone core, a transformation involving decarboxylation followed by hydroxylation. The fragment at m/z 284.1400 was formed by the loss of hydrogen fluoride (HF), followed by the loss of (C₄NH) moiety, resulting in the formation of the ion at m/z 221.0732. Further fragmentation by the loss of a CHNO unit produced the fragment at m/z 178.1226. It demonstrates the sensitivity of the –COOH group to radiation-induced oxidative attack in aqueous environments and supports the structural identification of CIP2 as a decarboxylated ciprofloxacin derivative26,33.

The mass spectrum of CIP6, corresponding to British Pharmacopeial Impurity B, exhibited protonated ion [M + H]1+ at m/z 314.1500, reflecting an 18 Da reduction in mass compared to the parent ciprofloxacin molecule. Direct defluorination causes the loss of a fluorine atom (F, 19 D). In fluoroquinolones, this kind of defluorination occurs when exposed to gamma irradiation or photolytic stress. It occurs when hydroxyl radicals or reactive oxygen species (ROS) attack the quinolone ring’s electron-deficient C–F link, displacing fluorine. The MS/MS spectrum revealed fragment ions at m/z 296.1392, 270.1602, 227.1192, 187.0868, 160.0644, and 87.0434. The fragment at m/z 296.1392 is formed by the loss of a water molecule (H₂O). Subsequent loss of an acetylene group (C₂H₂) yields the ion at m/z 270.1602. Further fragmentation involving the loss of an ethylamine group (C₂H₅N) results in the formation of fragment at m/z 227.1192, following the loss of a cyclopropane unit (C₃H₄) generates the fragment at m/z 187.0860.

The mass spectrum of CIP7 exhibited a protonated molecular ion [M + H]1+ at m/z 306.1249, which is formed due to the removal of a vinyl fragment (C₂H₂, 26 Da) from the piperazine ring side chain. This transformation likely results from oxidative cleavage initiated by hydroxyl radicals or other reactive oxygen species targeting the electron-rich nitrogen centres of the piperazine ring. The attack leads to C–N bond scission and ring opening, producing a linear or partially cleaved side chain, along with structural rearrangement or partial loss of side groups. Subsequent loss of hydrogen fluoride (HF, 20 Da) from this ion produces the fragment at m/z 268.107634,35. Loss of a fluorine atom and addition of a hydrogen atom from CIP7 led to the formation of CIP5, with a protonated molecular ion [M + H]1+ at m/z 288.1343. The MS/MS analysis of DP-5 (m/z 288.1343) revealed fragment ions at m/z 270.1237, resulting from the loss of water (–H₂O, 18 Da). Further loss of an ethylamine group (C₂H₅N) from the piperazine ring side chain generated the fragment at m/z 227.0815. Subsequent removal of carbon monoxide (CO, 28 Da) led to the formation of m/z 199.050028,36. Additionally, CIP5 may also be generated from CIP6 (m/z 314.1500) by the loss of a C₂H₂ moiety from the piperazine ring side chain, indicating the possibility of multiple degradation pathways for CIP5.

In silico toxicity prediction of ciprofloxacin degradation products

The in silico ADMET prediction of CIP and its degradation products was carried out using admetSAR (version 3.0, https://lmmd.ecust.edu.cn/admetsar3/predict.php) computational software. The analysis involved key parameters such as solubility, lipophilicity, hydrogen bonding capacity, blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability, clearance, half-life, and various toxicity endpoints such as respiratory, renal, ocular, dermal, and mutagenic potential, with the findings summarized in Table 5. The in-silico toxicity prediction suggested that ciprofloxacin and most of its degradation products share a broadly similar toxicity profile, with consistently high probability of respiratory toxicity, and moderate risk of nephrotoxicity, while eye, skin, and acute oral toxicities remain negligible. Repeated dose toxicity is predicted to be low for the parent drug and most degradation products, suggesting minimal risk from prolonged exposure. Notably, one degradation product (CIP2) shows a higher probability (53.1%), warranting further investigation. Mutagenicity predictions show variability, with CIP and several degradation products (CIP1, CIP2, CIP3, CIP6, CIP7) testing positive for Ames mutagenesis, suggesting potential mutagenic risk. In contrast, CIP4 and CIP6 show negative predictions with lower probabilities (~ 35–50%), indicating reduced mutagenic potential. These findings align with the known adverse effects of fluoroquinolones28.

Discussion

This study provides a systematic evaluation of the stability of ciprofloxacin under simulated space-relevant radiation conditions using gamma and photolytic irradiation. The findings demonstrate that ciprofloxacin stability is strongly influenced by radiation type, dose, physical state, formulation, and packaging configuration.

Gamma radiation is one of the prominent secondary radiations generated inside spacecraft as a result of interaction between space radiation and the spacecraft’s surface. Due to high penetrability, gamma radiation is capable of affecting pharmaceuticals through direct or indirect ionization9. Numerous articles have discussed the exposure of pharmaceuticals to gamma radiation for the sterilization, typically using a standard dose of 25 kGy over a minimal period37. It was reported that the drug behavior can vary depending on the total duration and dose of radiation. i.e., the degradation profile of the drug observed at a higher dose applied for a fraction of time was different than lower dose applied for a prolonged time. So, it can be extrapolated that changes in dose and dose rate of gamma radiation may have different effects on the stability of drugs. In the proposed Mars mission and Artemis program, the duration of the mission will be 2–3 years, during that time the total dose of gamma radiation will be much lower (approx. ~1 Gy) but for a prolonged period. Reichert et al. also reported that drugs stored for a prolonged period in LEO degrade at a rate that is approximately 1.5-fold greater than the rate of degradation in terrestrial storage3. Therefore, it is essential to evaluate drug stability at different radiation dose and dose-rate.

Very few studies are reported for the stability of CIP after gamma irradiation on Earth. Notably, most gamma degradation studies to date have been conducted in wastewater at very high doses, which are not representative of space radiation conditions. A study carried out on CIP in a solid state exposing it to different doses of 15 kGy to 100 kGy gamma radiation, followed by evaluation using XRD. The XRD pattern indicates the formation of amorphous CIP upon gamma irradiation38. The present study, aligns with these findings and the XRD analysis revealed noticeable changes in the diffraction pattern even at a dose of 400 Gy. Another study carried out on 0.1 mM CIP aqueous solution at various doses of 0 to 8 kGy gamma radiation. The results indicate degradation was observed at lower doses such as 500 Gy and extent of degradation increased as the radiation dose was raised39. The primary objective of the present study was to do comparison of degradation profile generated under gamma irradiation and photolytic irradiation conditions. So, 400 Gy gamma radiation dose was optimized to get approximately same % degradation as photolytic conditions as per ICH Q1B guidelines (23–24% degradation at 100ppm CIP API solution) and same dose is used for irradiation of all selected samples of CIP.

All liquid samples showed dose-dependent degradation after gamma irradiation. The degradation observed in liquid samples, possibly caused by an indirect ionization effect. Due to the interaction of radiation and water molecule, water molecule is hydrolyzed and produces free radicals (H2O·+, ·OH, H3O+, H·, O·, H2, e−) that are stable for a longer period of time9,40. Due to their high reactivity, these free radicals can trigger structural changes, bond cleavage, or oxidative degradation41.

Mass spectroscopic analysis identified a total of seven CIP degradation products. No new impurities were detected, and all observed impurities have been previously reported under different conditions and not specifically in the context of photostability or gamma degradation studies. However, distinct degradation products were observed in the photo irradiated and gamma irradiated samples, indicating irradiation specific degradation pathways.

It is very important to mention about degradation behavior of CIP liquid formulation with and without primary packaging material after radiation exposure. Here, low-density polyethylene (LDPE) plastic was used as a packaging material by the manufacturer in both mentioned CIP liquid formulations. The packaging material effectively protected the formulation from photodegradation, while approximate same percent of degradation observed in the 400 Gy gamma exposed CIP liquid formulation with and without primary packaging material. It indicates that gamma radiation penetrates in used primary packaging material and cannot protect the CIP formulation from degradation. It is also reported that repackaging of pharmaceuticals for spaceflight can further accelerate degradation, underscoring the critical importance of evaluating packaging material under extra-terrestrial environment. Current repackaging practices, which rely on non-protective polymeric zip-lock bags, allow permeation of atmospheric moisture, oxygen, and other gases, thereby accelerating chemical and physical degradation of susceptible medications and increasing the risk of therapeutic failure1. This highlights the need to explore alternative packaging materials for CIP to prevent its degradation.

In-silico toxicity profile for CIP and its degradation products was evaluated but in space, physiological changes caused by microgravity such as fluid redistribution, reduced renal and hepatic function, altered gastrointestinal absorption, and impaired immune responses can significantly impact the ADME profile of drugs42,43. These changes may exacerbate or mitigate the predicted toxicity of CIP and its degradation products. Hence, toxicity prediction of degradation products in terms of real-time space conditions needs to be evaluated.

Conclusion

Ensuring pharmaceutical stability is critical for long duration space missions beyond LEO, where resupply options are limited. Prolonged exposure to space conditions can lead to significant drug degradation, impacting their quality, effectiveness, and safety. On Earth, forced degradation and accelerated stability studies are routinely employed to predict long-term stability, shelf-life and degradation behavior of pharmaceuticals. In a similar manner, controlled exposure to ionizing radiation can be used as a stress condition to anticipate potential radiation-induced effects on drug stability during space missions. Chemical stability assessment of CIP revealed distinct degradation profiles between gamma and photolytic irradiation, confirming that ionizing radiation induces degradation pathways that are not fully predictable from conventional photostability studies. It is also important to note that gamma irradiation can generate secondary radicals, which may contribute to delayed degradation beyond the initial post-irradiation period in liquid samples. Hence, Electron Spin Resonance (ESR) spectroscopy is required to detect and characterize such persistent radicals. The selection of packaging material is critical for maintaining drug stability during storage. In the case of ciprofloxacin, PVDF was used as the primary packaging material which did not provide adequate protection against gamma induced degradation. The present study serves as preliminary work but systematic long term and accelerated stability data generation and development of stability evaluation protocol is essential to predict stability of drugs for long space missions. Such approach may include variations in radiation LET, dose rate, and other space environmental factors.

Additionally, in space missions the effective usability window of pharmaceutical products reduced, as drug payloads are often manufactured and procured well in advance of the actual mission duration. Therefore, future studies should consider experimental work related to selection of chemically inert excipients to minimize API excipient interactions and to reduce the risk of excipient induced degradation. Interactions between pharmaceutical products and packaging materials can lead to the release of extractable and leachable substances into the formulation. Therefore, further evaluation is required to check probable extractable and leachable components from primary packaging material in presence of gamma radiation. To improve the stability of liquid formulations, alternative approaches such as reformulation with stabilizing excipients, conversion to concentrated or reconstitute systems, and repackaging into more protective containers can be one of the approaches. These strategies may enable the development of more stable or ready-to-use liquid formulations suitable for long-duration space missions.
