H5 influenza: A virus that takes, then takes some more
Authors: Kay Russo
Published: 2024

August 2024

Influenza viruses do not follow our human rules. Instead, they follow the biology defined by a negative-sense, single-stranded, segmented RNA genome and interactions with their environment and hosts. They will not wait for us to work through our politics, our man-made rules, or even our science to exert their damage. They will merely follow their biology. This is a point we humans often fail to recognize, one that allows a virus, such as H5 influenza, to wreak havoc, leaving us in the dust. This is where we went wrong and where we need to do better.

A “mystery cow disease” was ripping through the Texas panhandle in early 2024. Herds were exhibiting a rapid onset of a disease with high morbidity and clinical presentations so varied that it left most bovine practitioners scratching their heads. Anorexia and abrupt cessations in rumination, elevated body temperatures, increased respiratory effort, dry, tacky manure, and the real oddball: mastitis with inconsistent culture results. The bovine practitioners in the thick of the initial outbreak furiously communicated with each other, sharing test results and theories, trying to figure out the enigma. Many theories were flashed around on webinars, such as a novel cattle coronavirus, Mannheimia haemolytica (vaccinate more cows!), a bad batch of feed additive from a foreign country, and the list went on. Diagnostics for the usual suspects came up short, and practitioners were experiencing burnout and frustration because they did not have answers regarding the etiology or treatment. As clinical cases in herds exceeded the capacity of hospital pens (up from 0.5% to 1% of cows in the hospital pen to >20%), farm staff and veterinarians scrambled to try to nurse ill cattle—a disproportionate number of which were lactation 2+ cows far into lactation—back to health and production, which was physically and emotionally overwhelming.

A small group of us veterinarians started texting in early March. I, as the “crazy bird lady” of the group, have experience with poultry and cattle, and had watched H5N1 (clade 2.3.4.4b) wreak havoc on the commercial poultry industry for 2 yr and jump into myriad mammalian hosts with ease. I suggested to the frontline veterinarians that the clinical signs were suggestive of influenza, and H5N1 was an agent that ought to be considered. The veterinarians, who had tested for all the usual pathogens, were well on the path to considering “zebras” and indicated that they had seen an increase in dead birds on affected operations. This crazy bird lady walked them through what samples should be collected and submitted for confirmation of H5N1 in the dead birds, and it was executed immediately. The wild birds were positive for H5N1.

The results of this positive test hung like a wet pair of insulated coveralls over the small group of veterinarians as the possibility that this was H5 in cows became more real. In poultry, flocks positive for highly pathogenic H5 influenza are depopulated. If this was H5 in dairy cattle, what would happen? The potential scenarios rolled through their minds, and each had their own existential crisis at some level. Would the government descend upon the farms and depopulate herds? Was chasing this down a mistake?

The veterinarians began to alert various entities, including those meant to serve the bovine veterinary community, state veterinarians, and the National Veterinary Services Laboratory (NVSL). The response was muted—even dismissive. The NVSL admitted that they received correspondence from the veterinarians suggesting the disease in cattle was H5N1 but ignored it because no one knew how to respond. At the same time, cats across many dairies were experiencing respiratory and neurological signs, and many succumbed to the disease. Workers were also complaining of symptoms, including severe conjunctivitis, malaise, and flu-like symptoms. One veterinarian spent hours on the phone trying to identify a clinic where workers could get rapid testing for influenza A in the Texas panhandle, and came up short.

Nevertheless, the practitioners persevered and immediately submitted bovine samples, including milk, to the Iowa State University Veterinary Diagnostic Laboratory for real-time PCR tests for the influenza A matrix protein. This resulted in the first positive bovine case in the outbreak. The NVSL genotyped and publicly confirmed H5N1 in a dairy cow from Texas on March 25, 2024, and communicated the results to the Texas commissioner of agriculture and bovine practitioners' group before the submitting veterinarian.

When highly pathogenic avian influenza is diagnosed in poultry, the government swiftly steps in and depopulates the affected flock within 24 to 72 h. The response to finding H5N1 in cattle was incongruent, minimized, and slow-walked. The state of Texas kept cattle movement open for “humane reasons,” and H5N1 spread like wildfire to multiple states with the movement of cattle. The concern that the government would swarm the dairies was answered with confusion when very little support was shown at all. Maybe it was the potential implications on trade or pressure from outside commodity groups to minimize the severity and geographical scope of the disease, but there was a lag. Vocal veterinarians who spoke publicly about the disease, advocated for human testing, and refused to write health papers when clients declined influenza testing were either applauded or vilified. Some even lost work as a punishment for bringing negative attention to the industry, being “antiagriculture,” or jeopardizing shareholders.

This brings us to now.

A now in which we have seen “bovine flu” (B3.13) spilling back into poultry, killing close to 20 million laying hens (some housed in shower-in facilities) and hundreds of thousands of turkeys in 4 mo time. Consider for a moment the humane impact on poultry and the mental health of workers caring for and depopulating these flocks. Furthermore, this is a time of year when poultry influenza cases are typically lower, which heightens concern for fall, with its cooler weather and waterfowl migration (another enzootic host).

A now in which we have 13 states with infected herds and the number grows each day. As states institute weekly bulk tank testing, we are starting to realize that the virus is not burning out as the United States Department of Agriculture suggested it would. That is not how influenza works! If given a constant naïve source to jump into, it will be there indefinitely, constantly mutating.

A now with limitations on what samples can be tested, particularly PCR and serology on nonlactating animals, and only through National Animal Health Laboratory Network labs. Where epidemiological investigations are limited to the United States Department of Agriculture and a lactating cow, fomite transmission narrative. Prefederal order serology work suggests heifers on dairies are seroconverting, albeit in lower numbers (10%–12% of heifer samples tested), but those data contradict the lactating cow–disease narrative. Prevalence and transmission risk in nonlactating cattle are especially relevant from a biosecurity standpoint to poultry growers who oftentimes also own beef cattle. When will testing requirements catch up?

A now in which the number of human cases is mounting, likely due to increased testing, making human health officials nervous. Human flu season is around the corner, and there is potential for a reassortment event between B3.13 and a human-adapted seasonal influenza virus coinfecting the same human. This could give rise to a human-adapted H5 influenza, and this is why the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention is recommending vaccination of dairy workers with seasonal flu vaccines. This is not fearmongering—it is simply the biology of the flu.

Do not let politics get in the way of science. Influenza does not care about politics or trade; it cares about surviving. H5 influenza is looking increasingly similar to an endemic disease in cattle. We have spent 5 mo tiptoeing around narratives and restricting testing. Five months ignoring the clinical feedback from frontline veterinarians that does not match this narrative. It is time to let go, breathe deeply, open the communication, free up testing to encompass comprehensive serology and nonlactating cattle, and figure out a path forward that makes sense.

Influenza is not going to wait for politics or publications. We must do better now.
