A UHV-compatible, time-resolved spontaneous Raman spectrometer for multi-messenger ultrafast studies: Design and applications to photoinduced dynamics
Authors: A M Finardi, C Fasolato, A Giugni, R Cucini, A Fondacaro, G M Pierantozzi, D Benedetti, F Sacchetti, P Postorino, G Panaccione, C Petrillo, G Rossi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTime-resolved (TR) Raman spectroscopy is a unique tool for studying the dynamic properties of quantum matter and can become a key element of the multi-messenger research in the time domain. We present here the features and results of a novel setup for TR Raman, designed to expand the NFFA-SPRINT facility by integrating it with TR optical, transient grating and electron spectroscopy and spin polarization techniques. The TR Raman setup is characterized by a wide energy tunability of the pump and probe pulses, owing to the presence of a laser system providing ultrashort (50 fs to 2 ps) light pulses from the near ultraviolet to the infrared spectral regions. The ultra-high vacuum sample environment allows for the measurement of air-sensitive samples and ensures the full compatibility with photoelectron spectroscopies, as well as a wide sample temperature range. The functionalities of the setup and the multi-messenger research approach are here demonstrated by presenting studies of the relaxation dynamics in photoexcited semiconductor systems, namely, Si and MoS2. In addition, the pump-probe response of magnetite across the Verwey transition is presented, highlighting the capability of TR spontaneous Raman spectroscopy to be a valuable tool for probing photoinduced phase transitions in the time domain.

INTRODUCTION

The complexity in condensed matter is encoded in the interplay among its multiple degrees of freedom (electrons, phonons, spin); there is much we do not yet know about phenomena arising from this interplay. One approach to the physics of quantum materials is to probe the relaxation pathways from a suitably prepared “pumped” excited state. The ultrafast pump-probe methodology can also lead to unveiling the dynamics and the driving factors of phase transitions, as well as the establishment of a specific system configuration among competing long-range ordered states.

Spectroscopy methods, in the perturbative regime, give access to the energy states of a system, from low-energy (meV) lattice, spin, or electronic collective excitations to high-energy (eV) electronic transitions. In the pump-probe scheme, the same information becomes accessible dynamically, as a system evolves over time in reaction to a stimulus.1–3 In pump-probe methods at least two, short (compared to the investigated dynamics) laser pulses are used in a controlled temporal sequence. A first one (or the first ones), acting as pump, optically drives the system out of equilibrium, triggering electronic transitions,4 or lower energy collective excitations, like phonons,5,6 magnons,7,8 or electronic modes.9 A second pulse (perturbative), delivered at a variable, controlled delay, probes the system evolution over time.

The integration of spectroscopic methods sensitive to different degrees of freedom at equilibrium has proven to be essential to reach a consistent description of physical systems.10–12 As a consequence, one of the frontiers of time-resolved (TR) studies is the integration of complementary spectroscopies, in a “multi-messenger” approach, holding the promise to deliver a complete description of dynamic processes in condensed matter.13

In this paper, we describe a TR Raman spectrometer which, being part of a multi-messenger TR laboratory,14 can operate under ultra-high-vacuum (UHV) conditions and at cryogenic temperatures on samples that can be UHV-transferred, e.g., to TR photoelectron spectroscopy (PES) and spin-polarimetry stations,15 or probed by other optical techniques (photoluminescence-lifetime,16 and transient grating spectroscopy and magnetometry17). The integrated optical and electronic spectroscopy system at NFFA-SPRINT Laboratory (at FERMI@Elettra experimental hall in Trieste, Italy) is unprecedented in terms of accessible information and dynamical range, owing to the remarkable tunability of the laser system used as a source in the facility. The complementarity of the available TR methods allows to design and carry out of advanced studies, as sketched in Fig. 1 for the exemplary case of relaxation after electronic photoexcitation. Here, the pump promotes an electronic transition to excited states, and the available probes interrogate the electrons or the lattice and their interplay in various stages of the relaxation pathway.

Time-resolved spontaneous Raman scattering (TRRS) can provide direct access to the incoherent phonon relaxation, as well as the electron–phonon and phonon–phonon coupling dynamics.1,2,18,19 Indeed, the Raman scattering cross section contains information on the phonon frequency, symmetry (by polarization selection rules), lifetime (from the line shape and width), and population (from the detailed balance relation and anti-Stokes/Stokes intensity ratio). Further information on the electron–phonon coupling can be retrieved from resonant Raman effects.20 Contrary to the stimulated Raman scattering technique, which probes the nonlinear response of the system down to the fs scale in a four-wave mixing scheme,21,22 we are here triggering the same scattering events as in an equilibrium experiment.23 Remarkably, spontaneous TRRS provides access to the ground-state phonon properties of the system under examination, while it is captured in an out-of-equilibrium condition, e.g., in the presence of a photoexcited hot carrier4 or hot phonon population.24 Since the typical ps duration of the used pulses exceeds the timescale of electron–electron interaction,25 one typically resorts to a “time-local” quasi-equilibrium picture for the interpretation of the TRRS data. That implies considering each subsystem in the sample (electrons and specific phonon modes) as obeying an internal equilibrium statistics, with a corresponding effective temperature, while the out-of-equilibrium nature of the process resides in the differences among these effective temperatures.

In the first section of the manuscript, we describe the TRRS experimental apparatus focusing on the specific characteristics of the exciting laser system (Sec. II A), the optical scheme (Sec. II B), and the sample environment (Sec. II C). In the second section, we report on original benchmark experiments. The content is presented focusing on different properties of matter probed by TRRS, rather than organized by the chosen material systems. This outline is selected to illustrate the relevant, different processes that can be studied with the present setup and to highlight its technical potentialities. Specifically: (i) electron–phonon relaxation dynamics in semiconductors is explored by integrating the TRRS study (Sec. III A) with a TR-PES investigation (Sec. III B); (ii) a structural phase transition in a ferrimagnetic system is tackled with a temperature-dependent TRRS study (Sec. III C). In conclusion, we illustrate the perspective of the setup as a user facility and summarize the potential developments of the proposed experimental design.

EXPERIMENTAL SETUP

Laser system

The laser system at the NFFA-SPRINT facility is characterized by a remarkable tunability and is designed to maximize the interoperability of the laser sources, to allow for the simultaneous use of multiple pump-probe setups under the same experimental conditions. It comprises two Yb:KGW-based laser amplifiers (PHAROS, Light Conversion) with a common oscillator, each one emitting pulses with time duration of 290 fs at 1026 nm, variable repetition rate from single shot to 1 MHz, pulse energy of 400 μJ (at 50 kHz), and average power up to 20 W. The lasers can seed a high-harmonic generation (HHG) source dedicated to TR-PES experiments, which allows access to the XUV range providing 17–31 eV pulses with 110 fs time resolution. A thorough description of the HHG source and the TR-PES beamline is reported in Ref. 15. The lasers can also seed two different optical parametric amplifiers (OPA), covering the wavelength ranges 210–2600, and 640–2550 nm. A pulse compressor at the output of the latter allows to reach a pulse duration between 30 and 50 fs. Each pump-probe technique requires the use of (at least) two different pulses, whose roles of pump/probe can be exchanged based on the energy requirements of the system.

Using a pulsed laser as a source in a TRRS experiment requires a fine-tuning of the pulse duration-spectral bandwidth. Indeed, given that we are triggering a spontaneous scattering event, the spectral resolution is limited by the bandwidth of the probe pulse, which is in turn connected to the overall experimental time resolution. The Fourier-transform limit ΔtΔν~∼15 cm−1·ps implies that a time resolution in the ps scale is needed for appreciating a 15 cm−1 phonon line shape. In our setup, that is enabled by two 4f pulse shapers on the pump and probe optical paths. The design and working principle of a 4f pulse shaper is sketched in Fig. 2(a). The incoming beam is dispersed by a diffraction grating and each chromatic component is focused by a cylindrical lens on a plane where a suitable optical mask designs the desired pulse spectral shape. A second cylindrical lens is used to re-collimate the beam, and a second diffraction grating, mirroring the first one, recomposes the shaped beam. Our experimental realization of the 4f pulse shaper is shown in the render in Fig. 2(b). In our case, a tuning of the spectral bandwidth and of its central wavelength is needed: to this aim, a slit mounted on a lateral micrometric displacement is sufficient. The effect of pulse shaping on the bandwidth and duration of an OPA pulse at 633 nm is shown in Fig. 2(c). The pulse duration, as measured by a commercial autocorrelator (APE pulseCheck), is displayed as a function of the achieved laser bandwidth. The laser shape dependence on the micrometric slit opening, measured through the Raman spectrometer after suitable attenuation, is shown in the inset. In our TRRS experiments, pulse durations are calculated from the time-bandwidth product, characterized preliminarily as being transform-limited ( ΔtΔν~∼15 cm−1·ps) for the fundamental laser emission and its second harmonic and ΔtΔν~∼27 cm−1·ps for the OPA-emitted pulses. In Fig. 2(d), we show the Raman spectra of an ethanol sample in the CH stretching region26 as a function of the pulse shaper slit opening. The spectral shape measured with the pulsed laser at 20 μm (30 cm−1 spectral width), or lower slit opening, resembles the spectrum obtained by continuous wave (CW) laser excitation, approaching the intrinsic line shape of the bands.

Optical scheme

In Fig. 3(a), we show a rendering of the laboratory table, comprising the laser sources (right side) and the TRRS setup (left side). The optical scheme of the TRRS setup is shown in Fig. 3(b). The “pump” and “probe” laser pulses enter the Raman setup through two 4f pulse shapers (see Sec. II A). Typically, the pump pulse is the one generated by the visible OPA, which allows for a larger tunability of the photon energy. The visible OPA operates at fixed repetition rate, which is currently set at 200 kHz. Instead, the probe typically is the second harmonic (SH) of the laser fundamental emission (1026 nm), generated through a nonlinear BBO crystal and spectrally purified using a dichroic mirror. If convenient, the role of pump and probe can be exchanged. The relative delay between the pulses is tuned through the use of a motorized delay line, which allows to choose the delays in the range from −1 to +1 ns for each pump-probe pulse configuration. The control over the pump and probe polarization is available through the use of achromatic half- and quarter-waveplates (Thorlabs Inc.). Similarly, the polarized dependence of the scattering intensity can be monitored introducing additional polarization optics in the collection module. The pulse fluence is adjusted by neutral filters of variable optical density. In the final part of the optical path [top in Fig. 3(b)], the pump and probe pulses are aligned on parallel axes, vertically superimposed and spaced by 8 mm, and then focused on the same spot on the sample surface by an off-axis parabolic mirror. The geometry of focusing and signal collection is detailed in the following (Fig. 4 and Fig. S1 in the supplementary material). In order to enable the full in situ sample characterization by conventional Raman spectroscopy [see e.g., Fig. 2(d)], two CW lasers are also available (laser lines 532 and 785 nm). They can be aligned on the pump and probe optical paths using removable mirrors.

The inelastically scattered signal is collected by a microscope objective and delivered to a single pass Czerny–Turner spectrometer (Triax model, Horiba Scientific). The spectrometer features a 320 mm focal length and is equipped with three diffraction gratings (300, 600 and 1800 lines/mm) providing a final spectral resolution between 20 and 3 cm−1. A back-illuminated, deep-depleted CCD with multiple stage thermoelectric and liquid cooling system collects the signal, which is acquired and elaborated in real time by the spectrometer company's proprietary software.

In Fig. 4, we show the sample excitation and signal collection geometry. To maintain it in a vacuum environment (see Sec. II C), the sample is inside a UHV chamber whose lower part is constituted by a cylindrical pyrex glass allowing for pan-optical access. A 45° off-axis parabolic mirror (50.8 mm focal length) provides both the focusing and the spatial overlap of the pump and probe beams on the sample surface. The output of the OPA (red in Figs. 3 and 4) is focused while remaining aligned on the horizontal direction, while the SH of the PHAROS laser (green in Figs. 3 and 4), aligned on a horizontal axis 8 mm below the OPA output, crosses the horizontal direction in the focus. In order to minimize astigmatic aberrations, the focus of the parabolic mirror (i.e., the nominal position where the two beams are both focused and overlapped) is aligned on the rotational center of the pyrex cylinder. Details about the optical access to the UHV chamber and its optimization can be found in the SM. The translational movements of the manipulator (see Sec. II C) allow the sample to be positioned in the focal point. The described geometry produces laser spot diameters of 25–30 μm (1/e2), as estimated from the diffusive scattering of a reference sample imaged with a properly calibrated CCD camera. Such an estimate is corroborated by the measurement of laser traces left on photosensitive sample surfaces. Two beam expanders placed along the optical paths of the pulsed lasers [see Fig. 3(b)] enable independent adjustment of each beam's divergence, thereby allowing precise control over the spot sizes at the focus.

A 10× long-working-distance objective (Mitutoyo Plan Apochromat Objective, 0.28NA, 34 mm WD), aligned along a radius of the pyrex glass cylinder, collimates the scattered radiation, which is then focalized on the entrance slit of the spectrometer. Unlike in conventional backscattering geometries, the non-coaxial design, with different optics for sample excitation and signal collection, allows for an independent regulation of the incident fluence and the collection numerical aperture. Two filter holders after the microscope objective host interchangeable notch/edge filters to remove the elastically scattered component of both the pump and probe beams. The optical imaging of the sample is enabled by a removable mirror, which can be inserted after the microscope objective, focusing the scattered radiation on a CCD camera when the sample is illuminated by an external source. The collection and imaging optics are mounted on micrometric movements allowing for a precise alignment of the objective and thus signal optimization.

Sample environment

The sample environment is a key originality of the present setup, offering fine control over spatial alignment, temperature, the preservation of sample cleanliness and stability, and the possibility of exchanging samples in UHV with other spectrometers and sample growth systems.

The sample is mounted on the cold finger of a cryostat (see below) attached to a motorized UHV manipulator (PMM 12, VAB Vakuum-Anlagenbau GmbH) providing four degrees of freedom: three orthogonal linear translations (X, Y, Z) with a minimum step size of 1 μm, and a full 360° rotation about the manipulator axis (vertical). This enables precise spatial alignment of the sample relative to the excitation and collection optics, as well as the control over the laser incidence angle, critical for applications such as polarization-dependent Raman measurements. The manipulator can be operated via either joystick or software, with a coarse manual control (10 μm step size) also available for linear translations.

The sample holder is hosted by a UHV chamber, with a pumping system which allows to reach a pressure of 1 × 10−8 mbar. The chamber is fully bakeable, if actual UHV pressure (10−10 mbar) is needed. A UHV-compatible transfer system (Ferrovac UHV suitcase) enables the introduction and removal of samples without breaking vacuum conditions. A CAD model of the chamber and manipulator is shown in Fig. 5.

While Raman spectroscopy can be performed under ambient conditions, the presence of a UHV environment significantly broadens the experimental capabilities of the current setup. This is demonstrated by recently reported developments of UHV-compatible Raman systems, also integrated with PES experiments.27–29 Such apparatuses are designed to perform experiments under equilibrium conditions, using CW sources. Operating under vacuum: (i) ensures minimal sample contamination during measurements, thereby enabling the investigation of air-sensitive materials and systems whose properties are strongly influenced by surface conditions; and (ii) allows, together with the integrated cryostat, measurements at cryogenic temperatures, essential for most studies involving phase transitions and strongly correlated materials. Moreover, the UHV conditions, combined with the UHV transfer system, ensure full compatibility with the electronic spectroscopy setups available at the NFFA-SPRINT facility. This integration enables a true multi-messenger approach, allowing experimental campaigns to be conducted under reproducible environmental conditions, from sample growth to measurements using multiple complementary techniques.

Our setup includes a UHV-compatible continuous flow cryostat system (Lake Shore Cryotronics ST-400), capable of operating with both liquid nitrogen and liquid helium, and reaching temperatures as low as 10 K at the sample stage. Thermal contact between the sample holder and the cooled manipulator is ensured by two clamping screws. Temperature monitoring and control is achieved via two DT-470 silicon diode sensors: one located inside the cryostat and the other mounted in vacuum on the backside of the sample. The second sensor provides the actual temperature on the sample via a properly calibrated correction factor. The sensors are connected to a Lakeshore 336 temperature controller, enabling continuous monitoring and data logging as well as proportional–integral–derivative temperature control with a precision of 0.1 K. Shielding against irradiation from the inner glass walls and external light will be implemented by a cooled rotating metal insert with beam windows. Temperature control and stabilization are achieved by the use of a resistive heater positioned inside the cryostat. which also allows to carry out measurements above room temperature (up to 500 K nominal heating power, tested up to 350 K).

BENCHMARK EXPERIMENTS

Electron–phonon and phonon–phonon relaxation dynamics in semiconductors

One of the main goals of the research on semiconductive materials is the possibility to control and tailor the electric and optical properties of the materials. It goes without saying that this objective requires a thorough understanding of the electrons/holes de-excitation pathways, which in turn depend strongly on the specific coupling with the phonon subsystem, the main reservoir with which the free carriers exchange energy. The natural first application of TRRS on these systems is thus to monitor the evolution of phonon population following photoexcitation via the anti-Stokes/Stokes intensity ratio. Since, in the presence of an optical pump, the energy is deposited in the electron subsystem, the timescale of the observed phonon population increase is representative of the relaxation time of the excited carries via coupling to that specific phonon mode. Moreover, TRRS allows to access the photoinduced modifications to the electronic population whenever they have a direct effect on the Raman spectra, either changing the phonon frequencies and lineshapes4,30 or creating dynamic modifications of the Raman tensor19,30 or the material optical properties.

Tuning anharmonic interaction by temperature in silicon

As a first benchmark experiment, we repeated the measurements carried out by Zhu et al.30 on Si, reproducing the main results they obtained at room temperature and investigating the effect of low temperature on the system. Pump pulses of 633 nm central wavelength and 0.3 ps duration are used to excite above bandgap an intrinsic (100)-oriented silicon wafer. The Raman signal is probed with 513 nm pulses of 15 cm−1 bandwidth (FWHM) and 1 ps duration, yielding an overall temporal resolution of 1.04 ps from the pump-probe cross-correlation. The probe and pump laser fluences used are, respectively, Fprobe = 1.6 mJ/cm2 and Fpump = 9.8 mJ/cm2. The beams are vertically polarized (polarization vector lying in the sample plane) and the scattered radiation is collected with no polarization selection. This polarization condition was maintained in all the following experiments. The experiment was repeated at room temperature and at 87 K.

Stokes spectra measured at two different pump-probe delays are displayed in Fig. 6(a), showing the peak corresponding to the zone-center longitudinal optical (LO) phonon at 520.7 cm−1 (∼523 cm−1 at 87 K). At positive delays, the main pump-induced variations of the Raman signal are found to be a transient increase in the anti-Stokes scattering intensity, a long-lived decrease in the Stokes intensity, and a modification of the phonon profile toward a broader and asymmetric line shape. The former is associated with the creation of an additional phonon population, while the latter are a direct consequence of changes in the electronic state occupancies.

C is a factor containing the incident intensity and the optical constants. As their frequency dependence is weak, C and χ are typically considered to be equal for Stokes and anti-Stokes processes. By measuring both sides of the Raman spectrum, it is thus possible to retrieve both C·χ2 and the equivalent phonon temperature Tν (from IASIS≈ωL+ωv3ωL−ωv3·nB+1nB=e−ℏωνkBTν) as a function of the delay. Tν is plotted in Fig. 6(b) for the two datasets at different temperatures. The anti-Stokes and Stokes intensities were retrieved from fitting the spectra with an appropriate asymmetric line shape (Fano line shape, see below), convolved with a Gaussian function to account for the spectral resolution (fixed to the FWHM of the measured probe laser line). Since the two sides of the Raman spectrum were acquired simultaneously, Stokes and anti-Stokes peaks were fitted within a single routine, with shared spectral characteristics (distance from laser line, width, degree of asymmetry) and independent values of the areas.

At room temperature, we observe a rapid rise of the phonon equivalent temperature from ∼342 K (measured before t0) to ∼410 K followed by an exponential decay with a 1.8 ps timescale. Considering the smearing due to the finite temporal resolution (accounted for in the fittings convolving the exponential function with a Gaussian), we can estimate the maximum phonon temperature reached to be ∼445 K corresponding to a ∼103 K increase. This is consistent with the work of Zhu et al. and with the characteristic expected timescale for the transient creation of optical phonons via electron–phonon coupling and their subsequent decay via phonon–phonon anharmonic coupling. The smaller increase in the phonon temperature they observe at similar excited carrier density is reasonably explained by the lower temporal resolution (i.e., increased smearing of Tmax) or the longer pump wavelength employed (740 nm). At 87 K, we observe a pump-induced increase in the phonon temperature of ∼145 K, significantly higher than the one measured at room T. Moreover, as clearly visible in Fig. 6(b), the relaxation dynamics is much slower, with a 3.4 ps timescale. This is an evidence of the reduction of the phonon–phonon anharmonic interaction: that is expected at lower T due to a smaller equilibrium population of all lattice modes. The slower decay of the additional phonon population may also underlie the greater ΔTν measured at 87 K.

On the other hand, the pump-induced changes of the phonon line shape are evidence of the creation of a photoexcited carrier density and, more specifically, of a hole density. As anticipated, at positive delays the LO phonon assumes an asymmetric line shape well described by a Fano function: I∝q+ε21+ε2, where q is the so-called Fano parameter (controlling the asymmetry) and ε=2ω−ωνΓ with ων the phonon frequency and Γ the linewidth. The temporal dependence of q retrieved by the above-mentioned fit procedure is reported in Fig. 6(c), highlighting the step-like decrease in the peak symmetry after the pump arrival. A Fano interference phenomenon in Raman scattering occurs whenever there is a resonance between the energy of a discrete state (phonon) and a continuum state background (electronic excitations). Such phenomenon is well-known to affect the LO line shape in heavily doped Si due to the presence of a continuum of accessible transitions between the uppermost heavy- and light-hole valence bands.31 In the present case, as explained by Zhu et al., the photoinduced hole population increases the number of available electronic transitions in the valence band, dynamically enhancing the Fano interference and thus the value of q (inversely proportional to the spectral density of the electronic continuum). We can also see from Fig. 6(c) that the system temperature has almost no effect on the dynamics of q: this is a further proof of the electronic origin of the asymmetry variation and points to a negligible temperature dependence of the carrier relaxation time.

Finally, at the pump arrival, the scattering strength C·χ2 undergoes a rapid decrease. This is observed at both measured temperatures and reflects the reduction of the Stokes intensity [see Fig. 6(a)], affected only weakly by the changes in the phonon population. Zhu et al. related this observation to the increase in the hole population, explaining it by the quenching of the resonant conditions of the Raman process. Indeed, the use of an above-bandgap probe energy, as in the present case, allows for an enhancement of the Raman cross section proportional to the number of real electronic transitions that can be excited with the specific probe energy chosen. The photoexcitation of a carrier density by the pump results in a ground-state bleaching/excited-state filling effect for these transitions, thus leading to a reduction of the Raman scattering strength.

Overall, we accurately reproduced the results of Zhu et al. on Si at room T and investigated the effect of lowering the temperature on the observed dynamics. We verified that the dynamics of spectral changes that arise from a change in the electronic states occupancies are quite independent of the system temperature, whereas the latter plays a major role in the decay of the photoexcited phonon population, regulated by phonon–phonon anharmonic interaction. The present experiment also highlights how, even in the absence of peculiar phenomena like the Fano interference, the intensity of the Stokes peak and the overall Raman scattering strength can provide reliable information on the lifetime of the photoexcited carrier density.

Non-thermal phonon distribution in MoS2

Acting as prototypical example of the family of layered semiconductors known as transition metals dichalcogenides, MoS2 has been widely studied in the last decade as it offers a vast playground for fundamental science investigations as well as promising properties for applications in the fields of optoelectronics, spintronics, and valleytronics. In MoS2, the crucial role of the coupling with the phonon subsystem in determining the hot carrier relaxation dynamics has emerged from transient absorption measurements.32,33 However, most TR experiment focused on the electron dynamics and only indirect access to the phonon dynamics was granted by studying the time-dependence of the exciton energy34 or by exploiting coherence effects.35

We carried out TRRS measurements on a bulk sample of MoS2 at room temperature, employing 513 nm probe pulses (pulse duration Δt ∼1 ps, fluence 2.4 mJ/cm2) and 633 nm pump pulses (pulse duration Δt ∼0.6 ps, fluence 15 mJ/cm2).4 The experiment was conducted using a preliminary configuration of the OPA, which required a laser repetition rate of 50 kHz. The pump pulses were in nearly resonant condition with the system excitonic bandgap ( ∼1.85 eV),36 thus very effective in promoting electrons from the valence to the conduction band. Spectra obtained with and without the pump pulse are reported in Fig. 7(a). In these experimental conditions, the MoS2 Raman spectrum consists of two main peaks at ∼ 407 cm-1 and ∼ 383 cm−1, associated with the out-of-plane A1g and in-plane E2g1 phonon modes, respectively.

As detailed in Sec. III A 1, by measuring simultaneously the Stokes and anti-Stokes sides of the Raman spectrum, we are able to retrieve the effective phonon temperature for both modes. The obtained temperature variations ΔTAt, ΔTEt are displayed in Fig. 7(b) as a function of the pump-probe delay. The temperature trends are fitted with a double exponential decay convolved with a Gaussian function accounting for the temporal resolution of the measurement. The behavior of the two modes shows a remarkable difference: the A1g mode temperature has a steep and marked increase in ∼ 300–400 K after the pump arrival, while the temperature of the E2g1 mode increases smoothly (requiring an additional factor in the fitting function with a rise time τr ∼5.5 ps) of only ∼ 100–150 K at positive delays. This is a clear evidence of how the electron de-excitation in MoS2 occurs preferentially via the creation of A1g rather than E2g1 phonons and therefore an evidence of a marked difference between the electron–phonon coupling of the two modes. The subsequent relaxation dynamics is well reproduced with a fast decay time constant of ∼ 5.4 ps for the A1g mode and ∼ 3.5 ps for the E2g1 mode, and a slow decay one ≫500 ps for both modes. From Fig. 7(b), we can also see how the population of the two modes becomes equal only at t≳20 ps. This value can thus be taken as an estimate of the time after which the non-thermal phonon distribution is equilibrated by anharmonic phonon–phonon scattering and a single temperature can adequately describe the entire phonon bath (or at least the optical phonons). More details about the phonon temperature relaxation dynamics in bulk and single-layer MoS2 can be found in Ref. 4. The observation of such different electron–phonon coupling (∼10 times greater for the A1g mode), even for two zone-center optical modes separated in energy by only a few cm−1, highlights the potential of using TRRS to go beyond the two-temperature description (one electronic, one phononic) often used to interpret out-of-equilibrium studies.

Moreover, in the TRRS spectra obtained on MoS2, a softening of both phonon modes, induced by the 633 nm pump, occurs on a nearly instantaneous timescale (much faster than our 1.2 ps temporal resolution). The tunability of the OPA source allowed us to verify that this frequency renormalization is associated with the specific resonance conditions of the pump wavelength. In fact, when employing a 820 nm pump (hence below the bandgap energy) with similar fluence, no shift of the phonon peaks is observed, as shown in Fig. 7(a). This points toward a strong dependence of the observed frequency shift on the photoexcited carrier density, motivating a direct electronic investigation.

Complementary info from TR Raman and TR-PES

As anticipated, the TRRS experiment on MoS2 described in Sec. III A reveals a strong softening of the phonon modes induced by the pump excitation. The comparison of the evolution of the phonon frequencies and their effective temperatures as a function of pump-probe delay, reported in Fig. 7(c), shows that the softening occurs before the increase in TA, i.e., before the energy transfer from electron to phonon bath. This allows to ascribe the redshift in the early time range to the transient photodoping of the system,37,38 rather than to lattice thermal expansion.39,40 The observed redshift is followed by a relaxation toward the initial values with a characteristic decay constant of ∼ 2.5 ps, as estimated from a fitting analogous to the one performed on the temperature trends. However, as shown in detail in Ref. 4, even at long delays (>500 ps) the phonon frequencies display a residual shift which cannot be completely accounted for by the measured excess of 55 K in the phononic temperature. This strongly suggests the presence of a significative density of long-lived excited carriers which continue to affect the phonon spectrum up to at least 500 ps.

The multi-messenger approach provides a route to validate the latter hypothesis. PES can indeed provide direct access to the electronic degrees of freedom. More specifically, angle-resolved PES allows to measure the valence band structure in specific points of the Brillouin zone (BZ), and its TR counterpart grants access to the conduction band, by probing states above the Fermi level transiently populated by a pump pulse.

A time- and angle-resolved PES (TR-ARPES) experiment was carried out on a bulk sample of MoS2. A clean surface was obtained from a MoS2 single crystal by cleaving in situ under UHV conditions (P ≈ 10−10 mbar). The crystal was properly oriented to measure along the Γ-K direction of the BZ. Photoemission spectra were acquired with probe pulses at 26.4 eV with 100 fs duration, generated by the HHG source (see Sec. II A). 50 fs pump pulses at 640 nm were used to resonantly excite the sample with a 2.5 mJ/cm2 fluence.

In Fig. 7(d), we report the intensity variation of the photoemission signal from the conduction band as a function of pump-probe delay. The plotted data represent the sum of the ARPES intensities measured above the Fermi level at the K and Σ points of the BZ, which correspond to the conduction band minima, providing a temporal trend representative of most of the electrons photoexcited by the resonant pump. The trend was fitted with the function f(t)=θ(t−t0)α(1−e−t−t0τr)(e−t−t0τ1+δe−t−t0τ2+γ), including an exponential rise factor, a double exponential decay, and a constant describing a long-delay plateau. f(t) was convolved with a Gaussian providing the temporal resolution achieved in the TR-PES measurements (∼130 fs). From the fit, the characteristic rise and decay time constants were extracted: τr∼150 fs, τ1∼300 fs, and τ2∼15 ps. The fast and slow decay components can be attributed to the fast radiative recombination of the electrons at the K point and to slower defect- or phonon-mediated processes. At delays longer than 50 ps, the intensity seemingly reaches a plateau, which is accounted for with a constant in the fitting function, testifying the presence of a significative amount of long-lived photoexcited carriers.

Remarkably, TR-PES allowed to confirm the presence of a photoexcited carrier population in CB on a timescale comparable to that of the phonon frequency renormalization observed in TRRS measurements.

Temporal analysis of a photoinduced structural phase transition

Understanding the interplay between lattice dynamics and phase transition in strongly correlated oxides is a major effort in condensed matter research. These materials typically present a metal-insulator transition intercorrelated with changes of lattice symmetry, charge ordering, and, in some cases, magnetic properties. Magnetite (Fe3O4), with its puzzling Verwey transition, is a prototypical member of this class: lowering its temperature below a critical value of about 120 K, magnetite undergoes a transition from a metallic to an insulating state marked by a two order of magnitude increase in the resistivity and, concomitantly, a structural transition from cubic to monoclinic crystal symmetry with associated anomalies in magnetic, electric, and dynamical properties.41,42

Despite decades of study, significant progress on understanding the Verwey transition was made only recently through the use of TR techniques. Ultrafast studies indeed highlighted the role of the coupling of charge ordering with specific phonons42,43 and revealed the presence of metastable hidden phases accessible via non-thermal pathways.44 Interestingly, Truc et al.44 showed by pump-probe electron diffraction experiments that different crystal symmetries are accessible depending on the pump energy. Raman spectroscopy can be exploited to probe the occurrence of the Verwey transition in magnetite, by assessing the onset of new phonon modes in the low-temperature monoclinic phase, and the occurrence of anomalies in the temperature dependence of modes already present in the high-temperature cubic phase.45,46 TRRS can thus accurately map the photoinduced phase transitions, occurring on the ps timescale, and detect any crystal symmetry variation.

To this aim, we carried out a TRRS experiment on a 1 μm-thick magnetite film grown by pulsed laser deposition on a MgO substrate.47 The CW laser at 532 nm was used to characterize the temperature dependence of the Raman spectrum of the film down to 80 K. The measured spectra, normalized to the total (integrated) area, are reported in Fig. 8(a). Data clearly show the appearance of new modes at ∼385 and ∼470 cm−1 upon lowering the temperature, signaling the onset of the monoclinic phase. For the TRRS study, 513 nm probe pulses ( Δt ∼1 ps, Fprobe = 6.8 mJ/cm2) were used to measure the system in the low-temperature phase (T = 85 K) after photoexciting it with 633 nm pump pulses ( Δt ∼0.6 ps, Fpump = 14 mJ/cm2). As in all the above-mentioned experiments, the signal stability over time and the visual inspection of the sample after the measurement were carefully monitored to be sure the laser fluence was kept below the sample damage threshold. The obtained spectra are reported in Fig. 8(b) as a function of the pump-probe delay. At negative delays, the mode at 470 cm−1 associated with the monoclinic phase is visible. Such peak is less apparent in these measurements than in the CW ones at similar temperature: that is attributed both to the broadening resulting from the use of a 1 ps probe, and to the lower average power employed to avoid possible damage due to high fluence (1.2 mW as opposed to the 2.5 mW of CW measurements). As in the MoS2 study of Sec. III B, indeed, this experiment was conducted using a preliminary configuration of the OPA working at a repetition rate of 50 kHz and thus posing stronger constraints on the average laser power to avoid sample photodamaging. A faint feature at 385 cm−1 is also observed at negative delays; however, its weak intensity, comparable to the noise level, prevents a reliable analysis of its pump-induced dynamics.

By comparing the spectra and their differences in Figs. 8(a) and 8(b), it is evident that the 633 nm pump induces the same spectral changes observed when the sample temperature is increased beyond a critical value. This indicates that the Verwey transition—or at least a structural transition to a phase with the same cubic symmetry as the high-temperature phase—has been photoinduced. In order to characterize the timescale of the occurred transition, we focus on the variation of the integrated area of the peak at 470 cm−1 (normalized to its maximum value), that is a marker for the monoclinic low T phase, for the two datasets. In the temperature-dependent data [Fig. 8(c)], the peak area significantly decreases between 80 and 120–130 K, falling below the noise level (∼0.1) above 130–150 K. This trend is in good agreement with the expected behavior of the Raman spectrum of magnetite across the Verwey transition,45,46 which has a nominal critical temperature TV∼125 K.41 Based on this characterization, we extract an equivalent lattice temperature in TRRS data, at each pump-probe delay, as shown in Fig. 8(d). Specifically, a calibration is obtained from a linear fit of the temperature-dependent data [Fig. 8(c)] below 130 K and is used to calculate the time-evolving temperature Teq in the TRRS data. The temporal trend of Teq is shown in Fig. 8(d) as a function of the delay. The pump-probe data display the quench of the 470 cm−1 mode within ~5 ps after photoexcitation, corresponding to an equivalent lattice temperature exceeding TV. The recovery of the low-temperature phase is barely noticeable even close to 1 ns. The 633 nm pump thus seems to have a disruptive effect on the low-temperature electronic ordering underlying the monoclinic phase, leading to a state of cubic symmetry with metastable character. This is analogous to what was observed by Truc et al.44 in an ultrafast electron diffraction experiment when using 800 nm laser pulses as pump.

These preliminary results highlight the potential of applying TRRS, in combination with temperature-dependent Raman studies, to the study of magnetite and, more broadly, of systems with intertwined structural and electronic phase transitions. In the future, measuring simultaneously both the anti-Stokes and Stokes sides of the Raman spectrum will enable us to monitor the time evolution of the mode-specific phonon populations transiently induced by the pump, along with the equivalent lattice temperature extracted from spectral signatures of the Verwey transition. Comparing these distinct dynamics could reveal the role of specific phonon modes in accompanying or driving the phase transition. The tunability of the present OPA source also motivates further studies to explore the effects of the pump photon energy in possibly promoting the photoinduced access to phases of different symmetry.44 Probing the time evolution of the electron subsystem in magnetite by TR-PES, in combination with the TRRS study here presented, can provide a general picture of the mechanism driving the photoinduced phase transition. TR-PES experiments are currently ongoing: the complete experimental results and their interpretation will be the subject of a forthcoming, dedicated paper. Remarkably, a multi-messenger approach would allow investigating the connection between wavelength-dependent photoinduced lattice symmetries and the specific electronic transitions excited by the pump.

PERSPECTIVE DEVELOPMENTS

While the present TR Raman spectroscopy setup enables a detailed investigation of ultrafast vibrational dynamics, further developments are planned, aiming to extend its tunability and to allow the integration of additional optical spectroscopy techniques within the same experimental framework.

Planned developments will focus on implementing a rotating collection optics module. The microscope objective, filters, and imaging optics will be mounted on a rotating platform attached to a ball bearing on the lower flange of the UHV chamber. With the spectrometer fiber-coupled to the collection optics, this design will allow for continuous variation of the collection angle. By removing the constraint of a fixed angle between excitation and detection directions, this upgraded setup will further enhance the possibility of exploring the Raman scattering cross-sectional rules varying independently light polarization direction, as well as incidence and scattering angle (determining the transferred momentum).

More importantly, this development will also enable angle-resolved TR reflectivity measurements to be performed within the same apparatus. The combination of collection optics rotation and manipulator rotation will allow for continuous adjustment of the incidence angle from nearly normal (limited only by the lateral dimensions of the parabolic mirror and the microscope objective) to grazing. The present setup is particularly well suited for this extension, owing to (i) the wide tunability of the OPA sources from the UV to IR range and (ii) the vacuum sample environment, preserving surface cleanliness. The latter feature, in particular, will ensure the accuracy and reproducibility of reflectivity measurements, highly sensitive to surface contamination. Such enhancement will significantly broaden the capabilities of the setup, enabling simultaneous investigation of electronic and lattice dynamics with improved geometrical flexibility.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, we have presented a time-resolved Raman spectroscopy setup which is included a multi-messenger time-resolved spectroscopy facility also comprising time-, spin-, and angle-resolved photoemission, time-resolved photoluminescence, and transient grating spectroscopy setups. The remarkable reliability and tunability of the laser system at the core of the facility, and the sample environment, which is UHV-compatible and allows for a wide temperature tunability, represent the key strengths of this facility that operates in the framework of the NFFA research infrastructure and will soon be open to users.

The capabilities of the TRRS technique and of the present setup were illustrated by presenting the results of recently conducted experiments, highlighting the relevance of the integrated information that can be gained from a multi-messenger, time-resolved spectroscopy study. That means mapping the time evolution of the electron, spin, and lattice degrees of freedom in condensed matter systems brought out-of-equilibrium by suitable laser pulses. In detail, we have shown how investigating the de-excitation dynamics in photoexcited semiconductor systems by monitoring both phonons (by TRRS) and electrons (by TR-PES) allows an unambiguous assessment of the dynamics of the electron–electron, electron–phonon, and phonon–phonon interactions. We have also discussed how TRRS is a powerful tool for the study of photoinduced phase transitions, allowing to retrieve information on the photoinduced crystal symmetries and on the role of specific phonon modes in driving the transition.

We highlight that the UHV environment renders this setup suitable for a broad class of experiments that require precise control of the sample environmental conditions. These include surface physics studies, such as investigations of chemisorption processes29 or monitoring the cleanliness of semiconductor surfaces,27 as well as TRRS measurements on air-sensitive materials, including several tellurides and halides compounds of current interest (LaTe3, VI3, CrI3, etc.).

In a broader context, our introductory studies suggest that a multi-messenger study of photoexcited condensed matter systems, requiring the interoperability of different experimental setups, can disclose a comprehensive view of the relaxation pathways toward equilibrium and provide key information on the role of coherent and incoherent relaxation processes. That will also allow envisioning new methods for tailoring material processes by optical manipulation.
