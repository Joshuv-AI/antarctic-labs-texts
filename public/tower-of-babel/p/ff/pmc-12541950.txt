The involvement of stakeholders in computational model development to support decision-making in public health: a scoping review
Authors: Maartje Vriens, Olaf J. Knoester, Judith E. Bosmans, Silke Gabbert, Johannes C. F. Ket, G. Ardine de Wit, Maartje Vriens, Maartje Vriens, Judith E. Bosmans, Silke Gabbert, G. Ardine de Wit, Germie van den Berg, Talitha L. Feenstra, Steef Konings, Dwayne Meijnckens, Jeroen Rodenburg, Laura Shields-Zeeman, Paul Ulrich, Hendrika J. Valkenburg, Ben F. M. Wijnen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe involvement of stakeholders in the development of computational models to support public health policy decisions has gained popularity, as it can enhance the quality of these models. This scoping review aims to synthesise the current body of literature on how stakeholders have been involved in the development of computational models in public health.MethodsA literature search was performed in MEDLINE, APA PsycInfo and Scopus, from inception to October 2023. In addition, supplementary articles were identified through snowballing and a Google Scholar search. We searched for studies that developed computational models, involved stakeholders in the model development process, and focused on public health domains. Two reviewers independently screened abstracts and full-text articles.ResultsA total of 3438 titles and abstracts were screened, 40 articles underwent full-text review, and 17 articles were included in this review, presenting information on 11 unique computational modelling studies. The studies spanned a diverse range of nine public health domains, including areas such as alcohol use and suicide prevention, with most studies developing system dynamics models. A key rationale for adopting a participatory approach was to ensure that the model accurately reflected the context-specific characteristics of the setting being modelled. Various stakeholder groups were involved, predominantly policy-makers, researchers and community representatives. During the development phases of problem mapping, model conceptualisation, and model validation, stakeholders were mostly engaged through participatory workshops. Studies reported that a major strength of the participatory approach was to facilitate transparent consensus-building processes during model development, while the management of stakeholder engagement timelines was experienced a major challenge.ConclusionsInvolving stakeholders in the development of computational models can improve the accurate representation of the model’s context, thereby enhancing the quality and applicability of these models to support decision-making processes. However, there are limited computational modelling studies available in the literature that provide detailed elaboration on their methods of stakeholder engagement. There is a need for improved reporting and evaluation of the impact of participatory modelling processes, as well as more guidance on effective stakeholder involvement, to fully realise the potential of participatory modelling in public health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24683-z.

Background

Within the domain of public health policy-making, computational models can serve as computer-based representations of real-world situations. These models are used to assess and compare the potential impact of various policy options on health outcomes [1–5]. While intervention studies focus on assessing the impact of the intervention on immediate outcomes of the enrolled participants, computational models facilitate the estimation of the long-term effects of policy decisions for a simulated population [4–7]. These models can combine information from various sources, including data derived from intervention studies, observational studies, and economic analyses, thereby facilitating better-informed policy decisions than those based solely on intervention studies. Consequently, computational models are increasingly used in the public health sector to support policy development [1–7]. A common example of such a model is an infectious disease transmission model, which is used to simulate the spread of an infectious disease within a population [8]. This model enables the evaluation of the number of individuals expected to become infected under different public health policy scenarios. In order to ensure that computational models represent reality, it is essential to obtain a thorough understanding of the public health system and the societal changes that are expected to occur following implementation of policy decisions for specific interventions [9, 10].

By incorporating stakeholders in the development process of computational models, a comprehensive understanding of the health system and its dynamics can be achieved [11–13]. Stakeholders may encompass a diverse range of groups, including policy-makers, researchers, public health professionals, healthcare providers, community representatives, industry actors, and individuals with lived experience of a certain condition. Stakeholder engagement in model development can help to ensure that the model is both beneficial and relevant in practical, real-world contexts beyond the academic setting, thereby increasing its utility for decision-makers [14–16]. Examples of stakeholder involvement in model development include reflecting on specific parameters included in the model, opening up discussions within the policy domain, and disseminating the model to a broader audience [2–5]. So far, including stakeholder perspectives from the onset of the model development process is quite rare [17–20]. Perspectives from stakeholders are often sought just after the computational models have been developed and are subsequently used merely as input for considerations by the modelling team, rather than being consistently incorporated into the modelling process [17–20]. This is surprising, as involving stakeholders from the beginning of the model development process, also known as participatory modelling, has proven useful to promote shared knowledge and understanding of a health system [9–12, 17–20]. Additionally, participatory modelling is not only assumed to enhance the credibility and legitimacy of the models developed but can also increase the acceptability of these models among decision-makers [21, 22]. The added value of participatory modelling has also been observed in the process itself, as it facilitates dialogue among different stakeholder groups and advances the accessibility and applicability of technical knowledge for the involved stakeholders [23–25].

While these benefits of participatory modelling in public health are widely acknowledged, involving stakeholders throughout the model development process presents challenges [26]. This participatory approach requires, for instance, the identification of relevant stakeholders for engagement and the formulation of suitable questions for their consideration, particularly considering the complex and technical queries that may arise during computational model development [27–29]. Another challenge relates to the difficulty of selecting the appropriate tools and methods at each stage of the modelling process, considering that participatory modelling comprises a variety of methodologies [30]. Examples include individual interviews, workshops, and constructing causal loop diagrams [30]. To date, there is still limited knowledge and experience regarding how to properly involve stakeholders in the development process of computational models for public health. This knowledge gap has prevented the broader application of participatory modelling, thereby limiting its potential to create more robust and comprehensive models that incorporate diverse perspectives, enhance the implementation of model findings, and reduce the reluctance among non-researchers to utilise these models as tools to support decision-making processes [31–33]. Despite some successful examples of stakeholder engagement in computational model development [34–36], a comprehensive synthesis of the literature on existing approaches to participatory computational modelling in public health is currently lacking. Such a synthesis could enhance the understanding of key processes in participatory model development and contribute to establishing appropriate practices in the domain of public health modelling.

Therefore, the aim of this scoping review is to explore how stakeholders have been involved in the development of computational models in public health. This includes identifying 1) what objectives have been pursued when incorporating stakeholders in computational modelling, 2) what types of stakeholders have been engaged, 3) what processes have been used to involve stakeholders, 4) what results have been produced by the participatory modelling process, and 5) what strengths and limitations have been observed in stakeholder involvement during the model development process.

Methods

Scoping reviews are more appropriate when the objective is to provide an overview of the current state of literature on key characteristics related to a certain topic, as opposed to systematic reviews [37–40]. Additionally, scoping reviews are especially useful when addressing broad research inquiries and clarifying methodological approaches within specific contexts. Therefore, we have chosen to conduct a scoping review. This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews [41]; the corresponding checklist has been provided in Additional file 1. The protocol for this review was prospectively registered on 20 December 2023 in Open Science Framework [42].

Literature search strategy

A comprehensive systematic search was conducted in three academic databases, OVID/MEDLINE, Ebsco/APA PsycInfo and Elsevier/Scopus, to ensure extensive coverage of public health literature. The search spanned from the inception of each database up to October 2023 and was conducted in collaboration with a medical information specialist (JCFK). The search included controlled and free text terms for synonyms of “model development”, “stakeholder involvement” and “public health”. The search was performed without restrictions for methodology, date or language. Duplicate articles were excluded by a medical information specialist (JCFK) using Endnote X20.0.1. (ClarivateTM), following the Amsterdam Efficient Deduplication-method [43] and the Bramer-method [44]. Supplementary articles were obtained through the snowballing technique applied to the references of the articles included in this review and a Google Scholar search for grey literature using comparable keywords to those employed in the academic database search. For the Google Scholar search, the initial 200 references were reviewed. This is in line with a study conducted by Haddaway et al. [45], who explored the utility of using Google Scholar to identify grey literature for literature reviews and concluded that reviewing the first 200 references is sufficient to capture all potentially relevant grey literature from Google Scholar [45]. The full search strategies for all data bases can be found in Additional file 2.

Study selection

Articles were included if they reported studies that 1) developed a computational model capable of performing calculations, 2) involved stakeholders from the onset of the model development process, 3) developed a model with the primary goal of predicting outcomes related to health or wellbeing, and 4) developed a public health focused model, characterised as targeting the health of (groups within) the general population, rather than specific patient populations. Conversely, studies were excluded if they 1) did not comprehensively describe how the input of stakeholders was used in the model development process, or 2) reported a literature review or study protocol.

The titles and abstracts of the retrieved articles were independently screened by two reviewers (MV, OJK). In case of discrepancies in screening decisions, the two reviewers initially attempted to resolve them through discussion. If consensus was not achieved, a third reviewer (GAdW) was consulted to resolve them through further discussion. During the screening process, a subset of the articles was evaluated by all three reviewers (MV, OJK, GAdW) to ensure consistent application of the selection criteria. Afterwards, the eligibility of the remaining full-text articles was screened by two reviewers (MV and either GAdW, JEB or SG). Conflicts were addressed and resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (GAdW, JEB). The reasons for excluding articles were documented throughout the screening process.

Data extraction and synthesis

The data from the studies were extracted by one reviewer (MV), while the other reviewer (GAdW) examined a subset of the extractions to ensure their accuracy. An extended version of the 4P framework, developed by Gray et al. [46], was utilised to extract the data. The 4P framework, which includes the dimensions purpose, partnership, processes, and product, was specifically designed to standardise the reporting of studies using participatory modelling (PM) [46]. One dimension, labelled perceptions, was added to the framework to extract information on the experienced strengths and challenges of stakeholder involvement in the model development process. The resulting framework consisted of five dimensions: 1) purpose; why was the PM approach selected, 2) partnership; who participated and why, 3) processes; how were stakeholders involved, 4) product; what was produced by the modelling process, and 5) perceptions; what were the experienced strengths and challenges of the PM approach [46]. A component of this framework involves assessing the level of stakeholder participation in the model development process. We employed the five levels proposed by the International Association for Public Participation to indicate these levels [47, 48], ranging from least to most involved: 1) inform, 2) consult, 3) involve, 4) collaborate, and 5) empower. Through consistent application of this framework, we ensured consistency in extracted information and facilitation of data synthesis [49]. A detailed explanation of the framework and the levels used is provided in Additional file 3.

Results

The literature search identified 3438 unique records that were subjected to title and abstract screening. Of the identified records, 3409 were excluded during title and abstract screening, with the main reasons for exclusion being that they did not develop a computational model (n = 2071) or did not involve any stakeholders during model development (n = 956). Additionally, 11 articles were identified through snowballing, of which none were eventually included after full-text screening. In total, 40 articles were included in full-text screening. Of these, 23 studies were excluded as a further in-depth review revealed that they 1) did not incorporate input from stakeholders in model development (n = 8), 2) did not develop a public health focused model (n = 6), 3) did not develop a computational model (n = 5), or 4) did not develop a model that focused on predicting health outcomes (n = 4). Finally, 17 articles were included for synthesis, presenting information on 11 unique modelling studies, as the participatory process was sometimes reported separately from the model development process. Purpose (P1): Why was the PM approach selected?

The primary reported rationale for involving stakeholders in model development was to ensure that the model accurately reflected the context-specific characteristics of the public health domain being modelled, including the integration of information on the local context, relevant issues, intervention priorities, and planning considerations [24, 34, 35, 52–54, 57–60]. Another frequently cited reason for participatory modelling was to identify the complexity of multiple interacting factors and their dynamic interactions within a specific public health domain [24, 52, 55, 57, 61, 62]. For example, in the modelling study by Urwannachotima [61, 62], stakeholders were involved to enhance the understanding of the complex oral health system, with a particular focus on the dynamic interactions between a sugar-sweetened beverage tax, sugar consumption, and its potential impact on oral health outcomes.

In all modelling studies, computational models were used to quantify the long-term and/or short-term impact of various interventions on specific health-related outcomes [24, 34–36, 50–62]. These models were developed to assist in selecting the most effective combination of interventions to enhance these health-related outcomes, thereby supporting decision-making processes.

Partnership (P2): Who participated and why?

Most modelling studies (n = 8, 73%) employed purposive sampling to select participants [24, 34, 35, 50–53, 55, 57–62], with a subset of those studies (n = 6, 55%) enlisting additional experts from their network to aid in the selection process [24, 34, 35, 50–52, 55, 57–60].

Table 3 provides an overview of the types and numbers of stakeholders engaged in the participatory processes of the modelling studies. The number of involved stakeholders ranged from ten to fifty individuals, with over half of the modelling studies incorporating more than thirty participants [24, 36, 53, 54, 56–60]. The modelling studies included a diverse selection of stakeholder types in their model development processes. All modelling studies included policy-makers, operating at local and/or national levels. Additionally, other frequently involved stakeholder groups included researchers (n = 10, 91%), community representatives from local communities or individuals with lived experience (n = 8, 73%), and non-governmental organisations (NGOs) such as health charity organisations (n = 7, 64%). Processes (P3): How were stakeholders involved?

All modelling studies conducted participatory workshops to engage stakeholders in the model development process, during which a diverse group of stakeholders convened to contribute to the modelling process. Some modelling studies conducted additional interviews (n = 2, 18%) [34, 35, 61, 62] or surveys (n = 1, 9%) [36] to obtain a more detailed understanding of the context before the participatory workshops took place.

The levels of participation varied among the modelling studies [47, 48]. The majority (n = 9, 82%) adopted the ‘involving’ level of participation [24, 34–36, 50–56, 61, 62], working directly with stakeholders throughout the modelling process to ensure that public concerns and aspirations were consistently understood and considered. Additionally, one modelling study engaged stakeholders at the ‘consulting’ level, which required slightly less involvement from stakeholders [57], whereas another study engaged stakeholders more intensively at the ‘collaborating’ level, partnering with them in each aspect of the model development process [58–60].

Table 4 presents an overview of the phases of computational model development in which stakeholders were involved, along with the corresponding number of participatory sessions. The number of participatory sessions ranged from a minimum of two to a maximum of four sessions, with the majority (n = 5, 45%) involving stakeholders in three sessions [24, 34–36, 50–53]. Various phases of model development were addressed during these sessions. All modelling studies engaged stakeholders during the problem mapping phase, where potential factors and mechanisms within the public health domain were identified, as well as during the model validation phase. Additionally, stakeholders were frequently involved in the model conceptualisation phase (n = 10, 91%) and in the phase of identifying and/or prioritising interventions (n = 8, 73%). Product (P4): What was produced by the modelling process?

The modelling studies employed four different modelling approaches to generate model outcomes. System dynamics models were the most frequently used approach, focusing on capturing overarching trends by representing a system as interconnected stocks and flows (n = 5, 45%) [24, 36, 53–55, 61, 62]. Other modelling approaches included agent-based models, which simulate the behaviour of individual agents and their interactions within an environment (n = 2, 18%) [24, 50, 51, 56], microsimulation models, which focus on individual-level characteristics to simulate how specific interventions might affect individuals over time (n = 2, 18%) [57–60], and hybrid models, which combine techniques from system dynamics, agent-based and discrete event modelling (n = 1, 9%) [24, 52]. Lastly, one study did not specify the modelling approach used (n = 1, 9%) [34, 35].

Social outcomes associated with the inclusion of stakeholders in the modelling process included facilitating dialogues among diverse stakeholder groups [24, 50, 51, 53, 57–60], and fostering the development or strengthening of relationships between these stakeholder groups [24, 34, 35, 52–54]. Furthermore, four modelling studies did not elaborate on the social outcomes achieved through stakeholder engagement in the modelling process [36, 55, 56, 61, 62].

Perceptions (P5): What were the experienced strengths and challenges of the PM approach?

The majority of modelling studies (n = 10, 91%) elaborated on the strengths they experienced through involving stakeholders in their model development process. The most frequently cited strength of employing a participatory approach was its contribution to a transparent consensus-building process in model development, which could ultimately facilitate transparent decision-making processes [24, 36, 50, 51, 54, 58–60]. Another strength highlighted by the modelling studies was the increased confidence of the target audience in the developed model when it was co-created with stakeholders, which enhanced the likelihood that the model’s findings would be used in decision-making processes [24, 34, 35, 52, 58–60]. Furthermore, modelling studies mentioned that adopting a participatory approach allowed for the development of a model that was highly tailored to the specific context it aimed to represent, facilitated by an enhanced contextual understanding derived from the lived and professional experiences of the stakeholders involved [34, 35, 57–60].

Most modelling studies (n = 6, 55%) did not address the challenges encountered when employing a participatory modelling approach. Among the modelling studies that did elaborate on the experienced challenges, the most frequently cited issue was the management of stakeholder engagement timelines, which involved balancing the need to sustain stakeholder commitment throughout the process while ensuring sufficient preparation time between sessions [34, 35, 58–62]. Another significant challenge associated with participatory modelling was the increased resource intensity, both in terms of time and financial investments [57–60]. Substantial preparation and planning were required to facilitate appropriate stakeholder involvement, and additional time was necessary to build and maintain relationships with stakeholders to ensure their ongoing commitment. Furthermore, the technical intricacies of a computational model, which could be difficult for stakeholders to fully comprehend and might have led to the perception among some stakeholders that they were unable to contribute meaningfully, were considered challenging [24, 52, 58–60].

Discussion

In this scoping review, we included 17 articles describing 11 computational models that were developed in collaboration with stakeholders to support decision-making processes in public health. All articles were published in the last decade, indicating a growing interest in adopting a participatory approach. We systematically explored how stakeholders were engaged in the modelling process and synthesised these findings to identify common practices of stakeholder involvement in the development of computational models within public health. To categorise the approaches used, we employed an extended version of the 4P framework, developed by Gray et al. [46]. By adding a fifth dimension, perceptions, we aimed to also extract information on the experienced strengths and challenges of participatory modelling.

Main insights from the analysis of included studies

Approximately half of the included modelling studies provided a comprehensive description of all dimensions of the participatory modelling framework utilised [24, 52, 57–62]. A key purpose of involving stakeholders was to ensure that the model accurately reflected context-specific characteristics, achieved through multiple participatory sessions involving stakeholders from different disciplines, often including policy-makers, researchers and community representatives [24, 34, 35, 52–54, 57–60]. However, the complexity of computational modelling was identified as an important challenge, limiting stakeholder involvement in the technically more complex phases, such as model quantification and execution of model runs [24, 27, 29, 52, 58–60]. Stakeholder participation was therefore primarily focused on problem mapping, model conceptualisation, and model validation.

Comparison with other literature

Sterling et al. [26] described lessons learned from previous participatory modelling studies in the field of environmental planning and conservation. These lessons emphasised the need for a diverse skill set beyond technical modelling expertise, including skills in relationship-building and a willingness to learn from local experts [26]. Additionally, the study highlighted the importance of carefully determining which activities of the modelling process should involve stakeholders and which should remain the responsibility of the modelling team. A related aspect is addressed in a study by Hämäläinen et al. [63], who explored whether the modelling team or the stakeholders should take the lead in participatory modelling. They argue that the primary responsibilities of the lead are to avoid technical errors and to ensure stakeholder input is integrated in the modelling process [63]. As this necessitates a comprehensive understanding of the technical aspects inherent to computational modelling, the modelling team should assume leadership and maintain responsibility for the technical processes, such as model quantification, while stakeholders should primarily provide input before and after these technical processes.

Over the past two decades, there has been a growing trend towards involving individuals beyond the scientific community into research activities, practices commonly referred to as citizen science and patient and public involvement in research [64–68]. Unlike traditional stakeholder engagement, which primarily involves groups with a direct stake or interest in a specific study, these approaches broaden participation by including a wider range of contributors from the general public. Several studies have indicated that a broader public involvement in research has been frequently implemented in recent years [64, 65]. However, researchers often demonstrate reluctance in adopting participatory research approaches, questioning whether members of the general public possess the necessary skills to effectively contribute to research [26, 66–71]. The findings of our review seem to support this view, particularly given the technical complexity characterising the focus of our review [24, 27, 29, 52, 58–60]. Therefore, the extent to which groups such as citizens can become more involved in the development of computational models also depends on scientific advancements in citizen science.

Although involving stakeholders is expected to increase the likelihood of model applicability and model utilisation [24, 31, 32, 34, 35, 52, 58–60], our review does not address whether this is indeed the case. Consequently, we cannot determine whether the additional time and effort required for stakeholder involvement is justified for specific studies. Oliver et al. [72] discussed whether the costs of engaging stakeholders outweigh the additional value for health research. They highlight the importance of carefully considering which stages of the process require stakeholder involvement and identifying circumstances where alternative approaches may be more appropriate [72]. Failure to do so can result in participatory processes that compromise, rather than enhance, the quality of the research [72]. Additionally, they emphasise the importance of studies to critically evaluate the impact of stakeholder involvement on model development. A framework such as the one proposed by Lee et al. [73] could assist in the systematic evaluation of participatory processes. This framework suggests that modelling studies should report on the following four dimensions: feasibility, value, change/action, and sustainability [73]. Reporting on these dimensions can support other researchers in assessing the realised impact of the participatory modelling process utilised, potentially encouraging them to adopt a participatory approach themselves.

Recommendations and implications for future research

Our review synthesises the most common practices for stakeholder engagement in the development of computational models in public health. While there seems to be recognition of the importance and added value of stakeholder engagement, researchers may lack the required expertise to systematically involve stakeholders in their modelling processes [26, 30]. Based on our findings, we have identified three recommendations for future research.

First, modelling studies used diverse approaches for their inclusion of stakeholders during the development of computational models for public health [30]. Although we identified several useful examples, establishing best practices is challenging due to the diverse approaches employed. The approach adopted should be tailored to the aims of the specific study and is dependent on the context and the stakeholders involved, as well as on the resources available for stakeholder engagement in the model development process. Still, more guidance on participatory modelling is essential. While general guidance on design principles for engaging stakeholders in research is available [74], there is a need to develop specific guidelines on how to meaningfully involve various stakeholders in computational modelling studies within the public health sector [29].

Second, current reporting practices in the field of participatory modelling should be improved. Many modelling studies lack detailed descriptions of the participatory processes they employed, and instead predominantly focus on the computational models developed [34, 46, 51]. A framework such as the one used in this review, proposed by Gray et al. [46], could assist in the systematic reporting of participatory processes. Reporting across multiple dimensions on stakeholder involvement can support other researchers in assessing the quality of the participatory modelling processes utilised [46]. The use of a framework promotes more rigorous and consistent reporting on all aspects of participatory modelling, which is essential for advancing the field and enhancing its application in public health [46].

Third, while studies acknowledge that involving stakeholders in model development requires additional time investments and imposes extra effort on modellers due to the iterative nature of the process [57–60], they frequently overlook the substantial time commitment made by stakeholders themselves. Therefore, it is important to ensure that stakeholders who participate in the process also derive benefits from their involvement. For example, once the development phase has been finalised, sharing the model results with stakeholders and empowering them to use the model independently provides a tangible deliverable from the modelling study [75, 76]. In addition to traditional dissemination methods, such as the distribution of reports and presentations to stakeholders [75, 76], recent literature indicates a growing trend towards the publication of open-source models, for example on cloud-based platforms [77]. Sharing models through these cloud-based platforms increases its accessibility, allowing not only the modelling team but also the general public to interact with the model and conduct simulations from any location [77].

Limitations

We identified only 11 participatory modelling studies for inclusion in the current scoping review. Since we included only studies that described their stakeholder involvement methods, the selected studies may not provide an exhaustive overview of all studies employing participatory computational modelling in the public health context. Additionally, another constraint of this study is the lack of an extensive search for grey literature, apart from the Google Scholar search that was conducted. Participatory modelling may be underrepresented in the academic literature, as certain participatory modelling activities, particularly those employed by public health professionals rather than academic researchers, may not have been documented academically, despite potentially offering valuable insights into stakeholder involvement. Another limitation is that we included only studies that developed computational models, which may have resulted in the exclusion of potentially relevant studies that described participatory processes for the development of non-computational models. Nonetheless, the participatory approach may be different for computational and non-computational models.

Conclusions

Throughout the past decade, the involvement of stakeholders in computational model development within public health has gained popularity, as it can improve the accurate representation of the model’s context, thereby enhancing the quality and applicability of the models to support decision-making processes. Our scoping review illustrates that the number of modelling studies that elaborate on their methods of stakeholder involvement is still limited. There is a pressing need to improve both the reporting and evaluation of the impact of participatory modelling processes, as well as to develop guidelines for effectively involving diverse stakeholders in such complex environments, to fully realise the potential of participatory modelling. Ultimately, fostering robust stakeholder involvement practices will be essential for advancing the effectiveness and sustainability of computational models in public health.
