Indoor Air Pollutants of PM2.5, Carbon Monoxide, Nicotine, and Carcinogens in Waterpipe Bars
Authors: Jung Jae Lee, Tzu Tsun Luk, Man Ping Wang, Mu-Hsing Ho, Yongyang Yan, Kin Fai Ho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWaterpipe smoking has become popular in East Asia and other regions. However, few studies have investigated the indoor air quality, including the presence of carcinogens, in waterpipe bars (WBs). This study aimed to compare indoor air quality between WBs and non-WBs in Hong Kong, focusing on differences observed during weekdays and weekends.MethodsAir samples from 8 WBs and 6 non-WBs were collected to investigate levels of fine particulate matter (PM2.5), carbon monoxide (CO), nicotine, volatile organic compounds (VOCs), and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) in the ambient air of these bars. Samples were collected for 2 h in each bar during both a weekday and a weekend evening. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and Wilcoxon signed-rank test.ResultsThe median PM2.5 levels in WBs was 332.1 μg/m3 on weekdays and 1001 μg/m3 on weekends. Similarly, CO levels in WBs were 22.5 ppm on weekdays 27.9 ppm on weekends. The comparison showed that PM2.5 (p = .0019) and CO (p = .0019) levels in WBs were significantly higher than those in non-WBs on weekdays. Airborne nicotine levels were 3.2 μg/m3 on weekdays and 16.1 μg/m3 on weekends. Most VOCs and PAHs levels were higher during weekends, with benzo[a]pyrene at 15.3 ng/m3 (weekday) and 22.5 ng/m3 (weekend), and Formaldehyde at 57 μg/m3 (weekday) and 66.5 μg/m3 (weekend)—Group 1 carcinogens.ConclusionThis study revealed alarmingly high levels of airborne PM2.5, CO, nicotine, and carcinogens such as Benzo[a]pyrene and Formaldehyde in WBs in Hong Kong, especially on weekends.ImplicationsThis study provides the first evidence of air quality in waterpipe establishments in Asia, demonstrating extremely poor air quality that pose significant health risks to both patrons and staff. These results can serve as evidence for the strict enforcement of tobacco control policies, such as total waterpipe bans. It is also crucial to assess compliance with occupational safety regulations in WBs and to ensure employees exposed to high levels of harmful substances such as PM2.5, CO, nicotine, and carcinogens are offered education and health screenings and education. These measures are vital to ensuring the safety and health of workers.

Introduction

The waterpipe, also known as shisha, hookah, or narghile, is a device used for smoking tobacco. This device heats the tobacco using lit charcoals, producing smoke that passes through water before being inhaled by the user. Initially, waterpipe smoking (WPS) gained popularity in the Eastern Mediterranean region, but over the past decades, it has rapidly expanded to become a global phenomenon.1–3 WPS negatively affects air quality, particularly in indoor environments such as home and waterpipe establishments. Research has consistently shown that first-hand, second-hand, and side-stream waterpipe smoke contains harmful toxicants. These toxicants include carbon monoxide (CO),4–6 particle matter (PM),7–9 nicotine,10,11 polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs),12,13 volatile organic compounds (VOCs),14,15 and heavy metals.9,15 Therefore, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis16 reported that WPS is associated with various negative health effects including cancers, CO poisoning, cardiovascular and respiratory diseases, and reproductive and birth outcomes.

Toxicants from WPS are often compared to those found in cigarette smoke. Systematic reviews indicated that waterpipe smoke contains as many, if not more, toxicants, and the volume of these toxicants in waterpipe smoke is typically higher than that in cigarette smoke.17,18 For instance, Daher et al.4 reported that a single session of WPS emits around four times more VOCs and 30 times more CO than cigarette smoking. This significantly higher volume of toxicants can be attributed to the longer duration of a WPS session (~1 h) compared to cigarette smoking, and the use of lit charcoal.19,20 Previous studies identified that the lit charcoal used for WPS significantly emits CO, VOCs, and PAHs.15,21 Notably, the air quality in WPS venues was worse than in cigarette smoking venues, with higher levels of airborne pollutants found particularly indoors.11,22 In response to the growing concern surrounding WPS, governments worldwide have made substantial efforts to implement smoke-free laws and develop WPS-specific regulations in order to control WPS activities.23,24 However, the effectiveness of these legal measures has been questioned because of reports of violations and non-compliance across various countries.23–26 In Hong Kong (HK), the Smoking (Public Health) Ordinance (Cap. 371) bans all smoking in public indoor areas, including WPS venues. Despite this, indoor WPS remains prevalent in Hong Kong venues,20,26,27 indicating a need for more stringent enforcement and increased public awareness about the health risks associated with WPS.

While the association between poor indoor air quality because of smoke emissions and disease has been well-established in laboratory settings, there is a scarcity of studies that investigate real-world measurements of indoor air quality and airborne carcinogens (eg, PAHs and VOCs) in WPS venues such as waterpipe bars (WBs) and cafes.13,15,28,29 Conducting measurements in real-life settings can help clarify the actual situation in WPS venues and raise public awareness about the harms of waterpipe smoke. WPS patterns and venue characteristics can vary between cities;29 this is especially important in high-density cities such as HK, where closely packed buildings and limited air flow may exacerbate the air quality in WPS venues. This could potentially make the air quality in these venues worse than both laboratory settings and WPS venues in less crowded cities.30 Consequently, this study aimed to measure indoor air pollutants, including airborne PM2.5, CO, nicotine, VOCs, and PAHs, in WPS venues in HK to better understand the real-world conditions and potential health risks associated with WPS.

Methods

Study Design and Venue Selection

Indoor air quality measurements, including PM2.5, CO, nicotine, VOCs, and PAHs, were conducted in WBs in Hong Kong between February 14 and April 30, 2018. To identify WBs that allow indoor smoking, we used internet search engines such as “Google Map,” “Apple Map,” “Yelp,” and “OpenRice,” with keywords “shisha,” “hookah,” “waterpipe,” “bar,” “cafe,” and “lounge.” Most WBs in HK are located in busy areas including Central, Causeway Bay, Wan Chai, and Tsim Sha Tsui. We conducted preliminary visits to each WB to confirm its suitability for the air quality measurements. WBs were included in the study if they allowed indoor WPS and were excluded if they (1) were located on the ground floor with doors or windows that opened toward traffic ways (open plan), to minimize traffic pollutants affecting data collection; and (2) operated a restaurant alongside the bar to prevent pollutants from cooking. We also identified non-WBs that do not offer WPS to compare the indoor air quality between WBs and non-WBs.

Field Sampling

The research team (the first author and two-trained research assistants) unobtrusively collected air samples in the selected WBs and non-WBs. The main bodies of measurement monitors and pumps were concealed in one backpack and two hand-carry bags, while tube tips, samplers, and monitor probes of the devices were protruded from the bags to collect air samples. Upon entering the bars, the research team sat in the center of the bars and placed the tube tips, samplers, and monitor probes ~1 m above the ground.

PM2.5 levels were measured using a TSI SidePak AM510 Personal Aerosol Monitor (TSI, Shoreview, MN, USA) with a calibration factor of 0.32.31 The collected data was downloaded using the TSL TrackPro Version 4.7.1.0 software. CO levels, along with temperature and humidity, were measured with a TSI Q-TRAK Indoor Air Quality Monitor 7565 (TSI, Shoreview, MN, USA) and transferred via the same software. All CO measurements in this study were within the device’s specified linear range (0-500 ppm) and within the detection limit of the instrument. All real-time measurement data were recorded at 1-min intervals.

For the measurement of airborne nicotine, PAHs (eg, Benzo[a]pyrene), and VOCs (eg, Formaldehyde), sampling began upon arrival at the bars and stopped immediately after leaving to prevent contamination by external pollutants. Notably, airborne nicotine and PAHs were only measured in WBs. Airborne nicotine was collected using XAD-2 sorbent tubes with a 44XR Universal Sample Pump (SKC Inc., Eighty-Four, PA, USA). VOCs were measured using Sep-Pak XPoSure Aldehyde Samplers (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) with a Gillian Air Flow Sampler Model HFS-513A (Sensidyne, St. Petersburg, FL, USA). PAHs were measured using 37 mm Pallflex Tissuquartz filters (New Star Environmental, Inc., Roswell, GA, USA) with an SKC Leland Legacy Pump (SKC Inc., Eighty-Four, PA, USA).

The air quality measurements in each bar lasted for ~2 h during peak times (ie, between 19:00 and 1:00). These measurements were conducted in each bar twice: once during a non-peak day (weekdays) and once during a peak day (weekends). Additionally, during the sampling periods, we observed and recorded the number of active waterpipes indoors every 15 min. We also noted other sources of indoor air pollution, such as cigarette and e-cigarette smoke, besides waterpipe smoke and charcoal emissions.

Quality Assurance/Quality Control

To ensure the accuracy of the data, the following strategies were adopted for real-time measurements. Before each PM2.5 data collection, we used a zero filter (TSI, Shoreview, MN, USA) to calibrate the PM2.5 monitor for optimal results. After collecting CO data, a TAPI Gas Calibrator Model T750 (Teledyne API, San Diego, CA, USA) was used to verify the accuracy of the CO monitor. The CO monitor was exposed to four concentrations of CO (0, 5, 10, and 15 ppm). The differences between the measured and reference concentrations ranged from −0.2 to +0.1 ppm, which is within the manufacturer’s specified accuracy (±1% of reading whichever is greater). The data were adjusted accordingly. Before sampling airborne nicotine, VOCs and PAHs, a BIOS DryCal DC-Lite air flow calibrator (Mesa Laboratories, Inc., Butler, NJ, USA) was used to calibrate the flow rate at 10 L/min ± 0.5 L/min, 1 L/min ± 0.05 L/min, and 1 L/min ± 0.05 L/min for the sampling pumps, respectively. The flow rate of each pump was recorded before and after sampling to calculate the volume of the samples. Prior to sampling, the filters were pre-heated at 780°C for 3 h to eliminate any absorbed organic vapors. Each filter was pre-conditioned by weighing at least twice before and after sampling, and they were equilibrated in a controlled environment with a temperature of 22 ± 2°C and a relative humidity of 35%–45% for a minimum of 24 h. The maximum differences between the replicate weights were <15 μg for blank filters and <20 μg for loaded filters. The collected samples were immediately sealed and stored in an ice bag after the measurements. Once at the lab, the samples were packed in pre-baked aluminum foil and stored in a freezer at −20°C to prevent semi-volatile losses before chemical analyses.

Data Analysis

To improve the accuracy of the indoor air quality analyses, we excluded PM2.5 and CO data recorded within the first minute after entering and the last minute before leaving the bars. Descriptive statistics were used to report the levels of PM2.5, CO, nicotine, VOCs, and PAHs in each bar during weekdays and weekends. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was conducted to compare the levels of pollutants between weekdays and weekends, while the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied to examine differences between WBs and non-WBs.

Using in-injection port-thermal desorption-gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (TD-GC/MS),32 non-polar organic compounds, including nicotine, VOCs, and PAHs, were quantified. Filter sections (0.53–2.63 cm2) were located into a TD tube for placement in the GC injector port at 50°C. The temperature was subsequently increased to 275°C for desorption in splitless mode, while maintaining the GC oven temperature at 30°C. Once reaching the specified injector temperature, the oven program commenced. The mass spectrometer detector conducted a full scan from 50 to 650 amu with electron impact ionization at a voltage of 70 eV and 230°C ion source temperature. Identification was achieved by matching characteristic ion fragments and peak retention times with those of the standards. Further information on how analytes were quantified, as well as details regarding the verification of their sources and purities, can be found in Supplementary File S1.

Results

We identified 8 WBs and 6 non-WBs for this study. The sizes of WB venues ranged from 38.5 to 101.45 m2, while non-WB venues ranged from 53.12 to 248.98 m2. Most of these bars were located in Central HK. The median PM2.5 levels in WBs was 332.1 μg/m3 on weekdays and 1001 μg/m3 on weekends, while non-WBs recorded levels of 36.7 μg/m3 on weekdays and 27.9 μg/m3 on weekends. Similarly, median CO levels in WBs were 22.5 ppm on weekdays and 27.9 ppm on weekends, compared to 0.8 and 0.71 ppm in non-WBs, respectively.

The analysis showed that PM2.5 (p = .0019) and CO (p = .0019) levels in WBs were significantly higher than those in non-WBs on weekdays. Additionally, PM2.5 (p = .0019) and CO (p = .0019) levels in WBs were also significantly higher on weekends compared to non-WBs (Tables 1 and 2).

Discussion

The analysis revealed that air quality in WBs in HK was significantly poor, including the presence of airborne carcinogens. Additionally, we compared the air quality between weekdays and weekends in both WBs and non-WBs. These are the first findings in East Asia.

As waterpipe smoke contains toxicants, WPS significantly pollutes indoor air quality.28 Several studies have measured indoor air quality in real-world settings (eg, waterpipe café and bars), using tobacco smoke markers such as nicotine and general air quality markers including PM2.5 and CO.7,8,28,29,31,33–35 These studies reported mean PM2.5 levels ranging from 92 to 1419 μg/m3 (median 554 μg/m3); mean CO levels ranging from 3.9 to 65 ppm (median 18 ppm); and nicotine levels ranging from 1.42 to 5.3 μg/m3 (median 3.75 μg/m3). While a direct comparison between these findings and the findings from this study is difficult because of different contexts, the PM2.5 (332.1 μg/m3; 1001 μg/m3), CO (22.5 ppm; 27.9 ppm), and nicotine (3.2 μg/m3; 16.1 μg/m3) levels in HK WBs appear more serious, particularly during the weekends. The high levels of air pollutants in HK can be attributed to the poor ventilation and the high density of buildings in the area.36 Additionally, WPS is more common on weekend nights in HK when young people have more free time to attend social gatherings and relax in WBs.26 This results in poorer air quality during weekends. United States (US) Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)37 and the World Health Organization (WHO)38 set the 24-h PM2.5 standard level as 12 and 15 μg/m3, respectively. Particularly, the EPA indicated that 24-h PM2.5 at 250.5 μg/m3 or higher is hazardous. WHO also recommended the 24-h CO level as 4 mg/m3 (=3.43 ppm).38 The levels of PM2.5 and CO in HK WBs significantly exceed standard and hazardous levels, particularly on weekends. High levels of PM2.5 and CO can cause cardiovascular and respiratory illnesses, increasing morbidity.38 Particularly, the hazardous level of PM2.5 in this study (maximum 5331 μg/m3 in WBs) could cause acute respiratory infections among the smokers and other patrons in the bars. Nicotine exposure is associated with significant health risks. Existing studies reported airborne nicotine levels in cigarette smoking venues to be between 1.83 and 3.5 μg/m3, while smoke-free bars showed levels between 0.003 and 0.2 μg/m3.39,40 These nicotine levels in cigarette smoking venues are comparable to those found in previous WPS studies. However, the findings in this study indicate significantly higher nicotine levels in WBs compared to the cigarette smoking venues and other WPS studies. This discrepancy may also be attributed to poor ventilation and the high density of buildings in HK.

Existing studies have predominantly focused on investigating air pollutants such as airborne PMs, CO, and nicotine in WPS premises.4–7,10 However, studies on PAHs and VOCs—many of which are known carcinogens—remain relatively limited, despite the well-established association between WPS and increased cancer risk.16,41 Additionally, most of the studies primarily took place in laboratory settings rather than real-world settings.13,15 Among the PAHs measured in the study, Benzo[a]anthracene, Benzo[a]pyrene, Benzo[b]fluoranthene, Benzo[k]fluoranthene, Chrysene, Dibenzo[a,h]anthracene, and Indeno (1,2,3-cd) pyrene are classified as carcinogenic (Groups 1, 2A, and 2B) according to IARC. Notably, Benzo[a]pyrene is classified as Group 1 carcinogen, indicating there is sufficient evidence to support its link to cancer in human. While there is no safe level, the European Union set the permittable value of Benzo[a]pyrene in ambient air at 1 ng/m3.42 The median levels of Benzo[a]pyrene in WBs in this study exceeded this limit by more than 15 times on weekdays and 22 times on weekends, indicating that patrons and staff in WBs are at high risk of skin and lung cancers.

VOCs are a group of chemicals emitted as gases or liquids from various objects. The adverse health effects of exposure to VOCs are well-documented in literature. Some VOCs such as Formaldehyde and Acetaldehyde are also known to be carcinogenic. According to IARC, Formaldehyde and Acetaldehyde are classified as Group 1 and Group 2B carcinogens, respectively. WHO43 recommended the indoor air quality for Formaldehyde should be 100 μg/m3 or lower for 30 min, while Health Canada44 set the limit at 123 μg/m3 for 1 h and 50 μg/m3 for 8 h. The median Formaldehyde level in WBs in this study was 57 μg/m3 (weekday) and 66.5 μg/m3 (weekend). Although these levels are lower than the permissible short-term exposure limit, it can be deduced that patrons and staff in WBs in HK are exposed to significant health risks from Formaldehyde because of their prolonged stays of around 2 h or longer.20

This study identified extremely poor air quality in WBs in HK, which poses serious health risks to both patrons and staff. These findings provide compelling evidence to support the implementation of a total ban on WPS. Additionally, the results underscore the need for stringent enforcement of tobacco control policies, including complete indoor smoking bans. Strengthening regulations related to WPS has been widely recommended in the literature as an effective strategy for protecting public health.41 Furthermore, it is essential to investigate the compliance with occupation safety laws in WBs and ensure employees exposed to high levels of PM2.5, CO, nicotine, PAHs, and VOCs are offered education and health screenings. This would help ensure occupational safety and health. Further research is necessary to explore the acute and long-term health impacts of WPS on both waterpipe smokers and staff in WBs using reliable biomarkers.

This study has several limitations. The generalizability of the findings may be limited because of the small sample size and the convenience sampling of the venues. The use of waterpipe tobacco, charcoal, and the building ventilation systems can vary across different venues, which may affect the accuracy of air quality measurements. Additionally, the smoking patterns of waterpipe smokers may vary, impacting the study results. We also had limited capacity to monitor all burning sources and ventilation activities, such as opening windows and doors, during each measurement period. The active waterpipe devices in use varied among the bars, further influencing the measurements. We did not measure nicotine and PAHs in non-WBs, as we assumed there were no burning sources or tobacco smoking in those venues. This absence of measurements may limit the understanding of the air quality levels in non-WBs. Lastly, some loss of highly VOCs during filter equilibration is possible. This potential underestimation should be considered when interpreting the VOC results.

Conclusion

This study provided the first evidence of indoor air quality in WBs in East Asia, highlighting significant differences between weekdays and weekends. We found alarmingly high levels of airborne PM2.5, CO, nicotine, and carcinogens such as Benzo[a]pyrene and Formaldehyde, particularly during weekends. This evidence underscores the urgent need for policymakers and healthcare professionals to implement stricter regulations to control indoor WPS, thereby protecting public health and the occupational health of WB staff.
