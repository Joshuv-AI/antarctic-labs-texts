Spatial heterogeneity and drivers of social vulnerability in chinese floodplains: a PCA-MGWR approach
Authors: Linzhen Yang, Ying Zhang, Quanyi Zheng, Mengxiao Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe spatial heterogeneity of social vulnerability in China’s floodplains remains underexplored, particularly regarding how rapid urbanization and unique demographic shifts-specifically the “floating population” and an aging society-reshape human-water relationships. This study quantifies flood exposure across 361 Chinese cities by integrating the 2020 Seventh National Population Census with high-resolution (250 m) floodplain maps via a pixel-based spatial aggregation approach. This methodology captures both absolute population scales and relative structural dependencies within hazard zones, effectively eliminating estimation bias from administrative-level averages. We propose a coupled Principal Component Analysis and Multiscale Geographically Weighted Regression (PCA-MGWR) framework to mitigate multicollinearity among socioeconomic variables while capturing spatially varying scale effects. The analysis identifies four distinct vulnerability dimensions: floating population (PC-FP), livelihood instability (PC-LI), low education (PC-EL), and lack of action capabilities (PC-AC). The PCA-MGWR model (R2 = 0.713) significantly outperforms traditional OLS and GWR models in explanatory power. Crucially, results reveal a “Paradox of Wealth Exposure” along the developed Yangtze River Economic Belt, where affluent regions exhibit higher flood exposure, challenging the conventional poverty-driven vulnerability narrative. Furthermore, the driving mechanisms exhibit significant spatial non-stationarity: (1) In eastern megacities, the floating population shows a statistical “risk aversion” effect driven by housing market sorting, yet remains vulnerable due to “institutional segregation” in emergency management; (2) In old industrial bases and eastern plains, an “in-situ aging” phenomenon has created a “spatial lock-in,” trapping elderly populations in high-risk zones. These findings suggest that “mobility” and “aging” have emerged as critical drivers of vulnerability in China alongside economic factors. Consequently, we advocate for spatially differentiated governance strategies, shifting from “one-size-fits-all” defenses to adaptive policies that address specific regional socio-hydrological traps.Keywords: Flood exposure, Social vulnerability, PCA-MGWR, Spatial heterogeneity, Floating population, Paradox of wealth exposureSubject terms: Development studies, Environmental social sciences, Environmental studies, Geography, Geography, Natural hazards, Social policy

Introduction

Due to the combined influence of global climate changes and rapid urbanization, flooding is projected to impact approximately two billion people globally, positioning it as one of the most catastrophic natural disasters1. Additionally, according to the “UN World Water Development Report 2020”, the effects of flooding are distributed unequally. This disparity compounds existing social inequities, driving Disadvantaged Populations (DPs) deeper into the vulnerability trap. Recognizing these challenges, the Sustainable Development Goals of the United Nations2, particularly Goal 13 (climate action) and Goal 11 (sustainable cities and communities), highlight the urgency of reducing climate hazards and promoting Environmental Justice.

As the country with the largest population, China has a long cultural history that, to a great extent, represents a history of coexistence between humans and flooding. However, since the 21st century, China’s unprecedented urbanization has led to the concentration of population and assets along riverbanks and low-lying areas. This creates a notable situation of “competition for land between humans and water”, with significant populations settling in floodplains. Data from “China Report of Flood and Drought Disasters 2023” indicates that 52.7893 million people were affected by flooding in 2023 alone, causing direct asset damage amounting to about USD 2.8 billion. This situation gives rise to a complicated scientific and ethical question: within the special context of China’s social and economic development, who actually lives in areas affected by flooding? Do these vulnerable populations reside in the dangerous areas involuntarily due to poverty, or do wealthy individuals live there actively, driven by the pursuit of economic gains along the rivers?

While existing work about flooding has largely centered on hydrological physical process simulation and assessments of economic losses3–5, the literature of hazard-bearing bodies remains insufficient, particularly regarding social fragility arising from socio-economic disparities. Existing studies have extensively utilized the Social Vulnerability Index (SoVI) to quantify community resilience, with a focus on static demographic indicators6. However, as environmental hazards become more frequent, scholars have begun to emphasize the dynamic nature of vulnerability, transitioning from simple exposure assessments to complex social-ecological-technological systems (SETS) frameworks7. Numerous empirical research has been conducted on social inequity in floodplains in the western world, such as African and Latin American communities8,9. However, these findings cannot be directly applied in China. Flood types in China are complex, encompassing fluvial floods, flash floods (mountain torrents), and urban pluvial floods (waterlogging). These are profoundly influenced by the household registration system, rapid population mobility, and regional development imbalances10. To date, studies on China’s entire territory have focused primarily on physical exposure11. Recent scholarship has started to integrate China’s unique socio-institutional factors—such as the “Hukou” system and the massive “floating population”—into the vulnerability discourse12. Despite these advances, the literature still lacks a nation-wide, high-resolution mapping that captures the non-stationary relationship between these unique Chinese drivers and flood exposure. However, there is insufficient mapping of social characteristics of floodplain populations (such as population mobility, age structure, and livelihood patterns). In particular, there is a lack of in-depth investigation into how these factors drive spatially differentiated exposure. While emerging studies have begun to utilize street-level data and local climate zones to analyze the complex driving mechanisms of vulnerability in Chinese megacities13, such high-resolution integrated assessments remain scarce in the context of nation-wide floodplains.

There are two methodological challenges to revealing the complex “human-land relationship”. First, China’s socio-hydrological mechanism exhibits significant spatial heterogeneity, ranging from dry river valleys in the northwestern interior to the extensive delta regions of the southeastern coast. Traditional global regression models overlook spatial heterogeneity, rendering them prone to ecological fallacy. Although GWR has been widely used to address spatial heterogeneity, its fixed bandwidth cannot capture the varying spatial influence ranges of different variables14. To overcome these limitations, the Multi-scale Geographically Weighted Regression (MGWR) has emerged as a superior tool for analyzing spatial processes operating at different scales, yet its susceptibility to multicollinearity when dealing with high-dimensional census data remains a significant hurdle15. Second, variations of socioeconomic factors, including income, education and employment rate, exhibit multicollinearity. This directly leads to biased coefficient estimations in regression models, thereby misleading policy formulation.

To bridge these gaps in theory and method, this study develops a coupled framework integrating principal component analysis and multiscale geographically weighted regression (PCA-MGWR). Utilizing the data from the 7th National Population Census of China (2020) and spatial data on flood hazards, this study covers 361 Chinese cities, aiming to address three core questions: (1) To investigate the spatial distribution of population exposure to flooding in China’s floodplains. (2) To identify the key dimensions of social fragility with Chinese characteristics. (3) To reveal how the impact mechanisms of these dimensions on flood exposure vary across different geographical divisions.

The contribution of this study lies in transcending the sole perspective of natural disasters, attempting to elucidate the spatial coupling mechanisms between environmental hazards and the social population structure. PCA was employed to eliminate multicollinearity and extract the fragility factors, such as population mobility and livelihood capabilities. By combining PCA and MGWR, this study captures the spatial heterogeneity of these processes. The study aims to reveal the complex relationship of “risk-development” in the Chinese context, providing scientific support for formulating inclusive flood control policies based on regional differences.

Data and methods

Study area

Given the vast territory and complex landscape of China, to facilitate analysis, the country is divided into seven regions according to physical geographical features and administrative divisions, including the Northeast (NE), Northern China (NC), Eastern China (EC), Central China (CC), Southern China (SC), Southwest (SW), and Northwest (NW). The blue areas along the Yangtze, the Yellow, the Pearl and Huai Rivers in Fig. 1 represent the floodplains, which serve as the primary hazard-affected bodies. These areas are not only the core regions for agriculture and economy, but also the most densely populated. Consequently, these regions are prone to significant casualties and economic losses from floods.

Study area.

From a spatial perspective (Fig. 1), the distribution of floodplains presents clear geographical features: in the SW region, it is mainly concentrated in plateau lakes and upstream basins of rivers; in the NC region, it is primarily located in downstream areas; while in the plains of middle and lower reaches of Yangtze River, it covers major urban agglomerations. Moreover, China is one of the countries most highly exposed to flood hazards. From 1990 to 2010, the four deadliest flood events globally all occurred in China. With increasing extreme precipitation due to climate change, and the growing concentration of economy and population in floodplains, unprecedented challenges from social vulnerability have emerged.

Floodplain data

The high-precision GFPLAIN250m dataset used in this study was derived from NASA SRTM elevation model and hydrological-geomorphological algorithm16. With a spatial resolution of 250 m, this dataset accurately identifies the floodplain boundaries worldwide. In contrast to hydrodynamic models that necessitate specific rainfall intensities as direct inputs, this hydro-geomorphic approach identifies the maximal potential hazard zones based on terrain and drainage characteristics. These zones represent the physical landscape most susceptible to inundation during extreme precipitation events, thereby serving as a robust and spatially consistent proxy for national-scale flood exposure assessments. Distribution of floodplains across 361 cities in mainland China was derived from the vector boundaries of administrative divisions published in 2024 by the National Geomatics Center of China (NGCC). Subsequently, the floodplain coverage ratio (representing relative exposure) was calculated by dividing the total area of the identified 250-meter binary floodplain mask within a city’s administrative boundary by the city’s total land area.

Although megacities like Shenzhen and Beijing have adopted avoidance strategies in their urban planning, Fig. 1 shows that avoiding floodplains imposes constraints on urban development. Differences in such physical exposure constitute the foundation of the spatial differentiation of social vulnerability.

Spatial downscaling of population data

To address the limitation of spatial resolution in conventional statistical data, this study utilizes a “top-down” spatial downscaling method. Data from the 7th National Population Census of China (2020) were used in this study, which serve as the latest and authoritative census data. The census data and 100-meter resolution gridded data from WorldPop Open Population Repository (WOPR) were calibrated and integrated to generate a high-resolution raster map of China’s population distribution.

The actual exposed population in each city was accurately calculated by spatial overlay of calibrated population raster and floodplain raster. Specifically, the absolute flood exposure (Ei) for each city i was quantified using a pixel-based aggregation method. We overlaid the 100-meter resolution calibrated population density grid with the binary floodplain mask (where 1 indicates floodplain and 0 indicates non-floodplain). The exposure level was determined by summing the population values of all pixels j situated within the floodplain boundaries of city i:

where Pij denotes the population count in pixel j. This spatial intersection approach effectively eliminates the estimation bias inherent in administrative-level averages.

Social vulnerability variables

Based on the framework of the Social Vulnerability Index (SoVI) proposed by Cutter et al.,6 and combined with China’s unique social policies, such as the household registration system and population mobility, nine key indicators were selected to reflect the multi-dimensional nature of disaster susceptibility. The selection criteria prioritized indicators that represent the three core stages of a flood disaster: preparedness, emergency evacuation, and post-disaster recovery. To ensure spatial consistency across all variables, the distribution of specific age groups (i.e., children under 5 and the elderly over 65) was generated using a spatial weight-based downscaling method. Specifically, we multiplied the city-level proportions of these demographic groups, as reported in the statistical yearbook and census, by the 100-meter resolution total population raster. This approach allows for the estimation of the spatial density of vulnerable age groups while preserving the high-resolution physical distribution patterns of the general population. Social vulnerability variables.

Economic dimension: urban-rural resident’s disposable income (DI), wages of on-the-job staff and workers (SL), and unemployment rate (UE). Economic capital directly determines the capacity for disaster preparedness and post-disaster recovery.

Demographic structure dimension: ratio of the elderly to the young population (AC), proportion of people living alone (LA), and the proportion of unmarried women (UW). These individuals usually face limitations in mobility and cognitive abilities during disaster evacuations17.

Social adaptation dimension: proportion of rental population (RP), proportion of illiterate people (EL), and medical insurance coverage rate (MI). Renters and less-educated groups tend to be deficient in both community attachment and channels for obtaining early warning information18.

Specifically, the Economic dimension (DI, SL, UE) reflects the financial ‘buffer’ required for post-disaster reconstruction; the Demographic dimension (AC, LA, UW) addresses physical and cognitive constraints during rapid flood onsets where mobility is critical; and the Social Adaptation dimension (RP, EL, MI) captures the information-access barriers and lack of social capital that often prevent marginalized groups from receiving timely early warnings.

There were distinct spatial distribution patterns across the different variables. MI and EL are spatially uniform, whereas UE and DI exhibit extreme variation and skewed distributions, suggesting that their spatial influence on floodplain vulnerability is likely non-stationary.

Data preprocessing

To quantify the social vulnerability variables, we constructed the spatial downscaling through the following three stages:

(1) Population Exposure Identification We identified population distribution relative to floodplains by spatially overlaying the calibrated 1-km WorldPop raster with the GFPLAIN250m dataset. Population grid cells were classified as “inside-floodplain” if their centroids intersected with the floodplain mask, and “outside-floodplain” otherwise. These grid-level counts were then aggregated to the prefecture-level to determine the baseline flood exposure for each city.

(2) Spatial Downscaling of Social Vulnerability To align coarse socioeconomic data with high-resolution floodplains, city-level social vulnerability indicators from the China City Statistical Yearbook (2020) were spatially downscaled to a 1-km raster. Following the top-down approach, we applied the assumption of intra-city spatial uniformity to estimate the population of specific vulnerable groups in each grid cell. This was achieved by multiplying the total grid population by the corresponding city-level proportions, ensuring consistent spatial resolution across all vulnerability layers.

(3) Multi-dimensional Integration and Aggregation The spatially classified exposure data and downscaled vulnerability layers were integrated to form a unified grid-scale database. Finally, these rasterized data were re-aggregated to the city level. The resulting database provides a comprehensive suite of city-level indicators, including total population exposure and the proportions of multiple social vulnerability groups both within and outside floodplain boundaries.

Results

Spatial patterns of flood exposure

Main distribution characteristic and pattern of “Hu Huanyong Line”

It can be seen that population exposure in China’s floodplains is significantly spatially unstable. The boundary aligns closely with the Hu Huanyong Line, as revealed by overlaying floodplain and population distribution (Fig. 4a and b). The primary exposure areas are concentrated in the third topographic step east of the line, particularly the flat plains of the NE, North, Central, and East China. These areas are flat plains with a dense water network and centers for agriculture, population, and industry. Conversely, the NW and SW are characterized by high elevations and arid climates, where exposure is minimal except in regional river valleys.

Relative exposure: “Locking effect” of the physical environment

According to the Floodplain Coverage Ratio (Fig. 4a and b), cities with high coverage ratio were mainly distributed in Jianghan Plain in the middle reaches of Yangtze River and Huaihe River Basin.

As illustrated in Table 1, the floodplain coverage ratio of Xiantao and Qianjiang reached 100% (1.000), followed by Tianmen at 98.1%. These cities, mainly located in CC, are typical “cities born by water”. Consequently, it is almost impossible for their construction land to avoid the floodplain. This extreme high overlap implies that flood risk is an unavoidable “environmental background” for some small and medium-sized cities, where social vulnerability is primarily derived from Geographical Lock-in.

Conversely, Low-High clusters are sporadically distributed in parts of the CC and EC regions, where cities with relatively low exposed population shares are embedded within broader high-exposure environments, suggesting localized topographic protection or urban form differentiation within high-risk basins.

To avoid the multicollinearity among socioeconomic variables and to extract the representative vulnerability features, this study analyzed nine original variables with principal component analysis (PCA). The suitability of the data for factor analysis was confirmed by the KMO measure (0.762) and Bartlett’s test of sphericity (p < 0.001).

PCA (Fig. 6) illustrates that the principal components (PCs) can reduce the dimensionality of data. The eigenvalues of the first four principal components are all greater than one, and their cumulative variance contribution rate reach to 90.18%. This indicates that these four synthetic variables explain more than 90% of the information variance in original dataset, with minimal loss of information. This is sufficient to represent the overall social vulnerability in floodplains in China.

PC-FP: Floating population index

Load characteristics: This component has extremely high positive loading on “one-person household” (0.898) and “proportion of renters” (0.894). This clearly outlines the profile of “new residents” or the migrant population in the urbanization process in China. This group is predominantly composed of cross-regional migrants, relies on rental housing with no ownership of private property, and features an atomized family structure (characterized by living alone). In the sociology of disasters, they often become “social islands” due to a lack of community belonging, weak social networks, and limited economic accumulation, making them marginalized in flood warning and evacuation.

PC-LI: Livelihood instability index

Load characteristics: This component is dominated by “disposable income” (0.633) and “proportion of registered urban unemployed population” (0.595). This represents a complex dimension with distinct Chinese characteristics. Interestingly, income, as the core indicator variable characterizing economic strength, and unemployment rate, as the key proxy variable representing economic risks, exhibit a positive correlation in the same component dimension. This reflects the dual characteristics of “high risk - high return” in China’s urban economy: economically developed cities (high income) often have higher living costs and more intense employment competition, which is manifested as a high unemployment rate in statistics. In comparison, there’s lower income alongside lower unemployment rates in rural areas. Therefore, the PC-LI fundamentally reflects the livelihood fragility in a highly marketized economy, which is that even this group earns a high income, they face a financial predicament caused by cash flow interruption if work stops due to a flood.

PC-EL: Low educational level index

Load characteristics: This component is strongly driven by “illiterate people aged 15 and above” EL(0.773). This dimension directly points to cognitive vulnerability. Low education not only constrains this group’s ability to acquire and understand complex disaster warning information but is strongly related to low socio-economic status. This group represents an “information blind zone” in disaster prevention and mitigation, and they require special communication strategies utilizing visualization or graphical representation.

PC-AC: Lack of action capabilities index

Load characteristics: This component is obviously loaded on the “proportion of elderly and children aged over 65 or under 5” (0.544). This represents physical vulnerability. The elderly and children have limited power to transfer by themselves and thus have to rely on families and public assistance. With the accelerated arrival of China’s aging society, PC-AC has become a key indicator for measuring the pressure of regional flood control and evacuation.

Based on above, PC-FP, PC-LI, PC-EL, and PC-AC have constructed the social vulnerability profile of China’s floodplain population from perspective of social structure, economic livelihood, cognitive level and physiology function. This framework further constitutes the core variables for the MGWR model analysis.

Characteric differences between the global and locol scale models

First, the conventional global regression model (OLS) exhibits extremely weak explanatory performance. As shown in Table 6, the R2 of OLS model is only 0.159653, and the AICc is as high as 16.85. (Note: The AICc value is scaled here for comparison with R2 and differs from other spatial model.) This indicates that if the relationship between economic variation and flood exposure is assumed constant across the entire country, as the homogeneity assumption, the model could explain only 16% of the data variance. The results demonstrate the presence of spatial non-stationarity between flood exposure and social vulnerability, indicating that the global linear model could not capture these complex spatial patterns.

Effects of spatial regression model

After being incorporating the spatial perspective, the model’s performance has been improved significantly. GWR Model: The R2 could be increased to 0.5029 with this model, which indicates that considering local spatial differences can explain more than half of the variable relationships.

MGWR Model: The R2 could be further increased to 0.6955 with this model, and the AICc could decrease from 911.25 in the GWR model to 808.45 (a lower AICc value indicates a better model fit). This significant improvement indicates that the influence range (bandwidth) of different social vulnerability factors, such as floating population vs. aging, on flood exposure varies. Compared with the GWR that forces a uniform bandwidth, MGWR allows variables to have distinct spatial scales, which makes it more in line with the real operation mechanism of the socio-hydrological system.

Optimization of PCA in multicollinearity reduction

Socioeconomic indicators describing social vulnerability are often intrinsically correlated, which poses a challenge for regression-based inference. In this study, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were used to diagnose multicollinearity before and after PCA transformation (Tables 7 and 8).

For the original variables, relatively elevated VIF values are observed for LA (VIF = 4.003) and RP (VIF = 4.154), indicating notable redundancy among demographic and housing-related indicators. Although these values do not exceed critical thresholds, they suggest potential instability in coefficient estimation when directly incorporated into spatial regression models.

After PCA transformation, the VIFs of all retained principal components decrease substantially, ranging from 1.217 to 1.969, well below conventional multicollinearity thresholds. This confirms that PCA effectively removes linear dependence among explanatory variables, providing a statistically stable input structure for subsequent MGWR modeling. Importantly, this dimensionality reduction preserves substantive vulnerability dimensions while filtering out redundant information.

In addition, the benefit of minimizing multicollinearity is clearly observable. Incorporating the PCA-processed variables into the MGWR model (PCA-MGWR) proves to be the best approach. Its R2 reached the highest value in this study (0.7125), with an adjusted R2 of 0.6290, and the AICc further decreased to 781.00. Compared with the standard MGWR, PCA-MGWR not only enhances model fit, but also minimizes the collinearity disturbances among variables (such as income and unemployment, rent and living alone) to stabilize the local regression coefficients through the input of original PCs.

Based on the above, the ranking of model performance is: PCA-MGWR > MGWR > PCA-GWR > GWR > PCA-OLS > OLS. The PCA-MGWR model is proven to be the best statistical approach to explain the spatial heterogeneity of social vulnerability in China’s floodplains. Therefore, the following analysis of the driving mechanism will be conducted based on the regression coefficients of the PCA-MGWR model.

PC-FP: Positive driving effect in Inland areas and risk aversion effect in central-northern region

Figure 8a illustrates the obvious “NW-SE difference” in the effect of mobile population indicator on flood exposure. The NE and SW areas (positive correlation): The coefficients appear in purple and blue (positive values) across most of the NW and SW regions. This means a higher proportion of mobility population is associated with a higher risk of flood exposure in the western inland. This is likely due to the scarcity of plains in the inland mountainous terrain. Migrants often concentrate in low-lying areas or “urban villages” on the edges of river valleys, which face relatively high risks of waterlogging and mountain torrents.

The NC and CC areas (negative correlation): In contrast, coefficients appear in yellow and red (negative values) in the NC and CC regions. This reveals a “risk-avoidance effect”. In these plain cities, the mobility population is pushed to live in newly developed areas or suburbs far from the city center (which are typically non-historical floodplains) due to limited housing costs. Statistically, the higher the floating population, the lower the flood exposure.

PC-LI: “Wealth Exposure” in the Yangtze river basin

Figure 8b reveals the complex relationship between livelihood instability (high income/high risk of unemployment) and flood exposure. Urban agglomeration in the middle of Yangtze River (positive relationship): The coefficients are purple (high positive values) in parts of CC and the SW This result proves the unique “wealth exposure” in China’s floodplains. Compared with the “slum-floodplain” in the western world, the Yangtze River region exhibits a “hydro-dependent” development pattern. High-income groups and high asset valuations are not negatively constrained; instead, they concentrate positively for zonal benefits (transportation, views, and convenience) brought by water. This concentration, coupled with high exposure, implies a greater need for financial resources for disaster recovery.

Northern zone (negative relationship): However, the coefficients in the NC and NE regions are red (negative relationship). This represents that high-income communities are often located in areas of high altitude with adequate drainage facilities in Yellow River area and northern cities with limited water resources, leading to effective flood avoidance.

PC-EL: Spatial mismatch between cognitive vulnerability and human capital

Figure 8c reveals the effect of education level, predominated by proportion of illiterate people, on the flood exposure. This exhibits an obvious pattern of “NW-NE dichotomous opposition”.

The “classical fragile trap” of the northwestern inland (positive relationship): The coefficients in the NW and SW regions are purple and blue (positive values). This means that in the developing western areas, there is a positive relationship between low-education populations and flood risk. This aligns with the classical disaster vulnerability theory: groups with relatively low education levels are often constrained by living hood patterns such as traditional agriculture and animal husbandry. They not only have difficulty conveniently accessing disaster early-warning information, but also lack sufficient cognitive and adaptive capacity to support relocation for risk avoidance, and thus have no choice but to remain in ecologically fragile areas such as river valley fringes and floodplains.

“Labor force concentration”of the NE and coastal areas (negative relationship): However, the coefficients appear red and yellow (negative relationship) in the NE, EC and SC regions. This means that a higher proportion of the population with low education level is associated with lower flood exposure. In contrast, people with higher education level face higher exposure risk.

This counter-intuitive phenomenon highlights a unique characteristic in China known as the “human capital is concentrating on water”. In the old industrial bases of the NE (e.g., Harbin and Shenyang) and the developed cities of the southeast, educational resources and high-tech jobs are clustered in central urban areas along rivers and coasts. Despite being distinct from conventional floodplains, these areas attract a highly educated workforce. Conversely, the less-educated population tends to reside in mountainous or agricultural hinterlands. This spatial mismatch of the “highly educated population - high exposure” reminds us that disaster prevention education should not be overlooked in developed regions either, as a high academic degree does not necessarily mean having a high level of knowledge in hydrology and geography.

PC-AC: Significant “East-west Gradient Disparity” (in mobility and aging)

Figure 8d exhibits a distinct geographic dividing line in the effect of the elderly and activity indicators on flood risk.

Western areas (negative relationship): The coefficients are red (negative relationship) in most parts of the NW and SW regions. This is due to terrain constraints. In the western mountainous areas, conventional settlements with a high proportion of elderly residents are often located in relatively safe areas at higher elevations of the river valley to avoid mountain torrents. Conversely, there are uninhabited areas in the low-lying river floodplains that carry a high risk of flooding. Therefore, areas with a high concentration of the elderly are low-exposure areas.

The eastern plains (positive relationship): This relationship reverses in the areas east of “Hu Huanyong Line”. The coefficients become blue and purple (positive relationship) in the NE, NC and EC regions. There is observed phenomenon of “Aging in Place”. In the old urban areas of eastern plains (often located in high-risk areas along rivers), there is a large population of long-term elderly residents, while many young people have moved to new cities. This phenomenon of intergenerational residential separation has resulted in elderly groups being “trapped” in high-risk flood areas, with a significant positive correlation observed between the two.

Discussion

The “Risk-Development” paradox in the Chinese context: agglomeration and exposure of wealth

Research on environmental justice in the West suggests that the “poverty trap” emerges because disadvantaged populations (low-income groups and minorities) are forced to live in areas of high environmental risk6,7. However, based on PCA-MGWR analysis, this study reveals a distinct pattern of floodplain distribution in China, characterized as the Paradox of Wealth Exposure. Unlike the “passive marginalization” mechanism prevalent in Western environmental justice literature-where socio-economic exclusion pushes the poor toward hazardous fringes -the driving force in China is a “proactive utility-seeking” mechanism. In this context, the pursuit of transport efficiency and high asset valuations in river-front areas outweighs perceived flood risks. This transforms the survival-based vulnerability of traditional agricultural societies into a systemic, asset-based vulnerability characteristic of rapid urbanization. This finding stands in contrast to the conventional “poverty trap” narrative often observed in the Western world, where disadvantaged populations are forced into high-risk areas due to economic marginalization. While global mappings by Fox et al.4 suggest a complex interplay between wealth and risk, our results demonstrate that China’s unique “water-dependent” development model has led to a proactive aggregation of high-value assets in floodplains, transforming survival crises into systemic economic risks. Historically, Chinese culture depended on the development of big river agriculture. Modern industry and urbanization have maintained this route, leading to a high concentration of population and assets in plains that are rich in water resources but also prone to floods.

This “wealth exposure” does not imply a decrease of fragility, but rather a transformation of risk. It represents a shift from the “survival risk” of conventional agricultural societies to the “systemic economic risk” of modern cities. Although these areas possess strong economic recovery capabilities, the high density of assets means that astronomical economic losses can result from damage to flood control engineering projects, such as “black swan” events triggered by extreme rainfall, leading to chain reactions within supply chains. This systemic risk shift highlights the critical need for advanced predictive modeling to evaluate hazard risks under climate change, ensuring the integrated protection of both regional economic assets and cultural heritage20. Therefore, flood management in China must move beyond the perspective of pure poverty relief and address the reality of “high risk anchored by high economies”, aiming to establish a disaster risk-sharing mechanism suitable for the scale of the economy. This pattern reveals a distinct flood “exposure–development” paradox shaped by China’s long-term “human–water” relationship. On the one hand, legacy agricultural settlement along major rivers has led to persistent population concentration on floodplains, where communities have gradually transformed into socially vulnerable groups—such as the elderly and low-income residents—who face “passive entrapment” in high-risk zones due to limited housing options and economic constraints. On the other hand, rapid, asset-intensive urbanization since the 21st century has reinforced flood exposure in developed metropolitan areas, where high-income groups proactively settle in floodplains to capture “waterfront” benefits, such as transport convenience and aesthetic value, often underestimating hazards. These two mechanisms produce a heterogeneous exposure pattern: while wealthy regions require market-based risk transfer like catastrophe insurance to protect concentrated assets, impoverished areas necessitate state-led social safety nets to break the “poverty-hazard” cycle. Furthermore, the adoption of explainable analytical frameworks and causal inference provides a robust pathway for unveiling the complex regional drivers of environmental hazards, thereby supporting more precise and science-based management strategies21.

Hidden social vulnerability: The double challenge of mobility and aging

Although “wealth exposure” is observed at the macro scale, society exhibits high heterogeneity and hidden vulnerability at the micro community scale. Here, the model identifies two core vulnerable populations, each driven by distinct mechanisms.

“Institutional Segregation” of The PC-FP

PCA results reveal that the migrant population (renters and living alone) has become the primary dimension explaining China’s flood vulnerability. MGWR analysis (Fig. 8a) reveals the double burden faced by this group: in underdeveloped inland cities, they are forced to concentrate in low-lying urban villages, facing direct physical exposure. In contrast, in developed coastal cities, while they may be pushed to live in outer suburbs by exorbitantly priced housing, which appears as a “risk aversion effect” in statistics, the effect is passive and renders them vulnerable. In first-tier cities such as Shanghai and Shenzhen, housing market sorting plays a pivotal role. Historic city centers (often at high risk of flooding) force the migrant population to relocate to higher elevations or the later-developed suburbs due to their distance from water systems. This “risk aversion” is not an autonomous choice based on risk perception, but rather a passive replacement driven by the cost of living. This suggests that low exposure does not equate to low vulnerability. They are confronted with difficulties in commuting and the risk of marginalized social isolation.

Non-local long-term mobile tenants often lack the motivation to actively invest in disaster prevention facilities due to their lack of historical memory regarding local disasters. More importantly, the existing community disaster reduction system is often based on household registration management, which causes the migrant population to become “invisible people” in terms of accessing early warning information and the allocation of emergency shelters. This “institutional isolation” is even more dangerous than physical exposure. This phenomenon resonates with the environmental injustice reported in Miami and Houston9, where Hispanic immigrants face heightened risks due to social and linguistic barriers. However, our study identifies that the driving mechanism for China’s floating population is rooted in “institutional segregation” rather than cultural barriers. The Hukou (household registration) system functions as a socio-political filter that renders mobile individuals administratively “invisible”. This institutional driver creates a unique vulnerability where migrants are physically present in high-risk zones but are excluded from the official community-based emergency management and early warning frameworks anchored to permanent residency status. Our study extends this by illustrating how China’s Hukou (household registration) system functions as a similar socio-political filter, rendering the floating population ‘invisible’ within official emergency management frameworks.

“Spatial Lock-in” of the PC-AC

As China enters a deeply aging society, the overlap of aging and flood risk (Fig. 8d) poses a safety hazard. The results showed that in the eastern plains and old industrial bases, there is a significant phenomenon of “in-situ aging”. Elderly residents remain in old urban areas along rivers where facilities are aging, while young people with mobility move out.

This intergenerational residential separation leaves the elderly in an “empty nest” predicament when floods strike, making it difficult for them to receive timely assistance from their families. Compared with the elderly in the western mountainous areas who naturally live in highlands due to terrain restrictions, the elderly in the eastern plains are actually “locked” in high-risk flood areas. If no targeted intervention is carried out, this group will be the one with the highest casualty rate in future extreme climate events. The identified “spatial lock-in” of the elderly in China’s eastern plains corresponds to the vulnerability patterns reported in other aging societies like Japan17, where physical impairments hinder disaster preparedness. A critical distinction in the Chinese driving mechanism is the “intergenerational hollowing” effect triggered by hyper-scale migration. While vulnerability in Japan is primarily driven by “demographic maturation,” China’s is driven by “residential decoupling”. The rapid outflow of young labor to new urban districts leaves the elderly “trapped” in decaying, high-risk riverine cores without traditional familial support networks. This “passive retention” mechanism creates a more severe deficit in immediate emergency assistance than in more stable aging societies. However, the Chinese context is further intensified by rapid urbanization-induced intergenerational separation, which effectively “traps” the elderly in aging urban cores without traditional familial support networks.

Differentiated governance strategies based on spatial heterogeneity

Given the distinct spatial heterogeneity of these driving mechanisms, traditional “one-size-fits-all” flood control policies are no longer suitable. Based on the spatial patterns recognized by LISA clustering (Fig. 5) and the driving factors revealed by PCA-MGWR, three distinct targeted recommendations have been proposed:

(1) High-high Concentration Areas (the Middle and Lower Reaches of Yangtze River and Huaihe River Basin): Based on Risk-oriented Planning and Market-oriented Risk Transfer.

This area is the core zone of “wealth exposure” driven by PC-LI (livelihood/wealth). The policy focus should shift from simple engineering defense to the combination of “Risk-informed Urban Planning” and “Catastrophe Insurance” system. At the planning level, high-precision flood risk assessment must be incorporated into the national spatial planning system. For new development in high-risk areas, the principle of “avoidance” should be strictly implemented. For areas with high asset inventory, promote the design of “resilient cities”, such as sponge city facilities and underground water storage systems, to alleviate the pressure of urban flooding. This governance shift requires a deep understanding of the spatial antagonism between urban morphology and ecological infrastructure to mitigate environmental sensitivities in high-density areas22. Furthermore, the effectiveness of these facilities should be ensured through integrated frameworks - combining hydrodynamic models and GIS - to achieve the precise spatial optimization of sponge structures and functional areas23. At the institutional level, given the limitations of physical defense and the systemic risks of asset exposure, a catastrophe insurance system is needed urgently. The government should encourage high-income families and enterprises to purchase flood insurance through fiscal subsidies, and utilize market mechanisms to socialize and disperse the huge potential losses caused by “excessive accumulation”, preventing poverty due to disasters or regional economic shutdown.

(2) Fragility-driven Regions (Central and Western Mainland): Senior-friendly Transformation and Community Mutual Aid.

In the NW and SW regions, low PC-EL and PC-AC are the dominant vulnerability factors. The core of governance lies in bridging the “cognitive gap” and the “action deficit”. On the physical level, in response to the “in-situ aging” risk revealed by PC-AC, infrastructure construction must shift from “flood prevention” to “elderly care”. It is suggested that “Age-friendly Evacuation Routes” be constructed in old communities and river valley settlements, including the addition of barrier-free access, night light guidance systems and gentle slope designs, to ensure that groups with limited mobility can evacuate independently or semi-independently in emergency situations. At the social level: to address the blind spots in early warning for low-education groups, a multimodal early warning system for the “last mile” should be established, such as local TV stations, mobile social media, dialect broadcasts, and illustrated manuals. More importantly, informal neighborhood assistance should be institutionalized and a “Community-based Mutual Aid” should be established. Before the flood season, grid-based registration was carried out for elderly people living alone and people with disabilities, and young volunteers or neighbors were assigned as their “First Responders” for emergency evacuation.

(3) The Edge of Metropolis: Inclusive Disaster Prevention and Information Barriers Removal.

To address the “institutional exclusion” of PC-FP in large eastern cities, policies need to embrace the principle of “Inclusive Resilience”. It is necessary to break the restrictions of the household registration system in the field of disaster reduction, incorporate the floating population into the community grid-based emergency management system, and ensure the right to open and equal use of emergency shelters. To tackle information imbalance in the rental market, it is essential to enforce a mandatory system for disclosing potential risks. Landlords and real estate agencies should be required to disclose to tenants the historical waterlogging records and flood risk levels of their houses, and provide basic emergency escape guidelines. These measures stimulate the active risk-avoidance ability of the floating population by eliminating information barriers, and thereby fill the “immunity gap” in disaster prevention and mitigation beyond physical exposure risks through institutional inclusiveness.

Conclusions

Based on the data of 2020 Population Census, this study constructed a PCA-MGWR coupled model to reveal the spatial heterogeneity and driving mechanisms of the fragile communities in China’s floodplains. Verification through methodological comparison demonstrates that the PCA-MGWR model can overcome the challenges of collinearity and spatial instability of muti-socio-economic data, supporting more stable tool for large-scale assessment of hazard risk.

There are three core conclusions in this study:

First, there has emerged a notably pronounced situation of the “Paradox of Wealth Exposure” in China. In contrast with conventional environmental justice theory, the high-exposure areas in China are mainly concentrated in the economically developed middle and lower reaches of the Yangtze River and coastal urban agglomerations. This spatial lock-in caused by the water-dependent development model means that flood risk has evolved from a survival crisis into systemic asset risks.

Second, the core motivation of social vulnerability to floods in China is undergoing a shift: from single factor “poverty” to “motility” and “aging”. It can be recognized by our model that non-household registered mobility population in eastern developed areas face potential risk of institutional isolation, whereas the “in-situ aging” in central and eastern plains exacerbates the risk of physical retention of the elderly.

Finally, based on the significant spatial differences in driving mechanisms, this study advocates a shift from “one-size-fits-all” defense to adaptive precision governance. For affluent high-exposure areas like the Yangtze River Basin, where “wealth exposure” dominates, the priority should be the establishment of market-oriented risk-sharing systems, such as mandatory catastrophe insurance, to mitigate systemic economic risks. In contrast, for fragility-driven regions in the Northwest and Southwest, precision governance must focus on bridging “cognitive gaps” and “action deficits” through senior-friendly infrastructure (e.g., age-friendly evacuation routes) and institutionalized community-based mutual aid. Furthermore, for megacities with high population mobility, policies should focus on “Inclusive Resilience” by removing institutional barriers for the floating population, ensuring they have equal access to early warning information and emergency shelters regardless of their household registration status. These illustrated strategies provide a roadmap for transitioning from blanket defenses to region-specific socio-hydrological interventions.

Although this provides a fine-grained spatial perspective, it is still constrained by the static property of census data, which could not reflect the dynamic population distribution when a disaster occurs. Future research will combine multi-source spatio-temporal big data with composite flood models to further explore the dynamic coupling mechanisms of socio-hydrological systems in the context of climate change.
