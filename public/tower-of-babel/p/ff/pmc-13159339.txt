Molecular basis of drought tolerance in Rosa xanthina: an integrated analysis of physiology, transcriptome, and NAC transcription factor
Authors: Ziguo Li, Xiaolong Zhang, Qingyang Kong, Chenjie Zhang, Yong Shi, Chao Yu, Huitang Pan, Qixiang Zhang, Le Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rosa xanthina, an important species for landscaping and ecological restoration in northern China, possesses strong drought tolerance. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying its drought stress response remain unclear. In this study, we systematically analyzed the physiological changes and transcriptomic responses of two-year-old R. xanthina plants to drought stress (including control, light drought, moderate drought, and severe drought treatments) and subsequent rewatering. Additionally, we conducted a specific examination of the NAC (NAM, ATAF, and CUC) transcription factor (TF) family. Physiological analyses revealed that with increasing drought severity, leaf relative water content (LRWC) decreased significantly, whereas the levels of osmotic regulators (proline - Pro and soluble sugars - SS) and the antioxidant glutathione (GSH) increased. The activity of superoxide dismutase (SOD) increased continuously. The content of chlorophyll a increased, whereas that of chlorophyll b decreased following moderate stress, indicating a significant suppression of the photosynthetic system. Transcriptome sequencing identified 32,857 expressed genes, among which 6,217 were differentially expressed under the experimental conditions. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses revealed that these differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were significantly enriched in pathways related to plant hormone signal transduction, glutathione metabolism, photosynthesis, and antioxidant activity. Among these DEGs, members of the NAC TF family constituted the largest group. Genome-wide analysis identified 174 NAC TFs in the R. xanthina genome. Among these, RxNAC105 exhibited significant up-regulation under drought stress. Preliminary functional validation via transient overexpression demonstrated that RxNAC105 significantly increased the activity of key antioxidant enzymes, suggesting its positive role in drought resistance. This study systematically elucidates the molecular mechanisms underlying the drought stress response of R. xanthina at both physiological and transcriptomic levels. The identification of RxNAC105 as a candidate gene for drought tolerance provides crucial genetic resources and a theoretical foundation for drought-resistant breeding in Rosa species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12797-6.

Background

Intensified global drought stress poses a growing threat to agricultural production and ecological security. Consequently, elucidating plant drought resistance mechanisms has become a major research focus worldwide [1, 2]. The advancement of high-throughput sequencing technologies has greatly accelerated the discovery of drought-responsive genes in plants [3, 4]. At the molecular level, research focus has shifted from characterizing individual gene functions to analyzing entire regulatory pathways [5, 6]. Several transcription factor (TF) families, including NAC [no apical meristem (NAM), Arabidopsis thaliana transcription activation factor (ATAF1/2) and cup-shaped cotyledon (CUC2)] [7, 8], ERF [9, 10], and MYB [11, 12], have been confirmed as core regulators of plant drought responses, acting as master switches of stress-responsive gene networks.

Rosa xanthina (synonym R. hugonis) is a deciduous shrub of the Rosa genus known for its strong adaptability and high ornamental value. It serves as an important species for landscaping and ecological restoration in northern China due to its remarkable drought tolerance [13]. Despite its ecological importance, the molecular mechanisms underlying the drought tolerance of R. xanthina remain largely unexplored. Current research on R. xanthina has primarily focused on physiological aspects [14, 15], including the accumulation of osmolytes (proline - Pro and soluble sugars - SS) and changes in antioxidant enzyme activities (superoxide dismutase - SOD, EC 1.15.1.1; catalase - CAT, EC 1.11.1.6 and peroxidase - POD, EC 1.11.1.7) [16, 17]. However, these physiological observations lack an underlying molecular explanation. Specifically, a comprehensive transcriptome analysis of its drought-responsive gene regulatory network has not been reported, and key regulatory genes—particularly TFs that orchestrate these physiological changes—remain to be identified.

Among the various TF families, the NAC gene family is particularly noteworthy. It has been widely documented to play a pivotal role in plant drought adaptation across diverse species [18]. BoNAC02 enhances the plant’s drought response capacity by positively regulating the ABA signaling pathway and the expression of genes related to antioxidant enzyme metabolism [19]. Maize TF ZmNAC2 enhances osmotic stress tolerance in transgenic A. thaliana [20]. TaSNAC4-3D encodes a NAC TF that negatively regulates drought tolerance by promoting reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and programmed cell death in wheat [21]. The expression of GhJUB1L1, a NAC gene in cotton, was induced by drought stress, and silencing it reduced drought tolerance [22]. The aforementioned studies proved the critical roles of NAC TFs in regulating drought tolerance. Functional genes act as direct molecular effectors enabling plants to respond to drought stress. For example, the upregulation of antioxidant-related genes, including those encoding glutathione S-transferases (GST, EC 2.5.1.1) and ascorbate peroxidases (APX, EC 1.11.1.11) [23], provides a molecular basis for enhancing ROS scavenging capacity and maintaining cellular redox homeostasis [24, 25]. However, the upstream regulators—especially NAC TFs—that control these effector genes in R. xanthina have yet to be characterized.

To address these knowledge gaps, this study systematically deciphers the drought-responsive molecular mechanisms in R. xanthina by integrating physiological assays with transcriptome sequencing. This integrated approach aims to identify key pathways and core TFs involved in drought response. Given the established role of NAC TFs in drought adaptation and their anticipated importance in R. xanthina based on the transcriptome data, we performed a genome-wide identification of this family. By integrating these transcriptome expression profiles, we pinpointed RxNAC105 as a key drought-responsive candidate and conducted its preliminary functional validation. Overall, this study provides comprehensive insights into the molecular basis of drought tolerance in R. xanthina and offers valuable genetic resources for improving drought resistance in Rosa species.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and drought treatments

Two-year-old healthy cuttings of R. xanthina with uniform growth and no visible disease symptoms were selected as plant materials. The cuttings were planted in plastic pots (upper diameter: 16 cm, lower diameter: 14 cm, height: 15 cm). The potting substrate was a 2:1 (v/v) mixture of loose humus soil and perlite. The experiment was conducted at the Beijing Daxing Flower and Seedling Base. In December 2023, the cuttings were transplanted into pots and maintained in a greenhouse. During the pre-treatment acclimatization period, plants were watered every three days. Soil relative moisture content (SRMC) was monitored using the core cutter method on soil samples collected from a depth of 2 cm [26].

The drought stress experiment commenced in March 2024. Throughout the experimental period, the greenhouse environment was maintained at 10–30 °C and 40%–80% relative humidity. Immediately prior to the initiation of the drought stress experiment, all pots were thoroughly watered to standardize the initial soil moisture content across all plants. The control group (CK) and drought treatments were defined by water-withholding durations of 6 (CK), 12 (light drought, LD), 18 (moderate drought, MD), and 24 (severe drought, SD) days. By the end of each treatment period, the SRMC had achieved target levels of 75–80% (CK), 55–60% (LD), 40–45% (moderate drought, MD), and 25–30% (severe drought, SD). Plants in the rewatering (RW) group were rewatered immediately after the SD group sampling, and then sampled on the fourth day after rewatering. Twenty plants per treatment group were sampled. For each plant, fully expanded, mature leaves from the upper-middle plant were collected. Sampling time points were March 31 (CK), April 5 (LD), April 11 (MD), April 17 (SD), and April 21 (RW). All samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C for subsequent analysis. Each treatment had three biological replicates. For RNA extraction, transcriptome sequencing, and physiological assays, each replicate consisted of a pooled sample from multiple plants.

Determination of physiological and biochemical indicators

The mature leaf samples collected as described above were used for physiological analyses. Fresh leaves were collected and immediately weighed to determine their fresh weight (FW). The leaves were then immersed in deionized water for 24 h. After removal, surface moisture was carefully blotted dry with filter paper. They were then weighed to obtain the turgid weight (TW). Subsequently, the leaves were dried to constant weight in an oven at 80 °C and then weighed to determine the dry weight (DW). The leaf relative water content (LRWC) was calculated using the following formula: LRWC (%) = [(FW - DW) / (TW - DW)] × 100%.

All physiological indicators, including the contents of Pro, SS, malondialdehyde (MDA), GSH, chlorophylls, and the activity of SOD, were determined using fresh samples. The contents of SS, MDA, and GSH were determined using commercial assay kits from Beijing Biobox Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (AKPL008C, AKFA013C, and AKPR008C, respectively). The Pro content, as well as the activities of SOD and CAT, were assayed using kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (A107-1-1, A001-1-1, and A007-1-1, respectively). Chlorophyll components were extracted and quantified using the ethanol extraction method [27]. The absorbances at 665 nm, 649 nm, and 470 nm were measured using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. RNA extraction, cDNA library construction, and sequencing

RNA was extracted from all 15 collected mature leaf samples (CK1, CK2, CK3, LD1, LD2, LD3, MD1, MD2, MD3, SD1, SD2, SD3, RW1, RW2 and RW3) using FastPure® Universal Plant Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). All surfaces and equipment were disinfected with 75% ethanol prior to extraction to prevent RNase contamination. RNA integrity was assessed by electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel, and the purity and integrity of RNA were further validated using the Agilent 4200 TapeStation.

All 15 RNA samples were then sequenced on an Illumina platform by Beijing Qingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The poly (A) mRNA was isolated from total RNA using oligo (dT)-conjugated magnetic beads [28]. After construction and normalization, 15 cDNA libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform and 150 base pairs (bp) paired-end reads were generated.

Raw data of fastq format were firstly processed through in-house perl scripts. In this step, clean reads were obtained by removing reads containing adapter, reads containing ploy-N and low quality reads from raw data. At the same time, Q20, Q30, GC-content and sequence duplication level of the clean data were calculated. All the downstream analyses were based on clean data with high quality. The clean reads were then aligned to the R. chinensis ‘Old blush’ reference genome by HISAT2 v2.1.0. Transcript reconstruction and quantification were performed simultaneously using StringTie v1.3.5. Gene expression levels were calculated as Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped fragments (FPKM).

Identification and functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

The identification of DEGs is a crucial step in transcriptomic analysis. For samples with biological replicates, differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 v1.26.0 [29]. DEGs were identified using stringent statistical criteria. DEGs were selected with |log2 FoldChange| ≥ 1 and P-value < 0.05. These DEGs performed Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis by the GOseq R Packages and KOBAS software (P-value < 0.05), respectively [30, 31].

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

The cDNA samples from five treatment (CK, LD, MD, SD, RW) were diluted threefold and used as templates for qRT-PCR. A total of 12 genes were randomly chosen to validate the gene expression levels by a qRT-PCR. The qRT-PCR analysis was performed using the 2 × HQ SYBR qPCR Mix (Without ROX) Kit (ZomanBio, Beijing, China) with Actin serving as the reference gene [32, 33]. The reaction was performed in a final volume of 20 µl, with the following amplification conditions: initial denaturation at 98℃ for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 57 °C for 10 s. A subsequent melting curve analysis was performed to verify amplification specificity. The primer sequences used for these DEGs are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method [34]. The reaction system and reaction program are listed in Supplementary Table S2, 3.

Genome-wide identification of the NAC TF family

The genome sequence and annotation file for R. xanthina were provided by our research group. Genomic data for the following species were retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database: R. chinensis ‘Old Blush’ (GCA_002994745.2), R. rugosa (GCF_958449725.1), Fragaria vesca (GCF_000184155.1), and Prunus armeniaca (GCA_902712645.1). The canonical NAC protein sequences of A. thaliana were downloaded from TAIR database (https://www.arabidopsis.org). Genome-wide identification of NAC family members across all target species was performed using TBtools software (v2.376), utilizing both BLASTp (E-value < 1e-5) and Hidden Markov Model (HMM) search strategies. Using the NAC protein sequences from A. thaliana as query sequences, the BLAST program package within TBtools (v2.376) was employed to perform BLASTp alignment searches. Concurrently, the Hidden Markov Model profile for the NAM domain (PF02365) [35] was obtained from the Pfam database. Next, domain analysis was further confirmed using the NCBI conserved domain database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/bwrpsb/bwrpsb.cgi). The same method was used in R. chinensis ‘Old Blush’, R. rugosa, F. vesca and P. armeniaca to identify and sort their NAC family members. Finally, candidate proteins were retained as putative NAC TFs only if they contained the conserved NAM domain.

Bioinformatics analysis of RxNAC proteins

The ExPASy online tool (https://web.expasy.org/compute_pi/) was used to analyze the physiological and biochemical characteristics of the RxNACs. The subcellular localization was predicted using WoLF PSORT (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/). Conserved motifs and structural domains within the RxNAC proteins were analyzed using Multiple Em for Motif Elicitation (MEME) Suite (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme) and the NCBI Conserved Domain Database (NCBI-CDD), respectively. A total of 2000 bp upstream of ATG (the start codon) of RxNAC genes were retrieved from genome sequence files to act as promoter sequences. The PlantCare platform (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) was used to predict cis-acting elements on these promoters. All resulting data were visualized using TBtools (v2.376).

A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Maximum Likelihood method (ML) in IQ-TREE software with 1000 bootstrap replicates. The generated tree is visualized using the iTOL website. The chromosomal locations and gene structures of the identified NAC genes (RxNACs, RcNACs, RrNACs, PaNACs, FvNACs) were visualized using TBtools (v2.376) based on the genome annotation files. Collinearity analysis of NAC genes was also performed in TBtools (v2.376).

Cloning of RxNAC105 and construction of the overexpression vector

For cloning and overexpression vector construction of RxNAC105, total RNA was extracted from R. xanthina leaves under SD treatment and reverse-transcribed into cDNA by Reverse Transcriptase Kit (Zoman, Beijing, China). The full-length coding sequence of RxNAC105 was amplified using PrimeSTAR® Max DNA Polymerase (TaKaRa, Shiga, Japan). The purified PCR product was ligated into the T-vector using the Onestep ZTOPO-Blunt/TA (Zoman, Beijing, China) and sequenced to confirm its identity. The confirmed fragment was then inserted into the PSpuer1300 overexpression vector via XbaⅠ and ApaⅠ double digestion using the Clone Express® II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme). The recombinant plasmid was verified by sequencing and subsequently transformed into Agrobacterium GV3101 (Beijing Qingke) using the freeze-thaw method for use in transient infiltration experiments. The primer sequence of RxNAC105 is in Supplementary Table S4, and the figures of the experimental process is shown in Supplementary Figure S5.

Transient overexpression of RxNAC105

Relatively tender stems were collected from mature R. xanthina plants and cut into segments approximately 10 cm in length. The infiltration solution was prepared as MES infiltration buffer (10 mM MES, 100 µM acetosyringone, and 10 mM MgCl₂, pH = 5.8) [36]. The Agrobacterium strain GV3101 carrying the PSpuer1300-RxNAC105 construct (or empty vector control) was cultured, harvested, and resuspended in the infiltration buffer to an appropriate optical density OD600 = 0.8. The stem segments were completely immersed in the Agrobacterium suspension and vacuum-infiltrated for 5 min at 0.1 MPa. After infiltration, residual solution on the surface of the segments was gently blotted away using sterile filter paper. The treated stem segments were then placed in conical flasks containing water (hydroponic culture) and incubated in a climate chamber under controlled conditions. The samples were first incubated in darkness for 16 h, followed by a 2 day standard culture period under long-day conditions (16 h light / 8 h dark) at 25℃.

After the culture period, total RNA was extracted from leaves of both empty-vector control and RxNAC105 overexpressing samples. The relative expression level of RxNAC105 was then quantified by qRT-PCR. Additionally, physiological indicators related to drought stress response (including CAT activity, GSH content, and MDA content) were measured in these leaf samples.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted using a one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons using Tukey’s honest significant difference test. P-values < 0.05 were considered significant. Data are presented as the means and standard deviations.

Results

Physiological responses of R. xanthina to drought stress

The morphological changes in R. xanthina plants and leaves under different drought stress levels are shown in Fig. 1A. Leaves in the CK maintained a relatively high water content (Fig. 1B). As drought stress intensified, LRWC decreased significantly, reaching its lowest level under SD treatment, which represented a 38.46% reduction compared to CK. This indicates that R. xanthina gradually loses water retention capacity under prolonged drought. However, upon RW, LRWC rebounded significantly, showing a 46.24% increase compared to the SD group, although it remained 9% lower than the CK level.

The levels of Pro, SS, MDA, and SOD activity in leaves increased progressively with drought severity, peaked under SD treatment, and declined after rewatering (Fig. 1C, F). GSH content showed an overall increasing trend from CK to SD, although a slight decrease was observed during MD treatment, resulting in a 5.13% reduction compared to LD in the MD group (Fig. 1G).

Drought stress significantly altered the photosynthetic pigment content in R. xanthina leaves. Chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b exhibited distinct response patterns (Fig. 1H, J). The content of chlorophyll a increased continuously with drought severity and decreased after rewatering, demonstrating rapid stress response and recovery characteristics. In contrast, chlorophyll b content increased during LD but decreased significantly under MD, suggesting a higher sensitivity to moderate drought stress. After rewatering, chlorophyll b content continued to decline, exhibiting a slower recovery rate compared to chlorophyll a. The trend in chlorophyll b content closely mirrored that of total chlorophyll.

RNA-seq and transcripts annotation

Sequencing on the Illumina platform generated approximately 137.58 Gb of clean reads, which was assembled and mapped to 32,857 genes. The Q30 base percentage was ≥ 96.21% for all samples, confirming high sequencing quality. The clean reads from each sample were aligned to the R. chinensis ‘Old blush’ reference genome, with mapping efficiencies ranging from 70.73% to 77.55%.

According to the BLASTx matching results in the GO database, 13,461 unigenes were assigned GO terms. These genes were distributed across 46 functional categories (Fig. 2A). Within the stress response-related GO subcategories, the significantly enriched functions included signal transduction and antioxidant activity.

In total, 11,281 unigenes were assigned to 135 KEGG metabolic pathways. The top 50 pathways, ranked by the number of annotated genes, are presented in Fig. 2B. Key stress-associated pathways included “Plant hormone signal transduction” (247 genes), “Peroxisome” (135 genes), and “Glutathione metabolism” (135 genes).

Number and functional enrichment of DEGs

Transcriptome analysis annotated a total of 6,217 DEGs. Using the CK as the reference, the number of DEGs increased progressively with the intensification of drought stress. The numbers of both up and down-regulated DEGs peaked under SD vs. CK with 1,196 up-regulated and 1,557 down-regulated genes. Following rewatering, the numbers of both up and down-regulated DEGs decreased in RW vs. SD, indicating an alleviation of drought stress. Compared to SD vs. CK group, the RW vs. SD group showed a reduction of 411 up-regulated and 554 down-regulated DEGs (Supplementary Figure S6).

To further investigate the biological functions of the DEGs, GO and KEGG analyses were performed. The enriched GO terms were broadly similar across all groups. However, the number of DEGs assigned to these enriched terms increased with drought severity (Supplementary Figure S7). This trend was consistent with the overall change in DEG numbers. The GO terms most significantly associated with drought stress were primarily related to “antioxidant activity” (GO:0016209) and “signal transduction” (GO:0004871).

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the sets of enriched pathways were largely similar across different drought stress levels (Supplementary Figure S8). Key enriched pathways included “plant hormone signal transduction” (ko04075), “starch and sucrose metabolism” (ko00500), “phenylpropanoid biosynthesis” (ko00940), and “glutathione metabolism” (ko00480).

Analysis of key enriched metabolic pathways

The plant hormone signal transduction pathway showed the highest enrichment of DEGs (Fig. 3A), with a total of 72 DEGs identified in this pathway. Among these, the auxin signaling pathway contained the highest number of DEGs (21 genes): LOC112173644、LOC112173987、LOC112169805、LOC112191317、LOC112173433、LOC112172149、LOC112190249、LOC112194766、LOC112201882、LOC112193093、LOC112186844、LOC112178859、LOC112173536、LOC112188016、LOC112170562、LOC112173257、LOC112200817、LOC121050448、LOC112173585、LOC112195874、LOC112195962 (Fig. 3C). The ABA signaling pathway ranked second, involving 12 DEGs. In LD vs. CK, MD vs. CK and SD vs. CK, genes encoding auxin proteins (AUX/IAA), abscisic acid receptor (PYL), and auxin responsive protein (SAUR) were generally down-regulated under drought but up-regulated in RW vs. SD. Genes encoding protein phosphatase 2 C (PP2C) were up-regulated to varying degrees under drought stress (LOC112166829、LOC112183442、LOC112184125、LOC112183508、LOC112182826、LOC112185404). Collectively, under drought stress, most genes within the plant hormone signaling pathways were down-regulated. Following rewatering, the expression of most hormone signaling genes shifted towards up-regulation.

Among DEGs related to the photosynthetic system, a substantial number encoded chlorophyll a-b binding proteins (nine genes) and photosystem II (PSb) proteins (12 genes; Fig. 3B, F). In LD vs. CK, MD vs. CK and SD vs. CK, the expression of all these genes, except for PsbS, decreased. In contrast, their expression increased in RW vs. SD. A comparison across drought treatments identified four genes that were consistently down-regulated during drought and rapidly up-regulated after rewatering. Among these four genes, two (LOC112191728 and LOC112168431) encode chlorophyll a-b binding proteins. Gene annotation confirmed their association with photosystem II, suggesting that specific components of this photosystem may exhibit particularly sensitive and sustained responses to drought stress.

Transcriptome data revealed significant enrichment of genes involved in the glutathione and ascorbate metabolic pathways, which are central to ROS scavenging. The GST gene family plays a key role in glutathione metabolism. The glutathione metabolic pathway was significantly enriched at all drought stress stages, and the number of associated DEGs increased with stress severity (Fig. 3D, G). Several GSTs (LOC112165480 and LOC112197719) were continuously up-regulated during drought, potentially promoting the production of reduced GSH. Conversely, seven other genes (LOC112173393、LOC112174157、LOC112169021、LOC112170317、LOC112182506、LOC112170318、LOC112175368) were continuously down-regulated during drought but showed rapid recovery in expression after rewatering. Within the glutathione pathway, besides GSTs, genes encoding glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PDH; LOC112187722) and glutathione reductase (GR; LOC112173104) were also differentially expressed. Under drought stress, chloroplast G6PDH activity is typically up-regulated to generate NADPH, which is essential for countering ROS bursts. Glutathione reductase (GR) is a key enzyme in the glutathione-ascorbate (ASA-GSH) cycle. The ascorbate metabolism pathway was enriched for 21 genes. Most of these genes were continuously up-regulated under both MD vs. CK and SD vs. CK. The gene encoding GDP-D-mannose-3’,5’-epimerase (GME; LOC112197732) was continuously up-regulated during drought and down-regulated after rewatering, suggesting it acts as a positive regulator of ascorbate biosynthesis (Fig. 3E, H). Within this pathway, genes encoding ascorbate peroxidase (APX; LOC112167733), aldo-keto reductase (AKR; LOC112193826, LOC112191370), and ascorbate oxidase (AO; LOC112167001) also showed significant differential expression. This indicates that the ascorbate metabolic pathway plays a crucial role in the response of R. xanthina to intense drought stress. The expression trends of these antioxidant-related genes in the ascorbate pathway were consistent with the physiological antioxidant measurements (GSH, SOD), cross-validating the reliability of both datasets.

Analysis of TFs and validation of RNA-seq data by qRT-PCR

A total of 1,736 TFs belonging to 68 families were identified using the Plant Transcription Factor Database (PlantTFDB). The ten most abundant TF families were NAC (156), MYB (136), AP2 (120), FAR1 (117), bHLH (106), C2H2 (97), B3 (93), WRKY (70), C3H (61), and GRAS (58) (Fig. 4A). Among these, the NAC, MYB, AP2/ERF, bHLH, and WRKY families contained the highest numbers of differentially expressed TFs, with 45, 40, 44, 33, and 34, respectively. Notably, the NAC gene family contained the most differentially expressed TFs (Fig. 4B).

Analysis of the NAC gene family

Among all differentially expressed TF families, the NAC gene family exhibited the highest number of DEGs (45 members), suggesting its predominant regulatory role in the drought stress response of R. xanthina. Given the well-documented functions of NAC TFs in plant drought adaptation across diverse species, we proceeded to perform a genome-wide identification and comprehensive analysis of the NAC gene family in R. xanthina. This systematic characterization, integrated with transcriptome-derived expression profiles, aimed to elucidate the evolutionary features and structural diversity of this important TF family.

Phylogenetic classification and protein properties

Using a combined BLASTp and HMM search strategy, we identified 174, 116, 161, 118, and 86 NAC family members in R. xanthina, R. chinensis ‘Old Blush’, R. rugosa, P. armeniaca, and F. vesca, respectively. The identified NAC genes were systematically renamed according to their chromosomal locations. The physicochemical properties of the R. xanthina NAC proteins were analyzed (Supplementary Table S9). The proteins ranged in length from 65 to 716 amino acids (aa), with an average of 333 aa. Their molecular weights (MW) ranged from 7.59 to 80.11 kDa, and their theoretical isoelectric points (pI) ranged from 4.36 (RxNAC57) to 10.23 (RxNAC6). Subcellular localization prediction using WoLF PSORT indicated that 138 NAC proteins (79.3%) were localized to the nucleus, supporting their putative function as TFs.

Based on protein sequence similarity, the 174 RxNAC proteins were classified into eight subfamilies (Group 1–8). Group 8 contained the largest number of members (63 genes, 36.2%), whereas Group 2 contained the fewest (seven genes, 4.0%) (Fig. 7A).

A phylogenetic tree constructed using NAC protein sequences from R. xanthina and A. thaliana revealed 13 distinct subfamilies (Fig. 5). The RxNAC proteins were unevenly distributed among these subfamilies. For instance, Group 12 contained only RxNAC proteins, with no orthologs from A. thaliana. Group 13 contained the most genes overall, including 56 RxNAC genes, representing the subfamily with the highest proportion of RxNACs. In contrast, Group 10 contained the fewest members (four genes) and the lowest RxNAC representation (only RxNAC121).

A separate phylogenetic analysis including NAC proteins from five Rosaceae species (R. xanthina, R. chinensis ‘Old Blush’, R. rugosa, P. armeniaca, F. vesca) and A. thaliana divided the family into nine subfamilies (Supplementary Figure S10), designated Groups 1–9. Based on their phylogenetic distribution, the NAC gene family of R. xanthina is most closely related to those of R. chinensis ‘Old Blush’ and R. rugosa, and most distantly related to that of A. thaliana.

We mapped the chromosomal locations of NAC genes identified in three Rosa species: R. xanthina, R. rugosa, and R. chinensis ‘Old Blush’ (Supplementary Figure S11). Analysis of gene structure, conserved motifs, and promoter cis-elements

To gain further insight into the evolution of the NAC family in R. xanthina, we analyzed the gene structure, conserved motifs, protein domains, and promoter cis-elements of the RxNAC genes. Gene structure analysis revealed that 34 of the 174 RxNAC genes did not contain any intron, while the remaining genes contained 1 to 8 introns (Fig. 7E). Genes within the same subfamily generally exhibited similar exon/intron structures. For example, among the 50 genes in Group 1, 2, 4 and 5, 92% possessed 3 to 5 exons. In contrast, within the 63 member Group 8, 32 genes (50.8%) contained only one exon. In subfamily 7, exon numbers varied from 1 to 9.

Analysis of conserved domains and motifs confirmed that all RxNAC proteins contain the NAM domain (Fig. 7D). Using MEME, 10 conserved motifs were identified (Fig. 7C). Motifs 1–7 were the most conserved, being present across Group 1, 2, 4, 5, 6 and 7. Motifs 8, 9, and 10 were exclusively found in Group 8, suggesting they may be characteristic motifs for this subfamily.

The putative promoter region (2,000 bp upstream of the start codon) for each RxNAC gene was extracted using TBtools (v2.376). Potential cis-acting regulatory elements were predicted using the PlantCARE database and categorized into four major types: hormone-responsive, development-related, stress-responsive, and light-responsive elements (Fig. 7B). In total, 3,096 cis-elements were predicted. Stress-responsive elements accounted for 787 (25.4%), while hormone-responsive elements were the most abundant (1,155 elements, 37.3%). Three genes (RxNAC41, RxNAC50, RxNAC51) lacked the predicted cis-elements in the analyzed region, suggesting they may be regulated by distinct mechanisms.

In summary, the analyses of phylogenetic relationships, gene structures, conserved motifs, and promoter cis-elements yielded consistent results. The RxNAC proteins are highly conserved, and genes clustered within the same phylogenetic subfamily likely share functional similarities, although this requires further experimental validation.

Expression patterns of NAC genes under drought stress

Analysis of the NAC gene family expression based on transcriptomic alignment and heatmap visualization (Fig. 8A) revealed that 84 genes showed no detectable expression across all treatments, while 90 genes were expressed. Of the non-expressed genes, a predominant proportion (51 genes, 81%) were concentrated in Group 8, which contains 63 genes in total. Within Group 7, down-regulated genes were the most abundant, constituting 70% of its members. In contrast, up-regulated genes were unevenly distributed among the subfamilies. Under drought stress, the expression of some genes, such as RxNAC50, RxNAC51, RxNAC105, and RxNAC54, progressively increased with stress severity. These genes are thus strong candidates for playing key roles in the drought resistance mechanism of R. xanthina.

Preliminary functional validation of RxNAC105

RxNAC105 exhibited sustained high expression throughout the drought stress period, peaking during the SD stage and declining after rewatering. Based on this expression, RxNAC105 was selected for preliminary functional validation. The qRT-PCR was performed on empty vector control and RxNAC105 overexpressing R. xanthina plants to quantify transgene expression levels.

Key physiological indicators were also measured in these plants. Following transient transformation, the relative expression level of RxNAC105 in RxNAC105 overexpressing leaves was 3.5-fold higher than in empty vector control leaves (Fig. 8B). The CAT activity in RxNAC105 overexpressing leaves was 3.4-fold higher than in empty vector control leaves (Fig. 8C). Compared to the empty vector control, GSH content in RxNAC105 overexpressing leaves increased by 12.16% (Fig. 8D), while MDA content decreased by 26.56% (Fig. 8E). In RxNAC105 overexpressing leaves, both CAT activity and GSH content were significantly elevated compared to the empty vector control, with the increase in CAT activity being particularly pronounced. This indicates that RxNAC105 likely promotes CAT biosynthesis, thereby enhancing its activity to improve drought tolerance.

Discussion

Physiological responses of R. xanthina to drought stress

LRWC is an effective indicator of plant water status under stress. A negative correlation exists between drought stress severity and LRWC, as reported in numerous studies [37, 38]. In this study, LRWC in R. xanthina leaves decreased most rapidly under SD, suggesting that the plants may have approached their tolerance limit. Concurrently, plants accumulate osmoprotectants, such as Pro and SS, to help maintain cell turgor pressure [39]. As drought stress intensified in R. xanthina, the levels of osmoprotectants (Pro and SS) significantly increased. This accumulation led to increased cellular solute concentration and decreased osmotic potential. These changes help maintain turgor pressure and ensure normal cellular function [40].

MDA content serves as a significant indicator of oxidative stress severity in plants [41, 42]. In this study, MDA content in R. xanthina leaves increased progressively with drought severity and was highest under SD treatment, indicating the most severe oxidative stress. Following rewatering, MDA content decreased significantly but remained higher than CK, suggesting that MDA metabolic recovery require an extended period. MDA is a terminal product of lipid peroxidation triggered by ROS; therefore, reducing MDA levels depends on efficient ROS scavenging. Through evolution, plants have developed a sophisticated antioxidant system to maintain ROS homeostasis, keeping ROS at non-toxic levels [43, 44]. In this study, we measured the activity of the antioxidant enzyme SOD and the content of the antioxidant GSH, both of which are involved in detoxifying ROS. During drought stress, SOD activity and GSH content generally increased, although a slight decrease in GSH was observed specifically under MD.

Chlorophyll is a vital plant pigment primarily involved in photosynthesis. In addition, some studies have suggested that chlorophyll—particularly chlorophyll a—may play an auxiliary role in ROS scavenging under stress conditions [45]. In R. xanthina, chlorophyll a content increased progressively with drought severity, whereas chlorophyll b content increased initially under light drought but decreased under moderate drought. This differential response between the two pigments suggests that their biosynthesis, degradation, or regulatory pathways may be differentially modulated under drought stress. However, the underlying regulatory mechanism remains to be further investigated.

Metabolic pathways under drought stress

Plant hormones are important low-molecular-weight plant growth regulators that function as signaling molecules to regulate a vast array of physiological processes in plants [46, 47]. Drought stress disrupts normal metabolism, inhibits growth, and activates complex hormone signaling networks [48, 49]. In this study, the number of DEGs associated with plant hormone signaling increased with drought severity, peaking under SD. This shows that R. xanthina mobilizes a substantial set of genes within hormone signaling pathways under intense stress conditions.

Concurrently, the number of DEGs associated with photosynthesis increased with drought severity, also peaking under SD, with most being downregulated. This suggests that the photosynthetic apparatus in R. xanthina is significantly suppressed under drought stress. Analysis revealed that these photosynthetic DEGs predominantly encode chlorophyll a-b binding proteins and photosystem II proteins. Chlorophyll a-b binding proteins are integral thylakoid membrane proteins located in both photosystem I (PSI) and II (PSII). They form light-harvesting complexes by binding chlorophyll and other pigments. PSII is generally more sensitive to environmental stresses, including drought, than PSI [50, 51]. Drought stress damages the photosynthetic apparatus, causing structural alterations such as thylakoid grana vacuolation and deformation, which can lead to the disassembly of the photon-capturing PSII complex. This structural damage impairs the PSII reaction center and disrupts its photochemical reactions and protein phosphorylation [52, 53]. Consistent with the down-regulation of photosynthetic genes, total chlorophyll content decreased under drought, reducing light-harvesting capacity. Concurrently, ROS accumulation impairs the function of photosynthetic proteins, further compromising PSII efficiency [54, 55].

Under drought stress, plants accumulate ROS, which can negatively impact numerous physiological processes [56]. In this study, antioxidant-related pathways, specifically the ascorbate-glutathione (AsA-GSH) cycle, were significantly enriched. GSTs, which are evolutionarily conserved enzymes, play a crucial role in cellular detoxification and ROS defense [57, 58]. GSTs contribute to plant tolerance against a range of abiotic stresses, including drought, salinity, and heavy metal toxicity [59, 60]. For instance, in Medicago ruthenica, overexpression of MruGSTU39 enhanced drought tolerance, resulting in higher survival rates, reduced ROS accumulation, and lower MDA levels compared to wild-type (WT) plants under drought stress [61]. This demonstrates the significant role of GSTs in modulating plant drought responses and recovery. In R. xanthina, most GST genes were highly expressed under drought stress. This, together with the elevated physiological antioxidant capacity, underscores their crucial role in ROS scavenging. Ascorbate (AsA) is a major antioxidant and a key substrate for ROS detoxification [62, 63]. APX is a key enzyme in the AsA-GSH cycle, catalyzing the reduction of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) by oxidizing ascorbate to monodehydroascorbate (MDHA) and then dehydroascorbate (DHA). DHA is subsequently reduced back to ascorbate by glutathione in the cytoplasm, completing the cycle. The ascorbate peroxidase-glutathione reductase (APX-GR) system, particularly in chloroplasts and cytosol, is highly effective in fine-tuning H2O2 detoxification, in contrast to CAT and POD [64]. In this study, the expression of both APX and GST genes was significantly up-regulated, and the number of related DEGs increased with drought severity. This indicates that the coordinated up-regulation of the antioxidant system plays a crucial role in R. xanthina drought tolerance by mitigating ROS damage.

Analysis of TFs and the NAC gene family under drought stress

TFs can bind to specific cis-acting elements in eukaryotic genes, thereby activating or repressing gene transcription. They are pivotal components of stress response networks in plants, activating adaptive responses to environmental cues like drought by modulating the expression of stress-related genes [65]. Our transcriptomic analysis revealed significant enrichment of differentially expressed TFs from families such as NAC and AP2 under drought stress. The AP2 family modulates drought responses, often by integrating with plant hormone signaling pathways [66, 67]. One AP2 DEG in our dataset showed persistently low expression, which may link it to ethylene signaling and a potential negative regulatory role in stress resistance. bHLH TFs can enhance drought tolerance by mediating ABA or jasmonate signaling [68, 69]. In this study, one bHLH gene showed expression that increased with drought severity and decreased after rewatering, indicating its sensitivity to water status.

The NAC TF family plays crucial roles in plant development and abiotic stress adaptation. NAC TFs enhance stress tolerance by transcriptionally regulating networks of stress-responsive genes, enabling plants to cope with harsh conditions [70, 71]. Studies on NAC genes in other Rosa species provide valuable insights for understanding their function in R. xanthina. For example, silencing RcNAC72 in R. chinensis ‘Old blush’ compromised leaf water retention [72]. RpNAC72 from R. persica enhances osmotic adjustment and alters ABA sensitivity when overexpressed in A. thaliana [73]. Similarly, A. thaliana plants overexpressing RhNAC3 from R. hybrida exhibit improved drought tolerance through reduced leaves water loss [74]. Collectively, these studies highlight the importance of NAC TFs in drought resistance across Rosa species. Consistent with this, our study identified multiple differentially expressed NAC genes in drought-stressed R. xanthina. The expression patterns displayed distinct temporal dynamics: most genes exhibited peak expression under SD stress, while a subset showed high expression only during MD phase followed by expression decline in the SD phase. These dynamic expression patterns suggest functional diversification among NAC members, with different genes contributing to distinct stages of the drought stress response in R. xanthina.

Among these, we focused on RxNAC105 for further functional characterization based on multiple lines of evidence. First, RxNAC105 exhibited sustained high expression throughout the drought stress period, with its expression pattern validated by qRT-PCR. Second, and more importantly, sequence analysis revealed that RxNAC105 is an ortholog of RcNAC72 from R. chinensis ‘Old Blush’ and RpNAC72 from R. persica—both of which have been functionally validated as key regulators of drought tolerance in their respective species. These findings demonstrate that RxNAC105 orthologs are repeatedly identified as key drought-responsive genes across multiple Rosa species, suggesting that they may play a conserved and critical role in drought adaptation within the Rosa genus.

Preliminary functional validation suggests that RxNAC105 overexpression is associated with increased CAT activity and enhanced antioxidant capacity. These results indicate that RxNAC105 may contribute to enhanced antioxidant capacity, consistent with the functions reported for its orthologs in related species. Promoter analysis of RxNAC105 revealed multiple cis-elements, including ABRE, ARE, G-box, and CGTCA-motif, suggesting that its expression may be regulated by ABA, oxidative stress, light, and jasmonate signaling pathways during drought response (Supplementary Table S12). This cross-species evidence, combined with our own expression and validation data, provides a strong rationale for prioritizing RxNAC105 for further functional characterization. However, direct evidence for drought tolerance enhancement was not performed in this study and warrants further investigation. Additionally, whether RxNAC105 directly binds to the promoters of antioxidant genes and activates their transcription remains to be determined through experiments such as yeast one-hybrid, or dual-luciferase reporter assays.

Conclusion

In summary, this study provides insights into the physiological and molecular mechanisms underlying the drought response of R. xanthina. At the physiological level, R. xanthina employs a synergistic strategy to mitigate drought damage, involving osmotic adjustment (Pro and SS accumulation) and enhanced antioxidant defense (increased SOD activity and GSH content), despite significant suppression of its photosynthetic apparatus. Transcriptomic analysis identified 6,217 DEGs, which were significantly enriched in key pathways such as plant hormone signal transduction, glutathione metabolism, and photosynthesis. Furthermore, a genome-wide analysis identified 174 NAC TFs in R. xanthina. Integrated analysis of transcriptomic expression data highlighted RxNAC105 as a key drought-responsive candidate gene. Transient overexpression of RxNAC105 significantly increased CAT activity, suggesting that RxNAC105 may contribute to enhanced antioxidant capacity. These findings elucidate the molecular basis of drought tolerance in R. xanthina and provide a valuable candidate gene resource (RxNAC105) and a theoretical framework for enhancing drought resistance in Rosa species through genetic improvement.
