Persistent Fifth Aortic Arch in a Right-Sided Aortic Arch Associated With Tetralogy of Fallot
Authors: Gajon Uthayakumaran, Matthew Liava'a, Julian Ayer, David Murphy, Claire Lawley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPersistent fifth aortic arch is a rare congenital cardiac anomaly. We report the imaging findings of a subtype of persistent fifth aortic arch in an infant with tetralogy of Fallot and a right-sided aortic arch.Key Words: aorta, hybrid imaging, tetralogy of Fallot

Clinical Vignette

A male newborn was delivered at 35 weeks of gestation via an emergency lower segment cesarean delivery with a birthweight of 1.49 kg. There was an antenatal diagnosis of tetralogy of Fallot with a right-sided aortic arch. Noninvasive prenatal testing did not identify aneuploidy in chromosomes 13, 18, and 21. A postnatal echocardiogram on day 2 of life confirmed the antenatal diagnosis (Figure 1A). The aortic arch was right-sided with a mirror-image branching pattern; however, it was noted that the aortic arch had a dual lumen consistent with a persistent fifth aortic arch (PFAA) (Figure 1B, Video 1). There was no coarctation. A computed tomography scan was performed at 3 months of life, which confirmed the findings of a PFAA in a right-sided aortic arch (Figures 1C to 1E). Postnatal single nucleotide polymorphism microarray analysis detected no clinically significant copy number alterations; specifically, there was no copy number change of clinical significance on chromosome band 22q11. The infant underwent surgical repair of tetralogy of Fallot at 3 months of age. Intraoperatively, there were limited views of the aortic arch; however, no obvious morphologic abnormality was identified. During aortic cannulation for cardiopulmonary bypass, it was noted that there was a tactile step during cannula insertion, which was presumed to be related to the presence of the PFAA. The infant is now 12 months after repair of tetralogy of Fallot with no clinical sequelae related to the PFAA.

In normal embryonic development, the dorsal and ventral aortas are connected by 6 aortic arches (Figure 1F). These arches undergo a predictable sequence of persistence or regression resulting in normal thoracic vascular anatomy. A right aortic arch occurs when the right dorsal aorta persists and the left dorsal aorta regresses. The fifth aortic arch usually regresses completely with no postnatal derivatives; however, failure to do so results in a PFAA. PFAA may take multiple forms, and it may occur as an isolated lesion or be associated with congenital heart disease.1 Oppido and Davies2 developed a simple classification system for PFAA based on embryologic and clinical relevance. The most common form of PFAA is the double-lumen aortic arch (type A1 using the Oppido and Davies classification) as described in the case above; however, a right-sided double-lumen aortic arch is a rare subtype of an already extremely rare arch abnormality.1 In any case, a double-lumen aortic arch is most commonly asymptomatic; however, it can be associated with aortic arch hypoplasia or interruption. Therefore, recognition of a PFAA is important to exclude associated anomalies, which may be clinically significant.
