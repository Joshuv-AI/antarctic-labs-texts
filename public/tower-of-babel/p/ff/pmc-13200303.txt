Dual-loaded homotypic exosomes with endogenous IR808 and pH-responsive DOX drive sequenced chemo-PDT and convert “cold” OSCC tumors “hot”
Authors: He Wang, Yang Yu, Wenbin Zhou, Baihui Miao, Yanqing Zhang, Jiahao Zhu, Ziyi Lin, Jiayang Han, Xiaoding Xu, Fenghe Zhang, Qing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChemotherapy remains central to oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) management but is constrained by poor specificity, systemic toxicity, multidrug resistance, and limited durability. Tumor-derived exosomes (TEX) offer a clinically relevant carrier owing to their biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and homotypic targeting; however, prevailing high-efficiency loading methods (electroporation/sonication/extrusion, etc.) may disrupt membrane proteins, promote cargo leakage and aggregation, and increase costs—hindering clinical translation.ResultsHere we report IR808/Doxorubicin@Tumor-derived Exosomes (ID-TEX) that combine endogenous IR808 loading (preserving membrane integrity and homotypic ligands) at cell level with covalent, pH-responsive anchoring of doxorubicin via a hydrazone linker on the exosome surface (HYD-DOX). Upon cellular uptake, acidification in endosomes/lysosomes rapidly cleaves the linker, releasing DOX to induce nuclear DNA damage, followed by IR808-mediated photodynamic therapy (PDT) to amplify oxidative stress—establishing a sequenced chemo-PDT cascade. Leveraging the native integrin repertoire and CD47 signaling of TEX, ID-TEX achieves active homing, prolonged circulation, uniform intratumoral distribution, and deep penetration. Mechanistically, the intensified oxidative and multi-pathway cytotoxic stress elicits immunogenic cell death (ICD), promotes dendritic cell maturation, and recruits CD8⁺ and CD4⁺ T cells, converting “cold” OSCC into “hot” tumors and transforming localized photochemotherapy into systemic antitumor immunity.ConclusionsCentering this chemo-photodynamic–immunotherapeutic cascade on enhanced ROS, ID-TEX concurrently improves efficacy, reduces toxicity, and strengthens immune modulation, underscoring strong translational potential for precision OSCC therapy.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04359-5.Keywords: Oral squamous cell carcinoma, Exosome, Photodynamic therapy, Chemotherapy, Immunogenic cell death, Endogenous loading

Introduction

Chemotherapy remains a cornerstone in the management of oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), which constitutes approximately 90% of all oral malignancies. Notably, OSCC is frequently diagnosed at an advanced stage, conferring a poor prognosis (1). For localized OSCC, primary management involves surgical resection, with adjuvant radiotherapy or concurrent chemoradiotherapy administered based on clinical indications (2). In contrast, recurrent or metastatic OSCC relies predominantly on systemic chemotherapy, with radiotherapy and immunotherapy utilized either in combination or sequentially (3). However, traditional radiation and chemotherapy lack tumor specificity, accumulate toxicities, and frequently encounter primary or acquired resistance, yielding mostly short-term disease control. Even with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, overall response rates and durability remain limited, due not only to resistance but also to insufficient antigenicity, impaired antigen presentation, immune exclusion, metabolically suppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), suboptimal timing or patient selection, and immune-related adverse events leading to treatment interruptions [1–3]. Therefore, upstream amplification of antigens and danger signals can trigger immunogenic cell death (ICD) via CRT exposure, ATP release and HMGB1 secretion; it also promotes DC maturation and activates CD4⁺/CD8⁺ T cell cascades, thus representing a more fundamental approach to boost the initiation and persistence of immune responses [4–7]. Clinically, there is an urgent need for precise strategies that jointly optimize efficacy, toxicity, repeatability, and immune amplification [8–10].

Given OSCC’s superficial, visually accessible lesions with limited tissue thickness, photodynamic therapy (PDT) offers minimally invasive and repeatable treatment advantages [11, 12]. Upon irradiation at specific wavelengths, photosensitizers enter the triplet state and react with oxygen/biomolecules to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), enabling spatially selective cytotoxicity [13]. PDT produces less toxicity than radiotherapy and can be repeated as needed [14, 15]. Chemotherapy and PDT are mechanistically complementary. The essence of chemo-photodynamic synergistic therapy lies in its construction of a multi-layered cascade treatment system [16]. This system establishes a self-reinforcing therapeutic loop within the tumor through sequentially triggered and amplified biological events: At the initiation of treatment, the synergistic effect concurrently induces an explosive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), provoking an “oxidative storm” that launches multifaceted assaults on lipids, proteins, and DNA, rapidly leading to organelle dysfunction and irreversible cell death [17, 18]. It provides preliminary proof-of-concept evidence that ID-TEX may hold potential as a therapeutic strategy for oral squamous cell carcinoma. The resulting ROS then effectively initiated ICD, activated CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells, and directes their efficient infiltration into the tumor tissue.

[19]. This complete cascade reaction chain not only achieves “reduced toxicity and enhanced efficacy” while lowering the chemotherapy dose but also successfully transforms local physicochemical treatment into a systemic antitumor immune response [20–22]. In vivo, however, both chemotherapeutics and photosensitizers still suffer from inadequate targeting, heterogeneous intratumoral distribution with limited penetration, and rapid clearance/off-target accumulation—narrowing the exposure and safety windows and blunting synergy [23–25]. Although nanodelivery can improve biodistribution, conventional synthetic nanoparticles predominantly rely on passive enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) or exogenous ligands, which are vulnerable to ligand shedding, reticuloendothelial system (RES) uptake, and tumor heterogeneity, leading to inconsistent accumulation and distribution [26, 27]. A delivery platform combining intrinsic, stable targeting with controllable release is therefore critical [28]. Tumor-derived exosomes (TEX) naturally recapitulate the membrane composition and surface protein profile of their parent cells, conferring high biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and homotypic homing [29–31]. Their CD47 “don’t-eat-me” signal prolongs circulation, while receptor-mediated endocytosis or membrane fusion enables efficient cargo delivery [32–34].

Different exosome-loading strategies not only influence drug-encapsulation efficiency but also determine whether the vesicle membrane remains intact and whether its native targeting capacity is preserved. Electroporation achieves high loading yet frequently ruptures the exosomal membrane and triggers nonspecific aggregation [35, 36]. Sonication promotes drug entry but fragments the vesicles and strips away surface-targeting ligands [37, 38]. Liposome fusion can improve the loading of hydrophobic or macromolecular drugs [39], yet it risks membrane damage and the introduction of exogenous lipids that may increase in-vivo immunogenicity.Moreover, these methods requires specific equipment and precise conditions, increasing the cost and difficulty of exosome drug development [40]. Endogenous loading and simple co-incubation help preserve exosomal membrane integrity and protect functional proteins, streamline the loading procedure, and facilitate large-scale production and quality control; however, they may suffer from suboptimal loading efficiency [41, 42]. Therefore, the loading strategy and the drug itself must be carefully matched to maximize cargo content while retaining the biological activity of the vesicles.

Compared with other photosensitizers, IR808 offers distinct advantages: unlike the clinically used ICG, it enables simultaneous imaging, PDT and photothermal therapy (PTT) under a single 808 nm irradiation, avoiding the phototoxicity associated with UV excitation. Literature [43] show that the photothermal conversion efficiency (PCE) of IR808 is significantly higher than that of ICG. Due to its lipophilicity, IR808 can cross the cell membrane into the cytosol and thus be efficiently loaded via the endogenous route. The engineered conjugate DSPE-PEG-HYD-DOX carries a hydrophobic DSPE tail that is highly compatible with the phospholipid bilayer of exosomes. During co-incubation, the DSPE moiety rapidly inserts into the hydrophobic core of the exosomal membrane through hydrophobic interactions, anchoring and embedding the entire construct within the vesicle wall.

The hydrazone (HYD) linkage is acid-labile but stable during the loading process; it remains intact at neutral pH, ensuring that DOX is retained within the exosome, and only cleaves in the acidic tumor microenvironment to trigger drug release.

In summary, endogenous loading of IR808, coupled with homotypic targeting and pH-triggered sequential release, orchestrates a synchronized therapeutic cascade: (i) Precise tumor homing and deep tissue penetration—intrinsically facilitated by the native biological properties of exosomes—are further potentiated by endogenous drug loading, which enables stable encapsulation and targeted delivery of therapeutic payloads; (ii) Acid-responsive doxorubicin (DOX) release and near-infrared (NIR)-activated IR808-mediated reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation elicit synergistic oxidative damage and multi-pathway cell death via a sequentially executed chemo-photodynamic therapy (chemo-PDT) process; (iii) Induction of immunogenic cell death (ICD) promotes dendritic cell (DC) maturation; and (iv) Subsequent activation of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells, culminating in robust systemic antitumor immunity. By anchoring this “chemo-photodynamic-immunotherapeutic” cascade to enhanced ROS production as the pivotal mechanistic link, ID-TEX concurrently augments therapeutic efficacy, mitigates off-target toxicity, and reinforces immune modulation (Scheme 1)—providing preliminary proof-of-concept evidence that ID-TEX may hold potential as a therapeutic strategy for oral squamous cell carcinoma.

Results and discussion

Tumor microenvironment-responsive exosomes (ID-TEX) possess robust drug-loading and homotypic tumor-targeting delivery capabilities

Initially, MOC1 cells were pretreated with IR808 to induce IR808-loaded exosome secretion (IR808-TEX). Then, these exosomes were co-incubated with DSPE-PEG2K-HYD-DOX, using DSPE-PEG’s lipophilicity to insert it into the exosome bilayer, achieving DOX loading. Particle size analysis was performed on TEX, IR808-TEX, and ID-TEX using a particle sizer (Fig. 1a). The average sizes were 105.9 ± 5.43 nm for TEX, 131.1 ± 5.96 nm for IR808-TEX, and 157.2 ± 6.98 nm for ID-TEX. After drug loading, all systems remained within the exosome size range. Moreover, all three exhibited normal size distributions, with ID-TEX having a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.172 ± 0.002, indicating uniform and concentrated size distribution (Fig. 1b). The zeta potentials of TEX, IR808-TEX, and ID-TEX were − 9.5 ± 1.03 mV, -11.6 ± 0.48 mV, and − 12.2 ± 1.08 mV. The higher absolute zeta potential of ID-TEX suggested stronger electrostatic stability. UV-Vis spectroscopy revealed characteristic absorption peaks (Fig. 1c). Spectral overlay analysis showed that ID-TEX exhibited absorption peaks at 485 nm (DOX) and 779 nm (IR808), confirming successful loading of both drugs onto TEX. Over an 8-day period in PBS and BSA solution (Fig. 1d and e), ID-TEX demonstrated good stability in size, zeta potential, and PDI. Size maintained at 140 nm with minimal fluctuation, indicating strong stability. PDI was consistently low at 0.2, showing uniform size distribution and good monodispersion.

Standard curves were calculated by measuring IR808 and DOX absorbance at different wavelengths and concentrations. These results indicated that ID-TEX successfully loaded IR808 and DOX with good encapsulation efficiency. To enhance drug release precision and reduce chemotherapy toxicity, this study introduced the HYD functional group, giving ID-TEX tumor microenvironment-specific responsiveness. Tumor cells mainly metabolizes through the glycolysis pathway, leading to the accumulation of lactic acid and an acidic tumor microenvironment (TME) with a pH of 6.5–6.8. ID-TEX leverages the cleavage of HYD under acidic conditions to achieve precise and controllable drug release at the tumor site, thereby reducing systemic toxicity. As shown in the drug release curve in Fig. 1f, the cleavage of HYD effectively triggered the release of DOX from ID-TEX under pH = 6.5 conditions, with a release efficiency significantly higher than that in a neutral environment, reaching a cumulative release rate of 91.8% ± 0.92% after 72 h. The drug-release profile of IR808 from ID-TEX is shown in Fig. 1g. After 72 h, the cumulative release reached 53.27 ± 0.31% at pH 7.4, whereas it attained 72.83 ± 0.92% at pH 6.5. Thermal stress decreased membrane-bending rigidity, while ROS oxidation further weakened inter-lipid van der Waals forces; the two effects cooperated to induce a rapid “pore–fusion–rupture” cascade within seconds, leading to immediate drug leakage. Quantification of ID-TEX against the corresponding standard curves gave entrapment efficiency of 2.77% ± 0.03% for IR808 (EE_IR808) and 25.81% ± 1.35% for DOX (EE_DOX); drug loading capacity of 7.36% ± 0.58% for IR808 (DLC_IR808) and 10.22%± 0.86% for DOX (DLC_DOX).

This result confirmed that ID-TEX has a pH-responsive effect and can achieve rapid and relatively complete drug release under the weakly acidic conditions of the TME. TEM images clearly displayed the morphological characteristics of ID-TEX (Fig. 1h). ID-TEX had a typical bilayer membrane and smooth surface, consistent with exosome morphology. No changes occurred during drug loading, showing structural integrity. ID-TEX was stable, with no lysis or irregular shapes, highlighting its drug delivery potential.

Therapeutic efficacy of ID-TEX relied on its targeting delivery and endosomal escape capabilities. ID-TEX retained tumor exosome targeting molecules such as, integrins and fusion proteins to selectively bind tumor cell receptors, enhancing uptake and avoiding immune clearance. Western blot analysis of specific protein expression in TEX (Fig. 1i) showed distinct bands for exosome-specific proteins such as CD9, CD63, and TSG101. The protein expression confirmed exosome presence and good surface protein activity during drug loading. Calnexin and histone were negative, indicating that the sample contained essentially no cellular debris, endoplasmic reticulum or nuclear contaminants and thus met the Society for Extracellular Vesicles (SEV) criteria for “high-purity exosomes”.The dual-drug-loaded exosomes carrying IR808 and DOX were successfully constructed, providing a new direction for exploring combined antitumor effects of chemotherapy and PDT, and laying the foundation for homotypic tumor-targeted delivery and immune evasion.

Although the results showed exosomes with a clear, intact “saucer-like” bilayer membrane, and their size, zeta potential, and PDI remained stable over eight days, and Fig. S5 demonstrated that ID-TEX exosomes specifically accumulated in the target tumor tissue within 24 h after injection, direct validation—such as ligand-binding assays or single-particle tracking of exosome–cell interactions—was lacking. Whether the targeting proteins retained their native conformation and binding affinity remains to be mechanistically verified and will be a central focus of future work.

Unlike the synthetic nanocarriers (liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, etc.) employed in chemo -PDT immune-integrated systems [44–46], TEX possesses intrinsic tumor-homing capacity, low immunogenicity, and high biocompatibility [47]. These features prevent the rapid clearance and systemic toxicities associated with synthetic vectors, while the pH-responsive module ensures drug release occurs only within the acidic TME, minimizing off-target side effects.

An additional limitation of this study was the lack of a head-to-head comparison with exosomes prepared by physical loading methods; consequently, functional evidence demonstrating that endogenous loading confers superior homotypic targeting was not provided. Thus, the presumed targeting advantage of endogenous loading over sonication- or extrusion-based approaches remains unverified.

Effects of ID-TEX on Oral squamous cell carcinoma MOC1 cells

As shown in Fig. 2a, the uptake of ID-TEX by MOC1 cells was observed under a fluorescence microscope. The DOX in ID-TEX exhibited distinct fluorescence, which was rapidly and efficiently taken up by the cells within 1 h, indicating high uptake efficiency. In contrast, the red fluorescence of ID-L929-EXO was significantly weaker than that of ID-TEX. This suggests that ID-TEX, derived from tumor cells, retains integrin family proteins on its surface. These proteins enable specific recognition and targeting of overexpressed receptors on tumor cells through integrin-mediated “homing effects”, enhancing cellular uptake and demonstrating excellent homotypic targeting ability. Endosomal escape capability of ID-TEX was observed using confocal microscopy (Fig. 2b). The results indicated that ID-TEX successfully released drugs from endosomes into the cytoplasm. Fluorescence analysis revealed a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.156 for ID-TEX, indicating its superior endosomal escape ability. This prevented drug degradation in endosomes and ensured efficient release in the cytoplasm, triggering therapeutic effects.

As shown in Fig. 2c, in the absence of laser irradiation, the IR808 + DOX and ID-TEX groups exhibited higher fluorescence intensity, mainly due to the conversion of DOX to semiquinone, which reacted with oxygen molecules to induce the generation of ROS. With laser irradiation, fluorescence intensity increased significantly in all groups. IR808 absorbed laser energy to produce ROS, causing oxidative stress and activating apoptotic pathways.

According to the CCK-8 assay results (Fig. 2d), the tumor cell viability in the IR808-TEX (-) group was 96.43% ± 1.32%, indicating no significant cytotoxicity without laser irradiation. Fluorescence staining (Fig. S1.a) was used to assess the cytotoxic effects of different treatment groups. Without laser, dead cell proportions rose in Control, IR808 + DOX, and ID-TEX groups. With laser, dead cells increased significantly, especially in IR808 + DOX and ID-TEX groups. Results aligned with CCK-8 data, confirming cytotoxic effects and safety. Laser activation was crucial for IR808’s photosensitizing activity.

Flow cytometry was used to detect apoptosis rates in each group. Compared to the Control (+) group, the apoptosis rate in the ID-TEX (+) group significantly increased to 61.03% ± 0.87%, indicating that the combination of DOX and PDT enhanced the apoptotic induction efficiency compared to monotherapy. Further analysis of apoptotic subpopulations revealed that the early apoptotic cell proportion in the ID-TEX (+) group increased from 1.79% ± 0.30% in the control group to 33.6% ± 0.86%, and the late apoptotic cell proportion increased from 1.25% ± 0.30% to 30.47% ± 1.07%. Notably, the apoptotic induction efficiency of the ID-TEX (+) group was significantly higher than that of the IR808 + DOX and IR808-TEX groups, suggesting that ID-TEX further enhanced the activation of apoptotic pathways in tumor cells by optimizing drug delivery efficiency and photosensitizer activity (Fig. 2e-f). If a group treated only with DOX could be added, it would better reflect the effect of combination therapy, and it is necessary to explore it more deeply in future studies.

The results from the 3D tumor spheroid model further validated the deep penetration ability of ID-TEX in tumor cells. Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) imaging (Fig. 2g) showed that ID-TEX had stronger penetration than conventional chemotherapy drug DOX at different depths within the tumor spheroids. ID-TEX penetrated deeply into tumor spheroids, with strong fluorescence at 100 μm depth. The IR808 + DOX group had weaker penetration, with drugs mostly on the surface. This highlights ID-TEX’s ability for deep penetration, boosting efficacy and showcasing its carrier advantages.

The immunological effects of ID-TEX on oral squamous cell carcinoma MOC1 cells

ID-TEX not only directly kills tumor cells but also induces ICD, releasing DAMPs to recruit and activate APCs, which then activate CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells to migrate to the tumor site [48]. These T cells specifically recognized and eliminated tumor cells, thereby establishing an efficient and durable antitumor immune response [49]. This effect transforms OSCC from a “cold” to a “hot” tumor, boosting the body’s antitumor efficacy [50, 51]. CRT on the cell membrane acts as an “eat me” signal for APCs, while ATP release attracts immune cells. HMGB1 secretion activates the inflammatory response. These form the core of ICD, with CRT binding to CD91 in the early stage to promote APC recognition of tumor antigens. In the middle stage, ATP recruits phagocytes via the P2 × 7 receptor. In the late stage, HMGB1 activates TLR4/RAGE, maturing dendritic cells and releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines, enhancing antitumor immunity. In this study, the ICD-inducing ability of ID-TEX was evaluated by detecting the above markers. The ID-TEX (+) group had the highest levels of CRT exposure (Fig. 3a) and ATP (Fig. 3b) and HMGB1 (Fig. 3c) release, indicating that the combination of PDT and DOX significantly enhanced ICD induction. Tumor cells could release more immunogenic markers, more effectively activating the immune response and promoting tumor cell clearance. In addition, the experiment also detected a significant increase in IL-6 and IL-12 levels in the ID-TEX group (Fig. 3d-e). Photosensitizers can be rapidly cleared from the body [52], and a single-burst release strategy often fails to elicit sustained immune activation. Moreover, simultaneous activation of multiple therapeutic agents may cause an explosive ROS surge—an uncontrolled burst in both tumor and normal tissues—resulting in severe systemic side-effects and impaired immune priming [17]. In contrast, the spatiotemporal control provided by our sequential cascade not only enhances direct killing of OSCC cells but also evokes antitumor immunity through ICD.

To determine whether ROS enrichment contributed to the antitumor and immunomodulatory effects of ID-TEX, we conducted a ROS-scavenging experiment. As shown in Fig. S4a, the experimental concentration of NAC exhibited no overt cytotoxicity. The efficacy of ROS elimination after each treatment is presented in Fig. S4b. Figure 3h–l further demonstrated that NAC pretreatment markedly, yet not completely, attenuated ID-TEX-induced ICD and the subsequent promotion of DC maturation in co-culture. These data indicated that ROS accumulation importantly propelled the antitumor and immunoregulatory actions of ID-TEX, but was not the sole mechanism. This is likely because NAC can only block oxidative addition but cannot reverse the DNA strand breaks mediated by DOX through TOPO II poisoning [53]. Additionally, the photothermal conversion effect of IR808 under 808 nm irradiation is not cleared by NAC.

Research on the treatment of mouse oral squamous cell carcinoma model with ID-TEX

To further investigate the therapeutic effects of ID-TEX on oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), we established a mouse tongue orthotopic OSCC model. The C57 mouse OSCC model construction and in vivo antitumor experiment are shown in Fig. 4a. Biosafety of ID-TEX was a key focus in our experiments. By monitoring the body weight changes of mice during the treatment period (Fig. S2a), we found that the body weight of mice in all groups remained stable in the early stage of treatment and slightly decreased in the later stage, which might be due to the increased size of oral tumors affecting the mice’s feeding. The results of complete blood count (CBC) analysis (Fig. S2b-d) showed that the peripheral blood parameters WBC, LYM, and GRAN were within the normal range in all groups. H&E staining (Fig. S2e) showed no significant drug-induced damage in major organs, confirming ID-TEX’s good biosafety. In vivo imaging (Fig. S2f) showed weak tumor fluorescence in the Cy5.5-L929-EXO group due to passive EPR targeting and lack of active ligands. In contrast, the Cy5.5-TEX group showed stronger and sustained tumor fluorescence. Avoiding systemic drug distribution is crucial in tumor therapy. ID-TEX accumulated in tumors, evaded immune clearance, and prolonged drug residence. Strong fluorescence was also seen in the liver and kidneys, key for drug metabolism and excretion. The pharmacokinetic profiles of IR808 + DOX and ID-TEX in vivo (Fig. S2e) showed a typical decline trend over time, consistent with pharmacokinetic rules. The IR808 + DOX group had lower blood drug levels, indicating faster free drug metabolism. ID-TEX had higher levels and longer half-life due to strong immune evasion. The “don't-eat-me” signal inhibited phagocytosis, evaded RES uptake, and extended circulation time. This enabled ID-TEX to provide sustained therapeutic effects in tumor treatment. It is worth noting that the experiments lacked calculations of the pharmacokinetic parameters for ID-TEX to demonstrate the long-term in vivo circulation of ID-TEX. These aspects should be explored in depth in future studies.

Current exosome-based immune-nanoplatforms often failed to reverse the immunosuppressive TME of advanced tumors (e.g., high levels of Tregs and M2 macrophages). In contrast, ID-TEX coupled chemotherapy–PDT-induced ICD with the intrinsically immunostimulatory properties of tumor-derived exosomes, achieving a “two-pronged” immune activation without any exogenous ligand modification.

Prior studies identified ICD activation as the critical upstream event driving CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and the conversion of cold into hot tumors [54, 55]. The resulting increase in CD8⁺ T cell density and the decrease in Treg expression therefore signify the immunotherapy-induced cold-to-hot transition [56–58]. This study did not perform loss-of-function experiments to block ICD signaling or deplete CD8⁺ T cells; consequently, the causal relationship between ID-TEX-induced ICD and the cold-to-hot tumor transition remained at the level of association and awaits mechanistic confirmation in future investigations.

It should be noted that, although the present study observed that ID-TEX markedly up-regulated canonical ICD read-outs (CRT, ATP, HMGB1) and promoted DC maturation and T-cell activation, loss-of-function experiments (e.g., neutralising antibodies against CRT/HMGB1, TLR4 gene deletion, DC depletion, or P2 × 7R blockade) were not performed to rigorously establish a causal link between ICD induction and downstream immune activation. Intervening in the DAMP–receptor axis to clarify this relationship is therefore an important direction for future research.

Additionally, it should be noted that the tumor-suppressive effect of ID-TEX was not achieved solely through immune-mediated mechanisms. Apart from triggering ICD, ID-TEX could also kill tumor cells via photothermal conversion and direct cytotoxicity. Moreover, the extent to which the antitumor efficacy of ID-TEX depends on immunity has not been directly verified. Future studies must therefore disentangle the respective contributions of direct cytotoxicity and immune-mediated tumor inhibition to the overall in vivo therapeutic outcome, and formally establish how much of ID-TEX’s therapeutic benefit is immune-dependent.

Conclusion

Here, we report ID-TEX, a homotypic-targeting exosome platform with endogenous drug loading that orchestrates a cascaded therapeutic approach for OSCC. Engineered from tumor cell-derived exosomes intrinsically loaded with IR808 and surface-anchored with HYD-DOX, it enables precise tumor targeting and deep tissue penetration. In the acidic tumor microenvironment, pH-responsive DOX is released: DOX exerts chemotherapeutic effects and generates ROS, while NIR-activated IR808 further enhances ROS production, forming a synergistically amplified ROS burst. This elevated ROS level not only initiates a sequential chemo-photodynamic cascade but also directly triggers ICD. ICD promotes the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which in turn induce DC maturation and enhance CD4⁺/CD8⁺ T-cell activation, thereby converting localized treatment into systemic antitumor immunity with minimal toxicity. By integrating this “chemo-photodynamic-immunotherapeutic” cascade with enhanced ROS as the key link ID-TEX optimizes therapeutic efficacy, safety, and immunomodulation.

This study offered preliminary proof-of-concept evidence indicating that ID-TEX holds potential as a therapeutic strategy for oral squamous cell carcinoma. To advance its clinical translation, a comprehensive set of follow-up studies is still required, including systematic formulation optimization and process scale-up, standardized pharmacokinetic and toxicological evaluations, and more rigorous pharmacodynamic validation.

Preparation of drug solutions

IR808 solution was prepared by dissolving 10 mg of IR808 in 1 mL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to make a 10 mg/mL solution, which was then stored at -20℃ in the dark for later use. DSPE-PEG2K-HYD-DOX solution was prepared by dissolving 1 mg of DSPE-PEG2K-HYD-DOX in 1 mL of tetrahydrofuran (THF) to make a 1 mg/mL solution, which was then stored at -20℃ in the dark for later use.

MOC1 cell recovery, culture, passaging, freezing, and pre-treatment

(1) Cell recovery and culture: MOC1 cells were taken out from liquid nitrogen and warmed in a 37℃ water bath. Then they were transferred to a 15 mL centrifuge tube in a biosafety cabinet disinfected with UV light for 30 min. Pre-warmed complete culture medium was added, and the cells were centrifuged at 1000 r/min for 5 min. After discarding the supernatant, the cells were resuspended and transferred to a 10 cm culture dish. The dish was placed in a 37℃ incubator with 5% CO₂ for culture. After 24 h, the old culture medium was aspirated, and the cells were washed twice with pre-warmed PBS. New culture medium was added, and the cell growth was regularly observed.

(2) Cell passaging: When the cell density reached 70% − 80%, the cells were passaged. The old culture medium was aspirated, and the cells were washed twice with pre-warmed PBS. 2 mL of trypsin was added for digestion for 2 min. When most cells became round and floated under the microscope, 4 mL of complete culture medium was added to terminate the digestion. The cell suspension was collected and centrifuged at 1000 r/min for 5 min. After discarding the supernatant, the cells were resuspended and seeded into new culture dishes at a 1:4 ratio. The culture dishes were shaken to evenly distribute the cells and then placed in a 37℃ incubator with 5% CO2 for culture.

(3) Cell freezing: Cells in the logarithmic growth phase (confluence of 80% − 90%) with normal morphology were selected for freezing. After centrifugation to collect the cells, 1 mL of serum-free freezing medium was added and mixed well. The mixture was then aliquoted into cryovials, which were first placed in a -80℃ freezer and then transferred to a liquid nitrogen tank for long-term storage after 24 h.

(4) Cell pre-treatment: When the cells grew to 80% − 90%, they were washed three times with PBS. The culture medium was replaced with exosome-free serum medium, and 10 µg/mL IR808 was added for incubation in the dark for 48 h. The supernatant was collected for subsequent experiments.

Differential ultracentrifugation for the isolation of tumor cell exosomes

(1) Preparation of exosome-free serum: Fetal bovine serum was centrifuged at 100,000×g for 18 h. The supernatant was gently collected to 0.5 cm above the precipitate, sterilized with a 0.22 μm filter, and stored at -20℃.

(2) Differential ultracentrifugation for exosome isolation: The supernatant of MOC1 cell culture and the supernatant after IR808 pretreatment were collected and processed as follows: Centrifugation at 300×g for 10 min to remove cell debris. The supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 2000×g for 10 min to remove dead cells. The supernatant was then centrifuged at 10,000×g for 30 min to remove cell debris. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter and transferred to ultracentrifuge tubes. After centrifugation at 100,000×g for 70 min, the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was washed with PBS and centrifuged again at 100,000×g for 70 min. The pellet was resuspended in PBS to obtain MOC1 cell exosomes (TEX) and IR808-loaded exosomes (IR808-TEX).

Preparation of tumor microenvironment responsive exosomes (ID-TEX)

The DSPE-PEG2K-HYD-DOX solution was added to the MOC1 cell exosomes to achieve a final concentration of 5 µg/mL. After thorough mixing, the mixture was incubated at 37℃ for 2 h. Following the incubation, PBS was added, and the mixture was centrifuged at 100,000 ×g for 70 min at 4℃. The supernatant containing the unbound drug was discarded, and the precipitate was the MOC1 cell exosomes loaded with IR808 and DSPE-PEG2K-HYD-DOX, namely IR808-TEX@DSPE-PEG2K-HYD-DOX, which is hereinafter referred to as ID-TEX.

Grouping description

In the experiment, the following groups were used: Control (−), Control (+), IR808 + DOX(−), ID-TEX (−), IR808 + DOX(+), and ID-TEX (+). Control (−) referred to the group that received no treatment. Control (+) was the group exposed only to near-infrared light. IR808 + DOX(−) designated the group treated with the indicated concentrations of IR808 and DOX. ID-TEX (−) was the group incubated with exosomes loaded with IR808 and DOX. IR808 + DOX(+) indicated the group treated with IR808 and DOX followed by near-infrared irradiation. ID-TEX (+) represented the group treated with IR808- and DOX-loaded exosomes followed by near-infrared irradiation. For in vivo experiments, groups requiring irradiation were exposed to an 808 nm NIR laser at 0.5 W cm− 2 for 2 min.For in vitro experiments, after 6 h of incubation the laser groups received 808 nm irradiation at 0.5 W cm− 2 for 10 min.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurement of ID-TEX size and polydispersity index

TEX, IR808-TEX, and ID-TEX were diluted to appropriate concentrations with PBS. The samples were gently pipetted to ensure uniform dispersion without generating bubbles. Aliquots of the samples were transferred into cuvettes pre-rinsed with PBS. The cuvettes were then placed into the particle sizer, and the instrument’s detection temperature was set at 25℃. The measurement parameters were adjusted according to the instrument’s requirements to measure the size and polydispersity index (PDI) of the exosomes.

Electrophoretic light scattering (ELS) measurement of ID-TEX Zeta potential

TEX, IR808-TEX, and ID-TEX were diluted with PBS and gently mixed. One milliliter of the sample was added to a folded capillary cell without touching the electrodes. The capillary was placed in the particle sizer, and the temperature was set to 25℃. The zeta potential was measured using ELS.

The stability of ID-TEX

TEX, IR808-TEX, and ID-TEX were placed in PBS solution and 40 g/L BSA solution. TEX, IR808-TEX, and ID-TEX were stored in the dark at 4℃. The size, PDI, and zeta potential were measured on days 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 using the aforementioned methods to evaluate their stability.

Protein quantification of exosomes by bradford assay

The Bradford assay was used for quantitative analysis of exosomal protein content. Calibration curves for bovine serum albumin at known concentrations were constructed. Protein samples were reacted with Bradford’s reagent for 10 min at room temperature, and absorption readings of 595 nm were recorded using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer.

Determination of drug loading capacity and drug entrapment efficiency(EE) of ID-TEX

Stock solutions of IR808 (10 mg/mL) and DOX (1 mg/mL) were diluted with PBS to 100 µg mL and further serially diluted to 12.5, 25, 37.5, 50, 62.5, 75 and 87.5 µg/mL. Using PBS as the blank, the absorbance (optical density, OD) of each concentration was measured at 779 nm and 485 nm for IR808 and Dox with a microplate reader (three replicates per concentration, mean values used). Standard curves were constructed from these data and used to calculate the drug concentrations in the samples, from which the entrapment efficiencies of IR808 and DOX were determined.

m: total amount of medication.

m1: total amount of free medication.

md: total amount of loading IR808 and Dox.

me:total protein content in exosomes.

Total protein content in exosomes was measured by Bradford assay.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis of ID-TEX morphology

After diluting TEX, IR808-TEX, and ID-TEX with PBS, 10 µL was dropped onto a copper grid to form droplets. After 1 min of sedimentation, the supernatant was gently absorbed with filter paper. Then, 10 µL of 1% uranyl acetate staining solution was dropped onto the copper grid. After 1 min of sedimentation in the dark, the supernatant was absorbed with filter paper. After drying at room temperature, TEM imaging was performed at 80–120 kV to observe their morphology and size.

Western blot (WB) analysis of specific protein expression in TEX

Total proteins were extracted using pre-cooled RIPA lysis buffer, with PMSF added at a ratio of 100:1 to maintain protein stability. After determining the protein concentration using the BCA method, the samples were mixed with loading buffer at a ratio of 4:1 and heated at 100℃ for 5 min in a metal bath for denaturation. The denatured samples were then separated by electrophoresis on a 15% SDS-PAGE gel. After electrophoresis, the proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk at room temperature for 1 h and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4℃. The membrane was then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, and the proteins were visualized using ECL chemiluminescent reagent.

UV-Vis spectroscopy analysis of characteristic absorption curves

Solutions of TEX, IR808, DOX, and ID-TEX at certain concentrations were prepared, and the characteristic absorption curves of each drug were detected using a micro-volume spectrophotometer.

Detection of the encapsulation efficiency of each component drug in ID-TEX

Stock solutions of 10 mg/mL IR808 and 1 mg/mL DOX were prepared, which were diluted with PBS to 100 µg/mL and further diluted to concentrations of 12.5, 25, 37.5, 50, 62.5, 75, and 87.5 µg/mL. Using PBS as a blank control, the absorbance (Optical density, OD) of IR808 at 779 nm and DOX at 485 nm was measured for each concentration using a micro-volume spectrophotometer. Each concentration was tested in triplicate, and the average value was used to plot a standard curve. The drug concentration in the samples was calculated based on the standard curve, and the encapsulation efficiency of IR808 and DOX was then calculated using the formula.

Detection of in vitro release of DOX from ID-TEX by dialysis

Buffer solutions were prepared by dissolving 10% BSA in PBS buffer at pH = 7.4 and pH = 6.5. Equal amounts of ID-TEX were dissolved in 1 mL of buffer solutions at pH = 7.4 and pH = 6.5, respectively, and transferred into 1000 Da dialysis bags, which were then sealed. The dialysis bags were placed into 50 mL centrifuge tubes containing 20 mL of buffer solutions at pH = 7.4 and pH = 6.5, respectively, and incubated in a 37℃ water bath shaker at 70 r/min in the dark. At predetermined time intervals, 2 mL of buffer solution was removed and replaced with the same volume of fresh buffer solution, which was then mixed and continued to be incubated under the same conditions. The drug release experiment lasted for 72 h. The OD values of the collected buffer solutions at different time points were measured at 485 nm using a micro-volume spectrophotometer, and the cumulative release was calculated based on the DOX standard curve.

Cell uptake experiment with ID-TEX

Logarithmic growth MOC1 cells were seeded in 24-well plates with 5 × 10⁴ cells per well and incubated in a 37 °C incubator for 24 h. The experimental group was added with equal concentrations of ID-L929-EXO and ID-TEX, and the cells were incubated for 1 h in a dark environment to effectively uptake exosomes. After culture, the cells were gently washed with PBS 3 times for 5 min each to adequately remove exosomes that are not taken up by the cells. Add Hoechst 33,342 dye and incubate at 37 °C for 10 min to label the nuclei. The samples were washed with PBS three times to ensure the residual Hoechst 33342 dye was removed. Finally, multichannel imaging was performed by fluorescence microscopy to observe cellular uptake of ID-L929-TEX and ID-TEX.

ID-TEX connotative escape experiment

MOC1 cells were seeded in 24-well plates with 5 × 10⁴ cells per well, incubated for 24 h, then ID-TEX was added and incubated for 4 h in a dark environment to promote adequate uptake of ID-TEX by cells. After incubation, the cells were gently washed with PBS 3 times for 5 min each to remove the uningested ID-TEX. The inclusions were labeled with Lyso-Tracker Green dye and incubated for 30 min in a dark place according to the recommended concentration and operating instructions, followed by another 3 light washes with PBS to remove excess dye. Hoechst 33342 dye was added and the samples were incubated at 37 °C for 10 min to label the nuclei. The samples were washed three times with PBS to ensure that residual Hoechst 33342 dye was removed. Finally, multichannel imaging was performed by laser confocal microscopy to observe the escape of ID-TEX connotations.

In vivo circulation study

Female C57 mice aged 4–6 weeks were randomly divided into two groups: IR808 + DOX group and ID-TEX group, with three mice in each group. After intravenous injection via the tail vein, blood samples were collected from the retro-orbital plexus at the following time points: 15 min (to determine the initial drug concentration), and then at 30 min, and 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, and 24 h. Blood samples were collected into heparinized tubes and kept in the dark. After centrifugation at 3000 r/min for 15 min to separate plasma, 10 µL of the supernatant was diluted 1:10 with PBS and placed in a 96-well black microplate. The fluorescence intensity of DOX was measured using a fluorescence microplate reader at 485 nm excitation wavelength.

Detection of high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) in mice by ELISA

The cell culture medium was transferred to a sterile centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 1000 ×g for 10 min at 4℃. 300 µL of 1× wash buffer was added to the wells of the microplate and allowed to stand for 30 s. After discarding the wash buffer and drying the plate on absorbent paper, the washing process was repeated twice. 50 µL of the sample to be tested was added to the sample wells. 100 µL of the detection antibody working solution was added to the reaction wells. 50 µL of the sample diluent was added to the reaction wells. The plate was sealed with a plate sealer. The plate was incubated at 4℃ for 16–18 h. The liquid was discarded, and 300 µL of wash buffer was added to each well to wash the plate, repeating the washing process five times. 100 µL of the chromogenic substrate TMB was added to each well, and the plate was kept in the dark at room temperature (25 ± 2℃) for color development for 10–30 min. 50 µL of the stop solution was added to each well, with the order of addition being as close as possible to the order of the chromogenic substrate.

Detection of IL-6 and IL-12 by ELISA

The cell culture medium was transferred to a sterile centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 1000 ×g for 10 min at 4℃. 300 µL of 1× wash buffer was added to the wells of the microplate and allowed to stand for 30 s. After discarding the wash buffer and drying the plate on absorbent paper, the washing process was repeated twice. 50 µL of the sample to be tested was added to the sample wells. 100 µL of the detection antibody working solution was added to the reaction wells. 50 µL of the sample diluent was added to the reaction wells. The plate was sealed with a plate sealer. The plate was incubated at 37℃ for 1 h. The liquid was discarded, and 300 µL of wash buffer was added to each well to wash the plate, repeating the washing process five times. 100 µL of the chromogenic substrate TMB was added to each well, and the plate was kept in the dark at room temperature (25 ± 2℃) for color development for 15 min. 50 µL of the stop solution was added to each well, with the order of addition being as close as possible to the order of the chromogenic substrate.

NAC reactive oxygen species scavenging assay

Cells in the NAC and ID-TEX(+) + NAC groups were pretreated with 5 mM NAC for 1 h, after which the drugs were added according to the grouping. Twenty-four hours later, cell proliferation and intracellular ROS levels were measured.

In vivo distribution study

(1) Construction of C57 Mouse Oral Squamous Cell Carcinoma Model: MOC1 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were digested, centrifuged, washed with PBS, resuspended, and counted, then kept on ice for later use. The cell suspension was inoculated on the right side of the mouse tongue, and the experiment started 3 days later.

(2) Cy5.5-L929-EXO and Cy5.5-TEX containing DSPE-PEG2K-Cy5.5 were prepared as described above. Mice were randomly divided into two groups and injected via the tail vein with the same concentration of nanomedicine. Mice were anesthetized at 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, and 24 h post-injection, and the accumulation of the drug in the tumor site was observed using an in vivo imaging system. After 24 h, mice were sacrificed, and the tongue tumor, muscle, and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) were dissected and imaged to detect the distribution of the drug in the body.

In vivo antitumor study

The C57 mouse oral squamous cell carcinoma model was constructed as described in the in vivo distribution experiment. Mice were randomly divided into six groups (n = 4): Control(-), Control(+), IR808 + DOX(-), IR808 + DOX(+), ID-TEX(-) and ID-TEX(+). Mice were injected via the tail vein with PBS, IR808 + DOX, or ID-TEX (dosed at an IR808 concentration of 5 mg/kg). Six hours later, the laser groups were irradiated with 808 nm laser (power 0.5 W/cm²) for 10 min. Mice were dosed every 2 days, and tumor length and width were measured with calipers to calculate volume. After five doses, treatment was stopped to observe antitumor efficacy. On day 15, mice were sacrificed with CO₂ gas, tumors were dissected and imaged, and relative tumor inhibition rate was calculated.

Flow cytometry analysis of DC maturation and T cell status

In vitro, the maturation of DC cells was assessed after co-culture with MOC1 cells. In vivo, flow cytometry was used to detect the expression of CD3+CD8+ T cells in tumor tissues and the maturation of dendritic cells in lymph nodes.

Detection of imunogenic cell death markers by immunohistochemistry

Tumor tissue sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated in ethanol, then immersed in preheated sodium citrate buffer and subjected to antigen retrieval by microwave heating at a sub-boiling point for 10 min, followed by cooling to room temperature. Sections were blocked with 5% BSA at room temperature for 30 min, then incubated with primary antibodies against CRT or HMGB1 overnight at 4℃ in a humidified chamber. The next day, sections were stained with DAB, counterstained with hematoxylin for 1 min, dehydrated in ethanol and xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam. Expression was observed under an Olympus microscope, with sections washed three times with PBS between each step.

Detection of immune activation by immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical staining was performed on mouse tumor and spleen tissues as described above to analyze the infiltration of CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells.

Immunofluorescence staining for 4-HNE, CD163, Foxp3, PD-1 and Tim-3

Tissue sections were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 15 min, blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h, and incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. After three PBS washes (5 min each), fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min, and slides were mounted with antifade mounting medium.

In vivo biosafety study

(1) Weight Monitoring: During the treatment period, mice were weighed every 2 days and the weight changes were recorded.

(2) Complete Blood Count (CBC): On day 15 of the experiment, blood was collected from the retro-orbital plexus of mice in each group into anticoagulant tubes, and the number of white blood cells, granulocytes, and lymphocytes was measured within 2 h using an automated hematology analyzer.

(3) Histological Analysis: Major organs of mice (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution at 4℃ for 24 h. After fixation, samples were washed with running water for 4 h, dehydrated with gradient ethanol, made transparent with xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Continuous sections of 4 μm thickness were prepared using a microtome, spread at 42℃ to reduce wrinkles, and baked at 65℃ to adhere the tissue to the slide. Hematoxylin and eosin staining was performed: sections were dewaxed with xylene, rehydrated with gradient ethanol, and stained successively with hematoxylin, differentiating solution, bluing solution, and eosin, with each step followed by rinsing with running water. Finally, sections were dehydrated and made transparent with gradient ethanol and xylene, mounted with neutral balsam, and observed for pathological changes under an Olympus microscope.

Statistical analysis

Comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired t-tests, and differences among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). All experiments were repeated at least three times. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, with levels of significance indicated as: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001. Non-significance was denoted as P > 0.05, marked with ns.
