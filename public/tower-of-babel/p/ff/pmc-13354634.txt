Association between the timing of palliative care consultation and end-of-life outcomes in patients with cancer
Authors: Jung Sun Kim, Wan Taek Lee, Bhumsuk Keam, Jin-Ah Sim, Shin Hye Yoo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeWhile growing evidence supports the benefits of early palliative care (PC) referral for patients with cancer, the optimal timing of referral remains unclear. This study aimed to examine the association between the timing of PC consultation and end-of-life (EOL) outcomes in patients with cancer.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included patients with cancer who were referred for PC consultation at a tertiary hospital in the Republic of Korea between January 2018 and December 2022 and had died by June 2023. Clinical data were linked to the National Health Insurance Service database. Referral timing was categorized as late (≤ 30 days), intermediate (31–90 days), or early (> 90 days) before death. Primary outcomes included completion of advance statements, hospice use, aggressive care during the last month of life, and total medical costs near the EOL.ResultsAmong 6,151 patients, 43.3% received late referrals, and 23.8% received early referrals. Earlier referrals were more common among older patients and those with longer disease duration, Medicaid coverage, or lung and non-gastrointestinal solid tumors. Compared with late referrals, early referrals were associated with a higher likelihood of completing advance statements (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 1.98; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.73–2.27), greater hospice use (aOR 1.30; 95% CI 1.12–1.51), lower rates of aggressive care, and reduced medical costs near EOL.ConclusionEarly PC consultation offers an important opportunity to enhance advance care planning, promote hospice use, and reduce unnecessary healthcare utilization at EOL, supporting earlier integration of PC into routine oncology practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00520-026-10916-3.Keywords: Palliative care, Terminal care, Advance care planning, Hospice care

Introduction

Early integration of palliative care (PC) for patients with advanced cancer is increasingly recognized as a key component of high-quality, patient-centered oncology care [1–4]. When initiated early, PC supports shared decision-making, alleviates symptom burden, and facilitates care consistent with patients’ values and goals [5, 6]. Randomized and observational studies have shown that early PC involvement improves quality of life, reduces unnecessary interventions, and enhances patient and family experiences near the end-of-life (EOL) [7–15]. Accordingly, international oncology guidelines recommend PC referral within 8 weeks of diagnosis for patients with advanced or life-limiting disease [16].

Despite these well-established benefits, early PC referral remains uncommon in routine oncology practice. In many settings, fewer than one-third of patients with advanced cancer are referred to PC [17], and referrals often occur only within the last month of life [18]. Surveys of oncologists consistently show that referrals are frequently delayed until disease-directed treatments are no longer effective or symptoms become severe [19, 20]. Late referral limits the opportunity for advance care planning (ACP), goal setting, and symptom management, often forcing patients and families to make critical life-sustaining treatment (LST) decisions under crisis conditions. Delayed integration of PC also contributes to avoidable emergency department (ED) visits, intensive care unit (ICU) admissions, and escalating healthcare costs at the EOL [14, 21–23].

Prior research has largely evaluated PC referral in a binary manner (present or absent) rather than examining the timing of consultation [24]. Moreover, most studies have been conducted at single institutions [25–27] and have frequently focused on specific cancer types [9, 17, 28], limiting the understanding of patients’ care trajectories beyond the PC setting. Consequently, the optimal timing of PC referral and its associations with broader EOL outcomes, such as ACP, hospice use, and healthcare intensity, remain less well characterized in real-world oncology practice. To address this gap, this large retrospective cohort study aimed to evaluate how the timing of PC consultation relates to multiple EOL outcomes across the full continuum of care.

Material and methods

Study design, setting, participants, and data sources

This retrospective cohort study included patients with cancer who were referred to the PC consultation service at Seoul National University Hospital, a tertiary referral center in South Korea. The hospital operates a multidisciplinary, consultation-based PC program that provides both inpatient and outpatient services. Patients were identified from the institutional electronic medical records using ICD-10 diagnostic codes for malignant neoplasms (C00–C97). Eligible participants were those referred to the PC consultation team between January 1, 2018, and December 31, 2022, who subsequently died by June 15, 2023. Clinical data were linked to the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) claims database, allowing longitudinal assessment of healthcare utilization and outcomes beyond the hospital setting. Patients were excluded if the date of death was unavailable or could not be linked, or if essential variables were missing.

Exposure: timing of PC referral

The primary exposure was the timing of PC consultation, defined as the interval (in days) between the first PC consultation and the date of death. Referral timing was categorized into three groups: late (≤ 30 days before death), intermediate (31–90 days), and early (> 90 days before death). These cutoffs were selected a priori based on previous literature examining referral patterns and EOL outcomes in oncology populations. As this study was conducted at a national referral center where many patients had received prior treatments at outside institutions, accurately determining the onset of advanced disease was not consistently feasible. Therefore, the interval between PC consultation and death was used as a pragmatic and reliable measure of referral timing.

In a secondary exploratory analysis, the presence of an active cancer treatment plan at the time of referral was evaluated as a binary variable (yes or no), determined from documentation in the consultation notes.

Outcomes

The outcomes of interest were key indicators of EOL care quality, including completion of an advance statement, LST implementation, hospice utilization, receipt of aggressive EOL care, and total medical costs near death.

Completion of an advance statement was defined as the documentation of an Advance Directive (AD) or a Physician’s Order for Life-Sustaining Treatment (POLST), both of which are legally completed by the patient under Korean law [29]. LST implementation referred to the official documentation of withholding or withdrawal of LST, either by patient determination or by family determination when the patient lacked capacity [30]. Hospice utilization was categorized into inpatient, home, and consultation-based hospice services [2, 31], all requiring written consent and certification of terminal status by two physicians. Aggressive EOL care was defined as the receipt of any of the following during the final month of life: ED visit, ICU admission, cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), mechanical ventilation, renal replacement therapy, or chemotherapy within the last 2 weeks of life. Total medical costs were measured in the NHIS claims database as direct healthcare expenditures reimbursed by the NHIS during the final 6 months, 3 months, 1 month, and 2 weeks before death. Costs represented the sum of insurer payments and patient copayments recorded in claims. Non-medical costs, including transportation, caregiver time, productivity loss, and out-of-pocket expenses not captured in NHIS claims, were not included.

Covariates

Covariates included demographic and clinical characteristics that were considered potential confounders of the association between referral timing and EOL outcomes. These variables were age, sex, type of health insurance (National Health Insurance vs. Medicaid), household income quartile, region of residence, cancer type, and the time from cancer diagnosis to PC referral. Comorbidity burden was assessed using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), excluding cancer-related components, and calculated from diagnostic codes in the claims data. The score was included in multivariable models as a measure of baseline health status. Covariates were selected a priori based on previous literature and clinical relevance.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient characteristics across the three referral timing groups. Continuous variables are presented as means with standard deviations (SDs) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), as appropriate, and were compared using one-way analysis of variance or the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to assess the associations between referral timing and binary outcomes, including advance statement completion, LST implementation, hospice utilization, and aggressive EOL care. Referral timing was modeled as a three-level categorical variable, with the late referral group as the reference. Models were adjusted for age, sex, insurance type, household income quartile, region of residence, cancer type, time from cancer diagnosis to PC referral, and non-cancer CCI score. For cost outcomes, mean total costs were descriptively compared across referral timing groups within each prespecified EOL interval during the final 6 months, 3 months, 1 month, and 2 weeks before death. Formal multivariable adjustment was not performed for cost outcomes; therefore, these comparisons should be interpreted as descriptive. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated for intermediate and early referrals. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Of 6,721 patients initially identified, 6,151 with cancer who received PC consultation in either outpatient or inpatient ward settings and had complete data were included in the final analysis. Patients were excluded if consultation occurred in other settings (n = 530), key information was missing (n = 29), or death dates could not be verified (n = 11) (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Trends in referral timing and cancer treatment plan

From 2018 to 2022, referral patterns to PC consultation remained largely stable in timing, with a median interval from consultation to death ranging from 32 to 39 days. However, the proportion of patients referred while an active cancer treatment plan was ongoing increased steadily over the same period, from 12.3% to 26.0% (Fig. 1).

Baseline characteristics by referral timing

Among 6,151 patients, 2,666 (43.3%) were referred late (≤ 30 days before death), 2,022 (32.9%) intermediate (31–90 days), and 1,463 (23.8%) early (> 90 days). Compared with late referrals, earlier referrals were more common among older patients, those covered by Medicaid, and those with lung or other non-gastrointestinal solid tumors. In contrast, patients with hematologic and non-colorectal gastrointestinal cancers were more frequently referred late (Supplementary Table S1).

Advance care planning by referral timing

Earlier referral was strongly associated with a higher rate of advance statement completion (68% in the early group vs. 52% in the late group; aOR, 1.98; 95% CI, 1.73–2.27) (Table 1). Among patients who completed an advance statement, the distribution of AD and POLST did not differ significantly across referral groups. Overall, LST documentation was more frequent in the late referral group (65.2% vs. 49.8% early), whereas patient-determined documentation increased with earlier referral (77.6% vs. 60.5%; aOR, 2.28; 95% CI, 1.86–2.80).

Hospice utilization by referral timing

Overall hospice utilization differed by referral timing and was highest in the intermediate group (78.1%), followed by the early (76.6%) and late (72.0%) groups (Table 2). Compared with late referral, the odds of any hospice use were significantly higher in both the intermediate (aOR, 1.39; 95% CI, 1.21–1.59) and early (aOR, 1.30; 95% CI, 1.12–1.51) groups.

Patterns also varied by hospice type. Inpatient hospice use was most common among intermediate referrals (47.8%), slightly lower among early referrals (42.9%), and least frequent among late referrals (24.6%). Consultation-based hospice predominated in the late group (59.8%) and was less frequent with earlier referral. In contrast, home hospice and home nursing care increased steadily with earlier referral, reaching 12.6% and 13.5% in the early group compared with 2.4% and 5.0% in the late group, respectively. Earlier referrals were also associated with earlier initiation and longer duration of both inpatient and home hospice services (Fig. 2).

Aggressive EOL care by referral timing

Earlier referral was consistently associated with less aggressive EOL care (Table 2). ED visits occurred among 60.8% of patients in the late referral group, compared with 38.1% and 32.5% in the intermediate and early referral groups, respectively (aOR, 0.39; 95% CI, 0.35–0.44 and aOR, 0.31; 95% CI, 0.27–0.35, respectively). ICU admissions followed a similar pattern (5.4% in the late group vs. 2.2% in both the intermediate and early groups). Chemotherapy within the last 2 weeks of life was also more common with late referral (51.3% vs. 35.8% intermediate and 30.1% early). Use of renal replacement therapy and mechanical ventilation was lower with earlier referral, with a significantly reduced adjusted odds only for mechanical ventilation in the early group (aOR, 0.73; 95% CI, 0.54–0.98). CPR rates were low and similar across groups (2.7–2.8%).

Total medical cost by referral timing

Total medical costs decreased consistently with earlier referral across all time intervals before death (Fig. 3). At 6 months before death, the mean costs were $20,183 in the late group, $18,905 in the intermediate group, and $18,193 in the early group. The difference became most pronounced in the final month, with corresponding costs of $5,795, $3,712, and $3,096, respectively. During the last 2 weeks of life, the mean costs were $1,899, $1,249, and $1,087, respectively.

Comparison by cancer treatment plan at the time of referral

In an exploratory analysis, patients with an active cancer treatment plan at the time of referral were more often referred early (36.0% vs. 20.6%) and slightly more likely to complete an advance statement (62.7% vs. 58.3%) (Supplementary Table S2). The interval from POLST completion to death was longer in this group, whereas the timing of advance statement completion was similar between groups (Supplementary Figure S2). Overall hospice utilization was comparable. However, consultation-based hospice was more common among patients with an active treatment plan (67.0% vs. 56.0%). In contrast, inpatient and home hospice were more frequent among patients without a treatment plan (38.4% vs. 29.6% and 7.5% vs. 5.2%, respectively).

Discussion

In this single-center cohort linked to nationwide claims data, the timing of PC referral was clearly associated with a broad range of EOL outcomes. Patients referred more than 90 days before death were more likely to complete advance statements, document their own LST preferences, and experience fewer intensive interventions in their final months. They also incurred lower medical costs and accessed hospice services more frequently. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that earlier PC consultation can meaningfully reshape the trajectory of EOL care in real-world settings, aligning it with both patient values and value-based oncology practice.

A notable aspect of earlier referral was its support for patient participation in decision-making. Patients referred more than 90 days before death were not only more likely to complete advance statements but also to document their own LST preferences rather than relying on surrogate decision-makers. Early referral provides sufficient time and preserved decisional capacity for patients to engage in goals-of-care discussions, increasing the likelihood of both advance documentation and patient-directed LST implementation. Similar findings have been reported in prior studies. A randomized trial by Temel et al. [9] showed higher EOL preference documentation with early PC involvement. Additionally, a retrospective study [32] linked earlier referral (> 90 days before death) to more timely do-not-resuscitate documentation. The growing social recognition of self-determination following Korea’s Life-Sustaining Treatment Decisions Act may also have contributed to this trend [33, 34], reinforcing the impact of early PC involvement.

The relationship between referral timing and hospice utilization was more complex. Both early and intermediate referrals were associated with higher overall hospice enrollment than late referrals. However, the difference between the early and intermediate groups was modest, particularly for inpatient hospice use. This pattern likely reflects the relative lateness of what was defined as “early” referral—more than 90 days before death—compared with international recommendations [16], which advocate PC integration within weeks of diagnosis. Nonetheless, this categorization corresponds to referral patterns commonly recognized by oncologists in Korea and reflects real-world practice [20], in which the timing of palliative engagement is shaped by resource availability, patient preferences, cultural context, and disease trajectory [35]. Within this context, our definition offers a practical reference point for interpreting referral timing in real-world clinical settings. The observed increase in the utilization of home hospice and home nursing services with earlier referral further indicates a gradual shift toward greater continuity of care once palliative involvement begins before the terminal phase.

As anticipated, earlier referrals were associated with substantially lower use of aggressive EOL interventions, including ED visits, ICU admissions, and chemotherapy in the last 2 weeks of life. Similar associations have been consistently observed in prior studies [1, 22, 23, 36]. The only notable exception was CPR, which remained uncommon across all groups. This likely reflects prevailing cultural norms and a strong aversion to CPR once palliative discussions had occurred. Earlier referral was also linked to lower healthcare expenditures, particularly during the final month of life. Collectively, these findings reinforce that early PC engagement mitigates costly, crisis-driven care and promotes a trajectory that is less burdensome. This timing aligns EOL care with both patient preferences and value-based oncology [3].

The distinct decrease in costs closer to death should be interpreted considering how costs were summarized in this study. We analyzed cumulative total costs within shorter windows before death during 6 months, 3 months, 1 month, and 2 weeks. Therefore, the absolute cost was expected to be lower in the shorter time windows and should not be interpreted these lower absolute costs in the shorter intervals as a decline in spending intensity near death. Rather, the relevant comparison should be between referral timing groups within the same EOL interval. Because our cohort consisted of patients who had already received PC consultation at a tertiary hospital, it may have reduced crisis-driven care, which is a major contributor to cost increase near death. In addition, because non-reimbursed out-of-pocket expenses and informal caregiving costs were not captured, our estimates reflect reimbursed direct medical costs rather than the full societal cost of EOL care.

The subgroup of patients referred while an active cancer treatment plan was in place highlights a nuanced interface between oncology and PC. Even while receiving anticancer therapy, these patients showed comparable or even higher advance statement completion rates. This suggests that the presence of ongoing treatment does not hinder—and may even facilitate—timely goals-of-care discussions [37]. In this context, PC involvement helps anchor decision-making in anticipation rather than reaction, ensuring that patients retain agency even as disease-directed therapies proceed. However, their subsequent patterns of care revealed important distinctions: consultation-based hospice predominated, while inpatient and home hospice use was less common than among those without a treatment plan. These findings underscore both the feasibility and the limitations of concurrent PC: although patient-centered conversations can proceed, the structural linkage to hospice services often remains constrained until anticancer treatment is discontinued.

Among patients referred while an active cancer treatment plan was in place, the rate of ACP completion was comparable or slightly higher, suggesting that ongoing therapy does not preclude timely goals-of-care discussions [37]. This finding is consistent with guideline recommendations for early PC integration alongside anticancer therapy [16, 38], showing that such coordination can support meaningful ACP without undermining hope [39, 40]. However, access to hospice services remained limited, because current regulations generally require discontinuation of anticancer treatment before enrollment. Accordingly, while early PC referral promotes timely ACP, deciding on the appropriate time to discontinue active therapy remains essential for a seamless transition to hospice care [41–43].

In our cohort, the median interval between PC consultation and death remained largely unchanged over the study period, despite the clear advantages of earlier referral. This stability suggests that substantial barriers to earlier referral may persist for both oncologists and caregivers [19, 44, 45]. Potential barriers may include prognostic uncertainty and concern among oncologists that referral may reduce patients’ hope or be perceived as abandonment, as well as misconceptions among patients and caregivers that palliative care signify giving up or is limited to terminal care. At the same time, the growing proportion of patients referred while receiving active cancer treatment reflects a changing perception of PC from a service reserved for the terminal phase to a concurrent component of comprehensive care. This pattern suggests that referral decisions are increasingly guided by patient needs rather than by time since diagnosis, indicating a shift toward a more flexible, needs-based model of PC integration. This model may be more practical and culturally adaptable than rigid, time-based definitions of “early” referral [35, 46]. Future models of PC integration may also require more scalable shared-care approaches, including greater involvement of oncology teams in delivering basic palliative and supportive care alongside specialist PC services.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective, single-center design may limit the generalizability of the findings. Nevertheless, linkage with a nationwide claims database enabled comprehensive assessment of outcomes beyond the hospital setting. Second, referral timing was defined retrospectively based on the interval from PC consultation to death. This retrospective definition introduces hindsight bias and may not fully capture the evolving disease trajectory or prospective decision-making process in clinical practice. Third, unmeasured variables, such as disease stage, symptom burden, functional decline, prognosis awareness, or patient and family preferences, as well as institutional factors, including staffing or referral practices, may have influenced both the timing of referral and the outcomes examined, resulting in potential residual confounding and selection bias. Fourth, as the study was observational, temporal relationships between referral and outcomes cannot be determined with certainty; therefore, these associations should not be interpreted as causal. Fifth, the exclusion of patients with life-limiting non-cancer conditions such as advanced heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or neurological diseases limits the generalizability of this study. Finally, while our categorization of early referral was pragmatic and grounded in local practice, it remains later than the optimal window suggested by international guidelines. Nevertheless, the large cohort size, integration of real-world clinical and claims data, and inclusion of multiple, objective EOL outcomes strengthen the robustness and relevance of the findings. Future studies are warranted to evaluate more qualitative outcomes, including perceptions of care quality, family experiences [15], and quality of ACP as well as factors influencing timely integration of PC.

In conclusion, earlier PC referral was associated with more patient-directed decision-making, less aggressive and costly EOL care, and greater use of hospice services. These findings support integrating timely PC referral as a standard component of oncology practice, enabling patients to approach the EOL with greater agency, care aligned with their values, and more judicious use of healthcare resources.
