Efficacy and safety of a one-step warming protocol of vitrified blastocyst stage embryos
Authors: Emma R Ebinger, Helena Misurac, Alexa M Giovannini, Vrunda B Desai, Nadia De Rosa, Rita Vassena, Paul J Atkinson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo study the effect of a one-step warming technique on survival and clinical outcomes of vitrified-warmed blastocysts in a consecutive cohort of patients.DesignRetrospective consecutive cohort study.SubjectsThis study included 1,303 transferred embryos from 950 unique patients treated at three clinics.ExposureThe exposure group included embryos warmed using a one-step warming protocol with a 1M sucrose solution for 1 min. The control group included embryos warmed using traditional, multistep warming, where embryos were exposed to 1M sucrose for 1 min, followed by 3 min in 0.5M sucrose, and 10 mins in washing solutions.Main Outcome MeasuresThe goal of this study was to compare survival, clinical pregnancy rate (CPR), ongoing pregnancy rate (OPR), and live birth rate (LBR) between multi- and one-step warming techniques. Additionally, subgroup analyses by maternal age, embryo morphology, day of vitrification, and mode of fertilization were also performed.ResultsSurvival rates were comparable across all comparisons. Pregnancy rates were comparable between multistep and one-step groups (CPR: 43.2% vs. 44.4%; OPR: 34.3% vs. 37.9%; LBR: 34.0% vs. 35.6%). Pregnancy probabilities between warming techniques were comparable between groups at any age point (32 – CPR: 42.6% vs. 47.4%; OPR: 34.9% vs. 39.6%; 42 – CPR: 25.8% vs. 20.7%; OPR: 12.6% vs. 12.9%). Good quality embryos (G2) had a lower chance of pregnancy than top quality embryos (G1) overall, but pregnancy rates were similar between groups (G1 – CPR: 52.2% vs. 55.9%; OPR: 45.7% vs. 49.2%; G2 – CPR: 39.0% vs. 39.4%; OPR: 29.2% vs. 33%). Similarly, Day 6 embryos were less likely to achieve pregnancy than Day 5 embryos, but pregnancy rates were comparable between groups (D5 – CPR: 45.5% vs. 46.5%; OPR: 36.3% vs. 40.2%; D6 – CPR: 27.3% vs. 27.6%; OPR: 18.2% vs. 22.4%). Pregnancy rates between intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) for male factor infertility and in vitro fertilization (IVF) were again comparable between groups (ICSI – CPR: 42.3% vs. 40.7%; OPR: 35.6% vs. 34.5%; IVF – CPR: 45.7% vs. 46.8%; OPR: 35.5% vs. 41.5%).ConclusionOne-step embryo warming provides similar survival and pregnancy outcomes compared with classical multistep warming while decreasing the procedure time by more than 90%.Key Words: Vitrification, ICSI, embryo warming, one-step warming, blastocysts

Vitrification and warming of embryos are essential tools in assisted reproductive technologies (ART). The introduction of these techniques has improved treatment safety, efficacy, and patient outcomes, enabling efficient fertility preservation (1), delayed embryo transfer in cases of elevated progesterone (2) or risk of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS) (3), as well as allowing for uncoupling of ovarian stimulation from embryo transfer in donor cycles (4).

One of the main goals of cryopreservation is preventing ice crystal formation in both intra- and extracellular spaces, to avoid disrupting subcellular structures such as microtubules and organelles, and causing osmotic stress by concentrating solutes in the residual unfrozen solution (5). Vitrification bypasses ice crystal generation through the formation of an amorphous glassy solid by increasing the viscosity of a liquid during cooling. Essentially, the solution remains a liquid and retains fluidic properties, but its viscosity is so high that it acts like a solid (6). This process is achieved through high concentrations of cryoprotectants (CPAs) and fast rates of cooling (7). Vitrification utilizes both permeable and nonpermeable CPAs. Permeable CPAs, typically small molecules such as ethylene glycol or dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), penetrate the plasma membrane and form bonds with water molecules, preventing ice crystal formation. Additionally, permeable CPAs increase the viscosity inside the cells and lower the glass transition temperature. Nonpermeating CPAs, such as sucrose and trehalose, remain outside the cell and work by drawing out water and dehydrating the intracellular space. This decreases the available water inside the cell, which minimizes crystallization and reduces the amount of CPAs needed to permeate into the cell. Furthermore, nonpermeating CPAs regulate osmotic shifts to prevent osmotic shock and increase the viscosity of vitrification solutions (8). Warming of vitrified embryos consists of the removal of CPAs and rehydration of the cells. This process is equally as delicate as vitrification and requires high warming rates to prevent ice crystallization. Current warming techniques include stepwise rehydration, allowing for the gradual exchange of water and CPAs to prevent osmotic shock and cell rupture (9). Although vitrification and warming have increased the efficacy of ART, the use of high concentrations and prolonged exposure to CPAs remains cytotoxic to oocytes and embryos.

Recent innovations in cryopreservation include shorter vitrification and warming protocols, which minimize both the time embryos spend outside of the incubator and exposure to CPAs. Further, a simplified protocol can increase lab efficiency through shorter procedure times, reduced chance of error, and streamlining of training efforts. In particular, there is a focus on the efficacy of a ‟super-fast”, one-step thaw protocol. For instance, a recent study evaluated a protocol that removes the subsequent dilution steps involved in standard embryo warming and utilizes only one step in 1M sucrose solution, resulting in comparable survival and clinical pregnancy rates, while improving the ongoing pregnancy rate and reducing miscarriage rate overall, compared with traditional, multistep warming techniques (10). Other investigators have reported comparable implantation and pregnancy rates between multistep and one-step warming, while saving embryologists over 14 minutes for each warming procedure (11). Other, similar protocols have also been tested, with comparable live birth rates with standard warming (12). These protocols have additionally been tested in cleavage-stage embryos with studies showing reduced miscarriage rates and comparable pregnancy rates after one-step warming (13). Additionally, cleavage-stage embryos warmed with a one-step protocol were able to develop to blastocysts at a similar rate to those warmed using multistep warming (14).

Although one-step warming protocols are promising, several unanswered questions remain. For instance, most reports focus on embryos of high morphological grade, from good-prognosis patients or oocyte donors. Recently, one-step warming was shown to cause overexpansion of embryos, leading to cell lysis (15). One could hypothesize that this cryodamage may become more apparent in embryos of lower morphological grade, especially in the trophectodermal compartment, or those with lower developmental competence, such as those developing more slowly, or from advanced maternal age patients. The aim of this study was to assess the safety and effectiveness of a one-step blastocyst warming technique both in the general and in selected patient populations, such as advanced maternal age, poorer quality embryos, and embryos derived from male factor infertility.

Materials and methods

Study population

This is a retrospective analysis of a consecutive cohort of 1,303 treatment cycles, corresponding to 950 unique patients and 1,303 transferred embryos at Adora Fertility Labs from August 2023 to April 2025. Up to July 2024, all warming of blastocyst-stage embryos in all clinics were performed according to a traditional, multistep protocol. From July 2024, all embryo warmings were switched to a one-step protocol in all clinics as a matter of clinical practice. All cycles were performed with the patients’ own gametes and without preimplantation genetic testing. Only transfers with a single embryo transferred were included. The number of patients and embryos included at each stage of the analysis is presented in Figure 1. Women included in this analysis were on average 33.9 ± 4.5 (range: 21–46) years of age at the time of embryo vitrification. Detailed demographic and cycle characteristics can be found in Table 1 and Figure 2. The study was approved by the local Scientific Advisory Committee (approval code EMB-ADO-181225).

Ovarian stimulation and oocyte retrieval

The most important point to emphasize is that controlled ovarian stimulation regimens and oocyte retrieval procedures did not change over time, and therefore the way the embryos were created between the two analyzed groups, one- and multistep warming, was the same. Patients underwent controlled ovarian hyperstimulation according to standard and accepted clinical practice. The majority of patients used antagonist protocols. Agonist protocols and flare protocols were used where clinically indicated. The specific type of gonadotropic injection used was according to the clinician's preference and clinical indication. Treating clinicians at the three clinics did not change over time. Oocyte retrievals were completed under heavy sedation according to standardized practice and technique and equipment did not change over time.

Oocyte fertilization and embryo culture

Sperm samples utilized were either produced fresh or frozen and thawed on the day of retrieval. Sperm preparation was performed the same day of oocyte retrieval and was evaluated according to the World Health Organization standards (16).

After oocyte retrieval, oocytes were inseminated by either conventional insemination (in vitro fertilization, IVF) or intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI). The ICSI was only performed if indicated by male factor infertility. Conventional insemination, with 100 × 103 motile sperm/well, occurred 4–6 hours after oocyte retrieval. For ICSI, cumulus cells were removed 2 hours after oocyte retrieval using 80 IU/mL hyaluronidase (Origio SynVitro Hyadase, CooperSurgical, Trumbull, CT) and evaluated. Mature (MII) oocytes were then inseminated using ICSI approximately 4 hours after retrieval.

Fertilization was assessed 16 to 18 hours after insemination. Normal fertilization was characterized by the presence of two pronuclei. Zygotes were cultured in culture media (Sage 1-Step, CooperSurgical) overlaid with mineral oil (Sage, CooperSurgical) and cultured in an incubator set at 37 oC, 5% CO2, 5% O2. Blastocyst development was assessed on Days 5 and 6 after insemination. Embryo morphology was assessed on the basis of expansion, appearance, and number of cells. Embryos were then allocated to one of two groups. Grade 1 embryos were classified as top quality, and Grade 2 embryos were classified as good quality. Equivalence of grades to Gardner scores is presented in Supplemental Table 1 (available online).

Embryo vitrification and warming

Embryo vitrification

Embryos were vitrified on D5 or D6 of development. Blastocysts were artificially collapsed by laser pulse at cell-cell junctions of the trophectoderm or by manual collapsing with 175 or 275 μm Stripper Tips (CooperSurgical) before vitrification. All embryos were vitrified individually and using Vitrifit (CooperSurgical). Embryos were placed in solution 1 (10 mM HEPES solution) for up to 10 minutes. Embryos were then placed in solution 2 (8% ethylene glycol, 8% DMSO) for 2 minutes and finally exposed to solution 3 (16% ethylene glycol, 16% DMSO and 0.68M trehalose) for 20–30 seconds, before being loaded onto cryopreservation device and plunged into liquid nitrogen. All vitrification procedures were performed at 37 °C using a blastocyst vitrification kit (Sydney IVF Blastocyst Vitrification Kit, Cook Australia, Brisbane, Australia).

Multistep warming

On the morning of the embryo warming, 1M sucrose was warmed to 37 °C for a minimum of 30 minutes, whereas 0.5M sucrose and MOPs solutions (Sage, CooperSurgical), were warmed to room temperature for a minimum of 30 minutes. Immediately before embryo warming, 0.5 mL of each solution was aliquoted into separate wells of a 4-well dish (Nunc, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Scoresby, Australia). The cryodevice was removed from liquid nitrogen and plunged into the 1M solution for 1 minute. Subsequently, the embryo was moved to the 0.5M sucrose solution for 3 minutes. Then, the embryo was passed through two wells of MOPs solution for 5 minutes each. The embryo was then washed through culture media (Sage 1-Step, CooperSurgical) and deposited in 20 μL of the same media for recovery in an incubator set to 37 oC, 5% CO2, 5% O2. Transfer of surviving embryos was performed within 1–4 hours of warming.

One-step warming

On the morning of the embryo warming, a 1M sucrose solution (Sage Warming Kit, CooperSurgical, Trumbull, CT) was warmed for a minimum of 30 minutes at 37 °C. Immediately before embryo warming, 0.5 mL of warm 1M sucrose solution was aliquoted into a 4-well dish (Nunc, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Scoresby, Australia) and placed on a heating stage. The cryodevice was removed from liquid nitrogen and plunged into the 1M solution for 1 minute. The embryo was gently removed from the device. Subsequently, the embryo was removed from the sucrose solution and washed in culture media (Sage 1-Step, CooperSurgical) and deposited in 20 μL of the same media covered with 1.0 mL oil pre-equilibrated overnight. Embryos were placed in an incubator set to 37 oC, 5% CO2, 5% O2. Embryo transfer of surviving embryos was performed within 1–4 hours of warming.

Endometrial preparation and embryo transfer

The most important point to emphasize is that the clinical management of patients and the embryo transfer (ET) technique did not change over time and therefore both cohorts analyzed are comparable. Frozen cycle management and ET were performed in line with accepted standard clinical practice.

In ovulatory women, the majority of cycles were natural frozen cycles. In some cycles, a trigger was used if indicated. In anovulatory women, letrozole frozen cycles were used and for some women stimulated frozen cycles were used where clinically indicated. Hormone replacement therapy cycles were used in a minority of cases where clinically required.

Embryo transfer was performed as per clinical standards using standard embryo placement 1–1.5 cm from the uterine fundus under US guidance.

Statistical analysis

Embryos were categorized into two vitrification groups on the basis of the thawing technique: multistep warming (MSW) and one-step warming (OSW). Embryos were grouped by grade at freezing, using the classification reported in Supplemental Table 1 (Group 1: top quality, Group 2: good quality). Survival after warming was defined as preserved integrity of at least 70% of the embryo 1–2 hours after warming. Biochemical pregnancy was defined as the presence of 50 IU/L β-hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin) in blood 10 days after ET. Clinical pregnancy was defined as the presence of a beating fetal heart, fetal sac, or otherwise clinically confirmed implantation. An ongoing pregnancy was defined as a beating fetal heart after 12 weeks of gestation.

The primary outcome was ongoing pregnancy, with embryo survival, biochemical pregnancy, clinical pregnancy, live birth, and neonatal outcomes as secondary outcomes.

All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.3). To model the relationship between warming protocol and pregnancy outcomes while allowing for nonlinear effects of age, a generalized additive models (GAM) was used. The model controlled for additional confounders such as embryo quality. To account for the nonindependence of multiple embryos from the same patient, a random intercept was included for patient ID. Model estimation was performed using restricted maximum likelihood. Model diagnostics confirmed a good fit, and smooth terms were visualized using plot.gam. The predict function was used to compute marginal probabilities at fixed ages, and pairwise comparisons of predicted outcomes were conducted to assess the interaction between protocol and pregnancy outcomes at specific maternal ages.

Neonatal complication rates were compared using χ2 tests or Fisher’s exact tests, where appropriate.

A level of 0.05 was selected to determine statistical significance in all cases

Results

One-step warming of embryos and reproductive results

We compared the embryological and reproductive outcomes between embryos allocated to MSW and OSW groups, focusing on survival, biochemical pregnancy, clinical pregnancy, and ongoing pregnancy outcomes. This analysis included a total of 1,303 transferred embryos from 950 unique patients: 720 embryos from 557 patients in the MSW group and 583 embryos from 393 patients in the OSW group, with 61 patients appearing in both groups. Overall, embryo survival rates were 98.6% for MSW and 98.4% for OSW groups (P = .63). The biochemical pregnancy rates were 46.0% for MSW and 47.5% for OSW groups (P = .74). Clinical pregnancy rates were 43.2% for the MSW and 44.4% for the OSW groups (P = .87). Finally, ongoing pregnancy rates were 34.3% for the MSW group and 37.9% for the OSW group (P = .27). Overall, rates for survival, biochemical pregnancy, clinical pregnancy, and ongoing pregnancy were comparable between groups when controlling for maternal age, embryo quality, and number of transferred blastocysts. Age, as expected, showed a significant, nonlinear correlation with pregnancy rates, and the effect of advancing age differed between the two study groups, with a more pronounced effect on the OSW group (edf = 1.4 for MSW and edf = 2.9 for OSW) (Fig. 3).

One-step warming of embryos from advanced maternal age patients

To evaluate whether maternal age affected embryo survival, biochemical, clinical, and ongoing pregnancy chances between warming groups, we predicted the probabilities from the fitted model. Survival rates showed no significant differences across ages and groups (P>.05 for all comparisons). As expected, we observed a progressive decline in the probability of pregnancy with increasing maternal age in general. For instance, compared with age 32, at age 42, the predicted probabilities for biochemical, clinical, and ongoing pregnancy dropped by an average of 19 percentage points in MSW and 26 percentage points in OSW, respectively. However, no significant interaction was observed between groups at any age, indicating that the effect of age on pregnancy probability was comparable between one-step and multistep warming. For a more detailed view of predicted probabilities at different ages and Society of Assisted Reproductive Technology age by warming group, see Supplemental Table 2.

One-step warming and embryos with variable morphological grade

Embryos were divided into two groups on the basis of morphological grade. Embryos considered top quality were classified as G1 (MSW: n = 223, OSW: n = 177), whereas embryos considered good quality were classified as G2 (MSW: n = 497, OSW: n = 406). Embryo survival, biochemical pregnancy, clinical pregnancy, and ongoing pregnancy rates were analyzed by test group and morphological grade. Embryo survival rate for G1 embryos was 98.4% in MSW and 98.9% in OSW groups. Embryo survival rate for G2 embryos was 98.6.% in MSW and 98.2% in OSW groups (P=.72). Biochemical pregnancy rate for G1 embryos was 56.5% in MSW and 60.5% in OSW groups, whereas for G2 embryos it was 41.2% in MSW and 41.9% in OSW groups. Clinical pregnancy rate for G1 embryos was 52.5% in MSW and 55.9% in OSW groups. Clinical pregnancy rate for G2 embryos was 39.0% in MSW and 39.4% in OSW groups. Ongoing pregnancy rates were 45.7% in MSW and 49.2% in OSW for G1, and 29.2% in MSW and 33.0% in OSW for G2 embryos. We observed significantly lower odds of biochemical, clinical, and ongoing pregnancy for G2 embryos across both MSW and OSW groups (average of 46% lower chances of pregnancy, P<.0001 for all comparisons), but no significant differences between groups in biochemical (P=.74), clinical (P=.87), and ongoing (P=.27) pregnancy rate.

One-step warming of slowly developing embryos

Embryos were additionally segregated on the basis of their day of development at vitrification. Embryos vitrified on Day 5 (MSW: n = 600, OSW: n = 492) and Day 6 of development (MSW: n = 88, OSW: n = 58) were compared for survival, biochemical, clinical, and ongoing pregnancy rates in both MSW and OSW groups. Embryo survival rate for D5 embryos was 98.9% for MSW and 98.8% for OSW groups, whereas for D6 embryo survival was 98.9% for MSW and 98.5% for OSW groups (P=.88). Biochemical pregnancy rate for D5 embryos was 48.3% for MSW and 49.4% for OSW groups, whereas for D6 embryos it was 30.7% for MSW and 32.8% for OSW groups (D5 vs. D6 P=.004). Clinical pregnancy rates for D5 embryos were 45.5% for MSW and 46.5% for OSW groups, and for D6, 27.3% for MSW and 27.6% for OSW groups (D5 vs. D6 P=.014). Ongoing pregnancy rates were 36.3% for MSW and 40.2% for OSW for D5 and 18.2% in MSW and 22.4% in OSW for D6 embryos (D5 vs. D6 P=.227). Overall, D6 embryos displayed significantly worse reproductive outcomes than D5 embryos when controlling for age, as expected. However, there was no difference between MSW and OSW in biochemical (P=.82), clinical (P=.96), and ongoing (P=.71) pregnancy rates.

One-step warming of embryos from patients with male factor infertility

Further, reproductive and embryological outcomes were compared on the basis of fertilization method, because ICSI was only performed in cases of an indication for male factor. Embryos derived from male factor infertility patients (MSW: n = 194, OSW: n = 145) and fertilized through ICSI were compared with embryos from nonmale factor infertility fertilized through classic IVF (MSW: n = 341, OSW: n = 265). Embryo survival rates were 98.8% for MSW and 99.1% for OSW groups in male factor cycles, and 99.3% for MSW and 100% in nonmale factor cycles, with no differences across groups or indications (P = .25).

The biochemical pregnancy rates were 44.8% for MSW and 42.8% for OSW groups in male factor cycles, and 49.0% and 50.2%, respectively, in nonmale factor cycles (P=.38). Clinical pregnancy rates were 42.3% for the MSW and 40.7% for the OSW groups in male factor cycles and 45.7% and 46.8% in nonmale factor cycles (P=.21). Ongoing pregnancy rate was 35.6% for MSW and 34.5% for OSW in male factor cycles and 35.5% in MSW and 41.5% in OSW for nonmale factor cycles (P=.57). Overall, rates for survival, biochemical pregnancy, clinical pregnancy, and ongoing pregnancy were comparable between groups when controlling for age and embryo quality.

Live birth and neonatal outcomes

A total of 1,239 transfers were included in the live birth analysis, with 430 infants born (MSW: 234 vs. OSW: 196). Live birth for MSW was 34.0% and 35.6% for OSW. After controlling for maternal age, embryo quality, and day of vitrification, live birth rates were comparable between MSW and OSW (P=.908).

The MSW group had an average birth weight of 3,477.2 g (SD: 1215.2), whereas OSW had an average birth weight of 3,574.6 g (SD: 1359.2; P=.0619). The sex distribution for MSW was 48.5% male and 51.5% female, whereas for OSW it was 56.2% male and 43.8% female (P=.107).

Maternal complications were similar between groups, with complications reported in 40/234 (17.1%) of clinical pregnancies in the MSW group and 33/196 (16.8%) of clinical pregnancies in the OSW group (P=.94). The most common maternal complication in both groups was gestational diabetes (MSW: 50.0%, n = 20; OSW: 51.5%, n = 17).

Additionally, neonatal complications were compared between groups with complications reported in 5/234 (2.1%) of infants in the MSW group, (jaundice: n = 1, respiratory support/neonatal intensive care unit admission: n = 1, neonatal hypoglycaemia requiring intravenous [IV]: n = 1, preterm birth requiring special care nursery admission: n = 1, and asynclitism: n = 1) and 2/196 (1.0%) in the OSW group (jaundice: n = 1, and ventricular septal defect: n = 1; P=.46).

Discussion

‟Super-fast”, one-step protocols are poised to streamline operations for clinics, especially considering the continuous increase in frozen embryo transfer cycles worldwide. Traditional, multistep vitrification and warming, however, are well-established techniques with an extremely high survival rate proven across millions of embryos. Responsible innovation mandates that operational improvements are not made at the expense of patient outcomes; it is therefore imperative that these new protocols are not just evaluated in good prognosis patients, but especially in those that stand to lose the most from a decrease in efficacy, i.e. women with few embryos, those with embryos of lower quality or otherwise worse prognosis.

Here, we evaluated 1,303 single transferred embryos across three centers, and showed that, overall, the use of a one-step warming protocol provides comparable results with traditional, multistep warming protocols. Previous studies have shown that one-step warming provides comparable or improved pregnancy rates with traditional warming techniques (10, 17, 18). However, few have addressed the question of the impact on the most fragile embryos. We provide support for the efficacy and safety of one-step warming in subpopulations of embryos that are expected to be more susceptible to cryodamage, such as lower quality, slowly developing embryos or those from patients with male factor or advanced maternal age. Although some comparisons lack statistical power to detect a clinically relevant difference, for most of them we could reliably identify a decrease in pregnancy rate of less than 10%, if there were one.

As expected, we identified a trend toward lower pregnancy odds as maternal age increased and embryo quality decreased, however this trend did not specifically affect the embryos warmed with the one-step protocol. A recent study compared pregnancy outcomes between multi- and one-step warming and found comparable implantation, clinical pregnancy, spontaneous abortion, and live birth rates after adjusting for maternal age, among other variables (19). Our work extends and improves previous reports: importantly, the effect of maternal age on reproductive outcomes is not linear; for instance, the effect of age between 33 and 35 years old is less pronounced than the effect between 38 and 40 years. Age should therefore be modeled considering this nonlinearity, e.g. using GAMs. We found that the effect of the one-step warming procedure is also nonlinear, something that has not been previously described.

Further, we found that embryos of poorer quality have reduced rates of pregnancy; however, this was observed across both groups. Our results support and extend those of a study comparing the rates of embryo survival, clinical pregnancy, negative pregnancy, biochemical/ectopic pregnancy, and spontaneous abortion between one-step and multistep warming techniques, which also found no differences in outcomes when comparing good- with poor-quality embryos (20).

Moreover, we show that one-step warming remains effective on slower-developing embryos that have been vitrified on D6. Our results align with those of a very recent study, where multistep and one-step warming procedures were stratified on the basis of development day, and found comparable clinical pregnancy rates (21).

We additionally focused on embryos derived from poor quality sperm, where ICSI was indicated. A mounting body of evidence indicates that the contribution of the sperm to the developmental potential of the embryo is substantial (22) and may affect the embryo’s response to vitrification and warming protocols. We found comparable rates for survival, biochemical, ongoing, and clinical pregnancy between multi- and one-step warming protocols. This is the first time that the contribution of the sperm is taken into account in the evaluation of one-step warming and further reassures its safety.

Finally, the inclusion of live birth and neonatal outcome data strengthens the clinical relevance of our findings. Importantly, we found similar live birth and neonatal outcomes across groups, with no increase in maternal or fetal complications in the OSW group.

Although the results from the present study are promising, we acknowledge some limitations in our study. First, as in all retrospective studies, some relevant variables may be unaccounted for in the controlled analysis, and this may affect the observed outcomes. Finally, the data presented here were collected in one group of associated clinics. This allowed for the inclusion of a high number of cycles, while controlling for variation in protocols and practices, although it may limit the generalizability of results.

Nevertheless, the large dataset, the consecutive cohort design including all patients, and the adequately controlled statistical analysis all provide significant strength to this work and support the introduction of one-step warming in clinical practice.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our work supports the potential of ‟super-fast”, one-step blastocyst warming techniques. One-step blastocyst warming leads to comparable survival, biochemical, clinical pregnancy, and live birth rates, while lessening procedure time, CPA exposure, and room air exposure, and strengthening the chain of custody and lowering the chance of error.
