Sinogram‐based flow estimation in computed tomography using a physics‐informed neural network: Impact of gantry rotation speed, X‐ray fluence and pulsed acquisition on accuracy
Authors: Jinyuxuan Guo, Gurnoor Singh Khurana, Alejandro Gonzalo Grande, Juan C. del Alamo, Francisco Contijoch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon‐invasive imaging‐based assessment of blood flow plays a critical role in evaluating heart function and structure. Computed Tomography (CT) is a widely‐used imaging modality that can robustly evaluate cardiovascular anatomy and function, but direct methods to estimate blood flow velocity from movies of contrast dynamics have not been developed.PurposeThis study evaluates the impact of CT imaging parameters on Physics‐Informed Neural Networks (PINN)‐based flow estimation and proposes an improved PINN‐based approach, SinoFlow, which uses sinogram data directly to estimate blood flow.MethodsWe generated pulsatile flow fields in an idealized 2D vessel bifurcation using computational fluid dynamics and simulated CT scans with varying gantry rotation speeds, photon flux, and pulse mode imaging settings. We compared the performance of PINN‐based flow estimation using reconstructed images (ImageFlow) to SinoFlow.ResultsSinoFlow significantly improved flow estimation performance by avoiding temporal inconsistency errors introduced by filtered backprojection. SinoFlow was robust across all tested gantry rotation speeds and consistently produced lower mean squared error and velocity errors than ImageFlow. Additionally, SinoFlow was less susceptible to noise in the sinogram and was compatible with pulsed‐mode imaging and maintained higher accuracy with shorter pulse widths.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates the potential of SinoFlow for CT‐based flow estimation, providing a more promising approach for non‐invasive blood flow assessment. The findings inform future applications of PINNs to CT images and provide an alternative which avoids limitations associated with image‐based estimation.

INTRODUCTION

Non‐invasive imaging‐based assessment of blood flow plays a critical role in the evaluation of cardiovascular function and structure. For example, the severity of aortic stenosis is classified by estimating peak flow velocity and deriving a mean transvalvular pressure gradient. 1 , 2 , 3 In addition, valvular regurgitation can cause morbidity and mortality and is graded using the extent of regurgitant flow. 4 Intricate flow patterns, such as vortices, can serve as risk factors for blood stasis 5 , 6 and correlate with mean pulmonary artery pressure, 7 , 8 atrial thromboembolism, 9 left ventricular function 10 and other cardiovascular conditions. 11

Conventionally, flow maps and associated metrics are obtained using either Doppler echocardiography or phase‐contrast magnetic resonance imaging (PC‐MRI). 12 , 13 Digital subtraction angiography has also served as an imaging modality from which to quantify blood velocity. 14 , 15 Despite its ability to evaluate cardiovascular anatomy and function, 16 x‐ray computed tomography (CT) has not been used to quantify blood flow. However, current CT systems can provide time‐resolved volumetric images through a fast, volumetric, high‐resolution acquisition. 17 Overall reductions in imaging dose and the capability to perform dose modulation has enabled CT imaging to be performed across one or more cardiac cycles. 18 This has enabled work which evaluates the dynamics of contrast evolution through the cardiovascular system. 19 , 20 Unfortunately, even with these improvements, a clinically robust method to estimate blood flow velocity from CT‐derived time‐resolved angiography images is not available. Such a method, which quantifies blood flow fields over space and time by analyzing the spatiotemporal change in iodine‐based contrast agent intensity, could augment the anatomical and functional CT assessments already available and have a significant clinical impact.

Physics‐Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) have demonstrated the ability to estimate fluid flow and pressure fields from spatiotemporal images of the transport dynamics of a passive tracer, such as CT contrast. 21 Estimates are obtained by training a neural network to learn an implicit representation of the observed images, while penalizing both deviations from the measured tracer concentration field and residuals of the governing physical equations: the Navier–Stokes equations, the continuity equation, and a convective transport equation. More recently, this inverse problem has been addressed with Optimizing a Discrete Loss (ODIL), which minimizes a loss constructed from grid‐collocated discretization of the governing equations based on standard numerical approximations (e.g., finite differences). 22 These emerging frameworks can be viewed as generalizations of the classic optical flow method, 23 embedding additional physical constraints beyond tracer convection and leveraging modern machine learning and optimization tools. PINNs have also been applied for data assimilation in cardiovascular flows, 24 , 25 super‐resolution in 4D flow MRI, 26 , 27 and Doppler echocardiography. 28 , 29 , 30 However, the use of PINNs for the assessment of contrast evolution in CT is more limited. While PINNs have been used to evaluate brain perfusion, 31 no methods currently exist to reconstruct cardiovascular flow fields from contrast‐enhanced 4D CT imaging. Notably, prior proof‐of‐concept studies 21 have focused on reconstructed image‐to‐flow inference under the assumption of perfect image reconstruction without temporal inconsistency artifacts.

Here, in a simulation study, we evaluate the impact of CT scan parameters on PINN‐based flow estimation and propose an improved framework that integrates the physics of flow, contrast transport, and CT image formation. Our first hypothesis is that a PINN's ability to infer hidden flow fields from contrast dynamics is limited by errors in reconstructed images. CT scanners have limited spatiotemporal resolution and noise in the sinogram data. 32 Therefore, reconstructed movies of concentration are subject to spatial blurring, temporal inconsistency artifacts, and noise which will be particularly pronounced with high velocities and rapid flow variations. To test this hypothesis, we evaluate the impact of both spatiotemporal artifacts and sinogram noise on estimation of flows using a PINN where images are generated using a conventional filtered back‐projection (FBP) reconstruction. This setup, which we term ImageFlow, does not account for the fact that blood and contrast advance through anatomy during CT data acquisition. Therefore, a collection of views may violate the static‐object assumption of FBP, leading to temporal inconsistency‐induced artifacts in the reconstructed images. As these artifacts increase, we expect the accuracy of PINN‐derived flow estimates to deteriorate. In contrast, we expect flow estimates to be more robust to sinogram noise as the implicit representation of the PINN is constrained by the governing physics.

Our second hypothesis is that PINN‐based flow estimation can be improved by avoiding explicit image reconstruction and instead solving directly for the concentration and flow fields from the sinogram data, a new framework we term SinoFlow. In SinoFlow, the forward CT acquisition process is embedded within the PINN as a flow‐aware time‐dependent Radon transform. As a result, contrast motion can occur throughout CT data acquisition and may enable improved consistency between the flow physics and the measured data. We demonstrate that SinoFlow avoids “learning” temporal inconsistency artifacts and enables robust estimation across various gantry rotation speeds and photon flux levels.

Lastly, we hypothesize that sinogram‐based PINN‐estimation is compatible with pulsed‐mode (PM) imaging, an advanced CT acquisition strategy now available on some CT scanners. During PM imaging, the x‐ray beam intensity is rapidly modulated, delivering x‐rays in short “on” pulses separated by “off” periods. This enables lowering patient exposure without uniformly reducing overall fluence 33 , 34 but subsamples the sinogram angularly. The impact of angular subsampling as defined by PM parameters ‐ namely, the pulse width, the number of consecutive imaging views “on” during each rotation, and duty cycle–on PINN‐based flow estimation accuracy is unknown. We test whether SinoFlow can accurately evaluate imaging data obtained under different PM settings to evaluate the impact of this dose reduction approach.

METHODS

Generation of flow fields

We generated pulsatile flow fields throughout an idealized 2D vessel which bifurcates into two daughter vessels of the same caliber, shown in Figure 1a, using computational fluid dynamics (CFD). The parent vessel is modeled as a straight channel of height H and length L=5H, while the daughter vessels have height h=2H/3 and length l=8H. The angle between daughter vessels is α=30∘, forming a Y‐shaped bifurcation symmetric with respect to the parent vessel centerline. Importantly, flow split between the daughter vessels is imbalanced as one daughter vessel is simulated to be obstructed by a downstream thrombus or stenotic region. This feature will be reflected in the concentration evolution movie but is unknown from the anatomy in the region of interest (blue region in Figure 1a). The spatial average of flow within the vessel at the inlet is shown in Figure 1b. The occlusion in the lower daughter vessel is modeled with a semicircle of radius r=0.15H, resulting in a maximum occlusion of 45% of the diameter (d).

Black lines mark the inlet and outlet cross‐sections used for velocity and concentration measurements. (b) Velocity profile at the inlet, with the black curve representing the ground truth and the green curve showing the PINN prediction. The yellow‐shaded area between the two curves indicates the root mean square error (RMSE). At the edges of the computational domain, we prescribed a Womersley inflow profile for the parent vessel at x=0, utilize convective outflow conditions at x=25H, and free‐slip at the top and bottom edges (y=−12.5H, and y=12.5H). The inflow boundary condition is prescribed using the pulsatile plane‐channel Womersley solution, obtained by solving flow between two parallel flat walls separated by a distance H, as described in Guerrero‐Hurtado et al. 35 The characteristic velocity u was set to 0.3 m/s. Addition details regarding the CFD boundary conditions are described in Appendix A.1.

Prescription of contrast agent concentration

Spatiotemporal concentration gradients are necessary for PINN‐based flow estimation as they drive the error metrics. The goal of this work was to evaluate how CT imaging parameters impact PINN‐based flow estimation. Therefore, we simulated imaging with contrast agent injections that created concentration gradients throughout the vessel in both space and time. Our starting point to simulate contrast agent concentration dynamics was the convection‐diffusion equation for a passive scalar carried by the flow, ∂cCFD∂t∼+∇·u∼cCFD=1Pe∇2cCFD, where Pe=ucH/DcCFD is the Péclet number that measures the relative importance of convection over diffusion, and DcCFD is the mass diffusion coefficient. For a mass diffusion coefficient of 3×10−10m2/s and characteristic velocity uc and vessel height H of the idealized bifurcation, the Péclet number is ∼107, indicating that diffusion is negligible. Therefore, the evolution of Computational fluid dynamics (CFD)‐derived concentration fields, cCFD is governed by pure advection, so we modeled it as ∂cCFD∂t∼+∇·u∼cCFD=0.

To solve this first‐order partial differential equation, it is sufficient to prescribe inlet boundary conditions at the parent vessel, cCFD(x=0,y,t). In our model, we impose a spatially uniform profile, cCFD(x=0,y,t)=C0(t), as illustrated in Figure 1c. The CFD simulation was computed using a Δt=0.015ms which leads to >5500 timesteps per cardiac cycle. Additional details regarding the numerical scheme used to solve for the concentration are provided in Appendix A.2.

Of note, the solution domain for cCFD (blue region in Figure 1a) was deliberately restricted to exclude the daughter vessel narrowing, in order to mimic a scenario where the anatomical cause of the flow imbalance is absent from the CT images.

CT scan simulation

CFD‐derived concentration fields, cCFD(x,y,t), were used as the basis to simulate 2D fanbeam CT scanning. We aimed to simulate imaging of the central slice of a third‐generation, clinical, cone beam CT system. Therefore, imaging was simulated for a 2D 50cm field‐of‐view with a fanbeam geometry based on the geometry of the Revolution CT scanner (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL). Specifically, SDD was set to 1097.6mm with a SAD = 625.6 mm (fan angle of 43.6 degrees) and 984 views were acquired per gantry rotation. 36

To generate sinograms, 1600 px × 1600 px concentration frames cCFD (n = 100) spanning two cardiac cycles, with HR = 70 bpm, were selected and intensity normalized to span 0 to 1. Within these images, the vessel was located centrally and had a diameter of 1.5cm, approximating the size of the human inferior vena cava (IVC).

Then, fan‐beam imaging was simulated using 1600 detector elements. A detector's value was obtained as the average of 5 equally spaced, neighboring line integrals, to simulate area integration. Each line integral sampled 4800 points between the source and detector (0.22 mm step size). This resulted in 2186 points sampled inside the 50 cm FOV. Gantry rotation speed (GRS) was varied to evaluate the impact of temporal inconsistency artifacts. Specifically, we tested gantry rotation speeds (GRS) at 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, and 10 Hz. These correspond to rotation times ranging from 1 s to 100 ms. This reflects both current limit of scanner rotation speeds (≈4 Hz or 250 ms) as well as the potential improvement, if faster rotations were enabled.

For each GRS, the sinogram was generated by forward projecting cCFD images. Specifically, the time of each projection was used to select the nearest cCFD image. Then forward projection was performed via integration using bilinear interpolation. For a GRS of 1 Hz, ≈5 degrees of the sinogram comes from each cCFD image. For 10 Hz scanning, ≈54 degrees of the sinogram comes from each cCFD image.

Lastly, we simulated scans with different gantry starting angles (0, 30, 60, 90, 120, and 150 degrees) since different gantry positions lead to different temporal inconsistency artifacts and the starting angle is unknown and arbitrary at the time of scanning. 37 Different starting angles were analyzed as repetitions (leading to boxplots on graphs and median and interquartile range values in tables).

Addition of noise to sinogram data

Imaging noise was modeled by adding Poisson‐distributed fluctuations to the obtained sinogram values. The x‐ray attenuation through a non‐homogeneous material was computed using the Beer–Lambert law, (1) I=I0exp−∫0Lμ(x)dx where I is the intensity of the x‐ray beam after passing through the material, I0 is the incident x‐ray intensity, μ(x) is the linear attenuation coefficient at position x along the beam path, x represents the position variable, and L is the total length the x‐ray travels through the object. Noisy measurements were then obtained as (2) In∼Poisson(I) The initial x‐ray intensity, I0, was varied from 105 to 1015 to generate images with varying contrast‐to‐noise ratios, thereby mimicking the effect of variable CT imaging dose. The peak intensity of the contrast agent was held constant across all simulation studies. Consequently, variations in the contrast‐to‐noise ratio (CNR) were produced solely by adjusting the incident x‐ray intensity (I0).

Filtered backprojection reconstruction

Images were reconstructed using a standard filtered backprojection (FBP) algorithm with a RAM‐LAK filter. 38 This reconstruction yielded imaged concentration fields cCT(x,y,t), represented as time‐resolved movies, which approximate but do not exactly equal the true concentration. The movies had different amounts of temporal inconsistency artifacts due to the range of gantry rotation speeds and noise‐driven errors due to different initial x‐ray intensity value, I0, which leads to variation in measurement accuracy (via the Poisson noise process) and results in variations in contrast‐to‐noise (CNR).

Physics‐informed neural network architecture

In all cases, a Multi Layer Perceptron (MLP) network with 10 hidden layers and 200 neurons per layer and Sigmoid Linear Unit (SiLU) activation function was used to create an implicit representation of the concentration field evolution as a function of time. 39 This model was implemented in Python (version 3.8.5) using the PyTorch (version 2.3.0) library with CUDA 12.2 and trained on NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPUs. Neuron coefficients were determined using a Adam optimizer over 3×105 iterations with a learning rate of 10−3. These and other hyperparameters, such as the loss weight coefficients and the number of sampling points, were selected heuristically and tuned experimentally to balance the contributions of sinogram data and flow information in model supervision.

Due to GPU constraints, both loss function terms were evaluated by randomly subsampling the spatiotemporal domain of CFD simulation each epoch. In ImageFlow, 10,000 spatiotemporal locations were evaluated while SinoFlow evaluated 256 projections (each generated using 2186 points inside the field‐of‐view).

This framework is visualized in Figure 2, with further details provided below for ImageFlow and SinoFlow, respectively.

PINN‐based Flow Estimation Approaches In both ImageFlow and SinoFlow, the same image domain PINN is used to predict concentration (c), flow field (u,v) along x and y, respectively, and pressure (p) movies. However, SinoFlow includes the forward rendering process in the pipeline to generate a predicted sinogram gpred and therefore calculates a sinogram loss as the data loss, while ImageFlow compares generated concentrations cpred to FBP‐reconstructed concentration values.

ImageFlow

The input to ImageFlow is (t,x,y), where t represents the non‐dimensional time elapsed in the contrast evolution movie, while x and y are the non‐dimensional spatial coordinates. The network outputs a quadruplet of values (c^,u^,v^,p^) representing the estimated concentration c^, x− and y−velocity components u^,v^, and pressure p^ at the specified spatiotemporal position. The PINN weights are computed by minimizing a loss function that aggregates data loss and residuals from physical constraints, (3) L=Lphysics+Ldata,

In ImageFlow, the physical loss is the L2 norm of the residuals of partial differential equations governing contrast transport, mass conservation, and fluid momentum balance (i.e., the Navier‐Stokes equations) 21 whereas the data loss is the L2‐norm between estimated and imaged contrast agent concentration, (4) Ldata=λ0Lconc=λ01n∑nc^(x,y,t)−cCT(x,y,t)2, and λ0 is a hyperparameter controlling the relative weight of data misfit and physical constraints. In ImageFlow, λ0 was set to 1. More details are provided in Appendix B.

SinoFlow

In SinoFlow, we modified the PINN architecture to learn the (x,y,t)⟶(c^,u^,v^,p^) representation from the CT sinogram, that is, the original measurement domain. This avoids the propagation of temporal inconsistency‐driven artifacts generated by filtered back‐projection. We hypothesized that this would mitigate image errors (in this case, due to the motion of the contrast) introduced during the image reconstruction processes. To do so, the forward rendering operation, that is, the Radon transform, was added to the inference framework as a hard constraint based on the time‐evolving PINN‐inferred concentration field, (5) g^(s,θ)=∫L(θ)c^[x(s,θ),y(s,θ),t]dl where g^(s,θ) and c^ is the inferred sinogram and inferred concentration field, respectively. [x(s,θ),y(s,θ)] represents the projection ray for a certain position s and gantry angle θ=θ0+ωt, with ω being the gantry rotation speed. To be compatible with forward rendering, a set of collinear points corresponding to randomly sampled locations on the sinogram were sampled during training (instead of random spatiotemporal locations as done by ImageFlow).

This architecture allows for the data loss to be redefined as the difference between the inferred sinogram g^(s,θ) and the measured sinogram g(s,θ), (6) Ldata=λ1Lsino=λ1n∑ng^(s,θ)−g(s,θ)2.

To balance data and physics loss magnitudes, λ1 was set to 1/np, where np is the number of spatial points used to calculate g(s,θ). In our experiments np was equal to 1600.

Description of computational experiments

Impact of gantry rotation speed on image and sinogram‐based flow estimation

First, the impact of increasing temporal inconsistency (due to slower gantry rotation speed) was measured by comparison of FBP images to ground truth images via root mean‐square‐error. Then, we compared the performance of ImageFlow to SinoFlow for varying rotation speeds (§ 2.3). The quality of the concentration fields c^(x,y,t) estimated by ImageFlow and SinoFlow was compared to ground truth images, cCFD(x,y,t). ImageFlow‐ and SinoFlow‐based flow estimates were evaluated by measuring the accuracy of inferred velocity components [u^(x,y,t),v^(x,y,t)] at the inlet and outlets and the ratio of outlet flows and comparison to ground truth CFD‐generated data [u(x,y,t),v(x,y,t)].

Robustness of ImageFlow and SinoFlow to CT sinogram noise

The GRS experiments focused on the effect of temporal inconsistency artifacts, assuming noise‐free data. In those experiments, increasing GRS reduces temporal inconsistencies. However, if we maintain dose per projection constant, then the increased number of projections (obtained during the same total scan time) means the study has a higher imaging dose. Conversely, if the total imaging dose was held constant, then a higher GRS lowers dose per projection which increases noise. To evaluate the dose–noise trade‐offs, we performed experiments where we modeled the imaging noise by adding Poisson‐distributed fluctuations to the sinogram data (see Section 2.3.1). The resulting reduction in x‐ray fluence (lower CNR) was assessed in terms of its effect on both the reconstructed contrast concentration and velocity fields, as in the previous section. Likewise, the performance of both ImageFlow and SinoFlow was compared. To isolate the effect of noise independently from motion blur, all scans were performed at a fixed gantry rotation speed of 4 Hz.

CNR was computed from a single FBP image at the inlet when the first bolus arrives as the ratio of the intensity inside the vessel at inlet relative to the standard deviation measured in the air immediately outside the vessel. CNR experiments all had the same GRS so temporal inconsistency artifacts are held constant and the source of the noise in the FBP images is the Poisson noise process applied to the sinogram values.

Pulsed mode imaging

Finally, we evaluated the impact of angular subsampling during CT scanning (pulse mode PM imaging) on SinoFlow. In PM imaging, x‐ray acquisition occurs in discrete intervals interleaved with periods without data sampling. To simulate this acquisition mode, the PINN was trained with missing data and the data loss Ldata was limited to portions of the sinogram during which the pulses were obtained. Experiments were performed varying two PM parameters: the PM duty‐cycle (10 to 100 percent) and the pulse width (10 and 50 views) where the pulse width is the number of consecutive views that are obtained between non‐imaging intervals. The 10 view pulse duration was selected such that even a high duty cycle (such as 75%, which corresponds to ∼3 “off” views) is feasible given previously reported pulse rates. 40

Analysis of flow fields and CT parameters

To support the definition of flow fields and CT gantry rotation speeds we prescribed, we can consider a simplified one‐dimensional advection model. Over one gantry rotation, Δt=2π/ωgantry, a first‐order Taylor expansion with the advection relation (∂tc=−u∂xc) gives the concentration mismatch Δc∼2πuωgantryLc, where Lc denotes the bolus‐front thickness along the vessel centerline. Temporal inconsistencies are negligible when this term is small, which yields ωgantry≫2πu/Lc. Using the flow Strouhal number Stflow=ωflowH/u, we obtain ωgantryωflow≫2πStflowHLc. In our Y‐shaped anatomy, Stflow≈0.37, ωflow≈7.3s−1, and Lc/H≈5, implying a threshold of fgantry∼3.8 Hz. This is close to the 4 Hz imaging scenario (which is a common GRS with current scanners). More broadly, the Strouhal‐based framework may offer a general basis for predicting threshold behavior across different flow conditions and geometries.

Statistical analysis

Accuracy of the reconstructed concentration images and the predicted concentration fields from ImageFlow and SinoFlow was assessed using root mean‐square error (RMSE) against the ground truth CFD‐generated concentration fields. Flow accuracy at the inlet and outlets was quantified using RMSE together with peak velocity, minimum velocity, and velocity range (peak–minimum) at the inlet and outlet locations. In addition, the ability to detect the small outlet flow imbalance was evaluated using the ratio of outlet flows (Outlet Ratio) and compared to the CFD‐generated ratio.

A two‐way repeated‐measures analysis of variance (RM‐ANOVA) was conducted to assess the effects of GRS, I0, neural network framework (ImageFlow and SinoFlow), and Duty Cycle or PM views on metrics of interest. Given each condition had n = 6 observations (corresponding to different gantry starting angles), we did not test for normality. However, ANOVA's have been shown to be robust against small variations in normality. 41 For significant main effect or interaction, post‐hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using paired t‐tests, with Bonferroni correction applied to adjust for multiple comparisons. Additionally, two‐sample t‐tests were performed to compare reconstruction results against the ground truth (when available) and between the two neural network frameworks. Statistically significant differences (p<0.05) are indicated by an asterisk (*) in the corresponding figures.

RESULTS

Impact of GRS on flow estimation

Figure 3a shows the FBP‐reconstructed concentration field, cFBP, together with the PINN‐predicted concentrations from ImageFlow (c^ImageFlow) and SinoFlow (c^SinoFlow) at different GRS. For each case, the absolute concentration error, relative to the ground‐truth concentration from CFD simulations cCFD, is visualized. The images demonstrate that temporal inconsistency leads to errors in reconstructed CT images which increase as GRS decreases, and that the spatial distribution of errors also depends on GRS. At the lowest GRS values, where overall errors are larger, the discrepancies appear concentrated near the vessel walls and in the bifurcation region. ImageFlow and FBP yield similar concentration fields, whereas SinoFlow consistently produces lower errors across all GRS. These findings were confirmed in quantitative assessment, shown in Figure 3b. Concentration RMSE increased from ≈0.2 at 8–10 Hz to ≈0.375 at 1Hz for both FBP and ImageFlow. At 1,2, and 4 Hz, there was no statistical difference in RMSE between FBP and ImageFlow. This suggests the errors in FBP translate into errors in concentration fields predicted by ImageFlow (shown in blue). This is somewhat expected as ImageFlow PINN was trained on the FBP‐reconstructed concentration fields.

Errors in FBP images also led to errors in velocity estimates when solving with ImageFlow. Inlet results are shown in Figure 3b while Figure S1 demonstrates errors in outlet estimates. Inlet velocity RMSE, velocity range, high velocity error, and low velocity error increased sharply as GRS decreased beyond 4 Hz. The outlet ratio (used for detection of the small flow imbalance between the daughter vessels) could be estimate when imaging errors were small. The CFD‐derived Outlet Ratio is 1.086. At GRS ≥ 4 Hz, ImageFlow predicts a small imbalance (1.036) which is close to the correct value. However, Figure 3b shows how estimates become highly variable and inaccurate for GRS ≤ 4 Hz. Combined, these results suggests that the flow physics terms are unable to overcome errors in imaging.

SinoFlow predictions were more accurate and robust with respect to gantry rotation speed (Figure 3a,b). The concentration fields inferred by SinoFlow had RMSE of ≈0.133. This was significantly lower than the RMSEs obtained with FBP or ImageFlow, even when comparing SinoFlow at GRS=1 Hz to ImageFlow at the highest rotation speed, GRS=10 Hz. Further, the difference in RMSE between GRS=10 Hz (0.189) an GRS=1 Hz (0.375) was small. SinoFlow also produced stable and accurate flow curves. Specifically, velocity RMSE, velocity range error, high velocity error, and low velocity error all remained near 0.01–0.03 m/s regardless of GRS. Moreover, Outlet Ratio predicted by SinoFlow (1.092) closely matched the ground‐truth value (1.086) and exhibited markedly less variability than those obtained with ImageFlow.

Impact of imaging noise on flow estimation

Figure 4a shows how increasing sinogram noise leads to artifacts in the concentration fields reconstructed by FBP, ImageFlow, and SinoFlow. At high CNR, artifacts were largely confined to near‐wall regions and, in the case of FBP and ImageFlow, also appeared in the inlet region. As CNR decreased, these near‐wall and inlet errors intensified, and additional artifacts emerged within the parent vessel lumen. The relationship between image CNR and concentration field RMSE is shown in Figure 4b (top left panel) and Table 2. At low CNR (CNR = 12), all three methods yielded similar errors (RMSE≈ 0.29). With increasing CNR, SinoFlow exhibited a more pronounced improvement, achieving an RMSE of 0.142 at CNR = 75, significantly lower than imageFlow (0.237) and FBP reconstructed images (0.253).

SinoFlow also resulted in better velocity error metrics, both at the inlet (Figure 4b) and outlet (Figure S2). Overall, ImageFlow underperformed relative to SinoFlow. Velocity RMSE, velocity range, high velocity error, and outlet ratio error were all higher for ImageFlow than SinoFlow, and outlet ratio predictions were generally less accurate. However, low‐velocity error was consistently better for ImageFlow across all tested CNR levels.

Accuracy of SinoFlow during PM imaging

The accuracy of SinoFlow under pulsed mode imaging was studied by characterizing the PINN's inference accuracy as a function of two PM parameters: duty cycle and pulse width. As in previous sections, error metrics were assessed at the inlet (Figure 5) and outlet of the flow model (Figure S3). As duty cycle decreased, all four error metrics increased. However, inferences using a 10‐view pulse width consistently outperformed 50‐view pulse width predictions, with particularly pronounced differences at the lowest duty cycles tested. This suggests denser temporal sampling (with more frequent, but smaller gaps in sampling) improves PINN estimation accuracy over longer scan periods with larger gaps.

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates that CT acquisition parameters, particularly gantry rotation speed and photon flux, strongly influence the accuracy of PINN‐based flow estimation from CT contrast dynamics. We compared two different PINN frameworks ‐ ImageFlow, which learns from FBP‐reconstructed contrast movies, and SinoFlow, which integrates sinogram data directly ‐ and demonstrated how the sinogram‐based approach improved flow estimation presumably due to the fact that it avoids the effects of temporal inconsistency artifacts present in FBP images.

Prior work has shown that PINNs can infer flow from the dynamics of a passive tracer under idealized assumptions. 21 , 25 However, these studies sidestepped the impact of imaging artifacts and noise that inevitably arise in practice. Addressing this limitation is essential for clinical translation. Our results provide the first systematic evidence that PINN inference from CT contrast dynamics benefits substantially from moving beyond image‐domain representations. By enforcing consistency between acquisition physics and tracer transport directly in the sinogram domain, SinoFlow achieves robust performance even under challenging conditions, such as slower gantry rotation speeds or reduced‐dose protocols, whereas ImageFlow‐based flow estimation accuracy deteriorates.

We evaluated SinoFlow and ImageFlow using a CFD model of flow through a bifurcating vessel with partial downstream obstruction, used here as an exemplar case. Gantry rotation speeds below ≈4 Hz produced pronounced temporal inconsistency artifacts in reconstructed CT images, leading to higher mean squared error and velocity inaccuracies that propagated into ImageFlow estimates. This observation is consistent with the threshold predicted under the assumption that contrast remains nearly steady during advection over a single gantry revolution (Section 2.5.4). This finding suggests that the influence of temporal inconsistency artifacts on image‐based flow estimation can be reasonably approximated from the relative gantry and flow timescales, although such estimation may become more challenging in complex geometries (e.g., aneurysms or cardiac chambers).

In contrast, SinoFlow remained robust beyond the predicted 4 Hz cutoff and consistently yielded lower concentration errors and more accurate velocity fields. These results suggest that SinoFlow may better support CT‐based flow estimation in practice, since it maintained accuracy in our benchmark case where vessel bifurcation and obstruction produced rapid contrast dynamics that amplified temporal inconsistency artifacts in conventional reconstructions.

While gantry rotation speed was a major determinant of temporal inconsistency artifacts and flow estimation accuracy, other imaging parameters also affect PINN performance. To evaluate the role of imaging dose, we conducted two complementary experiments: varying sinogram noise and simulating pulsed‐mode imaging. This aimed to evaluate practical considerations for optimizing CT‐based flow measurements. We found that increasing sinogram noise degraded flow estimation accuracy when using ImageFlow, despite the denoising capacity of the PINN framework. Decreasing CNR also led to increasing errors in the reconstructed concentration field for SinoFlow. However, SinoFlow provided higher flow estimate accuracy. This suggests that an additional benefit of performing sinogram‐based training, over image‐based assessment, is increased robustness to noise in the sinogram.

We also found that SinoFlow is compatible with pulse mode imaging but that choice of PM imaging parameters impacts performance. Specifically, shorter pulse widths (10 views) maintained higher accuracy than longer pulse widths (50 views), particularly at lower duty cycles. This suggests that higher temporal sampling density achieved with shorter pulses provides better information than longer periods of “continuous sampling”. Given that both scans yield the same imaging dose, it appears the benefit stems from frequent information about contrast dynamics (or lack of “blindspots” during imaging).

These findings aim to inform future application of PINNs to CT images and provide a solution (SinoFlow) which avoids errors observed with image‐based estimation. For specific applications, the extent of temporal inconsistency artifact (ratio of flow to gantry rotation speed) and the impact of imaging noise should be carefully evaluated. Specifically, PINN‐based flow estimation does not provide a unique mapping of flow as estimates depends on the presence of concentration gradients. Therefore, specific use cases should be directly evaluated.

We note that by evaluating oscillating flows, we have developed a rigorous benchmark for the PINN since contrast transport is more complex for such flows. Specifically, traditional methods such as indicator dilution analysis have been shown to struggle with oscillating velocities. 42 Further, in optical flow, temporally oscillating concentration fields break the constancy assumption and therefore optical flow misreads temporal change as motion which biases velocity – this has been described as the “varying illumination” issue. 43

Our study had several limitations. First, we evaluated a single, idealized 2D bifurcating geometry aligned with the axial imaging plane. The Y‐shaped model was chosen deliberately for its simplicity, while still embedding hidden information in the imaging data. Specifically, a flow imbalance was introduced by inserting an obstruction located outside the field of view. This would not be apparent to CFD‐based approaches or analysis based on the geometry alone. Therefore, this design provided a tractable platform for analysis while ensuring that accurate inference required sensitivity to contrast dynamics rather than purely geometric cues.

Flow estimation accuracy in a broader range of imaging scenarios needs to be evaluated. Specifically, estimation of flow along the z‐direction will be subject to different temporal inconsistency artifacts than our 2D inplane evaluation. Further, more complex geometries such as varying vessel diameters, multiple vessels and branches or analysis of flow inside cardiac chambers should be evaluated. The presence of other tissues in the imaging field‐of‐view will also change the noise as certain projections may have higher noise than others. The impact of this on flow estimation accuracy should be evaluated using more realistic imaging scenarios. Further, while this study utilized a periodic concentration profile to ensure robust spatiotemporal gradients, the underlying PINN framework only requires the presence of gradients and is not dependent on a specific frequency. In clinical practice, venous injections remain relatively unmixed as they enter the pulmonary circulation, making pulmonary vascular flow a primary candidate for this technique. However, mixing within the cardiac chambers will result in more uniform systemic bolus profiles when the contrast enters the left heart. This could limit the ability to estimate complex flow features like vorticity but we expect the framework will remain capable of estimating forward flow. To address this, future applications could utilize optimized injection protocols or direct intra‐arterial injections to enhance contrast dynamics for specific vascular territories.

Modalities such as digital subtraction angiography (DSA) and x‐ray fluoroscopy allow for intra‐arterial contrast administration. This provides control over the contrast dynamics in the imaging field of view and would enable sharp spatiotemporal gradients required for high‐accuracy flow derivation. However, the lack of fast gantry rotation means that the PINN must solve for 3D flow fields from limited projections.

Second, the vessel was stationary during imaging. In certain applications, the vessel of interest may move or deform significantly during imaging. If the motion is minor (and PINN‐based flow estimation succeeds), the reported flows will include both fluid flow as well as bulk motion. This would require postprocessing to separate the two measures and provide fluid flows in the vessels frame of reference. More complex, time‐varying translations or rigid‐body motions can introduce inertial accelerations. Unlike methods such as optical flow, a PINN can incorporate these effects directly by augmenting the governing equations with the appropriate inertial terms. For more substantial nonrigid deformations, the computational domain itself changes over time, and the boundary motion may become tightly coupled to the resulting flow. In this setting, a PINN can be extended to a fluid–structure interaction formulation by adding vessel mechanics and enforcing continuity of deformation and stress at the fluid–wall interface, enabling accurate flow estimation given sufficient spatiotemporal data. These extensions are planned as future studies as the specific vessel motion (amount and trajectory) depends on the clinical application (e.g., coronaries, aorta) and imaging paradigm (e.g., number of beats imaged).

Third, 2D fan‐beam imaging was simulated. This simplification enabled the evaluation of key CT parameters while avoiding significant increases in computational complexity. Specifically, while the PINN architecture is able to solve for 3D concentrations and flows, 21 doing so would necessitate higher computational demands, making it difficult to perform the large number of independent inferences presented here to evaluate PINN performance vs. parameters like GRS, sinogram noise, pulse mode duty ratio, and pulse width. However, 2D simulations provided limited insight into potential “cross‐talk” between overlapping vessels. In a clinical 3D volume, vessels will overlap in projection data, creating complex intensity variations that were not captured in this 2D model but could impact flow estimation accuracy. Given the promising results here, extension to 3D imaging is planned as future work.

Fourth, we intentionally designed the contrast injection to have high spatiotemporal variations to enable successful PINN‐based flow estimation. Because PINNs infer velocity from the evolving tracer field, frames or locations in which contrast is absent or uniformly distributed yield little to no usable flow information. To mitigate this, we imposed an oscillatory injection profile that generated graded concentration gradients across the vessel. In practice, however, achieving such patterning may be challenging. The degree of oscillation that can be introduced is limited by clinical constraints, and as the bolus travels from the injection site to the imaging region it will undergo mixing and dispersion. A systematic investigation of the sensitivity of PINN‐based flow estimation to injection protocols will be an important direction for future work. One possible improvement in this area could come from dual‐energy or photon‐counting CT systems. In both methods, iodine images/data can be generated. This data would be better for PINN‐estimation than the conventional sinogram or images which mix contrast agent concentration data with “background” intensity values. By supplying higher fidelity contrast data, dual‐energy and photon‐counting CT may better guide PINN‐based blood flow predictions, potentially improving robustness and accuracy of flow field estimation in clinical settings. 44

CONCLUSION

In this study, we evaluate image‐ and sinogram‐based flow estimation using physics‐informed neural networks. By evaluating the impact of temporal inconsistency artifacts (via varying gantry rotation speed), sinogram noise (via changes to photon flux), and sinogram subsampling (via pulse mode imaging), we evaluated the ability of PINNs to provide accurate flow estimates in settings where the images of the concentration are not ideal. Our findings suggest that a sinogram‐based approach (SinoFlow) provides more robust performance and should be used when evaluating the use of PINNs to estimate flow from CT data.
