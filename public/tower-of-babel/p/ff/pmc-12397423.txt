Evaluation of peri-implant health and whole salivary cortisol levels in adults with mental health disorders
Authors: Munerah Saleh BinShabaib, Abdulrahman A. Al-Wahibi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The null hypothesis was that there is no difference in whole salivary cortisol levels (CL) and peri-implant health status among adults with and without a diagnosis of mental health disorders (MHD). The aim was to assess whole salivary CL and peri-implant health status among adults with and without MHD. The inclusion criteria were: (a) adults with at least one dental implant in function for at least one year; (b) adults with and without a diagnosis of MHD. Patients were divided into two-groups: (a) patients with depression (test-group); and (b) patients without MHD (control-group). Information on duration and treatment of depression and implant-related parameters were recorded. Periodontal parameters (plaque and gingival indices [PI and GI], probing depth (PD), clinical attachment loss [CAL] and marginal bone loss [MBL]) were measured. Peri-implant modified plaque and gingival indices (mPI and mGI), PD and crestal bone loss (CBL) were also recorded. Whole salivary cortisol levels were assessed in unstimulated whole saliva. Group comparisons were done and level of significance was set at P < 5%. Sixty-five individuals (33 and 32 in the test and control-groups, respectively) were included. Peri-implant mPI (P < 0.01), mGI (P < 0.01), PD (P < 0.01) and mesial (P < 0.01) and distal (P < 0.01) CBL were higher in the test than control group. Scores of full -mouth PI (P < 0.01), GI (P < 0.01), PD (P < 0.01), CAL (P < 0.01) and mesial (P < 0.01) and distal (P < 0.01) MBL were higher in the test than the control group. Unstimulated whole salivary flow rate was higher in the control than in the test group (P < 0.01). Whole salivary CL were higher in the test than in the control group (P < 0.01). There was a correlation between periodontal and peri-implant PD and whole salivary CL (P < 0.05) in the test group. Patients with MHD are more prone to peri-implant diseases compared to healthy individuals, with inadequate oral hygiene maintenance being the main contributing factor in the former group. Patients with MHD are more prone to periodontal and peri-implant diseases; and poor routine oral hygiene maintenance plays a critical role in this regard.

Introduction

Dental implants are often used to rehabilitate partially and/or completely edentulous individuals due to their high success and survival rates (over 90%)1–3. Nevertheless, the occurrence of peri-implant diseases (peri-implant mucositis and peri-implantitis) remains a persistent challenge for clinicians and researchers as a delayed diagnosis and management of such diseases may even result in implant failure. Poor oral hygiene is a common risk factor for peri-implant mcositis and peri-implantitis4; however, it is well-known that a compromised systemic health status may also jeopardize the success and survival of dental implants5–7. One such condition that can influence peri-implant health is a psychiatric/mental health disorder, depression8. Depression is a mood disorder characterized by a lack of interest in daily activities, fatigue, cognitive impairments, and sometimes a feeling of sadness, all of which can impact an individual’s oral hygiene and health behaviors9. It is a major contributor to morbidity and disability on a global level, imposing significant costs on individuals, healthcare systems, and society10,11. According to a recent systematic review and meta-analysis, the pooled global prevalence of depression after the COVID-19 pandemic is 23%12. Moreover, the use of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRI) by patients with mental health disorders (MHD) (such as depression) has also been linked with an increased risk of implant failure in these individuals8. In a retrospective cohort study with approximately 6 years of follow-up, Wu et al.13 assessed the failure rates of 94 implants placed in SSRI users and controls (individuals not using SSRI). The results showed that among SSRI users and non-users, implant failure rates were approximately 11% and 5%, respectively13. The study concluded that the risk of implant failure is higher among users of SSRI than controls13.

Cortisol, often referred to as the “stress hormone,” is a glucocorticoid produced by the adrenal cortex in response to stress and is a crucial component of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis14. Cortisol regulates various physiological processes including metabolism, immune response, and inflammatory control14. In a recent study, Herzog et al.15 showed that a high level of cortisol in unstimulated whole saliva (UWS) is positively correlated with suicidal intent among patients with major depression. From a periodontal perspective, studies16,17 have shown that cortisol levels (CL) are elevated in the UWS of patients with than without periodontitis, which makes this biological fluid a useful and non-invasively collected medium for comprehending the pathophysiology of oral diseases in susceptible populations. It is also noteworthy that a correlation between raised CL in the peri-implant sulcular fluid and peri-implant diseases has been reported in a limited number of studies18,19; however, after a critical appraisal of pertinent indexed literature, the authors of the present study observed that there are currently no investigations that have assessed whole salivary CL in relation to peri-implant status among patients with and without MHD.

The aim was to assess the whole salivary CL and peri-implant health status among adults with and without MHD. The null hypothesis was that there is no correlation between whole salivary CL and peri-implant health status among adults with and without a diagnosis of MHD.

Materials and methods

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) adults with at least one dental implant in function for at least one year; (b) adults with and without medically diagnosed depression. Tobacco product users and individuals with other systemic diseases such as diabetes mellitus (DM), cancer and/or cardiovascular disorders were excluded. Moreover, pregnant and/or nursing females were excluded. Furthermore, from a clinical point of view supernumerary teeth, maxillary and mandibular bilateral third molars, and grossly carious dentition with embedded root remnants were not evaluated.

Study groups

Patients with functional dental implants were divided into two groups: (a) patients with depression (test group); and (b) patients without depression (control group).

Demographics

A trained investigator administered a detailed questionnaire to individuals in the test and control groups. The questionnaire was used to collect demographic and oral hygiene information from the patients. The demographic data included age, gender, and highest education level (school level, college level or university level)20. Oral hygiene practices were assessed through specific questions. Patients were asked how frequently they brush their teeth and performed flossing of interproximal spaces, with the following options provided: once daily, twice daily, irregularly/sometimes, or not at all. Moreover, participants were asked about the frequency of their dental visits (dentist and/or dental hygienist) with the following options: biannually, every 12 months, or only when there is pain/discomfort. The questionnaire included a section on general healthcare practices, where patients were asked how often they visit their medical healthcare provider. The options for this question were: every year or only in case of an emergency. Furthermore, patients were asked if they take medications provided by their healthcare providers regularly and as recommended, with answers being yes or no.

Evaluation of healthcare records

The digital medical and dental healthcare records of the participants were evaluated by a trained investigator. From the patients’ medical records, the following information was retrieved: (a) duration since diagnosis of depression; (b) family history of psychological disorders; (c) treatment for depression; and (d) date of the last visit to a psychiatrist/psychologist/medical healthcare provider. From the patients’ dental records, the following data were collected: (a) number of implants; (b) jaw location of the implants; (c) implant dimensions (diameter x length); (d) depth of implant placement (either crestal or subcrestal); (e) implant loading protocol (immediate or delayed); (f) whether bone grafting was performed; (g) type of implant prosthesis (cement-retained or screw-retained); and (h) duration the implants have been in function.

Assessment of peri-implant and periodontal clinical and radiographic status

In all patients, clinical (Kappa score 0.86) and radiographic (Kappa score 0.89) peri-implant and full-mouth periodontal examinations were performed by a trained, blinded and calibrated investigator. The peri-implant modified plaque index (mPI), modified gingival index (mGI), probing depth (PD), and crestal bone loss (CBL) were assessed in all participants using standardized protocols. In summary, the mPI21 and mGI21 were assessed on four sites per implant. The peri-implant PD was measured on six sites per implant using a graded plastic probe (Hu-Friedy, Chicago, IL, USA), with measurements taken to the nearest millimeter22. The CBL was digitally on digital bitewing radiographs that were standardized using the long-cone paralleling technique. The CBL was measured to the nearest millimeter on the mesial and distal surfaces of each implant on a calibrated computer screen23,24. The CBL was defined as the linear distance two millimeters below the implant-abutment connection to the crest of the alveolar bone25,26. Full mouth plaque27 and gingival28 indices (PI and GI, respectively) were assessed on 4 sites per tooth; and periodontal PD29 was assessed on six sites per tooth using a graded periodontal probe (UNC-15, Hu-Friedy, Chicago, IL, USA). In all teeth, clinical attachment loss (CAL)30 and marginal bone loss31 (MBL) were assessed using the long-cone paralleling technique. Staging and grading of periodontal diseases was also performed as described elsewhere32.

Collection of whole saliva and assessment of cortisol levels

The UWS samples were collected from all participants using a standardized protocol as described in previous investigations33–35. Participants were instructed to refrain from eating, drinking, or performing oral hygiene maintenance (OHM) activities for at least one hour before sample collection. Participants were seated in a relaxed, upright position and asked to allow saliva to passively accumulate in the mouth without swallowing or stimulation for five continuous minutes. The UWS was then collected by having the participants gently spit into a sterile collection tube. The collected UWS samples were immediately centrifuged at 1500 x g in a cold room. The supernatant was collected and stored in plastic tubes with lids (Fisherbrand™ Premium Microcentrifuge Tubes, MA, USA) at -80 °C for further analysis. All samples were assessed for whole salivary CL within 24 h of collection. The supernatants were analyzed in duplicates for cortisol levels (CL) using commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (RayBio®, RayBiotech Life, Inc., Atlanta, GA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The detection range for cortisol levels, as specified by the kit, was between 100–1,000,000 picograms per milliliter (pg/mL). The ELISA plates were read at 450 nanometers using a microplate reader (StatFax 2100, Awareness Tech. Inc., Palm City, FL, USA). All laboratory-based investigations were performed by a trained, blinded, and calibrated investigator (kappa score 0.84).

Power analysis

A power analysis was conducted to determine the appropriate sample size for the study using data from a pilot study. The primary outcome variable was peri-implant PD, a key indicator of peri-implant health. It was estimated that at least 31 and 31 individuals should be included in the test and control groups, respectively to detect a difference of at least 3 mm in peri-implant PD (alpha of 5% with a 95% confidence interval). With this sample size (n = 31 per group), the power of the study was established at 80% to reduce the likelihood of a type-II error. This calculation was based on the assumption of a normal distribution of the outcome variable and accounted for the variability observed in the pilot data.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was done using computer software (SPSS version 21, Chicago, IL, USA). Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 22, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics, including means and standard deviations for continuous variables and frequencies for categorical variables, were calculated to summarize the demographic characteristics and clinical findings of the study participants. Before conducting inferential analyses, the normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, given its suitability for small to medium sample sizes. For variables with a normal distribution, independent samples t-tests were used to compare mean peri-implant probing depths and cortisol levels between the depression and control groups. Linear regression analysis was done to assess the correlation between peri-implant clinical and radiographic parameters, age, gender, and whole salivary CL. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study cohort

Ninety one individuals (63 males and 28 females) were invited to participate in the present. study. Sixteen male self-reported tobacco-smokers were excluded and ten females who refused to sign the written informed consent form were excluded. These females (n = 10) did not provide any reason for non-participation. In total, 65 individuals (33 and 32 in the test and control-groups, respectively), agreed to participate in the present study and signed the informed consent form.

Patient demographics

In the test and control groups, 22 and 25 participants, respectively were males. There was no statistically significant difference in the mean ages of participants in the test and control groups. Individuals in the test group (42.4%) more often reported a family history of psychological/psychiatric disorders than the control group (3.2%). The highest level of education among most patients in the test group (81.8%) was “school-level” compared with the control group in which 71.8% of individuals reported to have graduated from a university. Most of the participants in the test group (72.7%) reported that they sometimes brushed their teeth and none of the patients in this group reported to have ever used dental floss. In the control group, 87.5% of participants reported that they brushed teeth twice daily and 31.3% of participants in this group reported that performed flossing of interproximal spaces once daily. Most of the patients in the test group reported that they visited their medical healthcare providers only in times of emergency compared with the control group where 87.5% of individuals reported that they visited their medical doctors every year for routine check-ups. Most of the patients in the test group (81.8%) stated that they were not taking their medications as prescribed. In the control group, none of the participants were using any prescription-based medications (Table 1). As per digital medical records, all patients diagnosed were diagnosed with major depressive disorder; and were prescribed SSRI (Capsule Fluoxetine 20 mg p.o. once daily).

Implant-related characteristics

According to the patients’ digital dental records, all implants were placed in healed sites, and replaced a single missing tooth. In the test and control groups, 40 and 37 implants, respectively were placed at bone level using insertion torques ranging between 30 and 35 Ncm by trained oral surgeons. Most of the implants were placed in the posterior mandible in both groups. In test and control groups, implants were in function for 5.4 ± 0.2 and 5.8 ± 0.4 years, respectively. In the test and control groups, the diameters and lengths of implants ranged between 3.3 and 4.8 × 10–14 mm and 3.3-5 × 6–14 mm, respectively. In both groups, the implants were restored with screw-retained restorations (Table 2).

Peri-implant mPI (P < 0.01), mGI (P < 0.01), PD (P < 0.01) and mesial (P < 0.01) and distal (P < 0.01) CBL were significantly higher in the test than the control group. Whole salivary flow rate and cortisol levels

The salivary flow rate was significantly higher in the control (0.22 ± 0.003 ml/min) than in the test group (0.07 ± 0.005 ml/min) (P < 0.01). The whole salivary CL were significantly higher in the test (3191.1 ± 116.7 pg/ml) compared with the control group (184.6 ± 29.4 pg/ml) (P < 0.01).

Linear regression analysis

There was a statistically significant correlation between periodontal and peri-implant PD and whole salivary CL (P < 0.05) in the test group. There was no statistically significant correlation between whole salivary CL and mPI, mGI, CBL, PI, GI, MBL, age, gender, duration of implants in function, and duration of depression in both groups (Fig. 1).

Discussion

In summary, results of the present retrospective observational study showed that patients diagnosed with MHD such as depression are more susceptible to peri-implant diseases in contrast to individuals without systemic diseases. A vigilant review of patients’ digital healthcare records demonstrated that all patients in the test group were prescribed SSRI for the management of depression. The SSRI target serotonin-receptors and obstruct differentiation of bone forming cells (osteoblasts)36. Moreover, it has also been reported that loss of alveolar bone around teeth and CAL are markedly higher among users of SSRI than non-users37. It is therefore compelling to conclude that the SSRI are mainly responsible for the compromised periodontal and peri-implant health status among patients in the test than the control group. However, based on the questionnaire administered to the participants, nearly 82% of the patients in the test group reported that they were not taking the SSRI as prescribed by their physicians. In this context, attributing the compromised periodontal and peri-implant health of patients in the test group solely to SSRI is challenging. In a recent cross-sectional, observational and power-adjusted clinical investigation, AL-Harthi et al.34 evaluated the clinical and radiographic status of periodontal and peri-implant health in users of SSRI and non-users. Additionally, this study34 measured levels of the proinflammatory cytokine interleukin 1-beta in whole saliva, finding low concentrations in both SSRI users and controls. The results showed no significant differences in inflammatory biomarkers of the periodontal and peri-implant soft tissues, nor in bone levels among users and non-users of SSRI. AL-Harthi et al.34 concluded that with strict OHM, both users and non-users of SSRI can maintain healthy periodontal and peri-implant health statuses. Notably, as shown in Table I, approximately 73% of the individuals in the test group reported did not brush teeth every day and none of these individuals (100%) reported having ever used dental floss. Moreover, individuals in the test groups also reported that they visited oral healthcare providers only when they were in pain. As shown in Table I, approximately 73% of patients in the test group reported not brushing their teeth daily, and none (100%) reported ever using dental floss. Additionally, participants in the test group stated that they only visited oral healthcare providers when experiencing pain. It is possible that irregular adherence to prescribed medications for managing depression may have exacerbated depressive symptoms in these patients, leading to neglect of daily activities, including OHM. This may also explain the results shown in Table III according to which, periodontal and peri-implant soft tissue inflammatory parameters and marginal and crestal bone levels were compromised among individuals in the test than the control group. Although implant failures were observed in the test group, the authors of the present study hypothesize that continued neglect of both medical and dental health could increase the risk of implant failure in the future among patients in this group. It is also hypothesized that if the individuals in the test group had consistently adhered to their prescribed anti-depressant treatments, they might have been more attentive to daily activities, including OHM. This could have contributed towards the stabilization of their systemic as well as oral health which could lead to a superior overall quality of life in these individuals. Further studies are needed to test these hypotheses.

The present results showed that the whole salivary flow rate (SFR) was significantly higher in the control than the test group. According to Russ and Ackerman38, depression is linked with a reduced SFR, though the exact mechanisms behind this connection remain unclear. One reason for this is that appetite-related factors, which are known to affect SFR, have not been sufficiently addressed in studies examining the relationship between salivation and depression. The lower SFR observed in depression could be more closely associated with appetite disruptions often seen in depressive conditions rather than with the mood-disorder itself. This perspective raises important considerations when interpreting SFR as a physiological marker of depression and warrants additional research. Cortisol, a hormone released in response to stress, plays a role in immune function and inflammation, both of which are important in periodontal as well as peri-implant health39,40. Elevated cortisol levels, particularly during chronic stress or in conditions such as depression, can compromise the immune system41; and have several effects related to periodontal and peri-implant inflammation and increased PD39,40. The present results showed a statistically significant correlation between whole salivary CL and periodontal and peri-implant PD. Continued stress in depressed patients may likely weaken their immunity thereby compromising their periodontal health statuses, which may clinically manifest as increased probing depths around teeth as well as dental implants. Further well-designed and power-adjusted studies are needed to test this hypothesis.

One limitation of the present study is that patients with systemic diseases other than depression were excluded. It is well-known that type-2 DM is a common manifestation in patients with depression42,43. Moreover, type-2 DM is also a risk factor of periodontal and peri-implant diseases31,44. Similarly, habitual use of combustible and non-combustible nicotinic products is known to jeopardize periodontal and peri-implant soft and osseous tissues31,45. It is hypothesized that the severity of periodontal and peri-implant diseases is higher in type-2 diabetic smokers with depression in contrast to non-smoking patients with depression alone. Furthermore, microbiological evaluations such as the assessment of whole salivary yeast carriage and the assessment of microbes in subgingival plaque were not assessed in the present investigation. It is hypothesized that the salivary and subgingival load of pathogenic microbes is higher among patients with than without depression. To test these hypothesis, further studies are warranted.

Conclusions

Patients with depression are more prone to periodontal and peri-implant diseases compared to healthy individuals, with inadequate oral hygiene maintenance being the main contributing factor in the former group.
