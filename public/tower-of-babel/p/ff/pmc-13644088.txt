ATAC sequencing and transcriptomics reveal OsCHR732 function in regulating chromatin accessibility and gene expression in pollen and ovule development in rice
Authors: Weichen Xu, Lei Wang, Xianyang Zhang, Bingxu Chen, Mei Liu, Sijia Fang, Li Yu, Sackitey Mark Kofi, Shuya Wei, Heming Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe fertility of pollen and ovules directly influences seed set and overall yield in rice, however, the molecular regulatory mechanisms governing rice pollen and ovule development remain poorly understood.ResultsIn this study, we discovered that functional knockout mutants of the rice chromatin remodeling factor OsCHR732 exhibited significantly reduced pollen viability, accompanied by abnormal ovule development. Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) and β-glucuronidase (GUS) staining revealed that OsCHR732 was highly expressed in young panicles, ovaries, and anthers during the mid-to-late developmental stages. RNA-Seq analysis of young spikelets demonstrated that several genes involved in MAPK signaling and plant hormone signal transduction pathways were significantly down-regulated in the oschr732 mutant. ATAC (Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin)-Seq analysis revealed a decrease in chromatin accessibility at the promoter regions of several genes in the mutant. Integrated ATAC-Seq and transcriptomic analysis further demonstrated that six key genes involved in mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), abscisic acid (ABA), jasmonic acid (JA) and ethylene (ETH) signal transduction were significantly downregulated, including MAP1, WRKY53, ABI5, ABIL1, JAZ11, and ETR2, which expression pattern were similar to that of OsCHR732.ConclusionsThese findings provide a critical foundation for further elucidating the molecular mechanisms by which OsCHR732 regulates pollen and ovule fertility in rice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09483-w.Keywords: Rice, Chromatin Remodeling Factor, Pollen Fertility, Ovule Development, Chromatin Accessibility, Gene expression

Introduction

Chromatin remodeling factors are pivotal regulators in maintaining chromatin architecture and play indispensable roles in various aspects of plant growth and development [1]. During gene transcription, DNA replication, and repair processes, chromatin structure is continuously modified, thereby exposing specific genomic regions and facilitating the binding of DNA-interacting enzymes to DNA [2]. Chromatin remodeling complexes utilize ATP hydrolysis to supply the energy required for the repositioning, assembly, sliding, and restructuring of nucleosomes, thereby altering chromatin architecture.

Current studies have classified these complexes into four major categories: SWI/SNF, CHD/Mi-2, ISWI, and INO80 [3]. BRM (BRAHMA) is the core catalytic subunit of the SWI/SNF complex and participates in gibberellin-mediated flowering through the formation of a DELLA-BRM-NF-YC module [4]. Additionally, LFR has been identified as a novel component of the Syd-SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex in Arabidopsis, where it modulates multiple developmental processes, including leaf, flower, and fertility development [5]. In rice, the CHD3 protein, a member of the CHD/Mi-2 family, functions as a dual-functional chromatin regulator and is likely involved in modulating gene transcription programs during plant development [6]. In Arabidopsis, two ISWI-type ATPases, CHR11 and CHR17, have been identified [7]. Loss-of-function mutants of OsINO80 exhibit dwarfism, narrow leaves, and reduced tiller number, indicating that OsINO80 plays a crucial role in the morphogenesis and growth regulation of rice vegetative organs [8]. These chromatin remodelers play crucial roles in regulating vegetative and reproductive growth, flowering time, floral organ characteristics, and fertility [9–12].

The sexual reproduction process in rice directly affects its yield. The anther and ovary are critical reproductive organs in rice [13]. The pistil produces egg cells, which develop into grains after pollination and fertilization. The number of male gametes in rice is always significantly higher than that of female gametes. While partial pollen sterility may not necessarily impact the seed-setting rate, partial sterility of the female gametophyte directly affects seed production [14]. Studies have shown that the loss-of-function mutant of the OsRab7 gene exhibits abnormal pollen fertility and a significantly reduced seed-setting rate, whereas its overexpression leads to enhanced pollen viability and improved fertility [15]. The OsTIE1 gene, a regulator of JA biosynthesis, plays a crucial role in pollen development, as its loss-of-function mutant (ostie1) exhibits pollen abortion, resulting in complete male sterility [16]. Previous studies have shown that OsMADS13 is predominantly expressed in the ovules of rice. Its loss-of-function mutant exhibits female sterility, with ovules transforming into carpel-like structures, and in some cases, an additional carpel forming within the inner carpel structure [17, 18]. The DL gene encodes a YABBY domain protein that plays a crucial role in carpel and floral meristem development [19]. In dl mutants, the pistil undergoes complete stamenization, and leaves lack a midrib, preventing them from maintaining an upright posture [20]. OsMADS13 and OsMADS3 coordinately regulate ovule and floral meristem development, while OsMADS3 and DL act together to suppress floral meristem activity. It is further speculated that OsMADS13 and DL may play similar roles in ovule and floral development, with DL potentially acting upstream of OsMADS13 as a positive regulator of its expression. Conversely, OsMADS13 may inhibit DL expression in the ovule [21].

Despite significant progress in rice fertility research, the functional characterization of fertility-related genes remains insufficient. Further exploration of key regulatory genes is essential to refine the molecular network governing rice pistil and stamen development. In this study, we utilized the CRISPR-Cas9 gene-editing mutant oschr732 as a research model, conducting molecular characterization, agronomic trait analysis, cytological observations, expression profiling, and multi-omics analyses. These efforts aim to elucidate the molecular regulatory network of OsCHR732, providing a foundation for a deeper understanding of its role in rice fertility.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

In this study, the japonica rice variety Zhonghua 11 (Oryza sativa L. ssp. Japonica cv. Zhonghua11) was used as the genetic background. Three OsCHR732 mutant lines (oschr732-1, oschr732-2, and oschr732-3) were generated using the CRISPR-Cas9 gene-editing system. All plant materials were cultivated at the Fengyang Experimental Farm of Anhui Science and Technology University under common field management conditions.

Molecular identification of transgenic plants

Leaf samples were collected from individual OsCHR732 gene-edited mutant plants, and genomic DNA was extracted using the CTAB (hexadecyl trimethyl ammonium bromide) method. Transgenic-positive plants were identified via PCR amplification using hygromycin resistance gene-specific primers located in the CRISPR-Cas9 vector (Table S1). The PCR reaction was carried out in a 20 µl system containing 1 µl genomic DNA template, 10 µl PCR Mix, 1 µl of each forward and reverse primer, and ddH₂O to a final volume of 20 µl. The PCR amplification program was as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 56 °C for 15 s, and extension at 72 °C for 12 s; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min.

Analysis of mutation types in transgenic plants

To characterize the mutation types in transgenic plants, specific primers were designed to amplify the target sites based on the genomic sequence of OsCHR732 (Table S1). Genomic DNA from transgenic plants was used as the template, and PCR was performed using the same 20 µl reaction system and cycling conditions as described above. After amplification, the PCR products were analyzed via 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and subsequently sent to Chuzhou General Biotechnology Company for sequencing. The sequencing results were analyzed using DNAMAN and SnapGene software to determine sequence variations and mutation types in transgenic plants. At the maturity stage, 20 individual plants from both wild-type and mutant lines were randomly selected for phenotypic evaluation. Agronomic traits including plant height, tiller number, panicle length, grain number per panicle, and seed-setting rate were recorded and analyzed.

Analysis of OsCHR732 gene expression pattern

To investigate the expression pattern of OsCHR732 across different rice tissues and organs, samples were collected from the following tissues: roots (R) from 7-day-old seedlings, stems (ST) from 70-day-old plants, leaves (YL) from 7-day-old seedlings, and panicles at different developmental stages: P1 (0–3 cm), P2 (3–5 cm), P3 (5–10 cm), P4 (10–15 cm), P5 (15–22 cm), and P6 (22–30 cm). All collected samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and total RNA was extracted using the Trizol method. Reverse transcription was performed using the MonScript™ RTIII All-in-One Mix with dsDNase (Monad Biotech, Wuhan, China) to synthesize cDNA, which was subsequently used as the template for quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR). Gene-specific primers for OsCHR732 (Table S1) were used for qRT-PCR analysis.

The qRT-PCR reaction system was prepared in a total volume of 20 µL, consisting of 10 µL ProQ™ qPCR EvaGreen Master Mix (Biomed), 2 µL cDNA, 0.5 µL forward primer, 0.5 µL reverse primer, and ddH₂O added to a final volume of 20 µL. The qRT-PCR program was set as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 60 °C for 15 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s. The Actin gene was used as an internal reference, and the relative expression levels of the target gene were evaluated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. Statistical analysis was performed in SPSS 26.0, with data presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) (P < 0.05). Data visualization was conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0.

GUS staining

A 2470 bp promoter sequence upstream of the OsCHR732 gene’s start codon was used to construct a GUS fusion vector. The recombinant construct was introduced into Zhonghua 11 (ZH11) via Agrobacterium-mediated transformation, performed by Wuhan Biorun Biotechnology Co. Ltd., to obtain transgenic positive lines. For GUS staining, tissues from T₂ generation positive plants, including germinating seeds, young leaves, and spikelets, were collected. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, samples were incubated in GUS staining solution from a GUS staining kit (Wuhan Biorun Biotechnology Co. Ltd.) at 37 °C in the dark for 24 h. Chlorophyll was removed using 70% ethanol, and stained tissues were observed and photographed under a microscope.

Fertility analysis in oschr732

To assess male fertility, matural spikelets of oschr732 mutants were collected, and anthers were dissected under a microscope. Pollen grains were stained with 1% iodine-potassium iodide (I₂-KI) solution, and their staining patterns were observed and photographed under a microscope.

For female fertility analysis, whole-mount stain clearing laser scanning confocal microscopy (WCLSM) was used to examine ovule development. Spikelets at different developmental stages, including the archesporial cell (AC), functional megaspore (FM), and mature ovule (MO), were collected before and after heading. Samples were fixed in FAA solution (50% ethanol: glacial acetic acid: formaldehyde = 89:6:5) at room temperature for 48 h, then washed with 50% ethanol and transferred to 70% ethanol. Ovaries were dissected under a stereomicroscope and rehydrated with a gradient ethanol series (50%, 30%) followed by distilled water. Samples were mordanted with 2% aluminum potassium sulfate for 20 min, stained with 10 mg/L eosin B (dissolved in 4% sucrose solution) for 10 h, and destained in 2% aluminum potassium sulfate for 20 min. After three washes with distilled water, samples were dehydrated through an ethanol gradient (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, absolute ethanol) and then transferred to a transition solution of absolute ethanol: methyl salicylate (1:1) for 2 h. Finally, samples were cleared in pure methyl salicylate for 5 h.

Cleared ovules were mounted on concave slides with methyl salicylate, and fluorescence signals of ovules were visualized using a Leica SP2 laser scanning confocal microscope with a 543 nm excitation wavelength for optical sectioning and imaging.

RNA-Seq and ATAC-Seq analysis

For the omics analysis, early spikelets (1.5–2 mm in length) from both ZH11 and oschr732-1 mutant were collected from young panicles (4–6 cm in length) and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen for storage. RNA-Seq and ATAC-Seq library construction and sequencing were entrusted to Wuhan Biorun Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Three biological replicates were used for each assay.

Gene expression values in RNA-Seq were quantified as FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads). The criteria for selecting differentially expressed genes were |log2(Fold Change)| ≥ 1 and padj ≤ 0.05. The analysis results were then subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis. For ATAC-Seq, the same samples used for RNA-Seq were employed. For cell nucleus preparation, approximately 100 mg of fresh spikelets were placed in a mortar and ground into powder with liquid nitrogen. The powder were resuspended in nuclear isolation buffer (NIB), and the mixture was filtered through a 40 μm cell filter to a new tube, followed by centrifugation at 1000 g for 5 min (4 ℃). Then, we discarded the supernatant, and treated cells with precooled NIB and 0.1% Triton x-100 for several minutes, and lysed the cells, followed by centrifugation at 1000 g for 5 min (4 ℃). Then we washed the cell nucleus twice with 1 mL of NIB, discarded the supernatant, and preserved the cell nucleus precipitate. These cell nuclei were digested with Tn5 transposase, and the recovered DNA fragments were ligated with sequencing adapters, followed by the selection of 300–500 bp fragments. PCR amplification was performed following 72℃ for 3 min, 98℃ for 30 s, then 15 cycles of 98℃ for 15 s, 60℃ for 15 s, 72℃ for 8 s and finally extend at 72℃ for 2 min. All peak calling was performed with MACS2 (v2.2.7.1), significant peaks were identified with parameters “-g 2e8 -nomodel - q < 0.05”. During the differential accessible regions (DARs) detection process, the screening criteria were |log2 (Fold Change)| ≥1 and p value < 0.05.

For the integrated omics analysis, pathways enriched in both RNA-Seq and ATAC-Seq datasets were selected. Gene heatmaps were generated using software such as TBtools, and downstream genes potentially regulated by the OsCHR732 gene were identified.

Results

Molecular identification of gene editing target site in OsCHR732

To develop functional knockout mutant of the rice chromatin remodeling factor OsCHR732, the CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing vector targeting OsCHR732 had been constructed by our research team. The vector was then introduced into ZH11 using agrobacterium-mediated genetic transformation, which was carried out by Wuhan Biorun Biotechnology Co. Ltd., resulting in T₀ transgenic plants. Genomic DNA was extracted from T0 transgenic plants, and molecular detection was conducted using hygromycin-specific primers and target-specific primers ( Table S1).

The T0 plants were self-crossed to produce T1 generation. DNA from individual T1 plants was amplified via PCR using target-specific primers for OsCHR732. Sequencing analysis of the PCR products revealed three different types of homozygous mutations: the first one with an insertion of a T base at position 1523 bp of OsCHR732, the second one with an insertion of an A base at position 1522 bp, and the third one with a 3-base deletion from positions 1547 bp to 1549 bp. These mutations were named oschr732-1, oschr732-2, and oschr732-3, respectively (Fig. 1).

Black letters indicate the normal nucleotide sequence; Red letters represent inserted bases; “-” denotes deleted bases. Blue letters correspond to the translated protein sequence, and “*” indicates a stop codon. nt, nucleotide

Protein sequence analysis indicated that these three mutant lines led to premature translation termination or amino acid deletions, thus disrupting the normal protein synthesis of OsCHR732 and effectively knocking out the gene function (Fig. S1).

OsCHR732 played an important role in growth and development in rice

To explore the function of OsCHR732 on the growth and development in rice, at the maturity stage, agronomic traits of the wild-type ZH11 and the three mutant lines (oschr732-1, oschr732-2, and oschr732-3) were observed and measured, including plant height, tiller number, panicle length, and grain number per panicle. The results showed that, compared to the wild type, all three mutant lines exhibited a significant reduction in plant height (Fig. 2A and D) and a noticeable decrease in tiller number (Fig. 2A and E). Additionally, the panicle length and primary branch length were significantly shortened in the mutant lines (Fig. 2B, C, F and H), while the number of primary branches and the total grain number per panicle showed no significant difference compared to the wild type (Fig. 2C, G and I).

OsCHR732 positively regulated pistil and stamen fertility in rice

To identify the changes in fertility in oschr732 mutants, we performed a preliminary observation and statistical analysis of the seed-setting rate in ZH11 and oschr732. The results revealed a significant reduction in the seed-setting rate in the mutants compared to the wild type (Fig. 3A). The average seed-setting rate of ZH11 reached 95%, while the oschr732 mutant lines showed a drastic reduction to approximately 25% (Fig. 3B).

To further investigate the cause of the reduced seed-setting rate, pollen was stained with iodine-potassium iodide, and ovules were analyzed using whole-mount staining and clearing techniques, followed by laser scanning confocal microscopy. Iodine-potassium iodide staining indicated that nearly 100% of the pollen grains in ZH11 were normally developed, whereas only about 50% of the pollen grains in the three oschr732 mutant lines were properly developed (Fig. 3C). Pollen grains from ZH11 were uniformly full and normally stained (Fig. 3D), while approximately half of the oschr732 mutant pollen grains exhibited abnormal staining patterns (Fig. 3E-G). Meanwhile, the seed-setting rate and pollen fertility of three heterozygous lines of oschr732 were normal, with no significant difference compared to ZH11 (Fig. S2).

For ovule development analysis, whole-mount staining and clearing techniques were used to observe ovules from ZH11 and oschr732-1 mutant at three developmental stages: Archesporial cell (AC), Functional megaspore (FM), and Mature ovule (MO). Confocal microscopy imaging showed that in the oschr732 mutant, abnormalities were present in all three developmental stages, leading to defective embryo sac formation. Some ovules failed to develop the typical “seven-cell, eight-nucleus” structure (Fig. 3H-M). These findings indicate that the loss of OsCHR732 gene function affects both pollen fertility in stamens and ovule fertility in pistils, ultimately leading to a significant reduction in seed-setting rate.

OsCHR732 was dominantly expressed in ovary and late anther in rice

The expression pattern of OsCHR732 in different rice tissues was analyzed using quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR). The results indicated that OsCHR732 exhibited significantly higher expression levels in young leaves compared to roots and stems. During inflorescence development, its expression peaked at the late developmental stage (Fig. 4A).

To further investigate its spatial expression pattern, a GUS reporter construct driven by the OsCHR732 promoter was generated and introduced into ZH11 via genetic transformation. GUS staining was performed on T2 generation positive transgenic plants, revealing strong GUS signals in the coleoptile base after seed germination, young leaves, and young panicles (Fig. 4B-D). Throughout the entire spikelet development process, strong GUS staining was consistently observed in ovaries (Fig. 4E-I). No apparent GUS signal was detected in early-stage anthers (2–4 mm); however, staining gradually intensified in late-stage anthers (6–7 mm) and mature pollen grains (Fig. 4J-N). These findings suggest that OsCHR732 was predominantly expressed in young panicles, ovaries, and late-stage anthers, implying its potential role in rice reproductive development, particularly in the formation of both male and female reproductive organs.

OsCHR732 regulates the expression of MAPK and hormone signaling genes

To explore the role of OsCHR732 in regulating reproductive development in rice, RNA-seq analysis was performed to profile the transcriptomes of both ZH11 and oschr723-1. At the booting stage of rice, spikelets measuring 1.5–2 mm from young panicles (4–6 cm in length) were sampled from ZH11 and oschr732-1. Library construction and sequencing for RNA-Seq were outsourced to Wuhan Biorun Biotechnology Co. Ltd. The reproducibility and consistency of biological replicates within and between oschr732 and ZH11 groups were assessed by calculating the correlation coefficients between samples (Fig. 5A). The results showed that the correlation coefficients for biological replicates within groups were all greater than 0.9, indicating high data quality and reliability of the RNA-Seq results. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using the RNA-Seq data to compare oschr732 and ZH11. The volcano plot illustrated the distribution of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), revealing a total of 2927 DEGs, of which 1082 were upregulated and 1845 were downregulated. The number of downregulated genes was significantly higher than that of upregulated genes (Fig. 5B and Table S2). The distribution of significance and fold changes (FC) in gene expression indicated notable differences in gene expression profiles in the oschr732 samples (Fig. S3).

The KEGG enrichment analysis for DEGs revealed that the upregulated genes were primarily enriched in pathways related to DNA replication and base excision repair (Fig. S4 - S5, Table S3). Differently, the downregulated genes were significantly enriched in pathways associated with MAPK signaling pathway, plant signal transduction, and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (Fig. 5C and Table S4). Notably, genes involved in the MAPK signaling pathway (Fig. 5D), ABA signaling pathway (Fig. 5I), JA signaling pathway (Fig. 5L), and ethylene signaling pathway exhibited significant differential expression between oschr732 and ZH11. Focusing on signaling pathways associated with key biological processes, we observed a marked downregulation of MAP3Ks (e.g., MKKK55, MKKK70), MAP2Ks (e.g., MKK10-2), MAPKs (e.g., MAP1), and WRKY transcription factors (e.g., WRKY53) in the MAPK pathway (Fig. 5E-H). In the plant hormone signaling pathway, key genes in ABA signaling pathway, including ABF family members (ABIL1 and ABI5) and PP2C family genes (e.g., PP2C8, PP2C30), were significantly downregulated in oschr732 (Fig. 5I-K). In JA signaling pathway, multiple key genes, including JAZ family members (e.g., JAZ11) and MYC2 family genes, exhibited a downregulation in oschr732 samples (Fig. 5L-N). Similarly, in the ethylene signaling pathway, multiple genes from the ETR (e.g., ETR2), CTR, and ERF families were significantly downregulated (Fig. S6). These findings suggested that OsCHR732 might be involved in regulating the expression of the genes related to MAPK and hormone such as ABA, JA, and ethylene signaling.

OsCHR732 affects chromatin accessibility in rice

To define whether OsCHR732 regulates chromatin accessibility in rice, ATAC-Seq was performed in ZH11 and oschr732-1. Correlation analysis of the ATAC-Seq samples demonstrated high reproducibility (Fig. 6A). we quantified reads within a 2-kb window flanking the transcription start site (TSS) and observed a pronounced peak near the TSS in both ZH11 and oschr732 samples (Fig. 6B), confirming the high reliability of the sequencing data. Examination of the MAplot revealed a subset of peaks with significant differential accessibility between ZH11 and oschr732, particularly those at the extremes of the y-axis, which were selected for further analysis (Fig. 6C). Analysis of differential accessible regions (DARs) indicated that, relative to ZH11, the oschr732 exhibited 13,354 genes with decreased accessibility and 13,067 genes with increased accessibility (Fig. 6D).

Peaks in ATAC-Seq data were further annotated based on their localization within various genomic regions, including coding sequences (CDS), 5′ untranslated regions (UTRs), 3′ UTRs, intergenic regions (defined as regions more than 2 kb upstream or 300 bp downstream of a gene), introns, and promoters (within 2 kb upstream of genes). The results showed that the majority of peaks in both ZH11 and oschr732 were located in promoter regions, accounting for 42.802% and 42.679% of the peaks, respectively (Fig. 6E). Analysis of three replicates each from ZH11 and oschr732 identified a total of 25,178 peaks in ZH11 and 25,371 peaks in oschr732 (Fig. 6F). Genome-wide chromatin accessibility analysis revealed that, for downregulated genes, the oschr732 mutant exhibited significantly stronger ATAC-Seq signals near the TSS compared to ZH11 (Fig. 6G). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the ATAC-Seq data showed that genes with increased accessibility were primarily enriched in pathways related to ribosome biogenesis and RNA transport (Fig. 6H), while genes with decreased accessibility were mainly enriched in plant hormone signal transduction, starch and sucrose metabolism, and the MAPK signaling pathway (Fig. 6I). These findings indicate that OsCHR732 may affect chromatin accessibility in rice.

OsCHR732 might regulate downstream target genes in rice

To determine whether changes in chromatin accessible regions were associated with gene expression in rice, we compared the differential accessible regions (DARs) identified from ATAC-Seq (noting that a single gene may correspond to multiple DARs) with the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified via RNA-Seq (Fig. 7A). Our analysis revealed an overlap of 273 upregulated genes with upregulated DARs, 1,044 downregulated genes with downregulated DARs, 326 downregulated genes with upregulated DARs and 111 upregulated genes with downregulated DARs (Table S5).

B-G Multi-omics analysis of the signals for JAZ11, ETR2, MAP1, WRKY53, ABIL1, and ABI5 as visualized using the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV). KEGG pathway analysis of the commonly downregulated genes identified through integrated RNA-Seq and ATAC-Seq data revealed significant enrichment in the MAPK signaling pathway, plant hormone signal transduction pathway, and starch and sucrose metabolism pathway, with 31, 34, and 19 genes significantly downregulated, respectively. The plant hormone signal transduction pathway primarily includes genes involved in ABA, ethylene and jasmonate signaling, with 7, 8 and 8 genes downregulated, respectively (Table S6). Out of them, several genes previously reported to be involved in rice fertility regulation were identified, including JAZ11, ETR2, MAP1, WRKY53, ABIL1, and ABI5 (Fig. S7). Meanwhile, quantitative RT-PCR analysis also confirmed that four genes, OsWRKY53, OsABI5, OsABIL1 and OsJAZ11, were significantly down regulated in the mutant oschr732-1 (Fig. S8).

To further assess the chromatin state of these genes in the oschr732 mutant, we visualized the RNA-Seq and ATAC-Seq data from ZH11 and oschr732 using the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV), focusing on the signal intensity and binding patterns within their promoter regions. The ATAC-Seq data showed that the peaks corresponding to JAZ11, ETR2, WRKY53, and ABIL1 were broader in the oschr732 mutant compared to ZH11, whereas the RNA-Seq signals for these genes were significantly lower in oschr732 (Fig. 7B, C, E and F). In contrast, both the ATAC-Seq and RNA-Seq signals for MAP1 and ABI5 exhibited broader peak coverage in oschr732 relative to ZH11 (Fig. 7D and G). Among these genes, the expression pattern of ABIL1 is the closest to that of OSCHR732 (Fig. S9). These results suggested that OsCHR732 might regulate the expression of genes related to MAPK and hormone signaling by affecting chromatin accessibility in the promoters of target genes.

Discussion

Chromatin remodeling proteins have been demonstrated to play critical roles in regulating various aspects of plant growth and development. However, their functions and molecular regulatory mechanisms during rice pollen and ovule development remain largely elusive. In this study, we systematically characterized the expression pattern and biological function of the rice chromatin remodeling factor OsCHR732 across different tissues. Our results indicate that OsCHR732 widely associates with gene promoter regions and modulates the expression of hormone- and MAPK signaling-related genes by altering chromatin accessibility (Figs. 6 and 7), ultimately affecting pollen and ovule development in rice. These findings provide important insights into the molecular mechanisms by which OsCHR732 regulates reproductive development in rice.

OsCHR732 is a key gene regulating rice pollen and ovule development

As a staple food for more than half of the global population, normal fertility in rice is fundamental to achieving high yields. Phenotypic analysis of CRISPR-Cas9 gene-edited mutant oschr732 revealed a significantly reduced seed-setting rate compared to wild-type ZH11. Detailed observations indicated that more than 50% of pollen in the oschr732 mutants were nonviable, and a subset of ovules exhibited developmental abnormalities. Quantitative real-time PCR analysis demonstrated that OsCHR732 expression peaked during the late stage of inflorescence development (P6) (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, promoter-GUS fusion assays confirmed high-level expression of OsCHR732 in young panicles, throughout various stages of ovule development, and in mid-to-late stage anthers (Fig. 4B-N). Transcriptomic data revealed that a substantial number of genes significantly downregulated in the oschr732 mutant were predominantly expressed in the shoot apical meristem (SAM), during inflorescence development, and in ovaries, many of which have been previously reported to be associated with pollen or ovule development (Fig. 7). Collectively, these results suggested that OsCHR732 might be a key regulatory gene in controlling the development of rice pollen and ovules.

OsCHR732 is a member of the SNF2 (Sucrose Non-Fermenting 2) gene family in rice. This family comprises a large and diverse group of multi-subunit complexes, where chromatin remodeling factors (CHRs) function as ATPases capable of repositioning nucleosomes on chromatin [22]. Several SNF2 family genes in Arabidopsis have also been implicated in developmental regulation. The BRAHMA (BRM) gene directly activates the flowering repressor gene SVP, thereby modulating vegetative development and influencing fertility [23]. Similarly, mutations in CHR5 significantly suppress the expression of the immune-related gene SNC1 in the bon1 mutant background, thereby affecting disease resistance and indirectly impacting growth and fertility [24]. DDM1 mutations cause genome-wide DNA methylation defects, leading to epigenetic alterations that impair reproductive development in Arabidopsis [25]. These studies underscore the intricate network of epigenetic and cellular processes governing plant reproduction and emphasize the significance of chromatin remodelers in reproductive development. Additionally, OsCHR4 has been shown to play an essential role in early chloroplast development in adaxial mesophyll cells [26]. CHD3 is a bifunctional chromatin regulator that recognizes and modulates the methylation of histone H3 lysines 4 and 27 (H3K4me and H3K27me), thereby influencing tissue-specific gene expression and regulating transcriptional programs during plant development [6].

Most SNF2 family genes in rice exhibit high expression levels in tillers, SAM or inflorescence [27], indicating their potential role in regulating developmental processes. Previous studies have shown that the mutant of OsCHR741 exhibits an abnormal development in inflorescence and seed, suggesting its potential impact on rice fertility and seed set [28]. Similarly, OsLFR has been reported to be predominantly expressed in rice spikelets and seeds, and its loss-of-function mutant lfr-1 displays phenotypic defects such as leaf curling, an increased number of petals, and reduced fertility [29]. OsCHR732 is the rice homolog of INO80 in Arabidopsis. Previous studies had been reported that OsCHR732 was highly expressed in late-stage anthers and mature ovaries [28]. Loss-of-function mutations in OsCHR732 result in defective pollen development, suggesting a critical role in reproductive development. In the SNF2 gene family, OsCHR732 was located on the same branch as OsCHR702, OsCHR711 and OsCHR729 in the phylogenetic tree, with a close evolutionary relationship [27]. At the same time, their expression patterns are also similar, with high expression in SAM and young panicles [28]. These data indicated that OsCHR732 might have functional redundancy with these genes, which may also be the reason why the pollen fertility of the oschr732 mutant is only 50%. These findings align with our observations, and the disruption of OsCHR732 also led to severe developmental defects in ovules, indicating its importance in chromatin remodeling and fertility regulation in rice.

Despite progress in characterizing SNF2 family genes, their functional roles in rice fertility remain incompletely understood. Given the significance of chromatin remodeling in reproductive development, elucidating the function of OsCHR732 and its regulatory network is of great importance for understanding fertility mechanisms and improving rice yield.

OsCHR732 may regulate the expression of hormone- and MAPK signaling-related genes by affecting chromatin accessibility

To further elucidate the regulatory network of OsCHR732, we performed RNA-Seq and ATAC-Seq analyses on spikelets from ZH11 and oschr732 at the archesporial cell stage. The ATAC-Seq data revealed that OsCHR732 mutation significantly alters chromatin accessibility. Peak annotation analysis demonstrated that the majority of accessible chromatin regions are located within gene promoter regions (Fig. 6B). However, the chromatin accessibility signal detected near transcription start sites (TSS) was significantly reduced in the oschr732 mutant compared to the wild type, particularly in downregulated differentially expressed genes (oschr732 vs. wild type) (Fig. 6E). Integration of ATAC-Seq and RNA-Seq data indicated a strong correlation between OsCHR732-mediated chromatin accessibility and gene expression levels (Fig. 7E-P). These results suggested that the OsCHR732-mediated chromatin remodeling complex might regulate the expression of target genes by modulating their chromatin accessibility in promoter regions.

Our integrated analysis of RNA-Seq and ATAC-Seq data revealed that the commom DEGs were significantly enriched in the MAPK signaling pathway, plant hormone signal transduction pathway, and starch and sucrose metabolism pathway. The plant hormone signal transduction pathway primarily included ABA, ethylene, jasmonate, auxin, and gibberellin signaling pathway (Table S4). Within the ABA signaling pathway, the downregulated genes include one encoding an ABA receptor, five encoding protein phosphatases, and three encoding bZIP transcription factors. The bZIP transcription factor LOC_Os01g64000 (ABI5) has been reported to function as a positive regulator of pollen development in rice, and the mutant of OsABI5 exhibit reduced fertility compared to the wild type [30]. Given the phenotypic similarity between oschr732 and OsABI5 mutants, we hypothesize that ABI5 might be a downstream regulatory target of OsCHR732, jointly contributing to fertility regulation in rice. In the ethylene signaling pathway, overexpression of ETR2 has been shown to reduce the number of effective panicles and seed-setting rate in rice [31]. Furthermore, in JA signaling pathway, LOC_Os03g08320 (JAZ11) has been reported to positively regulate grain weight by increasing grain width. Moreover, overexpression of OsJAZ11 results in a significant reduction in seed-setting rate and a decrease in the number of grains per panicle [32]. Overexpression of WRKY53 in the MAPK signaling pathway results in reduced seed setting rate. According to the microrray expression data, the genes, MAP1, WRKY53, ABIL1, ABI5, JAZ11, and ETR2 were predominantly expressed in SAM, or/and inflorescence (Fig. S7 and Table S5), similar to the expression pattern of OsCHR732, which indicating that these genes might jointly participated in the regulatory progress of pollen and ovule development in rice. In summary, our findings suggest that OsCHR732 modulates chromatin accessibility at the promoter regions of genes involved in MAPK, ABA, ethylene, and JA pathways, thereby influencing their expression and subsequently regulating pollen and ovule development in rice. This study marks a critical step toward elucidating the regulatory role of the chromatin remodeler OsCHR732 in rice reproductive development. However, many unknowns remain, and further research is required to refine this knowledge framework, ultimately laying a solid foundation for deciphering the molecular regulatory mechanisms governing rice pollen and ovule development.
