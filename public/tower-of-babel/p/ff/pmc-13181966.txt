Urinary IL-18 predicts progression of IgA nephropathy
Authors: Yuhong Tang, Xiaolei Tao, ChunLin Huang, Siyuan Teng, Xin Lin, Jianwei Tian, Yating Liao, Jing Mei, Jun Ou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is currently a lack of noninvasive biomarkers that effectively predict the progression of IgA nephropathy. We investigated the value of urinary IL-18 in predicting the progression of IgA nephropathy and whether its combination with clinical variables improved risk prediction.MethodsA total of 136 patients with IgA nephropathy were followed up for a median of 36 months in four academic medical centers. The levels of three biomarkers, urinary IL-18, urinary KIM-1 and urinary NGAL, were measured via ELISA in patients with 136 IgA nephropathy. The progression of IgA nephropathy was defined as a > 50% decrease in the eGFR or end-stage kidney disease. Multivariate Cox regression analyses of urine biomarkers for predicting the progression of IgA nephropathy were performed, and the AUCs of the clinical prediction models were calculated.ResultsKaplan–Meier analysis revealed that high levels (> 28.1 pg/mg of creatinine) of urinary IL-18 were associated with a significantly poor renal outcome (P < 0.01), and Cox analysis further confirmed this result. High levels of urinary IL-18 were associated with a 4.7-fold greater risk for IgA nephropathy progression in adjusted analyses. For predicting IgA nephropathy progression, urinary IL-18 yielded a C-statistic of 0.77 (95% CI, 0.67–0.86), renal injury molecule 1 yielded 0.75 (95% CI, 0.65–0.86), and uNGAL yielded 0.70 (95% CI, 0.59–0.80). Urinary IL-18 levels significantly improved the C statistic from 0.77 to 0.89, outperforming the clinical and MEST-C scores.ConclusionUrinary IL-18 is a significant predictor of poor renal outcomes and improves the risk prediction of IgA nephropathy.Keywords: IgA nephropathy, Interleukin-18 (IL-18), Biomarker, Prognosis, Risk prediction

Introduction

The prevalence of IgA nephropathy varies geographically, with the highest prevalence in the Asia‒Pacific region and the lowest in Africa [1]. In North American and Western European populations, the male-to-female ratio of the disease is approximately 2:1 in both adults and children, but there is no sex difference in East Asia [2, 3]. IgA nephropathy is the most common type of primary glomerulonephritis worldwide, and it is also the main cause of end-stage renal disease [4].

The prognostic clinical factors include the presence of persistent and severe proteinuria, elevated serum creatinine level, hypertension, and histological lesions [5]. However, they are nonspecific. How to delay the progression of IgA nephropathy in the kidney is an important question. Therefore, identifying a new biomarker that can predict the progression of IgA nephropathy is crucial. Recent efforts have focused on developing biomarkers to predict early disease progression in patients with IgA nephropathy. Promising biomarkers identified in serum include galactose-deficient IgA1 (Gd-IgA1), autoantibodies against Gd-IgA1, fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF-23), and matrix metalloproteinase 7 (MMP-7) [6–9]. Nevertheless, most of these biomarkers are invasively derived from blood, which has several disadvantages, such as vascular damage, infections, and patient discomfort. It is worth exploring noninvasive biomarkers to alleviate patient suffering during prolonged invasive procedures.

Interleukin-18 (IL-18) is a cytokine derived from macrophages and was first discovered by Okamura et al. [10] in the livers of mice in toxic shock, its structure is similar to that of the IL-1 family, and its function is similar to that of the IL-12 family. It is a member of the IL-1 family and has been studied in detail in preclinical ischemia reperfusion models. IL-18 was detected in the urine of mice suffering from ischemic acute kidney injury (AKI) and appeared to play a significant role in the development of ischemic AKI [11]. Urinary IL-18 has also been investigated as a biomarker for contrast-induced nephropathy [12]. It has been reported that IL-18 expression is increased in podocytes, infiltrating interstitial macrophages, interstitial myofibroblasts, and distal tubular epithelial cells in the kidney during active ANCA-associated vasculitis [13]. Indeed, accumulating evidence has shown that IL-18 is upregulated in acute kidney injury, where it functions as an inflammatory and repair-related biomarker reflecting ongoing injury and repair processes [14, 15]. Furthermore, IL-18 has been implicated in other autoimmune-mediated glomerulonephritis, including lupus nephritis and IgA vasculitis, through its involvement in inflammasome activation and subsequent inflammatory cascades [16]. For these reasons, we hypothesized that urinary IL-18 would predict renal prognosis in IgA nephropathy.

We conducted a multicenter prospective study of 136 patients with IgA nephropathy to investigate whether IL-18 can predict nephropathy progression or improve risk prediction models compared with those of other biomarkers.

Material and methods

Patients

This prospective, multicenter cohort study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Affiliated Hospital of Guilin Medical University. Written informed consent was obtained from all study participants. The research was conducted in four academic medical centers located in different cities (Guilin, Guangzhou, Guiyang and Dalian) between April 2013 and September 2016. The eligible participants in the study were patients who were diagnosed via biopsy and (1) were aged 18–75 years and admitted to one of the four participating hospitals; (2) had eGFR values > 20 ml/min/1.73m2; and (3) only exhibited primary IgA nephropathy. The exclusion criteria included (1) patients with secondary IgA nephropathy, such as systemic lupus erythematosus; Henoch–Schoenlein purpura; liver disease; acute urinary or respiratory infection; pregnancy; and cancer and (2) patients who were taking corticosteroids or immunosuppressive agents prior to renal biopsy. All enrolled patients were followed up for more than one year.

Procedures

This was an observational study in which all of the study patients received the standard of care for IgA nephropathy. All patients received standard care based on renin-angiotensin system (RAS) blockade (ACEi/ARB). For those with inadequately controlled proteinuria, corticosteroids or immunosuppressive agents were added in some cases according to clinical indications. The sample collection methods used in the study were as follows. We collected spot urine when patients were admitted. The urine samples were centrifuged at 3000×g for 10 min, and the supernatants were stored at -80 °C. All patients were followed up after admission every 3 months in a designated outpatient clinic or by phone until the end of the study. The serum creatinine level was measured to determine changes in renal function. The eGFR was determined using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation. Proteinuria was measured by collecting a complete 24-hour urine sample, recording the total urine volume and determining the total protein content.

Renal histological evaluation

Renal biopsy samples were reviewed independently by two pathologists who were blinded to the identity of the tissue. The Oxford classification was applied as previously described, which considers the following measurements: mesangial hypercellularity (M), endocapillary hypercellularity (E), segmental sclerosis (S), and tubule–interstitial fibrosis (T). Additionally, we evaluated the crescents, which were scored as C0 (no crescents), C1 (crescents in at least 1 but < 25% of the glomeruli), or C2 (crescents in at least 25% of the glomeruli).

Laboratory measurements

The collected urine samples were shipped by commercial cold chain transportation from the participating hospitals. All biomarkers were analyzed in a central laboratory following a standardized protocol, and all of the samples were labeled using study identification numbers without personal identifiers or clinical conditions. Urinary IL-18 levels were measured with an ELISA kit (DY907; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. uKIM-1 and uNGAL levels were quantified using the ELISA kits (uKIM-1: DY1750B, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN; uNGAL: Bioporto) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All biomarkers were measured in triplicate, and investigator-calculated intra- and interassay variability ranged between 2% and 6% and 5%–9% on the basis of blinded replicate samples from study patients [17]. Urinary and serum creatinine levels were measured using an automatic biochemical analyzer (AU480; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). All urinary biomarkers were normalized to urinary creatinine and expressed as picograms or nanograms per milligram of creatinine. The laboratory measurements was primarily based on the measurement approach described by Yang X. et al. [17].

Outcome definitions

The primary outcome was the development of composite events, defined as a > 50% decrease in the eGFR or end-stage kidney disease. End-stage kidney disease was defined as an eGFR < 15 mL/min/1.73 m², indicating the need for kidney replacement therapy. We evaluated this outcome, including calculation of the eGFR using the CKD-EPI equation and verification of patients requiring renal replacement therapy according to hospital records.

Statistical analyses

SPSS 23.0 software was used for all analyses. The Pearson chi-squared test was used to compare categorical variables. We used a two-sample t test or a Mann–Whitney rank sum test to compare continuous variables. The median uIL-18 level was categorized as a categorical variable or a continuous variable (log-transformed). We defined a high median as high risk and then conducted Cox regression on the created variables. The adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) of IgA nephropathy progression with multiple Cox regression analysis with random intercepts were calculated for each center. The selection of covariates for the adjusted model was based on known risk factors for CKD progression in patients with IgA nephropathy, including mean artery pressure, proteinuria, eGFR and MEST-C scores. To evaluate the performance of uIL-18 and other biomarkers at different cutoff values, an AUC was calculated. All tests were two-tailed, and P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Description of the baseline cohorts

A total of 136 patients with IgA nephropathy were enrolled in this study. The characteristics of the cohort (Table 1) are presented according to progression status. The median length of follow-up was 36 months (ranging from 12 to 55 months). In the cohort, 28 patients reached the composite endpoint of a 50% decline in the eGFR (n = 11) or ESKD (n = 17). The median levels of uIL-18, uKIM-1 and uNGAL were 28.1 pg/mg Cr, 0.7 pg/mg Cr and 21.2 pg/mg Cr, respectively. The incidence of proteinuria was greater and the eGFR was lower in the progression group than in the nonprogression group. The majority of patients in both the progression group and the nonprogression group received RAS inhibitors, regardless of progression status.

The level of uIL-18 predicted the risk of IgA nephropathy progression

According to the univariable models, there were significant associations with IgA nephropathy progression between low and high levels of these 3 biomarkers (P < 0.05 for all). However, the associations with uNGAL were no longer significant after adjusting for clinical variables and the MEST-C score (Table 2). Among the 3 biomarkers, uIL-18 had the strongest association with IgA nephropathy progression. According to the adjusted model (Model 1), patients with high levels of uIL-18 (> 28.1 pg/mg Cr) at baseline had a 6.7-fold greater risk of IgA nephropathy progression than did those with low levels (≤ 28.1 pg/mg Cr) (Table 2). As a continuous variable, a 1 SD increase in uIL-18 was associated with a 2.1-fold greater risk of IgA nephropathy progression (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] 2.1; 95% CI, 1.2–3.7) in a multivariable model. According to the Kaplan‒Meier analysis, high median uIL-18 levels were associated with lower survival rates and composite renal outcomes in patients with IgA nephropathy.

The level of uIL-18 correlated with clinical variables

Spearman correlation analysis revealed that uIL-18 was strongly correlated with proteinuria (r = 0.42, P < 0.01) and also correlated with MAP (r = 0.29, P < 0.01), but it was not correlated with eGFR (r=-0.11, P = 0.20 > 0.05), as shown in Fig. 1.1, Fig. 1.2 and Fig. 1.3.

Urinary IL-18 predicted the risk of IgA nephropathy progression

In our cohort, uIL-18 was tested for its ability to predict the risk of IgA nephropathy progression and had an AUC of 0.77 (C statistic, 0.77; 95% CI, 0.67–0.86). A clinical model consisting of MAP, proteinuria, and eGFR at the time of biopsy predicted the 3-year risk of a composite renal outcome, with a C statistic of 0.82 (C statistic, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.74–0.90). As shown in Table 3, the addition of uIL-18 to the model containing clinical data at the time of biopsy and MEST-C (the reference model) significantly improved the risk prediction of IgA nephropathy progression (C statistic, 0.89; 95% CI, 0.82–0.96). Incorporating additional biomarkers into the model that already included uIL-18, clinical data, and MEST-C did not significantly enhance the predictive performance (C statistic, 0.86; 95% CI, 0.82–0.90) (Table 3). Other predictive biomarkers, when combined with clinical data at the time of biopsy and MEST-C, also produced higher C statistics than did the reference model, although the increase did not reach statistical significance.

Discussion

In this prospective multicenter study, uIL-18 correlated significantly with proteinuria, consistent with the pathophysiological expectation that heavy proteinuria directly induces tubular epithelial cell injury, leading to uIL-18 release. Notably, multivariable analysis demonstrated that uIL-18 predicted 50% eGFR decline independently of proteinuria. This finding suggests that uIL-18 is not merely a surrogate marker for proteinuria, but rather reflects the severity of tubulointerstitial damage resulting from proteinuria. Our findings revealed that urinary IL-18 levels independently and strongly predict the progression of IgA nephropathy, defined as a > 50% decrease in the eGFR or progression to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD). Therefore, uIL-18 may help identify patients with similar proteinuria levels but more active tubular injury.

In predicting IgA nephropathy progression, the AUC and HR of urinary IL18 levels were slightly greater than those of uKIM-1 levels and uNGAL levels. Importantly, the addition of urinary IL-18 levels to major clinical risk factors (MAP, eGFR, and proteinuria) and the MEST-C score improved the risk prediction of IgA nephropathy.

IL-18 has been reported to be associated with a number of diseases. Recent studies have suggested that IL-18 may provide substantial prognostic information in different settings, especially in the context of tubular injury. It has been reported that urinary IL-18 levels are associated with decreased renal function in chronic kidney disease (CKD) [18]. Shi. et al. [19] explored the relationship between serum IL-18 and the prognosis of IgA nephropathy in a single-center study. Serum IL-18 is considered an independent predictor of renal prognosis. However, few studies have investigated the relationship between urinary IL-18 and poor renal prognosis. Compared with serum IL-18, urinary IL-18 has the advantages of being noninvasive, convenient and easily accessible. Therefore, we hypothesized that IL-18 is a predictor of poor renal prognosis in IgA nephropathy in our study. We found that urinary IL-18 is a predictor of poor renal prognosis, with an AUC of 0.77 (Fig. 2). It has been reported that serum IL-18 level is associated with tubulointerstitial injury, with an AUC of 0.64, in IgA nephropathy [19]. It is reported that increased urinary interleukins often do not correlate with their plasma levels, suggesting that they are produced locally in the kidneys due to intrarenal inflammation. These findings suggest that urinary IL-18 may have better predictive performance than serum IL-18. Moreover, the survival rate of the high urinary IL-18 group was greater than that of the low urinary IL-18 group (Fig. 3). According to the univariate Cox regression analyses, the high-uIL-18 group had a worse outcome than the low-uIL-18 group did (hazard ratio [HR] 6.7; 95% CI, 2.3–19.3). After adjusting for factors such as MAP, proteinuria, eGFR, hemoglobin and serum albumin, urinary IL-18 remained an independent predictor of poor renal prognosis, and the HR in Model 1 was 5.3. After adjusting for covariates in Model 1 plus the Oxford MEST-C score, the HR value was 4.7, which was lower than the HR value before adjustment (Table 2).

Many biomarkers have been shown to be predictors of the progression of IgA nephropathy, including proteinuria, eGFR, and hypertension [5, 20–21]. Few studies of urinary IL-18 have combined clinical variables and the MEST-C score for risk prediction. In our study, we predicted the risk of IgA nephropathy using urinary IL-18, urinary NGAL, and urinary KIM-1 levels. Clinical data at the time of biopsy revealed that MEST-C in combination with urinary IL-18 significantly improved the risk prediction of IgA nephropathy (C statistic, 0.89; 95% CI, 0.82–0.96) (Table 3). Improving risk prediction can accurately predict the risk of disease progression and provide new ideas for early intervention in and personalized treatment of patients with IgA nephropathy to mitigate disease deterioration.

The underlying mechanism by which elevated urinary IL-18 leads to poor prognosis in IgA nephropathy is unclear. IgA nephropathy is characterized by mesangial proliferative glomerulonephritis, where IgA is deposited in the glomerular mesangial area. This deposition triggers mesangial cells to release substances such as IL-1, IL-6, monocyte chemotactic protein-1, TNF-α, TGF-β, and PDGF [22], which cause tubular epithelial cell (TEC) and inflammatory changes in the renal interstitium. The interaction between IgA and glomerular mesangial cells stimulates cellular proliferation [23]. This may lead to further deterioration of the kidney. Therefore, IL-18 might be involved in the inflammatory response and promote the release of cytokines and other factors that exacerbate the disease process and lead to a poor renal prognosis.

There were several advantages in our study. First, few studies have explored the effects of IL-18 levels in urine samples on adverse outcomes in patients with IgA nephropathy. We posit that high levels of urinary IL-18 promote kidney deterioration in IgA nephropathy. Additionally, we found that uIL-18 is correlated with clinical data, and a clinical prediction model including clinical data, MEST-C scores and uIL-18 levels was constructed, which improved the prediction performance. Second, this was a multicenter trial, which reduces the limitations that exist in a single research center. Third, we used urine as a source of predictive biomarkers. Urinalysis is a noninvasive diagnostic tool that provides useful information and is easy to perform in outpatient and hospital settings. Compared with invasive modalities, this method is significantly easier in terms of obtaining patient consent. Moreover, it has the advantages of noninvasive collection, a lower risk of infection, a lower cost and easier access.

There were several limitations in our study. First, our study included a small study population. It is necessary to expand the study population so that we can estimate the value of urinary IL-18 levels in IgA nephropathy and reduce the bias in our study. Second, this was a prospective study. Research results may be affected by external factors that are difficult to control. These findings provide suggestions for us to further investigate the mechanism of this phenomenon.

Conclusion

Urinary IL-18 has prognostic value for the progression of IgA nephropathy and is promising for predicting the risk of poor renal outcomes when combined with clinical data and MEST-C scores. However, more multicenter, large-sample cohorts are needed to validate the broad applicability and stability of this model.
