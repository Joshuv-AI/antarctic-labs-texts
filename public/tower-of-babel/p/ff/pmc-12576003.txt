Solitary Fibrous Tumor of the Renal Hilar in a 52‐Year‐Old Woman: A Case Report
Authors: Yang Zhang, Chuan‐Chuan Wu, Bang‐Wei Che, Kai‐Fa Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSolitary fibrous tumors (SFTs) are rare spindle cell tumors that primarily originate from the pleura, with infrequent occurrences in the kidney. To date, only a limited number of cases has been reported. This report presents the case of a 52‐year‐old woman diagnosed with an SFTs. Abdominal CT and magnetic resonance imaging revealed a mass lesion in the right kidney, characterized by well‐defined borders and the absence of necrosis or cystic degeneration. Immunohistochemical staining of the tumor tissue demonstrated positive expression of CD34, STAT‐6, and CD99, thereby confirming the diagnosis of SFTs in the right kidney. A laparoscopic radical nephrectomy was performed. A comprehensive literature review was conducted to systematically assess the current understanding of renal SFT. The diagnosis of renal SFTs is established through the synthesis of clinical, pathological, immunohistochemical, and molecular characteristics. Surgery is the treatment of choice. In cases of advanced disease, this is considered the optimal therapeutic approach.

Introduction

Solitary fibrous tumors (SFTs) are rare mesenchymal tumors that account for approximately 2% of all soft tissue neoplasms [1]. The etiology of SFTs is unknown; however, they are typically slow‐growing tumors with a favorable prognosis. These soft tissue tumors can be found in any part of the body, with the thorax identified as the most common site. Renal SFTs are relatively rare, with only 105 cases of renal SFTs reported in the literature [1]. The clinical presentation of renal SFTs varies widely, ranging from asymptomatic incidental findings to abdominal discomfort, hematuria, locally confined renal masses, and large abdominal masses [2]. Furthermore, owing to the nonspecific nature of radiologic features, renal SFTs are frequently misdiagnosed and managed as Renal Cell Carcinoma (RCC) [3]. Surgical resection remains the primary treatment modality for localized renal SFTs and yields favorable outcomes in most cases.

Key Clinical Message

Renal SFT is rare; this case highlights presentation with abdominal mass/pain. Diagnosis relies on histopathology (STAT6+ CD34+) and imaging. Complete surgical excision achieved favorable prognosis. It is emphasized that SFTs should be considered in the differential diagnosis of renal tumors, and the best treatment is surgery.

Case History

A 52‐year‐old female presented with 7‐day intermittent swelling and pain in the right flank and abdomen. Another hospital found a right‐kidney mass, leading to her admission here. She had no hematuria and denied significant past medical conditions. Physical examination showed a right‐lower‐abdomen mass and positive right‐renal‐region percussion tenderness.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

Preoperative tests (blood, biochemical, cardiac ultrasound, chest computerized tomography (CT)) were normal. Laboratory examinations are shown in Table 1. Enhanced CT of the urinary system revealed an 8.5 × 5.0 × 5.0 cm (Figure 1A) round mass in the right kidney parenchyma, protruding outside the renal contour, with significant enhancement, well‐defined margins, and no necrosis or cystic changes. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed a T1W hypointense 8.5 × 5.0 × 5.0 cm mass in the right renal region (Figure 1B). The patient was diagnosed with a right‐kidney tumor likely to be sarcoma. Under combined intravenous general anesthesia, she underwent radical nephrectomy via a retroperitoneoscopic approach. The tumor was well‐demarcated from normal renal tissue and was completely resected with an intact capsule. Postoperative pathology: The right kidney was surrounded by an easily separable perirenal fat capsule. A 7.0 cm gray‐white infiltrative lesion at the renal hilum had a nodule breaching the capsule (Figure 1C). No obvious lymphadenopathy was found. Microscopy showed spindle‐shaped and fibroblast‐like cells in fascicles (Figure 1D). The nuclei appeared uniform, oval, elongated spindle‐shaped, and vesicular, exhibiting eosinophilic staining and localized collagen degeneration, with no evidence of mitotic activity, necrosis, or pleomorphism (Figure 1E). Immunohistochemical staining confirmed the pathological diagnosis of SFT in the right kidney (Figure 1F–H). Based on imaging, morphological, and immunohistochemical features, the final diagnosis was SFT of the right kidney. The patient had an uneventful postoperative recovery, with pain resolution in the lower right abdomen. Urinary function was normal, and routine urinalysis revealed no abnormalities. No edema, gross hematuria, or other complications were observed.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

The patient was discharged after 7 days. At the 13‐month follow‐up, the patient's routine blood tests, liver and kidney function, electrolyte levels, ultrasound examinations of the liver, gallbladder, and spleen, and abdominal CT scan revealed no signs of tumor recurrence or metastasis.

Discussion

Most patients with renal SFT clinically present with palpable masses, flank, abdominal, or lumbar pain, or general abdominal discomfort [4]. Gross hematuria may occur in more severe cases. However, some patients may remain asymptomatic, and numerous urinary tract tumors exhibit similar clinical manifestations. Consequently, reliance solely on clinical symptoms for the diagnosis of renal SFT is not entirely reliable. Small tumors are often asymptomatic and contribute to delays in diagnosis [1].

However, SFTs of the kidney may arise from metastasis of SFTs from other sites, and the likelihood of this is associated with the location of the primary tumor identified at diagnosis [5]. Therefore, it is crucial to consider the possibility of metastasis during diagnosis to avoid missing potential metastatic lesions. The differential diagnosis of renal SFTs is also very broad, including hemangiopericytoma, fibroepithelial polyp of the renal pelvis, angiomyolipoma, fibroma, inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor, leiomyoma, benign fibrous histiocytoma, benign peripheral nerve sheath tumors (schwannoma/neurofibroma), renal mixed epithelial/stromal tumor, simple fibrous synovial sarcoma, fibrosarcoma, sarcomatoid renal cell carcinoma, leiomyosarcoma, and malignant fibrous histiocytoma [6]. However, only the final histopathological markers can distinguish them. The imaging features of SFTs are similar to those of renal cell carcinoma, so they are often initially diagnosed as renal cell carcinoma [5]. Blood tests have a limited diagnostic value in this context. Imaging characteristics are not specific for the diagnosis of SFTs and primarily assist in preliminary assessment; the definitive diagnosis relies on immunohistochemistry (IHC) [2]. SFTs typically exhibit a spindle cell growth pattern with vascular dilation, rendering CD34 positivity as the most useful marker for this tumor type. Further immunohistochemical staining for Bcl‐2, CD99, and vimentin supports the diagnosis of SFTs [1]. Recently, STAT6 positivity has emerged as a sensitive and specific marker for SFT, particularly following the discovery of the NAB2‐STAT6 gene fusion associated with these tumors. Both genes are located on chromosome 12, and studies indicate that the incidence of this fusion is approximately 90%–100% in SFTs [7], which is particularly valuable in cases of diagnostic ambiguity. Radical surgical resection remains the most common and preferred treatment modality for SFTs. For SFTs that have metastasized distantly or are deemed unresectable, systemic chemotherapy or combined radiotherapy may be considered [8]. While most renal SFTs exhibit favorable outcomes following complete resection, there remains a paucity of clinical evidence regarding their malignant potential and likelihood of recurrence and metastasis post‐surgery.

Consequently, the 5th WHO classification can be referenced to develop both a three‐variable and an enhanced four‐variable risk model for predicting the risk of metastasis in SFTs, incorporating factors such as patient age, mitotic activity, tumor size, and tumor necrosis [9]. Patient age was categorized as < 55 years (0 points) or ≥ 55 years (1 point). Mitoses were scored as follows: 0, 1–3, or ≥ 4 per 10 high‐power fields (HPF), receiving 0, 1, or 2 points, respectively. Tumor size was classified as follows: < 5 cm (0 points), < 10 cm (1 point), < 15 cm (2 points), and ≥ 15 cm (3 points). The risk stratification for SFTs categorizes patients into low‐risk (0–2 points), medium‐risk (3–4 points), and high‐risk (5–6 points) [9]. Long‐term follow‐up of the patients is recommended. Currently, the definitive diagnosis of renal SFTs is typically established through histological and immunohistochemical examinations of surgical specimens [10]. Consequently, SFTs are frequently misdiagnosed as renal cell carcinoma, leading to inappropriate treatment. The comparative effectiveness of preoperative biopsy and nephron‐sparing surgery in enhancing surgical and chemotherapy outcomes remains unclear.

In conclusion, this case of renal SFTs underscores the importance of establishing a diagnosis through the integration of clinical, pathological, immunohistochemical, and molecular characteristics, with surgical resection remaining the preferred treatment. SFTs may transform malignantly despite benign behavior. Recurrence or metastasis can occur after resection, requiring continued follow‐up.
