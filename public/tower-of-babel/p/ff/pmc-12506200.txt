MaxiMoM InForM: individualised fortification of human milk for very low birthweight infants— protocol of a three-arm randomised clinical trial
Authors: Megan R Beggs, Dubraiicka Pichardo, Annette Chrzaniecki, Kirsten Kotsopoulos, Rosine Bishara, Eugene Ng, Christopher Tomlinson, Douglas Campbell, Simone Vaz, Alex Kiss, Sharon Unger, Deborah L O’Connor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTo meet the elevated nutritional requirements of very low birthweight (<1500 g, VLBW) infants during hospitalisation, many neonatal units routinely add a fixed dose of multinutrient fortifier to human milk. Individualised approaches that either tailor macronutrient additions to routine analysis of milk composition (target fortification) or an infant’s metabolic response to nutrition (blood urea nitrogen (BUN) adjustable fortification) show short-term improvement in growth, but important outcomes such as longer-term neurodevelopment and major in-hospital morbidity have not been systematically assessed. The aim of our study is to determine whether target or BUN adjustable fortification of human milk, compared with standard fortification, during hospitalisation improves cognitive (primary), language and motor development of VLBW infants at 18–24 months corrected age (CA). Secondary outcomes include growth, body composition, mortality and serious morbidity.Methods and analysisThis is a three-arm, pragmatic, multicentre, double-blind, randomised clinical trial of 615 human milk–fed infants born either (1) ≤1250 g or (2) <30 weeks and <1500 g. Once enteral feeds reach 100 mL/kg/day, and until 36 weeks corrected gestational age (CGA), infants are fed according to one of three fortification protocols: (1) standard fortification, in which a fixed dose of nutrients is added to human milk; (2) target fortification, in which values from twice weekly direct analysis of mother’s milk and each batch of donor milk are used to provide 4 and 4.8 g/kg/day true protein and fat, respectively; and (3) BUN adjustable fortification, in which weekly BUN values are used to adjust protein fortification of milk. Neurodevelopment is assessed using the Bayley-IV scale at 18–24 months CA. Growth, morbidities and nutrient intakes are recorded daily during hospitalisation, and skinfolds are measured at 36 weeks CGA and at 4 months CA. At 4 months CA, body composition is determined by air displacement plethysmography on a subset of infants.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval was obtained from Clinical Trials Ontario (CTO) and local research ethics boards that are not CTO members. Study findings will be disseminated to clinicians at seminars and conferences and in peer-reviewed publications.Trial registration numberNCT05308134.Keywords: Nutrition & dietetics, Neonatology, Neonatal intensive & critical care

Introduction

Very low birthweight (<1500 g) (VLBW) infants represent an estimated 1.1% (~150 000 annually) of all live births in North America, Australia and New Zealand, central Asia and Europe.1 Despite comprising a small percentage of births, VLBW infants experience a prolonged hospital stay due, in part, to serious morbidity and account for a disproportionate percentage of total healthcare costs compared with healthy term-born infants.2,5 We recently reported that the mean health-related costs from birth to 18 months corrected age (CA) for VLBW infants fed human milk during hospitalisation was $217 624 (95% CI $197 697 to $237 551) in 2015 Canadian dollars.6 Further, our data, consistent with that of the Canadian Neonatal Network, suggest that ~20%, 42% and 23% of VLBW infants show evidence of cognitive, language and motor neuroimpairment, respectively, at 18–24 months CA.5 7 8 Thus, while efforts to prevent preterm birth are crucial, cost-effective strategies to mitigate neuroimpairment associated with VLBW are urgently required.

Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have demonstrated that human milk feeding (ie, breastfeeding or providing expressed breastmilk) protects infants against infectious morbidity and is associated with increased intelligence.9 The incremental benefit of mother’s milk versus formula on cognitive development and reduction in serious infection appears greatest for the VLBW infant.10,15 A meta-analysis of 12 trials of very preterm or VLBW infants concluded that feeding mother’s milk with supplemental pasteurised donor human milk (PDHM) instead of formula resulted in a 50% reduction in the risk of necrotising enterocolitis (NEC), a severe bowel emergency, although the authors also concluded that donor milk had little or no effect on infection or mortality.16 In part due to findings of NEC protection, the WHO recommends feeding VLBW infants with supplemental donor milk when mother’s milk is unavailable.17 Mother’s milk and PDHM differ in composition, which might account for why reductions in infection and mortality and improved neurodevelopment are not observed in trials comparing supplemental PDHM versus formula feeding.18 19

Due to the increased nutrient requirements for the rapid growth of VLBW infants, a fixed dose of multinutrient fortifier is routinely added to mother’s milk or PDHM feeds in most neonatal intensive care units (NICUs), including units participating in the current trial.20 21 This standard fortification method assumes that the nutrient content of human milk is constant. Macronutrient intakes are consistently associated with weight, length and head circumference (HC) gains during hospitalisation, yet despite routine multinutrient fortification of human milk, macronutrient intakes frequently fall below recommendations.22 23 Variability in the nutrient composition of human milk, the infant’s metabolic response to nutrition or both likely contribute to these observations.24 The macronutrient concentration of human milk is not constant. It varies based on gestational age (GA) at delivery, lactation stage, maternal body mass index (BMI), volume of milk and time of day the milk was expressed.25,28 For example, de Halleux and Rigo reported that in 804 human milk samples analysed prior to nutrient fortification in their NICU, protein, fat and energy contents ranged from 0.8 to 2.4 g/dL, 1.8 to 6.6 g/dL and 47 to 85 kcal/dL, respectively.26 For donor milk, additional freeze/thaw cycles, container changes and pasteurisation may also alter its nutrient content.19 29 30 A strategy of compensating for lower macronutrient content in some human milk by routine addition of a higher fixed dose of nutrients to all milk may result in feeding intolerance and overwhelm immature metabolic pathways, and thus is unlikely to be associated with better outcomes for all infants.31,34 Our own work suggests that early enteral energy, protein-to-energy ratio and lipid intakes within the second and third quartiles were associated with the highest cognitive score, but not motor or language scores, in VLBW infants at 18–24 months CA.35 Effects of early nutrition are long lasting; VLBW infants who met recommended enteral intakes for protein, fat and energy in the first postpartum month showed improved white matter maturation on MRI at 5 years, which was associated with improved processing speed.36 Still, further evidence is needed to inform optimal macronutrient intakes for individual infants, particularly with respect to long-term developmental outcomes.21

Two promising methods of individualised fortification have been proposed.24 31 One method, target fortification, relies on analysis of the macronutrient content of the milk. If feeds prepared with the milk do not meet requirements, multinutrient or single-nutrient modules are added to the feeds in addition to the standard fortification.2637,43 Researchers testing this strategy found that supplementation of fat, protein and carbohydrate beyond initial standard fortification is frequently required to reach the nutrient recommendations of the European Society for Paediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition (ESPGHAN).26 37 44 Importantly, this approach allows for a non-invasive method of providing a precise nutrient composition in feeds, thereby reducing the intra-individual and inter-day variability.26 37 Analysis of milk can be performed with a 1.5 mL aliquot (3mL for duplicate analysis) with a minimum of one to two analyses per week.39 44

The second approach to individualised fortification, blood urea nitrogen (BUN) adjustable fortification, considers the infant’s nutritional status or metabolic response. Urea and the nitrogen it contains are waste products of protein metabolism through the urea cycle and are excreted in the urine. Therefore, while several factors influence BUN concentration, including renal function and fluid balance, it is also used as a marker of protein intake and metabolism. Indeed, evidence suggests a significant correlation between protein intake and BUN by the time a VLBW infant is 21 days old and reaches full enteral feeds.45 46 Using the BUN adjustable fortification approach, the infant’s feeds are initially fortified following a standard fixed protocol using a multinutrient fortifier. The infant’s metabolic response is then evaluated from plasma BUN values as part of routine bloodwork in the NICU.47,49 If the infant’s BUN falls below a threshold value, extra protein is added to the feeds in a fixed amount.

A systematic review examined whether these individualised fortification approaches improved growth or reduced mortality or morbidity compared with standard fortification.24 Seven randomised controlled trials (RCTs) with a total of 521 participants and study duration of 2–77 weeks were included. Compared with standard human milk fortification, individualised approaches resulted in increased weight gain (mean difference (MD) 1.88 g/kg/day), length gain (MD 0.43 mm/day) and HC gain (MD 0.14 mm/day) during the intervention. The authors concluded that the certainty of the evidence was too poor to form conclusions regarding in-hospital mortality or morbidity, and no study reported neurodevelopmental outcomes.

Importantly, to date, trials employing individualised fortification have focused on efficacy but have not addressed concerns about potential incremental costs, which could be significant. For example, target fortification requires milk analysis, equipment, and consumables. BUN adjustable fortification potentially requires extra bloodwork. Both approaches require individualised recipes which use more personnel time and may include increased risk of errors. Conversely, if individualised fortification reduces morbidity and/or hospital stay or improves neurodevelopment, it could be cost saving. The balance of short-term and long-term costs and savings can only be estimated through a formal economic evaluation.

Overall, individualised fortification strategies attempt to mitigate concerns related to variable human milk nutrient levels and infant metabolic response and appear to improve in-hospital growth. However, there is no evidence regarding the potential impact of individualised fortification on short-term morbidity and long-term neurodevelopment.50 Additionally, an economic evaluation of individualised fortification costs will provide context to administrators considering implementing these feeding approaches.

Objectives

Primary research question

Does target or BUN adjustable fortification of human milk fed during initial hospitalisation, compared with standard fortification, improve cognitive development of infants born either ≤1250 g or <30+0 weeks GA and <1500 g at 18–24 months CA?

Secondary research questions

Does target or BUN adjustable fortification of human milk fed during initial hospitalisation, compared with standard fortification, of infants born either ≤1250 g or <30+0 weeks GA and <1500 g improve language and motor development at 18–24 months CA; improve weight, length, HC gains (in-hospital and 4-month follow-up); improve body composition (in-hospital and 4-month follow-up); impact mortality/morbidity (NEC, sepsis, chronic lung disease, retinopathy of prematurity (ROP)); show an acceptable cost-effectiveness from a healthcare sector perspective?

Exploratory research questions

Does target or BUN adjustable fortification of human milk fed during initial hospitalisation, compared with standard fortification, of infants born either ≤1250 g or <30+0 weeks GA and <1500 g improve the likelihood that recommended energy and nutrient intakes are achieved? Whether maternal diet quality during lactation influences the efficacy of individualised fortification will also be explored.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This is a three-arm, pragmatic, multicentre, double-blind, randomised clinical trial designed to evaluate the effectiveness of target and BUN adjustable fortification versus standard fortification on the study outcomes of growth, morbidity and neurodevelopment. Aside from the research dietitians and diet technicians involved in calculating daily study feed recipes and preparing feeds in one of three designated milk preparation rooms, all research study team members (including outcome assessors), clinical teams and families are blinded to feeding allocation. Mother’s milk samples are collected twice weekly (once weekly if low volume) and infant BUN is measured every 7 (±3) days for all infants in all three arms of the study to avoid inadvertent unblinding of the study. A sample from each unique batch of PDHM is also collected daily from the Rogers Hixon Ontario Human Milk Bank that serves the province of Ontario and Atlantic provinces in Canada. The feedings are prepared in one of three milk preparation rooms at tertiary NICUs separated from the units by designated diet technicians and dietitians not caring for the infants, collecting data or assessing study outcomes. The contents of the feeds are indistinguishable by visual inspection. A flow chart of the study design is provided in figure 1. As a point of clarification, while the term ‘mother’s milk’ is used throughout this protocol, all parent milk will be provided first to infants, whether an infant’s biological mother or non-biological mothers or parents who are transgender or non-binary. The term ‘human milk’ is used to describe both parent milk (including mother’s milk) and PDHM.

Recruitment

Recruitment is occurring at two tertiary care perinatal NICUs in Toronto, Canada (Mount Sinai Hospital and Sunnybrook Health Sciences Centre). Inclusion and exclusion criteria are listed in box 1. Before research staff speak to families about the study, eligibility is confirmed from the medical record and approval to approach the family is secured from the most responsible physician caring for the infant. Written informed consent is secured after the study is explained and if the family is interested (see anonymised consent form in online supplemental material). Based on previous clinical trials from our group with similar inclusion/exclusion criteria to the present trial, we anticipated an ~50% rate of consent among eligible participants.5 51 Enrolment started at Mount Sinai Hospital in November 2021 and at Sunnybrook Health Sciences Centre in February 2023. Based on the current enrolment rate, we anticipate that recruitment will be completed in July 2026. As multiples and small-for-gestational-age infants represent a significant proportion of eligible infants (25 and 10%, respectively), they are included. Given their prevalence and random assignment, we anticipate a balanced distribution of multiples (treated as individual entities) in the three arms of the study. Infants are typically transferred to a Level II NICU for convalescence when acute care is no longer required. There are 15 participating Level II NICUs. These hospitals recently collaborated on two highly successful feeding trials, including the outcome variables proposed in this current study.551,54 Families of participants can withdraw consent at any point during the study.

Randomisation

Prior to the multinutrient fortification of feeds (study day 1), each infant is assigned to one of the three feeding groups using an online randomisation schedule restricted to unblinded research staff. Feeding group allocation is randomly assigned in a ratio of 1:1:1 in random blocks of 3 or 6 with stratification by recruitment centre, birth weight group (<1000 g, ≥1000 g) and sex.

Feeding intervention

The clinical team at tertiary care NICUs determines when enteral feeds are initiated and how they are advanced according to their unit protocol. Mother’s milk is always provided first, followed by PDHM whenever mother’s milk is unavailable. Starting study day 1, infants are fed according to detailed feeding guidelines for each of the three treatment arms as outlined in table 1. For clarity, study day 1 is the day an infant reaches an enteral feeding volume of ≥100 mL/kg/day and a multinutrient fortifier is added to the milk. Clinically, at the participating units, at full enteral feeds (typically 160 mL/kg/day), the following are typical energy and protein goals: 110–140 kcal/kg/day and 3.5–4.0 g/kg/day, respectively.21 55 56 As this is a pragmatic study, the clinical team has full discretion to adjust the rate of advancement of enteral feeds, suspend the study feeding protocol or withdraw the infant from the feeding intervention if the team believes it is required to facilitate clinical care (eg, feeding intolerance, illness, hypoglycaemia, cow’s milk protein intolerance). All recipes for prepared feeds are double-checked and prepared by diet technicians or registered dietitians in milk preparation rooms at tertiary centres. The multinutrient fortifiers used routinely at each NICU are used in the present study and may differ by unit and patient. The products in use include Enfamil Human Milk Fortifier (powder), Enfamil Liquid Human Milk Fortifier Standard Protein, Enfamil Liquid Human Milk Fortifier High Protein and Similac Human Milk Fortifier Concentrated Liquid. Research diet technicians and registered dietitians prepare study feeds for the Level II NICUs in a secure milk preparation room at The Hospital for Sick Children. As we have done in the past, after infants are transferred to a Level II NICU, their mother’s milk is transported as required to The Hospital for Sick Children and their prepared feeds are delivered back to the Level II units daily in cooler boxes by courier.5,51 Participating Level II NICUs do not have milk preparation rooms and this strategy ensures blinding of feeding assignments. If a patient requires surgery or higher acuity care, they may be transferred to The Hospital for Sick Children NICU where the study will continue as at the recruitment sites.

Energy and macronutrient analysis of human milk

Routine practice at participating NICUs is that mother’s milk is thawed overnight to prepare part or all of the feeds for the next 24 hours. Slightly more milk is thawed to ensure a sufficient volume is available to prepare feeds. Twice each week (usually Monday and Thursday), pooled mother’s milk not required for preparation of the feed (ie, that would be otherwise discarded) is collected (minimum 1.5 mL required, typically 3–10 mL). Only one sample is collected weekly if mother’s supply is very limited. Milk samples are analysed for macronutrient content using a mid-infrared human milk analyser (Miris AB, Uppsala, Sweden). These results are validated monthly using the Kjeldahl method and creamatocrit for protein and fat quantification, respectively.58,60 Similarly, each batch of PDHM is analysed as described above (values are not provided by the milk bank). For infants in the target fortification group, fat and true protein values are used to adjust fortification of human milk to ensure nutrient recommendations are met. At study completion, direct assessment of milk in all study arms will be used to report macronutrient and energy intake.

End of intervention

Except as indicated below, participants continue to receive their feeds fortified as per study allocation until the infant is 36+0 weeks CGA, is discharged home, takes two oral feeds daily for 3 consecutive days without top-up, or at least 50% of daily feed volume is taken orally. The exception to the 36+0 weeks intervention end-date is in the situation where a more mature infant (eg, >36+0 weeks CGA) remains hospitalised but has not completed at least 4 weeks of the feeding intervention (ie, 4 weeks following study day 1). Among the latter group of infants, all start weaning no later than 40+0 weeks CGA. Infants are removed from the feeding intervention earlier than described above if (1) the parent(s)/guardian(s) request withdrawal from the feeding protocol; (2) the infant has inadequate weight gain despite nutrient concentration or increase in the volume of feedings as per the study guidelines (table 1); or (3) the attending physician decides that participation in the study is no longer in the best interest of the infant. If an infant is withdrawn from the study feeding protocol, the family is asked to allow research staff to continue data collection until the end of the study (18–24 months CA). The timeframe for the feeding intervention ensures a minimum exposure period of 4 weeks and is similar to or longer than reported in the literature of three to 4 weeks or until 36 weeks CGA, thus facilitating comparison of results to other studies.24

Neurodevelopment: The primary outcome for this trial is the cognitive composite score on the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development–4th Edition (Bayley-IV) at 18–24 months CA. The language composite and motor composite scores are secondary outcomes. The Bayley scale is globally the most widely used instrument by clinicians and researchers to assess the developmental functioning of infants, toddlers and young children 1–42 months of age across cognitive, language (receptive, expressive) and motor (fine, gross) domains.61 62 Tertiary NICUs in our consortium aim to assess all infants born <1250 g using the Bayley scale at 18–24 months CA as part of routine care. Leveraging this practice and providing parents funds for transportation and a home-visit option, we have been successful in prior studies at having >90% of surviving infants assessed using the Bayley scale at 18–24 months CA.52 As the Bayley scale is frequently used in neonatal trials evaluating the efficacy of early feeding strategies for preterm infants,62 using this instrument will enable us to compare our study findings with others in the literature. Limitations of the Bayley scale include the fact that it is a global assessment tool and subtle deficits could be missed. Further, like other developmental assessment tools used at this age, the Bayley scale provides a picture of current functioning and may not be predictive of the child’s capabilities at school age; although more recent versions have shown improved predictive validity in the very preterm infant.63

The Bayley-IV maintains basic elements of the Bayley-III, its predecessor, with new tasks to increase sensitivity (eg, precursors of executive function), removal of redundant tasks to decrease administration time and the option of digital administration (some tasks) and scoring.61 62 Cognitive, language and motor composite scores are standardised to a mean of 100 with an SD of 15. Children unable to complete the Bayley-IV due to severe disability or who perform below the test threshold for individual composite scores will be assigned a score of 49.5 52 64 65 In exploratory analyses, the proportion of infants who die or have composite scores <85, defined by the Bayley-IV as ‘low average, borderline or extremely low,’ will be compared between individualised fortification methods and standard fortification.

Other secondary and exploratory outcome measures

Weight, length and HC gains (secondary outcome)

Daily weights are extracted from medical records and, to increase precision, weekly length and HC measurements are determined by research staff alongside the bedside nurse during initial NICU hospitalisation using length boards and non-stretchable tape measures.22 Growth as an outcome of the intervention will be assessed from study day 1 to 36 weeks CA or hospital discharge, whichever occurs first. Growth is also assessed at follow-up. Absolute measures will be converted to z-scores using sex-specific norms.66 67 Weight, length and HC gains in the NICU will serve as early indicators of the effectiveness of individualised fortification and are on the causal pathway to neurodevelopment, hence their inclusion as a secondary outcome.22

Body composition (secondary outcome)

In addition to assessment of BMI (kg/m2) using sex-specific reference ranges appropriate for VLBW infants,68 69 body composition is evaluated at the end of the feeding intervention (±3 days) by measuring mid-arm circumference and triceps and flank skinfold.70 71 Improving the quality of body mass accretion is fundamental to evaluating the success of a feeding intervention.72 Preterm infants demonstrate growth irregularities, such as decreased fat-free mass gain and increased adiposity as a percent of total body weight during hospitalisation and decreased linear growth for the first 2 years compared with term-born infants. Early differences in body composition resolve with provision of adequate nutrition and promote better neurodevelopment.73 At 4 months CA (±10 days), in addition to BMI and skinfold measurements, body composition is assessed using air displacement plethysmography (PEA POD, Cosmed) for a subset of infants by research staff. This non-invasive method enables assessment of fat and fat-free mass by direct measurement of body mass (ie, infant’s weight) and volume using classic densitometric principles.74 75

Serious morbidity and mortality (secondary outcome)

Serious morbidities, including late-onset sepsis (≥day 5, positive blood or cerebrospinal fluid culture), NEC (Bell stage ≥II), chronic lung disease (oxygen support at 36 weeks), ROP requiring treatment and death are collected prospectively from the medical chart during the intervention.5 Approximately half of infants born <1250 g will be diagnosed with one of these morbidities.76 Clinicians making decisions about the best way to fortify human milk from the study findings will want to see monitoring data for these morbidities as they have been associated in the literature with both feeding type (eg, mother’s milk, PDHM, formula) and/or quantity of nutrients.77 78 While serious brain injury is an exclusion criterion, events occurring after enrolment will be recorded and considered in interpreting findings from neurodevelopmental assessments. As patent ductus arteriosus may impact the volume and nutrient concentration of enteral feeds provided, this information will be collected to aid in interpretation of findings.22

Health economics (secondary outcome)

Following best practices for the collection and reporting of cost-effectiveness analyses, healthcare direct and indirect costs will be obtained from standardised cost-accounting systems and physician billing.79 The total costs incurred for in-hospital care will include the medical costs for professional salaries, enteral feed preparation, pharmacy, diagnostic imaging, laboratory services and surgery. Indirect costs to the hospitals include administration, human resources and environmental/plant operations. We recently completed a similar cost-effectiveness analysis of supplemental donor milk alongside an RCT using the aforementioned data collection approaches.6 A comprehensive protocol detailing the cost-effectiveness analyses will be completed prior to unblinding the study.

Milk type received and the energy and nutrient intakes of infants (exploratory outcome)

Data regarding the type of milk consumed (eg, mother’s, PDHM), energy and nutrient intake from parenteral and enteral nutrition, and micronutrient supplements are prospectively collected daily. Data sources include product monographs, recipes for feeds (from milk preparation rooms), volumes administered (from medical records) and energy and macronutrient values of mother’s own milk and PDHM assessed by mid-infrared analysis. These data will enable the assessment of whether individualised fortification results in macronutrient intake more consistent with recommendations and if the proportion of PDHM fed is influential, both of which will aid in the interpretation of neurodevelopment findings. Post-discharge infant feeding practices, including duration and exclusivity of breastfeeding, are collected using a validated questionnaire at 4 months CA.80

Maternal diet during lactation (exploratory outcome)

Our own work, as well as that of others, suggests that maternal diet may have a more significant impact on the macronutrient composition of human milk than previously appreciated. Thus, we are collecting data on maternal diet during the first month of the intervention. As such, the infant’s mother will be asked to complete the Canadian Diet History Questionnaire II on study day 35±3.2581,83 The aim of collecting these data is to explore whether maternal diet quality may be influential in determining whether an individualised approach to fortification of human milk is warranted.

Data management

All data are collected and entered into a PostgreSQL database with a generated customised personal home page front-end.51 The database is held on a server backed up and maintained by the Research IT group at The Hospital for Sick Children. For the primary and secondary outcomes, all data entered into the database will be double-checked against source documents.

Sample size calculation

A 5-point difference in the cognitive composite score on the Bayley scale was used to determine the proposed sample size. The literature suggests that this difference could translate into fewer children born preterm requiring special education services (with associated costs) and an improvement in longer-term academic achievement.84 Secondary data analyses from our recent donor milk RCT showed children with lipid and energy intakes during initial hospitalisation closer to recommended values had higher mean cognitive composite scores, approximately of this magnitude, compared with those with intakes in the lowest quartiles, suggesting a 5-point effect size is achievable with improved nutrition.35

Using a 5-point effect size in the cognitive composite score with an estimated SD of 15, 80% power and an alpha level of 0.025 (0.05 divided by 2 to account for comparison of two interventions against the control group), we require 175 infants in each of the three arms of the study. As we recruit infants following the highest mortality period (ie, when enteral feeds have started and are being progressed) and based on our previous work where we have been able to obtain neurodevelopmental assessment for >90% of surviving infants, we anticipate a 15% total loss to follow-up to 18–24 months CA including mortality and withdrawals5 52; hence, we will need to randomise 205 infants in each of the three feeding groups. The sample size calculation was carried out using PASS.85

Statistical analysis plan

The analysis will be conducted using an ‘intention to treat’ approach. Descriptive statistics will be calculated for all variables of interest. In order to achieve balance on key variables, the randomisation schedule is stratified by recruitment site and birth weight (<1000 g, >1000 g) and sex. If clinically relevant differences are found to exist at baseline, they will be included in adjusted models assessing study outcome measures as appropriate. Differences in outcomes between treatment groups will be assessed using linear regression models for continuous variables and logistic regression for categorical variables to test the null hypothesis of no intervention effect, first without adjustment. To improve the precision of estimates and test for potential interactions, as the sample size permits, analyses will be re-run using randomisation strata (ie,recruitment site, birth weight group (<1000 g, >1000 g)), sex, size for gestation, proportion of feeds as mother’s milk or donor milk and where appropriate clinically relevant differences found between treatment groups at baseline. Additionally, parental education will be included in models evaluating neurodevelopment. In adjusted models, we will also test for interactions between feeding allocation and other variables. If interactions are not statistically significant, they will be removed from the model, and the analysis will be re-run. The primary analysis for this intent-to-treat study will include all infants as randomised, regardless of adherence to the feeding protocol. In addition to assessing costs incurred for in-hospital care between feeding groups, a cost-effectiveness analysis will be conducted. A detailed protocol for the cost-effectiveness analysis, including statistical analyses, will be developed prior to unblinding the study.

Ethics and dissemination

The protocol for this study was designed and is conducted according to International Conference on Harmonisation and Good Clinical Practice guidelines and is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05308134). The study has been approved by Clinical Trials Ontario (Study ID 1861); the research ethics board at The Hospital for Sick Children serves as the board of record. Written informed consent is obtained from legal guardians of all participants.

The standard fortification protocol in the present study is patterned after routine care. As this is a pragmatic study, for all three arms of the study we are using the base multinutrient fortifier used in the unit where each participant is hospitalised. While each participating NICU has agreed to follow the feeding guidelines for all three arms of the study, decisions about initiation of study feeds, advancement, suspension or removal of an infant from the feeding protocol will continue to be directed by the clinical team. Growth, including weight, length and HC, is reviewed twice weekly while infants are in the intervention. If insufficient weight gain persists, even with additional fortification or increasing feed volume within the feeding guidelines, the infant is removed from the feeding intervention (see table 1).

An independent Data Safety and Monitoring Committee (DSMC) consisting of a team of experts in neonatology and nutrition who are not involved in the trial has been assembled. They convened after 200 (May 2024) and 400 (May 2025) infants completed the feeding intervention. The following descriptive data for each feeding group were provided to the DSMC: (1) serious adverse events; (2) composite of death, NEC, late onset sepsis, chronic lung disease, severe ROP, as well as individual morbidities; and (3) weight-for-age and length-for-age z-scores at the beginning and end of the feeding intervention. The DSMC could have recommended halting the study if a statistically significant difference between feeding groups was detected in the (1) mortality and morbidity composite or (2) death or the individual morbidities listed or (3) a clinically significant difference (1 SD) in z-scores was observed between feeding groups (p<0.05). A change in z-score of 1 SD is equivalent to crossing over one major percentile curve (3rd, 15th, 50th, 85th and 97th) on the Fenton revised growth chart for preterm infants.66
