“Doing the Best I Can”: Qualitative Outcomes and Participant Feedback From a Combined Communication and Counselling Treatment for Primary Progressive Aphasia
Authors: Kristin Schaffer Mendez, William S. Evans, Christina D. Dutcher, Christina Philburn, Lisa D. Wauters, Megan M. Young, Maya L. Henry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThere is a growing evidence base supporting the utility of restitutive speech‐language treatments for individuals with primary progressive aphasia (PPA). In contrast, there is limited research investigating treatment approaches that offer counselling to this population. Given that people with PPA are susceptible to negative impacts on their psychosocial well‐being, research is needed to address this underrepresented area of inquiry. This paper provides qualitative research findings from a pilot and feasibility study in which individuals with PPA completed a novel, telehealth‐based treatment program comprising both tailored speech‐language treatment and counselling (via aphasia‐modified cognitive behavioural therapy).MethodsNine participants with PPA (three with nonfluent/agrammatic variant; three with logopenic variant; three with semantic variant) completed the novel treatment program. A phenomenological analysis was used at pre‐ and post‐treatment timepoints to analyse qualitative treatment outcomes and participant‐derived feedback regarding the intervention.ResultsFollowing treatment, group‐level themes identified across both timepoints included Communication Difficulties, Loss, Communication Strategy Usage, and Value of Support Systems. Themes identified only at pre‐treatment included Negative Emotions and Social Disconnection, while those observed only at post‐treatment were Acceptance, Gratitude, and Counselling and Coping Strategy Usage. Participant‐generated feedback regarding the intervention resulted in themes of Positive Life Impact of Treatment, Satisfaction with Treatment Framework, and Satisfaction with Telerehabilitation.ConclusionQualitative results from this pilot study indicate positive/adaptive themes surrounding psychosocial functioning following treatment for participants with each PPA subtype. Furthermore, participant‐generated feedback reflects overall satisfaction with this holistic communication and counselling‐focused treatment approach, indicating treatment acceptability.WHAT THIS PAPER ADDS What is already known on this subject People with primary progressive aphasia (PPA) often experience a negative impact on their emotional well‐being and quality of life. While preliminary research indicates that these individuals may benefit from personal‐adjustment counselling, research is limited pertaining to psychosocial approaches in PPA. Thus, additional research is warranted to investigate the benefit, acceptability and feasibility of these interventions. What this paper adds to existing knowledge Preliminary qualitative results provide evidence that a novel treatment approach comprising communication‐focused intervention and aphasia‐modified cognitive‐behavioural therapy (CBT) results in positive psychosocial outcomes amongst nine individuals with heterogeneous PPA presentations. Participant‐generated feedback following treatment supports the acceptability of this combined intervention. Individuals reported satisfaction with the intervention overall and recognized the value of engaging in a program that aims to optimize both communication and emotional well‐being. What are the potential or actual clinical implications of this work? Speech‐language therapists/pathologists may consider incorporating counselling approaches to optimize a person with PPA's emotional health and holistically meet patient needs. Aphasia‐modified CBT, provided by a speech‐language therapist/pathologist, requires further investigation to ultimately determine the clinical utility of this approach.

Background

Communication is an essential aspect of the human condition; as such, a diagnosis of aphasia can impact a person's emotional well‐being in multifaceted ways (Code and Herrmann 2003). Aphasia often ushers in a redefining of roles and relationships within one's family, social network and community (Simmons‐Mackie and Kagan 2007). Thus, tailored, whole‐person approaches are needed to provide individuals with layered support that will optimize communication and overall well‐being.

Although decades of research have highlighted the psychosocial impact of stroke‐induced aphasia, there is limited literature on psychosocial functioning in primary progressive aphasia (PPA; Schaffer and Henry 2021). PPA is a language‐prominent dementia that is phenotypically and prognostically distinct from stroke‐induced aphasia. Regarding prognosis, communication functioning can be expected to decline over time for people with PPA, while communication will likely improve or stabilise for people with stroke‐induced aphasia (Holland and Nelson 2018). A greater contextualisation of the unique psychosocial factors specific to PPA is needed to understand lived experiences and, in turn, inform the development of psychosocial‐inclusive treatment approaches that may be most impactful (Schaffer and Henry 2021).

PPA Clinical Classification

Individuals with PPA initially present with impaired communication functioning, which may include deficits in language as well as speech (Mesulam 1982). With time, PPA advances to a global dementia presentation, encompassing cognitive, motoric and/or social‐behavioural impairments (Harciarek et al. 2014). Per international consensus criteria, there are three PPA phenotypes (Gorno‐Tempini et al. 2011). People with the nonfluent/agrammatic variant (nfvPPA) demonstrate core features of agrammatism and/or apraxia of speech. Individuals with the semantic PPA variant (svPPA) present with core deficits in naming and single‐word comprehension. People with the logopenic PPA variant (lvPPA) exhibit core features of impaired naming and repetition. Moreover, some individuals demonstrate unclassifiable or “mixed” PPA presentations, exhibiting features of more than one clinical variant (Tippett 2020).

PPA Psychosocial and Neuropsychiatric Factors

In addition to these core clinical features, individuals with PPA are vulnerable to psychosocial and neuropsychiatric ramifications of living with a neurodegenerative disorder. Psychosocial sequelae include low self‐esteem, reduced confidence and social isolation, as well as neuropsychiatric features such as depression, anxiety and apathy (Lo et al. 2022; Modirrousta et al. 2013; Ruggero et al. 2019). An awareness and understanding of these factors is important when considering treatment candidacy, identifying potential intervention barriers and providing impactful counselling (Schaffer and Henry 2021; Singh et al. 2015).

Counselling in PPA

A substantial number of studies support the benefits of restitutive interventions for communication in PPA (Wauters et al. 2023). However, only within the past several years have interventions for counselling in PPA emerged in the literature. Counselling has taken the form of support groups for patients and care partners (Jokel et al. 2017; Morhardt et al. 2019; Schaffer and Henry 2023) or has also been included alongside other intervention components as part of multi‐component treatment protocols, with counselling provided by social workers, clinical psychologists or speech‐language therapist/pathologists (SLT/SLPs). The multi‐component approaches include Communication Bridge, which incorporates restorative and communication participation‐based intervention components, as well as care partner training and informational counselling that is centred upon disease‐related education (Roberts et al. 2022; Rogalski et al. 2016). Another combined treatment pairs speech‐language intervention with personal‐adjustment counselling, to address the thoughts, feelings and behaviours surrounding PPA (Schaffer et al. 2021). Results from these studies indicate that counselling, whether focused on the person with PPA or their care partner, and whether delivered individually or in a group context, is feasible to implement and yields positive psychosocial outcomes when measured qualitatively and/or quantitively.

People with PPA may benefit from counselling, be it provided by a licensed counsellor, an SLT/SLP, or via interprofessional collaboration. While licensed counsellors can directly treat mood and/or anxiety disorders that may accompany a PPA diagnosis and can provide treatment that encompasses how PPA may broadly impact life, an SLT/SLP can provide counselling specific to impacts on communication. Unfortunately, people with aphasia, including PPA, may be under‐referred to licensed mental health professionals for counselling and these providers may in turn be reluctant to treat individuals with aphasia/PPA (Morrow‐Odom & Barnes, 2019; Schaffer Mendez and Gallée 2024). Thus, SLT/SLPs can seize a powerful opportunity to offer counselling in a way that these individuals may not otherwise be provided. Specifically, as communication experts, SLT/SLPs can communicate with patients using multimodal and aphasia‐friendly communication techniques to ensure that comprehension is bi‐directionally maintained.

In the Schaffer et al. (2021) study, an SLP administered counselling in the form of aphasia‐modified cognitive behavioural therapy (aphasia‐modified CBT) alongside script training to target linguistic and motor speech deficits in a person with PPA. CBT (Beck 1964) was selected because it is one of the most widely researched types of counselling, proven to be transdiagnostically effective and highly adaptable (Schaeuffele et al. 2024), with positive outcomes including symptom reduction, improved mood, enhanced quality of life, and increased use of coping skills (Fordham et al., 2021; Hoffman et al., 2012). With CBT, negative/maladaptive thoughts are identified, examined and modified, to enhance mood and behaviour more broadly (Beck 2011). To our knowledge, the Schaffer et al. (2021) paper is the only example of a study in which an SLP provided counselling centred upon the emotional ramifications of PPA. Mixed methods outcomes were positive, which indicates that this novel approach was feasible for a single participant. These pilot findings inform the focus of the current paper.

Description of the Current Study

The current study is part of a multi‐stage line of clinical research inquiry. Per the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Stage Model for behavioural intervention development, the first research phase (Stage 1A; Onken et al. 2014) involved intervention development and an initial pilot study with a single participant (Schaffer et al. 2021). Within the current Stage 1B pilot and feasibility study, different tailored speech‐language interventions were paired with counselling (i.e., aphasia‐modified CBT) for a series of participants presenting with each of the PPA variants. In this paper, the terms counselling and aphasia‐modified CBT will be used interchangeably.

The intervention was conducted from 2020 to 2022 using telehealth‐based delivery. It was not possible to hold in person sessions due to pandemic restrictions and because participant distance from the research lab rendered it unfeasible. The overarching aims of this research stage were to examine the acceptability, feasibility and preliminary mixed methods outcomes of this treatment. This paper focuses on qualitative outcomes. Inclusion of qualitative methodologies was intentional, as this type of research allows for a nuanced understanding of human behaviour and intervention responses (Creswell and Poth 2018). Information pertaining to feasibility and quantitative results is forthcoming (Schaffer Mendez et al. in press).

Aims

This paper investigates whether a telehealth‐delivered intervention comprising speech‐language treatment and counselling yields positive psychosocial outcomes for people with different PPA phenotypes. We predicted that participants would demonstrate prominent positive/adaptive themes at post‐treatment relative to pre‐treatment, captured by phenomenological analysis of semi‐structured interviews. A secondary aim was to obtain qualitative feedback from participants regarding the treatment itself, to gather perceptions of treatment acceptability and to inform further treatment protocol refinement. We predicted that a thematic analysis of responses from post‐treatment interviews would support the acceptability of this approach and would reveal aspects of the intervention that participants found valuable, as well as treatment components that may benefit from further modification.

Methods

Participants

Using purposive sampling, nine monolingual speakers of English with PPA (five females; four males) were enrolled in this study. All participants lived in the United States or Canada. The average participant age was 73 (standard deviation (SD) = 8.83; see Table 1 for other participant details). Participants were recruited via electronically distributed research flyers, the lab website, and through existing PPA referral pipelines. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Texas at Austin (Project ID: 2020010119). All participants gave informed consent to participate prior to study participation.

Race/

Ethnicity

Initially, 10 participants were enrolled. Of these participants, one individual exhibited a steep decline in cognition shortly after treatment began. Her clinical presentation required alterations to both the communication and counselling intervention components. Although the participant completed a modified intervention, the simplified treatment protocol was too different from the original protocol to be considered for inclusion. Thus, this participant was removed from the cohort. Additionally, one other individual qualified for enrolment, but elected not to participate, due to time constraints. Notably, one of the participants in this study (LV3) likely contracted COVID, requiring a 3‐week pause in treatment. She completed the study and her data are included.

Criteria for participation included a diagnosis of PPA by a neurologist, corroborated by comprehensive speech, language, and cognitive testing completed by an SLP. Additionally, participants needed to have a home‐based internet connection to enable treatment via telerehabilitation. Exclusion criteria included: concomitant neurological diagnoses (e.g., traumatic brain injury); having an acute, severe mood disorder requiring immediate intervention by a psychotherapist (score of 20 or higher on the Patient Health Questionnaire‐9 (Kroenke et al. 2001); no participants had a mood disorder); being recently prescribed psychotropic medications/not yet being stabilized on psychotropic medications for at least three months; being a non‐native speaker of English; refusing to participate in any components of the proposed intervention; scoring below 15 on the Mini Mental State Exam (Folstein et al. 1975), below 76 (the cut‐off for mild impairment) on the Western Aphasia Battery‐Revised (WAB‐R; Kertesz 2006) Aphasia Quotient, below 75 on the WAB‐R Repetition subtest, and/or below 50 on the WAB‐R Reading Comprehension of Sentences and Reading Commands subtests.

Clinician

The treating clinician (K. S. M.) was a female SLP who pursued a specialization in counselling during her doctoral studies. The clinician had completed training in CBT, grief counselling, mindfulness, and motivational interviewing, and was provided with supervision from two CBT‐trained counsellors during the study.

Experimental Design

The intervention included tailored speech‐language treatment and counselling. Every treatment session included speech‐language intervention, and a subset of sessions included counselling at the end of the session. The speech‐language treatment approach was selected based on the participant's clinical profile. Participants with nfvPPA were administered a form of script training called Video Implemented Script Training for Aphasia (Henry et al. 2018). Participants with svPPA or lvPPA were provided Lexical Retrieval Cascade Treatment, a naming intervention (Henry et al. 2019). Participants who engaged in script training completed two sessions per week for six weeks, with sessions lasting 45–60 min. Participants enrolled in Lexical Retrieval Cascade Treatment attended sessions twice weekly for between eight and 12 weeks, with sessions lasting 45–60 min. The total number of sessions was contingent upon mastery of trained words. Additional speech‐language treatment details can be found in Supplementary Material 1.

All participants were provided with aphasia‐modified CBT (Schaffer et al. 2021; see the current treatment manual at https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/HDWKE Schaffer Mendez et al. 2025), which lasted around 30 min. and was delivered at the end of select intervention sessions, following speech‐language treatment. During the first counselling session, each participant was given psychoeducation regarding the social‐emotional impact of aphasia, information about CBT and an overview of the structure of counselling sessions. Following psychoeducation, the participant and clinician collaboratively developed communication‐centred counselling goals. Subsequent counselling sessions closely followed the sequence of traditional CBT sessions (Beck 2011; see Table 2). However, the counselling was “aphasia‐modified” in two distinct ways. To facilitate comprehension of counselling concepts, multimodal aphasia‐friendly supports were offered, when appropriate. These supports included visuals and key words/phrases that were typed or drawn during the session via a virtual Whiteboard (see Figure 1). Additionally, the CBT was “aphasia‐modified” from a content perspective, as sessions were oriented towards the impact of PPA on the participant's life (see Supplementary Material 2 for an example of aphasia‐modified CBT in‐session dialogue).

Consistent with brief CBT dosage parameters (Cully and Teten 2008), aphasia‐modified CBT was formally provided during eight treatment sessions. However, if the participant exhibited emotional distress at any point during the speech‐language training portion of the session, then aphasia‐modified CBT was offered as a teachable moment.

Between treatment sessions, participants were instructed to complete daily independent practice tasks. These included practicing with their specific script or words‐in‐training as well as completing tailored CBT practice tasks. Examples of practice counselling tasks include writing in a Thought Records journal (reflecting on thoughts, feelings, and behaviours surrounding communication challenges), reading Coping Cards to counter maladaptive self‐talk, or engaging in mindfulness practices.

Qualitative Methodology

We evaluated psychosocial functioning qualitatively using a group‐level phenomenological analysis of pre‐ and post‐treatment semi‐structured interviews (Creswell and Poth 2018; Moustakas 1994). To inform iterative treatment refinement and treatment acceptability, we also analysed participant‐generated feedback regarding the intervention through additional interview questions administered only at post‐treatment. The COnsolidated criteria for REporting Qualitative research Checklist (COREQ; Tong et al. 2007) was used to ensure comprehensive reporting of qualitative methods.

The interviewer (L. D. W.) was a female SLP and doctoral student trained by the principal investigator (K. S. M.) in qualitative research and interviewing best practices. The interviewer was not the treating clinician for any participant. During each interview, only the participant and the interviewer were present. The interviewer informed the participants that the researchers were interested in hearing about their experiences with PPA, as their perspectives can help inform development of treatments that are best suited to meet their needs.

The 15–30 min. semi‐structured interviews were video‐recorded through the videoconferencing platform, Zoom. Interviews began with rapport‐building before segueing into open‐ended questions that focused on the lived experiences of each participant in managing life with PPA (see Supplementary Material 3 for the interview questions). Of note, these interview questions were previously used in the Schaffer et al. (2021) pilot study. Questions were slightly re‐worded or follow‐up questions were provided, as needed, to ensure comprehension and to obtain a comprehensive understanding of participant lived experiences. At post‐treatment, a set of 13 additional questions were administered, to obtain participant‐generated feedback on the intervention itself.

Following completion of the interviews, research assistants transcribed each interview in Microsoft Word. These transcripts were checked for accuracy by the principal investigator (K. S. M.) prior to being analysed.

Phenomenological Analysis

An iterative and inductive phenomenological research investigation was subsequently conducted to gain insight into the phenomenon of living with PPA prior to and following treatment. The sample size of nine individuals is well‐justified, per phenomenological sample size guidelines, which range from 3 to 9 individuals (Dukes 1984). To establish the credibility and validity of our findings, we used triangulation. This refers to a qualitative research method in which multiple data sources, theories and/or investigators are used to corroborate the findings (Creswell and Poth 2018). Specifically, in this study, two co‐authors with qualitative research expertise (K. S. M. and M. M. Y.) independently analysed 18 pre‐ and post‐treatment interviews using both videos and transcripts. They were not informed regarding study timepoint, in order to minimize bias. While watching the interviews, the researchers took observational field notes. Immediately afterward, the researchers bracketed their a priori assumptions or beliefs to maintain objectivity. Next, the researchers conducted horizonalization, in which they identified significant statements from the interviews, which provide insight into the phenomenon of having PPA. Those statements were entered into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. Subsequently, the researchers generated meaning units (words or phrases) from extracted significant statements to create preliminary themes. The researchers then convened and compared their results to reach a consensus on final themes for each participant. Alongside final themes, a collaboratively developed narrative description of each participant's lived experience at each timepoint was derived. Themes were subsequently entered into Dedoose, a qualitative research software program (Dedoose Version 9.2.12 2024) that facilitates group‐level phenomenological analyses. Through a recursive process of re‐reading and analysing significant statements and themes, these themes were further refined, consolidated, and organized into superordinate themes and associated subthemes (see Table 3 for further analytic details).

Theoretical perspectives that guided the research process included an ontological assumption, wherein it is acknowledged that different individuals may experience different realities, rather than there being a single reality (Moustakas 1994). Additionally, a complementary theoretical framework of postpositivism was used, whereby iterative steps are taken to collect data from multiple perspectives, from more than one investigator, and through qualitative software to assist with analysis (Creswell and Poth 2018).

Results

Pre‐ and Post‐Treatment Qualitative Results

A group‐level phenomenological analysis revealed themes that were ubiquitous, as well as those that were salient only at one timepoint. A composite description of the phenomenon of managing life with PPA is described below, along with direct quotes.

Universal Themes

Four superordinate themes were prevalent across pre‐ and post‐treatment timepoints. The first theme is Communication Difficulties. Participants described various ways that PPA impedes communication in different expressive and receptive modalities. This superordinate theme was divided into related subthemes, specific to various forms of communication (i.e., Speaking Difficulties, Auditory Comprehension Difficulties, Reading Comprehension Difficulties, Written Communication Difficulties, and Pragmatic Difficulties). Discussion

This study looks beyond the traditional restitutive speech‐language treatment standard of care in PPA to holistically address the whole person. That is, in addition to treating the core communication impairments inherent in PPA, this intervention also provides counselling to support psychosocial well‐being, in alignment with the tenets of the Life Participation Approach to Aphasia (Chapey et al. 2000). Given that existing literature in this area is sparse, not only in PPA, but also more broadly in the field of speech‐language pathology, this study contributes to a modest body of evidence supporting the implementation and value of psychosocial approaches as a complement to speech/language treatment.

Positioned as Stage 1B research per the NIH model (Onken et al. 2014), this study is an extension of earlier Stage 1A research (Schaffer et al. 2021). Drawing from the promising findings from our initial pilot study with one individual with PPA, this study offers qualitative evidence across a larger sample size of individuals with PPA, representing heterogeneous clinical subtypes. Results from our phenomenological analysis indicate a prominence of adaptive and/or positive themes following the intervention.

Per the phenomenological analysis, across pre‐ and post‐treatment timepoints, we identified that the essence of the PPA lived experience is multidimensional, involving ongoing loss and daily communication challenges that require continual effort and perseverance to face and internally process. Yet, in the context of these challenging realities, people with PPA proactively manage their communication difficulties through an array of strategies. They also recognize and cherish the myriad types of support they receive from their families, friends, and those within their community. Before engaging in a speech‐language and counselling‐centred treatment program, people with PPA acknowledged that negative emotions prevailed in their minds. Relatedly, they felt socially isolated from others, unable to foster interpersonal connection with ease. Yet, after completing their course of speech‐language and counselling intervention, people with PPA reached a place of understanding and acceptance of their diagnosis. They adopted a mindset of gratitude, giving thanks to the life they have led, while being mindfully aware of positive factors in the present moment. When moments of stress, unease, or maladaptive thoughts arose, people with PPA knew how to utilize coping strategies, which enabled them to feel better and behave in ways that were aligned with their life values.

From a clinical standpoint, these qualitative results suggest that the provision of counselling in a short‐term format by an SLT/SLP can contribute to positive shifts in a person with PPA's mindset and emotional well‐being. Aphasia‐modified CBT was a component of the intervention in this study, but it was not the sole focus. Relatedly, counselling was not formally provided every session. This schedule holds ecological validity, as licensed SLT/SLPs who work with people with PPA typically spend most of their treatment sessions engaged in speech/language intervention rather than counselling.

Notably, the treating SLP in this study pursued a specialization in counselling during her doctoral studies. This level of training was instrumental in laying a strong foundation for this research and informing the modification of CBT for those with PPA. However, obtaining a specialization in counselling is not expected for practicing SLT/SLPs. The authors envision that, ultimately, 1–2 day aphasia‐modified CBT trainings may be offered to provide clinicians with the skills to integrate this approach into clinical practice.

Importantly, this intervention was conducted using a telehealth platform. Offering service delivery models beyond the traditional face‐to‐face modality holds strong ecological validity and provides increased accessibility to treatment. That is, due to factors such as geographical limitations, transportation challenges, and/or health concerns, meeting a clinician for in‐person sessions may not always be feasible. This study contributes to a small evidence base (Dial et al. 2019; Meyer et al. 2016; Rogalski et al. 2016; Schaffer et al. 2020; Schaffer et al. 2021), which unequivocally supports the utility of this service‐delivery platform in PPA. That said, for future stages of research, it will be important to investigate outcomes for both in‐person and virtual treatment.

Other Considerations

Qualitative Methodology

Some caveats and considerations regarding the qualitative research methodology merit discussion. Following the interview, we did not return typed interview transcripts to the participants to provide an opportunity for them to offer additional comments or modifications to their original statements. We also did not share our phenomenological findings with the participants. We made this decision in the interest of minimizing pre‐ and post‐treatment testing burden and fatigue. In future iterations of this research, we will consider incorporating these additional steps, to ensure that interview transcripts are truly reflective of the information that the participants wish to convey.

Participant Consideration

Participant LV3 likely had COVID during the study. Despite the disruption to treatment and the emergence of cognitive issues related to her illness, she still responded positively to treatment. That said, her responses at post‐treatment were more brief than those of other participants.

Participant‐Generated Feedback Regarding the Intervention

Analyses of participant‐generated feedback indicate a resounding consensus across participants that this intervention is beneficial, thus supporting the acceptability of this approach. However, a subset of individuals provided suggestions for further enhancement of the treatment experience. Considering that some respondents noted that schedules were busy and that technological support was required, some future adjustments may be made to the treatment protocol (Schaffer Mendez et al. 2025). Before enrolment, the clinician can provide more details regarding the time investment required to participate. The clinician can meet with the prospective participant and family members to jointly discuss the best way to manage schedules, as well as how to optimally support the individual with PPA through the treatment program. If the individual decides to enrol in telerehabilitation and they are not technologically savvy, a discussion regarding how best to navigate the provision of technological support may be incorporated. For example, clinicians or family members can use an application such as TeamViewer, so that they can remotely log into the participant's device to access email, the Zoom platform or independent practice tasks.

Care Partner‐Directed Informal Feedback

Although care partners were not formally included in this study, they anecdotally expressed noteworthy comments. Some care partners reported that treatment sessions were long and speculated that their family member with PPA may have benefited from shorter sessions. Moreover, a few care partners expressed that personality and cultural background may play a role in receptiveness to counselling. One care partner reported that their spouse with PPA demonstrated a long‐standing temperament of not being attuned to or concerned with their feelings. Two other care partners commented that their family member with PPA's cultural heritage, in which being practical and stoic was highly valued, was in opposition to opening up about feelings. Another care partner noted that generational attitudes and a fixed mindset against certain counselling components (e.g., mindfulness exercises) were likely present. This information is important to investigate further in the future, to identify optimal dosage, culturally responsive counselling considerations, and treatment candidacy considerations. Additionally, future studies should enrol care partners to enable formal gathering of qualitative data specific to the perspectives of close others.

Future Directions

This qualitative study, conducted as part of a pilot and feasibility study, represents an early‐stage research endeavour. Findings regarding the feasibility and preliminary quantitative benefit of this intervention will be presented in a forthcoming paper (Schaffer Mendez et al. in press). Within the framework of a programmatic research agenda, the next steps will be to investigate the efficacy of this novel approach in a controlled research environment (Stage II in the NIH stage model; Onken et al. 2014) and in clinical settings (Stage III in the NIH stage model). Through these future research stages, responsiveness to treatment will be established, such that practicing SLTs/SLPs will have evidence regarding the efficacy and application of this holistic treatment approach in clinical practice.

Conclusion

Given the knowledge that PPA negatively impacts emotional well‐being and generalized health states of individuals affected by this disorder, it is imperative that treatment frameworks are developed to account for and sensitively address these vulnerabilities. This study contributes to this underrepresented area of inquiry, providing promising qualitative evidence in support of the application of aphasia‐modified CBT interwoven into speech‐language pathology treatment paradigms for people with different PPA subtypes. Given these positive outcomes, further research is needed via an efficacy trial to determine the clinical utility of this intervention.
