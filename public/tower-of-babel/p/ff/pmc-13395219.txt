Comments on evolution of transcranial magnetic stimulation services in psychiatry in India
Authors: Gurvinder Pal Singh, Khushpreet Kaur, Mahak Aggarwal, Pratiksha Bugalia
Published: 2026

Sir,

We appreciate the efforts of the author in describing the journey of transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) service delivery in our country.[1] The author highlighted the utility of TMS intervention in India in various clinical conditions. The promising intervention with TMS in treatment-resistant cases has been depicted in the article. This timely summary of three decades of work aligns with earlier Indian reviews that similarly recognized TMS as a growing neuromodulation modality in the country.[2] The author’s effort in compiling historical, clinical, and organizational developments is commendable and valuable for clinicians and policy planners. Despite these advances this article has certain lacunae need further elaboration and discussion as below:

Primarily, the article is silent about the financial implications of such interventions and such modalities are localized to metropolitan cities that are mostly centers of Excellence and have abundance of financial budgets. The significant affordability barriers persist in ground reality in developing countries like India. TMS equipment and its required setup remain expensive, making it inaccessible to most patients in low-resource settings.[3] Only a limited number of centers in India can afford or sustain such installations.[2] High treatment costs and lack of standardized pricing further widen the service gap and restrict routine use. In international arena some of the socioeconomic studies have revealed positive findings related with its low cost and effectiveness in outdoor services without need of anesthesia team network.[456]

Secondly, infrastructure limitations are another obstacle. Advanced systems such as Deep-TMS with H-coils are available only in few centers; a recent industry report indicated that only one major chain in India had acquired multiple Deep-TMS units.[7] Many institutions lack the dedicated physical space, coil-cooling systems, shielding, and trained technical manpower required for safe TMS operation.[8] These barriers highlight the need for structured capacity building. Moreover, the technicality associated with the implementation of TMS intervention needs trained manpower, technical and infrastructural requirements, training framework, and considerable material investment.[7]

Thirdly, integration of TMS with pharmacological treatment protocols remains inconsistent. Evidence from Indian clinical studies shows that TMS is typically used as an adjunct to antidepressants,[9] yet uniform guidelines on sequencing, dosage adjustment, or combined treatment planning are lacking. Inconsistent integration can dilute expected clinical benefits and create variability in patient outcomes. Further the comparative data of TMS from other modalities like ECT and psychological intervention is another serious limitation of this review. In current time many new institutes of national importance are being established in many states where such highly costly equipment’s are being bought primarily in the service delivery and posing many challenges in its implementation. The long-term efficacy studies of TMS were lacking in this area.

Lastly, the clinical indications for the TMS intervention are not specified in the review beyond major depressive disorders and obsessive-compulsive disorders. The position statement regarding TMS intervention by various recognized Indian societies would have provided strength to such review. However, such type of debatable reviews was thought provoking and were commendable. These concerns gain importance considering India’s substantial mental-health treatment gap, with surveys indicating that 70–85% of individuals with psychiatric disorders receive no treatment.[9] With only ~0.75 psychiatrists per 100,000 population[10] and limited access to specialized neuromodulation tools, TMS must be positioned within an integrated care framework alongside medications and psychosocial interventions rather than as an isolated specialty service. Without this integration, its impact on public mental health remains limited.
