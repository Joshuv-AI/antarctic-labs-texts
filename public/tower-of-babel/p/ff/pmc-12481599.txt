Fontan-Associated Diastolic Dysfunction and Bile Acids
Authors: Ashish H. Shah
Published: 2025

I read with great interest the article by Khayyat et al,1 which thoughtfully explores the emerging concept of diastolic dysfunction in Fontan patients. I commend the authors for addressing this important area and would like to highlight 2 additional considerations that may enrich the discourse and prove valuable for clinicians managing this complex population.

First, although Fontan patients have a primary cardiac abnormality, they also exhibit progressive multisystem dysfunction, including hepatic fibrosis and a blunted hemodynamic response to exercise. This pattern parallels cirrhotic cardiomyopathy, which is characterized by impaired cardiac stress response and combined diastolic and systolic dysfunction, albeit in the absence of intrinsic heart disease. Elevated circulating bile acids (BAs) are among the proposed etiologies for cirrhotic cardiomyopathy.2 Recently, we reported elevated BAs in Fontan patients and their association with Fontan-related pathophysiology.3 Mechanistically, excess BAs impair mitochondrial ATP production and fatty acid oxidation within cardiomyocytes, contributing to diastolic dysfunction. They also activate pro-inflammatory signaling and matrix metalloproteinase expression pathways that can independently impair diastolic function. Notably, intestinal BA sequestration has been shown to reverse diastolic impairment in experimental models.4

Second, while the authors classify Filing Fontan hemodynamics into 4 phenotypes (systolic dysfunction, diastolic dysfunction, normal hemodynamics, and lymphatic abnormalities), clinical presentation often reflects overlap among these categories. Ohuchi et al (2017) proposed a hemodynamic classification using cardiac index (<3 and ≥3 L/min/m2) and Fontan pressure (<14 and <and ≥14 mm Hg), identifying a subgroup with high cardiac index and elevated Fontan pressure as having the worst survival. Patients with cirrhotic cardiomyopathy may exhibit abnormally high cardiac output or high-output heart failure. Similarly, we have reported elevated cardiac output state in a subset of Fontan patients,5 underscoring the need for more nuanced subclassification strategies within this population.

Over the past decade, BAs have emerged as critical mediators beyond their traditional role as emulsifiers, contributing to cardiac dysfunction, arrhythmia, hepatic fibrosis, and skeletal muscle wasting. Interestingly, ursodeoxycholic acid has shown potential in mitigating cardiac and hepatic fibrosis, reducing hepatocellular carcinoma risk, and improving overall survival in Fontan patients. Given this expanding landscape, it is imperative to explore systemic biochemical perturbations such as BA dysregulation as unifying mechanisms of multisystem dysfunction in the Fontan population. This could pave the way for novel diagnostic markers and therapeutic interventions.
