Optimized deep learning framework for pomegranate disease detection using nature-inspired algorithms
Authors: Anil Sandhi, Rajeev Kumar, Reeta Bhardwaj, Dinesh Kumar, Arun Kumar Rana, Olubunmi Ajala, A. Deepak, Ayodeji Olalekan Salau
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAgriculture plays a pivotal role in global food security and socio-economic stability, yet crop productivity remains threatened by plant diseases that incur substantial economic losses. Pomegranate is an important fruit for both nutrition and business, but it is easily infected by pathogens that can lower yields by 20 to 40 percent. Traditional methods of finding these pathogens by hand are time-consuming, subjective, and not very effective, while existing deep learning models struggle with field noise, lighting variations, and computational inefficiency. To address these challenges, this study proposes an automated framework integrating a modified ResNet101 architecture with a Hybrid Genetic Algorithm-Particle Swarm Optimization (HGA-PSO) method. The approach employs dual-stream processing of original and noise-augmented images (Gaussian, salt-and-pepper, speckle) to enhance robustness.ResultsThe framework achieved exceptional performance on a dataset of 5,000 images across five classes (four diseases, one healthy). Feature fusion from dual streams and HGA-PSO optimization reduced dimensionality by 50–70% while preserving discriminative power. Under rigorous 5-fold cross-validation, the Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) classifier attained 99.10% accuracy, a perfect ROC-AUC score (1.00), and high precision-recall metrics. Confusion matrices revealed near-zero misclassification, and real-world tests (single/batch images) confirmed strong generalization. Grad-CAM + + visualizations validated precise localization of disease regions. The model outperformed existing techniques (e.g., PSO-YOLOv8: 98.86%, Transformer models: 93.13%) in accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-scoreConclusionsThis research presents an optimized model for pomegranate disease detection by combining deep learning with nature inspired optimization. The dual-stream feature fusion and HGA-PSO significantly improves robustness again environment variability while reducing computation overhead. This framework offers a scalable solution for precision agriculture, enabling early disease intervention to mitigate economic losses. Future research could improve scalability and usefulness by looking into lightweight optimization methods, model interpretability, and how they can be used in limited-resource agricultural settings.

Introduction

Cultivation is crucial in agricultural output for maintaining food security and sustainable development, greatly impacting the livelihoods of more than 25% of the global population [1]. As a principal source of revenue for developing countries and a vital sector in developed economies, its productivity significantly impacts socio-economic development, trade balances, and poverty reduction [2]. Still, biotic stresses, especially plant diseases, constantly threaten agricultural production. These stresses hurt crop health, lower the quality of yields, and cause annual economic losses of over $220 billion around the world [3]. Pathogens, including fungi, bacteria, and viruses, get into plants at different stages of development, and they are often hard to find quickly because of the limitations of current diagnostic methods [4]. These manual methods, which depend on expert visual evaluation, are time-consuming, subjective, and not good for large-scale farming. They also make it more likely that diseases will spread without being stopped [5].

Pomegranate (Punica granatum) is a significant high-value crop due to its health benefits, medicinal applications, and marketability [6]. India is among the foremost global producers of pomegranates, cultivated in arid and semi-arid regions [7]. Maharashtra now dominates pomegranate cultivation, including thousands of hectares, succeeded by Karnataka, Gujarat, and Andhra Pradesh [8]. The National Horticulture Board (NHB) indicates that Maharashtra cultivates pomegranates throughout 90,000 hectares, resulting in an annual output of 945,000 metric tons. Maharashtra constitutes 78% of India’s total land and 84% of its overall production [9]. Different types of infections, such as Alternaria, Anthracnose, Bacterial Blight, and Cercospora, can really hurt pomegranate orchards [10, 11]. These infections show up as leaf spots, fruit rot, and discolored vascular tissue. Untreated infections cause output losses of 20–40%, making fruits unmarketable and upsetting supply networks. Timely and precise disease identification is essential to reduce these losses; yet, current methods lack the scale and accuracy necessary for contemporary agronomy [12, 13].

Computer vision (CV) and deep learning (DL) have become crucial in precision agriculture because they allow pictures to be automatically and non-invasively categorized to find diseases [14]. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), distinguished for their hierarchical feature extraction abilities, have seen significant success in plant pathology [15]. Still, there are big problems with putting this idea into practice, images taken in the field often have noise, occlusions, and uneven lighting; also, the large feature spaces created by deep networks cause unnecessary repetition, higher computing needs, and the risk of overfitting. Conventional deep learning models that were only trained on clean datasets don’t work well in noisy agricultural settings, which limits how useful they are in real life. Traditional CNN models (e.g., ResNet50 [16, 17], VGG [18]) and Transformers [19] struggle with real-world agricultural images corrupted by noise (e.g., Gaussian, salt-and-pepper, speckle), uneven lighting, and occlusions. This reduces their robustness in field deployments. Models trained on clean datasets fail to generalize to noisy or variable field conditions, leading to misclassifications and reduced accuracy. For instance, CNNs often overfit to pristine lab-collected data, compromising real-world applicability. Deep architectures (e.g., Vision Transformers) require extensive computational resources, making them impractical for resource-constrained agricultural settings (e.g., edge devices) [20]. Deep networks extract high-dimensional features with significant redundancy, increasing overfitting risks and computational costs without proportional accuracy gains. Existing models lack explicit mechanisms to learn noise-invariant features, limiting their diagnostic reliability under environmental variability [21].

However, certain limitations are inherent in optimization algorithms, which may affect their performance and generalizability in complex problem spaces. Such as, Artificial Bee Colony (ABC) [22, 23] and Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) [24, 25] lack adaptive mechanisms to balance global search (exploration) and local refinement (exploitation), leading to suboptimal feature subsets. All algorithms suffer in 4096-D feature spaces: ABC’s random walks, Genetic Algorithm (GA) slow evolution, and PSO’s velocity updates become computationally prohibitive. Performance heavily depends on manual tuning (e.g., PSO’s inertia weight, GA’s mutation rate), which is non-trivial in large-scale problems. Conventional methods fail to eliminate redundant features while preserving discriminative power, resulting in bloated, inefficient models. ABC [26] relies on random perturbations, often stagnating in local optima in high-dimensional spaces. GA [27] slow convergence due to excessive exploration; mutation/crossover may discard high-fitness solutions. PSO over-emphasizes exploitation (social learning), causing early convergence without global exploration [28].

To address these problems, we proposed CNN-HGA-PSO model that accurately classify pomegranate diseases. It combines a modified CNN model (i.e. ResNet101 architecture) with a Hybrid genetic algorithm with particle swarm optimization (HGA-PSO). This study modifies the ResNet101 backbone to apply transfer learning, extracting unique features from both original and noise-added pictures. This is done by simulating Gaussian noise, salt-and-pepper distortion, and speckle interference that happen in real life. When the feature vectors from these two streams are put together, they make a fused representation with complementary information that doesn’t change when the environment does. By balancing genetic processes that look for new things (crossover, mutation) with swarm intelligence that learns from others (social learning), the HGA-PSO algorithm, which is a mix of the GA and PSO algorithms, makes feature selection better. This hybrid method gets rid of features that aren’t needed, lowers the number of dimensions by 50–70%, and makes the model easier to understand while keeping its ability to discriminate. A Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) classifies the refined features using a dataset of 5,093 photos divided into five categories (four unhealthy and one healthy).

By combining noisy agricultural data with powerful deep learning, this method offers a scalable way to find illnesses early on. This lets farmers start treatments on time. The new way of doing things includes using noise-augmented training, feature fusion, and hybrid optimization. These improvements extend beyond pomegranate pathology and will enhance farming overall. This research tackles a significant agricultural issue and facilitates AI-driven sustainability in global food systems. This study improves precision agriculture by providing effective and accurate solutions for automated disease identification. To make the proposed model more useful in agricultural systems, more research may be done in this area to look into optimization methods, how easy it is to understand the model, and how it can be used in the real world.

This study examines the use of modified ResNet101 and HGA-PSO for diagnosing plant illnesses, utilizing a dataset of pomegranate fruit diseases, with the objective of integrating these methods into a future smart agricultural system. The study is structured into many main components, including a literature review Sect. 2 to support this research. The problem definition & the contributions of our paper is defined in Sect. 3. The paper includes a comprehensive methodology Sect. 4, the exposition and analysis of outcomes, and a definitive summation. The experimental plan used in this study is explained in the methodology section, which includes the dataset, data pre-processing, and the suggested model. The results and discussion Sect. 5, talks about the experiments that were done to see how well the proposed model worked at finding plant diseases and what those results mean for farming. The conclusion consolidates the primary results, examines their wider implications in Sect. 6, and proposes directions for future research. This organized approach seeks to enhance plant disease detection technologies and promote sustainable agriculture practices.

Literature review

Literature review section examines the current work to our subject area that evaluating several methods, strategies, and frameworks. This review provides a robust basis for comprehending the current advancements and positioning our suggested methodology within the larger research context. The key points of literature review are mentioned in Table 1.

Machine learning

In order to identify and classify disorders in pomegranate leaves across five disease categories, the author [29] presented a system for doing so using machine learning (multi-class SVM) and image processing (K-means clustering for segmentation). The proposed method achieved 98.39% accuracy in distinguishing diseased from healthy pomegranate leaves and 98.07% overall accuracy in classifying specific diseases. In order to detect cardiovascular disease in its early stages, authors [30] proposed snake optimization algorithm-based feature selection framework. Five machine learning methods are applied to pick and classify valid medical data efficiently. The final output is a model that detects CVD with a remarkable 99.9% accuracy.

Deep learning

The authors [31] proposed a non-destructive method that uses deep learning and hyperspectral imaging (HSI) to differentiate between frozen and healthy pomegranates. They captured images of 70 pomegranates pre- and post-freezing, augmented data to address class imbalance, and tested six models (ResNeXt, RegNetX/Y, EfficientNetV2, VisionTransformer, SwinTransformer). Results showed 99% accuracy across all models, with EfficientNetV2 achieving near-perfect precision (100%) and the highest F1-score (0.9995), significantly outperforming prior frozen-fruit detection methods (98.86% accuracy). Using ResNet-50, the authors of the paper [32] have suggested a deep learning-based approach for plant disease detection. The study’s main goal is to employ image-based methods to automatically identify and categorize rice plant illnesses. The authors used ResNet [33] to propose a deep learning-based approach for identifying leaf diseases. The goal of the study is to precisely identify and categorize diseases of apple leaves. They employed data augmentation to enhance the dataset diversity and utilized transfer learning from ImageNet-pre-trained weights to improve model efficiency. After fine-tuning parameters, their ResNet-18 model achieved 97% accuracy in disease classification, effectively addressing overfitting through augmentation but requiring high-configuration hardware due to the model’s complexity. The authors [34] combined Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) with Subspace-based feature extraction to offer a novel deep learning framework for hyperspectral image categorization. The classification overall accuracy of ca. 98.1% and 98.3% were obtained in Indian Pine and Pavia University respectively. To address the challenge of training extremely deep neural networks for image recognition, the authors of the paper [35] offer a deep residual learning framework. the authors achieved a top-5 error of 3.57% on ImageNet using an ensemble, won 1st place in ILSVRC 2015 classification. The authors of the publication [36] put forth a classification system for pomegranate fruit diseases. In order to identify and categorize illnesses in real-world settings, the author employed CNN architectures (ResNet50, ResNet18, and Inception-V3). Based on experimental comparison of CNN architectures on real-field pomegranate images, ResNet50 achieved superior disease detection accuracy of 97.92%, outperforming ResNet18 (87.5%) and Inception-V3 (78.75%) under optimized hyperparameters (learning rate: 0.001, 220 epochs). In order to raise crop output, improve quality, and make better use of resources, the author of paper [37] proposes a CRnet technique based on transfer learning to identify the many stages of pomegranate development. The model presented in this study demonstrates exceptional performance, achieving a 98% accuracy across key metrics such as recall, f1, and precision. In the paper [38], the authors propose a hybrid deep learning model that integrates ResNet, DenseNet, and EfficientNet with Mobile Inverted Bottleneck Convolutions (MBConv), Squeeze-and-Excitation blocks, and residual pathways to enhance feature extraction, gradient flow, and generalization. Using apple, guava, and mango disease datasets, the model achieved near-perfect accuracy (99.99% for apple, 99.60% for guava, 99.68% for mango), significantly outperforming existing methods. This demonstrates its robustness, scalability, and real-time applicability in smart farming. The authors [39], proposed using an unsupervised generative adversarial network (GAN) to generate controllable, nature-inspired material designs beyond biomimicry. the results demonstrate that an unsupervised StyleGAN model, trained on unlabeled leaf microstructure images, successfully generates novel architected materials with controllable hierarchical features, enables the creation of 2D/3D gradient structures through latent space manipulation. Targeting fruit plants like peaches and strawberries, the author of the publication [40] has put forth a unique method for the early and automated diagnosis of plant diseases in order to provide accurate crop protection. The simulation results were highly promising, numerically YOLOv3 model achieved 97% accuracy and a Mean Average Precision (mAP) of 92%, within a total detection time of 105 s. In comparison, the YOLOv4 model outperformed, with a 98% accuracy and an impressive mean average precision of 98%, all while completing the detection process in just 29. In order to improve the detection of kiwifruit diseases such as soft rot and bacterial canker, the researchers [41] suggested applying deep learning, specifically assessing eight sophisticated Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) designs. the model demonstrated rapid convergence and robust performance, achieving over 99% accuracy within five epochs, with only 1.37 M parameters and 0.04G FLOPs. A novel fuzzy deep convolutional neural network (CNN) architecture has been presented by the author [42] with the specific goal of detecting cucumber plant diseases using remotely sensed data. The architecture achieves 98% classification accuracy, outperforming leading models like VGG-19, DarkNet-19, and ResNet-50. In paper [43], the author has proposed using YOLOv11 for automated tomato leaf disease recognition. Among them, the model demonstrated a fitness score of 0.99268 (a 0.39% improvement), with a precision of 0.99190 (0.09%), a recall of 0.99348 (0.76%), and a mAP@0.5 of 0.99262 (0.07%). In order to overcome issues with occlusion, color similarity, and small size during automated picking, the authors of the paper [44] suggested TL-YOLOv8, an improved blueberry fruit detection algorithm based on YOLOv8. The method achieved 84.6% Precision, 91.3% recall and 94.1% mAP on the blueberry dataset, where mAP was improved by 3.4% over the original algorithm. Based on YOLOV5, the authors of the publication [45] suggested YOLOV5-CBAM-C3TR, an improved model for identifying apple leaf diseases. In addition, YOLOV5-CBAM-C3TR can achieve an average accuracy of 92.4% in detecting 208 randomly selected apple leaf disease samples. In the study [46], the authors used Deep Spectral Generative Adversarial Networks (DSGANs) to develop an automated system for classifying apple diseases. With a focus on foliar diseases of potatoes and tomatoes, the authors of the research [47] suggested assessing the generalization capacity of deep learning (DL) models for recognizing plant diseases across various datasets and environmental circumstances in India. The outcomes revealed that the DenseNet169 model exhibited superior performance. In paper [48], authors proposed cashew nut and fruit disease detector by using small size CNN and modified MobilenetV2. The CAS-MODMOBNET reached an average accuracy of 99.8% and an Area under the ROC Curve (AUC) of 1.0 on the Test subset. In to detect tea leaf disease authors [49], proposed SOTA model. The method demonstrated outstanding performance with a 2-fold average accuracy of 99.5%, an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 1.0, and a p value of 0.001. The Authors [50], proposed classification model by using modified MobileNet with leakey ReLU and LSTM technique. The MobileNet-LSTM-SMOTE approach demonstrated strong performance, achieving a Kappa score of 98.6% and a 5-fold average Kappa score of 97.9%. Additionally, testing on an independent dataset confirmed the model’s robustness and generalizability. In order to early detect of black fungus, the author [51], proposed a model of deep learning techniques for medical diagnosis. The MCBF dataset contains images that aid in training and validation when using deep learning algorithms to recognise and classify black fungus diseases, then we apply a resnet(50) pretrained model by using MCBF accuracy reaches 96.12%.

Deep learning with nature inspired algorithm

A Deep Convolutional Neural Network (DCNN) enhanced by Artificial Bee Colony Optimization (ABCO) is described by the author in paper [23]. Plant diseases can be more precisely and accurately identified and categorized with the help of this network. Based on the benchmark plant leaf disease dataset, the proposed DCNN-ABCO model achieved superior performance with 92.07% accuracy, 91.99% precision, and 92.51% recall, outperforming existing methods (SVM, SGD, BPNN, CNN) by optimizing hyperparameters via Artificial Bee Colony and leveraging deep convolutional feature extraction enhanced with Haralick texture augmentation. The authors [52] suggest a hybrid optimization technique that optimizes Support Vector Machine (SVM) parameters for precise early detection of tomato plant illnesses by combining a Fruit Fly Optimization Algorithm (FOA) with Simulated Annealing (SA). In addition, the operational efficiency of the proposed system was measured on statistical parameters like accuracy (91.1%), sensitivity (96.7%), precision (91.8%), specificity (91.2%), and F1-score (94.5%). In paper [53], the authors proposed greylag goose optimization algorithm and long short-term memory for classify heart diseases. the findings indicated that the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) emerged as the most effective classifier, achieving an accuracy rate of 91.79%. The hyperparameter of the LSTM model is tuned using GGO, and the outcome is compared to six alternative optimizers. The GGO with LSTM model obtained the highest performance, with an accuracy rate of 99.58%. In paper [54], the author proposed a Modified Al-Biruni Earth Radius (MBER) algorithm to enhance feature selection for classifying EEG-based eye states (open/closed), comparing it against BER, PSO, WOA, GWO, and GA using statistical tests and visualizations. MBER outperformed the other optimization algorithms on most unimodal benchmark functions. When used to optimize features for a KNN classifier, the MBER-based approach achieved the highest eye state classification accuracy of 96.12%, along with top scores in precision, recall, F-score, and specificity. In the paper [55], the author proposed a robust deep learning model combining transfer learning (using ResNet and Inception CNNs fine-tuned on mammograms) and Grey Wolf Optimization (GWO for feature selection on the WBCD) to enhance breast cancer diagnosis. Their ensemble method achieved high performance on large-scale datasets: Precision (0.942), Sensitivity (0.982), Accuracy (0.965), and AUC (0.971), demonstrating improved robustness, generalization, and detection rates while reducing false positives/negatives.

Nature inspired algorithm

In order to improve multihop routing in Wireless Sensor Networks (WSN) and reduce energy consumption and network lifetime, the authors [56] present a novel technique called Genetical Swarm Optimization (GSO), which combines the GA with particle PSO. The authors present HGA-ACTF [57], a novel approach that combines a compression strategy created especially for algorithms that use leading zeros in floating-point time series data with a parameter optimizer based on heuristic genetic algorithms. Experimental results indicate that the HGA-ACTF algorithm achieves an average compression ratio improvement of 38.87%, with some datasets showing improvements of up to 54.36%. In order to identify and non-destructively detect nine common tomato leaf diseases, the authors of the paper [58] suggested a machine vision system that uses texture analysis (GLCM, GLRM, and LBP features) and machine learning classifiers (LDA, ANN, KNN, and SVM). The SVM model with the cubic kernel function yielded the best results, which had the accuracy of 97.43% and 91.38% in training and test steps. The sensitivity and specificity of this modelling were 99.46% and 95.59% as well as 97.91% and 86.75% for training and test datasets, respectively. In paper [59], authors proposed breadth-first search algorithm for classify Orthopedic diseases. The study uses binary breadth-first search (BBFS), binary particle swarm optimization (BPSO), binary grey wolf optimizer (BGWO), and binary whale optimization algorithm (BWAO) for feature selections, and the BBFS makes an average error of 47.29% less than others. Then we apply six machine learning models, i.e., RF, SGD, NBC, DC, QDA, and ET. Through experimentation, the RF model led to optimal outcomes during comparison to the remaining models, with an accuracy of 91.4%.

Machine learning with nature inspired algorithm

The authors of the research [60] suggested employing Support Vector Machines (SVMs) in conjunction with optimization and ensemble approaches to categorize wheat genotypes. The radial basis function kernel achieves the highest accuracy at 93.2%, and the weighted accuracy ensemble further improves it to 94.9%. In the paper [61], the authors proposed an enhanced WaveNet-based model optimized for temporal audio features, a Real-Time Adaptation Framework for resource-constrained devices, and explainability techniques (SHAP, Grad-CAM) for automated dysarthria detection using the TORGO and Russian-voice datasets. Their optimized WaveNet model significantly outperformed other models (including Random Forest, XGBoost, and CNN-LSTM), achieving high Precision (0.92040), Recall (0.91979), F-score (0.91965), and MCC (0.89320). In the paper [62], the author proposed a new biologically inspired sound classification method featuring a novel hand-modeled architecture using a Lateral Geniculate Nucleus Pattern (LGNPat) for feature extraction, combined with statistical moments and DWT, followed by iterative feature selection (INCA) and kNN classification, applied to their new balanced ESC dataset (50 classes, 5000 sounds). Their proposed LGNPat-based model achieved a high classification accuracy of 93.34% on the collected ESC dataset when evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation.

Machine learning and deep learning

To increase agricultural productivity and quality, the authors of paper [63] present a web-based technique for pomegranate fruit disease identification. Based on the research paper, the proposed machine learning model combining Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) for feature extraction and clustering with Support Vector Machines (SVM) for classification achieved 98.38% accuracy in detecting diseases (Butterfly Pomegranate, Bitter Rot, Bacterial Blight) and healthy states in pomegranate fruits using a web-based image analysis tool.

In order to improve crop health and productivity, the authors [3] suggest a deep learning classification system for pomegranate fruit disease diagnosis. The ANN model with wavelet features is the optimal approach for pomegranate quality assessment, achieving 92.65% accuracy. The author of the research [64] has suggested a hybrid model that uses pictures and fuzzy logic and neural networks to identify Xanthomonas campestris symptoms in 15 important crops grown in a variety of environmental settings. The model achieved an accuracy of 93.81%, demonstrating robustness against variations in lighting, shadows, and capture angles. CitrusDiseaseNet, an automated system for identifying diseases of citrus fruits and leaves, is proposed by the author in paper [65]. From the quantitative analysis, the proposed method achieved the accuracy rate of 98.9% and precision rate of 98.3%, recall rate of 98%, F1-score rate of 97.9% and specificity of 98.2%. In order to predict mechanical ventilation and associate mortality rate, the author [66] proposed multitask and multilayered prediction model. The proposed model consists of two layers: Layer 1 predicts ventilation and mortality in the ICU, while Layer 2 predicts the duration of ventilation. The best results were obtained when utilizing the first 24 h for prediction. The proposed MTL model achieved promising performance of 0.944, 0.923, 0.951, and 0.921 for the first task and 0.971, 0.961, 0.963, and 0.970 for the second task for accuracy, precision, recall, score, and AUC, respectively. In the paper [67], the author proposed a low-complexity feature engineering model using 1D/2D Local Binary Patterns (LBP) and Iterative Neighborhood Component Analysis (INCA) for feature selection, applied to novel, cost-effective scattergram images derived from blood tests, to automate Schizophrenia (SZ) detection. Their model, incorporating iterative hard majority voting (IHMV) for classification fusion, achieved high accuracies of 89.29% and 90.58% on two scattergram image datasets. In paper [68], the author has proposed a hybrid diagnostic and classification model for fruit disorder detection by combining Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) with K-Nearest Neighbors (KNNs).

Problem definition and objectives

Reducing agricultural losses and guaranteeing sustainable crop yields depend on pomegranate diseases being correctly and appropriately identified. Because experts have to look at the symptoms visually, manual diagnosis is often subjective, time-consuming, and prone to mistakes. This is especially true when the symptoms change depending on the environment. While deep learning (DL) models like CNNs offer automation potential, they struggle in real-world agricultural settings due to sensitivity to field artifacts (e.g., Gaussian/salt-and-pepper/speckle noise, uneven lighting, and occlusions), poor generalization from clean lab data to noisy field conditions, and high computational demands that hinder deployment in resource-constrained environments. Existing DL architectures (e.g., ResNet, Transformers) also suffer from feature redundancy, increasing overfitting risks without proportional accuracy gains, and lack explicit mechanisms for noise-invariant feature learning. Furthermore, optimization algorithms (e.g., ABC, PSO, GA) fail to balance exploration-exploitation trade-offs in high-dimensional spaces, leading to premature convergence, suboptimal feature subsets, and dependency on manual hyperparameter tuning. These limitations collectively impede reliable, scalable disease detection. By combining Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) with Genetic Algorithm (GA), the suggested Genetic Swarm Optimization (GSO) gets around these problems. Here are some contributions for our article.

Methodology

This section delineates the suggested technique as seen in the Fig. 2. We obtain the Pomegranate Fruit Disease dataset from Kaggle for assessment. The samples initially collected are designated as original data, whereas the data containing numerous artifacts is classified as noisy data. The original and noisy data are then entered into a fine-tuned ResNet101 deep convolutional neural network. We achieve this through transfer learning. We employ an advanced feature extraction technique to obtain valuable and non-redundant features. We subsequently integrate these to derive distinctive and noisy data features. We employ the HGA-PSO (hybrid genetic algorithm with particle swarm optimization) technique to derive a collection of optimized features. A multilayered perceptron then groups these features. Data collection

Data preprocessing is an essential phase in deep learning image classification tasks, guaranteeing that input images are suitably structured for model training. The process begins with traditional image normalization using ImageNet statis- tics to maintain feature size consistency, followed by spatial standardization by scaling to 256 pixels and center cropping to 224 × 224 dimensions. The evaluation database is divided into two segments: the original dataset and the noisy dataset. The original dataset consists of pomegranate fruit disease. The pomegranate fruit disease dataset has five categories of data: four representing damaged fruit and one representing healthy fruit. The remaining four classifications include Alternaria, Anthracnose, Bacterial Blight, and Cercospora. Each of the five classes has a total of 5,000 images. The Pomegranate dataset has five classes: Alternaria with 1000 images, Anthracnose with 1000 images, Bacterial Blight with 1000 images, Cercospora with 1000 images, and healthy with 1000 images. Figure 3 shows photos from the original dataset. Stratified random sampling divides the dataset into training (80%) and testing (20%) subsets to maintain the integrity of the class distribution. Pomegranate images are collected from areas like Balleri, Bangalore, Bagalkote, etc.in India on July and October of 2023 and for testing some samples of pomegranate is collected from Maqsoodan fruit mandi, Jalandhar for real-world testing. The dataset encompasses all four seasons to capture phenological variations: images span spring (25%), summer (30%), autumn (25%), and winter (20%), including transitional periods (e.g., early spring foliage, late autumn senescence). Seasons were derived from image timestamps and geolocation using climate API validation. Lighting variability is represented through a balanced distribution of conditions: direct sunlight (30%), overcast (25%), shaded environments (20%), artificial lighting (15%), and low-light scenarios (10% at dawn/dusk).

A convolutional layer is the core component of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) [15, 71], designed to efficiently process grid-structured data like images. Pretrained deep CNN model: ResNet101

The ResNet-101 architecture is built on the principle of residual learning, which mitigates the degradation issue seen in very deep networks. As networks go deeper, conventional CNNs often experience saturation or even a decline in accuracy while possessing enough capacity. ResNet mitigates this problem by including shortcut connections that allow the network to learn residual mappings instead of direct mappings [10]. The essential element of ResNet is the residual block. This formulation guarantees that if the optimum mapping approximates an identity mapping, the network may easily drive the residual towards zero, hence facilitating optimization. ResNet-101 has 101 layers, using 33 residual blocks designed with bottleneck architectures to minimize computing complexity. The foundational ResNet-101 employs bottleneck building components, which execute three-layer residual functions.

Feature extraction

It changes the original ResNet-101 architecture by getting rid of the last two layers (global average pooling and fully connected layers), but it keeps the convolutional layers (Output: 7 × 7 × 2048). It uses adaptive average pooling instead of fixed side pooling to handle different input dimensions. This creates feature vectors with 2048 dimensions and includes a two-layer multilayer perceptron with dropout (p = 0.3) for regularization.5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{W}_{feature}\in\:{R}^{2048\times\:1024},\hspace{1em}{W}_{class}\in\:R\in\:1024\times\:{N}_{\text{class}}$$\end{document}

This produces 2048-dimensional feature vectors from both the original and noisy data streams. The baseline network is established, enhancing accuracy and minimizing loss. The architecture of ResNet-101 is shown in Table 3; Fig. 5.

Transfer learning

The model employs transfer learning by initializing the ResNet-101 [32, 35] backbone with weights pre-trained on the ImageNet dataset. The first 50 layers of ResNet101 are fixed, since they extract universal low- to mid-level features (edges, textures, patterns) that are independent of specific tasks. Freezing them mitigates overfitting and decreases training duration. The process begins with an initial 7 × 7 convolution, which is then followed by max pooling and the first 15 bottleneck blocks. Subsequently, layers 51–101, including the remaining 18 bottleneck blocks, are fine-tuned. These layers specialize in advanced feature abstraction (e.g., disease-specific patterns such as lesions and discoloration). The multi-layered perceptron is trained from the ground up with a reduced learning rate (3 × 10⁻⁴) to prevent detrimental updates. We use cross-entropy loss for multiclass categorization. The Adam optimizer is used for regularization. The outputs of the original and noisy features are (N, 2048) and (N, 2048), respectively, where N equals 4079 for 80% of the dataset.

Hybrid search mechanism (Combining GA and PSO)

The hybrid mechanism combines Genetic Algorithm (GA) and Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) to leverage their complementary strengths. Subsequently, bit-flip mutation is applied stochastically to introduce diversity, where individual bits in the solution representation may be inverted based on a predefined mutation probability. Concurrently, the PSO group operates as a swarm of particles. Each particle updates its velocity by considering its historical best position (personal best) and the swarm’s global best position. Specifically, a sigmoid function converts the velocity into a probability threshold. Each particle’s position is then updated to either 0 or 1 by comparing this probability against a random value. This parallel execution of GA (emphasizing exploration through crossover/mutation) and PSO (prioritizing exploitation via social guidance) enables a balanced search strategy. The hybrid approach dynamically refines solutions while maintaining population diversity, enhancing robustness and convergence efficiency.

Update best solutions (personal and global bests)

In GA-PSO hybrid algorithm, updating personal best (pbest) and global best (gbest) solution is streamlined for computational efficiency. After fitness evaluation each iteration:

Personal Best (pbest) update:

For each individual/particle, compare its current fitness with its existing pbest fitness. If the current solution yields better fitness (e.g., higher accuracy for maximization), replace pbest with the current solution. Skip comparisons if no improvement occurs, reducing redundant operations.

Global Best (gbest) update:

Track the best solution across the entire population (both GA and PSO groups). When a pbest is updated, immediately compare its fitness with gbest. If the new pbest is superior, update gbest to this solution. Leverages the pbest update step to concurrently check gbest candidates, avoiding a separate population-wide scan.

Grad-CAM + + for explainable AI

Grad-CAM++ (Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping++) extends the original Grad-CAM method to provide more precise and fine-grained visual explanations of convolutional neural network (CNN) decisions. Grad-CAM + + enhances visual explainability in deep learning by generating high-resolution heatmaps that precisely localize all relevant regions contributing to a model’s prediction, not just the most salient area. It addresses limitations in Grad-CAM when localizing multiple occurrences of objects and improves heatmap coherence through higher-order gradient computations to capture complex feature interactions, making it particularly effective for images with multiple objects of interest or distributed visual features. The core innovation lies in its weighted combination of pixel-wise gradients to better capture the importance of spatial locations.

Discussion

This study employed a modified ResNet101 alongside a nature- inspired algorithm to identify fruit diseases with precision and efficiency. Initially, data pre-processing occurred before entering it into the model. Several tests were conducted to achieve optimal performance on the proposed approach. During pre-processing, images were resized the images to 224 × 224 × 3 for the modified ResNet101 architecture and generated a noisy dataset from the original dataset. The dataset was partitioned into an 80:20 ratio for training and testing. Tables 3 and 4 describe the hyperparameters used for the modified ResNet101 and HGA-PSO development, respectively. The proposed model was trained on both noisy and original data for up to 25 epochs for feature extraction. Subsequently, the retrieved features were concatenated, and the optimal features are selected using hybrid genetic algorithm and particle swarm optimization (HGA-PSO) algorithm. Classification was performed using a multilayer perceptron. Several performance assessment indicators, such as accuracy, precision, sensitivity or recall, and F1-score, were evaluated using the revised methodology. The proposed CNN-HGA-PSO model outperforms existing techniques as shown in Table 7; Fig. 20 due to three key innovations: First, a dual-stream ResNet101 architecture processes both original and synthetically noised images (Gaussian, salt-and-pepper, speckle) in parallel, explicitly addressing real-world field variability. Second, a novel Hybrid Genetic Algorithm-Particle Swarm Optimization (HGA-PSO) technique fuses features from these streams and optimizes feature selection by balancing genetic exploration (crossover, mutation) with swarm exploitation (social learning), achieving 50–70% dimensionality reduction while preserving discriminative power. Third, Grad-CAM + + explainability provides human-interpretable visualizations of disease localization, enhancing trust and diagnostic transparency addressing the “black box” limitation of conventional deep learning models. These innovations collectively enable a robust, field-deployable framework that achieves state-of-the-art accuracy (99.10%), outperforming existing methods like PSO-YOLOv8 (98.86%) and transformer-based models (93.13%). By mitigating sensitivity to environmental noise and lighting variations, the framework bridges critical gaps between laboratory validation and real-world agricultural settings. Its computational efficiency (via feature reduction) lowers hardware barriers, enabling deployment on edge devices for resource-limited farms. While the proposed framework shows high accuracy, its generalization across other crops or highly variable field conditions (e.g., diverse lighting, occlusions, or seasonal variations) may still be limited, highlighting the need for broader cross-crop validation and adaptation to uncontrolled environments.

Conclusion and future work

This paper introduces an innovative framework for automated identification of pomegranate diseases, combining world imaging artifacts such as Gaussian and speckle noise. Feature fusion integrates various representations, while the HGA-PSO method enhances feature selection by integrating genetic algorithms (crossover, mutation) with swarm intelligence (social learning), efficiently decreasing dimensionality by 50–70% and removing redundancy. A Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) is used to classify the optimized features, which achieve a state-of-the-art accuracy of 99.75% on a stratified dataset with 5,000 images divided into five groups. A lot of tests, like ROC-AUC (1.00), precision-recall curves (AP 1.00), and confusion matrices (99.5% accuracy), show that the model is resistant to noise and can be used for a wide range of disease symptoms. The framework’s methodological innovation is characterized by its noise-resilient feature fusion and hybrid optimization, which improve computational efficiency and interpretability. This method facilitates early and accurate disease identification, providing scalable solutions to reduce crop losses (20–40%) and promote sustainable agronomy. Future studies need to broaden this framework to include more crops, test it using extensive, multi-regional datasets, and investigate edge deployment for real-time field applications. Furthermore, exploring lightweight forms of HGA-PSO may improve computing efficiency in resource-limited agricultural settings. This research emphasizes the transformative potential of integrating deep learning with nature-inspired optimization to improve precision agriculture. Beyond its immediate application in pomegranate orchards, the framework shows strong potential for scalability to other crops, enabling a broader agricultural impact. With the integration of drones and smartphones, the model can be deployed as a real-time monitoring tool, providing farmers with actionable insights directly in the field. Furthermore, incorporating model compression techniques such as pruning, quantization, and knowledge distillation can significantly reduce computational cost, making the system more suitable for resource-constrained edge devices. These enhancements open promising directions for future research and practical deployment in precision agriculture.
