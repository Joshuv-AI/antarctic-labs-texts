Behavioral and Exumbrellar Integumental Defenses in Jellyfish: The Hydromedusan Spirocodon saltatrix Releases Oxidized Derivatives of Unsaturated Fatty Acids Potentially to Avoid Predation and Bacterial Biofouling
Authors: Nana Miura, Ryogo Suzuki, Koto Shimotori, Kazufumi Osako, Yudai Akimoto, Takeshi Terahara, Gaku Yamamoto, Aya Adachi, Haruto Ishii, Kazuya Fukuhara, Hiroshi Nagai, Michiya Kamio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCnidarian jellyfish—the medusa phase of medusozoans—are gaining recognition as important prey for a variety of predators in marine trophic webs. However, their traits as prey remain poorly understood. In this study, we examined the defensive traits of jellyfish, focusing specifically on cnidocyte-independent chemical defenses that serve as strategies against consumers, pathogens, and biofouling. To investigate these anti-consumer chemical defenses, we selected three sympatric jellyfish species: the hydromedusan Spirocodon saltatrix and the scyphomedusan moon jellyfish Aurelia coerulea as prey, and the scyphomedusan Japanese sea nettle Chrysaora pacifica as the predator. Using these species, we conducted feeding experiments, ablation of potential defensive organs, and analyses of compounds released by the prey. We found that: (1) in feeding experiments, S. saltatrix individuals exhibited an involution behavior, in which they strongly contracted and retracted their tentacles inside the umbrella when captured by C. pacifica, thereby escaping, whereas A. coerulea individuals were consumed without escaping; (2) a series of surgical ablation experiments indicated that the umbrella integument of S. saltatrix possesses a defensive function; (3) gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) analyses showed that S. saltatrix releases C8 to C9 alcohols and aldehydes when its integument is damaged; (4) behavioral experiments demonstrated that these compounds deter feeding by the predatory jellyfish; (5) these compounds inhibited biofilm formation by the sympatric bacterium Ectopseudomonas sp. isolated from sediment, indicating their antifouling role, though their effects on planktonic biofouling remain to be tested; and (6) the experimentally determined effective concentrations were higher than the bulk measured concentrations. Therefore, we hypothesize that the natural microscale concentrations of these compounds at the injured integument are sufficiently high to match the effective thresholds, suggesting that they function as defensive agents. Thus, our study demonstrates that S. saltatrix utilizes involution behavior and its integument to avoid predation. To our knowledge, this is the first report of exumbrellar integumental defense in a cnidarian jellyfish with suggestive evidence of chemical defense using oxidized derivatives of unsaturated fatty acids.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10886-026-01740-4.Keywords: Antifouling, Gelatinous plankton, Chemical ecology, Defensive behavior, Japanese sea nettle Chrysaora pacifica, Moon jellyfish Aurelia coerulea, Exumbrellar integumental chemical defense

Introduction

Jellyfish Have Been Studied as Predators

Cnidarian jellyfish play important roles in marine trophic webs (Hays et al. 2018; Richardson et al. 2009; Stoltenberg et al. 2021) and have been recognized as predators that consume zooplankton (Purcell 2003), fish eggs, and fish larvae (Purcell 2018). Therefore, predatory traits of jellyfish, diet selection and feeding rates, and competition for food with fish are relatively well studied (Nagata and Morandini 2018; Purcell and Sturdevant 2001). Morphological and biochemical traits used to capture their prey have been studied in detail in some species. Cnidarian jellyfish use nematocysts on their tentacles (Purcell 1984) to capture their prey. The nematocyst consists of a minicollagen-walled capsule and a long tubule that is tightly coiled inside the capsule which bears long arrays of spines. On explosive discharge, the capsule everts its tubule bearing the dart-like spines within milliseconds. There are variations in morphology of the nematocysts within and among species (David et al. 2008; Kitatani et al. 2015). The nematocyst contains venom, and there are variations in the toxicity of venoms across species (Kawabata et al. 2013; Tibballs 2006).The venoms contain enzymes, potent pore forming toxins, and neurotoxins (Jouiaei et al. 2015), and the chemical structure of one toxic protein was identified in species that are harmful to humans, e.g., CqTX-A from Chiropsalmus quadrigatus (Nagai et al. 2002) followed by several toxins from other jellyfish species including CfTX-1 and −2 from Chironex fleckeri (Brinkman and Burnell 2007).

Jellyfish Have Been Less Studied as Prey Than as Predators

Less is known about jellyfish as prey than as predators because they are rarely considered as food in trophic webs (Verity and Smetace 1996). This is mainly because 1) their gelatinous tissues are quickly digested in the stomach of predators so thus cannot be identified as prey from studies of gut contents of predators, 2) they have low nutritional value, and 3) predators have difficulty finding them because they are transparent (Verity and Smetace 1996). However, recent studies show that jellyfish play a more important role as prey in marine trophic webs than previously thought (Stoltenberg et al. 2021). Stable isotope analysis of predator tissues, animal-borne cameras, and DNA analysis of fecal and gut samples (i.e. metabarcoding) indicate that many taxa routinely consume jellyfish (Hays et al. 2018). A number of cnidarians and ctenophores as well as fish including opportunistic species (Marques et al. 2016) utilize jellyfish as prey (Arai 2005). Furthermore benthic and pelagic predators such as cnidarians and decapods feed on jellyfish when they come in contact (Ates 2017). As prey, jellyfish should have anti-predation strategies. In general, jellyfish have transparent bodies that have low visibility to predators and thus can deter predation (Johnsen and Widder 1998). Jellyfish can also discard a part of their own soft body to escape from predator (Kreps et al. 1997). Other defensive behaviors, including involution by contraction of umbrella, intimidation by bioluminescent, and escape by swimming, are reviewed by Mackie (1995). In scyphomedusan Stomolophus meleagris, the discharge of mucus was hypothesized to be an antipredator chemical defense because S. meleagris discharged sticky mucus when it was experimentally agitated. The water containing discharged mucus and nematocyst killed fish and crustaceans (Shanks and Graham 1988). However antipredator effects of the discharged matter were not directly tested to determine if it impedes the feeding behavior of the predators (Shanks and Graham 1988). Thus, how jellyfish avoid predation is poorly understood. Among the defensive strategies, we focused on the exumbrellar integumental chemical defenses.

Jellyfish are Exposed to Risk of Fouling by Sessile Organisms

The external surface of all living organisms, including jellyfish, is susceptible to bacterial colonization (Wahl 1989). Based on this logic, jellyfish might have antifouling strategies. An expected antifouling strategy is continuous discharge of mucus. Mucus has exceptional properties including elasticity, changeable rheology and an ability to self-repair by re-annealing, and is therefore an ideal medium for trapping and immobilizing pathogens and serving as a barrier to microbial infection (Bakshani et al. 2018). Continuous production of mucus (Wild et al. 2004) and/or production of antimicrobial compounds that are incorporated into the mucus as in corals (Kelman et al. 2006) could be an antifouling strategy in jellyfish. More research is needed to understand antifouling strategies in jellyfish.

Aim of this Study: Find Defensive Traits in Jellyfish

In this study, among the various possible anti-predation strategies, we are interested in chemical defenses because various marine organisms use chemical cues to find and select food (Kamio and Derby 2017) and in other intra- and inter specific interactions, e.g. to find mates and conspecifics (Kamio et al. 2022). Chemical defenses are employed by diverse taxa of marine organisms from sessile organisms (Paul et al. 2007) to mobile invertebrates (Kamio et al. 2010a), plankton e.g. harmful algal bloom forming taxa (Brown et al. 2019; True et al. 2018) and fish (Reverter et al. 2018). Defensive compounds are discharged from defensive glands into the surrounding water (Johnson et al. 2006) or stored in external body tissues (Pennings and Paul 1993). To test for chemical defenses in cnidarian jellyfish, we choose the hydrozoan jellyfish Spirocodon saltatrix and the scyphozoan moon jellyfish Aurelia coerulea as prey because the predatory scyphozoan Japanese sea nettle Chrysaora pacifica feeds on A. coerulea but not on S. saltatrix in aquaria experiments (personal observation). Additionally S. saltatrix releases chemicals that are perceived by humans as cucumber-like, and the volatile compounds that cause this odor were hypothesized to play a defensive role within an ecological context (Yoshida et al. 2019). These three species could be an intraguild predation systems because they are sympatric in Tokyo Bay (Nomura and Ishimaru 1998). The intraguild predation in which jellyfish eat jellyfish is known for all jellyfish groups including Scyphomedusae, Hydromedusae, Siphonophorae, and Ctenophora (Purcell 1991). For example predation of the scyphomedusan Cyanea capillata on A. aurita has been estimated from gut contents of animals caught in a field (Hansson 1997), and on Mnemiopsis leidyi was observed in laboratory conditions (Hosia and Titelman 2011) and at early development stages (Båmstedt et al. 1997). We also aimed to test the antifouling effect of the defensive compounds for epibiont bacteria (Wahl 1989) using biofilm forming bacteria collected in Tokyo Bay.

Experimental Approach

First, we tested whether Chrysaora. pacifica consumes or rejects Aurelia. coerulea and Spirocodon saltatrix. Then we searched for the location of the defensive organ(s) on the animals, and the chemical compounds discharged from injured animals. Then we tested the deterrence of the discharged chemical compounds in feeding assays to evaluate the compounds as defensive molecules and in biofilm inhibition experiments using sympatric bacteria Ectopseudomonas sp.

Materials and Methods

Animals, Biofilm Forming Bacteria and Chemicals

Individuals of Spirocodon saltatrix were collected from coastal waters near Yokohama City, Kanagawa, and Echizenchou, Fukui, Japan. Chrysaora pacifica and Aurelia coerulea were raised at the Enoshima Aquarium, Kanagawa, Japan. These jellyfish were fed with nauplii of Artemia sp. in filtered natural seawater at temperature 15.2 to 15.3 °C, pH 7.96 to 8.08, and salinity 31 to 32 ‰. A bacterial strain isolated from a marine sediment collected in Tokyo Bay was identified as Ectopseudomonas sp. on the basis of 16S rRNA gene sequence. The bacterium was selected because it is living in Tokyo Bay and successfully produced biofilm in laboratory experiments. It is isolated from sediments; however, we assumed that it could settle on the surface of other organisms. The chemicals nonanal, 1-octen-3-ol, (E)−2-nonenal, (E,Z)−2,6-nonadienal, (E)−2-octen-1-ol, 1-nonanol, and (E)−2-nonen-1-ol (GC-analytical grade) were purchased from Tokyo Chemical Cooperation (Tokyo, Japan). These compounds were used as ethanol solutions. The concentrations (mol/L) of 50% solution of each compound are as follows: nonanal 2.88, 1-octen-3-ol 3.24, (E)−2-nonenal 3.00, (E,Z)−2,6-nonadienal 3.11, (E)−2-octen-1-ol 3.28, 1-nonanol 2.88, (E)−2-nonen-1-ol 2.95. Solutions of 5%, 0.5% and 0.05% are further diluted tenfold from 50% solutions. Mix is a mixture of all seven compounds in equal proportions. Cyclohexanol (Reagent Plus🄬, 99%) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (MA, USA). For feeding assays, Tetra Krill-E (Spectrum Brands Japan, Yokohama, Japan) was used as dried krill. The dried krill is not a common food for jellyfish but selected as an ideal food to test oily low polar compounds because oily compounds do not stay on wet food but stay on dried food.

Observation of Predatory Interactions Between Predator Chrysaora pacifica and Prey Spirocodon saltatrix and Aurelia coerulea

Predatory jellyfish Chrysaora pacifica and putative prey jellyfish Spirocodon saltatrix (1.5–2.2 cm wide, 1.9–6.2 g) and Aurelia coerulea (2.0–3.8 cm wide, 2.4–7.4 g) were kept 15 h without food before the experiment to make all the tentacles food-free. C. pacifica was placed in a pseudo kreisel aquarium (45 × 47 × 22 cm, depth × width × thickness) for 1 h before the experiment to acclimate. One individual of S. saltatrix or A. coerulea was introduced to the aquarium to touch the cnidocyst-laden tentacle of C. pacifica and the behavior of the jellyfish was recorded using video cameras, Olympus Tough TG-6, SONY HANDYCAM HDR-CX370 or Panasonic HC-W585M for 80 min. In the predation, C. pacifica captured the prey using its cnidocyst-laden tentacle, kept the prey using the tentacle and oral arm, transferred the prey to its mouth, and transferred the prey into the gastrovascular cavity. The progress was ranked as, 4: took into gastrovascular cavity, 3: transferred to oral arm pocket which is a pocket below mouth, 2: kept the prey using the tentacle and oral arm, and 1: released the prey (Fig. 1A). Thus, the observation started at rank 2. Fifteen individuals each of S. saltatrix and A. coerulea were tested with different individuals of C. pacifica and analyzed by two tailed Mann–Whitney’s U-test (α = 0.05).

Defensive Role of the Integument of Spirocodon saltatrix

We tested if the integument itself has defensive traits. S. saltatrix without umbrella integument was prepared by gently scrubbing the outer umbrella surface of the animals. When the integument was removed, the frosted appearance of the animal turned transparent. Sham controls were prepared that were the same as the experimental group except the integument was not removed. The predatory behavior of C. pacifica (6.0–9.0 cm wide) on S. saltatrix (1.0–2.4 cm wide, 0.5–2.9 g) without integument (n = 15) and sham control (n = 15) was observed and analyzed as described in 2.2.

Analysis of Discharged Compounds From the Injured Integument of Spirocodon saltatrix

We focused on low molecular aldehydes and alcohols that are released from S. saltatrix when they are exposed to air (Yoshida et al. 2019). Discharged compounds were determined using solid-phase microextraction and gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (SPME–GC–MS). A live individual of S. saltatrix was placed in a glass dish with 100 ml of aquarium seawater in which the jellyfish were stored and scrubbed with kimwipe paper to injure the integument. The animal and the paper were maintained in the seawater for 10 min with the aquarium covered with a lid. Then, 3 ml aliquots of this seawater, labeled as injured cuticular water, were removed from the aquarium and placed in 20 ml headspace glass vials and capped. Sham controls were also prepared. Ten samples of injured cuticular water and 10 sham controls were made and stored at −60 °C. Cyclohexanol was added as an internal standard to give a final concentration of 0.1 μg/mL before the measurement. Then, the vial was flushed with nitrogen gas and hermetically sealed using a screw cap with silicone septa. A 50/30 μm divinylbenzene/carboxen/polydimethylsiloxane (DVB/CAR/PDMS) SPME fiber (Supelco, Bellefonte, PA, USA) was used to collect volatile compounds from the headspace above the samples at 30 °C for 30 min to reach the extraction equilibrium under magnetic stirring. The fiber was then immediately inserted into the gas chromatograph injector for 5 min to desorb the volatile compounds.

Analysis was performed on a Shimadzu GCMS-QP2010 ultra (Kyoto, Japan) equipped with a Supelcowax TM10 fused silica capillary column (60 m length × 0.25 mm i.d. × 0.25 μm thickness; Supelco, Bellefonte, PA, USA) using helium with a constant flow at 1.2 mL/min as the carrier gas. The injection port and detector temperature were set to 250 and 260 °C. The oven temperature was programmed as follows: an increase from 40 to 250 °C at 4 °C/min, after which the temperature was maintained at 250 °C for 10 min. The mass spectrometer was recorded at 70 eV ionization energy by scanning MS from m/z 45 to 500. Volatile compounds from four replicate samples were analyzed.

The compounds were identified by comparing and matching their mass spectra with those reported in the mass spectra libraries (NIST11). Identification was defined as at least three of four sample replications containing a peak at the same retention time with a higher than 90% similarity to the NIST mass spectrum. Quantities of each volatile compound were estimated by calculating its peak area ratio to the cyclohexanol internal standard peak area, which was obtained from the total ion chromatograms. The concentration obtained by calibration curb was divided by the area of ​​the umbrella of S. saltrix. To calculate approximate area of the umbrella the S. saltrix was placed on a flat surface to be flattened and the length and width of the umbrella was measured. Double of the area calculated from the length and width was used as the umbrella area. The amount of compound was normalized to the umbrella area (1 mm2) and compared between injured integumental water and sham control by two-tailed Student’s t-test (α = 0.05).

Feeding Deterrent Assay of the Compounds Discharged From Spirocodon saltatrix with Predator Chrysaora pacifica

To test if the compounds discharged from S. saltatrix have defensive roles against C. pacifica, dried krill was used as food. Krill with 175–200 mg dry weight were immersed in the ethanol solution that contained the compound at 50, 5, or 0.5% (v/v) for 10 s. The concentration (mol/L) of each compound in 5% solution is as follows: nonanal 1.44 × 10–2, 1-octen-3-ol 1.62 × 10–2, (E)−2-nonenal 1.50 × 10–2, (E,Z)−2,6-nonadienal 1.56 × 1–2 mol/L, (E)−2-octen-1-ol 1.64 × 10–2, 1-nonanol 1.44 × 10–2, (E)−2-nonen-1-ol 1.48 × 10–2. These compounds are oily liquid at ambient temperature and atmosphere pressure and are not soluble in water. The krill were dried to remove ethanol under air for 5 min and used for the experiment. Since ethanol was evaporated and only the C8 and C9 compounds remained on dried shrimp, the concentrations of the compounds are 100% as oil and the amount cannot be expressed as concentration. Thus, we do not discuss effective concentration in water. Krill dipped in ethanol and dried were used as controls. The method in 2.2 was used except shortening the observation time from 80 to 2 min and changing the prey from jellyfish to dried krill with treatments. Each C. pacifica individual was held in a kreisel glass aquarium and a piece of krill was placed in the aquarium to touch the tentacle, and observed for 2 min. C. pacifica individuals were fed with the control krill treated with ethanol first and then a krill with one of the compounds at a concentration were tested. Only C. pacifica individuals that ate the control were used for the experiment. When krill touched the tentacle, C. pacifica kept the krill on the tentacle and released it depending on the food. To protect against observer bias, the experimenter presented stimuli blind to their identity; however, because the odors of compounds are different, it was not possible to completely hide their identity from the experimenter. The duration that the krill was kept on the tentacle was compared between control and treatment as paired data using McNemar tests (α = 0.05).

Biofilm Formation Assay

Ectopseudomonas sp. was cultured at 27ºC in Lysogeny broth (LB) with 3% NaCl and adjusted to 0.05 (OD630). Seven kinds of components and all mixtures were prepared to dilute 0.05%, 0.5%, and 5% each by ethanol. In a 96-well plate, 180 μl of LB with 3% NaCl, 10 μl of the adjusted culture and 10 μl of the component were added to the individual well. The concentrations (mol/L) of each compound in 5% solution are as follows: nonanal 1.44 × 10–2, 1-octen-3-ol 1.62 × 10–2, (E)−2-nonenal 1.50 × 10–2, (E,Z)−2,6-nonadienal 1.56 × 1–2 mol/L, (E)−2-octen-1-ol 1.64 × 10–2, 1-nonanol 1.44 × 10–2, (E)−2-nonen-1-ol 1.48 × 10–2. 0.5% and 0.05% solutions are further diluted tenfold from that. Mix is a mixture of all seven compounds in equal proportions. For negative control, 10 μl of ethanol were added. Two wells were used for every component. After incubation of the 96-well plate at 27ºC for 1 or 2 days, biofilm formation assay was performed according to Sriram et al. (2011). The OD595 values of two wells were measured with an iMark Microplate Reader (Bio-Rad, CA, USA) and averaged as the biofilm stained by crystal violet. This assay was performed in triplicate. The mean and the standard deviation were calculated, and inhibition rates of biofilm formation were then calculated by the ratio of the value of every component to negative control.

Results

Predatory Behavior of Chrysaora pacifica and Defensive Behavior of Prey Spirocodon saltatrix and Aurelia coerulea

All 15 individuals of A. coerulea were captured by C. pacifica by the end of the 80 min observation period (Fig. 1). Among them, 6 were taken into the gastrovascular cavity, 5 reached the mouth, and 7 were retained on the tentacle and/or oral arm. Thus, 11 out of 15 were taken to the mouth and none of the 15 were released following capture. On the other hand, more than half of the S. saltatrix individuals were released before the end of the observation period and none of them were eaten. Among the 15 S. saltatrix individuals, none were taken into the gastrovascular cavities or reached the mouth, 7 were retained on the tentacle and/or oral arm, and 8 were released from the tentacle and oral arm. Progress of predation by C. pacifica was significantly different between A. coerulea and S. saltatrix (p = 2.1 ×10−5, n = 15, two-tailed Mann-Whitney U-test). Thus, C. pacifica preyed on A. coerulea but not on S. saltatrix.

S. saltatrix showed defensive behavior. When they were captured by C. pacifica, they hid all their tentacles into the umbrella and shrank the umbrella into a ball shape to stop pulsation of the umbrella. This defensive form continued until they were released from capture and after the release the jellyfish started to swim using umbrella pulsation.

Defensive Role of the Integument of Spirocodon saltatrix

Since the defensive behavior of S. saltatrix involves hiding its tentacles in its umbrella, indicating that the integument of its ectoderm is responsible for the defense, we tested if S. saltatrix has defenses on the integument. Initially, we planned to test mucus released from the umbrella integument. However, no notable mucus was released from S. saltatrix. Then we tested if the integument itself has defensive traits. Fourteen out of 15 S. saltatrix individuals without integument were taken into the gastrovascular cavity of C. pacifica and another individual was retained on the tentacles and oral arm until the end of the 80 min observation period (Fig. 2). In contrast, all 15 of sham controls were released and none of them reached the mouth or gastrovascular cavity. Progress of predation by C. pacifica was significantly different between integument ablation treatment and the sham controls (p = 1.1 × 10−7, n = 15, two-tailed Mann-Whitney U-test); indicating that the integument of S. saltatrix has defensive functions against C. pacifica. The tentacles separated from the body (n = 1) and the gonads separated from the body (n = 1) were brought to the mouth. Two of the half portions of the umbrella were tested and one was brought to the mouth. Intact umbrellas, either without gonad (n = 2) and without tentacle (n = 2), were rejected before they reached the mouth.

Discharged Compounds From the Injured Integument of Spirocodon saltatrix

Among the compounds that were reported as volatile chemicals discharged from homogenate of S. saltatrix (Yoshida et al. 2019) (Fig. 3), three compounds, (E)−2-nonenal, 1-nonanol, and (E)−2-nonen-1-ol, were detected as being in significantly higher amounts, 23, 2.2, and 6.6 times, respectively, in injured integumental water (p = 0.00094 for (E)−2-nonenal, p = 0.0266 for 1-nonanol, p = 0.000027 for (E)−2-nonen-1-ol., two-tailed t-test) (Fig. 4). 1-octen-3-ol showed slightly higher amounts in injured integumental water (p = 0.12, two-tailed t-test). Nonanal was considered as artifact and was not quantified because it was detected in any sample including artificial seawater. (E,Z)−2,6-nonadienal and (E)−2-octen-1-ol were not detected in any samples.

The seven compounds and mixtures of them were tested at various concentrations to determine their ability to inhibit biofilm formation by Ectopseudomonas sp. (Fig. 6). 5% of the components (E,Z)−2,6-nonadienal, 1-octen-3-ol, and (E)−2-octen-1-ol and of all mixtures inhibited the biofilm formation more than 90%. Discussion

Chemical Defense in Cnidarian Jellyfish

Our results in this study showed that hydrozoan jellyfish Spirocodon saltatrix releases C8 and C9 alcohols and aldehydes from injured integuments to avoid predation by scyphozoan Japanese sea nettle Chrysaora pacifica in a cnidarian medusozoan intraguild predation system (Fig. 7). These molecules are volatile compounds that cause cucumber like odor in this species (Yoshida et al. 2019). These anti-predatory defensive compounds can inhibit the growth of a biofilm forming bacterium Ectopseudomonas sp. as well. This is the first report of chemical defenses in medusa of cnidarians. In our experiment, C. pacifica captured and consumed the scyphozoan jellyfish Aurelia coerulea but did not consume S. saltatrix. After a contact, C. pacifica held S. saltatrix and then released it. These results indicate that C. pacifica recognized A. coerulea and S. saltatrix as potential prey and consumed A. coerulea but did not consume S. saltatrix because it detected the defensive molecules. The chemosensory system in jellyfish (Weir et al. 2020) should mediate the recognition of potential prey and the decision to eat or not the chemically defended species. In addition, the chemosensory system on the tentacles should be responsible for self-recognition. Cnidarians have nematocysts which could be harmful to themselves; however, the tentacles recognize their own body surface and conspecifics do not capture members of own species (Gundlach and Watson 2018). Indeed, C. pacifica does not capture itself and each other even after contact by their tentacles (personal observation). Our results coincided with the intraguild predation observed in aquarium; Chrysaora pacifica feeds on Aurelia coerulea but not on Spirocodon saltatrix. This relationship must be tested in the field to understand the jellyfish ecology in Tokyo Bay.

The Defensive Behavior and Predator Recognition

Spirocodon saltatrix escaped predation by the jellyfish eater Chrysaora pacifica (Fig. 1) by releasing defensive molecules and by defensive behavior. When an individual S. saltatrix was captured by the predator, it contracted its umbrella, withdrew its tentacles, and stored its tentacles in the umbrella to expose only the surface of the umbrella to the predator (Fig. 1). This behavior indicates that the integument of the umbrella is responsible for the chemical defense. The defensive behavior lasted for a while after the captured S. saltatrix was released. Similar behavior has been reported in cnidarian medusae Sarsia, Euphysa, and Stomotoca, and this was called involution in which external stimuli caused the tentacles to contract strongly and retract inside the umbrella. These behaviors help them to protect fragile tissues such as reproductive tissues, nerves, and tentacles (Mackie 1995). In addition to involution, Mackie (1995) categorized two other anti-predator defense strategies of planktonic cnidarians. The second defensive behavior is intimidation, which was observed in Euphysa japonica and Hippopodius hippopus. Intimidation is the emission of light to deter predators. The third type of defensive behavior is escape, observed in Euplokamis dunlapae and Nanomia cara. Mackie (1995) concluded that these behaviors are primarily intended to reduce damage from other organisms, rather than to avoid predation. In our study S. saltatrix showed only involution and Aurelia coerulea did not show any effective defensive behavior. It is still unknown if S. saltatrix recognizes predators and distinguishes it from physical agitation by other organisms or nonliving matter to initiate contraction of the umbrella and discharge of the defensive compounds. This will be a future direction of the study on the defensive strategy of this species.

Integumental Chemical Defense Against Predator

Spirocodon saltatrix individuals without integument were eaten by the predator (Fig. 2), confirming that the integument has the defensive role. On the other hand, the tentacles, which carry the nematocysts that capture small plankton, were contracted and hidden in the umbrella and thus are not used as weapons in defense against the predators but rather suffer injury under predation. The integument is a reasonable body part to equip the defensive devise. Chemical defense is a survival strategy employed by almost all taxa from plants to animals. The defensive molecules are stored in tissues that are effective in deterring predators. For example, in marine animals, the sea hare Aplysia californica stores defensive pigments, amino acids, and an amino acid oxidase in its ink and opaline glands and releases a mixture of these molecules in water flow against predator when they are attacked (Kamio et al. 2010a, 2010b; Kicklighter et al. 2005). Aplysia juliana releases dimethylsulfoniopropionate and acrylate from their integument (Kamio et al. 2016). Many dorid nudibranchs often sequester defensive compounds along the mantle border (Avila and Paul 1997), and fish accumulate detergent molecules in their skin (Boylan and Scheuer 1967; Shiomi et al. 2000; Tachibana and Gruber 1988). Puffer fish larvae use tetrodotoxin stored in their skin to deter predators (Itoi et al. 2014). Thus, the integument is an effective place to locate defensive molecules, and the active release of the molecules in response to attack of predator is effective. S. saltatrix releases defensive molecules from the integument on the outer surface of the umbrella when attacked by predators.

Antifouling Chemical Defense

Jellyfish, like other marine organisms, are always exposed to the danger of biofouling by biofilm-forming bacteria (Wahl 1989). Mucus is an antifouling strategy in many cnidarians including jellyfish A. coerulea and C. pacifica (Hubot et al. 2022; Wild et al. 2004), however, S. saltatrix did not release a noticeable amount of mucus in this study. Instead, it has chemical defenses. The aldehydes and alcohols that constitute the integumental chemical defenses of S. saltatrix inhibit biofilm forming by Ectopseudomonas sp. This result indicates that S. saltatrix may defend its body surface from biofouling using these molecules. S. saltatrix may save chemicals and energy by reducing production of mucus and replacing it with a small mass of the defensive molecules for compensation for antibacterial-fouling defense. This antifouling chemical defense is the case of S. saltatrix, though there are bacteria that are associated with other jellyfish and were hypothesized as important players in the host defense against pathogens and fouling organisms (Tinta et al. 2019). S. saltatrix might have the symbiotic host defense bacteria as well. The bacterium used in this study was isolated from sediment. Experiments with more ecologically relevant species, biofilm forming planktonic bacteria, are necessary to evaluate the antifouling strategy of S. saltatrix.

The Defensive Molecules: Break Down Products of Oxidated Poly Unsaturated Fatty Acids, PUFAs

Our results suggest that Spirocodon saltatrix can avoid predation from its predators by discharging volatile C8 and C9 alcohols and aldehydes.

Occurrence and Biosynthesis of Defensive Molecules

These molecules are released by fish, for example, the ayu Plecoglossus altivelis and the rabbit fish Siganus fuscesens, and cucumber Cucumis sativus, and they are produced from enzymatically oxidated poly unsaturated fatty acids, PUFAs (Grosch and Schwarz 1971; Hirano et al. 1992; Jiarpinijnun et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 1992). In addition to these example, structurally related unsaturated aldehydes are known to be break-down products from the oxidative transformation of fatty acids in the plant and algae (Feussner and Wasternack 2002; Gerwick 1994). Diatoms have similar volatile compounds polyunsaturated aldehydes, PFAs, and they are hypothesized to be used for chemical defense (Pohnert 2005). The precursors of these diatom defenses are also PFAs, arachidonic acid, and eicosapentaenoic acid, and lipoxygenase oxidizes them. When the diatom cells are wounded PUFAs are released from phospholipids and glycolipids by lipase and then oxidized by lipoxygenase (Pohnert 2005). Therefore, we assume that phospholipid-derived PFAs in injured integument of S. saltatrix are enzymatically cleaved to produce C8 and C9 alcohols and aldehydes and discharged against predators.

Activities of the Defensive Molecules

The compounds found in S. saltatrix and related compounds have been reported as defensive molecules in other organisms. (E)−2-nonen-1-al and (E,Z)−2,6-nonadienal are found in crushed cucumber, and they show repellent activity to American cockroaches Periplaneta americana (Scriven and Meloan 1984). When stink bugs Cosmopepla bimaculata are disturbed, they release volatile compounds, n-tridecane, (E)−2-decenal), (E)−2-decenyl acetate, (E)−2-hexenal, hexyl acetate, n-dodecane, a tridecene isomer, and n-undecane, n-tetradecane, and n-pentadecane to repel their predators, including killdeer Charadrius vociferus, starlings Sturnus vulgaris, robins Turdus migratorius, and anole lizards Anolis carolinensis (Krall et al. 1999). Thus, chemical defenses based on small volatile alcohols or aldehydes exist in both marine and terrestrial organisms. In this study (E,Z)−2,6-nonadienal, 1-octen-3-ol, and (E)−2-octen-1-ol and of all mixtures inhibited the biofilm formation of Ectopseudomonas sp, indicating that these molecules are antifoulant in S. saltatrix. The antibacterial activities of the small aldehydes have been reported. For example (E,Z)−2,6-nonadienal and (E)−2-nonenal, which cause the aroma of cucumber (Forss et al. 1962; Kemp et al. 1974), inactivate the growth of the bacteria Bacillus cereus, Escherichia coli O157:H7, Listeria monocytogenes, and Salmonella typhimurium (Cho et al. 2004). These reports together with our results indicate that S. saltatrix uses small aldehydes and alcohols to prevent the growth of biofilm forming bacteria to avoid biofouling on their body.

Honest Deterrent

Defensive molecules should have deleterious effect on the consumers to be honest signals. If not, the consumer will evolve to ignore dishonest deterrents. We did not test if the molecules are harmful to predators, however, negative effects of related compounds have been reported. When copepods consumed PFAs produced by diatoms, the hatching rate of their eggs decreased and caused teratogenic effects in offspring of copepods. PFAs also have deleterious effects on embryos of sea urchins, sea stars, and polychaetes (reviewed in Pohnert 2005). Of the breakdown products of PUFAs produced by theteropteran insects, (E)−2-hexenal induced permanent and temporal paralysis, and (E)−2-hexen-1-ol, (E)−2-hepnal, and (E)−2-octenal induced temporal paralysis in the house cricket Acheta domesticus (Noge and Becerra 2015). Thus, the breakdown of PUFAs is likely to have deleterious effects on predators of Spirocodon saltatrix and to be honest deterrent.

Low Polar Compounds Staying on Microscale Injury Could Work as Chemical Defense

A discrepancy between natural concentration and effective concentration can be controversial when the measured concertation is significantly lower than the active one. In this study the compounds are low polar and are not soluble at high concentration in water. The calculated amount of the compounds discharged from S. saltatrix was on the order of f mol/mm2, but it is based on the concentration in the surrounding water. Thus, we assume that the natural amounts of the compounds are higher than the value calculated based on the SPME GC–MS measurement. Low polar oily compounds should stay on the injured part of the body surface of the prey in aquatic environments. This feature makes them suitable as defensive molecules in aquatic environments. When a predator contacts prey, the contact transfers the low polar compounds to the surface of the chemosensory organ of the predators and stimulates the chemosensory cells. Regarding the detection of these molecules in predatory jellyfish, Gustatory Receptors (GRs) have not been found in cnidarians, though in the sea anemone Nematostella vectensis homologues of GRs, called Gustatory receptor-like (Grl) proteins, are expressed in early developmental stages, the blastula and gastrula, but not at later stages when a putative chemosensory organ forms (Saina et al. 2015). Injury made by predatory jellyfish stinging could occur in microscale and in the microscale enough compound could be produced. Chemosensory proteins like GRs could detect the compounds. Even if the measured concentration in surrounding water using SPME method is low, the amount of the compound in microscale injury can be high enough to deter predators. Thus, the natural amount of the compounds produced on the prey body surface injury is higher than f mol/mm2 which supports the idea that the C8 and C9 compounds are defensive molecules in S. saltatrix.

How Jellyfish Survive in Marine Trophic Web

Jellyfish are food sources for a wide range of predators, and each species of jellyfish might have its own defensive strategy. Potentially defensive behavioral traits, such as involution by contracting umbrella and tentacles, light intimidation, and escape by fast swimming, have been hypothesized but how jellyfish really use these traits is not fully understood (Mackie 1995). For example, involution can protect fragile and important organs, such as gonads, nerves and tentacles by hiding them in umbrella. However, when large fish bite the umbrella or swallow the entire body of the jellyfish, involution cannot protect them. In this study, we showed that a combination of involution behavior and chemical defense is effective against a predatory cnidarian jellyfish C. pacifica. Under capture by the predatory jellyfish, S. saltatrix protects its important body parts from the tentacles of predator by involution while it releases defensive molecules to remove the tentacle of the predator from its own umbrella. The other prey species, Aurelia coerulea, could not escape from capture by predatory C. pacifica. Does this mean A. coerulea does not have the ability to escape from predation? The answer might depend on the predator. Observations on the interaction of kin species Aurelia aurita and predatory Phacellophora camtschatica showed that tentacles of the predator did not adhere to the exumbrellar surface of A. aurita covered by mucus layer and A. aurita escaped by swimming fast (Strand and Hamner 1988). In our study the size of A. coerulea was small compared to the predator and the mucus layer was not thick enough to avoid adhesion of the predatory tentacle. Thus, each jellyfish species has its own survival strategies to survive in encounters with predators, and integumental chemical defense is a part of it. Exumbrellar surface defense using defensive molecules and mucus in S. saltatrix and A. coerulea may work as antibiofouling against a variety of small organisms that are seeking a free surface to settle. More species especially hydromedusa species which show involution (Mackie 1995) are likely to have the chemical defense.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrated that the hydrozoan jellyfish Spirocodon saltatrix possesses integumental and behavioral defenses against the sympatric predatory jellyfish Chrysaora pacifica. These results indicate that S. saltatrix experiences significant predation pressure and serves as important prey within the marine food web. Specifically, S. saltatrix released small aldehydes and alcohols that deterred the predator, suggesting their role as defensive compounds. However, a discrepancy remains between the concentrations measured from injured S. saltatrix and those required to actively deter C. pacifica. Therefore, future studies must evaluate the natural concentration of these compounds at a microscale on the jellyfish body surface. Interestingly, a very similar set of molecules is produced by certain fish, such as ayu, Plecoglossus altivelis, and rabbitfish, Siganus fuscescens, as well as the cucumber, Cucumis sativus. The biosynthetic pathways for these aldehydes and alcohols are highly conserved from algae and plants to animals, where they are produced through the oxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids. Furthermore, these potential defensive compounds inhibited biofilm formation by Ectopseudomonas sp., indicating an additional antifouling function in S. saltatrix. This antifouling effect against bacteria in the planktonic environment warrants further investigation. Finally, S. saltatrix exhibited a behavioral defense known as involution, a response well-documented in many hydromedusans. A combination of behavioral defense (involution) and exumbrellar integumental chemical defenses may be widely utilized across hydromedusan species.
