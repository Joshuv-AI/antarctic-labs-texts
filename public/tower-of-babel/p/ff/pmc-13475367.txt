Personalizing Modern Heart Failure Therapy in HFrEF: Bridging Evidence, Clinical Practice, and Future Perspectives

1. Introduction

Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) remains a major global health challenge, affecting millions of individuals and imposing substantial morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs worldwide [1,2]. Although the introduction of disease-modifying pharmacologic therapies has significantly improved survival and reduced hospitalization rates, many patients continue to experience persistent symptoms, recurrent decompensation, impaired quality of life, and premature death. These observations underscore a persistent gap between therapeutic efficacy demonstrated in clinical trials and outcomes achieved in routine clinical practice.

Over the past three decades, randomized controlled trials have firmly established guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) as the cornerstone of HFrEF management. Neurohormonal modulation through inhibition of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system and the sympathetic nervous system, complemented by newer therapies such as angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitors (ARNI) and sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), has transformed the natural history of the disease [3–6]. Contemporary international guidelines therefore strongly recommend early initiation and optimization of multiple disease-modifying therapies in all eligible patients with HFrEF [7,8].

Despite this robust evidence base, implementation of GDMT in routine clinical practice remains consistently suboptimal. Large registries and real-world studies demonstrate that many patients do not receive all recommended therapies or remain on subtarget doses for prolonged periods [9,10]. Importantly, these treatment gaps persist even in healthcare systems with broad medication availability, suggesting that barriers extend beyond access alone.

A major contributor to this implementation gap lies in the complexity of bedside clinical decision-making. Current guidelines provide clear recommendations regarding which therapies should be used in HFrEF, yet they offer limited practical guidance on how these agents should be prioritized, sequenced, and adapted across diverse clinical phenotypes. Patients with hypotension, renal dysfunction, hyperkalemia, atrial fibrillation, frailty, polypharmacy, or multiple comorbidities frequently challenge a one-size-fits-all approach, leaving clinicians to rely on experience rather than structured evidence-informed strategies [7,8].

In parallel, emerging data increasingly support a paradigm shift away from traditional slow stepwise treatment toward rapid initiation of multiple foundational therapies at low doses followed by structured uptitration. This approach may accelerate prognostic benefit but also amplifies the need for practical frameworks integrating hemodynamic status, renal function, heart rate, and comorbidity burden into individualized treatment decisions [10–12].

In this context, a contemporary state-of-the-art perspective on GDMT in HFrEF is needed—one that moves beyond summarizing established evidence to critically appraise evolving concepts, identify unresolved clinical gray zones, and support personalized decision-making in everyday practice. This review synthesizes current evidence on foundational therapies, examines emerging strategies for sequencing and optimization, discusses real-world implementation barriers, and proposes a phenotype-based decision framework designed to bridge the gap between guideline recommendations and routine care.

2. Methods

This state-of-the-art review was conducted using a structured narrative approach to identify and synthesize contemporary evidence regarding guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF). Relevant literature published from January 2000 to February 2026 was searched in PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and Google Scholar.

Search terms included combinations of the following keywords: heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, HFrEF, guideline-directed medical therapy, beta-blocker, angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitor, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor, sequencing, optimization, implementation, frailty, and clinical phenotypes.

Priority was given to randomized controlled trials, landmark clinical studies, international guideline documents, meta-analyses, and high-quality observational registries relevant to GDMT initiation, sequencing, tolerability, and implementation in routine practice. Additional references were identified through manual review of bibliographies from major publications.

Studies focused exclusively on heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, experimental preclinical models, or non-relevant pharmacologic interventions were not prioritized unless directly applicable to HFrEF management concepts. Given the narrative nature of this review, formal systematic review methodology, risk-of-bias assessment, and meta-analysis were not performed.

3. What is established in guideline-directed medical therapy for HFrEF

Over the past three decades, robust evidence from randomized controlled trials has unequivocally established GDMT as the cornerstone of management for HFrEF. These therapies target maladaptive neurohormonal activation and have consistently demonstrated substantial reductions in mortality, heart failure hospitalization, and disease progression across diverse patient populations [3–6].

3.1. Foundational pharmacologic therapies

Contemporary GDMT for HFrEF is built upon four foundational pharmacologic classes that exert complementary and synergistic effects on the pathophysiologic mechanisms driving disease progression. International guidelines uniformly endorse early initiation of these agents in all eligible patients unless contraindicated [7,8].

4. Renin–angiotensin system inhibition and ARNI

Renin–angiotensin system inhibition, preferably with ARNI in eligible patients, represents a central pillar of HFrEF therapy. Compared with conventional angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, sacubitril/valsartan demonstrated superior reductions in cardiovascular death and heart failure hospitalization in PARADIGM-HF [4]. In hospitalized or recently stabilized patients, PIONEER-HF further supported early initiation with favorable biomarker and clinical outcomes [13].

4.1. Evidence-based beta-blockers

Beta-blockers counteract chronic sympathetic activation, improve ventricular remodeling, and reduce the risk of sudden cardiac death. Large trials have consistently shown survival benefits with carvedilol, metoprolol succinate, and bisoprolol when initiated in clinically stable patients and titrated appropriately [3,7,8].

4.2. Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists

Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists provide incremental prognostic benefit by attenuating aldosterone-mediated sodium retention, fibrosis, and inflammation. When added to background therapy, these agents further reduce mortality and hospitalization, provided renal function and potassium are carefully monitored [7,8,14].

4.3. Sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors

SGLT2 inhibitors have emerged as foundational therapy in HFrEF, with consistent reductions in heart failure hospitalization and cardiovascular death irrespective of diabetes status. Benefits have been demonstrated in both DAPA-HF and EMPEROR-Reduced, with rapid onset of effect and favorable tolerability profiles [5,6,15].

5. Principles of early initiation and combination therapy

Beyond the efficacy of individual drug classes, it is now well established that early initiation of comprehensive GDMT confers greater prognostic benefit than delayed or incomplete therapy. Observational studies and post hoc analyses indicate that a substantial proportion of survival benefit occurs shortly after treatment initiation, even before target doses are achieved [9–12].

Consequently, current guidelines emphasize initiation of all four foundational therapies as early as clinically feasible rather than prolonged sequential strategies that delay exposure to lifesaving treatment [7,8]. This paradigm reflects a fundamental shift from dose-centric to therapy-centric management.

More recently, the STRONG-HF trial demonstrated that rapid initiation and accelerated uptitration of evidence-based therapies combined with close follow-up significantly reduced 180-day death or heart failure readmission, further supporting intensive early optimization strategies [11].

6. Summary of established evidence

Taken together, the evidence supporting GDMT in HFrEF is robust and mature. The mortality and morbidity benefits of the four foundational pharmacologic classes are no longer debated, and their early combined use represents the standard of care for eligible patients. However, while consensus exists regarding what therapies should be used, important questions remain regarding how these agents should be prioritized, sequenced, monitored, and individualized across heterogeneous clinical phenotypes—issues addressed in subsequent sections of this review.

7. What is evolving and where gaps remain

Despite the robust evidence supporting guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), important aspects of treatment implementation remain dynamic and incompletely defined. In particular, optimal strategies for therapy sequencing, timing of initiation, dose escalation, and adaptation across heterogeneous clinical phenotypes continue to evolve. This reflects a growing recognition that guideline recommendations alone may not fully address the complexity of real-world bedside decision-making.

7.1. From sequential therapy to rapid initiation

Traditional approaches to HFrEF management emphasized slow sequential initiation of individual drug classes over several months, often prioritizing dose optimization of one agent before introducing another. However, this paradigm is increasingly being challenged.

Contemporary analyses suggest that delaying exposure to multiple disease-modifying therapies may unnecessarily prolong periods of elevated risk, particularly during the early vulnerable phase after diagnosis or hospitalization [10–12]. Post hoc analyses and registry data indicate that a substantial proportion of prognostic benefit is achieved soon after therapy initiation, even at low doses.

These observations have prompted a shift toward rapid initiation strategies in which all four foundational therapies are introduced early at tolerable doses, followed by structured uptitration. This concept is further supported by STRONG-HF and implementation studies demonstrating improved outcomes with early optimization pathways [11,16].

8. Uncertainty in optimal sequencing

Although early multidrug therapy is increasingly endorsed, the optimal sequencing of GDMT remains uncertain. Current guidelines recommend initiation of all foundational drug classes but provide limited direction regarding prioritization when clinical constraints coexist, such as borderline blood pressure, renal dysfunction, bradyarrhythmia, frailty, or recent decompensation [7,8].

For example, SGLT2 inhibitors exert minimal effects on blood pressure and heart rate and display favorable renal profiles, making them attractive early options in hypotensive or renally vulnerable patients [5,6,15]. Conversely, beta-blockers may provide particular early benefit in patients with tachycardia, ischemic burden, or arrhythmia but can be more difficult to initiate in patients with severe congestion or low-output states.

Similarly, ARNI therapy may be highly effective in hemodynamically stable patients, whereas mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists may be limited by hyperkalemia risk or renal impairment. These trade-offs underscore the absence of a universally applicable sequencing strategy and highlight an important gap between guideline recommendations and bedside practice.

8.1. Clinical gray zones beyond guidelines

Several patient populations remain underrepresented in randomized trials and insufficiently addressed in current guidelines. Patients with advanced age, frailty, multimorbidity, fluctuating renal function, cachexia, or recurrent hypotension frequently challenge standardized treatment pathways.

In such cases, clinicians must balance competing priorities, often prioritizing tolerability, quality of life, adherence, and safety over strict achievement of target doses. This is particularly relevant in older adults, in whom polypharmacy, cognitive impairment, falls risk, and reduced physiologic reserve may substantially influence treatment decisions [17].

Hypotension remains a common barrier to GDMT optimization. While asymptomatic low blood pressure should not routinely trigger discontinuation of life-saving therapy, uncertainty regarding drug prioritization often contributes to therapeutic inertia. Similar concerns arise with hyperkalemia, where fear of adverse events may lead to premature withdrawal of renin–angiotensin system inhibitors or mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists despite proven benefit [18].

8.3. Safety monitoring after initiation

Successful GDMT implementation depends not only on prescribing therapies but also on timely reassessment after initiation or dose escalation. Monitoring of blood pressure, renal function, electrolytes, heart rate, congestion status, and symptoms is essential, particularly during the first weeks after therapy changes. Structured follow–up pathways, nurse-led clinics, and remote monitoring programs may substantially improve safe optimization [11,19].

8.3.1. Equity, access, and cost

Access to contemporary therapies remains uneven across healthcare systems. Medication cost, insurance coverage, specialist access, and health literacy may significantly influence whether patients receive optimal therapy. Even when guidelines are clear, affordability barriers may prevent initiation or long-term adherence to ARNI and SGLT2 inhibitors. Therefore, implementation science and health-system policy are increasingly important components of heart failure care.

8.4. Dose-centric versus therapy-centric management

Another evolving concept concerns the relative importance of achieving target doses versus ensuring exposure to all foundational therapies. While guidelines traditionally emphasize titration to target or maximally tolerated doses, emerging data suggest that earlier initiation of multiple therapies at submaximal doses may confer greater benefit than delayed attainment of target dosing with fewer agents [10–12].

This shift toward a therapy-centric rather than dose-centric model has major clinical implications. It supports prioritizing breadth of evidence-based therapy first, followed by individualized dose optimization according to tolerance and clinical trajectory.

8.4.1. Need for structured decision frameworks

Collectively, these evolving concepts reveal a major gap in contemporary HFrEF management: the evidence base clearly defines which therapies improve outcomes, yet provides limited practical guidance on how to integrate them across diverse real-world scenarios.

The absence of structured, phenotype-informed decision pathways contributes to variability in care, delayed optimization, and persistent underuse of proven therapies. Addressing this unmet need requires synthesis of established trial evidence with bedside clinical reasoning.

While current guidelines clearly define the four foundational pillars of GDMT, they provide limited guidance on how to prioritize and sequence therapies when competing hemodynamic, renal, and rhythm-related constraints coexist.

8.5.1. Interpretation of the decision matrix

This matrix emphasizes prioritization according to dominant clinical constraints rather than rigid adherence to a uniform treatment sequence.

SGLT2 inhibitors emerge as frequent early options across several phenotypes because of their rapid onset of benefit, favorable safety profile, and minimal impact on blood pressure or heart rate. Beta-blockers may be prioritized in tachycardic phenotypes, whereas decongestion and stabilization may precede aggressive titration in congested or low-output states.

Importantly, the framework supports a therapycentric philosophy in which early exposure to all disease-modifying agents is favored over delayed attainment of target doses with fewer therapies.

8.6. Positioning relative to current guidelines

The proposed matrix is deliberately complementary to existing guideline recommendations. While international guidelines emphasize early initiation of all foundational therapies, they stop short of offering phenotype-specific prioritization strategies.

By synthesizing established evidence with evolving clinical insights, this framework seeks to bridge the gap between guideline directives and bedside implementation.

9. Discussion

The major challenge in modern HFrEF management is no longer lack of effective therapies, but failure to deliver proven therapies rapidly, safely, and sustainably. This implementation gap is increasingly recognized as one of the principal determinants of residual morbidity and mortality in contemporary practice.

Our review highlights an important tension between evidence derived from controlled clinical trials and the realities of routine care. Trial populations are often younger, more stable, and less multimorbid than real-world patients. In contrast, everyday practice frequently involves frailty, fluctuating renal function, hypotension, polypharmacy, and fragmented follow-up systems. As a result, clinicians commonly face trade-offs that are not directly answered by guidelines.

Another key insight is that therapeutic inertia is often driven more by uncertainty than by contraindication. Many patients remain untreated not because therapy is impossible, but because clinicians lack confidence regarding sequencing, monitoring, or tolerability under imperfect conditions. In this context, pragmatic phenotype-based frameworks may help translate evidence into action.

Importantly, future progress in HFrEF may depend less on discovering a fifth or sixth foundational drug class and more on optimizing delivery of existing therapies. Implementation pathways, multidisciplinary clinics, digital monitoring, pharmacist-led titration, and system-level affordability interventions may yield substantial gains.

Finally, personalization should not be mistaken for deviation from evidence-based care. Rather, precision implementation means applying proven therapies intelligently according to phenotype, vulnerability, and patient priorities.

9.1. Non-pharmacologic and device-based therapies

Although this review primarily focuses on pharmacologic guideline-directed medical therapy, comprehensive management of HFrEF extends beyond medication optimization alone. Device-based therapies such as implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICD) and cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) remain essential in appropriately selected patients, particularly those with persistent left ventricular systolic dysfunction or conduction abnormalities despite optimized GDMT [20,21].

In addition, structured exercise rehabilitation, multidisciplinary heart failure management programs, transcatheter interventions for secondary mitral regurgitation in selected patients, and timely referral for advanced heart failure therapies including left ventricular assist devices or cardiac transplantation are important complementary components of contemporary care [22–24]. Integration of these strategies alongside GDMT is essential to achieving optimal long-term outcomes.

10. Clinical implications and future directions

The transition from guideline awareness to effective implementation remains a central challenge in HFrEF management. While contemporary guidelines clearly define therapies that improve outcomes, real-world practice continues to be characterized by delayed initiation, incomplete treatment combinations, and failure to achieve durable optimization.

The phenotype-based decision matrix proposed in this review offers a practical tool to support clinicians in translating guideline recommendations into individualized treatment strategies. By prioritizing therapies according to dominant clinical constraints―such as hypotension, renal vulnerability, tachycardia, congestion, or frailty―this framework may facilitate earlier exposure to life-saving therapies while preserving safety.

Successful implementation requires more than prescribing decisions alone. Structured follow-up, laboratory reassessment, patient education, multi-disciplinary heart failure teams, nurse-led titration clinics, pharmacist-supported medication review, and digital decision support tools are increasingly relevant to achieving sustained GDMT optimization.

Future guideline updates should move beyond simply listing recommended therapies toward providing more actionable implementation pathways, including phenotype-informed sequencing strategies, pragmatic monitoring schedules, and approaches for older or multimorbid patients. Prospective validation of personalized treatment frameworks through pragmatic trials and registry-based studies is warranted.

10.1. Toward precision implementation of GDMT

Ultimately, the next phase of progress in HFrEF management is likely to depend less on discovery of new pharmacologic classes and more on improving the precision, speed, and durability with which proven therapies are delivered. Aligning established evidence with practical bedside decision-making may enable broader and earlier use of life-saving treatments in everyday care.

10.2. Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, it was conducted as a structured narrative state-of-the-art review rather than a formal systematic review or meta-analysis; therefore, study selection may be subject to selection bias despite a structured search strategy. Second, although priority was given to landmark randomized trials, international guidelines, and high-quality contemporary evidence, the rapidly evolving nature of heart failure therapeutics means that emerging data may further refine some of the concepts discussed.

Third, several proposed phenotype-based sequencing strategies are derived from indirect evidence, subgroup analyses, and expert clinical interpretation rather than prospective randomized comparisons. Finally, implementation barriers vary substantially across healthcare systems, potentially limiting universal applicability of some recommendations.

11. Conclusions

Guideline-directed medical therapy has fundamentally transformed the prognosis of HFrEF, with robust evidence supporting substantial reductions in mortality and heart failure hospitalization through comprehensive neurohormonal modulation. The benefits of the four foundational pharmacologic classes are well established, and early combined initiation represents the standard of care for eligible patients.

Despite this strong evidence base, persistent gaps between guideline recommendations and real-world implementation continue to limit the full therapeutic potential of GDMT. Delayed initiation, incomplete treatment combinations, therapeutic inertia, frailty, multimorbidity, and health-system barriers remain common obstacles.

This state-of-the-art review synthesizes established evidence with evolving clinical insights and proposes a phenotype-based framework to support individualized implementation of GDMT beyond generic treatment algorithms. Future advances in HFrEF management are likely to depend less on the development of novel therapies and more on optimizing the use of currently available life-saving treatments.
