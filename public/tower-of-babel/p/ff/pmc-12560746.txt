Reply: Quantifying Research Productivity From First-year US Integrated Plastic Surgery Residents After USMLE Step I Pass/Fail Conversion
Authors: Andrew Hannoudi, Benjamin A. Sarac, Jeffrey E. Janis
Published: 2025

We respectfully address the methodological concerns posed by Bohler and Chaiyasate1 with regard to the interpretation of our recent study investigating the research productivity of first-year US integrated plastic surgery residents following the transition of the United States Medical Licensing Examination Step I examination to a pass/fail scoring system.2 Regarding the concern challenging the inferred causality of our findings, we must clarify that the primary aim of our study was not to attribute causation between the match trends and the pass/fail transition, but rather to comprehensively depict the publication profile of the 2024 residency cohort. Careful phrasing was selected in our article to emphasize that the notable rise in research productivity has been influenced by various factors, in addition to the Step I pass/fail transition. We acknowledge that inclusion of data from the 2 immediately preceding residency classes may have offered stronger statistical power to explore causality. However, we argue that the 2022 and 2023 cohorts were also subject to a time lag bias, given that the limited interval between the announcement of a pass/fail examination transition and the residency application deadlines plausibly constrained the timeframe for meaningful behavioral adaptation. As such, less conclusive data may be extrapolated from these cohorts relative to the more recent group.

Furthermore, the intention of referencing the study by Mellia et al,3 which effectively characterized pre–pass/fail cohort publication characteristics from 2019 to 2021, was not to offer a direct control group, but rather to draw contextual comparisons between the publication characteristics. Regardless, we recognize the methodological variability between the 2 studies and acknowledge that the 3-month cutoff date discrepancy likely weakened the validity of this comparison. We do, however, counter the notion of a modest discrepancy between the inclusion criteria of the 2 studies based on the inclusion of book chapters in our publication count, as these media comprised a debatably negligible 15 (1.0%) of the 1429 total counted publications from the 2024 cohort.

Finally, we acknowledge the stated limitations of variability in application profiles. Applicants who received numeric Step I scores as a result of dedicated research years, reapplication, extended training pathways (eg, MD/PhD and MD/MPH programs), or other explanations were included in this study without further screening by our authors. As stated by Bohler and Chaiyasate,1 we agree that this is an inherent limitation of the use of public data sources rather than an indication of poor methodological oversight.

We appreciate the opportunity to respond to the constructive academic dialogue pertaining to our published study and acknowledge the limitations to which it was subject. Future research assessing the publication output characteristics of upcoming residency cohorts will continue to assess the evolving landscape of our research-driven field, to which we are pleased to have offered a contribution with our findings.
