Formation of a reducing microenvironment and regulation of protein supersulfidation by gut microbial supersulfides
Authors: Jun Uchiyama, Yoshimi Shimizu, Takamitsu Unoki, Shingo Kasamatsu, Tomoaki Ida, Hideshi Ihara, Yun-Gi Kim, Koji Hase, Yuri Miura, Tomohiko Maehama, Maiko Kusano, Keitaro Umezawa, Masahiro Akiyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSupersulfides, sulfur species containing catenated sulfur atoms, are potent reducing agents produced by diverse organisms. Although their intracellular functions are increasingly recognized, the ecological and physiological importance of gut microbial supersulfides remains poorly understood. In this study, we explored two complementary aspects of gut microbiota-derived supersulfide. First, by assessing the reducing activity, we found that bacterial supersulfides contribute to the enhancement of the extracellular reducing capacity. In particular, Dorea longicatena and Enterocloster bolteae exhibit strong cystine-dependent supersulfide production, which is associated with protection against oxidative stress. Second, beyond their ecological roles, supersulfides influence protein supersulfidation, which is a reversible post-translational modification. Supersulfidated proteins have been detected across multiple commensal taxa with species-specific profiles. This modification is redox-sensitive and modulated by extracellular supersulfides. Members of the Lactobacillaceae family are particularly susceptible to exogenous supersulfides. Supersulfidation involves proteins linked to core microbial processes, including bile acid metabolism, suggesting their potential role in tuning bacterial functions. Together, these findings suggest that microbial supersulfides act as dual regulators: (i) contributing to a protective reducing milieu and (ii) modulating bacterial protein function through supersulfidation. By highlighting post-translational control in gut bacteria and their sensitivity to the local redox environment, this work broadens the current models of microbial redox biology and provides a basis for linking bacterial sulfur metabolism with gut ecosystem stability and host-microbe interactions.Keywords: Redox, Gut microbiota, Supersulfides, Oxidative stress, Supersulfidation

Introduction

The human gut harbors trillions of microorganisms collectively referred to as the gut microbiota [1]. Advances in microbiome research have established gut microbiota as a central modulator of host physiology and pathophysiology through the production of diverse metabolites. Well-characterized examples include short-chain fatty acids, bile acids, and tryptophan-derived compounds, which are often studied for their roles as receptor ligands and signaling molecules in the host [2]. However, these compounds are not solely synthesized to support host health.

Metabolite synthesis serves fundamental ecological purposes, such as promoting survival, niche adaptation, and competitiveness within the gut ecosystem. Among these, interactions with the host immune system are particularly significant: microbial metabolites help establish an immunologically tolerant environment that favors bacterial persistence [[3], [4], [5]]. Certain microbial metabolites act as chemical mediators that modify the intestinal lumen. For example, they influence acidity [[6], [7], [8]] and oxygen availability [[9], [10], [11]], thereby shaping the microbial community structure. These ecological roles highlight the fact that microbial metabolites function not only as host-directed signals but also as environmental determinants within the gut ecosystem.

In addition to pH regulation, oxygen tension, and interspecies competition [12,13], the redox status of the intestinal lumen is a critical, yet underexplored factor in gut microbial physiology [14,15]. Although the lumen is a highly reducing environment, the molecular contributors to this state and their significance to the microbes remain poorly defined.

Sulfur-containing metabolites exhibit potent reducing capacity and versatile biochemical reactivity [[16], [17], [18]]. The colon is one of the most active sites for sulfur metabolism, and gut microbiota plays a central role in this process [19,20]. Supersulfides, such as cysteine hydropersulfide (CysSSH), are a particularly notable class of sulfur metabolites. These molecules contain a sulfane sulfur atom covalently bound to another sulfur atom, which confers unique reactivity. Compared with canonical thiols, such as cysteine (CysSH), supersulfides have a lower pKa, making them highly reactive and efficient scavengers of reactive oxygen species, reactive nitrogen species, and lipid radicals [21]. Consequently, supersulfides are key contributors to cellular redox homeostasis.

In mammalian cells, supersulfides are produced from cystine or cysteine via enzymes, such as cystathionine γ-lyase, cystathionine β-synthase, and cysteinyl-tRNA synthetases [[22], [23], [24], [25]]. 3-Mercaptopyruvate sulfur transferase (3-MST) is another key enzyme that produces cysteine hydropersulfide (CysSSH) and glutathione hydropersulfide (GSSH), along with hydrogen persulfide (H2S2) and hydrogen trisulfide (H2S3) [26,27]. Recently, we identified supersulfides as previously unrecognized metabolites produced by gut microbiota [28]. Our previous work demonstrated that certain bacterial species produce supersulfides at high levels and thereby enhancing the host antioxidant capacity and protecting against oxidative stress-induced injury. Despite this progress, the biological significance of supersulfide production by gut bacteria remains unclear.

In addition to their antioxidant properties, low-molecular-weight supersulfides serve as essential sulfur donor facilitators of the post-translational modification of protein thiol (–SH) groups, termed supersulfidation [22,23,27,29,30]. This modification protects cysteine residues from irreversible oxidation and can directly modulate enzyme activity, thereby conferring a signaling role to supersulfides [[31], [32], [33], [34], [35], [36]]. Supersulfidation is now recognized as an important post-translational modification in mammalian redox biology. However, the occurrence and function of protein supersulfidation in commensal gut bacteria are poorly understood.

We hypothesized that microbiota-derived supersulfides shape the reducing microenvironment of the gut lumen and bacterial physiology. In this study, we investigated two complementary aspects of gut microbiota-associated supersulfides: (1) their role in maintaining the reducing environment of the intestinal lumen and (2) their function as mediators of protein supersulfidation in gut bacteria. We further demonstrate the widespread occurrence of protein supersulfidation across diverse commensal species and provide evidence for its regulatory role in bacterial physiology. Together, these findings reveal that microbial supersulfide production not only supports host antioxidant defenses but also shapes the redox landscape and functional biology of the gut microbiota.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

Escherichia coli K12 was purchased from the National Bio Resource Project (Shizuoka, Japan). Anaerotruncus colihominis 15631T, Bacteroides caccae 9498T, Bacteroides ovatus 5824T, Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron 5827T, Bacteroides uniformis 5828T, Bifidobacterium adolescentis 1275T, Bifidobacterium longum 1217T, Blautia hansenii 14655T, Clostridium indolis 1380T, Clostridium scindens 6567T, Collinsella aerofaciens 10188T, Coprococcus comes 31264T, Dorea formidigenerans 31256T, Dorea longicatena 11232T, Enterocloster bolteae 12243T, Ligilactobacillus murinus 1717T, Limosilactobacillus reuteri 1112T, Parabacteroides johnsonii 13406T, Parabacteroides merdae 9497T, Pseudoflavonifractor capillosus 32126T, and Ruminococcus gnavus 6515T were purchased from the Japan Collection of Microorganisms (Ibaraki, Japan). All bacteria except for L. murinus 1717T and L. reuteri 1112T were grown in AccuDia GAM Broth, Modified (Shimadzu Diagnostics Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), whereas L. murinus 1717T and L. reuteri 1112T were grown in MRS broth (BD Difco, Becton, Dickinson and Company, Sparks, MD, USA) at 37 °C under anaerobic conditions. Optical density at 600 nm (OD600) was measured using the SpectraMax i3 (Molecular Devices, LLC, San Jose, CA, USA) to assess bacterial growth.

Mice

C57BL/6 mice were kept under conventional conditions and fed an MF diet (Oriental Yeast Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) ad libitum throughout the experimental period. All mice used in this study were female (8–10-week-old) and acclimated for at least 1 week before being used in experimental procedures. All mice were housed at the animal facility at Showa Medical University. They were kept at 21–22 °C under a 12 h alternating light–dark cycle. All animal studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Showa Medical University (23A022). All experiments were conducted in accordance with the Guidelines for the Proper Conduct of Animal Experiments issued by the Science Council of Japan.

Animal treatment

Eight-week-old conventional mice were provided access to water with or without antibiotics–1 mg/mL ampicillin (Nacalai Tesque, Inc., Kyoto, Japan) and 0.5 mg/mL vancomycin (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Osaka, Japan)–for 2 weeks and fecal samples (freshly voided fecal pellets and distal colonic luminal contents; collectively referred to as “feces”) were subsequently collected. Eight-week-old mice were administered a CE-2 diet (CLEA Japan, Inc., Tokyo, Japan) or high cystine diet (CE-2 containing 4% cystine); their feces were also collected.

LC-ESI-MS/MS analysis

The levels of sulfur nucleophiles, including supersulfides, were measured by Liquid Chromatography-Electrospray Ionization-Tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC-ESI-MS/MS) analysis using β-(4-hydroxyphenyl)ethyl iodoacetamide (HPE-IAM; Toronto Research Chemicals, Toronto, Canada). Fecal samples and bacterial cells were disrupted by bead beating at 1500 rpm for 10 min in RIPA buffer [20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 1% IGEPAL CA-630 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 0.1% SDS, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate (Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan)] containing 1% protease inhibitor cocktail using a Shake Master NEO (Bio Medical Science, Tokyo, Japan). The supernatant obtained by centrifugation (49 μL) was treated with 250 mM HPE-IAM (1 μL) at 37 °C for 30 min. Likewise, bacterial culture supernatants were treated with HPE-IAM for 30 min at 37 °C. These reaction solutions were diluted 4-fold with methanol (MeOH) to remove protein. A solution of 0.1% formic acid (FA) containing known amounts of isotope-labeled internal standards was added, and samples were analyzed using LC-ESI-MS/MS to determine the levels of sulfur nucleophiles [23,37]. Cholic acid was quantified according to previously reported methods [38], using cholic acid-d4 (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI, USA) as an internal standard. Cystine was quantified using an amino acid internal standard mixture (#293-73701, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Osaka, Japan) and 3-aminopyridyl-N-hydroxysuccinimidyl carbamate (APDS) (#014-23841, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corp.) according to the manufacturer's instructions. To avoid the oxidation of cysteine, samples were incubated with HPE-IAM as described above. The protein-depleted solutions, diluted 4-fold with MeOH and acidified with 0.1% FA, were used for analysis.

Single bacterial culture

After overnight culture, bacteria were pelleted using centrifugation at 2380×g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was removed and the bacterial pellets were resuspended in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) containing 100 mM HEPES (pH 6.5) with or without 1 mM l-cystine. Each sample was anaerobically incubated for 60 min at 37 °C. After incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 2380×g for 20 min at 4 °C and the supernatants were collected for analysis. Bacterial cells were washed with D-PBS(−), and the resulting pellets obtained by centrifugation were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. The H2O2-removing capacity was assessed using a cell-based hydrogen peroxide assay kit (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Reducing capacity assay

For bacterial intracellular fractions, 100 μL of bacterial lysate (1.0 mg/mL protein in 0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 7.4) were mixed with an equal volume of 10 mM 5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid (DTNB) and incubated for 5 min at room temperature. Next, 190 μL of bacterial lysate were mixed with 10 μL of WST-8 reagent (Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) and incubated for 2 h at room temperature. For bacterial extracellular fractions, 100 μL of sample was mixed with an equal volume of 1 mM DTNB, and 190 μL was mixed with 10 μL of WST-8 reagent. The fecal samples were homogenized in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 6.4) to a final concentration of 200 mg/mL. The suspensions were centrifuged at 20,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected for analysis. The WST-8 reaction was carried out for 9 h.

Oxidative stress measurement by flow cytometry

Each bacterial strain was anaerobically cultured in 2 mL of AccuDia GAM Broth, Modified until stationary phase. Bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation at 2380×g for 20 min at 4 °C and washed with 0.22 μm-filtered 0.9% NaCl. The resulting pellets were resuspended in 1 mL of 0.22 μm-filtered 0.9% NaCl containing 100 mM HEPES buffer (pH 6.5) with or without 1 mM l-cystine and incubated for 60 min at 37 °C under anaerobic conditions. The bacterial suspensions were then anaerobically exposed to H2O2 (500 μM for D. longicatena and 1 mM for E. bolteae) for 15 min at 37 °C. The samples were then labeled with 4 μM CellROX Deep Red (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for 30 min at 37 °C under anaerobic conditions to detect intracellular reactive oxygen species. The stained samples were diluted 4-fold with 0.22 μm-filtered 0.9% NaCl for subsequent flow cytometric analysis. Flow cytometric analysis was performed using a BD LSR Fortessa (Becton, Dickinson and Company, San Jose, CA, USA), and data were analyzed using FlowJo software version 10.6 (BD Biosciences, Ashland, OR, USA).

Treatment with N-acetyl-cysteine tetrasulfide (NAC-S2)

L. reuteri was cultured overnight in MRS broth and harvested by centrifugation. The medium was replaced with fresh MRS broth containing 250 μM NAC-S2 (Peptide Institute, Inc., Osaka, Japan), and the cells were anaerobically incubated for 30 min at 37 °C, followed by washing with D-PBS(−). The bacterial cells were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen.

Measurement of intracellular reactive polysulfide contents

Intracellular reactive polysulfide contents were measured as previously described, with slight modifications [39]. Bacterial cells were briefly disrupted by bead beating at 1500 rpm for 10 min in 105 μL of 100 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4) with 0.1 mm zirconia–silica beads (Central Scientific Commerce, Inc., Tokyo, Japan) and two 3 mm zirconia beads (BioSpec Products, Bartlesville, OK, USA) using a Shake Master NEO. After collecting 5 μL for protein quantification, the remaining samples were supplemented with N-ethylmaleimide, MeOH, and Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4) to achieve final concentrations of 10 mM, 80% (v/v), and 100 mM, respectively. The reaction mixtures were then incubated at 37 °C for 2 h, after which the reaction was terminated by adding 650 μL of 1% FA to 150 μL of the reaction mixture. Following centrifugation at 20,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C, the obtained supernatants were diluted 10-fold with 0.1% FA.

Ex vivo bacterial incubation with fecal suspension

Frozen fecal samples were suspended in AccuDia Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) Broth (Shimadzu Diagnostics Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) containing 100 mM HEPES buffer (pH 6.5) with or without 1 mM l-cystine to a final concentration of 20 mg/mL and incubated under anaerobic conditions for 30 min at 37 °C. The reaction mixtures were centrifuged at 2380×g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane to remove large debris and bacterial cells. The resulting supernatants were used as a fecal suspension. Bacterial cells were cultured anaerobically in AccuDia GAM Broth, Modified, for B. ovatus and B. thetaiotaomicron and MRS broth for L. murinus and L. reuteri until the stationary phase. They were harvested by centrifugation at 2380×g for 20 min at 4 °C. Cell pellets were resuspended in the prepared fecal supernatant or medium at a final bacterial density of OD600 = 0.7. Bacterial cells were incubated anaerobically for 30 min at 37 °C. Following incubation, samples were centrifuged at 2380×g for 20 min at 4 °C and washed with D-PBS(−). The resulting pellets were collected and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen.

Isolation of bacterial fraction from fecal samples

Isolation was performed as previously described [40] with slight modifications [38]. First, 50–100 mg of fecal samples were weighed, and 1 mL of D-PBS(−) was added. The mixture was vortexed thoroughly and centrifuged at 200×g for 5 min at 4 °C and the supernatant was collected. This extraction procedure was repeated three additional times (four times in total). Approximately 4 mL of pooled supernatant was produced, which was then centrifuged at 20,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C to collect the pellet.

Gel staining of supersulfidated protein (pull-down assay)

Each sample was disrupted by vigorous shaking with a Shake Master NEO at 1500 rpm for 10 min in 300 μL of lysis buffer. The lysis buffer contained 40 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100 (Nacalai Tesque, Inc., Kyoto, Japan), 0.1% SDS, 3 mM l-tyrosine, and 1% protease inhibitor cocktail. Disruption was performed with 0.1 mm zirconia–silica beads and two 3 mm zirconia beads. Bacterial lysate supernatants were collected by centrifugation at 16,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Takara Bio, Inc., Shiga, Japan). After adjusting the protein concentration, samples were incubated with 0.5 mM EZ-Link Iodoacetyl-PEG2-Biotin (Thermo Scientific, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for 30 min at 37 °C to biotinylate thiol (-SH) and supersulfidated thiol (-SSH) residues.

High molecular weight (HMW) fractions were obtained by concentration using a 3K Amicon Ultra Centrifugal Filter Unit (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany). The resulting samples were adjusted to 300 μL with lysis buffer, and 100 μL of Pierce™ Control Agarose Resin (Thermo Scientific, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was added to block non-specific binding. After 2 h of incubation at 4 °C, the samples were centrifuged at 1000×g for 5 min at 4 °C, and 300 μL of supernatant was collected and incubated with 100 μL of Pierce High Capacity NeutrAvidin Agarose (Thermo Scientific, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for 16 h at 4 °C. Next, the samples were washed four times with wash buffer [40 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS, and 1% protease inhibitor cocktail] (300 μL each wash, centrifuged at 1000×g for 5 min at 4 °C). The samples were split into two equal portions: one received 50 μL of wash buffer for all proteins, and the other received 50 μL of elution buffer [40 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS, 40 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP), and 1% protease inhibitor cocktail] for supersulfidated proteins, following incubation for 40 min at 4 °C. The reaction mixtures were centrifuged, and the resulting supernatants and beads were mixed with 4 × SDS-PAGE sample buffer (Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) containing 2-mercaptoethanol (4:1) at a sample-to-buffer ratio of 3:1 (v/v), then boiled at 95 °C for 10 min. Gel staining was performed using Flamingo™ Fluorescent Protein Gel Stain (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA).

Pronase digestion

The intact (-SH) and supersulfidated (-SSH) thiol residues were quantified as previously described, with slight modifications [23,31]. Samples were briefly homogenized with RIPA buffer and then centrifuged at 16,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was treated with 5 mM HPE-IAM at 37 °C for 30 min to label the protein –SSH. To obtain HMW fractions, the reaction mixture was concentrated using a 3K Amicon Ultra Centrifugal Filter Unit. Protein concentration was measured using a BCA Protein Assay Kit. Protein at a concentration of 0.6 mg/mL was digested with 1.8 mg/mL pronase from Streptomyces griseus (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) in 50 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.5) for 7 h. After digestion, samples were analyzed by LC-ESI-MS/MS.

Ex vivo exposure of fecal suspensions to H2O2

Frozen fecal samples were homogenized in a pre-reduced 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 6.4) to a final concentration of 50 mg/mL. Large debris were removed using a 100 μm cell strainer and the resulting fecal suspensions were exposed to 1000 μM H2O2 for 60 min at 37 °C in an anaerobic chamber. Pellets were harvested by centrifugation at 2380×g for 40 min at 4 °C and washed with D-PBS(−).

Sample preparation for proteome analysis of supersulfided proteins

Each sample was disrupted by vigorous shaking with a Shake Master NEO at 1500 rpm for 10 min in 300 μL of lysis buffer (50 mM HEPES (pH 7.0), 150 mM NaCl, 10% NP-40, 3 mM tyrosine, 1% protease inhibitor). Proteins were labeled with iodoacetyl-PEG-biotin at 37 °C for 30 min, then subsequently precipitated using the chloroform/methanol method. The resulting precipitate was washed with methanol and redissolved in RIPA buffer (50 mM HEPES (pH 7.0), 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.2% SDS). For the pull-down experiment, 20 μg of the protein was diluted to a final concentration of 0.2 μg/μL, and 10 μL of Pierce™ High Capacity NeutrAvidin™ Agarose bead slurry was added. The mixture was then incubated for 2 h at room temperature with gentle rotation. The beads were washed four times with RIPA buffer, four times with PBS, and subsequently transferred to a new 1.5 mL tube. The beads were treated with 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate containing 10 mM TCEP and 0.02% SDS by incubating the mixture at 37 °C for 30 min. The supernatant was collected into a new 1.5 mL tube. Alkylation was performed by adding 500 mM iodoacetamide (IAA) in water (to a final concentration of 25 mM) and incubating the solution in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. The protein solution was then subjected to enzymatic digestion by adding 0.5 μg of Trypsin Platinum (Mass Spectrometry Grade, Promega, Madison, WI, USA) and incubating overnight at 37 °C. Following digestion, the reaction was acidified (pH < 3) with trifluoroacetic acid (TFA). The resulting peptide solution was first purified using a GL-tip SCX (GL Science, Tokyo, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The eluates were combined and dried in vacuo, then subsequently redissolved in 2% MeCN, 0.1% TFA in water, and desalted using a GL-tip SDB (GL Science, Tokyo, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The final eluate was dried in vacuo, reconstituted in 4% MeCN, 0.1% FA in water, and subjected to LC-MS/MS proteomic analysis.

For the measurement of total proteome changes in the non-treated and reagent-treated samples (input samples), 10 μg of lysate protein was subjected to reduction, alkylation, and enzymatic digestion using the S-Trap column (ProtiFi, Fairport, NY, USA). The experiment was performed following the manufacturer's protocol, with the following modifications: dithiothreitol (DTT) was used as the reducing agent, and IAA was used as the alkylating agent. Peptide solutions eluted from the S-Trap column were dried in vacuo, then subsequently redissolved in 2% MeCN, 0.1% TFA in water. The solution was then desalted using a GL-Tip SDB according to the manufacturer's instructions. The eluate was dried in vacuo, reconstituted in 4% MeCN, 0.1% FA in water, and subjected to LC-MS/MS proteomic analysis.

Plasmid construction using the in-fusion cloning system

The insert DNA and vector backbone were amplified by PCR using primers containing 15-bp overlapping sequences (see Table S1 for primers). To remove the template plasmids, the PCR products were digested with DpnI (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan) at 37 °C for 1 h. The digested DNA fragments were purified using a FavorPrep™ GEL/PCR Purification Mini Kit (FAVORGEN, Pingtung City, Taiwan). The purified insert and vector were assembled using an In-Fusion HD Cloning Kit (Takara Bio, Inc., Shiga, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The reaction mixture was transformed into E. coli DH5α (Nippon Gene Co., Ltd., Toyama, Japan) competent cells. Plasmids were extracted from the transformants using the FavorPrep™ Plasmid DNA Extraction Mini Kit (FAVORGEN, Pingtung city, Taiwan), and the insert sequence was verified by Sanger DNA sequencing.

Protein expression and purification

The expression plasmid was transformed into BL21(DE3) DynaCompetent® Cells (BioDynamics Laboratory Inc., Tokyo, Japan) under kanamycin selection. Overnight cultures grown in fresh LB medium with kanamycin (50 μg/mL) were diluted 1:500 and grown at 37 °C. Expression was induced at an optical density (OD600) of 0.4–0.5 by the addition of 500 μM isopropyl-β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG; Nacalai Tesque, Inc., Kyoto, Japan), and the cells further incubated at 24 °C for 18 h. The cells were harvested by centrifugation at 6000×g for 5 min at 4 °C. The pelleted cells were then resuspended in lysis buffer (20 mM bicine (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, 0.25 mM TCEP, and 20 mM imidazole). The resuspended cells were sonicated, and the lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 11,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was then mixed with Ni Sepharose™ 6 Fast Flow resin (Cytiva, Uppsala, Sweden), which had previously been equilibrated with lysis buffer. The column was washed twice with 10 mL of lysis buffer to remove non-specifically bound proteins. Finally, the target protein was eluted with 20 mM bicine buffer (pH 8.0) containing 150 mM NaCl, 0.25 mM TCEP, and 200 mM imidazole. The collected fractions were analyzed by SDS–PAGE. The purified fractions were combined and concentrated, followed by dialysis against the storage buffer (D-PBS(−) at pH 7.5 with 0.25 mM TCEP and 5% glycerol).

Peptide analysis of recombinant BSH

rBSH in D-PBS(−) with 5% glycerol (0.6 mg/mL) was incubated with 100 μM Na2S4 (Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Inc., Kumamoto, Japan) at room temperature for 10 min. Samples were prepared as previously described [41], with minor modifications. The Na2S4/HPE-IAM-treated rBSH sample (3 μg) was diluted with ultrapure water and purified using an Amicon 3 K filter (15,000×g for 30 min). The concentrated protein solution (approximately 60 μL) was mixed with 20 μL of 200 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.0) containing 3 mM tyrosine and digested with 0.6 μg of Trypsin Platinum (Mass Spectrometry Grade, Promega) at 37 °C for 3 h. The solution was subsequently acidified by adding 10% TFA and the resulting peptide mixture was desalted using GL-Tip SDB (GL Science) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The eluate was dried in vacuo, re-dissolved in 2% MeCN and 0.1% FA in distilled water, and subjected to LC-MS/MS analysis.

Enzyme kinetics

rBSH, incubated with or without Na2S4 as described above, was added to D-PBS(−) containing 5% glycerol at a final concentration of 7.5 ng/μL. Sodium glycocholate hydrate (Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) solutions in phosphate buffer (pH 6.0) at various concentrations were pre-warmed to 37 °C in a heating block. The reaction was initiated by adding rBSH solution to the substrate solution (50-fold dilution). After a 2 min incubation, the reaction was arrested by 4-fold dilution with MeOH, followed by centrifugation at 9000×g for 5 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were mixed with equal volumes of water.

For the bacterial assay, L. reuteri was cultured overnight and treated with or without NAC-S2 as described above. A 10 mM solution of sodium glycocholate hydrate (Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was added to achieve a final concentration of 1 mM. At each time point, an aliquot (100 μL) of the mixture was collected and centrifuged at 9000×g for 2 min at 4 °C. The reaction was terminated in the same manner as for the rBSH assay.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (Version 10.6.0, GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), as specified in each Figure legend. Unpaired or paired Student's t-tests were used as appropriate. When data did not meet assumptions for parametric testing, nonparametric two-tailed Mann–Whitney U tests were used for two-group comparisons. For multiple-group comparisons, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's, Dunnett's, or Holm–Šídák multiple-comparisons test was used. For two-factor analyses, two-way ANOVA followed by the Holm–Šídák multiple-comparisons test was used.

Results

Gut bacterial supersulfides are linked to fecal reducing capacity in the distal gut

To examine the contribution of gut microbiota to supersulfide pools and reducing capacity in the distal gut, we treated mice with antibiotics and quantified supersulfides and related thiols in fecal samples using LC-ESI-MS/MS. Antibiotic administration markedly reduced total bacterial marker gene copies (tuf) per gram of feces, consistent with a marked reduction in total bacterial biomass (Fig. S1A). We also observed a reduction in the fecal abundance of Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae families previously implicated in CysSSH production [28] (Fig. S1B). Under basal conditions, CysSH levels were higher in the small intestine, whereas CysSSH was predominantly detected in the cecal contents and feces. Notably, CysSSH levels in these distal intestinal compartments significantly decreased following antibiotic treatment (Fig. 1A and Fig. S1C–D), suggesting a microbiota-associated contribution to distal-gut supersulfide pools. In contrast, cystine, a substrate for microbial supersulfide biosynthesis, accumulated in the cecal contents and feces of antibiotic-treated mice (Fig. 1B and Fig. S1E). Consistent with these observations, ex vivo co-incubation of fecal samples with cystine showed a significant reduction in cystine consumption in antibiotic-treated mice compared to controls (Fig. S1F). Taken together, these findings support that fecal bacterial activity may contributes to cystine consumption and is potentially associated with distal-gut supersulfide and thiol readouts.

(A, B) Fecal supersulfides, related sulfur metabolites, and cystine were quantified by LC-ESI-MS/MS. (C) The reducing capacity of fecal supernatants was evaluated using WST-8 (absorbance at 460 nm). (D) The effect of N-ethylmaleimide (NEM) on the reducing capacity of fecal supernatants was evaluated using WST-8. (E, F) Mice were fed a high-cystine diet for 2 weeks. (E) Fecal sulfur metabolites were quantified by LC-ESI-MS/MS. (F) The reducing capacity of fecal supernatants was analyzed using WST-8. Data are expressed as the means ± SEM. Statistical significance was assessed using unpaired Student's t-test in (A-C, E and F) or paired Student's t-test in (D). ∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001; ns, not significant.

Next, we assessed the impact of antibiotic perturbation on fecal reducing capacity by measuring the reducing activity of fecal supernatants. WST-8, which generates a colorimetric signal upon accepting two electrons as a measure of the reducing capacity, showed decreased absorbance in the antibiotic-treated group (Fig. 1C). This result suggests a potential contribution of the gut microbiota to fecal reducing capacity. To probe the contribution of CysSH, H2S and supersulfides to this reducing capacity, we treated fecal supernatants with NEM, which reacts with these compounds [42]. NEM treatment reduced these sulfur species in the fecal supernatant (Fig. S1G) and led to a decrease in reducing capacity (Fig. 1D), supporting a contribution of NEM-reactive sulfur molecules to fecal reducing capacity.

We further tested whether increasing dietary cystine alters fecal supersulfides and reducing capacity. A high-cystine diet increased fecal supersulfide levels without affecting CysSH or H2S levels (Fig. 1E) and was accompanied by higher reducing capacity (Fig. 1F).

Collectively, these results suggest that microbiota-associated sulfur molecules, including supersulfides, may contribute to fecal reducing capacity.

Supersulfides shape the extracellular redox environment and oxidative stress resistance

To identify the gut bacterial species responsible for these reducing activities, we used monoculture systems to examine intra- and extracellular reducing capacities in response to cystine supplementation. DTNB was used as a reagent to detect DTNB-reactive reduced sulfur species. As shown in Fig. 2A, cystine supplementation primarily enhanced extracellular DTNB-reactive reduced sulfur species and the reducing capacity. Among the tested strains, Dorea longicatena and Enterocloster bolteae, previously identified as high-CysSSH-producing bacteria [28], exhibited the most pronounced increase in extracellular reducing capacity.

Consistent with this observation, cystine supplementation induced greater accumulation of supersulfides, CysSH and H2S in the extracellular space than that in the intracellular compartment (Fig. 2B and Fig. S2A). Importantly, OD600 remained stable during the 1-h cystine treatment, suggesting no major change in bacterial biomass and providing no evidence for gross cell lysis (Fig. S2B). Because these measurements do not distinguish among active export, leakage, or passive diffusion, we refer to this as extracellular accumulation of thiols and supersulfides, without implying a specific transport mechanism. We further confirmed that NEM treatment significantly suppressed the cystine-induced increase in thiol compound levels and reducing capacity, lowering them to levels below those in the control group (Fig. 2C and D).

Next, we assessed the biological significance of this reducing environment by evaluating its antioxidant capacity. Bacterial culture supernatants were collected after incubation with or without cystine and subjected to functional assays. As anticipated, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)-scavenging capacity was significantly higher in supernatants from cystine-supplemented cultures (Fig. 2E). Together with the NEM sensitivity of the extracellular reducing signal (Fig. 2C and D), these results support a contribution of NEM-reactive sulfur species in culture supernatants to H2O2 clearance. Notably, under our defined buffer assay conditions, supersulfides exhibited faster and more potent H2O2-scavenging activity than either CysSH or sodium sulfide (Na2S), a commonly used H2S donor (Fig. S2C). We also investigated the effect of the cystine-induced extracellular sulfur species on oxidative stress resistance in gut bacteria. Cystine supplementation suppressed H2O2-induced oxidative stress in D. longicatena and E. bolteae, supporting the notion that extracellular sulfur molecules, including supersulfides, contribute to bacterial antioxidative defense (Fig. 2F).

Collectively, these results suggest that bacterial sulfur metabolism promotes extracellular accumulation of sulfur molecules, thereby enhancing extracellular reducing capacity and oxidative stress protection.

Supersulfides mediate interbacterial crosstalk

Extracellular supersulfides can enter mammalian cells and increase intracellular supersulfide levels [43,44]. This raises the possibility that extracellular supersulfides may also influence bacterial intracellular reactive sulfur levels. Bacteroidaceae and Lactobacillaceae are two major families of gut microbiota that are closely associated with host physiology [45]. First, we confirmed that cystine supplementation of fecal cultures significantly increased CysSSH levels in the supernatant (Fig. 3A). Supernatants from these cultures were incubated with four representative strains of Bacteroidaceae and Lactobacillaceae families. Given the structural diversity of intracellular supersulfides, we quantified reactive sulfur as a means to evaluate the overall changes in total intracellular supersulfide levels [39,46]. Measurement of intracellular reactive sulfides revealed elevated levels in the Lactobacillaceae strains (Fig. 3B). Notably, this increase was not observed when the bacteria were directly cultured in cystine-supplemented medium (Fig. 3B). A similar increase was observed after treatment with the supersulfide donor NAC-S2 (Fig. 3C). Collectively, these findings suggest that in certain gut bacterial species, intracellular supersulfides levels can be modulated by supersulfide concentration in the surrounding extracellular milieu.

Protein supersulfidation occurs in fecal bacteria

To investigate whether diverse commensal gut bacteria also harbor supersulfidated proteins, we isolated bacterial cells from fecal suspensions using centrifugation [40]. Supersulfidated proteins were identified using a pull-down method to selectively enrich such proteins (Fig. 4A) [41,47,48]. The resulting gel images revealed the presence of supersulfidated proteins in the gut microbiota (Fig. 4B).

Next, we quantified the proportion of supersulfidated thiols (-SSH) in fecal samples and compared it with that in host tissues. Fecal samples and host tissue lysates were labeled using the HPE-IAM method, digested with pronase into single amino acids, and analyzed to quantify the –SH and –SSH content. Notably, the proportion of –SSH in fecal proteins was higher than that in host tissues (Fig. 4C). Moreover, exposure of fecal suspensions to H2O2 resulted in a decrease in protein supersulfidation (Fig. 4D). It should be noted that a decrease in free thiol (-SH) levels was also observed, indicating that general thiol oxidation occurred under these conditions (Fig. S3A). However, the concomitant loss of supersulfidated thiols (-SSH) is consistent with high redox sensitivity of –SSH residues and suggests that they may be consumed under oxidative conditions, potentially serving as a redox buffer. In contrast, protein supersulfidation levels in the feces of mice fed a high-cystine diet were elevated compared with those in the controls (Fig. 4E), accompanied by a significant increase in the supersulfidation ratio (Fig. S3B). These results suggest that as in mammalian systems, protein supersulfidation in gut bacteria is a redox-sensitive modification.

Profiling and environmental modulation of the gut bacterial supersulfidome

Although protein supersulfidation was observed in fecal bacteria and shown to be influenced by sulfur metabolism and oxidative state, its distribution across commensal species and specific protein targets remains unknown. To address these issues, we first investigated species-specific patterns of protein supersulfidation in gut bacteria. As shown in Fig. 5A, supersulfidated proteins were detected in all tested strains, although their profiles varied among species. Notably, the strains belonging to the Lactobacillaceae family exhibited weaker band intensities, suggesting that they harbored lower levels of supersulfidated proteins.

To further characterize supersulfidation profiles across species, we performed a proteome-wide analysis of supersulfidated proteins and compared their abundance. Consistent with gel-based analysis, the quantified abundance profiles of the identified supersulfidated proteins differed significantly between E. bolteae and L. reuteri (Fig. 5B). H2O2 exposure reduced protein supersulfidation in E. bolteae (Fig. S5A), and proteomic analysis revealed a corresponding decrease in the levels of individual supersulfidated proteins (Fig. S5B and Table S2). These observations are consistent with the results of the H2O2-exposure experiment using fecal samples (Fig. 4D), supporting the notion that protein supersulfidation is sensitive to the cellular redox environment. Because intracellular supersulfide levels of L. reuteri were affected by extracellular supersulfide concentrations (Fig. 3B and C), we hypothesized that protein supersulfidation would be enhanced under elevated environmental supersulfide conditions. Consistent with this hypothesis, NAC-S2 treatment increased protein supersulfidation in L. reuteri compared to that in the controls (Fig. 5C and D and Table S3). These results suggest that in L. reuteri, supersulfidation levels in a variety of proteins are modulated by exogenous supersulfide levels.

To further narrow down the candidate protein targets whose supersulfidation levels are enhanced under supersulfide-enriched conditions, we performed an ex vivo analysis using fecal suspensions in which supersulfide production was induced by cystine supplementation. Supersulfidated proteins were ranked based on their abundance ratio between the cystine-supplemented and non-supplemented conditions, and the top 10 proteins most enriched in cystine (+) groups were identified (Fig. 5E and Table S4). These proteins represent candidate targets whose supersulfidation may be modulated under supersulfide-enriched conditions.

Regulatory role of protein supersulfidation in gut bacteria

To investigate whether protein supersulfidation directly modulates enzymatic functions in gut bacteria, we focused on choloylglycine hydrolase (also known as bile salt hydrolase; BSH) as a representative target. This enzyme was selected based on two key considerations: (i) BSH was among the proteins showing the most pronounced increase in supersulfidation in ex vivo analyses (Fig. 5E), and (ii) it contained a conserved N-terminal cysteine residue within the active site across diverse bacterial species [49], suggesting a potential regulatory hotspot. Sequence analysis of the L. reuteri BSH confirmed that it contained a single cysteine residue (Cys2) located at the active site (Table S1). We first verified that this residue underwent supersulfidation with purified recombinant L. reuteri rBSH (Fig. S5A). Mass spectrometry-based proteomics identified Cys2-supersulfidated peptides, where the observation of +31.97 and + 63.94 Da mass shifts in several cysteine-containing b-ions, relative to their non-supersulfidated counterparts, provided strong evidence for the supersulfidation at Cys2 (Fig. 6A and Fig. S5B–C [41,48]). Quantitative analysis revealed that approximately 20–30% of Cys2 was supersulfidated following incubation with the supersulfide donor Na2S4 (Fig. 6B and Table S5). Importantly, this Cys2-containing peptide was found to be enriched by pull-down from L. reuteri lysates, and its abundance increased upon treatment with NAC-S2. (Fig. S5D). This supports the notion that exogenous supersulfides promote site-specific protein supersulfidation at the cellular level.

Next, we examined the functional effects of Cys2 supersulfidation on BSH activity. Global fitting analysis using the extra sum-of-squares F-test indicated a statistically significant difference between the kinetic curves of control and Na2S4 treated rBSH (p = 0.0410). A comparison of the best-fit kinetic parameters revealed that the treatment increased the catalytic efficiency (Vmax/Km) of rBSH relative to that of the control (Fig. 6C and Fig. S5E). Because physiological substrate concentrations are often below the Km, this enhancement in catalytic efficiency is likely to translate into increased enzymatic activity under in vivo–relevant conditions [50]. Consistent with the rBSH results, bile acid deconjugation activity was significantly elevated in L. reuteri treated with NAC-S2 (Fig. 6D), suggesting that supersulfidation-dependent activation of BSH occurs at the cellular level.

Discussion

This study provides evidence that gut microbiota-derived supersulfides are linked to both the extracellular redox state and bacterial protein function. Supersulfides have been extensively studied as potent reducing agents and signaling molecules in mammalian systems; however, their ecological and physiological roles in gut bacteria remain largely unexplored. By showing that bacterial supersulfides contribute to fecal reducing capacity and modulate protein function via supersulfidation, we expand the current framework of microbial redox biology.

The colon is one of the few sites where a physiologically reducing environment is consistently maintained, underscoring its biological importance in resident anaerobes. In mice fed a high-cystine diet, fecal supersulfide levels increased independently of thiols and hydrogen sulfide, and this increase coincided with an enhancement in overall reducing capacity. Consistently, in a simplified monoculture system, cystine-dependent production of CysSSH elevated the extracellular reducing potential and was associated with H2O2 clearance and increased resistance to H2O2-induced oxidative stress. However, the chemical removal of nucleophilic sulfur metabolites, including supersulfides, reduced the overall fecal reduction capacity by approximately 30%. These results indicate that bacterial supersulfides contribute to fecal reducing capacity and antioxidant defense; however, they may represent only one component of a broader network of metabolites that collectively sustain the basal reducing environment.

In addition to their ecological roles, supersulfides exert regulatory effects via protein supersulfidation. Supersulfidation in fecal proteins was more prominent than that in host organs, suggesting that the sulfur-rich and reducing gut environment may be ideal for its persistence. In mammals, protein supersulfidation is increasingly recognized as a reversible post-translational modification (PTMs) that protects cysteine residues from irreversible oxidation, while modulating protein activity in processes, such as energy metabolism and stress responses [[51], [52], [53]]. Here, we observed that bacterial protein supersulfidation broadly occurs, with species-specific profiles. This modification was sensitive to the surrounding redox environment and can be modulated by extracellular supersulfides, suggesting a potential mechanism by which bacteria integrate environmental sulfur cues into their intracellular regulation. Notably, supersulfidated proteins include enzymes involved in central microbial functions, such as bile acid metabolism, raising the possibility that this PTMs contributes to host-microbe metabolic interactions.

While most microbiome studies have focused on the microbial composition, transcriptional activity, and protein abundance, our findings highlight the potential importance of PTMs as additional regulatory layers in bacterial physiology. Although some bacterial PTMs, such as phosphorylation, have been described, their functional significance and relevance in diseases remain poorly understood [54].

Our data indicate that supersulfidation affects proteins essential for bacterial function, suggesting that PTMs may play a broader role in microbial adaptation and activity within the gut. Systematic investigation of bacterial protein supersulfidation, along with other underexplored PTMs, will be crucial for elucidating how bacteria fine-tune their functions, and how these molecular processes shape gut ecology and host physiology.

This study has several limitations. First, although we showed that fecal supersulfides partially contribute to the reducing capacity of feces, their precise role in maintaining the intestinal redox balance remains unclear. In particular, the relative contribution of supersulfides compared with that of other sulfur metabolites, such as CysSH and H2S, has not been clearly defined. Moreover, the potential physiological and pathological consequences of supersulfide production, including its impact on gut homeostasis and disease processes, remain unexplored. Future studies are needed to identify the enzymes responsible for supersulfide biosynthesis in gut bacteria and to clarify their functional importance using gnotobiotic models colonized with the corresponding knockout strains. Additionally, the precise mechanisms governing the extracellular accumulation of supersulfides remain unclear; while our ex vivo assay demonstrates that intestinal bacteria can metabolize cystine, host excretion and abiotic factors (e.g., oxygen availability), as well as post-collection oxidation, may influence the fecal sulfur pool in vivo. Second, our antibiotic regimen should be considered a perturbation rather than complete microbiota depletion. tuf qPCR indicated a marked reduction in total bacterial biomass and targeted qPCR showed that Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae—families previously reported to produce high levels of CysSSH—were strongly reduced after antibiotic treatment. However, in the absence of direct in vivo assessment of thiol- or sulfur-metabolizing functional activity, these taxonomic readouts alone cannot establish the extent to which luminal sulfur or thiol redox capacity and overall sulfur-metabolic function are altered. Third, although we identified protein supersulfidation in representative commensals, the breadth of these processes across diverse gut bacteria remains unexplored. Addressing this issue requires the development and application of advanced metaproteomic approaches capable of systematically identifying supersulfidated proteins and their related modifications, which will be critical for understanding their ecological and physiological significance.

In conclusion, our findings support a role for gut microbial supersulfide production in extracellular reducing capacity and in the modulating bacterial protein activity through supersulfidation. These functions may contribute to bacterial ecological fitness and may influence the gut redox microenvironment.
