Left Ventricular Cardiac Metastasis Causing Cerebral Emboli
Authors: SB Haastrup, E Seven, M Taskiran, KF Kofoed, LR Pedersen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Cardiac mass, Metastasis, Echocardiography, Multimodal imaging

Introduction

Cardiac metastases are rare and typically present with varied and often subtle clinical features in the early stages of disease. The most frequent presentations are shortness of breath or arrhythmia, but they can also include life-threatening emergent presentations, such as ventricular arrhythmias, intracardiac obstruction, tamponade, and cerebral emboli.1

The purpose of this report is to present a very unusual case of left ventricular cardiac metastasis with a clinical debut as a cardioembolic stroke and to demonstrate how multimodality imaging can allow noninvasive etiological diagnosis of an intracardiac mass.

Case Presentation

A 48-year-old healthy person presented at the neurology emergency department with neurological deficits, including numbness and loss of control in the left extremities. Computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging of the brain showed multiple infarctions in multiple vascular territories; consequently, the patient was initially treated with intravenous thrombolysis.

The patient reported several weeks of fatigue, fever, and severe back pain before the onset of neurological symptoms. Clinically, the patient was normotensive and afebrile and had no cardiac murmur. The 12-lead electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm (Figure 1). Blood samples revealed elevated white blood cell count (16.4 × 109/L) and C-reactive protein (71 mg/L), with no effect from oral antibiotics prescribed by the patient's general practitioner 1 week before admission. This led to a suspicion of infectious endocarditis and spondylodiscitis, and the patient was started on intravenous antibiotics and transferred to the department of cardiology for echocardiography the following day.

Twelve-lead electrocardiogram demonstrates normal sinus rhythm (84 bpm), normal axis, normal conduction, and absence of significant ST-T wave abnormalities.

Echocardiography (transthoracic and transesophageal) showed a normally structured heart with a mass attached to the lateral wall of the left ventricle near the papillary muscle with the appearance of an oscillating excrescence (Figure 2, Figure 3, Figure 4A, and Videos 1-5). All heart valves were seen without suspicion of vegetations or significant regurgitation. On the basis of echocardiography, suspected differential diagnoses included tumor, thrombus, or endocarditis in an atypical location.

Two-dimensional transthoracic echocardiography, apical five-chamber diastolic (A) and systolic (B, C) views without (A, B) and with (C) color-flow Doppler, demonstrates a large heterogeneous, protruding mass (arrow) arising from and possibly infiltrating the anterolateral myocardial wall.

Two-dimensional transesophageal echocardiography, midesophageal long-axis (80°) diastolic (A) and systolic (B) views, demonstrates a large cardiac mass (arrow; dotted yellow border) on the anterolateral wall segment of the left ventricle.

Two-dimensional transthoracic echocardiography (A) and cardiac CT (B), comparison of apical 4-chamber displays, demonstrate the large, lobulated, hypodense cardiac mass (arrows) in the left ventricle.

The next day, cardiac CT was performed, showing a normally structured heart with a solid, lobulated, hypodense mass measuring 32.8 × 25.7 mm (Figure 4B). This was followed by positron emission tomography CT (PET-CT), describing a presumably malignant primary tumor in the right kidney with metastases to the heart, spine, and retroperitoneal lymph nodes, based on fluorodeoxyglucose F18 uptake (Figure 5A, B).

PET-CT of the chest (A) and abdomen (B) demonstrates cardiac metastasis (red arrow) in the left ventricle (A) and a presumably malignant primary tumor in the right kidney (blue arrow) and metastases in the retroperitoneal lymph nodes (green arrow) and spine (yellow arrow) (B).

A kidney biopsy confirmed renal cell carcinoma (RCC). The cardiac metastasis was not considered operable, and to prevent recurrent stroke, the patient was treated with low-molecular-weight heparin. This strategy was weighted between the risk of recurrent embolic stroke and the risk of tumor bleeding.

As the prognosis for the patient was poor, the only treatment options were palliative care, immunotherapy, and radiation therapy. The patient experienced progressive decline and died a few months later.

Discussion

There are 3 main causes of cardioembolic stroke: arrhythmias, cardiac wall and chamber abnormalities, and cardiac valve disease.2 Investigation of the source of cerebral emboli with echocardiography is standard practice. In our case, infectious endocarditis was suspected, and the patient was transferred to the department of cardiology for echocardiography.

Echocardiography is the primary tool to investigate potential cardiac disease because of its easy access and noninvasive modality. Evaluating cardiac mass, however, can be challenging, and the distinction of soft tissue characteristics is less specific compared with cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR).3

Paolisso et al. introduced a diagnostic echocardiographic mass scoring system (0-9) to predict whether a cardiac mass is malignant. A higher score indicates a significantly higher risk of a mass being malignant rather than benign.4,5 When retrospectively applied to our case, the diagnostic echocardiographic mass score was 4 points (polylobate = 2, sessile = 1, and inhomogeneous = 1), which equals a predicted probability of malignancy of 65%.

Cardiac metastases often remain clinically silent for extended periods and are typically identified only when patients with a known malignancy develops symptoms, such as dyspnea, chest pain, or palpitations.3 The presentation of cardiac metastases depends on the localization within the pericardium, epicardium, myocardium, or endocardium. Localization is mainly explained by which pathway the cancer spreads—lymphatic, hematogeneous, direct extension, or intracavitary diffusion—each of which is associated with different neoplasms.6 Endocardial metastases are estimated to account for 5% of cardiac metastases.7

Most endocardial metastases are the result of invasion from the bloodstream through the heart chambers and are most often found in the right ventricle. This is thought to be because of the anchorage of cancer cells to the right heart chambers, favored by low intracavitary pressure, slower blood flow, and lighter contractile strength, and because of the fact that the neoplastic cells usually enter the right atrium from the inferior and superior vena cava. Endocardial metastasis secondary to diffusion from myocardial metastasis is also possible.7

Cardiac metastasis from RCC without inferior vena cava involvement is extremely rare and is hypothesized to occur through 1 of 2 mechanisms. The first uses microdissemination through a venous hematogeneous pathway through the renal vein to the right heart. The second occurs through reversed flow in the intrathoracic lymphatic system,8 which is supported by observations that disseminated RCC generally involves supradiaphragmatic lymph nodes, lungs, and the pericardium.9 However, our case had disseminated RCC with left ventricular endocardial metastasis.

Cardioembolic stroke related to cardiac metastasis is uncommon. Our literature review identified one similar clinical case reported by Vacter et al. in which a patient presented with cerebral emboli as the primary manifestation of cardiac metastasis from breast cancer.10 In that instance, the authors emphasized the critical role of multimodality imaging, specifically the integration of echocardiography, CMR, and PET-CT, in achieving a noninvasive etiological diagnosis of an intracardiac mass. In a review performed by Tyebally et al., they presented a flowchart suggesting a diagnostic approach of cardiac tumors with multimodality imaging.1 This approach is also endorsed in the 2022 European Society guidelines on cardio-oncology.11 In the review by Tyebally et al., they highlight that cardiac tumors are rare and should be considered as part of the differential diagnosis of any space-occupying mass noted on cardiovascular and/or thoracic imaging modalities. Furthermore, it may be possible to get close to a diagnosis without a biopsy using a structured imaging approach, and finally, the prognosis and treatment of each tumor is different, although early diagnosis is usually associated with a better outcome.1

In our case, the clinical and echocardiographic findings raised suspicion of malignancy or endocarditis with spondylodiscitis, and therefore, the patient was scheduled for an acute PET scan. The patient had a cardiac CT performed to further visualize the mass and evaluate coronary status before considering surgical resection.

As PET-CT and kidney biopsy confirmed the final diagnosis of disseminated primary kidney cancer with cardiac, spinal, and lymphatic metastases, neither CMR nor cardiac biopsy was expected to contribute further to the diagnosis or treatment. These were therefore not performed, although CMR is considered the reference standard for evaluating cardiac masses. Choosing the most appropriate and cost-effective examinations for the patient is important to ensure fast and precise diagnosis and sustainable use of resources.5,12

As the patient had disseminated cancer with a poor prognosis and the intracardiac metastasis had no hemodynamic influence, surgical resection of the intracardiac metastasis was deemed to be of high risk. The risk of recurrent stroke could be reduced with anticoagulant treatment.

Our finding represents a rarity, and as a clinician, it is important to have the most common causes of cardiac masses (thrombus and infective vegetations) and, at the same time, speculate on alternative diagnoses when clinical findings are atypical.

Conclusion

Our case highlights a very atypical presentation: a young patient with no prior health issues who suffered a stroke caused by cardiac metastasis from a kidney tumor. This case illustrates how rare cardiac findings can sometimes be the first sign of an underlying cancer. In this instance, multimodality imaging proved to be a valuable tool, allowing us to accurately identify the mass and reach the correct diagnosis through a noninvasive approach.
