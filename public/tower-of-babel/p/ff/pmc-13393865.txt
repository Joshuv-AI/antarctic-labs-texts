Dihydroxyacetone nanoparticles ameliorate osteoarthritis via P21-mediated regulation of chondrocyte repair
Authors: Chao Fang, Zhou Ji, Yankai Pan, Zhi Li, Chenxu Liu, Xinzhe Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteoarthritis (OA) is a disease characterized by progressive cartilage degeneration. P21, as a cell cycle regulator, participates in maintaining cartilage homeostasis. This study aimed to evaluate the therapeutic potential of dihydroxyacetone (DHA) and its hyaluronic acid methacrylate (HAMA)-DHA nanoparticles in improving OA through the P21-mediated PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Findings from the analyzed human cohorts and animal models revealed significantly reduced P21 expression in OA cartilage, which negatively correlated with disease severity within the examined stages. P21 overexpression enhanced chondrocyte anabolism, alleviated oxidative stress, and suppressed catabolic responses, whereas P21 deficiency exacerbated cartilage degeneration. The combined analysis of RNA sequencing and metabolomics indicated that DHA is a key metabolic node regulating P21. DHA treatment restored P21 levels, alleviated oxidative stress via the P21-mediated PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, promoted expression of anabolic markers (SOX9, COL2, and ACAN), and suppressed catabolic markers (MMP13, ADAMTS-4, and COL10). Compared to free DHA, DHA@HAMA nanoparticles significantly enhanced stability, cellular compatibility, and chondroprotective effects while enabling sustained release. This approach was observed to further promote P21-mediated PI3K/AKT signaling, which corresponded with mitigated cartilage erosion, subchondral bone loss, and OA severity. This study establishes that DHA exerts critical protective effects in OA via the P21-mediated PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most prevalent degenerative joint disease, characterized by progressive articular cartilage erosion, subchondral bone remodeling, synovial inflammation, and chronic pain that collectively impair mobility and quality of life [1]. Despite its high global burden, current therapeutic strategies remain largely palliative, focusing on symptomatic relief through nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, intra-articular corticosteroids, and hyaluronic acid injections, and joint replacement remains the only definitive treatment for end-stage disease [2, 3]. However, these approaches fail to directly address the underlying molecular mechanisms of cartilage degeneration and have limited efficacy in halting disease progression. Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop disease-modifying OA drugs that can restore chondrocyte homeostasis, prevent extracellular matrix breakdown, and promote cartilage repair.

Chondrocytes, the sole resident cells in articular cartilage, play a central role in maintaining cartilage integrity by balancing anabolic and catabolic processes [4, 5]. Accumulating evidence indicates that cellular senescence and oxidative stress significantly contribute to chondrocyte dysfunction in OA [6]. Among senescence-associated regulators, cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor P21 (also known as CDKN1A) has emerged as a key mediator linking stress signaling to cartilage degeneration [7, 8]. Unlike previous assumptions of pathogenicity, recent studies demonstrate that P21 expression is markedly reduced in osteoarthritic cartilage, and its downregulation correlates with disease severity. Functionally, P21 plays a protective role by suppressing oxidative stress, attenuating catabolic remodeling, and preserving anabolic activity [9, 10]. Genetic studies further reveal that P21 overexpression alleviates OA progression, whereas its deletion exacerbates cartilage degeneration in vivo, underscoring its disease-ameliorating function and therapeutic significance [11]. Building upon these foundations, our current study, through multi-dimensional evaluation using specific clinical cohorts, animal models, and cell experiments, provides further corroborating evidence regarding P21 dysregulation. Within our analyzed human cartilage samples—encompassing distinct OA stages from weight-bearing regions—P21 was significantly downregulated. Its expression level exhibited a significant negative correlation with the severity of OA (including KL classification, degree of cartilage defect, and OARSI score). These observations support the emerging concept that P21 extends beyond its traditional role as a mere aging-promoting factor, acting instead as a non-classical protective regulator in maintaining cartilage homeostasis.

Recent advances in integrative transcriptomic and metabolomic profiling have identified metabolic alterations as critical drivers of OA pathogenesis [11, 12]. In particular, dihydroxyacetone (DHA), a glycolysis-related metabolite, may serve as a key regulatory factor that activates mTORC1 signaling, linking glucose availability to cell growth and lipid synthesis, potentially influencing chondrocyte repair mechanisms [13]. DHA has shown promising antidotal effects against both cyanide and phosphine toxicity in vivo. In the case of cyanide poisoning, DHA increases the LD50, prevents convulsions, and preserves cytochrome oxidase activity in vital organs, especially when used in combination with sodium thiosulfate [14]. Similarly, DHA mitigates phosphine (PH3) poisoning by restoring mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase activity and significantly increasing the LC50 of PH3, highlighting its potential as an effective antidote for both toxins [15]. These findings highlight DHA as a promising candidate for metabolic reprogramming in OA treatment. Notably, mounting evidence has established a critical mechanistic link between P21 and OA pathogenesis: the downregulation of P21 acts as a core driving force for chondrocyte dysfunction and cartilage degeneration in OA. DHA has been well-documented to exert chondroprotective, anti-inflammatory effects, and regulate the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [4, 16], which is tightly correlated with P21-mediated chondrocyte homeostasis. Collectively, these findings strongly support the rationale for selecting DHA as a promising agent to modulate the P21 signaling axis, creating a logical transition from mechanistic investigation of P21 to the development of DHA-based therapeutic strategies for OA. Nevertheless, the clinical translation of DHA is hampered by its poor stability, rapid clearance, and limited intra-articular retention [17]. Nanotechnology-based delivery systems have emerged as effective strategies to overcome these limitations, providing sustained release, enhanced bioavailability, and targeted delivery to joint tissues [17, 18]. Hyaluronic acid methacrylate (HAMA), a biocompatible and injectable hydrogel, has been widely utilized for intra-articular applications owing to its natural affinity for cartilage, viscoelastic properties, and ability to serve as a drug carrier [19]. Encapsulation of DHA within HAMA-based nanoparticles may not only stabilize the compound and prolong its release but also amplify its therapeutic efficacy by synergistically enhancing P21-mediated PI3K/AKT signaling and chondroprotection.

Given the pivotal role of P21 in OA pathogenesis and the promising regulatory function of DHA, this study aimed to investigate whether DHA-loaded HAMA nanoparticles (DHA@HAMA) could serve as an effective therapeutic strategy for OA.

Methods

DHA@HAMA Synthesis

HAMA was synthesized by dissolving sodium hyaluronate (200 kDa; Sigma-Aldrich) in deionized water (1% w/v) and reacting with methacrylic anhydride at 4 °C for 24 h (pH 8.0, adjusted with 1 M NaOH). The product was dialyzed (12–14 kDa cut-off) against deionized water for 5 days and lyophilized. For nanoparticle preparation, DHA ethanol solution was added dropwise to HAMA in PBS (pH 7.4) under stirring, followed by UV crosslinking with 0.05% Irgacure 2959 (365 nm, 5 min). Nanoparticles were collected using centrifugation (15,000 × g, 30 min), washed with PBS, and resuspended; blank HAMA particles were prepared identically without DHA.

Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS)

The hydrodynamic diameter and size distribution of DHA@HAMA nanoparticles were determined using DLS with a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern Instruments, UK). Freshly prepared nanoparticle suspensions were diluted in deionized water to an appropriate concentration to avoid multiple scattering effects. Measurements were performed at 25 °C with a scattering angle of 90°, and each sample was analyzed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. Zeta potential was simultaneously assessed to evaluate nanoparticle surface charge and colloidal stability.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

The morphology and ultrastructure of DHA@HAMA nanoparticles were examined using TEM (JEM-2100, JEOL Ltd., Japan). Diluted suspensions were deposited onto carbon-coated copper grids, air-dried after 1–2 min adsorption. To enhance image contrast, certain grids were negatively stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid (pH 7.0). TEM images were captured at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV, and representative fields were recorded to evaluate nanoparticle morphology and size.

X-ray Diffraction (XRD) analysis

The crystalline structure of DHA@HAMA nanoparticles was analyzed using XRD (D8 Advance, Bruker AXS, Germany). Freeze-dried powders were ground, mounted on a glass holder, and examined under Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). Patterns were recorded over a 2θ range of 5–80° with a step size of 0.02° and scan rate of 2°/min. Diffractograms were compared with reference standards to assess DHA crystalline phases before and after encapsulation, with changes in peak intensity or broadening confirming successful incorporation into the HAMA matrix and maintenance of a stable crystalline state.

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)

The chemical structure of DHA@HAMA nanoparticles was characterized using FTIR (Nicolet iS50, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Freeze-dried samples of free DHA, HAMA, and DHA@HAMA were ground, mixed with KBr (1:100, w/w), and pressed into pellets under vacuum. Spectra were recorded from 4000–400 cm⁻1 at 4 cm⁻1 resolution with 32 scans. Characteristic absorption peaks and spectral shifts were analyzed to confirm DHA encapsulation within the HAMA matrix, as well as hydrogen bonding and intermolecular interactions indicative of stable incorporation.

X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) analysis

The surface elemental composition and chemical states of DHA@HAMA nanoparticles were characterized using XPS (AXIS Ultra DLD, Kratos Analytical, UK). Freeze-dried powders were mounted on conductive carbon tape and analyzed under ultra-high vacuum (< 1 × 10⁻⁹ mbar) using a monochromatic Al Kα source (1486.6 eV, 150 W). Survey spectra were acquired over 0–1200 eV at 80 eV pass energy, and high-resolution spectra for C 1 s, O 1 s, and N 1 s were recorded at 20 eV. The binding energy scale was referenced to the C 1 s peak at 284.8 eV. Peak deconvolution used Shirley background subtraction with Gaussian–Lorentzian functions to resolve chemical shifts. Comparative analyses of free DHA, blank HAMA, and DHA@HAMA nanoparticles confirmed DHA incorporation, evidenced by characteristic C–O and C = O peaks and their intensity variations within the HAMA matrix.

Encapsulation Efficiency (EE) and Drug Loading (DL)

The EE and DL of DHA within HAMA nanoparticles were quantified to evaluate formulation performance. DHA@HAMA nanoparticles were prepared as described above and centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 30 min to separate free DHA. The supernatant was collected, and the concentration of unencapsulated DHA was determined using UV–Vis spectrophotometry at 231 nm using a standard calibration curve. The amount of encapsulated DHA was calculated by subtracting the free drug content from the total DHA initially added. EE% was calculated as the ratio of the actual amount of DHA encapsulated within nanoparticles to the initial amount of DHA added, expressed as a percentage, and DL% was determined as the proportion of encapsulated DHA relative to the total weight of nanoparticles, expressed as a percentage.

In vitro release study

The release profile of DHA from DHA@HAMA nanoparticles was assessed under simulated intra-articular conditions. Lyophilized nanoparticles containing a defined DHA dose were dispersed in PBS (pH 7.4, 0.1% Tween-80) and placed in dialysis bags (MWCO 10 kDa), which were immersed in 20 mL release medium at 37 °C with shaking (100 rpm). At predetermined intervals (0–168 h), 1 mL of medium was collected and replaced with fresh buffer. DHA concentrations were quantified using HPLC (Agilent 1260 Infinity, C18 column, 280 nm) using acetonitrile–water (60:40, v/v) as the mobile phase at 1.0 mL/min. Cumulative release was calculated relative to total DL, with free DHA in PBS as a control.

Cytotoxicity assay

The cytocompatibility of DHA@HAMA nanoparticles was assessed in murine ATDC5 chondrocytes seeded in 96-well plates (5 × 103 cells/well) and cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. After 24 h, cells were exposed to free DHA, blank HAMA, or DHA@HAMA nanoparticles (0–100 µM DHA equivalent) for 24 or 48 h. Cell viability was determined using the CCK-8 assay (Dojindo, Japan) by adding 10 µL reagent per well, incubating for 2 h, and measuring absorbance at 450 nm with a microplate reader (BioTek, USA). Relative viability was normalized to untreated controls. Additionally, Live/Dead staining (Calcein-AM/PI, Invitrogen, USA) was performed for fluorescence microscopy (Leica, Germany).

Clinical sample collection

Human articular cartilage specimens were obtained from patients with OA undergoing total knee arthroplasty and patients without OA with trauma-related knee fractures, and undergoing surgical treatment (n = 6 for each group). Diagnosis of OA was established according to the American College of Rheumatology criteria, and non-OA controls showed no clinical or radiographic signs of cartilage degeneration. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee, and informed consent was obtained from each participant in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Cartilage tissues were collected under aseptic conditions, immediately washed with sterile PBS, and processed for histology, immunohistochemistry, immunofluorescence, RNA, and protein extraction. Clinical information including age, sex, and Kellgren–Lawrence grade was recorded for correlation analyses.

Animal modeling and grouping

OA models were established in 8–10-week-old male C57BL/6 J mice, with an average body weight of 20–25 g, through destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM) surgery. Sham-operated mice, which underwent the same surgical procedure with only the joint capsule being opened and no further manipulation, served as controls. For functional studies of P21, chondrocyte-specific conditional knockout (ko) mice were generated and divided into four groups: Sham, DMM, ko-Sham, and ko-DMM. To assess the therapeutic effects of DHA, mice were randomly allocated into Sham, DMM, DMM + low-dose DHA, and DMM + high-dose DHA groups. For pathway validation, additional cohorts were assigned to Sham, DMM, DMM + DHA, DMM + inhibitor, and DMM + DHA + inhibitor groups. To evaluate the efficacy of DHA@HAMA nanoparticles, mice were grouped into Sham, DMM, DMM + DHA, DMM + DHA@HAMA, and DMM + HAMA groups. In vivo, DMM mice received intra-articular injections of either free DHA or DHA@HAMA nanoparticles. Free DHA was administered at 0.04 or 0.2 mg/kg and DHA@HAMA was administered at 0.05 mg/kg (containing 0.04 mg/kg DHA, 8 mg/mL, 15 μL) or 0.25 mg/kg (containing 0.2 mg/kg DHA). Treatments were performed once weekly for four weeks. Injections were conducted under 1% isoflurane anesthesia, with sites disinfected by 75% ethanol and povidone-iodine. Using a 30G microsyringe via the lateral approach (2 mm depth), 15 μL was slowly injected (> 10 s) to prevent excessive joint pressure. Post-injection, mice were individually housed for 24 h to monitor swelling or abnormal activity, excluding inflammation due to procedure-related injury. In all experiments, cartilage integrity and OA severity were assessed by histological staining, OARSI scoring, micro-computed tomography (micro-CT), and protein expression analysis. At designated time points, mice were sacrificed, and knee joint tissues were harvested under aseptic conditions, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histological evaluation, or snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for RNA and protein extraction. In addition, primary chondrocytes were isolated from articular cartilage by enzymatic digestion and used for transcriptomic (RNA-seq) and metabolomic profiling.

Gene knockout

To investigate the functional role of P21 in OA progression, conditional KO mice with chondrocyte-specific deletion of P21 were generated. P21flox/flox mice (C57BL/6 J background) were crossed with Col2a1-Cre transgenic mice (Jackson Laboratory, USA), in which Cre recombinase is expressed under the control of the type II collagen (Col2a1) promoter, thereby restricting gene deletion to chondrocytes. Genomic DNA was extracted from tail biopsies, and PCR-based genotyping was performed to confirm the presence of floxed alleles and Cre recombinase. Wild-type (WT) littermates (P21flox/flox without Cre) served as controls.

Cell culture and grouping

Murine ATDC5 chondrocytes were used for in vitro studies. ATDC5 cells were cultured in DMEM/F-12 with 5% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and C28/I2 cells were maintained in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C, 5% CO₂. Primary articular chondrocytes were isolated from WT and P21 conditional KO mice, digested with collagenase II, and cultured in DMEM/F-12 with 10% FBS. For functional assays, cells were treated with recombinant IL-1β (10 ng/mL) to simulate osteoarthritic stress. For P21 functional analysis, ATDC5 cells were divided into six groups: Control, OE-NC, IL-1β, OE-P21, IL-1β + OE-P21, and IL-1β + OE-NC. To assess DHA effects, ATDC5 cells were grouped as control, IL-1β, IL-1β + low-dose DHA, and IL-1β + high-dose DHA. Specifically, free DHA was applied at 50 μM (corresponding to the in vivo low dose) and 100 μM (upper safe threshold to avoid cytotoxicity), with an additional toxicity control at 200 μM. Cells were pretreated with IL-1β (10 ng/mL) for 24 h, followed by DHA exposure for 48 h, during which cell viability (CCK-8) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were measured to ensure safety of the high-dose treatment. For DHA@HAMA nanoparticles, concentrations of 62.5 μM (containing 50 μM free DHA) and 125 μM (containing 100 μM free DHA) were used. To confirm the P21-dependency of DHA, cells were assigned to Control, IL-1β, IL-1β + si-P21, IL-1β + DHA, and IL-1β + si-P21 + DHA groups. To confirm the dose-dependent nature of DHA, cells were assigned to Control, IL-1β, IL-1β + Low-DHA, IL-1β + High-DHA. For pathway validation, cells were divided into Control, IL-1β, IL-1β + DHA, IL-1β + AKT-inhibitor, and IL-1β + DHA + AKT-inhibitor groups. To evaluate DHA@HAMA nanoparticles, groups included Control, IL-1β, IL-1β + DHA, IL-1β + HAMA, and IL-1β + DHA@HAMA. For all experimental settings, samples were subjected to histological staining, immunofluorescence, ROS detection, western blotting, and qPCR as appropriate.

Cell transfection

ATDC5 chondrocytes were cultured under standard conditions and subjected to transient transfection to modulate P21 expression. Cells were transfected with a P21-overexpression plasmid (OE-P21), with corresponding empty vector and scrambled siRNA as negative controls, using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells at ~ 70% confluence were incubated with transfection complexes for 6 h in serum-free medium, followed by replacement with complete medium and culture for an additional 24–48 h. Transfection efficiency was validated at both mRNA and protein levels using qPCR and western blotting, respectively. Successfully transfected cells were used in subsequent assays, including IL-1β stimulation, ROS detection, immunofluorescence, and western blotting.

Histological staining

Cartilage samples from human patients with OA, non-OA controls, and mouse models (Sham, DMM, ko-Sham, ko-DMM, and DHA treatment groups) were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, decalcified in 10% EDTA, and paraffin-embedded. Sections (5 μm) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to H&E staining for structural assessment and Safranin O–fast green staining for proteoglycan content. Histological changes, including cartilage erosion and matrix loss, were evaluated, and degeneration was scored using the OARSI system by two blinded observers. The OARSI scoring system is a standardized histological grading method for OA severity, ranging from 0 to 6 (Grade 0: normal cartilage; Grade 1: surface intact but uneven; Grade 2: surface discontinuity; Grade 3: vertical fissures within the cartilage; Grade 4: erosion reaching the calcified cartilage; Grade 5: denudation of cartilage with deformation of the surface; Grade 6: complete loss of cartilage with bone exposure).

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin-embedded human and murine knee cartilage Sects. (5 µm) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). After blocking with 5% BSA, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against P21, SOX9, MMP13, COL2, and ACAN, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Visualization was achieved with DAB substrate and hematoxylin counterstaining. Negative controls were processed without primary antibodies. For quantification, positive cells were counted in five random high-power fields (n ≥ 5 per group) by two blinded observers, and staining intensity was semi-quantitatively assessed using ImageJ. Immunohistochemical scoring was integrated with OARSI histopathology grading (0–6, where 0 = normal cartilage and 6 = severe structural loss) to evaluate osteoarthritic severity.

Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescence staining was conducted on paraffin-embedded human and murine cartilage sections, as well as ATDC5 chondrocytes cultured on coverslips. Following fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilization with 0.3% Triton X-100, samples were blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Tissues and cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against P21, SOX9, MMP13, PI3K, and AKT (1:200; Abcam, USA). After PBS washes, samples were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-, 594-, or 647-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500; Invitrogen, USA) for 1 h, followed by nuclear counterstaining with DAPI. Coverslips and tissue sections were mounted with antifade medium, and images were acquired using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope under identical exposure conditions. Mean fluorescence intensity and colocalization analyses were performed with ImageJ software, ensuring quantitative comparability across groups.

ROS fluorescence detection

Intracellular ROS levels were assessed using the DCFH-DA probe (Beyotime, China). ATDC5 chondrocytes, subjected to different treatments, were incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. After PBS washing to remove excess probe, cells were immediately observed under a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany), and mean fluorescence intensity was quantified with ImageJ software. The assay provided a direct evaluation of oxidative stress under various experimental conditions.

CT Three-dimensional reconstruction

Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) was used to evaluate cartilage and subchondral bone changes in murine knee joints. Samples were scanned with a voxel resolution of 9 μm under standardized voltage and current settings, and three-dimensional reconstructions were generated using manufacturer-provided software. Structural parameters, including bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), number (Tb.N), separation (Tb.Sp), and subchondral plate thickness, were quantified within a defined region of interest encompassing the medial femoral condyle and tibial plateau. Cartilage degeneration was semi-quantitatively assessed by two blinded observers using the OARSI histopathology scoring system.

RNA sequencing

ATDC5 cells were treated with IL-1β in the presence or absence of P21 overexpression. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Invitrogen), and RNA integrity was assessed with the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Libraries were prepared from poly(A)-enriched mRNA using the TruSeq RNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, USA) and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate paired-end reads. Clean reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (mm10) with HISAT2, and transcript abundance was quantified using StringTie. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with DESeq2 (|log2FC|≥ 1, adjusted p < 0.05). Functional enrichment, including Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses, was performed using clusterProfiler. Data integration with metabolomics was conducted to identify P21-dependent transcriptomic–metabolic nodes.

Untargeted metabolomic analysis

The identical sets of ATDC5 cells that underwent RNA-sequencing analysis were also subjected to metabolomic profiling. For metabolomic profiling, cell pellets were washed with cold PBS, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at –80 °C until analysis. Metabolites were extracted using cold methanol–acetonitrile–water (2:2:1, v/v/v), vortexed, and subjected to centrifugation at 14,000 × g, 4 °C for 15 min. Supernatants were collected, vacuum-dried, and reconstituted in 50% acetonitrile for LC–MS analysis. Chromatographic separation was performed on a UHPLC system coupled to a high-resolution Q-TOF mass spectrometer operated in both positive and negative electrospray modes. Data acquisition used data-dependent MS/MS scanning to maximize metabolite coverage. Raw spectra were processed with XCMS for peak detection, retention-time alignment, and normalization. Metabolites were annotated using HMDB and KEGG databases, with pathway enrichment performed via MetaboAnalyst. Principal component analysis and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis were applied to visualize group separation and identify discriminant metabolites (VIP > 1.0, P < 0.05). Integrated transcriptomic-metabolomic correlation was conducted to link differential metabolites with RNA-seq-defined pathways.

Cellular Thermal Shift Assay (CETSA)

To investigate the direct interaction between DHA target proteins in OA, CETSA was performed. CETSA detects drug-protein interactions by assessing changes in protein thermal stability upon drug binding. ATDC5 chondrocytes were treated with DHA (50 µM) for 24 h, followed by protein extraction. The cell lysates were exposed to a temperature gradient (37–60 °C), with proteins binding to DHA expected to exhibit enhanced thermal stability, remaining soluble at higher temperatures.

Drug Affinity Responsive Target Stability (DARTS)

To explore the molecular mechanism of DHA in osteoarthritis (OA), we employed the drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS) assay to identify specific protein targets involved in P21-mediated PI3K/AKT signaling. DARTS detects drug-protein interactions by stabilizing target proteins upon drug binding, thereby protecting these proteins from proteolytic degradation. ATDC5 chondrocytes were treated with DHA (25 μM, 50 μM, 100 μM) for 24 h, followed by protein extraction. Additionally, parallel groups were set up with different ratios of trypsin (1:2000, 1:1000, 1:500). Samples were co-incubated with DHA at 4 °C for 1 h, then treated with a mild protease at 37 °C for 30 min. The resulting protein samples were analyzed by western blotting to detect P21, with BSA used as a loading control. It was expected that drug-bound proteins would remain resistant to digestion.

Molecular dynamics simulation

To verify the binding stability of DHA and the P21 protein, molecular dynamics (MD) simulation was employed to analyze the dynamic changes in the conformation of the complex. The structure of the P21 protein was derived from the AlphaFold protein structure database (UniProt ID: P38936), with the model number being AF-P38936-F1-v6. The small molecule DHA structure was obtained from the PubChem database (CID: 131,770,024), and its three-dimensional structure was constructed in the standard SMILES format. The MD simulation was carried out using the GROMACS 2025.4 software package, and the protein–ligand interactions were treated using the GROMOS force field. Subsequently, a 20 ns production phase simulation was executed. After the simulation, the root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), rotational radius (Rg), and solvent accessible surface area (SASA) were analyzed to evaluate the overall stability of the complex, the flexibility of the residues, the degree of structural compactness, and the exposure of the hydrophobic core. Based on principal component analysis (PCA), the three-dimensional free energy landscape (FEL) was calculated to determine the dominant conformation of the system and its thermodynamic stability. The binding free energy (ΔG) of the complex was calculated using the g_mx_MMPBSA tool to quantitatively assess the binding affinity of DHA and P21.

Microscale Thermophoresis (MST)

The direct binding affinity of DHA (MedChemExpress, Cat. # HY-Y0335) to the P21 protein (MedChemExpress, Cat. # HY-P704204) in vitro was quantitatively evaluated using MST. The interaction between protein and compound was measured by microscale thermophoresis using the NanoTemper Monolith NT.115 instrument. Protein was served as target and labeled with fluorescent by Monolith Protein Labeling Kit RED-tris-NTA (MO-L018, NanoTemper Technologies). Compound was employed as the ligand. For each assay, the labelled protein (20 nM) was incubated with the varying concentrations (1000 µM to 0.0305 µM) of the ligand in the MST buffer (137 mM Nacl, 2.68 mM KCL, 8.1 mM Na2HPO4, 1.76 mM KH2PO4, pH7.2–7.4, 0.05% Tween-20) at room temperature for 15 min. The samples were loaded into NT.115 standard capillaries. Analysis was performed at 25 ℃, 100% excitation power, 40% MST power. Apparent Kd value was calculated using MO Affinity Analysis V2.3. Data of at least three independently pipetted measurements were analyzed and Kd is expressed as mean ± SEM.

Molecular docking

Blind molecular docking between DHA and the P21 protein was performed using Gnina software. A total of 50 independent runs were executed across the entire protein surface to identify optimal binding sites. The generated poses were evaluated and ranked using a combination of CNN_Score, CNN_Affinity, and empirical Vina_Score, followed by the calculation of the theoretical dissociation constant (Kd) for the optimal conformation. To elucidate the molecular interaction mechanism, the highest-scoring docking complex was visualized using Discovery Studio and PyMOL to analyze the spatial localization, hydrogen bonding, and Van der Waals interactions within the binding pocket.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were independently repeated at least three times. Quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, USA) or SPSS 26.0 (IBM, USA). Comparisons between two groups were conducted using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied as appropriate. Correlations between P21 expression and osteoarthritic severity were assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. OARSI histological scores were analyzed using nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple-comparisons test. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

P21 Expression is downregulated in osteoarthritic cartilage and negatively correlates with disease severity

To determine whether P21 expression is associated with OA progression, articular cartilage samples collected from the weight-bearing regions from patients with advanced OA and non-OA controls were subjected to histological analysis. Macroscopic evaluation of clinical OA joints and corresponding radiographic imaging revealed severe advanced degeneration, characterized by extensive full-thickness cartilage loss, exposed eburnated subchondral bone, joint space narrowing, and prominent marginal osteophytes (Fig. 1A, B, right panels). H&E and Safranin O–fast green staining revealed severe cartilage erosion and proteoglycan loss in OA tissues compared with intact structures in non-OA samples (Fig. 1A, B, left panels). IHC staining further demonstrated significantly reduced P21 expression in OA cartilage, and quantitative analysis confirmed a negative correlation between P21 levels and the extent of cartilage degeneration (Fig. 1C). Western blotting consistently showed a significant downregulation of P21 protein in chondrocytes isolated from OA specimens (Fig. 1D). Moreover, immunofluorescence staining revealed decreased P21, diminished SOX9 and elevated MMP13 in OA cartilage compared to controls, suggesting that the reduction of P21 was linked to impaired anabolic activity and exacerbated catabolic stress in chondrocytes (Fig. 1E-G). Together, these findings indicate that P21 is aberrantly downregulated in the examined OA cartilage and that its expression is negatively associated with disease severity, implying a protective role in cartilage homeostasis.

To assess the dynamic changes of P21 in osteoarthritis, we analyzed human articular cartilage specimens with different grading severity and a mouse model of destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM) (Figure S1). Clinical studies demonstrated that P21 protein levels progressively decreased with disease severity (Figure S1A, B). This downregulation was accompanied by enhanced inflammatory responses (increased interleukin‑6 levels measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, Figure S1C) and exacerbated cellular senescence (elevated p16INK4a expression detected by flow cytometry, Figure S1D, E). Radiographic evaluation of the knee joints revealed intact joint spaces and normal bone architecture in healthy controls, whereas osteoarthritis (OA) patients exhibited progressive joint space narrowing, osteophyte formation, subchondral bone sclerosis, and cystic changes, with the most severe structural damage observed in the highest Kellgren-Lawrence (K-L) grade group (OA-4) (Figure S1F). Moreover, immunofluorescence also showed that as the K-L grade increased (labeled as KL1-KL4, corresponding to OA1-OA4), the expression level of P21 gradually decreased (Figure S1G-H). These radiographic alterations were consistent with the declining trend of P21 expression, further supporting a protective role of P21 in maintaining joint homeostasis. Moreover, correlation analysis showed that P21 protein levels were negatively correlated with K-L grade (Figure S1I). Consistent with these clinical findings, both mRNA and protein levels of P21 also decreased in a time-dependent manner within 8 weeks in the in vivo DMM model (Figure S1J, K). Collectively, progressive loss of P21 is closely associated with osteoarthritis progression, cellular senescence, and the inflammatory microenvironment.

P21 overexpression attenuates osteoarthritic progression In Vitro and In Vivo

To determine the functional role of P21 in OA, we performed both in vitro and in vivo studies with defined experimental groups. In vitro, ATDC5 chondrocytes were divided into six groups: control, OE-NC, IL-1β, OE-P21, IL-1β + OE-P21, and IL-1β + OE-NC. Western blotting confirmed successful P21 overexpression in the OE-P21 group (Fig. 2A). Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that compared to the Control group, IL-1β stimulation significantly reduced the expression of P21 and SOX9, while enhancing MMP13 expression. Notably, these IL-1β-induced pathological changes were effectively rescued in the IL-1β + OE-P21 group, which exhibited restored levels of P21 and SOX9 alongside decreased MMP13 expression compared to the IL-1β group (Fig. 2B, C, D). ROS fluorescence assays showed significantly decreased oxidative stress in OE-P21 cells (Fig. 2E). Consistently, western blotting demonstrated upregulation of anabolic markers (SOX9, COL2, and ACAN) and suppression of catabolic markers (MMP13, ADAMTS-4, COL10) (Fig. 2F). For the EdU assay (Fig. 2G), the number of EdU-positive (proliferating) cells was notably higher in the OE-P21 group compared with that in the control and OE-NC groups. When exposed to IL-1β, cell proliferation was inhibited, but this inhibition was alleviated in the IL-1β + OE-P21 group, as evidenced by a greater number of EdU-positive cells than that in the IL-1β + OE-NC group. IL-1β treatment reduced cell migration, while P21 overexpression in the IL-1β + OE-P21 group restored migration capacity, with more migrated cells than that in the IL-1β + OE-NC group. The wound healing assay (Fig. 2H) showed that OE-P21 accelerated wound closure compared to the control and OE-NC groups. IL-1β delayed wound healing, but the IL-1β + OE-P21 group exhibited faster wound closure than the IL-1β + OE-NC group, indicating that P21 could rescue the inhibitory effect of IL-1β on chondrocyte migration and wound healing.

DHA ameliorates OA by targeting P21

To investigate whether the protective effects of DHA on OA are mediated through P21, molecular docking was performed via Gnina software to predict their interaction. The optimal docking configuration exhibited favorable predicted binding potential, characterized by a CNN_Score of 0.885, a CNN_Affinity of 5.63, and a Vina_Score of − 5.68 kcal/mol, with a CNN_Affinity-derived estimated dissociation constant (Kd) of approximately 2.34 μM. Three-dimensional visualization revealed that DHA was stably accommodated within a compact hydrophobic pocket on the surface of P21. Detailed residue-level analysis demonstrated that DHA formed critical hydrogen bonds with ARG66 and SER69, while establishing extensive Van der Waals interactions with VAL67 and ARG68. These synergistic interactions provide a structural basis for the predicted stability of the DHA-P21 complex. To further verify the stability of the binding between DHA and p21, a 20 ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulation was conducted using the GROMACS 2025.4 software. After energy minimization and NVT and NPT equilibration of the system, a systematic analysis was performed on the dynamic stability and binding characteristics of the complex. The three-dimensional free energy landscape (FEL) constructed based on principal component analysis (PC1, PC2) showed that there was a concentrated low-energy valley region in the system, indicating that the complex could converge to a stable conformation during the simulation (Figure S2A, B). To validate the accuracy of the molecular docking predictions and quantify the actual binding strength in vitro, a MST assay was employed. The results demonstrated interaction between DHA and the P21 protein, yielding a fitted dissociation constant Kd of 6.21 ± 1.10 μM (Figure S2C). Notably, this experimentally determined micromolar affinity was within the same order of magnitude as the docking-derived theoretical Kd value of approximately 2.34 μM and the MD-derived estimated Kd of approximately 1.94 μM. These results support interaction between DHA and P21 at the micromolar level, rather than indicating an extremely strong or nanomolar-level binding interaction. The root mean square deviation (RMSD) results showed that the system quickly stabilized after 2 ns, with no significant fluctuations in the RMSD between the protein and ligand, and the overall conformation remained stable (Figure S2E). The root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) indicated that most of the protein residues had low flexibility, while only a local loop region showed slight fluctuations, and the core structure was stable (Figure S2G). The radius of gyration (Rg) remained within 2.5–3.2 nm and showed a slight downward trend, suggesting that the protein maintained a compact folded state during the simulation and did not undergo unfolding or structural collapse (Figure S2D). The solvent accessible surface area (SASA) was overall stable, indicating that there was no significant exposure of hydrophobic cores during the simulation (Figure S2F). The binding free energy was calculated using the gmx_MMPBSA method, with the total binding free energy ΔG being − 32.6 kJ/mol, corresponding to approximately − 7.8 kcal/mol. Based on the thermodynamic relationship Kd = eΔG/RT at 298 K, this ΔG value corresponds to an estimated Kd of approximately 1.94 μM. This MD-derived estimated Kd is consistent with a micromolar-affinity interaction and is broadly comparable to the MST-measured Kd of 6.21 ± 1.10 μM. Together, the docking, MD simulation, and MST assay support that DHA and P21 can form a thermodynamically stable and specific complex with micromolar affinity. Western blotting confirmed that the constructed siRNA could effectively reduce the expression level of P21 (Fig. 5B). CETSA results (Fig. 5C) showed that DHA treatment enhanced the thermal stability of P21, indicating binding and stabilization of P21 in chondrocytes. DARTS analysis (Fig. 5D) revealed that DHA protected P21 from proteolytic degradation, as evidenced by its higher retention in the insoluble fraction. To functionally verify whether DHA exerts its protective effects via P21, siRNA targeting P21 (si-P21) was used to transfect ATDC5 chondrocytes. Based on the experimental design, the cells were evaluated across four groups: Control, IL-1β, IL-1β + DHA, and IL-1β + si-P21 + DHA. ROS experiments indicated that DHA could significantly reduce IL-1β-induced oxidative stress; however, this antioxidant effect was markedly weakened when the expression of P21 was inhibited in the IL-1β + si-P21 + DHA group (Fig. 5E, upper panels). Similarly, immunofluorescence staining showed that in the IL-1β group, P21 and SOX9 were downregulated while MMP13 was upregulated. Following DHA treatment, P21 and SOX9 levels were restored, and MMP13 was significantly suppressed. Crucially, silencing P21 (si-P21) substantially abrogated these beneficial effects of DHA (Fig. 5E, lower panels). Western blotting further confirmed that DHA restored the expression of anabolic markers (SOX9, COL2, and ACAN) in IL-1β-stimulated cells and inhibited the expression of catabolic markers (MMP13, ADAMTS-4, and COL10). Consistent with the aforementioned results, these protective effects were noticeably weakened under si-P21 conditions (Fig. 5F). In conclusion, these findings suggest that DHA alleviates the progression of OA by enhancing P21-mediated chondrocyte repair, reducing oxidative stress, restoring anabolic activity, and inhibiting catabolic reactions. Importantly, the weakened protective effect of DHA under the inhibited expression of P21 indicates that the chondroprotective effect of DHA is at least partially dependent on P21.

DHA attenuates osteoarthritic progression In Vitro and In Vivo

The selection of DHA as a research target in this study was based on significant metabolic alterations observed in the ATDC5 chondrocytic cell line with P21 overexpression. Our metabolomic analysis revealed substantial changes in metabolites between the IL-1β + OE-P21 and IL-1β groups of ATDC5 cells, with DHA being notably upregulated in the IL-1β + OE-P21 group. This finding suggested the potential involvement of DHA in P21-mediated metabolic regulation. The volcano plot and heatmap of differential metabolites (Fig. 4A and 4B) highlighted DHA, along with metabolites like 3-(5-oxois-oxazolin-4-yl)-L-alanine and 9-Oxo-nonanoic acid, as significantly altered in the IL-1β + OE-P21 group, indicating its role in anabolic processes. The Z-score plot further demonstrated the distinct metabolic profile of DHA in the IL-1β + OE-P21 group, reinforcing its potential in modulating cartilage homeostasis. Additionally, pathway enrichment analysis of differential metabolites showed associations with the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, which plays a critical role in chondrocyte repair. Based on these findings, DHA was chosen as a key target for modulating P21-mediated metabolic changes, with the potential to enhance anabolic activity and inhibit catabolic processes in OA.

To evaluate the protective role of DHA in OA, both in vitro and in vivo experiments were performed with defined groups. In vitro, ATDC5 chondrocytes were divided into four groups: Control, IL-1β, IL-1β + Low-dose DHA, and IL-1β + High-dose DHA. Immunofluorescence staining showed that DHA enhanced P21 and SOX9 expression, and reduced MMP13 in IL-1β-treated chondrocytes (Fig. 6A, B, C). Western blotting confirmed the restoration of anabolic markers (SOX9, COL2, and ACAN) and suppression of catabolic markers (MMP13, ADAMTS-4, and COL10), with more pronounced protective effects in the IL-1β + High-DHA group (Fig. 6D). ROS assays further demonstrated that DHA attenuated oxidative stress induced by IL-1β (Fig. 6E). In vivo, mice were divided into four groups: Sham, DMM, DMM + Low-dose DHA, and DMM + High-dose DHA. H&E and Safranin O-fast green staining revealed that DHA treatment preserved cartilage structure and proteoglycan content compared with DMM controls (Fig. 6F, G). Western blotting of cartilage further demonstrated upregulation of anabolic proteins (SOX9, COL2, and ACAN) and downregulation of catabolic proteins (MMP13, ADAMTS-4, and COL10) following DHA administration (Fig. 6H). Together, these results demonstrate that DHA mitigates OA progression by enhancing P21 expression, reducing oxidative stress, restoring anabolic activity, and inhibiting catabolic responses in chondrocytes.

DHA attenuates OA via enhancing P21-mediated modulation of the PI3K/AKT pathway

To determine whether DHA exerts a protective effect through P21-mediated PI3K/AKT signaling, in vitro and in vivo experiments were conducted using pathway inhibitors. In vitro, ATDC5 chondrocytes were divided into five groups: Control, IL-1β, IL-1β + DHA, IL-1β + AKT-inhibitor, and IL-1β + DHA + AKT-inhibitor. Immunofluorescence indicated that DHA upregulated the expression of P21 in IL-1β -treated chondrocytes and enhanced the expression levels of PI3K and AKT, while pathway inhibition significantly weakened these effects (Fig. 7A). ROS assays indicated that DHA significantly reduced IL-1β -induced oxidative stress, but this protective effect was largely eliminated in the presence of pathway inhibitors (Fig. 7B). Western blotting confirmed that DHA restored the anabolic markers (SOX9, COL2, and ACAN) and inhibited the catabolic markers (MMP13, ADAMTS-4, and COL10). Crucially, these beneficial changes were markedly weakened by pathway inhibition (Fig. 7C). In vivo, the Sham, DMM, DMM + DHA, DMM + AKT-inhibitor, and DMM + DHA + AKT-inhibitor groups were examined. Histological evaluation using H&E and Safranin O–fast green staining indicated that DHA treatment alleviated cartilage erosion and degeneration, but these improvements weakened after the inhibition of the PI3K/AKT pathway (Fig. 7D). Consistent with the in vitro results, western blotting of cartilage further confirmed that DHA upregulated anabolic markers (SOX9, COL2, and ACAN) and downregulated catabolic markers (MMP13, ADAMTS-4, and COL10), while pathway inhibition reversed these effects (Fig. 7E). Furthermore, in vivo immunofluorescence validated that DHA enhanced the expression of P21, PI3K, and AKT, which was subsequently suppressed by the AKT inhibitor (Fig. 7F). Overall, these findings suggest that DHA alleviates OA progression by enhancing P21-mediated PI3K/AKT signaling to reduce oxidative stress and restore chondrocyte homeostasis, and pathway inhibition eliminates the protective effect of DHA.

Collectively, these results indicate that DHA@HAMA nanoparticles possess favorable physicochemical characteristics, laying a foundation for their potential application in OA therapy.

Before evaluating the therapeutic effect, the biological safety of these synthetic nanomaterials must be determined first. To assess the in vitro biological safety, we conducted CCK-8 assays on primary chondrocytes, ATDC5 cells, and osteoblasts for 7 days and 14 days (Figure S3A). Various treatments including Vehicle, low-dose/high-dose DHA (Low/High-DHA), HAMA, and low-dose/high-dose DHA@HAMA (Low/High- DHA@HAMA) all showed extremely low cytotoxicity, and the cell survival rate was comparable to that of the control group. Only when exposed to extremely high concentrations (UH- DHA@HAMA) for a long time would there be a certain degree of cell viability reduction, indicating a clear dose-dependent threshold. These results confirmed that DHA@HAMA nanoparticles have excellent cell compatibility and a strong biological safety window at the optimal therapeutic dose for in vivo application.

To further verify the in vivo biological safety of DHA@HAMA nanoparticles, we conducted a systemic safety assessment in a mouse model of osteoarthritis induced by DMM. Serum biochemical analysis (Figure S3B) showed no significant differences in alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine (Cr) levels among the Sham group, DMM group, free DHA group, HAMA group, and DHA@HAMA group (all p > 0.05, ns), indicating that DHA@HAMA nanoparticles did not cause liver or kidney dysfunction in mice. Further histopathological examination of major organs (spleen, liver, lung) (Figure S3C) further showed no obvious inflammatory infiltration, tissue damage, or pathological abnormalities in each group, which was consistent with the serum biochemical results. In conclusion, these in vitro and in vivo data comprehensively demonstrated the excellent biological compatibility and systemic biological safety of DHA@HAMA nanoparticles, laying a solid foundation for their translational application as a treatment agent for osteoarthritis.

Discussion

This study, through systematic verification using specific cohorts of clinical OA patient cartilage samples, DMM-induced mouse OA models, and in vitro cartilage cell experiments, provides compelling evidence that P21 is significantly downregulated in OA cartilage within our examined stages, and its expression level is significantly negatively correlated with the severity of OA disease. Furthermore, our results indicate that DHA and its nanoparticle formulation (DHA@HAMA) ameliorate OA progression through a P21-mediated mechanism, thereby restoring chondrocyte homeostasis and attenuating cartilage degeneration. Our findings collectively support a potential mechanistic link between P21 downregulation, PI3K/AKT signaling impairment, and disease progression, and highlight that restoration of P21 expression and activation of PI3K/AKT signaling mediate the protective effects of DHA, particularly when its sustained delivery is facilitated via HAMA nanoparticles.

P21 expression was found to be aberrantly reduced in human and murine OA cartilage, negatively correlating with disease severity. Functional experiments confirmed its protective role: overexpression alleviated IL-1β–induced oxidative stress, suppressed catabolic activity (MMP13, ADAMTS-4, and COL10), and enhanced anabolic programs (SOX9, COL2, and ACAN), whereas chondrocyte-specific deletion exacerbated OA progression in vivo. Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses further revealed that P21 exerts these protective effects via activation of PI3K/AKT signaling, leading to metabolic reprogramming that favors anabolic repair and reduces catabolic stress, indicating that P21 is not merely a senescence marker but an active regulator of cartilage protection through PI3K/AKT-mediated metabolic and transcriptional remodeling. P21, also known as CDKN1A, is a cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor that plays a pivotal role in cell cycle arrest, DNA damage response, and cellular senescence [20, 21]. In traditional research, P21 is widely regarded as a marker molecule for cellular aging. It is upregulated in various degenerative diseases along with tissue aging and promotes tissue degeneration by inducing cell cycle arrest and secreting senescence-associated secretory proteins. In addition to its classical function in cell cycle regulation, P21 has been implicated in a variety of diseases, including cancer [21–23], cardiovascular disorders [24, 25], and degenerative conditions, where its context-dependent role can be either protective or pathogenic. In cancer, P21 induction by the hypoxia-activated prodrug TH301 was shown to occur independently of p53 and circadian regulation, leading to cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, and enhanced therapeutic sensitivity [22]. Conversely, in melanoma, ZNF224 was found to amplify the oncogenic function of P21 by stimulating its p53-dependent transcription and promoting AKT-mediated cytoplasmic retention, thereby suppressing apoptosis and facilitating tumor proliferation [23]. In the cardiovascular system, P21 upregulation has been identified as a key mediator of vascular senescence and atherosclerosis, particularly under conditions of S-adenosylhomocysteine hydrolase inhibition [24]. However, the multi-dimensional evidence from this study suggests that within the specific context of mature articular cartilage, P21 exhibits specialized biological functions that may differ from its traditional roles in developing or immature tissues. Under physiological conditions, P21 maintains a basal expression specifically in mature, quiescent chondrocytes, where it functions to regulate cell cycle quiescence, inhibit excessive proliferation and oxidative stress, and maintain the structural homeostasis of the mature cartilage matrix. In contrast, in the pathological state of OA within our examined models, various damaging factors such as inflammatory cytokines and mechanical stress cause the continuous downregulation of P21 expression. This downregulation subsequently disrupts the metabolic balance of mature chondrocytes, triggering the inhibition of anabolic metabolism, the hyperactivity of catabolic metabolism, and the exacerbation of oxidative stress, ultimately leading to the progressive degeneration of the mature cartilage.

Furthermore, our research results indicate that DHA exerts chondroprotective effects by enhancing P21 expression, reducing oxidative stress, and restoring the balance between anabolic and catabolic programs in mature chondrocytes in a dose-dependent manner. To explore the specific mechanism by which DHA regulates P21, we integrated computational and biophysical approaches. Results from molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, MST, CETSA, and DARTS collectively suggest a potential interaction between DHA and P21. Subsequent signaling analysis revealed that DHA enhances the activation of the P21-mediated PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, and pathway inhibition reverses its protective effect, indicating that DHA alleviates the progression of OA by strengthening the P21-PI3K/AKT axis.

DHA (1,3-Dihydroxyacetone) is a naturally occurring triose sugar that serves as an intermediate in glycolysis and lipid metabolism, and beyond its metabolic role, it has recently gained attention for its therapeutic potential [13, 26]. In various disease contexts, DHA has been explored for its anti-tumor effects through modulation of cancer cell metabolism, as well as for its antioxidant and cytoprotective properties in cardiovascular and neurodegenerative disorders. In cancer, Smith et al. [27] reported that DHA induces ROS production, G2/M cell cycle arrest, and apoptotic cell death in A375P melanoma cells. In musculoskeletal research, DHA has emerged as a candidate regulator of chondrocyte function, with studies showing that it can modulate oxidative stress and metabolic homeostasis under osteoarthritic conditions. However, most previous investigations have primarily described DHA as a metabolic substrate or indirect antioxidant, without clearly delineating its mechanistic targets. Our study advances this field by proposing P21 as a potential molecular target of DHA, with molecular docking and binding analyses suggesting a functional, albeit potentially weak, interaction. Furthermore, our findings indicate that its chondroprotective activity is mediated through the enhancement of the P21–PI3K/AKT axis. This mechanistic insight not only clarifies the role of DHA in OA but also distinguishes our work from earlier studies by highlighting a specific signaling pathway through which DHA may exert its therapeutic effects.

Although free DHA exhibited protective activity, its therapeutic application is limited by poor stability and rapid clearance. To overcome these challenges, we encapsulated DHA into HAMA-based nanoparticles. Comprehensive physicochemical characterization confirmed uniform nanoscale morphology, stable crystalline incorporation, high encapsulation efficiency, sustained release, and excellent cytocompatibility. Biologically, DHA@HAMA nanoparticles demonstrated superior antioxidant capacity, stronger upregulation of P21 and PI3K/AKT activation, and enhanced restoration of anabolic markers compared with free DHA. In vivo, DHA@HAMA provided more pronounced protection against cartilage degeneration and subchondral bone loss, indicating that hyaluronic acid–based nanoparticle encapsulation not only improves drug delivery but also synergistically augments the chondroprotective effects of DHA through P21-dependent PI3K/AKT activation, making DHA@HAMA a promising candidate for intra-articular OA therapy.

HAMA-based nanomaterials have attracted considerable interest as biomimetic carriers owing to their excellent biocompatibility, tunable physicochemical properties, and intrinsic affinity for cartilage tissue [28–30]. Previous studies have demonstrated the utility of HAMA hydrogels and nanoparticles in drug delivery, tissue engineering, and regenerative medicine, where they provide controlled release, reduce inflammatory responses, and support extracellular matrix preservation [31–33]. Gupta et al. [31] have reported that polymeric hydrogels, through tunable DL and sustained release, provide a versatile platform for delivering therapeutic agents in arthritis treatment, thereby enhancing therapeutic outcomes and patient compliance. Xu et al. [32] have reported that injectable HAMA-CPC hydrogels loaded with 3D bioprinted adipose-derived stem cell extracellular vesicles significantly enhance bone regeneration by promoting BMSC osteogenesis and angiogenesis, making them a promising strategy for bone defect repair. In the context of OA, HAMA has been applied to improve intra-articular drug retention and enhance the bioactivity of therapeutic agents [31, 32], yet most research has focused on general cartilage repair rather than mechanistic disease modulation. Our findings extend this knowledge by demonstrating that HAMA not only improves the pharmacokinetics of DHA but also amplifies its disease-modifying activity through more effective enhancement of P21-mediated PI3K/AKT signaling. This dual contribution, serving both as a delivery system and as a functional enhancer of DHA, distinguishes our study from prior work and underscores the translational potential of HAMA-based nanocarriers in OA therapy.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although we demonstrated the therapeutic efficacy of DHA and DHA@HAMA nanoparticles in vitro and in murine OA models, the translational relevance to human OA remains uncertain, as interspecies differences in cartilage biology and immune responses may influence treatment outcomes. Second, while our findings highlight the central role of P21 and PI3K/AKT signaling in mediating the protective effects of DHA, additional pathways and interacting molecular networks were not fully explored, and thus the mechanistic landscape may be more complex than delineated here. Third, it should be noted that the core finding of this study—that P21 is downregulated in OA cartilage and is negatively correlated with the severity of the disease—is a result observed during a specific pathological stage of OA and in the cartilage tissue. Future research will further expand the clinical sample size to explore the dynamic change patterns of P21 in different pathological stages of OA (early, progressive, and late), clarify the expression differences in different regions of the cartilage (weight-bearing area, non-weight-bearing area), and elucidate the different roles of P21 in chondrocytes, synovial cells, and subchondral bone cells. This will further verify its clinical value as a biomarker for OA. At the same time, this study has confirmed that DHA exerts a cartilage-protective effect by targeting the activation of P21. The DHA@HAMA nanodelivery system can significantly enhance the expression and function of P21, providing a new strategy for targeted treatment of OA. However, its long-term efficacy and metabolic characteristics still require further in-depth research.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides evidence from our examined clinical cohorts and animal models that P21 is significantly downregulated in OA cartilage, and its expression level is significantly negatively correlated with the severity of OA. This finding suggests that P21 acts as a key protective factor for maintaining cartilage homeostasis and inhibiting the progression of OA. Our results indicate that DHA exerts potent chondroprotective effects in OA by enhancing P21 expression, thereby alleviating oxidative stress, suppressing catabolic remodeling, and restoring anabolic activity in chondrocytes through activation of PI3K/AKT signaling. Both in vitro and in vivo data consistently show that P21 downregulation contributes to disease progression, while DHA effectively counteracts this process, largely by modulating the P21 signaling axis. By providing prolonged local retention of DHA, this delivery system leads to superior therapeutic efficacy compared with free DHA, allowing for more effective maintenance of P21-mediated PI3K/AKT activation. Collectively, these findings not only elucidate a potential mechanistic link between P21 and metabolic regulation in OA but also highlight DHA@HAMA nanoparticles as a promising nanotherapeutic strategy for the treatment of degenerative joint disease.
