Leveling and alignment of ectopically erupted maxillary canines with continuous archwires: Insights from geometric morphometric analysis
Authors: Linda Schwarz, Philipp Mitteröcker, Michael H Bertl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the three-dimensional movements of ectopically erupted maxillary canines (EEMCs) during orthodontic leveling and alignment with continuous archwires.MethodsEight patients with EEMCs were treated with a 0.014-inch CuNiTi archwire fully seated in self-ligating maxillary brackets. Seven intraoral scans were obtained per patient from baseline (T1) to 12 weeks thereafter (T2–T7). Geometric morphometric analysis incorporating principal component analysis (PCA) examined the (1) positional and angular changes of the EEMC and adjacent teeth and (2) alterations in the triangular relationship formed by the brackets of the lateral incisors, canines, and premolars.ResultsThe PCA revealed consistent EEMC extrusion, despite differences in the initial conditions, with the canines reaching apicocoronal alignment with the neighboring teeth after 12 weeks. All EEMCs showed uprighting. The triangular bracket configuration changed systematically, with the distance between the lateral incisor and premolar increasing as the canine was extruded.ConclusionsDespite the small sample size, this brief report revealed a consistent space-opening effect and EEMC uprighting during alignment.Keywords: Tooth movement, Orthodontic treatment, Ectopic maxillary canine, Geometric morphometrics

INTRODUCTION

Ectopically erupted maxillary canines (EEMCs) have a prevalence of 2.21% to 3.06%1,2 and are mainly associated with dental crowding and arch-length deficiency.3 When alignment is possible without tooth extraction or distalization, a super-elastic NiTi archwire may be used in the initial treatment stage.4-6 Since EEMCs involve severe vertical and horizontal displacements, seating a continuous archwire causes substantial wire deflection, making resistance to sliding7 an important biomechanical concern. Sliding through the bracket is usually restricted when the deflection of the archwire exceeds the critical contact angle for binding,8,9 after which the material-dependent springback and recovery behavior of the wire determine the mechanical forces.10 A previous study suggested that horizontal forces are exerted on the lateral incisor and the first premolar in the three-bracket relationship of an EEMC, which cause the space-opening effect observed clinically.11,12 The length of the archwire between the EEMC and the neighboring lateral incisor and first premolar is expected to remain constant due to binding.11

Improving biomechanical models of tooth leveling and alignment requires three-dimensional (3D) quantitative analyses of clinical orthodontic situations.13 Therefore, to evaluate the 3D movements of EEMCs and their adjacent teeth, consecutive intraoral scans during the first three months of orthodontic treatment were analyzed using geometric morphometrics. This study aimed to investigate the horizontal and vertical movements of EEMCs and their neighboring teeth within the context of this specific three-bracket relationship.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient selection

This brief report included patients with unilateral or bilateral EEMCs who underwent orthodontic treatment at the Division of Orthodontics (University Clinic of Dentistry, Medical University of Vienna, Vienna, Austria) between April 2016 and August 2018. Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants. The inclusion criteria were fully erupted permanent dentition; at least one buccally erupted and vertically displaced maxillary canine; and no history of maxillary extractions, craniofacial anomalies, or previous fixed orthodontic treatments. Patients requiring extractions, distalization, or segmented archwires were excluded. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Medical University of Vienna (EK-Nr. 1159/2016).

Study protocol

Self-ligating brackets (Damon Q; Ormco, Brea, CA, USA) were bonded to all maxillary teeth, including the EEMC(s). A 0.014-inch super-elastic CuNiTi archwire (Damon Optimal-Force; Ormco) was fully seated in all brackets. Intraoral scans were conducted using an iTero Element scanner (Align Technology, Tempe, AZ, USA) with the archwire removed and the bracket-slide mechanism in the open position (Figure 1) immediately after bonding (T1) and subsequently at two-week intervals for 12 weeks (T2–T7), yielding seven scans per patient (Figure 2). After scanning, a new archwire of the same type was inserted.

Data collection

The stereolithography files from all the scans were imported into OnyxCeph (Image Instruments GmbH, Chemnitz, Germany) for superimposition via surface registration. The hard palate, excluding the nasopalatine papilla and extending at least 5 mm laterally from the gingival margins,14 was used as the reference area (Figure 3). This region was selected based on the findings of previous studies, which indicated the highest level of agreement.15 A more detailed description of the superimposition method is provided in the Supplementary Materials. The amount of anterior crowding was assessed using Little’s irregularity index.16 The vertical displacement of the EEMC was measured using a two-dimensional projection of the 3D model in the sagittal plane (Supplementary Materials and Supplementary Figure 1). Using MeshLab 2016 (ISTI, Pisa, Italy),17 12 3D landmarks on the four bracket vertices were recorded on the first premolar, canine, and lateral incisor for each time point (T1–T7), yielding 84 landmarks per patient for the cases with unilateral EEMCs and 168 landmarks for the case showing bilateral EEMCs (Figure 4).

Morphometric analysis

Geometric morphometrics and exploratory multivariate statistics were used to quantify the 3D spatial relationships among the three teeth and their temporal changes. In geometric morphometrics, landmarks are usually registered using Procrustes analysis before statistical analysis.18-21 However, the surface models were aligned on the basis of a stable palatal reference to ensure consistent landmark registration for each patient. To allow interpatient comparisons without disrupting the alignment within patients, Procrustes analysis was performed on each patient’s entire landmark set (T1–T7). Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to explore tooth movement trajectories and reorientation. Shape patterns corresponding to the first two principal components (PC 1, PC 2) were visualized using 3D reconstructions.

To further illustrate the movement patterns of the three tooth positions (neglecting tooth orientation), the centers of the three brackets were used to represent the vertices of the triangle (Figure 5). The same triangular configuration was subsequently analyzed to assess relative tooth movement (Figure 6). Changes in the geometric properties of the triangle over time, including distances and angular relationships, were evaluated to quantify the relative positional changes between the three teeth while excluding absolute displacements, such as overall intrusion or extrusion.

To assess the potential impact of measurement variability, landmark placement and subsequent PCA analysis were repeated for one patient (patient 2), resulting in essentially identical trajectories, indicating that measurement uncertainty had a negligible influence on the observed patterns (Supplementary Figure 2).

RESULTS

Seven patients with unilateral EEMCs and one patient with bilateral EEMCs were included in this study. All the patients completed the 12-week follow-up, resulting in a total of 9 analysed EEMCs. Little’s irregularity index indicated that the mean anterior crowding decreased substantially from 8.2 ± 2.8 mm to 2.1 ± 1.4 mm. Further details, including the participants’ sex and age, as well as the initial vertical displacement of the EEMC, are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

The first two PCs (PC1 and PC2) accounted for 84% of the total variance and represented the patients’ trajectories well. Although Patient 3 contributes two correlated trajectories, this duplication did not materially alter the trajectory pattern. The Eigenvalues of all PCs are listed in Supplementary Table 2. The trajectories followed a consistent direction along PC 1 and converged along PC 2 (Figure 7A). PC 3 and PC 4 did not exhibit shared trajectory trends (Figure 7B). The EEMCs demonstrated accelerated movement until approximately 6 weeks (T4) and slowed thereafter (Figure 7A). PC 1 reflects the extrusion of the EEMCs, which reached alignment with their neighboring teeth at the same apicocoronal level at the 12-week recall (Figure 8A). The magnitude of change along PC 1 differed among the patients, reflecting differences in the initial conditions of the dentition and varying treatment responses. PC 2 describes the lateral angulation of the canine relative to its neighboring teeth. The trajectory convergence along PC2 reflected the uprighting of the canine, while the angulation of the neighboring teeth remained stable (Figure 8B).

The relative positions of the teeth were assessed using the triangular shape formed by the three brackets (Figure 9). The vertical position of the canine shifted incisally from T1 to T7, whereas the horizontal base of the triangle formed by the lateral incisor and premolar increased. Initially, the canine–premolar distance exceeded the canine–incisor distance, producing an asymmetric triangle. By T7, the distances were similar, with the canine placed midway between the incisor and premolar.

DISCUSSION

This study is the first in vivo evaluation of the movement patterns of EEMCs during orthodontic leveling and alignment. Yun et al.22 evaluated orthodontic tooth movement using consecutive intraoral scans of the patients’ dentition; however, due to the large variability in malocclusion, they did not obtain consistent results. Thus, the present study focused on a single specific malocclusion: buccally displaced EEMCs. All patients were prospectively included in the study and received a standardized treatment protocol over 12 weeks.

The force system of a vertically displaced canine consists of two Burstone I geometries,23 wherein the extrusive forces on the canine accumulate, the moments on the canine cancel each other out, and the intrusive force and tipping moments on the lateral incisor and first premolar remain. Vertical wire deflection indicates the presence of binding, which is likely to restrict the sliding of the continuous archwire at the onset of this three-tooth geometry. Therefore, the springback and recovery behavior of the deflected archwire introduces horizontal forces on the lateral incisor and premolar at the bracket level,10 creating tipping moments counter to those introduced by the Burstone I geometries. This space-opening effect between the lateral incisor and premolar, previously described in biomechanical models11-13 and finite element analyses,24,25 was clearly observed in PC 1 (Figure 8A). The lack of tipping during the space opening suggests that the two opposing moments on the outer teeth are of similar sizes when using the 0.014-inch CuNiTi archwire. This specific wire was chosen for two specific reasons, both based on previously published in vitro measurements: First, the magnitudes of the intrusive and extrusive forces remain within the recommended margins for such tooth movements.26 Second, the tipping moments on the premolar and lateral incisors resulted from both intrusive and horizontally directed forces.12 This combined force vector has been shown to shift from a more horizontal direction to an increasingly intrusive direction when the canine is extruded. This shift decreases the distance of the force vector from the centers of resistance of the teeth. For the initial 4 mm of canine extrusion, this distance was calculated to decrease from 10 to 3 mm, with the force measured at 100–50 cN.12 This resulted in a mean tipping moment of 460 cNmm. The Burstone I-introduced moment was consistently measured at 380 cNmm in the opposite direction for these tooth positions, suggesting a clinically negligible tipping effect.12 While binding would likely make use of different bracket types or different modes of ligation irrelevant to this model, different archwires may skew the ratio of moments on the lateral incisor and premolar.

The clinically observed tooth arrangement of EEMCs differed slightly from that previously proposed. For example, the initial distance between the lateral incisor and premolar ranged from 8 to 20 mm, in contrast to the findings of earlier biomechanical models,12 where an initial distance of 5 mm was assumed. In addition, the initial EEMC positions were closer to the lateral incisors than described previously, resulting in a skewed triangular bracket configuration at T1 (Figure 9). Badawi et al.13 previously reported a distally directed force on the canine during alignment, which explained the observed central movement of the canine between T2 and T7.

Other methods for correcting positional discrepancies in single teeth with minimal undesired side effects have been suggested in the literature.27 Our results suggest that for a vertically displaced EEMC, implementing a continuous archwire results in a favorable force system, facilitating both space opening and sagittal and vertical alignment of the canine. The findings also emphasize the limitations of in vitro and computational studies, which do not account for all the complexities of clinical conditions. Factors such as tooth-to-tooth contact, saliva, presence of the periodontal ligament, and occlusal forces significantly affect orthodontic force systems.28,29 Thus, the method presented herein allows additional biomechanical analyses based on clinical observations.

CONCLUSIONS

This is the first study to investigate the 3D movement of EEMCs in vivo. This initial investigation observed a space-opening effect between the lateral incisor and the first premolar as the EEMC moved during orthodontic leveling and alignment.
