Correlation of body composition and anthropometric indices with electrodiagnostic and ultrasonographic findings in patients with carpal tunnel syndrome
Authors: Gholamreza Raissi, Tannaz Ahadi, Jalal Esmailpour, Mohammad Amin Khadembashiri, Mohamad Mehdi Khadembashiri, Seyed Pezhman Madani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveCarpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) is the most common entrapment neuropathy of the upper limb. Various risk factors have been described for this disorder. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between CTS with body composition indexes and hand anthropometric characteristics.DesignThis is a case–control study.SettingThe study conducted in physical medicine and rehabilitation clinic at Firoozgar Hospital, Iran University of Medical Sciences between 2019 and 2020.ParticipantsFifty patients with clinical symptoms of CTS and 50 controls were enrolled.InterventionThe median nerve distal sensory latency (DSL) and amplitude, and distal motor latency (DML) and amplitude, were measured using a Natus Synergy Ultrapro S100 instrument.Main outcomes measureAge, hand anthropometric index, body weight, body mass index (BMI), body composition indexes and sonographically measured cross-sectional area (CSA) of median nerve were evaluated in both groups, and the relation between these variables as the incidence of CTS and its severity were investigated.ResultsWe observed a significant correlation between CTS incidence and larger dorsovolar and mediolateral diameters of the wrist (all with P < 0.05). The right median nerve CSA was 14.94 ± 6.00 mm2 in the case group, and 10.88 ± 2.19 mm2 in the control group, and the left CSA was 14.17 ± 5.00 mm2 in the case group and 10.72 ± 2.17 mm2 in the control group, all with P < 0.001. The mean weight, BMI, fat mass, and total extracellular water (ECW) ratio were significantly higher in the case group. There was no significant relationship between CTS severity and weight and body composition indexes (all with P > 0.05) except for median nerve CSA.ConclusionsOur study showed that among the body composition indexes, the body fat mass, as an indicator of the body fat distribution, and total ECW ratio are associated with the incidence of CTS while not its severity. Higher body weight, BMI, fat mass, total ECW ratio, median nerve CSA and wrist diameters increase the risk of CTS. Higher median nerve CSA is also associated with the higher severity of CTS.Keywords: Carpal tunnel syndrome, Body composition, Body mass index

Introduction

Carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) is the prevailing condition of nerve compression in the upper limbs [1]. The incidence of CTS in the general population varies from 1 to 5%. and higher in females than males (3:1 ratio) [2, 3]. It results from compression of the median nerve at the wrist as it passes beneath the transverse carpal ligament through the carpal tunnel [4]. Electrodiagnostic and sonographic evaluations are sensitive and specific diagnostic tools for CTS [5, 6].

Higher weight and body mass index (BMI) have been considered to increase the risk of musculoskeletal disorders including CTS occurrence [7, 8]. Previous studies have shown that higher BMI contributes to CTS through increased fat deposition in the carpal tunnel, which raises hydrostatic pressure and compresses the median nerve [4]. Moreover, obesity is a pro-inflammatory condition that promotes perineural edema via adipokine secretion, exacerbating nerve entrapment [9]. BMI is the most common method for evaluating body weight and its fat content [10] and is the basis of obesity classification for the World Health Organization (WHO) [11]. It is estimated that a BMI of > 29 kg/m2 is associated with a 2.5 times greater risk of CTS [4]. An increase of BMI of 1 kg/m2 which is equivalent to 3 kg weight gain in a person with normal height, increases the risk of CTS by up to 8% [12]. Despite the association of BMI with body fat accumulation and metabolic health, it is not a sensitive method for determining the actual distribution of body fat [13]. Recent studies have increasingly emphasized the limitations of using BMI alone as a predictor of CTS, advocating for more comprehensive indicators of body composition.

It has been demonstrated that the fat mass index (FMI), which provides a more precise assessment of adiposity and its metabolic effects, is a better indicator of CTS-related risk than BMI [14]. Furthermore, excessive adipose tissue causes systemic inflammation by producing adipokines, which may exacerbate nerve entrapment and edema. BMI alone is not a good indicator of this process [15]. Additionally, the extracellular water (ECW) ratio is becoming a useful indicator for assessing CTS. It is indicative of tissue fluid changes and subclinical edema that could make the median nerve more vulnerable to compression. Weight-based indices might not be able to identify these fluid imbalances [16, 17]. Consequently, body composition indices could be a more suitable choice for evaluating patients regarding CTS [18].

Johnson et al. [19] first described the association of wrist characteristics with CTS. In addition to body composition, wrist and hand anthropometric indices—such as mediolateral and dorsovolar wrist diameters—have been identified as anatomical risk factors for CTS. Prior studies suggest that such measurements can help identify individuals predisposed to CTS due to variations in carpal tunnel dimensions [20–22].

The findings may help in prevention and treatment of CTS. We propose that body composition metrics, including fat mass, ECW ratio, and wrist anthropometric characteristics, are associated with the occurrence and severity of carpal tunnel syndrome. Then, the purpose of this study was to investigate body composition indices and hand anthropometric characteristics in CTS patients and healthy control group.

Material and methods

Study design and patients’ enrollment

This case–control study was conducted in line with the STROBE Checklists [23] on patients suspicious of CTS who attended the physical medicine and rehabilitation clinic at Firoozgar Hospital, Iran University of Medical Sciences between 2019 and 2020. The study has been approved by the ethical committee under the reference number IR.IUMS.FMD.REC.1397.224 June 2018. Clinical trial number: not applicable. The ethics of this study are in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards [24]. Prior to the individual’s participation, informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Fifty consecutive patients with signs and symptoms of CTS aged 18 years and older (including pain and sensory disturbances in the median nerve territory) were selected as the case group. The CTS diagnosis was approved by the nerve conduction study (NCS). Patients with known neurological disorders such as polyneuropathy, proximal medial neuropathy, mono neuritis multiplex, myopathy, or muscular dystrophy were excluded. Additionally, patients with prior CTS treatment, amputation, previous history of obesity surgery, pacemaker and pregnancy, or up to 3 months postpartum were excluded from the study. In patients with bilateral CTS, the side with a higher severity of CTS was included and reported. Fifty control participants were also enrolled, applying the same exclusion criteria as those used for the case group. Participants in the case and control groups were matched by age and sex Fig. 1.

Electrodiagnostic study

The electrodiagnostic evaluation was done using a Natus Synergy Ultrapro S100 instrument. The median nerve distal sensory latency (DSL) and amplitude, as well as distal motor latency (DML) and amplitude, were measured based on descriptions according to the American Association of Electrodiagnostic Medicine (AAEM) guidelines [25]. Ulnar and radial NCS and electromyography of muscles were also conducted to rule out other median nerve entrapment or peripheral neuropathies, cervical radiculopathy, or plexopathies. The CTS severity was described as mild, moderate, or severe based on median nerve DSL, DML, and sensory and motor amplitude. EMG severity was classified as follows: abnormal comparative tests or prolonged median DSL > 3.5 ms while normal median DML was considered as a mild abnormality; prolonged median DSL and DML (≥ 4.2 ms) were regarded as a moderate abnormality; absence of median sensory response and prolonged median DML or absent compound muscle action potentials were considered as a severe abnormality.

Ultrasound study

All ultrasound examinations were performed by an experienced radiologist blinded to electrodiagnostic results using an H60 V2.00 diagnostic ultrasound system (SAMSUNG MEDISON CO., LTD). The patients were positioned in an upright sitting position with their hands on a pillow. The forearm was supinated, and the wrist was in slight dorsiflexion using a rolled-up towel. A high-frequency linear transducer (13 MHz) was then placed transversely along the distal crease of the wrist. The median nerve was identified, and its cross-sectional area (CSA) at the level of the pisiform bone or the tunnel inlet was determined. The median nerve CSA was measured by tracing the internal border of the perineural echogenic rim over the hypoechoic median nerve [26, 27]. Although inter-rater reliability was not assessed in this study, intra-rater reliability was addressed by taking three repeated measurements and using their mean for analysis. Previous research has demonstrated that ultrasound measurement of median nerve CSA at the carpal tunnel inlet shows moderate inter-rater reliability but excellent intra-rater reliability when performed by experienced examiners [28].

Hand anthropometric measurements

Anthropometric measurements were taken using precision calipers, which were accurate up to 0.1 mm. These measures were the mediolateral and dorsovolar diameters of the wrist, the length of the third finger, and the length of the palm. Anthropometric indices were calculated and recorded for all patients bilaterally.

Body composition

In Body 720 tool was used to evaluate body composition. This device uses Bioelectrical Impedance Analysis (BIA) with the new Direct Segmental Multi-Frequency (DSM) method to assess the body composition indexes. It has acceptable reliability in various body composition analyses as compared to dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry [29].

The Body composition test was conducted under the supervision of a physiatrist. All evaluations were carried out in the morning. The environment temperature was 20–25° C. Prior to the test, all patients were instructed to refrain from engaging in physical exercise, consuming alcohol or excessive amounts of caffeine for a minimum of 24 h. Additionally, they were advised not to eat or shower within two hours before the test and to ensure their bladder was empty. Patients were asked to remove their clothes as far as possible without wearing any metal or jewelry. They were asked to stand on the device with bare feet with both arms at 15° abductions, and they held two hand-held electrodes. Patients were asked to avoid any movement during the test. The test was performed twice for each patient, and the mean measures were used for final analysis. For all patients, based on the body composition analysis, weight, BMI, total body water (BW), lean mass (LM), fat mass (FM), right and left arm Extracellular Water (ECW) ratio, and total ECW ratio were recorded.

Total BW consists of Intracellular Water (ICW) and ECW that were evaluated using the multi-frequency technique. BIA technique uses the resistance of electrical flow through the body to estimate body FM. LM is a component of body composition, calculated by subtracting body FM from total body weight. Edema or ECW Ratio is calculated using the following formula: [Edema or ECW Ratio = the ratio of ECW to Total BW]. BMI values were classified according to WHO criteria as normal (18.5–24.9 kg/m2), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m2), and obese (≥ 30.0 kg/m2) [11]. Total BW normally comprises 45–60% of body weight, with an ECW ratio between 0.36 and 0.39 considered normal [30]. Healthy FM percentages range from 6–24% in men and 14–31% in women [31]. Regarding lean mass, we refer to the 2019 revised European consensus on sarcopenia (EWGSOP2) [32].

Statistical analysis

The primary outcome of this study is the Median nerve CSA at the carpal tunnel inlet. The sample size was determined based on data from a previous study [33]. Using a significance level (α) of 0.05, an effect size of 1.96, a statistical power of 80% (1 − β), a mean CSA difference of 4.0 mm2, and an estimated standard deviation of 4.4 mm2, the initial calculation yielded a required sample size of 19 participants per group. Additionally, a moderate effect size (Cohen's d ≈ 0.5) was applied to ensure a more conservative and robust estimation, resulting in an adjusted sample size of 62 participants per group. To enhance our study's reliability and statistical power, we chose the final sample size between our two calculation methods, and the final sample size was 50 patients per group.

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (Version 27), SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test showed a normal data distribution. Unpaired t-test, Chi-square and repeated measures ANOVA were used for statistical comparison. In the case of non-normal distribution, non-parametric tests were used. The significant threshold was considered to be less than 0.05.

Discussion

In this case–control study, 50 patients with CTS symptoms were compared with the control group and the association of CTS and its severity with body composition indices, weight, BMI, hand anthropometric characteristics and median nerve CSA were evaluated. According to the present study, CTS was associated with higher weight, BMI, FM, total ECW ratio, larger mediolateral and dorsovolar wrist dimensions, and median nerve CSA. No significant association was observed between CTS severity and body composition or hand anthropometric indices. Patients with severe CTS exhibited a considerably larger CSA of the median nerve.

Few studies were conducted to investigate the relationship between body composition indices and CTS. The study conducted by Hsiao et al. aimed to assess the correlation between body composition indices and median nerve distal neuropathy in individuals with physical impairments. The study showed that individuals with a BMI above 24.5 kg/m2 and a leg exercise burden index (leg EBI) greater than 0.943 had an increased likelihood of developing median distal motor neuropathy and median distal sensory neuropathy, respectively [34], but LM and FM were not used independently for patients'assessment. Also, high waist circumference (WC) and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) increases the risk of CTS by two times. higher WHR and WC are a risk factor for CTS due to metabolic changes and could be a better predictor of CTS than BMI [27]. Komurcu et al. showed that the wrist to palm and waist/statue ratio were highly associated with severe CTS [35] Also, it was demonstrated that the wrist-palm ratio, waist-hip-height ratio, and waist-stature ratio had a moderate to high accuracy in distinguishing patients with severe CTS from normal people [36]. Several mechanisms may explain these observations. Excess adipose tissue can lead to mechanical compression of the median nerve by narrowing the carpal tunnel space through fat deposition within or around the tunnel [4]. Moreover, adipose tissue functions as an active endocrine organ, releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, promoting local inflammation and perineural edema and exacerbating nerve compression [9]. Additionally, fat infiltration of muscles and connective tissues in obese individuals may alter biomechanics and increase hydrostatic pressure in the carpal tunnel [37]. Thus, anthropometric features could be used for CTS screening. Contrary to BMI, these indices provide adequate information about the distribution of body fat and have a better association with abdominal [38].

In current study, among body composition indices, FM had a significant association with CTS. FM has advantages than BMI for CTS recognition. BMI lacks the ability to differentiate between fat weight and muscle weight. It merely calculates the ratio of weight to height squared. Conversely, FMI precisely quantifies the quantity of fat mass in relation to body size, offering a more precise depiction of body fat content which seems has effects on CTS. The presence of an excessive amount of adipose tissue can release inflammatory compounds termed adipokines. These substances can cause inflammation and swelling around the median nerve [37], hence raising the likelihood of developing CTS. FM is directly correlated with the adipokine-producing capacity of fat mass [39]. While FM has potential benefits over BMI in evaluating the likelihood of CTS, it is worth mentioning that FM is not as widely utilized or as straightforward to compute as BMI, which can be derived just from height and weight measurements [14].

As observed in most studies, higher BMI and wrist circumference were the most common indices associated with CTS [40, 41]. The relationship between BMI and CTS seems to be due to increased fat deposits in the carpal canal, followed by increased hydrostatic pressure, which leads to higher pressure on the median nerve and swelling [3]. This pressure increase can cause fibrosis and thickening of the sub-synovial connective tissue in the carpal canal [42]. Also, Obesity is known to be a pro-inflammatory state [9], and inflammation can lead to swelling of the tissues [43] surrounding the median nerve. This swelling can narrow the carpal tunnel and increase the pressure on the nerve [15]. Another hypothesis in justifying the increased risk of CTS in obese individuals is a metabolic mechanism in these patients, leading to endoneurial edema and inter-fascicular swelling of the median nerve, which could be an important cause of sensory conduction velocity delay in these patients [44]. This mechanism also can describe the mediolateral and dorsovolar diameters of the wrist size which were significantly greater in patients and increased median nerve CSA patients with CTS compared to the control group.

The observed associations between increased body FM, BMI, total ECW, and larger wrist diameters with the incidence of CTS highlight the potential of these parameters as accessible and practical indicators for identifying individuals at higher risk. In particular, FM—reflecting adiposity and related inflammation—plays a critical role beyond what BMI alone can capture. These results support the idea of earlier screening for CTS in overweight or sedentary individuals using simple body composition assessments combined with wrist anthropometric measurements. Early identification of at-risk individuals could facilitate timely interventions, such as weight management and ergonomic modifications, potentially preventing progression to more severe nerve involvement and the need for major and invasive treatments [45, 46]. Furthermore, in workplace environments where advanced diagnostic tools like nerve conduction studies or ultrasound may not be available, anthropometric and body composition measurements offer cost-effective and non-invasive means to screen and monitor populations vulnerable to CTS. Additionally, understanding the relationship between CTS and anthropometric indices highlights the potential role of weight management as an effective preventive strategy to reduce the risk or delay the onset of CTS.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several noteworthy strengths. It is one of the few prospective case–control studies that simultaneously evaluated anthropometric hand measurements, ultrasound evaluation of the median nerve, and a wide variety of body composition characteristics, such as fat mass and ECW ratio. The combination of high-resolution ultrasound and bioelectrical impedance analysis produced accurate, unbiased data, increasing our conclusions'validity. However, certain limitations must be acknowledged. The relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings. Some potentially relevant anthropometric measurements linked to CTS risk in earlier research, like waist circumference and wrist ratio, were not evaluated. Additionally, although we matched cases and controls by age and sex, we did not adjust for other potential confounders, such as occupational repetitive hand use, physical activity level, comorbidities like diabetes or thyroid disorders, and medication use, which are known to influence the risk of CTS [47–49]. Moreover, the study was conducted at a single tertiary care center, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to broader or more diverse populations. However, this study paves the way for future research, suggesting that longitudinal studies with larger cohorts and focusing on the metabolic, inflammatory and environmental factors and other anthropometric indices in patients with CTS could provide more comprehensive insights into CTS risk [50, 51].

Conclusions

This study showed that the body FM, as an indicator of the body fat distribution, total ECW ratio, body weight, BMI, mediolateral and dorsovolar wrist diameters are associated with CTS occurrence but not its severity. Higher body weight, BMI, FM, total ECW ratio, and larger wrist diameters increase the risk of CTS. Higher median nerve CSA is associated with both higher risk of occurrence and higher severity of CTS. Further studies should include larger populations and explore longitudinal changes in body composition, anthropometric indices and CTS progression. We also suggest evaluating the metabolic, inflammatory and environmental factors and other anthropometric indices, such as the waist-to-hip ratio and waist circumference in patients with CTS, in future studies.
