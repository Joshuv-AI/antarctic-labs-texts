RoMa Classification: When the Parts Do Not Equal the Sum
Authors: James P MacNamara, Siddhartha S Angadi
Published: 2026

Cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) derived peak oxygen uptake (peak VO2) is a strong predictor of all‐cause and cardiovascular disease mortality. 1 Decreased cardiorespiratory fitness, defined as a reduction in peak VO2 compared with predicted value for a patient's age, sex and body habitus, is prognostic in patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM). 2 A meta‐analysis of prognostic studies with CPET demonstrated that low peak VO2 (and to be discussed later, worse ventilatory efficiency [VE/VCO2]) were consistently associated with adverse outcomes in patients with HCM. 3 Peak VO2, which is derived from the Fick principle is an integrative measure that denotes the product of convective O2 delivery and oxygen extraction (during exercise). 4 Clinical CPETs produce a rich data set that include outcomes such as O2 pulse as well as peak heart rate. The RoMa classification previously described by Willixhofer et al., aimed to use these components of a CPET with the goal of better identifying exercise limitations in patients with HCM and enhancing prognostic outcomes in a recent report. 5

The RoMa classification is a model that uses percentage of predicted heart rate and oxygen pulse during peak cardiopulmonary exercise test classify patients with HCM: RoMa I: both are normal, RoMa II: normal heart rate, abnormal oxygen pulse, RoMa III: abnormal heart rate and normal oxygen pulse, RoMa IV: both are abnormal. In their initial study, the authors demonstrated significant differences in exercise performance, particularly peak VO2, peak workload, and VE/VCO2 slope, as well as cardiac output and stroke volume, derived from impedance cardiography after grouping patients with HCM. 5 In this issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA), the authors present the results of a validation study to examine if the RoMa classification predicts hard clinical end points. 6 Briefly, they performed a retrospective analysis of 292 patients with HCM and an average of ~6 years of follow‐up for hard outcomes: death (all cause, cardiac, sudden), aborted sudden cardiac death, heart failure hospitalizations, stroke, myectomy, or transplant. Each patient had undergone a CPET and predicted values for peak VO2, age, and oxygen pulse were determined through widely accepted equations. 7 , 8 , 9

Similar to their derivation study, the authors demonstrate a stepwise decrease in exercise performance from RoMa I to RoMa IV, particularly peak VO2, and resting echocardiographic characteristics, specifically maximal wall thickness and presence of left ventricular outflow tract obstruction. The decrease in exercise performance is expected given that heart rate and O2 pulse are direct functions of peak VO2 and demonstrate the patients were appropriately sorted. In terms of hard outcomes, patients in RoMa I had significantly fewer events than RoMa IIto IV, but within the “abnormal” groups, there was little difference. In addition, multivariate analyses demonstrated that the RoMa classification failed to provide additive value beyond CPET‐derived peak VO2 or VE/VCO2 slope in predicting adverse outcomes in patients with HCM. Based on these results, it appears that the RoMa score that is derived from CPET variables lacks predictive, clinical utility over existing parameters.

WHERE DOES RoMa CLASSIFICATION FIT INTO THE ASSESSMENT OF PATIENTS WITH HCM?

Peak VO2 has garnered significant attention in the wider HCM community due to its use as a surrogate clinical trial end point and recent success of cardiac myosin inhibitors. 10 , 11 Patients are generally more affected by the morbidity of HCM, symptoms, and exercise intolerance than mortality, which is often normal in patients who are at low risk for ventricular arrhythmias. 12 Thus, for the evaluation of novel therapeutics, patient symptoms and functional capacity are used as clinical end points, and the cardiac myosin inhibitors were evaluated in terms of patients' symptoms and peak VO2 in the EXPLORER‐HCM (Clinical Study to Evaluate Mavacamten [MYK‐461] in Adults With Symptomatic Obstructive Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy) and SEQUIOA‐HCM (Phase 3 Trial to Evaluate the Efficacy and Safety of Aficamten Compared to Placebo in Adults With Symptomatic Obstructive HCM) trials for mavacamten and aficamten, respectively. 10 , 11 In combination with mechanistic data showing sustained reductions in left ventricular outflow tract obstruction, both drugs have been approved by the Food and Drug Administration in the United States. In patients with nonobstructive HCM, the ODYSSEY‐HCM (A Study of Mavacamten in Non‐Obstructive Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy) trial failed to meet its primary end point of improvements in peak VO2 and Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire. 13 Thus, interest exists in identifying metrics amendable to modification as alternative to peak VO2 in the heterogenous population of patients with HCM, particularly in patients with nonobstructive HCM.

In these circumstances, the RoMa classification showed initial promise. Breaking down peak VO2 into its components, maximal heart rate and oxygen pulse, has the potential to identify subpopulations within HCM that may respond differently to therapeutic intervention. Chronotropic incompetence is common in HCM and is often related to beta blockers or calcium channel blockers used as first‐line therapies. 14 The oxygen pulse is derived by dividing VO2 by heart rate and is a controversial parameter. Measuring stroke volume during CPET requires specialized equipment, and the highest quality approach is to use invasive right heart catheterization and direct Fick method or thermodilution. Thus, oxygen pulse is often simply used as a “surrogate” of stroke volume, given link between cardiac output and peak VO2. When one delves into the equation of oxygen pulse, this is derived: oxygen pulse = stroke volume × oxygen extraction. Thus, it lays bare that the O2 pulse ignores the role of oxygen delivery as well as skeletal muscle extraction during exercise. Further, arteriovenous oxygen extraction is quite variable with patients with HCM and should not be neglected. 15 In the authors' analysis of their derivation cohort, they compared oxygen pulse to stroke volume, measured by impedance cardiography, and found only a modest relationship. 5 , 16 Despite these limitations, there was initial promise that RoMa classification could identify and differentiate physiological sources of exercise intolerance in HCM.

The results of the present study suggest that the RoMa classification fails to provide additive value both within classifications and above existing measures. Patients classified as RoMa I demonstrate better outcomes compared with RoMa II to IV. However, the lack of difference in outcomes between RoMa II, III, and IV limits the score's usefulness. At best, the classification identifies poor exercise performance but fails to demonstrate differential underlying mechanisms. Additionally, the classification fails to outperform established prognostic markers such as peak VO2 or VE/VCO2 slope. The VE/VCO2 slope is a measure of ventilation perfusion matching and provides discriminatory power in patients with heart failure and with HCM. 3 , 17 Thus, VE/VCO2 slope provides novel information separate from peak performance, which can be measured in setting of a submaximal test, and VE/VCO2 slope is a primary end point of the ongoing FORTITUDE‐HCM (A Trial to Evaluate the Efficacy and Safety of Ninerafaxstat in Patients With Symptomatic Non‐obstructive Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy) trial testing efficacy and safety of ninerafaxstat (NCT07023614). RoMa does not carry these advantages, because one must carry out a high‐quality, maximal effort CPET and measure peak VO2 to obtain the peak oxygen pulse and maximal heart rate. Based on the present report, RoMa fails to add additional prognostic value, above and beyond the assessment of peak VO2 and VE/VCO2 slope. We hope future analyses of RoMa find more success, and the score still has promise of differentiating response to therapy that may target either heart rate or stroke volume during exercise. Both cardiac myosin inhibitors increased peak heart rate, though the effect of oxygen pulse was mixed. 18 , 19 HCM is a complex disease with variable pathophysiology, and the physiological assessment of it is further complicated by pharmacotherapies that directly affect determinants of the Fick principle. RoMa had a promising start; however, the quest for outcomes beyond peak VO2 for prognostication in these patients continues.
