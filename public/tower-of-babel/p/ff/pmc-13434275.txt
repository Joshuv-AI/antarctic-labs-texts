A hybrid approach for suppressing ambipolar current in short-channel TFETs based on zigzag antimonene nanoribbons
Authors: Amirreza Ghazi, Arash Yazdanpanah-Goharrizi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this paper, we report a tunnel field-effect transistor (TFET) based on a zigzag antimonene nanoribbon (ZSbNR) with a 12 nm channel length, simulated using density functional theory (DFT). This study investigates the effects of the drain pocket (DP), underlap, and lightly doped drain (LDD) techniques on ambipolar current and overall performance in short-channel TFETs utilizing low-dimensional materials. However, the first two methods increased short-channel effects (SCEs), indicating limited effectiveness in short-channel TFETs. The third technique, LDD, despite decreasing the OFF-current, reduced the ambipolar current by only about 40 times. This motivated the adoption of a hybrid approach to mitigate both SCEs and ambipolar current simultaneously. The DP method shows strong capability in reducing ambipolarity. A 4 nm DP decreases the ambipolar current by two orders of magnitude, and further increasing the DP length enhances this effect. However, it also leads to a sharp increase in the OFF-current, degrading the subthreshold swing and severely limiting its applicability. The underlap method, by contrast, has a weaker influence on ambipolar suppression but causes less degradation in the OFF-state characteristics. When combined with the LDD technique, the underlap’s suppression effect is significantly enhanced, while its adverse impact on the OFF-current is mitigated. Specifically, employing a 3 nm underlap together with a 4 nm LDD with a modified doping concentration reduces the ambipolar current by more than 600 times, while keeping the OFF-current nearly unchanged compared to the initial TFET. Finally, the proposed hybrid approach reduces the intrinsic delay time by more than threefold, demonstrating its substantial effectiveness in improving the overall device performance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51485-z.

Introduction

According to Moore’s law, continuous downscaling of metal-oxide-semiconductor FETs (MOSFETs) is essential to increase computational capability. However, scaling in MOSFETs leads to the emergence of SCEs, degrading electrostatic control and consequently increasing static power dissipation. This problem leads researchers to increasingly investigate alternative materials and device architectures that can better withstand further miniaturization. One of the replacement options, the TFET, is under extensive consideration. Its innovative band-to-band tunneling (BTBT) operation mechanism enables, unlike MOSFETs, which use a thermionic mechanism for carrier injection, achieving a subthreshold swing lower than the thermionic limit of 60 mV/decade at room temperature1–3. As a result of this unique mechanism, TFET exhibits greater resistance to scaling and maintains stronger electrostatic control than MOSFET, leading to reduced static power dissipation. Therefore, TFET can emerge as a promising alternative to MOSFET. However, replacing TFET with MOSFET in CMOS technology poses significant challenges4,5. One of the most critical challenges is the undesirable ambipolar behavior of TFET, which is caused by BTBT current between the channel valence band and the drain conduction band under negative gate bias in p-i-n devices. To date, numerous methods have been proposed to suppress the ambipolar current6–11. However, these approaches have primarily been studied in TFETs with channel lengths exceeding 15 nm, while their impact on the performance of the short-channel TFETs remains uninvestigated. Whether these methods are equally effective in short-channel TFETs or may enhance SCEs remains to be seen. Furthermore, most of these methods have been applied to TFETs based on bulk materials, such as silicon and group III–V compounds. In contrast, their influence on TFETs based on two-dimensional (2D) materials has not yet been thoroughly investigated.

2D materials are emerging materials, and their exceptional properties, such as high carrier mobility, large surface-to-volume ratio, and strong resistance to strain, have attracted significant attention12–15. Their atomic-scale thickness, compared to that of their bulk counterparts, can significantly enhance electrostatic control of the gate. Hence, some of these materials, along with their nanoribbons with a suitable electronic bandgap, have been widely employed as FET channels in many theoretical and experimental studies involving TFETs16–22. β-Antimonene, a promising channel material, meets industry requirements23 and performs well in ultra-scaled FETs, even surpassing that of MoS224 and exhibiting anisotropic transport behavior25. There have also been reports of the synthesis of Antimonene26–28. Nevertheless, TFETs based on antimonene nanoribbons have not yet been reported. A nanoribbon is formed by cutting a 2D material along one direction, and depending on the cutting orientation, different types of nanoribbons can be obtained. The two primary types are armchair and zigzag. Compared to the armchair configuration, H-passivated ZSbNR exhibits a smaller and direct bandgap. This study investigates the effectiveness of three individual methods, gate underlap, DP, and LDD, as well as a proposed hybrid approach that combines the underlap and LDD techniques, in suppressing the ambipolar current in an H-passivated ZSbNR-based TFET with a 12 nm channel length and 2.5 nm width, and examines their influence on the device’s electrostatic integrity and delay performance.

Methods

All first-principles calculations in this study were performed based on DFT. To describe the exchange-correlation interactions, the Generalized Gradient Approximation (GGA), in the form of the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) method, was used29. The GGA-PBE functional is widely used in DFT-based simulations of 2D-material FETs30–32. Previous DFT-GGA simulations of short-channel MoS2 MOSFETs have also demonstrated good agreement with experimental transfer characteristics in terms of current magnitude and subthreshold swing, supporting the applicability of this approach for investigating transport in 2D-material-based FETs30,33.

The simulations were conducted in two steps: geometry optimization and calculation of transport properties. In the first step, to obtain the optimum geometry and calculate the electronic properties of the nanoribbon using the Quantum ESPRESSO package35, the projector augmented wave (PAW) pseudopotential was used36, with a 400 eV cutoff energy for the plane-wave basis set and a k-point grid of 1 × 1 × 7 and 1 × 1 × 25 for geometry optimization and electronic property calculations, respectively. The geometry was optimized until the total energy change was less than 10− 5 eV, and the residual force on each atom was below 0.01 eV/Å. To prevent van der Waals interactions between the nanoribbon and its periodic image, a 15 Å vacuum space was applied.

For the second part, the Non-Equilibrium Green’s Function (NEGF) formalism was employed to solve the Schrödinger equation in the ballistic regime, in conjunction with the Poisson equation, in a self-consistent manner using the TranSIESTA software package37.

The double-zeta polarized (DZP) basis was employed, and the real-space mesh cut-off energy was set to 30 Hartree. The k-point sampling used to describe the Brillouin zone for device self-consistent calculations was 1 × 1 × 301. The transport calculations were performed under the ballistic approximation. Therefore, the scattering mechanisms were not explicitly accounted for. Since all investigated structures were simulated within the same ballistic transport framework, the comparative suppression of ambipolar current is physically consistent across the studied configurations. To simulate the transport properties, two semi-infinite electrodes were used, each consisting of a repeated nanoribbon unit cell. Their structure and constituent atoms are the same as those of the zigzag nanoribbon unit cell, namely Sb and H. The extension value was determined to be two unit cells in the transport direction, corresponding to approximately 8.2 Å.

Doping in each region (source, drain, DP, and LDD) was modeled using an external potential of the atomic compensation charge type. The doping charge was uniformly assigned to the atoms in the doped region (e/atom) via this external potential, leading to abrupt changes in the doping profile at interfaces between adjacent regions. To model the gate underlap configuration, a region of specified length at the channel-drain boundary was defined as ungated. In the self-consistent NEGF-Poisson framework, this was implemented by defining the metallic gate region such that it does not overlap with the underlap section. The Poisson equation was solved using the conjugate gradient method. Neumann boundary conditions were applied along the vertical direction of the nanoribbon plane (where the metal and dielectric are located) and at the nanoribbon edge (where the passivation atoms are located). In contrast, the Dirichlet condition was imposed along the transport direction.

Device structure

In Fig. 1(a), the schematic of a double-gate n-type TFET is depicted. The channel material in this study was chosen as a monolayer H-passivated ZSbNR with a width of 14 atoms (~ 2.5 nm). For the device design, the channel length was set to 12 nm, and the supply voltage (VDD) was 0.6 V. The gate oxide was SiO2 with a thickness of 1 nm. The source and drain were p-type and n-type, respectively, with a primary doping level of 0.06 e/atom, while the channel remained intrinsic. To investigate three techniques for reducing ambipolar current, structural modifications were applied to the device, as illustrated in Fig. 1. In the underlap configuration, the gate length was shortened from the drain side, leaving a portion of the channel adjacent to the drain uncovered. In the DP configuration, a region of the channel near the drain was lightly doped with the same type as the drain, but at a lower concentration (0.02 e/atom). In the LDD approach, the doping concentration in the partial drain region adjacent to the channel was reduced to 0.01 and 0.006 e/atom, referred to as the LDD region. In the hybrid configuration, both the underlap and LDD techniques were simultaneously applied. Additionally, to further suppress the ambipolar behavior and enhance the ON-current of the TFET, the source and drain doping concentrations were set to 0.08 and 0.04 e/atom, respectively.

Results and discussion

Figure 2(a) shows the unit cell of the ZSbNR from the top and lateral views. The dangling bonds on the edges of the structure are passivated with H atoms to improve stability. After optimization, the Sb–Sb bond length is 2.89 Å, and the buckling height is 1.64 Å, which is in good agreement with previous studies41. In Fig. 2(b), the band structure of the ZSbNR with a 14-atom width is presented. The conduction band minimum (CBM) and the valence band maximum (VBM) are both located at the Γ point, confirming the direct band-gap of ZSbNR, as reported in previous studies. The calculated band gap is 1.42 eV, consistent with earlier results41. Increasing the width of the nanoribbon has a negligible effect on the band gap value; therefore, a width of 14 atoms (2.5 nm) was considered for further calculations. The OFF-state, ON-state, and ambipolar-state of the proposed TFETs are defined based on different gate voltages. According to the results of the present work, which will be explained in continuation, in the OFF-state (VGS = −0.5 V), the residual leakage current originates from direct BTBT between the source valence band and the drain conduction band. In the ON-state (VGS = VOFF + VDD = 0.1 V), the dominant transport mechanism is direct BTBT from the source valence band to the channel conduction band. In the negative-polarity, ambipolar-state (VGS = VOFF - VDD = − 1.1 V), the current is governed by direct BTBT from the channel valence band to the drain conduction band. The projected local density of state (PLDOS) for the ambipolar-state is illustrated in Fig. 3. ∆Φ denotes the tunneling window, defined as the energy range within which carriers can tunnel from the channel valence band to the drain conduction band, and W is the tunneling width. Reducing the ambipolar current requires either increasing W or decreasing ∆Φ through appropriate device-structure engineering.

Underlap

In Fig. 4, the transfer characteristics of the TFET without and with 2, 3, and 4 nm underlap configurations are compared. Underlap reduces the ambipolar current but simultaneously leads to an increase in the OFF-current. Increasing the underlap length enhances these effects, suppresses the ambipolar current more effectively, but also weakens the electrostatic control of the gate and increases leakage current. This reveals a trade-off between ambipolar current suppression and increased leakage current, ultimately limiting the approach’s efficiency. The PLDOS of the TFET without and with underlap at a gate bias of −1.1 V is illustrated in Fig. 5(a) and (b). It is observed that W increases due to the underlap. To clarify this behavior, the device’s electrostatic potential at the same gate bias is also shown in Fig. 6(a). In the underlap region adjacent to the drain, the absence of the gate electric field leads to a reduced electric field at the channel-drain junction. It increases W, thereby decreasing the tunneling current between the drain and the channel in the ambipolar-state. In Figs. 7(a) and (b) show the PLDOS of the TFET at the OFF-state without and with underlap; the presence of underlap reduces the effective tunneling distance. To determine the reason for this difference, the electrostatic potential is illustrated in Fig. 8(a). In the underlap region, unlike the gated area, the effect of the − 0.5 V gate bias is absent. Consequently, the conduction band of this region shifts downward, reducing the effective tunneling distance in the OFF-state and thereby increasing the OFF-current.

Drain pocket (DP)

The chosen doping concentration for the DP was 0.02 e/atom, as lower values significantly reduced its ability to suppress the ambipolar current. Based on Fig. 9, the DP has a significant influence on the ambipolar current. Introducing a 4 nm DP leads to more than a 100-fold reduction in ambipolar current, and further increasing the pocket length enhances this effect. However, this improvement comes at the cost of a significant increase in the OFF-current, compromising the TFET’s inherent advantages and limiting its suitability for low-power applications. Therefore, there is a severe limitation in applying this method. Figure 5(c) illustrates the PLDOS of the TFET with DP compared with the initial TFET in Fig. 5(a) at the ambipolar-state, where a significant increase in W is observed. To further clarify the DP’s operating mechanism, the electrostatic potential of the TFET with and without the DP is shown in Fig. 6(b). In the pocket region, the energy overlap, ∆Φ, between the drain conduction band and the channel valence band disappears, and consequently, the width, W, increases. The heavy n-type doping of the DP region causes a local shift in the conduction and valence band edges, which also results in a local modification of the DOS, limiting the availability of energy states for tunneling and thereby suppressing the energy overlap. This explains the reduction in ambipolar current. The PLDOS of the TFET with DP at the OFF-state in Fig. 7(c) reveals a significant decrease in the effective tunneling distance. Based on Fig. 8(b), which compares the electrostatic potential of the TFET without and with DP, at the location of the DP, a tunneling window is created, accounting for the reduction in the effective tunneling distance in the OFF-state and consequently an increase in the OFF-current.

Lightly doped drain (LDD)

The final individual technique is the LDD approach. Lowering the doping concentration in the partial drain region adjacent to the channel, known as the LDD, reduces the electric field at the drain-channel junction, thereby suppressing ambipolar current. First, an appropriate doping concentration must be selected for the LDD region. For this purpose, the doping levels in this region were set to 0.01 and 0.006 e/atom. These configurations are referred to as LDD1 and LDD2, respectively. As shown in Fig. 10, applying a 3 nm LDD1 region reduces the ambipolar current of the TFET, demonstrating the effectiveness of this technique. An essential advantage of the LDD method over the other two approaches is that, in addition to preventing degradation of the OFF-current, it actually reduces the OFF-current and, consequently, the overall power consumption. Furthermore, lowering the doping concentration in the LDD region, specifically in the LDD2 case, and increasing the LDD length further, very slightly enhances these beneficial effects. PLDOS of the TFET in the OFF and ambipolar-state under the LDD approach are shown in Fig. 7(d) and Fig. 5(d), respectively. To clarify the effect of the LDD on ambipolar conduction, Fig. 6(c) compares the electrostatic potential of the initial and LDD TFETs. It can be seen that the LDD-induced reduction in the electric field on the drain side increases W and suppresses ambipolar behavior. In Fig. 8(c), which shows the electrostatic potential of the initial and LDD structures at the OFF-state, the LDD increases the effective tunneling distance, explaining the reduction in OFF-current.

It is worth noting that none of the methods affect the ON-current of the TFET, and the slight increase observed across all techniques is due to increasing the source dopant concentration from 0.06 to 0.08 e/atom. Based on the results in Table 1, extending the DP length to 4 nm yields the most significant reduction in ambipolar current. However, increasing the pocket length to this value results in a tremendous increase in the subthreshold swing, reaching 89 mV/decade, which is very undesirable for low-power applications. Indeed, there is a strong correlation between increased DP length and reduced electrostatic control of the gate. By applying a 4 nm underlap, the ambipolar current decreases by more than 15 times, while the subthreshold swing increases from 62 to 76 mV/decade. Indeed, the underlap method has a smaller impact on the ambipolar current than the DP. Nevertheless, its destructive effect on increasing the OFF-current and, consequently, degrading the subthreshold swing is smaller. Finally, by applying 4 nm of LDD2, the ambipolar current is reduced by nearly 40 times, and the subthreshold swing decreases to 54 mV/decade, indicating superior electrostatic control of the gate.

Hybrid

The first technique, underlap, reduced the ambipolar current by no more than 20 times at best and also moderately increased the OFF-current and subthreshold swing. The second technique, DP, despite lowering the ambipolar current much more (about 100 times), increases the OFF-current and subthreshold swing to an irreparable extent. Another method, LDD, improves the electrostatic control of the gate but, at best, reduces the ambipolar current by nearly 40 times. Although increasing its length can enhance its effectiveness, the results show that this improvement is minimal. Therefore, a hybrid approach that does not degrade the OFF-current and suppresses the ambipolar current to an acceptable extent is required. For this purpose, the LDD method is combined with underlap to both enhance its effect on ambipolar current and compensate for the destructive impact of underlap on the OFF-current. Figure 11 compares the transfer characteristics of a 4 nm underlap TFET with those of an underlap TFET incorporating LDD. Adding LDD to the TFET with underlap moderately decreases the OFF-current and improves the subthreshold swing. Based on the results in Table 2, incorporating a 3 nm LDD1 into a 4 nm underlap configuration enhances ambipolar current suppression by more than 15 times. Overall, the proposed hybrid method reduces the ambipolar current by more than 250 times compared to the initial TFET. Meanwhile, the subthreshold swing decreases from 76 to 74 mV/decade. Of course, this value is not desirable compared to the initial state. According to the LDD results, reducing the LDD doping concentration and increasing its length have only a relatively small effect on this value. Therefore, to improve electrostatic integrity, the underlap length can be reduced, which slightly increases the ambipolar current. To compensate for this increase, LDD2 can be used instead of LDD1 to suppress the ambipolar current to an acceptable level. In this case, the subthreshold swing is 66 mV/decade, and the ambipolar current reduction becomes more than 300 times. Under this condition, the ambipolar current is significantly suppressed while the gate electrostatic control reaches the desired level. Furthermore, due to an increase in ON-state current resulting from the change in doping profile, the ION/IOFF ratio has also improved. Moreover, by increasing the length of LDD2 to 4 nm, further enhancement of the ambipolar current suppression was achieved, more than 600-fold, without significantly affecting the ION/IOFF ratio or subthreshold swing. Figure 12, illustrated PLDOS of the TFET under applying the hybrid approach at the ambipolar and OFF-state. Using the combined method significantly increased W, which explains the sharp decrease in ambipolar current.

To further investigate the influence of the applied approaches, two key performance metrics of the transistor, intrinsic delay time (τ) and power-delay product (PDP), were calculated for the initial TFET structure and modified devices, as reported in Table 3. The τ is defined as follows:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau =\frac{{{C_t}{V_{DD}}}}{{{I_{on}}}}$$\end{document}

Where Ct is the total capacitance, expressed as the sum of channel capacitance (Cch) and fringing field’s capacitance (Cf), the Ct is obtained as:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C_t}=\frac{{\partial {Q_t}}}{{W\partial {V_g}}}$$\end{document}

Where Qt represents the total charge variation in the device induced by the applied gate voltage. The induced charge on the transistor atoms was calculated using Mulliken population analysis. Since the induced charge on each atom of the transistor channel is extracted from the self-consistent DFT–NEGF solution, the charge response to the gate bias is governed by the finite density of states of the confined ZSbNR channel, and the resulting Ct inherently includes quantum capacitance (CQ) effects. Therefore, CQ is naturally incorporated into the extracted Ct rather than introduced as a separate correction term.

The second performance metric, which reflects the switching energy of the device, PDP, is calculated as:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$PDP={V_{DD}}{I_{on}}\tau$$\end{document}

Based on the results in Table 3, despite the increase in Ct caused by DP, the enhanced ON-state current resulting from the modified doping profile yields only a limited reduction in delay time. Applying underlap and LDD significantly reduces the delay time, and combining them yields a hybrid configuration that reduces it by more than threefold. Moreover, the highest PDP is observed in the DP TFET, while the lowest is observed in the hybrid configuration, confirming the effectiveness of the proposed hybrid approach. In low-dimensional materials, especially one-dimensional systems, the density of states is limited, which leads to a relatively small CQ compared to the oxide capacitance. As a result, the Ct becomes dominated by the CQ (i.e., Ct ≈ CQ)42,43. This behavior is also observed in ZSbNR-based TFETs. For further investigation, the CQ-VGS characteristics for both the initial and hybrid structures are plotted in Fig. 13. Based on this figure, the CQ in the hybrid structure is significantly reduced compared to the initial case, clarifying the origin of the reduction in Ct. In addition, another contributing factor to the delay time reduction is the increase in the ON-state current in the hybrid TFET, which arises from the modified doping profile. According to Eq. (4), the delay time is inversely proportional to the ON-state current. Therefore, the combined effect of reduced CQ and enhanced ON-state current leads to the observed delay time improvement.

Conclusion

This article focused on reducing the ambipolar current in a TFET based on a ZSbNR. To address this issue, three methods and a hybrid approach were investigated. Among these three techniques, the DP showed a much more substantial reduction in ambipolar current; however, it also sharply increased the OFF-current and weakened the electrostatic control of the gate. In contrast, the underlap method had a smaller impact on the OFF-current, making it more desirable despite its lower ability to suppress ambipolar current. The LDD technique reduced the OFF-current but had a limited effect on ambipolarity. By combining a 4 nm underlap with a 3 nm LDD, the suppression of ambipolar current was greatly improved; in contrast, the adverse effect of the underlap on the OFF-current was slightly reduced, and the subthreshold swing decreased from 76 mV/decade to 74 mV/decade. However, this value remains higher than the initial value of 62 mV/decade. Finally, by adjusting the underlap length to 3 nm and optimizing the LDD region length and doping concentration, the ambipolar current was reduced by more than 600 times, improving the subthreshold swing to 66 mV/decade. In addition, a threefold reduction in the TFET delay time was observed, demonstrating the significant effectiveness of the proposed hybrid approach.
