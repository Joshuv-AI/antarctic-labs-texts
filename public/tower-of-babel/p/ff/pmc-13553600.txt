Association of orthogeriatric co-management with the occurrence of secondary fragility fractures in patients with an initial hip fracture
Authors: Kilian Rapp, Daniel Schoene, Patrick Roigk, Clemens Becker, Claudia Konnopka, Hans-Helmut König, Thomas Friess, Carsten Schoeneberg, Andrea Jaensch, Dietrich Rothenbacher, Gisela Büchele
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSummaryThis study examined the association between the availability of an orthogeriatric co-management and secondary fractures in 97,976 hip fracture patients. We found that the presence of orthogeriatric co-management was associated with a small but sustainable reduction in secondary fragility fractures in patients with an initial hip fracture.PurposeThe risk of experiencing a subsequent fracture is particularly high immediately following an initial fragility fracture. Geriatricians are increasingly involved in the management of fragility fractures. However, there is currently no evidence indicating whether this orthogeriatric co-management (OGCM) can reduce the incidence of secondary fracture. This study aimed to analyse the association between OGCM and the occurrence of secondary fragility fractures in patients with an initial hip fracture.MethodsNationwide health insurance data from Germany were used to identify hip fracture patients aged ≥ 80 years. According to the presence of an OGCM, hospitals were categorised into those with OGCM and those without OGCM. Outcomes were secondary fragility fractures (i.e. humerus, forearm, hip, pelvis, spine) within different time periods after an initial hip fracture. Crude incidences and hazard rate ratios for a secondary fragility fracture were calculated.ResultsThe dataset included 97,976 hip fracture patients aged 80 and older from 716 hospitals (71% with OGCM). Within 12 months after the initial fracture, 5487 (5.6%) patients experienced at least one secondary fragility fracture. Patients treated in hospitals with OGCM had a lower incidence of secondary fragility fractures than those treated in hospitals without OGCM. For instance, after 1, 3 and 6 months, there was an absolute risk reduction of 0.1, 0.3 and 0.4% and a relative risk reduction of 27, 14 and 8%.ConclusionsThe presence of an OGCM was associated with a small reduction in secondary fragility fractures in patients with an initial hip fracture.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00198-026-07943-5.Keywords: Aged, Hip fracture, Geriatrician, Orthogeriatric co-management, Secondary fragility fractures

Introduction

Geriatricians are increasingly involved in the treatment of fragility fractures. Evidence from observational and a few interventional studies suggests that such an orthogeriatric co-management (OGCM) can reduce mortality [1–4] and improve physical function in patients with hip fractures [5]. Additionally, there is strong evidence indicating that the risk of a subsequent fracture is particularly high immediately after the initial fracture [6, 7].

This elevated risk may be attributed to factors like muscle weakness, impaired coordination due to immobilisation, postoperative delirium, changes in medication that increase the use of fall-risk-increasing drugs (FRIDs), or the challenges of adjusting to a new environment after institutionalisation [8–10]. Consequently, an objective of OGCM should be preventing secondary fractures. Given that most fractures in older adults result from falls and/or low bone mass, reducing fall risk and improving bone mass are logical strategies to mitigate this imminent risk [11].

However, there is limited evidence that OGCM reduces the incidence of secondary fractures. For instance, Hawley conducted a pre-post observational analysis using a large dataset from British hospitals with OGCM or fracture liaison services. The study observed a reduction in mortality among hip fracture patients over 10 years but found no decrease in secondary hip fractures [12]. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses evaluating OGCM often report outcomes such as mortality, length of hospital stay, or delirium, but rarely address secondary fractures as a specific outcome [1–3, 13, 14]. The only review to consider secondary fractures, conducted by van Camp et al. [15], concluded that evidence supporting the prevention of subsequent fractures is limited and inconclusive, based on just two small studies [16, 17].

While in Germany, OGCM has been mandatory for the treatment of hip fractures since 2020, during 2014–2018—the period from which our data originate—about one-third of hospitals still did not have an OGCM. Our previous research demonstrated that OGCM availability is associated with higher prescription rates of specific anti-osteoporotic medications and vitamin D following fragility fractures in Germany [18]. Additionally, we observed in a smaller dataset that OGCM may be associated with fewer secondary hip fractures after an index hip fracture during the first months after discharge [19]. However, the dataset was smaller, and other types of fragility fractures, such as those of the humerus, forearm, spine or pelvis, were not considered as potential secondary fractures.

The present study again analyses the association between the availability of an OGCM and secondary fractures in patients with an index hip fracture. However, the dataset is considerably larger, and we consider now as outcome not only hip fractures but also other fragility fractures that typically occur in older patients after a hip fracture.

Methods

Study design, data source and study population

The retrospective cohort study consisted of patients insured by the Allgemeine Ortskrankenkasse (AOK), Germany’s largest health insurance provider, which insures nearly one-third of the country’s population. It included all patients who experienced an index hip fracture between January 1, 2014, and December 31, 2018. Hip fractures were identified using hospital admission diagnostic codes S72.0 and S72.1 (ICD-10) alongside a surgical procedure code, as nearly all hip fractures are treated surgically. The study focused on patients aged 80 and older, who are considered geriatric due to their age-related vulnerabilities and are expected to benefit from geriatric treatment approaches [20]. The exclusion criteria are reported in Fig. 1. Data were provided by the scientific institute of the AOK (‘Wissenschaftliches Institut der AOK’, WIdO) in a de facto anonymised form.

Treatment systems

The objective of this study was to determine whether the availability of an orthogeriatric co-management (OGCM) within a hospital influences the occurrence of secondary fractures. However, routine administrative data do not contain explicit information on whether a hospital provides an OGCM service. What is available in claims-based routine data, however, is documentation of whether a so-called ‘complex early geriatric rehabilitation’ was performed. This intervention is identified by the procedure code OPS 8–550.

A complex early geriatric rehabilitation must, by definition, be delivered by a multidisciplinary geriatric team led by a geriatrician. Therefore, the OPS 8–550 code was used as a surrogate marker for the availability of a geriatrician/geriatric team, and consequently as an indirect indicator of the presence of an OGCM at the respective hospital. Given that OPS 8–550 is associated with substantial financial reimbursement, it is highly unlikely that a hospital would maintain an OGCM structure without performing and coding a complex early geriatric rehabilitation.

Since we hypothesise that a substantial proportion of the potential benefit of OGCM is mediated through the implementation of complex early geriatric rehabilitation, the following section provides a more detailed description of the components of this intervention.

Complex early geriatric rehabilitation is a multidisciplinary rehabilitation program initiated shortly after surgery of the hip fracture. It is delivered by a geriatric team led by a geriatrician and comprising physiotherapists, occupational therapists, specially trained nurses, psychologists, social workers and additional disciplines as required. Complex early geriatric rehabilitation includes a minimum of 20 therapy sessions of 30 min each, which must be provided by the therapeutic team within a 14-day period. The intervention incorporates a standardised comprehensive geriatric assessment, an ongoing medication review and adjustment, management of medical complications such as infections or delirium, and a general focus on geriatric syndromes. A central objective of the program is early mobilisation of patients. Regular interdisciplinary team meetings are conducted to develop an individualised rehabilitation plan with specific functional goals, to review the clinical course and to adapt the therapeutic strategy accordingly [21].

Hospitals were categorised based on their involvement in orthogeriatric co-management (OGCM) on a yearly basis into two groups: (1) no OGCM and (2) OGCM. Hospitals were classified as having OGCM if they recorded the procedure code ‘OPS 8–550’ (for reimbursement of the complex early geriatric rehabilitation) at least 10 times annually, regardless of the admission reason. Hospitals could appear in both groups because a hospital was considered ‘with OGCM’ from the first year in which at least 10 patients with ‘OPS 8–550’ were documented. OGCM can be provided in either orthopaedic or geriatric units, with typical models including shared responsibility on an orthogeriatric unit or a geriatric liaison service on an orthopaedic unit, often followed by a transfer to a geriatric unit. Hospitals with such transfers exceeding 5% of their patients were excluded from the analyses to avoid a) misclassification of exposure and b) selection bias of transferred (and not transferred) patients.

The OPS 8–550 procedure code was used to identify hospitals with the availability of an OGCM. We assume, however, that patients who do not receive complex early geriatric rehabilitation may nevertheless benefit from the geriatric infrastructure of hospitals providing OGCM and from the expertise of the geriatric team available at these institutions. Furthermore, analysing OGCM at the individual level based on the presence or absence of OPS 8–550 codes would introduce selection bias and immortal time bias (survivorship bias) [22] since the OPS 8–550 code requires patients to have a predefined functional status and to undergo a minimum of 14 days of complex early rehabilitation. Instead, identifying hospitals offering OGCM avoids these biases and enables an analysis of the association between the two treatment systems and secondary fragility fractures at the systemic, i.e. hospital, level.

Secondary fragility fractures

The outcome was a secondary fragility fracture after the index hip fracture within different time periods after surgery (0–1 month; 0–2 months; 0–3 months; 0–4 months; 0–5 months; 0–6 months; 0–12 months). Fragility fractures were defined as fractures of the humerus (ICD-10 code: S42), forearm (S52), hip (S72.0, S72.1), pelvis (S32.1, S32.3, S32.4, S32.5, S32.81, S32.83) or spine (S12.0, S12.1, S12.2, S12.7, S12.9, S22.0, S22.1, S32.0).

Covariates

The claims data included patient age (in years), sex and care needs prior to the index fracture. Care needs are assessed under the German Social Security Code XI (‘Sozialgesetzbuch’) to determine eligibility for long-term care benefits, which require support for basic daily activities (e.g. bathing, dressing) and instrumental activities of daily living (e.g. shopping, cleaning). The presence of care need (yes/no) in the month preceding the fracture served as a marker for disability [23]. The number of patients with hip fractures per hospital per year was used as an indicator of hospital size and trauma surgery expertise. Comorbidity coding can be influenced by reimbursement incentives, especially for patients undergoing the OPS 8–550 procedure. Therefore, a medication-based comorbidity score was used to minimise bias [24]. Medications prescribed in the year prior to the fracture were categorised, and a point was added to the comorbidity score for each of the 22 predefined disease groups. Since post-acute rehabilitation may also affect the risk of a secondary fragility fracture, the frequency of patients per hospital transferred to post-acute rehabilitation facilities with a corresponding fracture type was additionally included as a covariate.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarised using means with standard deviations for continuous variables or absolute numbers with percentages for categorical variables. In cases where a patient had multiple secondary fractures during the observation period, only the first secondary fracture was included in the analysis. Crude incidences and hazard rate ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) for a secondary fragility fracture were calculated. Adjustments were made for age, sex, care need (yes/no) in the month prior to the index fracture, transfer to post-acute rehabilitation (hospital-specific transfer percentages; categories: 0 for no transfer, 1–4 for quartiles), number of patients with hip fracture per hospital per year, and a medication-based comorbidity score. All continuous covariates were standardised to have a mean of zero and a unit variance. Event rates for the graphical presentation of cumulative secondary fragility fracture risk were analysed using a generalised additive Poisson model (GAM), using an expanded data format with one observation per person-day. The temporal trend of event rates was modelled using spline functions, allowing separate trajectories for each treatment group. The same adjustment variables were used as for the calculation of HRs. Subgroup analyses were performed stratified by age categories (80–84; 85–89; 90 + years) and sex. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS Version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC) and R version 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

The dataset included 97,976 hip fracture patients aged 80 and older from 716 hospitals (Fig. 1, Table 1). In the majority of the hospitals, an OGCM was available. Among the patients, 78.2% were women, and about 25% lived in nursing homes before their fracture. The rate of transfers to post-acute rehabilitation was lower in hospitals with OGCM (31.3%) than in hospitals with no OGCM (49.8%) (Table 1). Within 6 and 12 months after the initial fracture, 3161 (3.2%) and 5487 (5.6%) patients, respectively, experienced a secondary fragility fracture (Fig. 1). Approximately half of these secondary fractures were hip fractures.

Hospitals with OGCM had a lower incidence of secondary fragility fractures than hospitals without OGCM. For instance, after 1, 3 and 6 months, there was an absolute risk reduction of 0.1, 0.3 and 0.4% and a relative risk reduction of 27, 14 and 8% (Fig. 2). While the relative risk reduction decreased over time, the absolute risk reduction slightly increased during the 12-month observation period (Figs. 2 and 3). Analyses stratified by age and sex showed no consistent pattern (Supplementary Fig. 1). If the analyses were stratified according to nursing home status (yes/no), an association between OGCM and secondary fragility fractures was observed only among community-dwelling patients, but not among nursing home residents (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of geriatric patients with hip fractures, we observed an association between treatment in hospitals with OGCM and a reduced incidence of secondary fragility fractures. The relative risk reduction was highest during the first months and declined thereafter. The absolute risk reduction was only small but remained stable or even slightly increased within the 12-month observation period. The association between treatment in hospitals with OGCM and a reduced incidence of secondary fragility fractures was only observed among community-dwelling patients, but not among nursing home residents.

Given the small absolute effect, one secondary fragility fracture can be prevented for every 250 hip fractures within 12 months (number needed to treat). However, our results do not describe the risks or benefits for any single individual who receives OGCM. We identified OGCM in a hospital in the claims data by the German procedure classification code ‘OPS 8–550’, indicating the performance of a complex early geriatric rehabilitation. Since a complex early geriatric rehabilitation requires a pre-defined functional status and must be delivered over a minimum of 14 days, an approach that analyses the relationship of OGCM with secondary fragility fracture risk at an individual level would introduce strong selection and immortal time bias. To mitigate this, we applied a systemic approach, evaluating the potential benefits of OGCM based on its availability in hospitals treating patients with hip fractures. This approach can be compared to an intention-to-treat analysis, in which the evaluation is determined by the group to which the individual was assigned (e.g. hospital with OGCM), rather than by whether the individual actually received the intervention. Therefore, the degree of individual benefit of receiving OGCM is probably higher than our reported estimates.

Currently, there is only very limited data available regarding the impact of OGCM on preventing secondary fractures [15]. This is remarkable, considering that secondary fragility fractures are a significant clinical problem and that the objectives of OGCM include reducing fall risk and initiating anti-osteoporotic medication. Only our earlier study, which used a smaller dataset and considered only hip fractures as secondary fractures, suggested that OGCM might have a beneficial effect on secondary fractures [19].

The present study now shows, for the first time, that OGCM appears to have a possible, small, time-dependent effect on the occurrence of secondary fragility fractures. The reasons for this association are speculative. However, it seems likely that the initial association observed in the first few months may result from a reduced fall rate [25]. This could be explained by the more intensive therapy provided through complex early geriatric rehabilitation, which includes 20 units of therapy (e.g. physiotherapy or occupational therapy) within 14 days. The data show that nearly half of all hip fracture patients from hospitals with OGCM completed such a complex early geriatric rehabilitation. Another possible reason could be a higher awareness of the orthogeriatric team regarding fall risk factors and fall risk-increasing medical complications.

We have already shown that hospitals with OGCM are more likely to initiate specific anti-osteoporotic medications and vitamin D compared to hospitals without OGCM [18, 26]. However, the absolute rate of new anti-osteoporotic medications after a fragility fracture was still relatively low, even in hospitals with OGCM. Therefore, only a moderate effect on the occurrence of secondary fractures can be expected. The favourable association observed during the first few months in hospitals with OGCM persisted over the following months and increased slightly. This may be attributed to the higher rate of anti-osteoporotic medication initiated among patients treated in hospitals with OGCM.

There may be several reasons why nursing home residents did not appear to benefit from OGCM. First, these patients are often discharged relatively quickly after surgery back to their familiar care setting. Second, they frequently exhibit substantial functional impairment, which may limit their eligibility for inclusion in complex early geriatric rehabilitation. Third, nursing home residents may be less likely to receive specific anti-osteoporotic medication.

The strengths of our study are the large number of included hospitals and patients, and the chosen methodological approach to analyse the scientific question at a systemic level rather than an individual level. We believe that this approach is robust and conservative, significantly minimising the risk of selection bias. In addition, as we used claims data, almost no selection or response bias on the individual level is present. Our study has some limitations. It is an observational study analysing a heterogeneous situation of geriatric care in Germany [27]. Therefore, any form of bias cannot be completely excluded. Due to the use of health claims data, we could not adjust for further potential confounding factors such as cognitive function or social support. Additionally, the actual number of secondary fragility fractures may be slightly higher than reported, as our data only included fractures treated in hospitals. However, we do not believe this limitation introduces a systematic bias. Mortality is a competing risk and associated with OGCM [19, 28]. This may influence the association between OGCM and secondary fragility fractures. Secondary fractures are also influenced by post-discharge variables. This is particularly true for a transfer to a post-acute rehabilitation clinic, which was more common in hospitals with no OGCM. Therefore, the transfer rate was adjusted at the hospital level. Other factors, such as the prescription of physiotherapy or the outpatient initiation of anti-osteoporotic therapy, are also likely to influence secondary fractures. However, a systematic bias is not expected to arise from these factors. We applied proportional hazard regression analysis and censored patients who died during the observation period. Nonetheless, we cannot entirely rule out the possibility that varying mortality rates between hospitals with and without OGCM may have influenced our results.

As an observational study, our findings may be influenced by the specific structure of the German healthcare system, which may limit the generalizability of our results to other countries, for example those employing different models for secondary fracture prevention, such as fracture liaison services (FLS). However, OGCM represents a basic principle of fracture management that exists in many industrialised nations.

In conclusion, this retrospective cohort study examined the association between hospitals with the availability of OGCM and the occurrence of secondary fragility fractures in geriatric patients with an initial hip fracture. We found that the incidence of secondary fragility fractures was lower among hospitals with OGCM compared to hospitals with no OGCM. However, the absolute risk reduction was only small.
