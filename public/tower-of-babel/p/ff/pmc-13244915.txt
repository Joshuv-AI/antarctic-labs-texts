Long‐term outcomes following a 5‐year recurrence‐free interval in non‐muscle‐invasive bladder cancer
Authors: Peter B. Hjort, Jørgen B. Jensen, Andreas Ernst
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo describe the recurrence and progression trends of non‐muscle‐invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) during a 14‐year period in Denmark between 2009 and 2022. Furthermore, to evaluate the risk of late recurrence with or without progression in patients who initially experience a 5‐year recurrence‐free interval (RFI).Patients and MethodsWe conducted a nationwide cohort study of all patients diagnosed with NMIBC in Denmark between 2009 and 2022, linking data from population‐based registries (N = 21 037). Recurrence was defined as a new bladder tumour ≥90 days after transurethral resection of bladder tumour, and progression followed the criteria set out by Lamm et al. or cystectomy. Cumulative incidence and Cox regression analyses described risks of recurrence and progression within 5 years after primary diagnosis. A landmark analysis assessed late recurrences with or without progression among patients who were recurrence‐free for ≥5 years.ResultsWe identified 21 037 patients with NMIBC (median age 72 years; 76% male). The 1‐ and 5‐year cumulative incidence of recurrence was 24% (95% confidence interval [CI] 24–25%) and 44% (95% CI 43–45%), while progression risks were 4.4% (95% CI 4.1–4.7%) and 10% (95% CI 9.7–11%). Among the 6583 patients who were recurrence‐free for 5 years, the subsequent 5‐year cumulative incidence was 9.8% (95% CI 9.0–11%) for recurrence and 1.5% (95% CI 1.2–1.9%) for progression, with one‐third progressing to MIBC.ConclusionOur study confirmed previous findings that most recurrences in patients with NMIBC occur within the first years after primary diagnosis. Furthermore, we found improved recurrence‐free rates over the past 14 years. Our analysis suggests the risk of recurrence and progression persists after a 5‐year RFI; however, only in a limited proportion of patients. Future studies should identify specific risk factors able to predict patients’ long‐term risk, to tailor potential long‐term follow‐up schemes for patients with NMIBC.

Introduction

Bladder cancer is the 11th most common cancer worldwide, with >600 000 incident cases each year. In Denmark, 2000 new cases are diagnosed yearly [1]. Non‐muscle‐invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) constitutes 75% of these and is characterised by a high risk of recurrence and a lower but variable risk of progression, depending on risk factors, including tumour characteristics at primary diagnosis [2].

It is well established that the risk of recurrence and progression is highest within the first year of primary diagnosis [3]. In the European Association of Urology (EAU) guidelines, low‐ and intermediate‐risk patients are recommended follow‐up with cystoscopies at 3 months, and if negative, every 6 months for 2 years. The length of follow‐up depends on individual risk factors but is suggested to end after 5 years for low‐risk and 10 years for intermediate‐risk disease. In high‐ and very high‐risk patients, recurrences have been reported after a 10‐year recurrence‐free interval (RFI); thus, life‐long follow‐up is recommended [4]. However, the evidence underlying current recommendations is primarily based on sparse and retrospective data [5]. In Denmark, high‐ and very high‐risk patients have historically been recommended life‐long follow‐up, but in 2024, the Danish Bladder Cancer Group (DaBlaCa) updated the guidelines recommending to discontinue follow‐up after a 5‐year RFI in all patients with NMIBC, including high‐ and very high‐risk patients [6]. As data on the risk of recurrence after 5 years onwards are ambiguous and empirical support from large nationwide population‐based datasets is limited, studies are needed to support any of the follow‐up strategies in NMIBC. Furthermore, it remains unclear whether certain patient groups might be at higher risk of recurrences occurring after a 5‐year RFI. If identified, this could promote personalised follow‐up schedule in future guidelines.

Our nationwide, register‐based cohort study aimed to describe the recurrence and progression trends of NMIBC in Denmark between 2009 and 2022. Furthermore, we aimed to evaluate the risk of a late recurrence with or without progression in patients who experience a 5‐year RFI.

Patients and Methods

Design and Data Sources

We carried out a nationwide cohort study based on data obtained from the comprehensive Danish population‐based registries, which have been extensively validated for epidemiological investigations in the fields of urology and oncology [7, 8]. Specifically, the study utilised information from the Danish National Patient Register (DNPR), the Danish Cause of Death Register (CDR), and the Danish Pathology Register (DPR). These data sources were linked at the individual level via the unique Civil Personal Registration (CPR) number, assigned to all residents of Denmark at birth or upon immigration, as maintained by the Danish Civil Registration System [8, 9].

The DNPR has systematically collected information on all non‐psychiatric hospital contacts since 1977, including diagnoses coded according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD‐10), as well as data on diagnostic and surgical procedures related to the diagnostics, treatment and follow‐up of NMIBC [8].

The DPR, which achieved nationwide coverage in 1997, contains detailed records of all pathological examinations conducted in Danish hospitals [10]. We identified patients with NMIBC using the DPR, applying the following coding framework: T74 to restrict topography to the urinary bladder, M812–M813 to specify tumour morphology, ÆF18–ÆF2 to define pathological T‐stage, and ÆYY111–ÆYY113 to capture tumour grade (Appendix S8). Information on cause of death was obtained from the CDR, where all deaths among Danish residents have been systematically recorded using ICD‐10 codes since 1970 [11]. We used time of death to account for competing risks in our analyses. We did not use the cause of death as a measure of progression, as we were not able to validate the cause‐of‐death coding related to urogenital cancers in this registry.

Study Population and Setting

Individuals were included in the study if they received an initial diagnosis of NMIBC recorded in the DPR between 1 January 2009 and 31 December 2022. The study period commenced in 2009, as coding practice in the DPR did not provide uniform and reliable information regarding T‐stage and grading before that time. Additionally, this year marked the transition from the Bergkvist grading system to the currently adopted WHO classification [12]. Data on patients’ initial diagnosis, tumour stage (T‐stage), presence of carcinoma in situ (CIS), and WHO grade, along with detailed records of repeat transurethral resection of bladder tumours (re‐TURBTs), recurrences, and progressions, were extracted from the DPR.

During the study period, some patients had life‐long follow‐up according to the Danish Guidelines, depending on risk stratification. Typically, this would have been with annually cystoscopies and for some supplemented by cytology.

Covariates

Information on patients age, sex, comorbidities, and treatments such as intravesical therapies or cystectomies were obtained from the DNPR. The comorbidities for the Charlon Comorbidity Index were identified through ICD‐codes from the DNPR. A description and a list of all codes used for deriving the index can be found in Appendix S7.

Outcome

Using data from the DPR, a recurrence was defined as the identification of a urothelial tumour in the bladder occurring >90 days after the initial TURBT. We chose the 90 days of lag time to avoid misclassifying routine re‐TURBTs related to their primary diagnosis as a recurrence. A re‐TURBT was defined if any histopathological specimen from the bladder was registered in the DPR within 90 days following the primary TURBT. The worst diagnosis from primary TURBT and any re‐TURBTs defined the characteristics at primary diagnosis if any differences existed. Hierarchy determining the worst bladder tumour stage and grade can be found in Appendix S9. For all papillary tumours, diagnosis could include concomitant CIS. Tumour progression was classified according to the criteria outlined by Lamm et al. [13], encompassing stage progression to T1 or T2, or grade progression from low‐grade (LG) to high‐grade (HG) disease.

Statistics

Baseline characteristics were summarised using descriptive statistics and presented overall and stratified by 3‐year periods according to the year of primary diagnosis. Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages, while continuous variables were summarised using medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs, 25% and 75% quartiles).

For description of trends, cumulative incidence functions for recurrence and progression were plotted using the Aalen–Johansen estimator, treating death and cystectomy as competing risks. For analysis of cause‐specific hazards, Cox proportional hazards regression was used, both crude and adjusted for age, sex, and primary T‐stage. Estimates were stratified by 3‐year intervals of primary diagnosis and by primary tumour characteristics, including T‐stage, grade, and presence of CIS. Cumulative incidence at 1 and 5 years was calculated stratified by year‐groups, and cumulative incidence curves were visualised across calendar periods.

To evaluate the potential future implications of the change in the Danish guidelines going from life‐long to 5 years of follow‐up in the majority of patients with NMIBC, we conducted a landmark analysis restricted to patients who remained recurrence‐free 5 years after their initial diagnosis (n = 6583, Appendix S1). Among these, we calculated cumulative incidence for recurrence and/or progression. Furthermore, we conducted an adjusted Cox proportional hazards regression analysis to identify factors associated with increased risk of recurrence and progression in these patients with a 5‐year RFI.

We conducted sensitivity analyses on re‐TURBT timing, altering the limit for re‐TURBT from 90 to 60 and 30 days thereby trying to capture further early recurrences but at the cost of potential outcome misclassification. In a second sensitivity analysis, we included individuals with a 4‐year RFI in a landmark analysis. This was done to determine if 5 years was a valid cut‐off for discontinuation of follow‐up, or if there could be an argument for earlier discontinuation.

The study is presented following the modified STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE; REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely‐collected health Data [RECORD]) guidelines for register‐based studies. Data management and analyses were performed using R version 4.3.3. (R for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient characteristics for the patients with NMIBC between 2009 and 2022 are presented in Table 1. In total, we identified 21 037 patients with a primary diagnosis of NMIBC in the study period. Overall, patients had a median age at diagnosis of 72 years, and patients were predominantly male (3:1). Half of the patients had a Charlson Comorbidity Index ≥1. In all, 8% of patients received single installation chemotherapy at the time of TURBT, and 24% received adjuvant installation therapy (either BCG or mitomycin‐C [MMC]) following initial diagnosis. Of the 3903 patients who received BCG treatment in relation to their primary diagnosis, 1644 patients received exactly six instillations, and 1655 received more than six instillations. Ta diagnoses accounted for two‐thirds of primary tumour T‐stages, and grading was equally distributed between LG and HG disease. Patient characteristics stratified by year‐groups and by recurrence‐status can be found in the Appendices S2 and S3.

Trends in Recurrence and Progression

In total, 1709 patients received cystectomy, and 846 patients received radiotherapy. A total of 7089 patients died during follow‐up.

The overall cumulative incidence for recurrence in all the patients with NMIBC was 24% (95% CI 24–25%) at 1 year and 44% (95% CI 43–45%) at 5 years (Table 2). The cumulative incidence of recurrence stratified by year of diagnosis is presented in Fig. 1A. The highest cumulative incidence of recurrence was in the year‐group 2009–2011, with a cumulative incidence of 48% (95% CI 47–50%) after 5 years (Table 2). The overall cumulative incidence for progression in the study population was 6.4% (95% CI 6.1–6.8%) after 1 year and 13% (95% CI 13–14%) after 5 years. Figure 1B illustrates the cumulative incidence for progression stratified by years of diagnosis. Similarly to the findings for recurrences, the earliest year‐groups had higher cumulative incidences of progression compared to later year‐groups. For both recurrence and progression, the cumulative incidence in later year‐groups seems to level off at lower cumulative incidences after 2–3 years of follow‐up. However, the later year‐groups have shorter follow‐up durations (Appendix S4). The crude hazard ratio (HR) for recurrence in the late year‐group was 0.82 (95% CI 0.74–0.88), and the adjusted HR was 0.81 (95% CI 0.74–0.88) compared with the early year‐group, adjusting for age, sex, and primary T‐stage. For progression, the crude HR comparing the late and early year‐groups was 0.62 (95% CI 0.52–0.75), and the adjusted HR was 0.64 (95% CI 0.53–0.77).

Late Recurrence and Progression Rates

A total of 555 patients recurred with a tumour in the bladder after a 5‐year RFI, which corresponds to 2.6% of all the patients with NMIBC in the study period. However, only 6583 patients experienced a 5‐year RFI, and the cumulative incidence of recurrence after 5 years for eligible patients was 9.8% (95% CI 9.0–11%). The median (IQR) time to late recurrence was 7.25 (5.93–9.1) years after primary diagnosis. For late recurrences, 77% (428/555) were Ta‐stage, 11% (63/555) were CIS, 6% (36/555) were T1a, T1b and T1 unspecified, 5% (25/555) were ≥T2. For progression, cumulative incidence for patients who experienced a 5‐year RFI was 1.5% (95% CI 1.2–1.9%) after 5 years. In total, 85 of the 555 had progression during follow‐up, and of these, 25 had MIBC. The cumulative incidence plots after a 5‐year RFI are presented in Fig. 2.

Cumulative incidence of late recurrences (A) and late progressions (B), Denmark, 2009–2022.

According to the Lamm et al. [13] definitions on progression, 85/555 (15%) progressed. Of these 85 33 (5.9%) progressed from LG to HG disease, 27 (4.8%) progressed to T1 stage, and 25 (4.5%) progressed to ≥T2 stage.

We conducted a regression analysis using Cox proportional hazards model for late recurrences and progressions. Adjusting for age, and T‐stage group at primary diagnosis and prior recurrences, we found an increased hazard for males for both recurrence and progression. For progression, T1 of any kind had a decreased hazard. Prior recurrences were associated with higher hazard of recurrence and progression. No other factors were associated with difference in hazard. Discussion

This study investigated recurrence and progression trends in Denmark over a 14‐year period, with a particular focus on the risk of late recurrences with or without progression after at least a 5‐year RFI. This was done to inform and reflect on the diverging follow‐up practices for NMIBC around the world.

Comparison with Existing Literature

We observed a 1‐year cumulative incidence of recurrence of 24% (95% CI 24–25%) and a 5‐year incidence of 44% (95% CI 43–45%), with a modest decline during the study period. Progression risk was 4.4% (95% CI 4.1–4.7%) at 1 year and 10% (95% CI 9.7–11%) at 5 years.

The overall cumulative incidence of recurrence is comparable to other studies, but the progression seen in our data is lower than previous studies [2, 14]. However, a newer study from 2025 by Longoni et al. [15] on contemporary NMIBC outcomes showed similar results on progression risks, with 2‐ and 5‐year progression rates of 5.5% and 9.4%, respectively. Regarding recurrence, Longoni et al. [15] only include HG recurrences, which makes direct comparison to our findings difficult. Although our study included more patients, the Longoni et al. [15] study had data on tumour size and multifocality available, which allowed the authors to group patients by EAU 2021 risk categories. Further, they used another definition of progression. Our Cox regression analyses confirmed declining risk of recurrence with an adjusted HR of 0.81 (95% CI 0.74–0.88) while adjusting for confounding factors such as differences in primary T‐stage, age, and sex. Although the data in the Danish registers do not allow for exploring explanations for the declining trend, we speculate whether better equipment, better training of urologists, and a decrease in smoking habits have contributed to the trend observed [16]. Further studies are needed to confirm the decreasing trends of recurrence and progression and explore potential underlying mechanisms.

Among patients who were recurrence‐free for ≥5 years, the 5‐year cumulative incidence was 9.8% (95% CI 9.0–11%) for late recurrence and 1.5% (95% CI 1.2–1.9%) for late progression, of which one third were MIBC.

Previous studies have mainly described recurrences occurring within the first years after diagnosis, when the risk is considered highest. Prospective long‐term data exploring risk of recurrence and progression beyond 5 years remains limited. Our findings are broadly consistent with two existing retrospective studies: in 2025, Villegas et al. [14] reported 9% late recurrences among 529 patients from Canada with Ta LG and papillary urothelial neoplasm of low malignant potential. Further, a Japanese cohort from 2022 found that 12% of patients with NMIBC who were recurrence‐free at 5 years experienced a late recurrence [17]. Neither study provided specific progression risks for patients experiencing a 5‐year RFI. By using nationwide, unselected data with long‐term follow‐up, our study extends these findings and provides more precise estimates of both late recurrence and progression. Unfortunately, we were not able to perform further sensitivity analyses with stratification according to risk factors with the data at hand. However, we observed no differences when stratifying patients based on pathology at primary diagnosis: Ta, CIS presence, or T1.

Interpretation of Findings

In Denmark, NMIBC surveillance follows a standardised cystoscopic schedule. After primary diagnosis, patients undergo cystoscopy at 4 months; if no recurrence is detected, the interval is sequentially extended to 8 and 12 months. Patients are then followed with annual cystoscopies, and surveillance is discontinued after 5 years in the absence of recurrence. Any recurrence leads to a restart of the 4–8–12‐month schedule. The median (IQR) number of cystoscopies per patient was 11 (7–18) during the study period. Of note, the recommendation to discontinue surveillance after a 5‐year RFI was only implemented in 2024. Prior to this change, patients in the intermediate‐ and high‐risk groups were followed indefinitely, resulting in a higher number of cystoscopic evaluations before the introduction of the new guidelines. Furthermore, these patients underwent cystoscopic surveillance every 4 months during the first 2 years after the primary diagnosis, further increasing the number of cystoscopies.

The recent change in Danish guidelines recommends discontinuing cystoscopic surveillance after a 5‐year RFI, irrespective of initial risk classification [6]. Our results lend partial support to this recommendation; the absolute risk of late recurrence and progression was modest. Although the absolute risk is modest, our findings still demonstrate that a non‐negligible proportion of patients develop recurrences beyond the current 5‐year surveillance window. This included a proportion (25 patients) that progressed to MIBC after remaining recurrence‐free for ≥5 years. Such events, although infrequent, carry important implications for patient morbidity, treatment intensity, and long‐term prognosis, and highlight the need to carefully balance the benefits of extended surveillance against its costs and potential burdens. These findings suggest that while discontinuation of follow‐up is reasonable for most patients, extended surveillance may still be warranted in selected subgroups. We also conducted a sensitivity analysis of a 4‐year RFI instead of 5 years (Appendix S6). Cumulative incidence of recurrence after 5 years was 13% (95% CI 12–14%) compared to 9.8% (95% CI 9.0–11%). This could indicate that further de‐intensification of follow‐up could be implemented as the difference in cumulative incidence was only 3%; however, more evidence is needed. Our analysis did not determine specific patient groups, but based on previous literature, patients with multiple HG or CIS recurrences might benefit from extended follow‐up [2].

Intravesical therapy in Denmark consists of either MMC or BCG, administered according to risk stratification. Due to the Danish health care setup, single‐instillation chemotherapy is not used, and neither is double‐agent chemotherapy. At no point during the study period did Denmark experience a substantial impact from BCG shortages. The limitations in the register data on intravesical therapy restrict detailed risk stratification of recurrent events according to treatment. However, we analysed the number of adequate intravesical therapies administered for patients’ primary tumour, according to T‐stage (Appendix S5). For CIS with or without papillary disease, 60% of patients received adequate BCG treatment. However, for Ta HG only 22% received adequate BCG treatment. Hence, urological practice in Denmark does not adhere completely to guidelines regarding intravesical therapy, which could negatively impact the risk of both recurrence and progression for patients with NMIBC.

Our Cox analysis demonstrated increased hazards of both recurrence and progression among male patients, as well as among those with previous recurrences. Interestingly, a T1 diagnosis was associated with a lower hazard compared to Ta LG after adjusting for prior recurrences, age, and sex—a finding we attribute to more intensive treatment in T1 cases. In Denmark, T1 tumours are sub‐staged into T1a and T1b, and patients with T1b tumours are systematically offered cystectomy or radiotherapy, which could explain the limited number of progressions in T1 tumours. These results may support extended follow‐up for male patients with prior recurrences. Nevertheless, given the observational design and inherent limitations of this study, further evidence is needed before considering any changes to clinical practice.

Whether these late recurrences represent true late recurrences or de novo primary tumours cannot be determined within the current framework. This distinction is clinically relevant, as the biological and prognostic implications may differ. If such events predominantly reflect new tumour formation rather than recurrence, prolonged routine cystoscopic surveillance may have limited value, particularly given the relatively low risk of progression observed in this group. Moreover, the cost‐effectiveness and clinical utility of annual cystoscopy in long‐term recurrence‐free patients are questionable, as symptomatic tumours are likely to prompt medical attention regardless of scheduled follow‐up [16]. These considerations may support the rationale for terminating surveillance after 5 years, while highlighting the need for further research to clarify the nature of late events in NMIBC.

Strengths and Limitations

A major strength of this study is the use of comprehensive, nationwide register data, allowing for long‐term follow‐up and near‐complete capture of NMIBC diagnoses, recurrences, and progression events across all Danish hospitals. The unselected cohort and uniform data structure, as well as the long follow‐up and near‐complete coverage from national registers, reduce selection bias and enhance the generalisability of the findings to clinical practice. Furthermore, by focusing specifically on patients who achieved a 5‐year RFI, this study addresses a clinically important and understudied subpopulation in bladder cancer research. The use of cumulative incidence functions accounts for competing risks, such as death from causes other than bladder cancer, which provides a more accurate estimate of the true probability of recurrence and progression. Last, the long follow‐up period, as well as near‐complete coverage from national registers.

Several limitations should be noted. First, despite robust registry linkage, the observational nature of the study limits causal inference. Some relevant clinical details—such as smoking status, comorbidities, or details on intravesical therapy beyond initial TURBT and distinction between induction and maintenance therapy—are incompletely recorded or missing. Furthermore, as details on tumour size and multifocality are not present in the registers, we were not able to group patients according to the European Organisation for the Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC)/EAU risk groups or adjust for such important factors in our regression analysis. Second, histopathological classification may have varied slightly over the 15‐year period, especially across local pathology departments, potentially affecting grading consistency. Additionally, the study cannot account for differences in surveillance intensity or patient adherence that might influence the timing of recurrence detection. Finally, although the study includes long follow‐up, very late recurrences beyond 10 years may still be underestimated especially for the latest time periods. These would have allowed a comparison changes in risk of late recurrences, and evaluation on past vs current follow‐up practices.

Conclusion

Our study supports the dogma that most NMIBC recurrences and progressions occur within the first year after primary diagnosis. We found decreasing trends in NMIBC recurrence and progression over a 14‐year period. Recurrence and progression risks after a 5‐year RFI persist. In future studies, focus should be on more tailored approaches for NMIBC surveillance intensity and duration by investigating risk of late recurrences in subgroups of patients with NMIBC.
