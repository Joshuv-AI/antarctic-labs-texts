Sleep Health in Bulimia Nervosa: A Systematic Review
Authors: Samantha Kenny, Malka Hershon, Linda Booij, Rebecca Burdayron, Marie‐Hélène Pennestri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveResearch on sleep in bulimia nervosa (BN) is methodologically varied and yields inconsistent findings. This review synthesised BN‐sleep studies and examined whether comorbid mood disorders contribute to sleep disturbance.MethodPsycINFO, Medline (Ovid), and Scopus were searched for English or French quantitative studies comparing sleep health variables between individuals with diagnosed BN and healthy controls. Extracted data included study design, sample characteristics, sleep and BN measures, and key findings. Methodological quality was evaluated using a validated risk‐of‐bias tool.ResultsOf 1233 titles/abstracts and 39 full texts screened, 11 studies met inclusion criteria. Some studies using subjective measures reported greater sleep disturbance in individuals with BN, including poorer sleep quality, difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep, and increased daytime sleepiness, with moderate to large effect sizes (|d| = 0.60–1.43). Objective findings were mixed: one actigraphy study reported later sleep onset and wake times in BN, while findings for sleep efficiency and rapid eye movement (REM) sleep parameters were inconsistent, and most polysomnography (PSG) variables showed no reliable group differences despite some large effect sizes (range: |d| = 0.72–1.69). Sleep disturbances were reported in some BN samples with and without comorbid mood disorders, although the specific contribution of mood pathology remains unclear. Studies were generally small, cross‐sectional, and dated.ConclusionsSome studies suggest elevated subjective sleep disturbances in individuals with BN, but findings remain heterogeneous and are limited by methodological variability and small sample sizes. Future studies are needed to better characterise sleep health in individuals with BN.Trial RegistrationThe systematic review was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD42022354504)Keywords: adults, bulimia nervosa, eating disorder, sleep health

Introduction

Bulimia nervosa (BN) is an eating disorder characterised by recurrent binge eating followed by maladaptive compensatory behaviours such as self‐induced vomiting, laxative misuse, or excessive exercise. Individuals with BN frequently experience co‐occurring psychiatric symptoms, including depression, anxiety, and disturbances in sleep (Abdou et al. 2018). Indeed, approximately half of individuals with an eating disorder report significant sleep disturbances—particularly those with binge‐eating/purging presentations (K. R. Kim et al. 2010). Furthermore, sleep problems are among the most common reasons for seeking psychiatric help in clinical settings following eating/weight and emotional problems (Tseng et al. 2016). Despite this high prevalence, the sleep profile of BN remains poorly characterised. Given the role of sleep in emotion regulation, impulse control, metabolic function, and treatment engagement, clarifying sleep patterns in BN has direct clinical relevance for assessment and intervention.

Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain why BN and sleep disturbance frequently co‐occur. BN is characterised by heightened emotional reactivity, difficulties regulating affect, and increased cognitive and physiological arousal, all of which overlap with leading models of insomnia emphasising hyperarousal and impaired nighttime downregulation (i.e., the capacity to ‘wind down’ mentally and physically at bedtime; Harvey 2002; Riemann et al. 2010). Sleep problems may therefore reflect not only consequences of binge‐purge behaviours, but also shared vulnerabilities related to emotion regulation and stress reactivity (Aldao et al. 2010; Lavender et al. 2014). Nighttime may represent a particularly vulnerable period, as reduced structure and increased internal focus can exacerbate both difficulties initiating sleep and urges to binge or purge (Forester et al. 2023; Romo‐Nava et al. 2022). Disruptions in sleep timing and regularity may further contribute to this co‐occurrence, as later sleep timing and irregular sleep‐wake patterns have been linked to impulsivity, emotional dysregulation, and disordered eating behaviours (Adan et al. 2012; Gruber and Cassoff 2014; Kandeğer et al. 2021). Since mood disorders are highly prevalent in BN and strongly associated with sleep disturbance (Hudson et al. 2007), it remains unclear whether sleep problems in BN reflect broader mood‐related difficulties, BN‐specific mechanisms, or both. However, the role of comorbid mood disorders has been inconsistently addressed in the existing literature.

Although research on sleep in eating disorders has grown in recent years, most work has focused on anorexia nervosa (AN). A recent meta‐analysis reported that individuals with AN exhibit reduced sleep efficiency, shorter total sleep time, and diminished slow‐wave sleep compared to healthy controls (Burger et al. 2025). However, findings across objective sleep studies in AN remain highly variable; several PSG and actigraphy studies report minimal or no disturbances, and results frequently differ depending on the sleep measurement method used. In contrast, subjective complaints in AN—such as poor sleep quality, insomnia symptoms, early morning awakenings, and daytime fatigue—tend to be more consistently elevated (Malcolm et al. 2022; Romigi et al. 2021; Sauchelli et al. 2016). Thus, although AN has been investigated extensively, its sleep profile is not straightforward, and discrepancies between subjective and objective measures are common.

By comparison, BN has received far less empirical attention. The available literature on BN and sleep is relatively dated, with most PSG studies conducted in the 1980s and 1990s using small clinical samples, single‐night recordings, and variable reporting of comorbid mood disorders. More recent work has introduced subjective sleep measures and actigraphy, but studies specific for BN remain limited in number and methodologically heterogeneous. Findings across these studies are highly inconsistent: some report subjective difficulties falling or staying asleep or poorer overall sleep quality, whereas others find no differences compared to healthy controls. Objective findings are similarly mixed. Methodological variability—including inconsistent sleep metrics, sample characteristics, and handling of comorbidities—further complicates interpretation and raises questions about whether BN is associated with a distinct sleep profile or whether findings reflect broader eating disorder‐related factors or emotion dysregulation.

One reason for inconsistencies in the literature is that studies examine sleep at varying levels of detail. Some studies focus on broad features of sleep, such as how long people sleep, how efficiently they sleep, or how much time they spend in different sleep stages (i.e., macrostructural measures). Other studies examine sleep microstructure, which refers to transient physiological events that occur within sleep stages, such as sleep spindles, K‐complexes, or cyclic alternating patterns (CAP), brief arousals, and phasic versus tonic components of REM sleep (Parrino et al. 2026). While macrostructural measures capture overall sleep quantity and architecture, microstructural indices provide insight into the moment‐to‐moment stability of sleep. Focussing only on overall sleep duration or efficiency may therefore miss more subtle alterations in sleep regulation that are closely intertwined with emotion regulation, stress reactivity, and daytime functioning—domains centrally implicated in BN (Buysse 2014; Harvey 2002).

Although several broad ED reviews include BN within their scope (Allison et al. 2016; Cinosi et al. 2011; Cooper et al. 2020; Degasperi et al. 2024; Lauer and Krieg 2004), these reviews provide limited conclusions specific for BN. For example, in the meta‐analysis by Degasperi et al. (2024), BN was represented by a comparatively small number of eligible studies, and analyses were conducted at the level of broad macro‐sleep domains (e.g., sleep continuity, sleep depth, REM pressure). This approach masks variability in specific sleep variables such as REM latency, REM density, nocturnal awakenings, or sleep timing—some of which were reported in individual BN studies but not reflected in aggregated results. For instance, Della Marca et al. (2004) reported elevated cyclic alternating pattern (CAP) rates, indicating greater sleep instability in individuals with BN despite no differences in sleep efficiency or sleep stage distribution, while other studies (e.g., Byrne et al. 1990; Lauer et al. 1988) have observed altered REM parameters in the absence of differences in total sleep time, emphasising the need to examine more specific features of sleep architecture rather than relying solely on global sleep measures. Accordingly, reviews covering multiple eating disorders often summarise findings in BN as showing ‘no differences’ from controls, despite primary studies demonstrating subtle but potentially clinically meaningful differences. To date, only one narrative review has focused exclusively on BN and sleep (Royant‐Parola and Pelicier 1990), and it predates modern diagnostic criteria, validated subjective questionnaires, updated PSG scoring rules, and contemporary sleep health frameworks (Buysse 2014).

Taken together, limitations in the existing literature highlight the need for an updated, methodologically rigorous synthesis of the literature specifically focused on BN. The present systematic review aims to: (a) synthesise and critically evaluate the literature examining differences in sleep health variables between individuals with BN and healthy controls; (b) explore the role of mood disorders in these differences; and (c) identify gaps, contradictions, and limitations of the existing research and highlight areas for future study.

Methods

This systematic review was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD42022354504).

Ethics Statement

This study is a systematic review of previously published research and did not involve the collection of new data from human participants; therefore, institutional research ethics approval was not required.

Search Strategy

The search strategy (see Supporting Information S1: Appendix A) was developed in consultation with research librarian Emily Kingsland (McGill University) and the research team. Bulimia nervosa and sleep and related terms (e.g., sleep duration and polysomnography) were identified. The search strategy was reviewed by Dr. Lea Thaler, Assistant Professor in Psychiatry at McGill University and Psychologist at the Eating Disorders Continuum at the Douglas Mental Health University Institute. The systematic search was initially developed for Medline (1946 to Present) and adapted to PsycINFO (1806 to Present) and Scopus.

Eligibility Criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they: (a) included participants with a diagnosis of BN based on DSM‐III, DSM‐III‐R, DSM‐IV, or DSM‐5 criteria; (b) assessed one or more sleep health outcomes; (c) compared individuals with BN to healthy controls; and (d) reported quantitative data. Studies of any age group were eligible. Articles published in English or French were considered, including peer‐reviewed publications and dissertations and theses.

Studies were excluded if: (a) sleep health outcomes were not assessed; (b) group differences in sleep variables were not reported between individuals with BN and healthy controls; (c) the clinical sample included multiple eating disorder diagnoses and BN‐specific results were not presented separately; (d) no healthy control group was included; (e) participants were described as having BN symptoms or disordered eating without a formal BN diagnosis; (f) studies were available only as conference proceedings or published abstracts.

Article Selection Process

The search was conducted on October 6, 2025. Results were imported into Covidence (Covidence Systematic Review Software n.d.); duplicate records were identified and removed by Covidence and through a visual scan. Two raters (SK, MH) independently screened titles, abstracts, and full‐text articles against the predefined eligibility criteria using Covidence. Meetings were held to discuss and resolve discrepancies.

A total of 1556 references were identified across the three databases (i.e., Medline, PsycINFO, and Scopus). After de‐duplication, 1233 unique references remained. Initial screening excluded 1194 records. Following a full‐text assessment of the remaining 39 articles, an additional 28 were removed. The final sample included 11 articles. Data Extraction

Once the final sample of articles was identified, data were recorded independently on a researcher‐constructed data extraction table. Extracted data included study aims, sample size, participant characteristics (i.e., age, sex/gender, ethnicity), study design, measures (sleep and BN), principal results, and covariates. Effect sizes (Cohen's d) were calculated and reported for all variables where sufficient data were available and where calculation was appropriate (e.g., excluding circular time variables such as sleep timing and variables based on adjusted estimates). To enhance interpretability, effect sizes for objective sleep variables were grouped into standardised domains (sleep continuity, sleep architecture, REM sleep, and microstructure). For each study, ranges of effect sizes are presented in the main table, while full variable‐level effect sizes are reported in Table S1. The data extracted were verified by a second researcher for accuracy.

Quality Assessment

To assess risk of bias, the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute's Quality Assessment Tool for Observational Cohort and Cross‐Sectional Studies (https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health‐topics/study‐quality‐assessment‐tools) was used. The tool facilitates the assessment of 14 criteria covering the psychometric properties, sampling procedure, study design, and inclusion of covariates, that are rated as Yes, No, Not Applicable, or Not Reported. Each article is given an overall evaluation of good, fair, or poor. These ratings are reported in Table 1; brief reasons for each rating are provided in the Quality Appraisal column. Two researchers independently conducted the quality assessment for each article and consulted to reach a global evaluation.

Summary of the Risk of Bias Appraisals

Two of the 11 studies (18%) included in this review were rated overall as having good methodological quality and 9 were rated as fair (82%). In terms of strengths, all studies had clear research questions (100%), most had recruited their participants from the same or similar populations (91%), and most or all had clearly defined, valid, reliable exposure (100%) and outcome (91%) measures. Additionally, for all studies (100%), the exposure (BN diagnosis) was measured before the outcome (sleep).

The study populations were clearly defined (i.e., authors provided sufficient detail about the study population that would allow for study replication) by only two studies (18%). Additionally, the eligibility rate for participation was reported by only two studies (18%). Most studies (10 out of 11) did not provide a sample justification or a priori or post hoc power description (91%). Unfortunately, none of the studies examined different levels of exposure (i.e., levels of severity or duration) in relation to sleep variables. Furthermore, none of the studies assessed the exposure (i.e., BN diagnosis) more than once over time as all were cross‐sectional designs. Outcome assessors were blinded to the exposure status of participants in only two of 11 studies (18%). Finally, the majority of studies (73%) did not include relevant covariables (e.g., depression and anxiety symptoms, body mass index), while three studies controlled for age.

Results

Eleven studies reporting differences in sleep health variables between individuals with BN and comparison groups were included in the review. Results are first categorised by the type of sleep measurement reported in each study: 1. Objective (n = 9), 2. Subjective (n = 3); Notably, one of the 11 studies included both subjective and objective measures; its findings are therefore reported in the corresponding sections for each measurement modality. Results are further organised based on whether the clinical sample has comorbid mood disorders (i.e., depression or bipolar disorder). The extracted data (study aims, design and sample, measures, principal results, and covariates) from the included studies are documented in Table 1.

Objective Sleep (n = 9)

Most studies comparing sleep in individuals with BN to healthy controls used objective methods, particularly PSG. For clarity, findings are described across key sleep domains including sleep continuity, sleep architecture, REM measures, and, where applicable, microstructure and sleep timing. Three studies examined samples in which comorbid mood disorders were either explicitly excluded (Byrne et al. 1990; Waller et al. 1989) or not reported (Della Marca et al. 2004).

In the study by Byrne et al. (1990), nine women with BN were compared to a control group of 10 women without EDs, with no participants reporting symptoms of major depression. Recordings over two nights (following one adaptation night) of PSG indicated that although groups did not differ in total sleep time or efficiency, individuals with BN showed lower REM density and less REM in the second third of the night compared with controls, indicating an absence of depression‐like REM ‘markers’.

Della Marca et al. (2004) compared two nights of PSG recording in six pre‐day‐hospital‐treatment patients with BN to six age‐matched controls. When examining sleep macrostructure, researchers reported that the REM percentage was lower in individuals with BN than controls, but that they did not differ on other sleep stage percentages or sleep efficiency. On sleep microstructure, authors reported a shorter phase B mean length in BN than controls, suggesting greater NREM sleep instability and lower sleep quality.

In another study of patients at an eating‐disorder clinic who were diagnosed with BN but no major depression, Waller et al. (1989) compared the EEG recordings of 12 females to 20 age‐comparable healthy controls. Over two nights of PSG recording, when controlling for age, REM latency was shorter in the BN group than controls, but only when high REM latency (> 130 min) subjects were excluded and age was controlled for. Additionally, there were non‐significant differences in REM percentage or REM density between patients with BN and healthy controls. Aside from REM latency, the BN and control groups did not differ on other reported sleep variables including REM percentage, REM density, sleep latency, efficiency or arousal measures. The authors concluded that sleep architecture in individuals with BN was largely comparable to that of controls, with only limited evidence of shorter REM latency.

Seven studies reported on sleep using objective measures in samples with BN, among which some participants had comorbid mood disorders. Walsh et al. (1985) examined sleep using PSG over two nights in 14 patients with BN and compared several sleep variables to a control group of 14 healthy women. Six of the 14 patients with BN were in an episode of major depression, two had intermittent depression, and one had cyclothmic depression at the time of the study. There were no differences between the two groups on any sleep variables, suggesting that sleep in individuals with BN did not differ from that of controls or resemble the sleep disturbances typically observed in major depressive disorder.

Another study, conducted by Hudson et al. (1987), compared 11 female hospital outpatients with BN (five participants had major depression and one had bipolar disorder) to 20 healthy controls over two nights of PSG recording. Compared to the control group, individuals with BN showed no significant differences on sleep continuity (i.e., sleep latency, duration, and efficiency), sleep architecture (i.e., stage percentages) or REM sleep (i.e., REM latency and intensity). However, authors reported a trend towards an increased proportion of REM sleep containing eye movements (i.e., REM density) in the first REM period. There were also no differences in REM latency between subjects with or without concurrent major depression.

Lauer et al. (1988) compared 10 female inpatients with BN (five of whom also had major depression) to 10 age‐ and gender‐matched healthy controls in one night of PSG recording. Compared to controls, those with BN had a lower sleep efficiency and increased nocturnal awakenings. Sleep architecture and REM sleep measures did not differ between groups. When comparing depressed to nondepressed patients with BN, those who were depressed had significantly fewer early morning awakenings. The authors concluded that sleep EEG patterns in patients with eating disorders were largely comparable to those of healthy controls and did not exhibit depression‐like abnormalities.

Levy et al. (1987) sought to describe the REM latency, REM density, and delta sleep in nine female inpatients with BN to a control group of healthy volunteers. Within the clinical group, three had minor depression and two had major depression. Measured using PSG over two consecutive nights, those with BN did not differ from controls on REM sleep variables. There were no differences in REM latency between individuals with or without major depression. Overall, the findings suggested that REM sleep patterns in BN were similar to those of controls and did not resemble those typically observed in depression.

Another study conducted by Levy et al. (1988) compared nine hospitalised women with BN, two of whom had major depression and two had minor depression, to a control group comprising 10 healthy women. When measured over two nights of PSG, the sleep in the clinical group was very similar to sleep in the control group on any sleep variable, including sleep continuity, sleep stages, and REM measures. Authors conclude that sleep architecture in individuals with BN was largely indistinguishable from that of healthy controls.

A study by Latzer et al. (1999) used both subjective and objective measures (subjective results are presented in the section below). In this study, researchers compared sleep patterns of 25 female patients with BN at an eating disorder clinic (10 of whom had comorbid mood and personality disorders) in a medical centre to 21 controls (Latzer et al. 1999). Individuals in the control group were characterised as having no psychiatric history. Using actigraphy, when controlling for age, marital status, children, and permanent work status, the BN group had significantly later sleep onset and wake‐up times relative to controls. No significant differences were found for other sleep dimensions (see Table 1).

Overall, findings from studies using objective measures indicate some differences when comparing sleep variables between people with BN to healthy controls, although results were inconsistent. Across studies, several objective sleep variables showed moderate to large effect sizes; however, these effects were inconsistent across studies and were often not statistically significant—likely reflecting small sample sizes and limited power. Among the three PSG studies in which comorbid mood disorders were either explicitly excluded or not reported, there was some evidence of reduced REM latency and REM percentage and one study also reported increased sleep instability based on CAP microstructure. However, sleep duration and efficiency were similar to controls. In studies that did include participants with comorbid mood disorders, there was some evidence of reduced sleep efficiency and altered REM parameters, but many did not report group differences on sleep continuity, architecture or REM sleep; however, some reported moderate to large effect sizes. The presence of mood disorders was examined in several studies but was not consistently associated with sleep abnormalities. Actigraphy findings showed that the BN group had significantly later sleep onset and wake‐up times, even after controlling for demographic factors. However, there were no significant differences in other objective sleep variables, such as sleep duration, efficiency, or latency.

Subjective Sleep Measures (n = 3)

Three studies used subjective sleep measures—only one of which (i.e., Latzer et al. 1999) provided details about the presence of comorbid disorders.

In the first study using a subjective sleep measure, Tu et al. (2019) compared scores of the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), a well‐validated sleep questionnaire (Buysse et al. 1989), of 41 individuals with BN at a psychiatric outpatient clinic to 31 healthy controls. Compared to controls, those with BN reported a higher level of overall sleep disturbance, worse sleep quality, more use of sleep medications, and more daytime dysfunction. However, there were no differences between groups for sleep latency, duration, efficiency, or disturbance.

In another study, Y. R. Kim et al. (2022) compared 100 females with BN from an outpatient ED hospital clinic to a control group comprising 364 normal‐weight female students. Using an in‐house interview questionnaire, results indicated that sleep duration did not differ between individuals with BN and the control group.

Latzer et al. (1999) compared sleep patterns and quality of 25 female patients with BN at an eating disorder clinic (10 of whom had comorbid mood disorders) in a medical centre to 21 controls using the MSQ. Results showed that individuals with BN reported significantly more sleep disturbances (i.e., difficulty falling asleep, early morning awakening with difficulty falling back to sleep, headaches, and restless sleep) and more daytime sleepiness than the control group.

To summarise findings from the subjective studies, two of the three studies measuring sleep subjectively found that individuals with BN reported poorer overall sleep quality and greater daytime dysfunction relative to controls, with moderate to large effect sizes (|d| = 0.60–1.43). The specific sleep complaints (e.g., sleep latency and efficiency) varied by study; by contrast, no study reported a significant difference in self‐reported sleep duration. However, because mood disorders were assessed inconsistently across studies, it is difficult to assess the extent to which mood pathology contributes to the reported subjective sleep disturbances in BN.

Collectively, the studies using both subjective and objective sleep measures indicated that individuals with BN self‐reported more sleep disturbances, including difficulty falling asleep, early morning awakenings, headaches, restless sleep, and increased daytime sleepiness, compared to healthy controls. These findings corroborate other findings that sleep disturbances tend to be primarily subjective in nature and are not always captured objectively. Taken together, findings suggest that while objective sleep measures often do not capture consistent disturbances in BN, subjective reports suggest meaningful sleep complaints, including poor quality, later sleep onset and wake‐up times, and daytime dysfunction—indicating that perceived sleep disturbance may play a clinically relevant role even when objective sleep architecture appears intact.

Discussion

This systematic review synthesised and appraised the available research examining differences in sleep health variables in individuals with BN compared with healthy controls. Across the 11 included studies, findings were heterogeneous. Some studies reported greater subjective sleep disturbance or differences in specific objective sleep parameters among individuals with BN, whereas others found few or no differences relative to controls. Methodological quality varied, with most studies rated as fair, and only two as good. The literature was further characterised by small sample sizes, cross‐sectional designs, inconsistent assessment of psychiatric comorbidity, and considerable variability in sleep measures, all of which limit the strength of conclusions that can be drawn.

The present review also extends the only previous review focused specifically on BN and sleep (Royant‐Parola and Pelicier 1990). That narrative review was based primarily on the limited PSG literature available at the time and concluded that, outside of comorbid depression, there was little evidence for a specific sleep profile in individuals with BN. The current review, however, used systematic methodology, incorporated studies published over the subsequent three decades, included subjective, actigraphic and PSG measures, and formally evaluated methodological quality. Although some studies reported greater subjective sleep disturbance or alterations in specific objective sleep variables, findings were heterogeneous and often based on small samples. Accordingly, the present review provides an updated synthesis of the literature while highlighting the substantial methodological limitations that continue to constrain conclusions regarding sleep health in individuals with BN.

One pattern observed across the literature was a discrepancy between subjective and objective sleep measures. Some studies reported that individuals with BN reported poorer sleep quality, difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep, early morning awakenings, daytime sleepiness, and greater use of sleep medication (Latzer et al. 1999; Tu et al. 2019). In contrast, objective studies typically found comparable total sleep time and efficiency between BN and control groups, despite some moderate to large but inconsistent effects across sleep variables. Some studies, however, reported differences in specific parameters such as shorter REM latency, lower sleep efficiency, or later sleep onset and wake‐up times (Byrne et al. 1990; Della Marca et al. 2004; Latzer et al. 1999; Lauer et al. 1988), although these findings were not consistently observed. Taken together, the pattern of findings suggests that while some sleep variables in BN may show clinically meaningful differences in magnitude, these effects are heterogeneous and not consistently detected across studies.

These inconsistencies mirror those observed in insomnia research, where subjective sleep complaints often exceed what is objectively recorded. The study by Latzer et al. (1999) highlights this pattern: participants with BN self‐reported significant sleep difficulties, yet actigraphy revealed few objective abnormalities. This ‘sleep‐state misperception’ phenomenon, also seen in mood disorders (Kawai et al. 2022; Ma et al. 2021), may heighten anxiety about sleep and maintain sleep difficulties even when objective sleep parameters seem to be normal.

One of the aims of this review was to explore whether comorbid mood disorders contribute to observed differences in sleep health variables in individuals with BN. However, diagnostic comorbidity was handled inconsistently across studies: some explicitly excluded participants with mood disorders, while others included some participants with comorbid mood disorders (and indicated how many participants met criteria for each comorbid disorder), while several did not report comorbidity status at all. Although multiple studies assessed depressive‐symptom severity using questionnaires, none statistically controlled for mood symptoms in group comparisons. Among studies that included participants with comorbid mood disorders, some reported alterations in sleep timing or sleep efficiency (Latzer et al. 1999; Lauer et al. 1988), whereas studies that explicitly excluded mood disorders or did not report comorbidity status still identified alterations in REM parameters and significant subjective disturbances (Byrne et al. 1990; Della Marca et al. 2004; Tu et al. 2019). Taken together, these findings suggest that while comorbid mood pathology may exacerbate certain aspects of sleep disturbance, the current literature does not permit a clear test of its specific contribution, and sleep disturbances in BN cannot be assumed to be solely attributable to co‐occurring mood disorders.

When considered in the broader eating‐disorder literature, similar sleep patterns have been observed across diagnoses but with notable distinctions. Individuals with AN show poorer sleep quality, fragmentation, and reduced slow‐wave sleep—largely attributed to malnutrition (Sauchelli et al. 2016)—while those with BED often experience poor sleep quality and obstructive sleep apnoea which are linked to higher body weight (Kenny et al. 2018; Vardar et al. 2004). Some findings suggest the possibility of later sleep timing and subjective sleep disturbance, which may reflect a distinct pattern shaped primarily by behavioural and emotional factors, such as evening binge‐purge cycles and affective dysregulation, rather than by nutritional or weight‐related mechanisms. However, the evidence remains limited and inconsistent.

Although objective sleep duration and efficiency were largely preserved across studies, some findings suggest that specific aspects of sleep architecture, stability, and timing may still be relevant to BN. In the present review, objective studies identified alterations in REM parameters, NREM instability, or sleep timing. Such changes may have implications for emotional and behavioural regulation. For example, REM sleep supports emotional processing and is frequently altered in mood disorders (Riemann et al. 2025), while unstable or fragmented sleep has been linked to poorer emotional regulation and greater impulsive eating behaviours (Manasse et al. 2022). Circadian misalignment, including later sleep timing, may further increase vulnerability to evening and nighttime binge‐purge behaviours by shifting periods of greater appetite and reduced self‐control later into the day (Linnaranta et al. 2020). Taken together, even subtle alterations in sleep quality, stability, and timing may influence emotion regulation and impulse control—processes central to the development and maintenance of BN (Adan et al. 2012; Romo‐Nava et al. 2022).

Building on these potential mechanisms, one possibility is that sleep disturbances and BN symptoms may influence one another over time. Poor sleep can intensify BN symptoms, including binging and purging (K. R. Kim et al. 2010; Lombardo et al. 2015), while BN behaviours—such as evening binge episodes, compensatory exercise, or purging—can in turn disrupt sleep onset and continuity through heightened arousal, metabolic changes, and circadian misalignment (S. Kim and Lee 2020; Latzer and Tzischinsky 2012). Importantly, emerging theoretical and empirical work increasingly conceptualises binge eating as a time‐of‐day‐dependent or circadian‐related phenomenon. Specifically, binge‐eating episodes, cravings, and food intake tend to cluster later in the day and may be linked to delayed circadian phase or eveningness, as well as abnormalities in daily hormonal and activity rhythms (i.e., shifts or irregularities in the day‐night pattern of hormones and activity; Romo‐Nava et al. 2022). From this perspective, sleep and circadian disruptions may not merely be consequences of binge‐purge behaviours, but also contribute to their timing, frequency, and maintenance. Together, these factors provide a theoretical framework that may help explain associations between sleep disturbance and BN symptoms observed in some studies.

Methodologically, the quality of available evidence remains limited. Most studies had small samples, lacked power analyses, and used cross‐sectional designs. Few accounted for confounders such as comorbid psychiatric diagnoses or medication use, and many relied on outdated diagnostic criteria. Additionally, PSG studies were typically limited to one recording night, which may not capture habitual sleep patterns. Despite these weaknesses, most studies used validated measures and clearly defined research questions, underscoring the need for more rigorous and comprehensive approaches. Several studies observed moderate to large effect sizes for sleep variables; however, these effects were not consistently statistically significant and should be interpreted cautiously, particularly given the small sample sizes.

Beyond these methodological limitations, interpretation of findings is further complicated by the heterogeneity of sleep variables examined across studies as well as the nature of the samples included. Most studies assessed numerous sleep parameters—such as duration, latency, efficiency, and REM measures—with only a subset showing significant differences between groups. This variability makes it difficult to determine whether BN is associated with specific or global aspects of sleep disturbance. Moreover, all studies relied on treatment‐seeking clinical samples from hospital‐based inpatient or outpatient settings, which may overrepresent individuals with more severe ED psychopathology, a higher prevalence of comorbidities, and possible medical complications. Consequently, the findings may not generalise to community populations or those with less severe illness. Future research would benefit from using community‐based samples and focussing on a consistent set of sleep health dimensions, ideally guided by theoretical frameworks such as Buysse's (2014) SATED model, to clarify the scope and nature of sleep disturbances in BN.

A strength of the present review is the formal assessment of methodological quality, which has been largely absent from previous reviews of sleep and eating disorders. Earlier narrative syntheses (e.g., Allison et al. 2016; Cinosi et al. 2011; Cooper et al. 2020; Lauer and Krieg 2004) did not systematically evaluate study‐level bias, and even the recent ED‐wide meta‐analysis by Degasperi et al. (2024) relied on a brief global rating. In contrast, the present review used a validated risk‐of‐bias tool and integrated these appraisals into the interpretation of findings. This approach allowed us to identify key methodological issues—such as single‐night PSG protocols, inconsistent BN case definitions, and limited reporting of comorbidity—that likely contributed to heterogeneity across studies. By providing a transparent appraisal of study quality, this review offers a clearer foundation for interpreting existing findings and for guiding future research design.

Limitations of the literature include small and predominantly female samples, heterogeneous measures, a dearth of studies using subjective measures, and a lack of longitudinal or treatment studies. Future studies should integrate both subjective and objective measures (e.g., actigraphy over multiple nights), and incorporate relevant covariates such as caffeine use, napping, or medication (i.e., stimulant or psychotropic), as suggested by Irish et al. (2024) in their published forum on recommendations and best practices for the integration of sleep research into eating disorders research. Research would also benefit from examining mediators—such as mood symptoms, emotion regulation, and circadian misalignment—to clarify the mechanisms linking BN features with sleep disturbance. Longitudinal studies would also help clarify whether sleep disturbance plays a role in the development of BN, occurs alongside it, and/or lingers after recovery.

The present review is also limited by the search cutoff date (October 2025). A relevant study published shortly thereafter would have met inclusion criteria (Özcan and Çevreli 2025). This study, conducted in a large sample of female university students, reported elevated insomnia symptoms in individuals with self‐reported physician diagnoses of BN compared to controls, providing additional evidence that subjective sleep complaints may be present in some individuals with BN. Although this post‐search publication was not included in the formal synthesis, its findings are broadly consistent with studies suggesting elevated subjective sleep complaints among some individuals with BN.

Conclusion

Taken together, the available literature provides preliminary evidence that some individuals with BN may experience subjective sleep disturbances and alterations in specific sleep parameters. However, findings are inconsistent and limited by small samples, methodological heterogeneity, and the predominance of cross‐sectional designs. Additionally, well‐powered studies that use standardised and multidimensional assessments of sleep are needed before firm conclusions can be drawn regarding the nature, extent, and clinical significance of sleep disturbances in individuals with BN.
