A transdiagnostic mentalization-based parenting intervention versus care as usual for parents with mental disorders in adult mental health services in Denmark: protocol for a randomized clinical trial
Authors: Emilie Hestbaek, Mette Skovgaard Væver, Sophie Juul, Per Sørensen, Michelle Sleed, Sebastian Simonsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMental disorders can impact parenting negatively, thus placing their children at risk of adversity. The current service support for parents in adult mental health (AMHS) service is limited. The objective of this randomized clinical trial will be to evaluate the effects of a transdiagnostic mentalization-based parenting program (Lighthouse MBT Parenting Program) versus care as usual (CAU) for parents with mental disorders in AMHS in Denmark.MethodsThis trial is designed as an investigator-initiated single-center open-label randomized clinical superiority trial of the Lighthouse MBT Parenting Program compared to CAU for parents with mental disorders. Participants will be recruited from Danish outpatient mental health clinics by the local service providers and will be included if they have a child 0–17 years of age, whom they are in regular contact with. We plan to randomize 170 parents with a 1:1 allocation ratio stratified by parental gender and baseline parenting stress score. The primary outcome will be level of parenting stress (Parenting Stress Index version 4 - Short Form). Secondary outcomes will be child psychological problems and functioning (Strength and Difficulties Questionnaire – Extended version), parental quality of life (brief abbreviated version of the World Health Organization Questionnaire of Life), family functioning (McMaster Family Assessment Device – General Functioning), exposure to child adversity in offspring (Adverse Child Experiences Questionnaire), parenting competence (Parenting Sense of Competence), parental psychiatric symptom severity (Brief Symptom Inventory), parental health-related quality of life and functioning (European Quality of Life – 5 Dimensions – 3 Levels), and parental use of drugs and alcohol (Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test and the Drug Use Disorders Identification Test – Extended). The primary and secondary outcomes will be assessed before randomization and at 6, 12, and 24 months after randomization. The primary time-point of assessment will be 6 months after randomization for all randomized participants. Exploratory outcomes include key theoretical concepts, including parental mentalizing (Parental Reflective Functioning Questionnaire), mind-mindedness (representational Mind-Mindedness), and epistemic trust (Epistemic Trust, Mistrust, and Credulity Questionnaire). Exploratory outcomes will be assessed at baseline and at 6 months follow-up. Adverse events will be systematically monitored throughout the trial period using the e-journal system.DiscussionThis trial will provide evidence of the effects of a transdiagnostic mentalization-based parenting intervention (Lighthouse MBT Parenting Program) compared to care as usual for parents with mental disorders in adult mental health services in Denmark.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov: NCT06315114. Background and rationale

Epidemiological studies estimate that two in five Danish children have a parent with a mental disorder [1], many of whom receive psychiatric treatment. In adult mental health service (AMHS), it has been estimated that globally about 25% of the patients are parents [2–4]. Mental disorders vary in severity, prognosis, and duration, but in most cases, they are enduring and recurrent. A Danish survey found that for the majority, parental mental disorders persisted for more than five years [5]. Thus, growing up with a parent with a mental disorder is a prevalent and often long-lasting life circumstance for many children worldwide. Children of parents with mental disorders are at high risk of various immediate and long-term adverse outcomes, such as behavioral and emotional problems and psychiatric disorders [6–13]. This reflects an intergenerational transmission of adversity, which contributes to a significant burden on mental health and social services, leading to substantial societal and economic costs [14]. Several barriers hinder access to care for these families, including stressful life circumstances, stigma, low motivation, lack of trust in the system, and misconceptions that children are unaffected by parental mental health problems. Adult mental health service presents a key opportunity for prevention by addressing these barriers. Routine screening allows for systematic identification of parents affected by mental disorders, and the established therapeutic relationship can foster trust and motivation for treatment. Additionally,AMHS can provide a setting for integrating targeted, specialized support within existing psychiatric care. Despite these advantages, current support for parents with mental disorders in AMHS often remains inadequate and not evidence-based [2, 15, 16]. Evidence-based interventions in the context of AMHS are needed to mitigate the adverse impact of parental mental disorders on children.

Preventive interventions for parents with mental disorders in adult mental health service

Preventive interventions for parents with mental disorders holds significant, but often untapped potential to break the cycle of intergenerational adversity. Childhood and adolescence are critical developmental periods, offering a “window of opportunity,” to prevent adversity and promote resilience. Research shows that early childhood interventions yield the highest economic and social returns compared to those delivered later in life [17]. Thus, early interventions have the greatest potential to mitigate adversity and create lasting positive impact on individuals, families, and society. Numerous studies have evaluated the effectiveness of preventive interventions for this population. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have reported improvements in children’s mental health [18–20]. However, these reviews have focused solely on children’s risk of developing a mental disorder [18–20], and have not examined other crucial aspects, such as quality of life, childhood maltreatment and child developmental outcomes. To address this gap, we are currently conducting a comprehensive systematic review assessing the effects on a broader range of offspring outcomes [21]. While several interventions have shown positive effects [18–20], many of them are unsuitable for implementation in AMHS. A key limitation of existing programs is their disorder-specific focus (e.g. targeting parents with depression only), which makes them costly and impractical for implementation inAMHS. A transdiagnostic approach that target shared risk factors, such as impairments in parental mentalizing, may be more scalable and cost-effective [22]. Additionally, many interventions require participation of both parents and children, which can deter some parents from seeking support [15]. Research suggest that parent-only interventions are just as effective, making them a good alternative in AMHS [18]. In sum, transdiagnostic parenting group interventions may be more feasible, cost-effective, and effective in AMHS.

Mentalizing framework to understand and prevent intergenerational adversity

Adversity is often transmitted early in life through a complex interplay of biological factors (e.g., genetic heritability) and environmental influences (e.g., parenting). Theory and research underscore the critical role of a parent’s capacity to provide consistent, sensitive care in fostering healthy child development and well-being. However, parental mental health problems can negatively impact parenting, thereby affecting the child [23, 24]. One potential transdiagnostic mechanism implicated in the intergenerational transmission of adversity is the parent’s capacity to mentalize [25, 26]. Mentalizing refers to the ability to imagine and understand oneself and others in terms of mental states, such as feelings and thoughts [27, 28]. This capacity is key for relational functioning, particularly within the parent-child relationship. Parental mentalizing involves a reflective process of linking observed behaviors to underlying mental states in a meaningful and accurate manner, enabling the parent to better understand and respond to the child’s needs [27]. Severe and longstanding mental health problems can render parents more vulnerable to frequent, intensive, and prolonged periods of impaired mentalizing, which may result in insensitive or even harmful parenting practices. A growing evidence base have linked impaired mentalizing to negative outcomes, including elevated parenting stress and child mental health problems [29, 30, 30–35]. Conversely, well-functioning parental mentalizing is associated with positive outcomes, such as sensitive caregiving and secure child attachment [30, 31, 33–36]. Thus, well-functioning mentalizing may act as a protective factor, while impaired mentalizing may serve as a risk factor for parents and children. Importantly, the capacity to mentalize is not fixed, but rather plastic. Metaphorically speaking, mentalizing can be viewed as a muscle that can be strengthened and improved through targeted interventions [37]. This makes it a promising therapeutic target for parents with mental disorders. Enhancing mentalizing through preventive interventions could address instances of insensitive parenting, which may stem from impairments in this ability. Mentalization-Based Therapy (MBT) is an evidence-based intervention, rooted in attachment theory and developmental psychology, that has been associated with improvements across various parent populations [31, 38–41]. A recent systematic review showed positive effects of MBT in improving parental mentalizing, sensitivity, and child developmental outcomes, and in reducing parenting stress, child behavioral problems, and parental psychiatric symptoms [38]. Among these interventions is the Lighthouse MBT Parenting Program [39], showing promising results.

The Lighthouse MBT Parenting Program

The Lighthouse MBT Parenting Program (LPP) is a structured manualized mentalization-based parenting program [39], which was originally developed for high-risk parents who are often difficult to engage and typically unresponsive to standard universal parenting programs. The LPP program combines psychotherapy and psychoeducation with an explicit focus on working directly with mentalizing and attachment to promote sensitive caregiving, thereby supporting healthy child development. The program aims to address factors associated with child adversity by enhancing parental mentalizing, thereby promoting sensitive caregiving. The program was originally developed as a 20-week program combining parenting groups with individual therapy, often with participation of the child [39]. Since then, several adaptations of the program have been made, including a brief condensed 12-week parenting group-only version. The 12-week adaptation is less resource intensive and designed for implementation and scalability in resource limited settings such as public mental health services like AMHS.

Preliminary findings from feasibility studies and pilot evaluations of the program have shown positive outcomes [42]. A pilot evaluation of the 20-week LPP program in an English sample of 16 high-risk parents showed that LPP was associated with improvements in parental self-efficacy, and in reducing parenting stress [39]. Another pilot study examined an adapted 5-week version of the LPP in five mothers with different mental disorders in a psychiatric in-patient mother-child unity in Germany. The adapted 5-week program included three individual sessions, four group sessions, and one session with a social worker [42]. The results showed reduced parental stress and increased parental sensitivity and self-efficacy [42]. A pre-poststudy investigating the 12-week version of the LPP in a community mental health sample of 101 parents in Lithuania showed improvements in parental mentalization, parental adjustment, and family functioning, as well as decreases in coercive parenting practices [43] -with most of these effects still present at 3 months follow-up [44]. The improvements in several of the outcomes (i.e., parental stress and child behavior problems) were mediated by improvements in self-focused mentalizing c [44]. However, to date, there are no published results from randomized clinical trials of the 12- week version of LPP for parents with mental disorders

Based on these studies showing preliminary positive outcomes and high user acceptability, we conducted a feasibility study of the LPP with seven parents with various mental disorders in the context of AMHS[45]. For this purpose, we evaluated the 12-week parenting-group-based version of the program, as it is less resource-intensive, and more scalable within AMHS, while prior evidence also suggest that parent-only interventions can be equally effective as those involving children- and may even yield higher acceptability among parents. The findings from our feasibility study of the program showed good feasibility and acceptability with low drop-out rate and high attendance, and parents reported enhanced awareness of mental states, increased self-compassion and greater parental confidence [45]. Despite these encouraging findings, the evidence base remains limited. While our feasibility study, along with others, suggested that the program is both acceptable and potentially effective, its effects still need to be tested in randomized clinical trials using rigorous methodology. The aim of this trial therefore to evaluate the effects of the LPP to parents with mental disorders in the context of AMHS. For this specific trial, a change model was formulated proposing that the LPP leads to improved parental mentalizing, which in turn decreases parental stress and increases parental sensitivity, which in the long run is hypothesized to mitigates child psychosocial difficulties [39] (see Fig. 1). Parental mentalizing and parenting stress have been shown to be strongly correlated in both clinical and non-clinical parents [46–49]. Moreover, both parental mentalizing and parenting stress have been shown to be related to child internalizing and externalizing symptoms and social-emotional competencies [50, 51].

Objectives

The primary objective of this randomized clinical trial will be to evaluate the effects of a transdiagnostic mentalization-based parenting intervention (Lighthouse MBT Parenting Program) compared with care as usual (CAU) for parents with a mental disorder in AMHS.

Methods/design

This trial protocol has been developed in accordance with the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Clinical Interventions Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines [52]. The trial was registered prospectively at ClinicalTrials.gov (ID: NCT06315114). We will follow the applicable data protection regulations, including the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and the relevant national data protection laws in all steps of the trials. This trial has been approved by the regional data protection agency (ID: P-2023–15049) and the Research Ethics Committee of the Capital Region of Denmark (H-23064491).

Trial design

The trial is designed as an investigator-initiated, single-center, open-label, parallel group randomized clinical trial testing for superiority of a transdiagnostic mentalization-based parenting intervention (Lighthouse MBT Parenting Program) versus CAU in 170 parents with mental disorders. The groups will be allocated in a ratio of 1:1. The Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) flow chart for the trial is shown in Fig. 2 [53, 54] and will be filled in during and at the end of the trial. The SPIRIT participant timeline is shown in Fig. 3. In addition, we are currently working on a protocol for a mixed-method process evaluation as part of this trial evaluating feasibility, acceptability, fidelity, and implementation, in line with the Medical Research Councils (MRC) recommendation for research on complex interventions [55].

Setting and trial site

The trial will be conducted at Stolpegaard Psychotherapy Centre, AMHS, Gentofte, Capital Region of Denmark. Stolpegaard is a public outpatient mental health center consisting of three clinics specialized in providing psychotherapeutic treatment for adults with non-psychotic mental disorders: Clinic for anxiety disorders and obsessive-compulsive disorder, clinic for personality disorders and post-traumatic stress disorder, and clinic for patients with a dual diagnosis i.e., targeting individuals with a non-psychotic mental disorder, and a substance use disorder. Furthermore, we will have a trial steering committee consisting of all members of the research team, the head of the center, and the head of each clinic. The steering committee will be involved in overseeing recruitment, and in writing up the outcome manuscripts.

Eligibility criteria

Patients will be included in the trial if they comply with the eligibility criteria. There are inclusion and exclusion criteria as a part of the procedure for clinical intake at the trial site, and criteria specific to this trial. Inclusion criteria exclusive to the outpatient clinic are as follows: aged 18–65 years, seeking treatment for one of the following non-psychotic disorders according to International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10): obsessive-compulsive disorder and anxiety disorders (F40-42), posttraumatic stress disorder (F43), personality disorders (F60-61), or one of the previous disorders and a substance use disorder (F10-19) (i.e., dual diagnosis disorder). Inclusion criteria exclusive to the trial are as follows: parent of at least one child aged 0–17 years at baseline, fluent i.e., sufficient Danish language skills, parent is living with child or is in regular contact (min 5 days monthly), parent has provided written informed consent. Exclusion criteria exclusive to the outpatient clinic includes the following: possibility of learning disability (intelligence quotient (IQ) <75), a diagnosis of schizotypal personality disorder or antisocial personality disorder, presence of a comorbid psychiatric disorder that requires specialist treatment elsewhere, concurrent psychotherapeutic treatment outside the clinic. Exclusion criteria exclusive to the trial include: acute suicidal risk or state of crises, lack of informed consent, acute child endangerment, participation in another parenting focused intervention simultaneously. Neither the sites nor the individuals involved in delivering the intervention have to meet any criteria. For an overview of criteria for inclusion and exclusion, see Table 1.

Interventions

Experimental intervention: Lighthouse MBT Parenting Program

In this trial, we used the 12-week parenting group-only version of the LPP program, which consists of 12 weekly parenting group sessions (2 hours each) and an initial individual session (1 hour) before the group begins. At the individual session, a collaborative case formulation is created, which is a personalized document highlighting mentalizing difficulties and relational patterns that may affect the parent-child relationship and predicts their impact in the group setting. Group sessions include psychoeducation (e.g., mentalizing and attachment) and reflective activities where parents discuss their parenting experiences and work on attachment-related themes. Visual and metaphorical materials are used to help parents grasp key concepts. The original manual has been adapted based on results and experiences from participating parents as part of our feasibility study and in collaboration with the developer of the program to address the specific needs of parents with mental disorders. Groups are limited to eight parents and are closed to foster trust, consistency, and interpersonal bonds. Each group is facilitated by two therapists, all of whom undergo five days of basic LPP training, and two days of advanced LPP training. In addition, therapist attend supervision regularly (approximately every 3rd to 4th week by the developer of the intervention) throughout the trial. Fidelity to the manual will be assessed as part of the process evaluation.

Care as usual: Invitation to attend a next of kin session

Care as usual in the Capital Region of Denmark consists of an invitation to take part in a next-of-kin session, referred to as “Familiesamtale” (‘family conversation’), in accordance with the clinical guidelines of the Mental Health Services in the Capital Region. The next of kin session is not therapeutic nature but rather psychoeducational and supportive, aimed at providing information and creating a space for communication between the affected parent and their child, and in some cases also other relatives. Prior to the next-of-kin session, a preparatory session only involving the affected parent. Both sessions last about one hour and are facilitated by therapists from the parent’s clinic. The overall purpose is to address questions children may have and allow parents to share relevant information concerning the mental disorder in a supportive environment. Care as usual will be offered as a voluntary intervention reflecting the current practice [2].

Concomitant interventions

Both interventions are delivered as an add-on to the parents’ ongoing outpatient psychotherapeutic treatment in AMHS. The treatment duration, intensity, format, and order depend on the diagnosis of referral. Participants are also allowed to receive psychotropic medication throughout the trial period.

Procedures, instruments, outcomes, and timepoints for assessment

All participants will be assessed at baseline (before randomization), and at follow-up 6 (T1), 12 (T2), and 24 months (T3) after randomization, respectively. The instruments and outcome measures have been carefully selected and are based on experiences and results from our feasibility evaluation of the program [45]. Assessment at baseline and T1 includes a clinical interviews and questionnaires and will be carried out at the center by the principal investigator or trained research assistants. Exploratory outcomes will be assessed at baseline and 6-month follow-up. T2 and T3 include questionnaires only, which will be completed by parents at home. Parents and offspring can later be traced using the Danish registers, thus making it possible to examine even longer effects.

Baseline assessment at trial intake

Baseline assessments at trial intake include assessment of comorbid psychopathology, attachment, history of childhood adversity, and sociodemographic, familial, and psychiatric data.

Comorbid psychopathology in parents will be assessed with the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI), which is a structured clinical interview assessing symptoms of various psychiatric disorders. For this trial we assessed the following sections: suicidal ideation and attempts, affective disorders, anxiety disorders, eating disorders, and antisocial personality disorders. We only assessed the sections in which the patient was currently not receiving treatment for [56]. Studies have demonstrated good psychometric properties [57].

Personality Disorders will be assessed with the Standard Assessment of Personality – Abbreviated Scale (SAPAS) [58], which is a structured clinical interview for screening for personality disorders. Respondents are asked to indicate, with yes/no answers, whether each statement describes how they typically are as a person, most of the time across contexts, with higher scores indicating greater likelihood of personality disorder [58]. A cut-off score of ≥3 has been shown to yield good sensitivity and specificity in clinical populations [59], and studies have shown acceptable validity across different clinical populations [59, 60].

Childhood adversity in the parents with will be assessed with the Adverse Childhood Experiences Questionnaire (ACE). It comprises three broad categories: abuse, neglect and household dysfunction, and ten subcategories: emotional abuse, physical abuse, sexual abuse, emotional neglect, physical neglect, mother treated violently, parental separation or divorce, mental illness in household, household substance abuse, incarcerated household member. Response categories are either “yes/no” or “never, once/twice, sometimes, often, very often” [61, 62]. A count of total exposures in each of the categories are made as well as a total score for all exposures on the 10 subcategories. ACE has demonstrated good psychometric properties [62]. Parental attachment will be assessed with the Experiences in Close Relationships- Relationship Structures (ECR-RS) questionnaire [63]. The ECR-RS is composed of nine items (answered on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree) assessing attachment style in relation to one’s mother, father, and romantic partner, resulting in 27 items. The two-factor structure of the ECR-RS has been confirmed [64] and the internal consistency is adequate, with Cronbach’s alpha ranging from 0.87 to 0.92 for the avoidant attachment factor, and 0.75 to 0.91 for the anxious attachment factor [65, 66].

Sociodemographic (e.g., age and level of education), familial (e.g., living conditions), and mental health information (e.g., psychotropic medication) will be assessed with a self-developed clinical anamnestic interview.

Outcomes

Primary outcome

Parenting stress will be measured using the total score of the Parenting Stress Inventory – Short Form (PSI-4-SF) [67]. The PSI-4-SF is a 36-item questionnaire compromising three subscales: parental distress, parent-child dysfunctional interaction, and difficult child, and a defensive responding scale to assess the extent of report bias [68]. The PSI-4-SF total score is calculated as the sum of all 36 items, with higher scores indicating greater parenting stress. The primary analysis will assess change in mean PSI-4-SF total score from baseline to 6 months. The PSI-SF has demonstrated good internal consistency and test-retest reliability in parents of children aged 1 month to 17 years (.84). Parenting stress is a clinically relevant outcome as elevated levels of parenting stress are associated with impaired parenting and increased risk of adverse child outcomes.

Secondary outcomes

Child psychological problems and functioning will be assessed using the Total Difficulties score, internalizing and externalizing symptoms scores, and Impact score of the Strength and Difficulties Questionnaire – Extended version (SDQ). The SDQ is a 25-item parent-reported questionnaire comprising five subscales: emotional symptoms, conduct problems, hyperactivity, peer problems, and prosocial behavior [69]. Higher scores indicate greater psychological difficulties and functional impairments [70]. Secondary analyses will assess change in mean SDQ Total Difficulties and Impact scores from baseline to 6-month follow-up, with additional assessments at 12 and 24 months. The SDQ has demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties and the five-factor structure of the Danish version of the SDQ has been validated [71, 72]. Child psychological problems are clinically important outcomes, as this is associated with impaired daily functioning, reduced quality of life, and increased risk of persistent mental health problems across development.

Domain scores will be calculated according to the scoring manual, with higher scores indicating better quality of life. The WHOQL-BREF has demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency [73]. Parental quality of life is a clinically important outcome, as impaired quality of life is associated with greater psychiatric symptom burden, reduced daily functioning, and lower capacity to manage parenting demands.

Family functioning will be assessed using the General Functioning scale of the Family Assessment Device (FAD-GF) [74]. The FAD-GF consists of six items rated on a 4-point Likert scale, and the family functioning score will be calculated as the mean of all items, with higher scores indicating poorer general family functioning [75]. Secondary analyses will assess change in mean FAD-GF scores from baseline to 6-month follow-up, with additional assessments at 12 and 24 months. The FAD-GF6 has shown good psychometric properties [76]. Family functioning is a clinically important outcome as poorer functioning is associated with higher levels of parenting stress, impaired parent-child interactions, and heightened risk of adverse child and parental mental health outcomes.

Exposure to child adversity in offspring will be assessed using the parent-reported Center for Youth Wellness Adverse Childhood Experiences Questionnaire (CYW ACE-Q Child/Teen) [77]. CYW ACE-Q consists of 17–19 items depending on offspring age. The primary measurement variables will be the total number of endorsed ACE categories from the original ACE section and the total number of endorsed additional adversity categories from the expanded section, yielding two continuous scores [61, 77, 78]. Secondary analyses will assess change in mean ACE category counts from baseline to 6-month follow-up, with additional assessments at 12 and 24 months. The CYW ACE-Q is widely used in clinical settings, with formal psychometric validation ongoing [79]. Exposure to adverse childhood experiences is a clinically important outcome, as higher levels of early life adversity are associated with increased risk of mental health problems, impaired functioning, and poorer long-term health outcomes across development.

Secondary analyses will assess change in mean BSI GSI scores from baseline to 6-month follow-up, with additional assessments at 12 and 24 months. The BSI GSI has demonstrated good test-retest reliability (0.9) [84] and high internal consistency [85]. Psychiatric symptom severity is clinically important as higher symptom burden is associated with impaired functioning, reduced quality of life, and higher parenting stress.

Health-related quality of life and functioning in parents will be assessed using the European Quality of Life – Five Dimensions – Three Level Questionnaire (EQ-5D-3L). The primary measurement variable will be the index scores derived across the five dimensions (mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression), with higher scores indicating better health-related quality of life and functioning. Secondary analyses will assess change in the index scores from baseline to 6-month follow-up, with additional assessments at 12 and 24 months. The EQ-5D-3L has demonstrated good internal consistency [86]. Health-related quality of life and functioning are important as impairments in daily functioning and well-being are associated with increased symptom burden and poorer overall prognosis in adults with psychiatric disorders.

Exploratory outcomes

Parental mentalizing will be assessed using the Parental Reflective Functioning Questionnaire (PRFQ) [89]. The PRFQ consists of 18-items comprising three subscales: pre-mentalization (PM), certainty about mental states (CMS) and interest and curiosity (IC) [89]. Higher scores on the PM subscale indicate a less adaptive, non-mentalizing stance, while both high and low scores on the CMS and IC subscales indicate maladaptive parental mentalizing. Exploratory analyses will assess change in mean PRFQ subscale scores from baseline to 6-month follow-up. The PRFQ has demonstrated acceptable to good internal consistency, as well as good construct and convergent validity [89, 90]. Parental mentalizing is an important outcome as impairments in mentalizing have been associated with elevated parenting stress, less sensitive caregiving, and increased risk of child psychological difficulties.

Mind-mindedness will be assessed using the five-minute speech sample, which is then verbatim transcribed and coded using the representational Mind-Mindedness (MM) manual [91]. Parents are asked to freely describe their relationship with their child. The primary measurement variable will be the frequency with which mental state attributes are identified in parents’ narratives. These attributes will be derived through the standardized coding of segments. Exploratory analyses will assess change in the number of mind-related comments from baseline to 6-month follow-up. Mind-mindedness is a clinically relevant outcome as a greater tendency to attribute mental states to the child has been associated with more sensitive parenting and more adaptive parent-child interactions.

Sample size

The PSI has been used as an outcome measure in several pre-post studies with clinical samples similar to ours [39, 96–98]. Consistent with previous pre-post studies and a trial [99], we expect a minimal clinically important difference of 9 points and a standard deviation of 18, corresponding to an effect size of d =.44. To calculate an a priori sample size, we conducted a Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test (two groups). With power set at 80%, alpha = 0.5, two-tailed test, d =.44, and a 1:1 allocation, a total of 170 participants, corresponding to 85 participants in each group will be included in the trial.

Recruitment

Patients are referred to the center via a central visitation unit, where they are initially screened for eligibility before referral to Mental Health Center Stolpegaard. Yearly, about 1200 patients are referred to the trial site, all of whom will be considered for participation. We expect 25% of the patients to have children below 18 years of age, corresponding to 300 patients a year. Of these, we expect a consent rate of 50%—corresponding to 150 patients a year—and of these, we expect 50% to be included and randomized -corresponding in a total of 75 patients a year. Thus, to reach the acquired sample size, the estimated recruitment period is approximately 2 years (27 months) starting in March 2024 until June 2026. A key advantage of this trial is that the recruitment is based on the naturalistic flow of patients referred to Mental Health Center Stolpegaard. Even in the case of a higher non-consent rate, it is considered feasible to recruit the target number of participants within the trial period. Therapist will screen all patients for eligibility as part of the patient’s regular attendance at the clinic. The therapist will provide brief oral information about participation in the trial and collect informed consent from the patient to be contacted by a member of the research team (E. H.), which will be documented in a e-journal system (Sundhedsplatformen). A member of the research team (E.H.) will then contact the patient and schedule an information meeting, which can be held over phone or at the research unit. The aim of this meeting is to provide detailed oral information about participation in the trial.

Randomization

After enrolment and completion of baseline assessments, parents will be randomly assigned to the experimental or control intervention using a concealed allocation sequence with permuted blocks of various sizes generated by a computer algorithm in REDCap and kept unknown to all personnel involved in the trial. This is done to eliminate any predictability in the random sequence. The randomization was stratified by gender (men vs women) and high (equal to or above 96) vs low (below 96) parenting stress score (that is, a baseline parenting stress score on the PSI-SF below or above the median of 96).

Blinding

Due to the nature of the trial, blinding of participants and clinical staff is not possible. Baseline assessments will be conducted before allocation of participants, and outcome assessments will be performed by blinded assessors. Participants will be instructed to withhold any information concerning their group allocation to the outcome assessors throughout the trial period. All researchers will be blinded to block size and frequencies. The statistical analyses will be conducted with the intervention groups coded as A and B [100].

Data management

Data will be collected using electronic case report forms developed in the software program called Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap). The system has been approved by the Danish Data Protection Agency and fulfils the requirements for data security. Baseline and 6-month follow-up data will be entered directly into REDCAP on a tablet, and data from follow-up assessments 12 and 24 months will be entered directly into REDCAP using the Survey option. Participants will receive a link for the survey via email or Denmark’s secure digital mailbox system (e-boks).

Statistical analysis

We will use descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, frequencies, proportions, as appropriate) and t-test to describe and analyze baseline data, retention, and dropout rates for both interventions. The primary outcome—PSI-SF total score (continuous data) -will be analyzed using linear regression. The analysis will be based on an intention-to-treat population and will be adjusted for the baseline value of the PSI-SF total score. A per-protocol analysis will be carried out as a sensitivity analysis. Similar analyses will be performed for all continuous secondary outcomes at all assessment points. Missing data will be handled in line with the suggestions by Jakobsen et al. [101]. When approximately 50% of the participants has been randomized, we will perform interim analyses in accordance with the good clinical practice guidelines of the International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceutical for Human Use (ICH-GCP) [102]. The results will decide whether the trial should continue or stop in line with the criteria listed by Jakobsen et al. [103]. Aggregated and individual de-identified participant data may be available upon reasonable request.

Dissemination

The Danish population will be informed of the trial as well as its results through national media as well as social media, such as LinkedIn,The results of the trial will be submitted to international scientific journals with anonymous peer-reviewing and free of all restrictions on access. Positive, negative, and inconclusive results will be published. The results will also be presented at outpatient mental health centers in the Mental Health Service, Capital Region of Denmark, and at relevant international and national research conferences and meetings. A lay summary of the results will be shared with participants, as well as relevant national patients’ and relatives’ associations.

Discussion

This protocol outlined the need, aim, and methodology of a randomized clinical trial evaluating the effects of a mentalization-based parenting intervention (Lighthouse MBT Parenting Program), compared to CAU for parents with mental disorders in AMHS. This trial will contribute to the evidence base for early preventive interventions for parents with mental disorders. The project is innovative in that the parenting intervention is transdiagnostic and is delivered as an add on to the parents’ ongoing psychiatric treatment in adult mental health service. Knowledge about the effects is essential to inform both policy makers and clinical practice in providing early preventive evidence-based interventions for parents with mental disorders—a previously neglected area. The intervention is short, non-intrusive to the parents’ lives, and can easily be scaled up and implemented in other settings, if effective. Thus, it has the potential to be used widely in mental health service and social service across Denmark and in other countries given the established gaps in support services that it has the potential to fill. As such, this study may have a far-reaching impact nationally and internationally. Finally, it may improve the life of parents with a mental disorder and their children, and, for some, make the difference between child adversity or not. In this way, it could in the long run reduce the significant costs associated with parental mental disorders.

The present trial has several strengths. First, it has a high degree of external validity due to the relatively broad inclusion criteria. Second, we pre-registered the trial before randomization, and prespecified the methodology based on CONSORT. Third, the assessment battery is relatively brief, and we therefore expect high recruitment rates. However, our trial also has some limitations. First, no systematic review of the effects of preventive interventions for parents with mental disorders on various offspring outcomes is currently available. As mentioned earlier, we are currently conducting such as review. Second, the LPP intervention, which is 12 weeks of parenting groups in this trial, diverges in intensity (duration) and format (individual vs parenting sessions) from the original 20-week parenting program. The rationale for choosing this adapted yet manualized version was due to limited resources and fixed lengths of the treatment packages implemented in the Danish mental health service system. Third, we expect only a small proportion of parents randomized to CAU to take part in a family conversation. Fourth, we expect a large proportion of missing data as this group is considered hard to reach and retain in treatment and research.

Declarations

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Prior to commencing the trial, approval was obtained from the Regional Ethics Committee and from the Danish Data Protection Agency and Data Protection Act (ID: P-2023-15049). The trial will be conducted in compliance with the official trial protocol, the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki [104], ICH-GCP guidelines [102], and the European General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) standards [105]. There are some potential ethical concerns relevant to this trial. First, participation in research assessments may be a minor burden to the parents. Secondly, as the target population includes parents with a mental disorder, who constitute a high-risk group in terms of child maltreatment, all therapists and on-site members of the research team will receive training in terms of policies and procedures for handling concerns about children’s safety. Third, the interventions are not expected to be associated with any side effects or risk to parents or children. Both the experimental intervention and control intervention follow the clinical guidelines by the Mental Health Services, Capital Region. However, we are aware that participation in parenting-focused interventions for high-risk groups may be emotionally demanding and at times stressful, which could impact our outcome measures. In addition, random allocation of psychiatric patients may result in lack of trust and feelings of abandonment if allocated to an untoward intervention (typically CAU). This could potentially influence their motivation to continue receiving their usual psychiatric treatment and may thus pose a risk of the patients’ dropping out of the research project as well as their standard psychiatric treatment. The consent allows the investigators, sponsor, and representatives of the sponsor, as well as any regulatory authority, direct access to obtain information in the participant’s records, including electronic records, to review details about the participant’s health conditions that are necessary for the implementation of the research project, as well as for monitoring purposes, including self-inspection, quality control, and monitoring that they are obligated to perform. During the trial period, serious adverse events for participating parents will be carefully monitored throughout the trial, and we will assess, document, and report any adverse events to the regional research ethics committee. This committee monitors all reports of serious adverse events from trial sites. Exclusion from the trial only occurs in case of acute serious endangerment of the participant or his/her child. Participants can, at any time, withdraw their consent without any implications for current or future treatment in the Mental Health Service, Capital Region. The trial is covered by patient compensation.
