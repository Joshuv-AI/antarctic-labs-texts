Identifying and supporting trafficked individuals: provider and community organization perspectives on existing sociotechnical approaches
Authors: Michelle Gomez, Ellen W Clayton, Colin G Walsh, Kim M Unertl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTrafficked persons experience adverse health consequences and seek help, but many go unrecognized by health-care professionals. This study explored professionals’ perspectives on current approaches toward identifying and supporting trafficked persons in health-care settings, highlighting current technology roles, gaps, and future directions.Materials and MethodsWe developed an interview guide to investigate current human trafficking (HT) approaches, safety procedures, and HT education. Semistructured interviews were conducted via Zoom, iteratively coded in Dedoose, and analyzed using a thematic analysis approach.ResultsWe interviewed 19 health-care and community group professionals and identified 3 themes: (1) participants described a responsibility to build trust with patients through compassionate communication, rapport, and trauma-informed approaches across different stages of care. (2) Technology played a dual role, as professionals navigated both benefits and challenges of tools such as Zoom, virtual interpreters, and cameras in trust building. (3) Safety and privacy concerns guided how participants documented patient encounters and shared community resources, ensuring confidentiality while supporting patient and community well-being.DiscussionTechnology can both support and hinder trust in health care, directly affecting trafficked patients and their safety. Informatics can improve care for trafficked persons, but further research is needed on technology-based interventions. We provide recommendations to strengthen trust, enhance safety, support trauma-informed care, and promote safe documentation practices.ConclusionEffective sociotechnical approaches rely on trust, safety, and mindful documentation to support trafficked patients. Future research directions include refining the role of informatics in trauma-informed care to strengthen trust and mitigate unintended consequences.

Introduction

Human trafficking (HT) is defined as situations where force, fraud, or coercion is used to obtain labor or engage in commercial sex acts.1 Human trafficking occurs across the United States, with differences in prevalence and experiences of trafficked individuals by location.2–4 In 2024, the National Human Trafficking Hotline received reports of 11 999 trafficking cases,2 a number widely considered an undercount due to the hidden nature of the crime. Additionally, a single reported case may involve multiple individuals, meaning the number of individuals impacted is likely greater than the number of cases reported.2,5 The Global Slavery Index also estimates that 1.1 million people are living in modern slavery, a term often used to describe HT, on any given day in the United States.6 Research further underscores the invisible and pervasive nature of HT in both urban and rural areas,5,7–9 revealing that HT affects people from many backgrounds. Vulnerability is especially heightened among those with a history of substance abuse, childhood trauma, and/or physical or sexual abuse.10,11

Trafficked persons often seek health care, but HT frequently goes unnoticed in clinical settings.12 Medical intervention may be needed for complications from sexually transmitted diseases, unsafe underground procedures, and pregnancy, among other reasons.13–16 However, up to 88% of survivors report seeking health-care services prior to being identified as a survivor.17–19 This suggests that health-care professionals have the potential to identify and provide more timely support to trafficked patients.18,20 Previous studies show that failure to provide this care relates to lack of healthcare professional education on HT signs, limited sustainable screening opportunities, and unclear pathways after patient disclosure.21–23 Informatics-based tools, implemented as components of sociotechnical interventions, may have potential to improve identification and support for trafficked individuals in health-care settings. However, their implementation also carries risks of unintended consequences.

Through a qualitative investigation, this study examined the people, processes, and technologies involved in health care-facing sociotechnical approaches and their potential for identifying and providing resources to trafficked patients in TN. Tennessee is a hub for HT due to its extensive highway system including I-40, I-24, and I-65, which make transporting trafficked persons across state lines easier.24 Tennessee also shares its border with 8 states and its proximity to states (eg, GA, AL, MS) with high trafficking rates, combined with the ongoing opioid crisis,25 leaves many individuals in the state at risk of exploitation.26,27 Despite TN being a hub for HT, the impact of existing intervention approaches to help providers make accurate assessments of a patient’s HT status has not previously been evaluated.28,29

We define intervention approaches as a range of actions, strategies, tools, and procedures implemented to influence the identification and support of trafficked patients.30,31 Certain approaches may encompass clinical practices, technological solutions, and organizational protocols that affect how health-care professionals recognize signs of trafficking, engage with patients, and provide appropriate resources and care. This includes using screening tools, applying trauma-informed care (TIC) techniques—an approach that acknowledges the impact of trauma and prioritizes safety, trust, empowerment, and collaboration to prevent retraumatization32—and incorporating communication tools like electronic health records (EHRs) or medical documentation. These elements work together to support health-care professionals in delivering effective care for their patients.

In this study, we sought to answer the following research questions: What are the experiences of health-care and social service providers with trafficked patients? What are current approaches to identify and assist trafficked patients in health-care settings? How do providers view current approaches to HT interventions in health-care contexts? This examination identified how informatics contributes to current approaches and highlights the challenges encountered by health-care professionals as they care for trafficked patients. We provide recommendations based on our findings.

Objective

This study aimed to explore health-care and social service professionals’ perspectives on existing health care-focused sociotechnical approaches and their potential to support the identification and care of trafficked patients. We sought to understand the current role of technology, identify gaps, and determine future directions for these approaches in health care.

Methods

The Vanderbilt University Institutional Review Board (IRB) approved study procedures before recruitment and data collection.

Sampling strategy and recruitment

Our sampling strategy combined purposeful sampling33 with snowball sampling,33,34 continuing data collection until data saturation.35 Recruitment lasted from February to September 2022. Initial recruitment focused on health professionals from departments and clinics at an academic medical center with a focus on areas where trafficked people are more likely to visit, such as the emergency department (ED), primary care, and women’s health. We extended recruitment efforts to health and social service professionals working in community settings. Since TN spans a wide geographic region with connections to different interstate routes, we wanted to sample across the state. We sent targeted emails to potential research volunteers and organizational connections describing the project and included an e-flyer and a study information sheet. We also incorporated a snowball sampling question into interviews to help identify potential research volunteers. We limited recruitment to participants serving adults.

Semistructured interview instrument development

Guided by an initial literature review on HT tools and process in clinical practice, we developed a semistructured interview guide (Supplementary Material). Two authors (M.G., K.M.U.) refined questions for clarity and relevance to the research questions before initial use. The interview guide included questions about participants’ roles, their knowledge of current intervention approaches for identifying trafficked patients and providing support, and their awareness of safety processes to protect patients and care teams. Interviews also discussed prior education on HT and role-specific innovation needs and challenges. The semistructured interview approach provided a standard set of questions while allowing flexibility for follow-up questions and deeper exploration.

Data collection procedure

We conducted individual semistructured interviews with participants. Interviews were virtual and hosted on Zoom.36 We selected the audio only recording option in Zoom settings to record interviews. All research volunteers provided verbal informed consent before starting the interview. We offered a $25 honorarium to all participants. An IRB-approved professional transcription service transcribed audio recordings.

Data analysis

We imported transcripts into the qualitative research coding software, Dedoose,37 and used a thematic analysis approach as a guiding analytical framework.38 One researcher (M.G.) had primary responsibility for coding, but discussed analysis processes, emerging theoretical insights, assignment of codes to specific excerpts, and progress toward data saturation on a regular basis with a second researcher (K.M.U.). We assessed data saturation by examining whether existing codes adequately captured new incoming data and determined that no new codes or themes were emerging during the analysis process. To ensure consistent coding, we relied on the constant comparison method39,40 in which code applications were regularly compared within and between transcripts as new codes were generated and previous codes were revised. For example, a code initially titled “secret resources” was revised to “camouflaged resources” as the term “camouflage” more accurately described the excerpts within the code. Another example, a code initially titled “Interpreter use” was split into 2 separate codes: “virtual interpreter use” and “in-person interpreter use,” as distinct challenges and nuances of each were captured more accurately through interviews with participants. To safeguard participants’ meaning, we named codes using language commonly used by research participants, where appropriate. We also applied descriptive codes to summarize major ideas expressed in code units. Throughout the analysis, we engaged in memoing immediately after interviews and during the coding process to capture analytic reflections. We grouped open codes into broader categories and examined categories to identify themes and theoretical insights. The output of analysis included an in-depth description of existing trafficking-related approaches, the role of technology in processes, and gaps in existing approaches.

Results

We interviewed 19 health and social service professionals from across TN and from a variety of settings including emergency departments, primary care, community health centers, and community organizations. Interviews lasted an average of 43 min (range, 19-60 min). Participants with self-described primary roles beyond medical titles (n = 5) discussed educational backgrounds in either social work or nursing. Several participants (n = 7) were associated with the Sexual Assault Nurse Examiner (SANE) program.41

Theme A: Building and sustaining trust with trafficked and abused patients depends on compassionate and transparent communication throughout all care stages

Health-care and social service professionals prioritized building and maintaining trust with patients across several stages of a patient encounter, including during intake, medical evaluation, and discharge planning. To foster a safe environment for patients, health-care and social service professionals employed key strategies such as building rapport, using TIC, and empowering patients to seek help even if they were not ready to leave their perpetrator. These strategies were essential in facilitating open communication and ensuring patients felt supported.

Theme A.1: Health and social service professionals demonstrate a sense of responsibility and an elevated priority to create and maintain trust with patients experiencing trafficking

Participants in our study confirmed what has been reported in prior qualitative studies focused on health-care contexts and human trafficking.43–48 They emphasized that establishing and maintaining trust with patients is essential for identifying trafficking, supporting patients through trauma, and building lasting connections. According to our participants, trafficked patients often struggled with trusting others, and significant attention was needed to gain and maintain trust, in part due to understandable caution based on patients past experiences. Participants were touched by personal encounters with trafficked patients and felt a responsibility to construct and sustain a trusting relationship with them. A community organization professional shared,For most of their life, they have had people who have broken trust, and they have had people that they just couldn’t trust… I know with every single survivor that comes through our program, that I am going to have to earn their trust. And I’m going to have to earn it over, and over, and over again. (P19)

For most of their life, they have had people who have broken trust, and they have had people that they just couldn’t trust… I know with every single survivor that comes through our program, that I am going to have to earn their trust. And I’m going to have to earn it over, and over, and over again. (P19)

Many participants acknowledged the hardships of maintaining trust with trafficked individuals, and some described going to great lengths, like coming in outside of working hours, to ensure that trusted care team members are present during patient encounters. Study participants emphasized the importance of building and maintaining trust throughout stages (ie, initial intake, examination, treatment, discharge) of the entire clinical encounter.

Maintaining trust between health-care teams and patients was challenged by external factors. In one notable case, a patient declined intervention at the ED twice, first due to fear of the perpetrator finding out and second due to several hours of transportation delay, a service provided by a local community organization. While the social worker on call described being devastated about this situation, she got the impression that the patient “had convinced herself that this organization didn’t want to help her, and she didn’t know if she could trust them” (P10). Like other participants, the social worker reflected on how sustaining trust with patients is beneficial to patients as they continue care.

Theme A.2: Patient comfort and rapport building serve as foundational trauma-informed practices employed during the intake process

Ensuring patient comfort was essential for encouraging them to open up about difficult situations. Psychosocial screening questions, often asked at the start of health-care visits, were asked in isolation from others to ensure patient comfort and honest responses. In red-flag situations, such as a visitor’s answering for the patient, participants might isolate the patient by stating the need for privacy for radiology tests or restroom use. Building rapport through humanizing questions, like asking about enjoyable activities, was common before invasive questioning. While participants expressed their hope that patients would answer screening questions truthfully, the fast-paced environment of the ED presented challenges with building rapport. One ED nurse worried about honesty in answers from patients because it was “really hard to truly form that close-knit of a relationship with your patient” (P12). An ED physician felt similarly stating, “Physicians are pressed all the time about time… It’s hard to take the time to sit down and talk to people” (P1). Despite the time constraints, some participants vouched for the importance of seeing the whole patient and expressed a need for mandatory HT screening. Based on our interviews, participants were not aware if their organization had an HT-specific screening questionnaire.

Theme A.3: Trauma-informed approaches are embedded throughout the entire clinical encounter to support patient-centered and trust-building care

Participants applied principles of TIC as a foundation for patient-centered and trust-building interactions throughout all stages (ie, initial intake, examination, treatment, discharge) of clinical encounters.44 Sexual Assault Nurse Examiner nurses specifically highlighted the importance of providing care that acknowledges and validates the patient’s trauma. Multiple participants stressed the need to minimize repeated retelling of traumatic events, recommending that patients share their stories only once when all relevant professionals are present. One participant described a distressing care where a patient had to recount their abuse story 6 times in a single night at the hospital. Such experiences were viewed as retraumatizing and avoidable.

Participants also emphasized the importance of transparency by clearly explaining what they were about to do and what patients should expect during clinical encounters as a key strategy to build trust. This might be especially important for refugee patients and others unfamiliar with the American health-care system, where clear communication helps ease anxiety and supports understanding. As a community nurse shared:The language that we use and the way that we touch patients, you know, just informing them. I’m going to do this kind of exam, and this is what you might experience…I mean, communication sets the tone for how you want the visit to go, and it’s also a way to build trust with the patient…I always…introduce myself, and that’s important…especially for some of our refugee patients who are not familiar with the American healthcare system… (P18)

I’m going to do this kind of exam, and this is what you might experience…I mean, communication sets the tone for how you want the visit to go, and it’s also a way to build trust with the patient…I always…introduce myself, and that’s important…especially for some of our refugee patients who are not familiar with the American healthcare system… (P18)

Obtaining consent was also described as an essential practice in supporting patient-centered care, which involves respecting patients’ preferences, needs, and values in aspects of clinical decision-making. In addition, participants highlighted active listening, using the talkback method, and limiting the use of technology while the patient was speaking as important TIC approaches to foster connection, and subsequently, trust.

Theme A.4: Efforts are made during the discharge process to empower patients and preserve a supportive, open-door policy encouraging continued care

Discussions about leaving a perpetrator and creating a safety plan are typical at patient discharge. Participants emphasized patient empowerment especially as patients considered leaving a perpetrator. However, some patient may choose to remain in their current situation. Several participants noted they “can’t force them” to leave, as it undermines the patient’s control. Instead, they empowered patients by reminding them of their autonomy. Participants felt disheartened when patients were not ready to leave their trafficking situation but were hopeful they would seek future assistance. One participant said, “helping is not rescuing the patient every time, sometimes it’s just planting the seeds” (P4). In these moments, participants spoke openly and honestly with patients about the difficulty of leaving a perpetrator, quoting facts about the number of attempts typically made by victims, and reassured patients that they would be welcomed back.

Theme B: Health and social service professionals navigate the complex benefits and challenges presented by technology when working to establish and maintain patient trust

Participants voiced concerns that technology could hinder meaningful connections with patients. While participants recognized its advantages in certain situations, such as facilitating communication across language barriers and enabling remote appointments when in-person appointments were not feasible, participants also noted that technology should be avoided during sensitive examinations. Participants felt that using technology in these moments could detract from the patient telling their story, making it harder to build trust. However, when used thoughtfully, technology had the potential to enhance trust by improving access to care and providing alternative ways for patients to seek support.

Theme B.1: Zoom-based care increases accessibility while potentially hindering the development of trust, connection, and continuity of care

The COVID-19 pandemic transformed how patients received care by increasing virtual forms of communication. As community advocate (see Table 1 footnotes) consultations became virtual due to the pandemic, 1 nurse suspected that patients were hesitant to communicate with community organizations in this way. She added that patients might feel uneasy discussing sensitive topics via Zoom due to concerns about data safety and security. One participant described virtual communications as “cold, distant, more difficult to develop a rapport” (P9). She also mentioned that virtual appointments are easily forgotten, leading to frequent no-shows. These missed appointments can disrupt continuity of care by making it harder to build consistent relationships with providers and coordinate ongoing support. Over time, this fragmentation may delay early intervention, weaken trust, and reduce patient engagement in their own health. Multiple participants reported favoring being “face-to-face” with patients. A community professional added,If there’s a way that we can be in person, we try to be in person, as safely as possible. But we just know that in person, typically, is better. (P19)

Some participants noted that some patients may not be able to attend consultations at community organizations due to trafficker control or lack of transportation. A community member highlighted the “silver lining” of technology saying, it “may be the only option.” (see Table 2)

Theme B.2: Use of virtual and in-person interpreter services introduces accessibility and linguistic challenges that may affect patient trust

Most participants discussed using virtual translation services with nonnative English speakers, often via tablets or phones. Many preferred in-person interpreters, especially for communicating with an abused patient, but many recognized the importance of virtual interpreter services due to increasing language diversity in the region. A social worker shared,I mean there’s nothing like face-to-face. You know, so much is learned by innuendos and facial expressions and that kind of thing. It’s adequate. I mean it is what it is. We have so much diversity that there’s no way that you would ever be able to have an interpreter on-site that could do all languages. So, we do the best that we can. I don’t feel comfortable with it, to be honest with you, but you have to do what you’ve got to do. (P5)

Another participant noted difficulties in finding suitable interpreters as patients often speak different dialects, causing interpretation issues. Despite an overall preference for in-person interpreters, 1 ED nurse mentioned patients in small ethnic communities might avoid them due to fear of knowing the interpreter. Automated tools like Google translate were often used to initiate conversations about in-house interpreters. One participant favored Google translate despite other available services, noting that “it speeds up the process,” when clients are in a hurry.

Theme B.3: Minimizing technology use in forensic exams is employed to preserve patient comfort and examiner presence

Forensic nurse examinations serve victims of sexual abuse and human trafficking through evidence collection and collaboration with law enforcement. Forensic examination processes include photographing affected areas, but despite privacy precautions and consent requests, many patients decline this portion of the examination. One nurse shared,But that’s the one thing that they’re going to decide not to proceed. It’ll be with the pictures … it’s like, "This is your call." I mean the pictures are, obviously, not released. We keep these even in a separate secure area. So, it’s not even part of their medical record.” (P6)

But that’s the one thing that they’re going to decide not to proceed. It’ll be with the pictures … it’s like, "This is your call." I mean the pictures are, obviously, not released. We keep these even in a separate secure area. So, it’s not even part of their medical record.” (P6)

Participants understood patients’ privacy concerns but felt disappointed when they declined photos as this lack of documentation could weaken potential legal cases.

Although some SANE nurses using computers for efficiency, many believed writing notes by hand was more comforting for patients. Several SANE nurses discussed avoiding laptops during exams to focus on patients. Instead, they used paper interview guides and transcribed notes into the EHR later. One nurse felt laptops made the interactions less personal also stating that it “takes away from them trying to tell me what happened” (P7), while another nurse mentioned they did not want to seem like they were just checking boxes. One participant empathized with patients sharing “If I were in that position, I would want the nurse to write instead of type” (P12).

Theme C: Patient safety and privacy concerns guide how health and social service professionals documented care and share information

Participants highlighted patients’ fears of perpetrators’ discovering their disclosures, emphasizing the critical need for confidentiality. To address these concerns, health and social service professionals employed mindful documentation and nondocumentation practices to protect sensitive information. Additionally, innovative technology-based strategies were used to conceal resource information, ensuring that patients could access support when it was safe to do so. These approaches aimed to enhance both patient safety and trust in health care.

Theme C.1: Documentation methods vary to accommodate safety concerns, anonymity, and EHR system constraints

Participants emphasized the importance of documentation methods for patient safety. In primary care, 1 participant discussed social determinants of health (SDOH) collection might be useful for any future HT-related alerts, saying, “I think the alerts would be nice, but we’re probably just not capturing that information. (P2). They noted that there was an SDOH section in the EHR, which was often missing due to poor integration into the intake process. Instead, the information is likely documented in free text. Depending on the sensitivity of the situation and if the information would put the patient in harm’s way, 1 social worker mentioned that she has blocked information from being seen by the patient or others who may have access to their patient portal.

Documentation practices varied among community organizations, using both paper and electronic methods. Several participants mentioned that clients feared providing their legal names. As a result, social service organization allowed clients to use an alias to receive services. One participant observed that clients seeking anonymity were sometimes concerned about how anonymity would be maintained and if not providing a legal name would impact their access to resources. Respondents mentioned reassuring patients by showing how their information would appear under an alias in the database. Another participant shared that they were careful in the language they used when writing client case notes, knowing that these records could be accessed by attorneys and potentially lead to future criminalization. They state: “We operate under the idea that if defense gets a hold of this, you don’t want anything to incriminate your client” (P15).

Health-care professionals often secured patient charts by designating patients as “no-info” to protect patient privacy. A “no-info” status prevents the hospital from confirming a patient’s presence, limiting visitors to those approved by the patient. Locking charts in this way restricts access to the care team, safeguarding sensitive information. These measures, according to a social worker, “make sure the patient remains safe in our hands” (P10). Information about “no-info” status is shared across health-care teams through the EHR and with law enforcement stationed at the hospital via physical documents.

Theme C.2: Professionals navigate the tension between delivering timely patient support and ensuring the safety and privacy of the community

Participants recognized the dual challenge of aiding individual patients promptly while safeguarding other HT survivors. Health-care professionals aimed to support patients but were sometimes limited by available information. In a specific case, a social worker faced delays in arranging transportation for a patient to a safe house. Despite the urgency, the community organization declined to provide the address needed for the transport, citing safety concerns. The social worker recalled mentioning “Look, this is not being documented anywhere. I just need it to get the ride so they can get there” (P10), suggesting a promise of privacy but to no avail. The treating physician acknowledged the need for secrecy, mentioning, “organizations have to keep those locations secret and safe … we can’t know for the safety of the people that they protect” (P1).

Theme C.3: Camouflaging strategies are employed to provide informational resources in ways that prioritize patient safety

Providing patients with informational resources with community organization information was a common postdisclosure. Participants described these resources as “pocket sized”, “wallet-sized” or “small” items that could be easily concealed by the patient. Despite resource packets being small, patients were fearful to take physical pamphlets due to fear of perpetrator discovery. In response to these fears, health-care providers used creative technology-based approaches to provide patients with access to the resources with less fear. For example, 1 nurse taught a patient how to discretely store information on their phone using the hidden photo feature:I taught her how to hide a photo on her phone…I was like, “If you think this won’t get found, this is what we can do.” And I had her take a picture of domestic violence shelters, like three source locations… ‘To find it, you need this. This is how to find it. And this is where you can eventually go when the timing’s right.’ (P7)

Some participants shared cases where patients asked to disguise a domestic hotline number in their discharge paperwork. Furthermore, 1 community organization member brought up using office supplies with fake names but real phone numbers to disguise their hotline number.

Discussion

We identified 2 themes related to trust building and patient safety. First, participants emphasized their responsibility to build trust with trafficked patients by using compassionate and transparent communication, building rapport, and trauma-informed strategies throughout patient encounters. Second, participants described navigating both the benefits and challenges of technology, which could support or hinder trust. Third, safety and privacy concerns shaped documentation practices and information sharing to ensure confidentiality while maintaining access to resources. This study offers a foundation for future research to develop more comprehensive theory in this area.

This study supports prior research highlighting the importance of trust-building through TIC, and empowering strategies when caring for patients who have experienced HT.43–46 For example, prior work has investigated how health-care professionals in emergency care and pediatric settings perceive and engage with trafficked individuals.49,50 They often point to discomfort, lack of preparedness, and the need for trauma-informed protocols. Findings from these studies echo what our participants described, namely that identifying and supporting trafficked patients often requires time, rapport, and trust.

Trauma-informed care is guided by 6 foundational principles: safety; trustworthiness and transparency; peer support; collaboration; empowerment; and attention to cultural, historical, and gender issues.32,51 These principles help health-care professionals respond to the effects of trauma by fostering environments that prioritize emotional and physical safety, support patient autonomy, and encourage collaborative care.47,48 In the context of trafficking, as our participants highlighted, applying these principles can strengthen trust, reduce risk of retraumatization, and improve care engagement.48,51 Specifically, participants shared that building trust with patients that experienced trafficking often required sensitivity to cultural and emotional cues, clear communication and private space for disclosure. Other studies have highlighted the value of trauma-informed approaches across different areas of care and support, including fostering interdisciplinary and citywide collaborations,52 using trauma-informed psychological assessments,53 and offering case management practices that respect survivors’ voices and experiences.54 However, the use of technology—such as Zoom or Laptop use during examinations or iPads for virtual services—may complicate efforts to uphold these principles as they may limit personal connection or privacy. These tensions underscore the need for thoughtful integration of technology within trauma-informed frameworks that promote trust and minimize harm.

Research highlights that successful implementation of TIC within health-care systems require understanding of the mechanisms that support its effectiveness.55 In our study, participants described how specific technologies such as Zoom for virtual interpretation, laptops used during forensic exams, and EHR documentation practices shaped their interactions with patients who maybe be experiencing HT. While these tools supported care delivery and access, participants also noted that technology hindered trust-building.45,46 For example, when patients felt that technology was impersonal or when privacy and security concerns arose, such as the use of photo documentation or video consultations, they appeared uncomfortable or less forthcoming. This discomfort could reduce the patient’s willingness to engage with medical professionals, particularly among those who have experienced trauma.56 These findings suggest that trafficked patients are especially vulnerable to unintended consequences of poorly integrated technology and highlight the importance of designing and using tools in ways that uphold TIC principles.55,57,58

While research exists on the impact of technology on patient–provider communication,59–62 limited studies address its effects on patients who have experienced trafficking, particularly those with limited English proficiency or technology-related trauma stemming from their exploitation. For some trafficked patients, technology such as phones or surveillance tools like recording devices may have been used as part of their exploitation, and the presence of such tools in clinical settings can be triggering when used. Interpreter services, though essential, are limited to distinct dialects, raising concerns about accessibility and effectiveness, particularly for racial and ethnic minorities who are already at higher risk for HT11 and often mistrust the health-care system.63–65 Language barriers can further hinder their willingness to seek care and disclose their experiences, complicating care coordination for these groups. There is a pressing need for culturally informed antitrafficking strategies that prioritize trust building.12,66 Informatics tools designed with trauma awareness in mind, such as EHR features that provide patient comfort levels with when and how technology is used, could help reduce retraumatization. Additionally, developing technologies that bridge communication gaps, such as systems that facilitate patient–provider–interpreter interactions, could improve early recognition and strengthen patient trust. This is especially important as virtual interpreters, typically not physically present in the room, may miss subtle physical cues or nonverbal signals from patients, such as body language and innuendos, that could be critical in understanding their needs.

This need is highlighted by challenges identified in recent studies of virtual interpretation in clinical workflows.67,68 Informatics can play a key role in addressing these challenges by creating secure remote systems tailored for clinical interpretation that integrate with EHRs, ensure enhanced HIPAA compliance, and support workflows designed for interpreter use. These platforms could also enable multimodal communication (audio, visual, real-time secure messaging, etc.) to better capture both verbal and nonverbal signals. Future research should evaluate how such technologies improve communication and promote compassionate, trauma-informed care.

Participants reported challenges in documenting surrogate markers of trafficking and/or abuse through SDOH documentation. While many EHRs include SDOH sections, these are poorly integrated into workflows and see low adoption due to time constraints, uncertainty about how best to use the information, and safety concerns about documenting such sensitive information.69–72 Documenting SDOH data carries significant risk, particularly for trafficked individuals or abused patients. If such sensitive information is improperly accessed, it could put the patient as further risk, especially if their abuser gains access to their medical records. The 21st Century Cures Act’s “open notes” provision, which grants patients’ direct access to EHR data,73 adds another layer of complexity. While transparency is essential, it may inadvertently expose individuals to harm if sensitive information is visible to those who control or monitor their records. To mitigate these risks, clinicians often document sensitive information in free-text fields that are visible only to providers, blocking them from patient portals. However, this approach can limit the usability of critical data in clinical care, as free-text is difficult to parse through. Additionally, while providers can restrict patient access to certain notes in portals, these same restrictions may not apply within hospital systems where other staff may still view the documentation. These challenges highlight the need for clearer guidelines on documenting SDOH and trauma-related disclosures in ways that protect patient safety while ensuring that information is clinically useful.

Participants also highlighted the challenges of identifying trafficked individuals. There were no formal trafficking-specific screening tools or institutional protocols in use. While health-care professionals used standard psychosocial screening questions, these did not explicitly address trafficking. Although participants did not explicitly describe the lack of tools as a challenge, their accounts revealed that identification relied on building trust, intuition, and prior experiences, indicating that identifying trafficked patients was often indirect and nuanced. This underscores the need for better tools and training to support earlier and more reliable identification of HT.74

To address these gaps, we recommend embedding HT-specific screening tools into EHR workflows to ensure standardized, evidence-based assessments and streamline identification of trafficked patients. We also recommend developing alert system within EHRs to help notify social workers and/or SANE staff when patient data indicate patterns of concern, supporting timely intervention. Finally, careful consideration of ethical issues and potential unintended consequences, particularly regarding patient safety, is critical for all potential changes to processes and technology.

For technology to be effective in TIC, it must be designed with clear considerations for patient privacy, autonomy, and ease of use. Additionally, health-care providers must ensure that they communicate clearly with patients about the role of technology in their care, explaining how their information might be used and stored, and providing alternatives for those who prefer to rely less on technology. Meaningful and transparent use of technology is crucial for building trust and ensuring it serves as a tool for healing rather than creating obstacles.

Incorporating trafficked persons input is crucial in designing effective health-care intervention approaches and policies. Participants noted that patients preferred concealing resources in plain sight, particularly in digital formats, to avoid suspicion, rather than carrying a physical list of resources. Involving those with lived trafficking experiences helps identify and address potential unintended consequences, ensuring that strategies do not inadvertently jeopardize the safety of patients seeking help. By integrating such insights, health-care systems can develop more effective and trauma-informed interventions that meet the unique needs of trafficked individuals.

Limitations

A key limitation of this study is that it did not examine the influence of gender during patient encounters when trafficking is suspected or confirmed, nor the role of technology in shaping these interactions. Additionally, this study interviewed only health-care and social service professionals likely to interact with trafficked persons, using snowball sampling, which may introduce bias.80 Insights are based on participants’ perceptions rather than trafficked individuals’ experiences. Future studies must include trafficked persons and should consider the use of observational methods, with member checks,81,82 to provide additional insights.

In conclusion, effective sociotechnical approaches, supported by technology and informatics, are essential for building trust and ensuring safety to support trafficked individuals. Future research should focus on refining technology’s role in trauma-informed care in health-care settings. By leveraging technology thoughtfully and implementing careful documentation practices, health-care providers can enhance patient safety and create a foundation for meaningful, trust-based care.
