Dental age—based assessment of retromolar morphology in skeletal Class III patients: Implications for mandibular third molar development
Authors: Thuy-Trang Thi Ho, Tu Manh Nguyen, Loc Minh Lu, Fang-Yu Fan, Ying-Sui Sun, Yang-Che Wu, Sheng-Wei Feng, Chien-Fu Tseng, Pao-Chang Chiang, I-Ta Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/purposeIn skeletal Class III malocclusion, evaluation of mandibular third molar development is clinically important for orthodontic treatment planning, particularly in relation to posterior space conditions. This study aimed to assess the association between dental age of mandibular third molars and retromolar morphological characteristics in skeletal Class III patients.Materials and methodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study included panoramic radiographs from 157 patients diagnosed with skeletal Class III malocclusion based on clinical examination and lateral cephalometric analysis. Mandibular third molar development was evaluated using Demirjian mineralization stages (D–H), representing stages from crown completion to root maturation. Retromolar morphology was evaluated using retromolar space (RMS), the RMS-to-crown width ratio (Ganss ratio), and the mesiodistal angulation between the second and third molars (Beta angle). Correlations between dental age and these morphological parameters were analyzed.ResultsAs dental age progressed, RMS increased from 5.59 mm to 9.18 mm and the Ganss ratio from 0.45 to 0.70, whereas the Beta angle decreased from 35.82° to 24.80°. Dental age showed moderate positive associations with RMS and the Ganss ratio (P < 0.001), but no significant association with the Beta angle (P = 0.479). These findings indicate coordinated changes between third molar development and retromolar morphology in skeletal Class III patients.ConclusionDental age—related changes in retromolar morphology may provide useful radiographic information for describing posterior space conditions in skeletal Class III patients.

Introduction

Skeletal Class III malocclusion is a complex dentofacial deformity characterized by an imbalance in the development of the maxilla and mandible.1 In most cases, this condition presents as mandibular overgrowth and/or maxillary deficiency, resulting in an abnormal anteroposterior relationship between the two jaws.2 Such skeletal disharmony not only affects facial esthetics but also compromises masticatory function and poses challenges in orthodontic treatment planning.

Within the mandibular arch, the retromolar region is anatomically relevant to permanent dentition development and posterior tooth movement. Retromolar space (RMS), defined as the area distal to the second molar and bounded by the anterior border of the ramus, is an anatomical characteristic reflecting posterior space conditions in the mandible.3 When this space is adequate and morphologically favorable, it may be associated with more favorable posterior space conditions for distal tooth movement, whereas limited space may represent an anatomical constraint in orthodontic planning.4

In addition to its orthodontic significance, the retromolar region is closely associated with the development and eruption of mandibular third molars. This region represents the anatomical site where third molars undergo mineralization, growth, and positional adjustment within the dental arch. The relationship between RMS and third molar crown width, commonly expressed as the RMS-to-crown width ratio (Ganss ratio), has been used as a radiographic parameter to describe posterior space conditions related to mandibular third molars.5,6 In early developmental stages, mandibular third molars are typically mesially inclined relative to the second molars, reflected by a larger mesiodistal angulation, or Beta angle.7 Changes in posterior space have been discussed in relation to the positional development of mandibular third molars on radiographs. Moreover, the possible relationship between mandibular third molars and late mandibular anterior crowding has been discussed, particularly under conditions of limited posterior space.8–10 In skeletal Class III patients, these considerations may be of particular interest because posterior space conditions are assessed within an already distinct craniofacial pattern.11

Therefore, assessment of retromolar morphological characteristics in skeletal Class III patients may help describe posterior space conditions in relation to mandibular third molar development. However, chronological age alone may not fully reflect biological maturation, as individuals of the same age can exhibit considerable variability in dental development.12 To address this limitation, biological markers such as dental age have been widely applied in clinical and research settings, including forensic science and orthodontics.13,14 The method introduced by Demirjian et al. provides a standardized approach to dental age estimation based on mineralization stages, with demonstrated reliability and reproducibility.15,16 Previous studies have reported that mandibular third molar mineralization occurs over a broad age range, particularly between 8 and 24 years.17 This period also coincides with critical stages of craniofacial growth and skeletal maturation. 18 Given this extended developmental window, the dental age of the mandibular third molar may serve as a useful developmental reference when describing retromolar morphology. Because the mandibular third molar develops within the retromolar region itself, its mineralization stage may provide a tooth-specific developmental reference for evaluating local posterior space morphology beyond chronological age alone. In addition, both dental age and retromolar morphology can be readily assessed using panoramic radiographs, which are routinely employed in clinical orthodontic assessment.

Despite these considerations, the relationship between dental age of mandibular third molars and retromolar morphological characteristics in skeletal Class III patients remains insufficiently characterized. Accordingly, this study aimed to evaluate the association between dental age of mandibular third molars and retromolar morphological parameters, including RMS, Ganss ratio, and Beta angle, in skeletal Class III patients using panoramic radiographs.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted at the Faculty of Dentistry, University of Medicine and Pharmacy at Ho Chi Minh City. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Medicine and Pharmacy at Ho Chi Minh City (Approval No. 763/HÐÐÐ-ÐHYD). The study sample consisted of panoramic radiographs obtained from patients diagnosed with skeletal Class III malocclusion. The diagnosis was based on clinical examination and confirmed by lateral cephalometric analysis, with an ANB angle of ≤0°. Eligible subjects were aged between 8 and 24 years, corresponding to the active developmental period of mandibular third molars as reported in previous radiographic studies.19 To ensure accurate assessment of retromolar morphological characteristics, inclusion criteria were defined according to previously established measurement protocols.5 Specifically, subjects were required to meet the following criteria: (1) presence of mandibular third molars at Demirjian developmental stages D to H; (2) intact first and second mandibular molars without caries or significant occlusal wear; and (3) proper alignment of posterior teeth to allow reliable identification of the posterior occlusal plane (OP). Exclusion criteria included: (1) buccolingual displacement of mandibular third molars; (2) craniofacial anomalies, such as cleft lip and palate, Pierre Robin sequence, or Apert syndrome; (3) presence of jaw lesions or active infection in the region of interest; and (4) history of trauma involving the head, face, or neck. Panoramic radiographs with technical errors, including improper head positioning, inappropriate exposure settings, or compromised image quality affecting visualization of mandibular third molars, were also excluded. All radiographs were obtained by trained and licensed radiographic technicians using standardized imaging procedures.

Sample size

The required sample size for correlation analysis was estimated using Fisher’s Z transformation, based on a two-sided significance level of 0.05 (α = 0.05), a statistical power of 80% (β = 0.20), and an anticipated correlation coefficient of 0.5. This approach is commonly used to determine the minimum sample size required to detect a moderate association. A total of 157 eligible subjects were ultimately included in the analysis.

Data collection

From the database of the Faculty of Odonto-Stomatology, University of Medicine and Pharmacy at Ho Chi Minh City, nearly 3500 patient visits for orthodontic examination and treatment had complete records, including treatment forms and diagnostic radiographs, at the time of data collection. Using skeletal Class III malocclusion as the selection criterion, the investigator screened the records to confirm that the patients had been clinically diagnosed with skeletal Class III malocclusion and subsequently performed digital tracing to verify the diagnosis. As a result, 363 patients met the initial screening criteria, corresponding to 10.4% of the total records. Further screening excluded 58 records without mandibular third molars available for evaluation, 26 records outside the eligible age range, 10 records with insufficient image quality for developmental stage assessment, 12 records with buccally or lingually inclined third molar buds, 7 records with craniofacial anomalies including Down syndrome or cleft lip/palate, 16 records with extensive carious destruction of the posterior teeth that precluded assessment of retromolar morphology, and 77 records in which the mandibular third molars had not yet reached Demirjian stage D. Ultimately, 157 records satisfied all inclusion criteria and were included in the study. The sample selection process was conducted by two trained orthodontic specialists, and in cases of uncertainty, two additional specialists reviewed the records and made the final decision by consensus. All eligible records were anonymized and assigned unique identification codes to ensure confidentiality of personal information. Baseline variables, including chronological age and sex, were recorded for subsequent analysis. Data collection and handling procedures were standardized to ensure consistency and accuracy.

Lateral cephalometric analysis

Skeletal Class III malocclusion was initially diagnosed based on clinical examination and further confirmed by lateral cephalometric analysis. The ANB angle was measured on lateral cephalometric radiographs, with ANB ≤0°used as the diagnostic criterion. All measurements were performed using WebCeph software (version 1.0.3; AssembleCircle Corp., Seongnam-si, Gyeonggi-do, Republic of Korea) and were independently reviewed by experienced orthodontists to ensure measurement reliability.

Panoramic radiographic analysis

The mineralization stage of mandibular third molars was assessed by comparing panoramic radiographs with the standard reference images of the Demirjian classification system (1973).15 In this study, dental age was defined according to the Demirjian developmental stage of the mandibular third molar and was analyzed as an ordinal developmental variable rather than a converted age value in years. Previous studies on mandibular third molar development have provided reference data for dental age assessment across different populations.12,17,20 In addition, symmetrical development between left and right teeth has been reported, supporting the use of a single representative tooth for analysis.21 Therefore, the left mandibular third molar was selected as the representative tooth for analysis. When the developmental stage fell between two consecutive stages, the earlier stage was assigned, as each stage requires complete attainment of specific developmental features (Fig. 1). For morphologic assessment, the outlines of the mandibular first molar, second molar, third molar, and the anterior border of the mandibular ramus were manually traced on orthodontic acetate tracing paper using a 0.5-mm mechanical pencil and a radiographic viewing box. Based on these tracings, RMS, the mesiodistal width of the mandibular third molar crown (MDW), the Ganss ratio, and the mesiodistal angulation between the long axes of the second and third molars (Beta angle) were recorded (Fig. 2). Measurements of RMS and the Ganss ratio were performed from Demirjian stage D onward, as crown formation up to the cemento-enamel junction is complete at this stage. The Beta angle was measured from stage F, when the furcation becomes radiographically identifiable, allowing reliable determination of the tooth axis. After tracing, the acetate sheets were digitized to generate measurement templates, and all measurements were performed using AutoCAD software (version 2020; Autodesk, Inc., San Francisco, CA, USA) (Fig. 3).

Data analysis and reliability assessment

The investigator underwent training in dental age assessment under the supervision of experienced clinicians and completed a competency test using 100 panoramic radiographs. To assess intra-examiner reliability, weighted Cohen’s kappa coefficient was used to assess dental age, whereas the intraclass correlation coefficient was used to assess continuous variables, including retromolar space, mesiodistal width of the mandibular third molar crown, the Ganss ratio, and the Beta angle. Measurements were repeated on 28 panoramic radiographs after a 2-week interval, and the corresponding reliability values are presented in Table 1. All recorded variables are summarized in Table 2. Data were entered into Microsoft Excel (version 2013; Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) and analyzed using Stata software (version 14.2; StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean and standard deviation for each Demirjian stage. The Shapiro—Wilk test was used to assess data normality. Differences between sexes were evaluated using independent t tests or Mann—Whitney U tests, as appropriate. For statistical analysis, dental age was treated as an ordinal developmental variable based on Demirjian stages D to H. Pearson or Spearman correlation analyses were performed to examine the associations between dental age and retromolar morphological characteristics.

Results

Sample characteristics

The study sample comprised 157 subjects, including 52 males and 105 females. The mean age was 17.85 ± 3.58 years in males and 17.57 ± 3.43 years in females. The mean ANB angle was −2.56° ± 2.27° in males and −2.27° ± 1.99° in females. Welch’s t-test revealed no statistically significant differences between sexes in mean age (P = 0.650) or mean ANB angle (P = 0.433), indicating comparable distributions of chronological age and skeletal discrepancy between the two groups.

Retromolar morphological characteristics by mandibular third molar developmental stages

Mandibular third molar development progressed from Demirjian stage D to stage H, with the mean chronological age increasing from 12.99 ± 1.72 years at stage D to 15.18 ± 1.91 years at stage E, 17.44 ± 2.17 years at stage F, 19.83 ± 1.86 years at stage G, and 20.75 ± 1.58 years at stage H. The mean RMS increased progressively across stages, from 5.59 ± 3.02 mm at stage D to 7.22 ± 2.70 mm at stage E, 7.99 ± 2.61 mm at stage F, 8.47 ± 3.40 mm at stage G, and 9.18 ± 2.56 mm at stage H. A similar increasing pattern was observed for the Ganss ratio, with mean values of 0.45 ± 0.24, 0.58 ± 0.23, 0.63 ± 0.20, 0.66 ± 0.29, and 0.70 ± 0.23 for stages D through H, respectively. The mean MDW values were 12.40 ± 1.14 mm at stage D, 12.78 ± 0.94 mm at stage E, 12.62 ± 0.86 mm at stage F, 13.03 ± 1.24 mm at stage G, and 13.19 ± 1.18 mm at stage H. In contrast, the Beta angle, which was measurable from stage F onward, decreased from 35.82 ± 25.76° at stage F to 31.70 ± 30.70° at stage G and 24.80 ± 27.81° at stage H. These findings indicate stage-dependent changes in retromolar morphology associated with mandibular third molar development (Table 3).

Differences in retromolar morphological characteristics between sexes

No statistically significant differences were observed between males and females in RMS, the Ganss ratio, or the Beta angle (Table 4). However, these non-significant findings should be interpreted with caution because the sample was imbalanced by sex, with a smaller number of males than females, which may have reduced the statistical power to detect subtle sex-related differences.

Correlation between dental age of mandibular third molars and retromolar morphological characteristics

Dental age of mandibular third molars showed statistically significant moderate positive correlations with RMS and the Ganss ratio (P < 0.001). In contrast, the association between dental age and the Beta angle was weak and did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.479). Among the retromolar morphological variables, RMS and the Ganss ratio demonstrated a strong positive correlation (r = 0.97). The Beta angle showed moderate negative correlations with both RMS and the Ganss ratio (r = −0.36), indicating an inverse relationship between molar angulation and posterior space conditions (Fig. 4).

Discussion

In skeletal Class III malocclusion, the relationship between mandibular third molar development and retromolar morphology is of interest because both factors can be assessed on routine radiographs and may provide complementary information regarding posterior space conditions. In the present study, retromolar space increased from 5.59 mm at stage D to 9.18 mm at stage H, and a moderate positive correlation was observed between mandibular third molar developmental stage and RMS. These findings indicate that more advanced third molar developmental stages were associated with greater posterior space measurements in this study population.

The observed pattern may be interpreted in the context of established concepts of mandibular morphology. Björk22 and Enlow and Harris23 described remodeling of the mandibular ramus and body as part of craniofacial development. Although the present cross-sectional design does not permit direct evaluation of longitudinal skeletal change, the increase in RMS across developmental stages observed in this study suggests that retromolar morphology and third molar developmental status are radiographically associated in skeletal Class III patients.

A similar pattern was identified for the Ganss ratio. In the present sample, the ratio increased from 0.45 at stage D to 0.70 at stage H and showed a positive correlation with mandibular third molar developmental stage. Previous studies have used the RMS-to-crown width ratio as a radiographic parameter in the assessment of third molar eruption-related conditions.5,6 In the current study, the increase in this ratio across stages suggests that later developmental stages were associated with relatively more favorable posterior space conditions. However, even at stage H, the mean Ganss ratio remained below 1. This finding indicates that the available retromolar space was still limited relative to crown width in many subjects, and therefore the radiographic space conditions observed in this skeletal Class III sample may not necessarily be sufficient for uncomplicated eruption. This interpretation is in line with previous reports showing that limited RMS and unfavorable third molar position may be associated with reduced arch space and mandibular anterior crowding.24

The Beta angle showed a different pattern. Although the mean value decreased from 35.82° at stage F to 24.80° at stage H, its association with developmental stage was weak and not statistically significant. Previous studies have evaluated the mesiodistal angulation of the mandibular third molar as a radiographic characteristic related to third molar position and eruption-related conditions.25–28 In the present study, the Beta angle demonstrated moderate negative correlations with RMS and the Ganss ratio, indicating that smaller angulation values tended to occur together with greater posterior space measurements. This finding supports an association between molar angulation and retromolar morphology, but it should be interpreted cautiously because the present analysis was based on cross-sectional radiographic observations rather than longitudinal follow-up of tooth position.

Haavikko et al.26 and Hattab28 reported that lower angulation values may be associated with a greater likelihood of spontaneous uprighting and eruption. In our sample, however, the mean Beta angle at stage H remained relatively high. Together with the Ganss ratio findings, this result suggests that some patients may still present with radiographic conditions that are not clearly favorable for eruption. Nevertheless, because the present study did not evaluate actual eruption outcomes or longitudinal positional changes, these findings should be interpreted as radiographic associations rather than direct predictors of future eruption.

Although males and females may differ in craniofacial dimensions,29 no statistically significant sex-related differences were identified in RMS, the Ganss ratio, or the Beta angle in the present sample. Nevertheless, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because the male and female groups were unequal in size, and the study may have had limited power to detect small sex-related differences.

Overall, the present findings indicate that more advanced mandibular third molar developmental stages were associated with greater RMS and higher Ganss ratios in skeletal Class III patients, whereas the association with Beta angle was less pronounced. These results support the view that third molar developmental stage and retromolar morphology are related radiographically, and that considering both may be useful when describing posterior space conditions in this patient group. However, the clinical implications should be limited to radiographic assessment, because the present data do not directly establish eruption prognosis, treatment effects, or skeletal growth dynamics.

This study has several limitations. First, it was based on two-dimensional panoramic radiographs, which may not fully capture the three-dimensional spatial relationships of the retromolar region. Second, the retrospective cross-sectional design only allows assessment of associations at a single time point and does not permit inference regarding temporal developmental changes or causal relationships. Nevertheless, panoramic radiography remains a widely available diagnostic tool in orthodontic practice, and the present findings provide a practical radiographic description of the relationship between mandibular third molar developmental stage and retromolar morphology in skeletal Class III patients. More advanced mandibular third molar developmental stages were associated with greater retromolar space and higher Ganss ratios, whereas the association with Beta angle was weaker. These findings may provide supplementary information for describing posterior space conditions in skeletal Class III patients, but further longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether these radiographic associations are linked to actual eruption outcomes.
