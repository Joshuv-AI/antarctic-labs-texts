Parenting style profiles in families of children with ADHD using a person-centred approach
Authors: Marisol Cueli, Natalia Martín, Nerea Quiroga, Ada Mesa-Prado, Paloma González-Castro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAttention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) has been linked to problematic parent-child interactions in the family setting, which can influence the development of the disorder in terms of how it progresses and the types and severity of symptoms. The present study aims to determine parenting style profiles in a sample of families of children with ADHD and the relationship between those profiles and ADHD-severity and type of presentation (both reported by parents). A total of 189 parents of children with ADHD aged 6 to 16 years old participated in the study. Profiles of parenting styles were produced by considering a combination of variables associated with the two dimensions of parent–child interactions, responsiveness and demandingness (through the variables: warmth-communication, criticism-rejection, inductive, rigid, and indulgent discipline). We conducted Latent Profile Analysis and identified four parenting style profiles: authoritarian, authoritative, permissive-neglectful and mixed. The most common parenting style was an authoritative style. In contrast, the least common profiles were the authoritarian and the mixed styles characterized by higher levels of criticism-rejection. Profiles were examined with an exploratory and descriptive approach in terms of ADHD-Severity and ADHD-Type of presentation. In conclusion, we highlight the importance of defining the distinct parenting practices based on the dimensions of responsiveness and demandingness, and the importance of analysing family interactions in relation to ADHD in children, although more research is still needed related to the type, and especially the severity, of ADHD symptoms.Keywords: Parental styles, ADHD, Person-centred approach, Affect-communication, Criticism-rejection

Introduction

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is one of the most common conditions in childhood and adolescence (Polanczyk et al., 2014), characterized by developmentally inappropriate inattention, impulsivity and hyperactivity. In a relatively recent meta-analysis, Vasileva et al. (2021) estimated a prevalence of 4.3% for ADHD in children under 7 years old. The estimated prevalence in one region of Spain was reported at 5.5%, with a high proportion of comorbid presentation and mild severity (Canals-Sans et al., 2021).

The American Psychiatric Association’s Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (APA, 2022) differentiates three types of ADHD presentation—predominantly hyperactive/impulsive, predominantly inattentive, and combined—and three levels of severity—mild, moderate or severe. Mild ADHD is when there are few, if any, symptoms in excess of those required to make the diagnosis, and the symptoms result in no more than minor impairments in social or occupational functioning. Moderate severity indicates that symptoms or functional impairment between “mild” and “severe” are present. Severe ADHD identifies children exhibiting many symptoms in excess of those needed to make the diagnosis, or several symptoms that are particularly severe, or for whom the symptoms result in marked impairment in social or occupational functioning.

The severity, prognosis, and persistence of ADHD symptoms are affected by many factors including comorbid symptomatology (Arildskov et al., 2022; Vos et al., 2022), co-occurrence of emotional symptomatology (Soler-Gutiérrez et al., 2023), and family characteristics (Brinksma et al., 2021; Jendreizik et al., 2023). Sasser et al. (2016) examined the progression of ADHD symptomatology with 413 children in a longitudinal study and found that it did not persist during adolescence in every case, but instead there was greater severity and persistence of symptoms in those who had more hyperactive or aggressive behaviours or those whose parents used inconsistent parenting practices, highlighting the possible effect of parenting styles on the progression of the disorder.

Parenting styles

Parenting style can be conceptualized as a constellation of attitudinal and behavioural dimensions that define the parenting patterns scheme and is reflected in different types of child-rearing which form the overall context for child socialization (Grusec & Hastings, 2015).

One of the most influential categorizations of parenting practices is the typology from Baumrind (1971) and Maccoby and (Martin (1983), in which parenting styles are defined by the combination of the two dimensions: responsiveness and demandingness. These two dimensions (also called warmth and control, respectively) are theoretically orthogonal or unrelated (Ertema et al., 2025; Jacome-Mora et al., 2025). Responsiveness refers to parental warmth, involvement in and sensitivity to supporting and reinforcing the developing child's individuality (Garcia-Ros & Garcia, 2026; Steinberg et al., 1994). In contrast, demandingness refers to parental strictness, imposition and efforts to make the child conform to societal and family expectations (Garcia-Ros & Garcia, 2026; Lamborn et al., 1991). Considering combinations of these dimensions, authoritative parents are characterized by high responsiveness and high demandingness, authoritarian by low responsiveness and high demandingness, indulgent (permissive) parents by high responsiveness and low demandingness, and neglectful parents by low responsiveness and low demandingness (Alcaide et al., 2025; Garcia et al., 2019; Lamborn et al., 1991; Steinberg et al., 1994).

Parenting practices can be examined based on both responsiveness and demandingness (see Martinez-Escudero et al., 2020). Warmth-communication represents parental responsiveness. On similar lines, induction, which is a form of reasoning that parents use to explain the meaning of rules as well as to correct children for their misbehaviour, is characterized by high responsiveness (Fuentes et al., 2022; Hoffman, 2000). Both practices are associated with authoritative and indulgent (permissive) parents. In contrast, rigidity represents high demandingness and criticism-rejection reflects overall low responsiveness but also high strictness, a parenting practice that is common to authoritarian parents, who also use guilt (i.e., psychological control) as a form of correction (Marciano et al., 2020; Teng & Chen, 2026). Permissiveness represents low demandingness and is common to both indulgent and neglectful parenting styles, although indulgent parents are responsive, whereas neglectful parents are not (Garcia et al., 2019; Steinberg et al., 1991).

Contextual perspective

These parenting styles can be examined from a contextual or situational perspective (Darling & Steinberg, 1993; Garcia et al., 2019; Martinez et al., 2020). Normative parents seek to transmit social rules and values to their children and modify their responses to their child's actions, in line with transactional models, which propose that parents and children exert reciprocal influences on one another (see Cummings & Davies, 1999; Patterson, 1982). When children misbehave, parents can be affectionate and close (i.e., responsive), or they can lack responsiveness (Garcia et al., 2019; Martinez et al., 2020). Responsive parents may reason with misbehaving children (in this case, induction), a practice common to both authoritative and indulgent parents. They might also impose punishment by revoking privileges or behavioural control, which defines authoritative parents; or they may avoid punishment and refrain from reprimanding their children for their failures, which defines indulgent parents (Darling & Steinberg, 1993). On the other hand, less responsive parents may be cold and distant and fail to explain the rules and their meaning, a practice common to neglectful and authoritarian parents. However, those less responsive parents may make misbehaving children feel bad about the consequences of their actions by inducing feelings of guilt (i.e., psychological control), indicating authoritarian parents; or they might do nothing and not impose any punishments, suggesting neglectful parents (Harma et al., 2025; Teng & Chen, 2026).

Research in typically-developing children generally confirms that “positive” parenting dimensions, such as high warmth and authoritative control, are associated with better social, emotional, and academic functioning, while “negative” parenting dimensions, including low warmth and authoritarian control, are linked to problematic social, emotional, and behavioural adjustment, and poorer academic functioning in the child (see Bhide et al., 2017; Kaniušonytė & Laursen, 2021; Rothenberg et, al., 2020; Rothenberg et al., 2022; Sun & Wang, 2026; Vasiou et al., 2023). In a review of 1015 studies on associations of parenting dimensions and styles with internalizing symptoms in children and adolescents, Pinquart (2017) concluded that parental warmth, behavioural control, autonomy granting, and authoritative parenting showed very small to small negative concurrent and longitudinal associations with internalizing symptoms. In contrast, harsh control, psychological control, authoritarian, and, in part, neglectful parenting were associated with higher levels of internalizing symptoms (Pinquart, 2017).

Parenting styles and ADHD

In the context of ADHD, challenging child behaviours (hyperactivity, impulsivity and/or inattention) can make daily life more complex and represent an added difficulty for parents compared to the population without the disorder. The review from Johnston and Mash (2001) indicated that the presence of ADHD in children is associated to varying degrees with disturbances in family and marital functioning, disrupted parent–child relationships, specific patterns of parental thinking about child behaviour and reduced parenting self-efficacy, and increased levels of parenting stress and parental psychopathology, particularly when ADHD is comorbid with behavioural problems.

Past studies have shown that families of children with ADHD present higher levels of “negative” parenting dimensions (e.g., less affection/responsiveness, more coercion, and negative control) than non-ADHD controls (Montes & Montes, 2021; Wymbs et al., 2017). Bhide et al. (2017) examined whether parenting style was related to social, emotional, and academic functioning in school-aged children with ADHD, and whether these relationships were different for children without ADHD. Their results showed that parents reported less consistency and more anger in the ADHD group than in non-ADHD control groups, with no differences in warmth (Bhide et al., 2017).

In a longitudinal study, Brinksma et al. (2021) analysed whether the way early adolescents perceived their parents’ parenting styles would predict subsequent ADHD symptom trajectories during adolescence. They worked with a sample of 1730 adolescents and their results showed that children’s perceived parental rejection influenced the persistence of ADHD symptomatology. In a systematic review and meta-analysis, Jendreizik et al. (2023) examined the association between familial factors and symptom severity in children with ADHD. They looked at 43 studies in their meta-analysis and found that familial psychosocial factors (socioeconomic status, single-parent family, broken parental partnership, critical life events, parental ADHD, parental affective psychopathology, parental mental health, parenting stress, and negative parenting practices) demonstrated small but significant associations with symptom severity in children with ADHD. The study indicated that the proportion of the variance in child ADHD symptom severity that could be explained by familial factors was between 1% and 6%.

In contrast, in a study with two-hundred 10–13 year-old Polish children with ADHD, Walenista et al. (2023) found that the quality of family communication and satisfaction did not predict executive functioning in children with ADHD, and that ADHD severity did not play a mediating role (between family functioning style and quality of executive functioning) in boys or girls.

Johnston and Mash (2001) reported differences in previous results in the literature following a review aimed at evaluating what is known about family characteristics associated with ADHD. They noted inconsistent findings in existing studies and concluded that there was a lack of research about the developmental progression of ADHD or the processes that underlie observed associations of family characteristics and childhood ADHD.

Person-centred approach

One reason for the inconsistent findings from previous studies may be because the variables associated with parental styles can interact between each other in different patterns. Lee and Park (2026) highlighted that studies on children and parents have increasingly adopted a person-centred approach to identify patterns of parenting styles. For example, García-Linares et al. (2014a) analyzed parenting styles of 840 Secondary School students (using cluster analysis). Their results indicated four profiles (authoritative, permissive, affective-authoritarian and negligent), and showed that children reported lower levels of stress when they identified both parents as having an authoritative style.

Unlike variable-centred approaches, which examine associations between variables, person-centred approaches focus on identifying subgroups of individuals who have similar levels of one or more variables, and who can be differentiated using other sets of variables in this regard (Canedo et al., 2022). In addition, as Howard and Hoffman (2018) emphasized, the person-centred approach allows for a more precise and specific understanding of the relationship between each subpopulation and other variables. Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) is one person-centred approach that is particularly useful for exploratory research questions and is increasingly being applied in studies on parenting (see Lee & Park, 2026). Lee and Park (2026) examined latent profiles of foster parents' parenting styles and their associations with internalising and externalising behaviour problems and smartphone dependency in children in foster care. They identified three distinct parenting styles (controlling, permissive and ambivalent) and reported that children of controlling parents were at higher risk of internalising problems.

The present study

Despite the interest in person-centred studies in relation to parenting styles, to the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have addressed parenting profiles in ADHD. In this context and based on studies that identified an impact of parenting style on ADHD (e.g., Bhide et al., 2017; Brinksma et al., 2021; Jendreizik et al., 2023), the present study used a person-centred perspective and aimed to determine parenting style profiles in a sample of families of children with ADHD, along with the relationship between those profiles and ADHD severity and presentation type, using an exploratory and descriptive approach.

To identify the parent style profiles, we considered the combination of variables associated with the two dimensions of parent–child interactions repeatedly identified as influencing child development— responsiveness and demandingness (Baumrind, 1991, 1996). Specifically, in line with Garcia-Linares et al. (2014), the study examined the variables warmth-communication, criticism-rejection, and the three main ways to establish rules (inductive discipline, rigid discipline, and indulgent discipline).

Our hypothesis was that we would find at least three parental style profiles based on the levels of warmth-communication and criticism-rejection and how they are combined with the ways rules are set. We hypothesized that one profile would be characterized by high criticism-rejection, considering previous research in which ADHD has been associated with “negative” parenting dimensions (e.g., Bhide et al., 2017; Brinksma et al., 2021; Montes & Montes, 2021).

Understanding parenting styles and their relationship with ADHD is a key element in improving children’s well-being. In addition, providing knowledge about identifying the most beneficial and most detrimental parenting practices may allow health, education and psychology professionals to offer tailored strategies that promote more favourable environments for these children and their families and a better quality of life.

The results may contribute to the design of more comprehensive educational programs and psychoeducational interventions, not limited to addressing ADHD symptomatology but which also include parental training as preventive and management strategies in order to promote family environments that are more conducive to child development. According to Johnston and Chronis-Tuscano (2015), interventions adopting family-centred perspectives can play a significant role in improving parents’ knowledge and abilities to effectively interact with their ADHD children, ultimately leading to improved family functioning and dynamics.

Method

Participants

The participants in the study comprised 189 parents of children with ADHD aged between 6 and 16 years old (M = 11.42; SD = 2.59). The mean age of the fathers was 46.62 (SD = 7.30) and the mean age of the mothers was 45.23 (SD = 6.46). According to parents' reports of their children's sex assigned at birth, just under a third of the children were girls (n = 57; 30.2%) and 69.8% were boys (n = 132).

The criteria for participation in the study were: (1) Families of children aged 6 to 18 years old with ADHD who were part of associations or groups providing support to families of children with ADHD; (2) Previous diagnosis made by a qualified professional, accompanied by the corresponding diagnostic report; (3) Responses given to all questionnaire items.

Based on the parents’ information, almost half of the children’s ADHD diagnoses (43.9%) were from clinical psychologists, 8.2% from educational psychologists, 3.1% from paediatricians, and 44.9% from neuro-paediatricians.

In terms of parent-reported severity, 18.5% of the children had mild ADHD (n = 35), 52.9% had moderate ADHD (n = 100), and 28.6% had severe ADHD (n = 54). In terms of presentation type, 21.2% of the children had a hyperactive/impulsive diagnosis (n = 40), 32.8% an inattentive diagnosis (n = 62), and 42.9% a combined presentation (n = 81). Six families indicated that they did not know the type of presentation.

Parents also reported that 24.9% of the children presented a comorbidity (n = 47), associated with learning disorders (n = 29) or conduct disorder (n = 18). In terms of pharmacological treatment, 150 families (79.4%) indicated that their children were receiving medication, with the remaining 39 reporting that they were not (20.6%).

Instruments

Initially, information provided by the families was collected. This included who made the ADHD diagnosis (clinical psychologist, educational psychologists, paediatricians, or neuro-paediatricians), the type of ADHD presentation, the severity of the disorder based on the assessment from the diagnosing professional, presence of comorbidities, pharmacological treatment, and who completed the questionnaire (father, mother, or both together).

Data was collected using the Warmth Scale and the Rules-Demands Scale (Bersabé et al., 2001), which were designed to examine relationships between parents and children. In the present study we used the parental version, in which parents are presented with a total of 48 items about parenting styles and asked how often they feel that they engage in the indicated behaviours. Responses are given on a five-point Likert scale (1-never, 2-not very often, 3-sometimes, 4-often, 5-always or almost always). The instrument was completed by mothers in 81% of cases, by fathers in 7.4%, and by both parents together in 11.6% of cases.

The Warmth Scale has 20 items, of them, 10 items for warmth-communication (e.g., I talk with my child about issues that are important to him/her; I am warm and affectionate toward my child) and 10 items for criticism-rejection (e.g., I get angry with my child over almost anything he/she does; I take every opportunity to criticize my child). High scores in warmth-communication correspond to parents exhibiting higher levels of affection, warmth, interest, and communication with their children. High scores in criticism-rejection are associated with anger, lack of confidence, and lack of acceptance.

The Rules-Demands Scale is made up of three factors: inductive discipline (10 items; e.g., I explain to my child why he/she should follow the rules), rigid discipline (10 items; e.g., I require my child to follow the rules even if he/she does not understand them), and indulgent discipline (8 items; e.g., If my child disobeys me, I do not take any action), referring to the ways that parents set rules and make sure they are followed. Parents who inductively establish rules show more affection, interest, and communication with their children, as well as less criticism and rejection. Rigid rule-setting is associated with parents expressing criticism, rejection and lack of confidence in their children. Indulgent rule-setting indicates parents who encourage their children’s participation in creating rules, who do not use punishments, and who generally tolerate inappropriate behaviour, finding it difficult to set limits.

In terms of reliability, the original version of the scale had adequate indices of internal consistency, with Cronbach alpha coefficients between 0.60 and 0.90 (Bersabé et al., 2001). In our sample, the values for Cronbach’s Alpha were also adequate (warmth-communication α = .84, criticism-rejection α = .84, inductive α = .82, rigid α = .75, indulgent α = .72).

Procedure

In Spain there are 59 registered associations and groups that provide support to families of children with ADHD. Contact was made with 42 of these associations (the remaining 17 could not be reached) by email initially and then by telephone, to present the aims of the study. We held a meeting on the Microsoft Teams platform with members of the groups who had shown an interest. In Spain, ADHD associations provide information, education, and support to families of children with ADHD. Access to these organizations requires that children have received an ADHD diagnosis from a qualified professional, thereby ensuring that participants had undergone an evaluation and had a diagnostic report.

Although participants were required to have a diagnostic report issued by a qualified professional, this documentation was not provided to the research team, as it had already been verified and retained by the ADHD associations as part of their membership requirements.

A total of 19 associations agreed to collaborate in the study (yielding an organizational participation rate of 45.2%) by sending families a link to a web page containing information about the study along with the questionnaire. Before completing the questionnaires, the participants were informed that their responses would be anonymous and they were asked to agree for their results to be used for research, ensuring informed consent from each subject.

The study was performed in compliance with the World Medical Association code of ethics (Declaration of Helsinki), reflecting the ethical principles for research with human beings (Morris, 2013) and was approved by the Institution’s Ethical Committee.

Data analysis

Latent Profile Analysis (LPA; Lanza et al., 2003) was used to obtain the latent variables for grouping participants into classes based on characteristics of their parenting styles: warmth-communication and criticism-rejection, and rules and demands. Mplus v.8.10 (Muthén & Muthén, 2012) was used to determine which model best fit the data from a finite set of models, adding successive latent classes to the model (see Appendix A with syntax characteristics for Mplus). To determine the optimal number of classes, various models were evaluated using the Lo-Mendell-Rubin Adjusted Likelihood Ratio Test (LMRT; Lo et al., 2001), the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) and the BIC adjusted for the sample size (SSA-BIC), as well as the value of the entropy and the sample size of each subgroup. Lower values in AIC, BIC and SSA-BIC indicate more parsimonious models and better fit, while the p-value associated with the LMRT indicates whether the solution with more classes or less classes fits better (Nylund et al., 2007). Small classes (containing <5% of the sample) are typically considered spurious, a condition that is often associated with extracting too many profiles (Hipp & Bauer, 2006).

The relationship between the parenting styles profiles (latent classes) and ADHD severity and type of presentation, was assessed by exploratory and descriptive methods, using the chi-square test to analyse the distribution of participants across the profiles, and the z statistic for pairwise differences between groups (mild vs. moderate, mild vs. severe, moderate vs. severe; hyperactive/impulsive vs. inattentive, hyperactive/impulsive vs. combined, inattentive vs. combined) with confidence intervals of 95%.

Overall, the results indicated that (i) the warmth-communication and inductive dimensions exhibited the strongest positive correlation, suggesting that both practices represent positive forms of parental responsiveness; (ii) Warmth-communication and criticism-rejection were negatively associated with each other, suggesting that both elements represent parental responsiveness, through high levels in the case of warmth-communication and low levels in criticism-rejection; (iii) Criticism-rejection and rigidity were positively related, perhaps due to both parental practices sharing a pattern of high demandingness, which is typical of authoritarian parents and their psychological control; (iv) Indulgence (permissiveness) exhibited no significant relationship with warmth-communication, suggesting that permissiveness may be somewhat orthogonal to warmth (which may be more related to responsiveness) and more related to demandingness (at least weakly); (v) More unexpectedly, rigidity was positively related to indulgence—it seems that the stricter parents are, and the more they enforce rules, the more indulgent they also tend to be (see Table 2).

In general, the profiles can be described based on the levels of criticism-rejection and warmth-communication, with the way rules and demands are established providing additional differentiation. Profile 1 is characterized by high levels of criticism-rejection and very low levels of warmth-communication. This is combined with low inductive rule-setting (lack of reasoning and comprehension) indicating a parenting-style associated with an authoritarian profile.

Profile 2 is characterized by moderately low levels of criticism-rejection and high levels of warmth-communication, combined with moderate inductive rule-setting, in line with an authoritative parenting style.

Profile 3 combines moderate levels of criticism-rejection and low levels of warmth-communication with low inductive rule-setting, compatible with permissive-neglectful parents who can be moderately demanding but not responsive.

Finally, the fourth profile reflects high levels of criticism-rejection and warmth-communication and high scores in rigid and indulgent rule-setting reflecting a mixed parenting style.

Looking at the standardized scores (Table 5) and the profile interpretations, rigid and indulgent parenting variables were between normal ranges in profiles 1 to 3, indicating lower discriminating power. However, inductive rule-setting was congruent with levels of warmth-communication showing higher levels in those with more responsiveness.

Mild ADHD was more common in profile 4 (compared to its presence in other classes). Profile 4 indicates mixed practices combined with high warmth-communication and criticism-rejection and high scores in rigid and indulgent rule setting (well-established rules with reasoned limits and affection and tolerance for failure).

A higher percentage of parents of children with moderately-severe ADHD (compared to parents of children with mild or severe instances of the disorder) reported a parental style associated with profile 1 or authoritarian parenting. This profile is characterized by a high level of criticism-rejection and very low levels of warmth-communication, combined with low scores in inductive rule-setting (lack of reasoning and comprehension).

Parents of children with severe ADHD represented between 21.4% and 30.4% of parents in each profile, with the highest percentages in profiles 2 and 3. These two profiles are practically opposites, profile 2 is authoritative (29.4%) and profile 3 is permissive-neglectful (30.4%).

In terms of types of ADHD presentation, parents of children with predominantly hyperactive/impulsive ADHD were more represented in profile 4 followed by profile 1. Parents of children with predominantly inattentive ADHD were more common in profile 2, while the majority of the parents of children with combined ADHD appeared in profile 3.

In the case of diagnosis, differences in distribution were non-significant in classes 1 and 4. However, the differences were significant in classes 2 and 3 [Class 1: χ²(2) = 0.500, p = .779; Class 2: χ²(2) = 6.777, p = .034; Class 3: χ²(2) = 10.291, p = .006; Class 4: χ²(2) = 0.154, p = .926].

Discussion

The aim of this study was to identify different profiles of parenting styles in a sample of families with children with ADHD, as well as to determine the exploratory and descriptive relationship with ADHD severity and type of presentation. To do this, the study followed a person-centred approach, which allows such a highly complex reality to be addressed more comprehensively. Parenting style profiles were identified by considering the combination of variables associated with the two dimensions of parent-child interactions identified throughout the literature as relevant in child development: responsiveness and demandingness (Baumrind, 1991, 1996). More specifically, we considered the combination of the variables warmth-communication and criticism-rejection with the three main forms of rule- and demand-setting: inductive, rigid and indulgent control.

In essence, our results appear to emphasise how important it is to define the distinct parenting practices based on the two theoretically orthogonal dimensions (i.e., responsiveness and demandingness), giving rise to the parenting styles. Within the model of three parenting styles as categories (i.e., authoritative, authoritarian, and permissive), authoritative parenting is characterized by demandingness and responsiveness, authoritarian by demandingness but no responsiveness, and permissive by lack of demandingness. These styles were represented in the four profiles identified in the participating families and are compatible with the results reported by García-Linares et al. (2014b), who found (looking at children without ADHD) authoritative, permissive, affective-authoritarian and negligent profiles.

In our study, in families of children with ADHD, we found four profiles in accordance with our hypothesis. Profile 1 suggests a fundamentally authoritarian parenting style (demandingness but not responsiveness), characterized by high levels of criticism-rejection and low levels of warmth-communication. A second profile was associated with an authoritative style (demandingness and responsiveness), with moderately low levels of criticism-rejection and high levels of warmth-communication, as well as being characterized by inductive rule-setting (clear and reasoned limits and rules). The third profile was labelled permissive-neglectful (lack of demandingness and not responsive), combining moderate levels of criticism-rejection and low levels of warmth-communication with low levels of inductive rule-setting. Finally, the fourth profile indicated mixed practices, with high levels of criticism-rejection and warmth-communication and high scores in rigid and indulgent rule-setting. As Torío-López et al. (2008) highlighted, parenting styles are not fixed and stable, they can be mixed, combining different practices.

The most commonly reported parenting style by the parents in the sample was the authoritative style or profile 2. Previous studies have related this parenting style to less externalizing and internalizing problems, as well as to the promotion of children’s psychosocial development (Bagán et al., 2019; García-Linares et al., 2014a; Ruiz-Ortiz et al., 2017). In contrast, the least reported profiles were the authoritarian style (profile 1) and the mixed style (profile 4). Although we were able to detect two profiles characterized by high criticism-rejection—as hypothesized based on previous research (e.g., Bhide et al., 2017; Brinksma et al., 2021; Montes & Montes, 2021)—they were the two least common profiles in the sample.

In the literature, there is some disagreement in research on parenting styles related to children with ADHD (Johnston & Mash, 2001; Johnston et al., 2012). Some studies have reported that parents of children with ADHD adopt more punitive, negative parenting styles than parents of typically-developing children (Breaux & Harvey, 2019; Hawes et al., 2013). In this respect, authoritarian practices have been related to internalizing and externalizing problems in previous ADHD research (Stevens et al., 2019). Along similar lines, studies have also noted that these children’s mothers exhibit less affection and care, with the presence of more impaired relationships and less perceived parental support than in the case of typically-developing children (Chang & Gau, 2017). On the other hand, studies have also reported that parents of ADHD children use both authoritarian and permissive practices (Bhide et al., 2024; Stevens et al., 2019). Because these differences reported by previous studies might be related to the severity or the type of ADHD presentation, we included a descriptive analysis (in terms of ADHD-severity and ADHD-type of presentation) of the profiles we identified.

ADHD severity

Previous studies about ADHD severity have pointed to the involvement of family parenting practices in relation to the severity of the disorder (e.g., Bhide et al., 2017; Brinksma et al., 2021; Jendreizik et al., 2023). Although causal relationships have not been established, significant correlations have been highlighted, suggesting the need to consider the potential of these parenting practices in modulating the disorder’s symptomatology.

In our study, profile 4 (mixed style) included the most children with mild ADHD. Profile 1 (authoritarian style) had the most children with moderately severe ADHD, while profiles 2 (authoritative style), and particularly 3 (permissive-neglectful style), had the most children with severe ADHD. We can see that for both moderate and severe ADHD, there was a certain association with high levels of criticism-rejection and low warmth-communication. In this regard, our results are consistent with previous studies linking parenting styles and the severity of the disorder (e.g. Bhide et al., 2017; Brinksma et al., 2021; Jendreizik et al., 2023). For example, Johnston et al. (2012) found more authoritarian and controlling styles, with low warmth in families of children with high levels of ADHD symptomatology. Although our analysis is only descriptive, it indicates preliminary evidence of the association between the severity of ADHD and parenting practices involving criticism-rejection and low levels of warmth-communication.

However, it is also important to note that a notable number of families with severe ADHD severity reported an authoritative style (profile 2). This corresponds to a parenting style that supports and recognizes the child’s autonomy, with consistent, stable parenting practices, but without rigid and disproportionate control. It provides children with clear, specific information about what their parents expect, which allows them to engage in responsible and appropriate behaviour (Rodriguez-Meirinhos et al., 2020).

Type of ADHD presentation

Taking a descriptive and exploratory approach to look at the relationship between parenting style and the type of ADHD presentation, we found that the profiles with the highest presence of children presenting hyperactive/impulsive ADHD were the authoritarian (profile 1) and mixed (profile 4) profiles, although differences in terms of distribution were not significant. Parents in these two profiles reported high levels of criticism-rejection combined with low levels of warmth-communication in profile 1, but high responsiveness and the use of both indulgent and rigid rule-setting strategies in profile 4.

Given these results, it may be that parents of children with hyperactive/impulsive presentations—who frequently exhibit more behavioural problems—might respond by using more controlling parenting styles, such as the authoritarian style, with higher levels of criticism-rejection as well as more rigid and indulgent rule setting. In contrast, parents of children with inattentive ADHD are more represented using an authoritative style. This may be related to this presentation of ADHD having the lowest levels of externalizing behaviours and conduct problems, meaning that parents do not need to use such controlling styles to regulate their children’s behaviour. At the same time, these parents may engage in less stressful interactions, which may make them less self-critical of their parenting.

For the combined presentation of ADHD, we found a high percentage of parents reporting moderate levels of criticism-rejection and low levels of warmth-communication with a low score for inductive rule-setting, compatible with permissive-neglectful parents who can be moderately demanding but not responsive.

Some authors in previous studies have claimed that parents of children with ADHD rely on parenting styles characterized largely by permissiveness, while others have suggested that such parents tend to adopt a more authoritarian parenting style (see Muñoz Suazo et al., 2020). Our results—while very preliminary and based on exploratory analyses—suggest that these differences may, among other factors, be related to the type of ADHD presentation, highlighting the importance of considering the bidirectional influences between parents’ and children’s behaviours (which differ in the types of presentation) based on transactional models (Cummings & Davies, 1999; Patterson, 1982).

Practical implications

With this idea in mind, the present study highlights the importance of considering all of the elements surrounding children with ADHD, analyzing their context more comprehensively, and taking into account family interactions as well as how certain factors, such as excessive control, may negatively affect their development in ways that still need to be further explored in future research. Our study also highlights how important it is for interventions to consider families, promoting parenting styles that foster responsiveness (warmth and affection) and inductive approaches to rule-setting. High levels of parental responsiveness improve outcomes because children seem more likely to understand parental expectations and motivations. Furthermore, this focus on relational harmony and mutual understanding encourages children to voluntarily share personal disclosures with their parents (Garcia-Ros & Garcia, 2026; Palacios et al., 2022). This is why it is important to have effective parent training interventions that help reinforce the child's sense of family belonging and encourage them to see themselves as valued and appreciated members of the family unit (Ertema et al., 2025).

Parent training interventions that focus on modifying parenting practices have demonstrated effectiveness in reducing both ADHD symptoms and associated behavioural problems (Paiva et al., 2024). As Chronis et al. (2004) suggested, parents of children with ADHD should get behavioral parent training. However, the review by Chronis-Tuscano et al. (2017) indicated that behavioral parent training may be less effective in improving negative parenting when the parents also present with ADHD (which is relatively common).

In consequence, effective parent training programs and psychosocial interventions that produce positive effects on parents and family functioning are essential. Pachiti et al. (2023) examined the efficacy of “Child ViReal Support Program” for families with a child diagnosed with ADHD. Their results showed that after parents participated in the parenting training, they demonstrated reduced parenting stress, improved parental self-efficacy, and increased use of democratic parenting practices. Furthermore, they reported lower levels of children’s inattention and impulsivity/hyperactivity.

Limitations and future directions

Regardless of the study’s implications, it is important to note some of its limitations. Firstly, we did not consider children’s perceptions of parenting styles, which may be different to their parents’ (Authors, 2025). In addition, the most responses were given by mothers, which might have been affected by their own perspectives. Muñoz-Suazo et al. (2020) noted that mothers reported higher levels of warmth in terms of affection–communication responses and greater use of inductive disciplinary practices than fathers.

A second limitation relates to the type of measure used in the study. In line with previous research (e.g., Bhide et al., 2017; García-Linares et al., 2014b; Rothenerg et al., 2022), we used parent-reported information to measure parenting styles and children’s symptoms. However, parents’ perceptions may not be consistent with reality, or they may present negative or positive patterns in their responses. Future studies should incorporate observational measures to assess parenting practices and children’s symptoms. Despite that, the most common measure in previous studies is parents’ ratings for assessing both parenting style and ADHD symptoms, and Rothenberg et al. (2022) noted that major systematic reviews and meta-analyses have established that parent measures for the assessment of parental styles have demonstrated excellent reliability.

On the other hand, the analyses relating profiles to ADHD severity and type of presentation are exploratory and descriptive, which did not allow for consideration of potential confounders such as children’s age and sex, comorbidity, socioeconomic status, or responding parent.

Although this was a cross-sectional study—something that prevents us from drawing causal conclusions—the results represent a step closer to understanding the disorder and its great complexity. Future studies should seek to analyse both parents' and children's perceptions, analysing discrepancies between the two. At the same time, future studies may collect attention, hyperactive and impulsivity measures to analyse the link between profiles and severity and type of presentation in more detail, for example considering the three-step approaches in Mplus (Asparouhov & Muthén, 2014). Future longitudinal analyses would also be desirable in order to produce a more accurate picture of the results and the long-term implications.

Conclusions

This study sought to emphasise the importance of family interactions and provide knowledge related to ADHD in children. Results were consistent with previous lines of research, not only regarding the negative impact of criticism-rejection practices on ADHD in children, but also about the importance of parenting practices based on warmth and communication as possible protective factors in the progression of the disorder. Furthermore, the study reinforces how important it is to analyse parenting style profiles in parents of children with ADHD from a person-centred approach, and to go beyond analysis of individual variables and focus on how these parenting styles affect children’s characteristics, including attention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity, as well as other emotional variables associated with the disorder.
