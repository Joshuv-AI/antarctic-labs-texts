Healthcare professionals’ perspectives on implementing the H.O.M.E mobility intervention in acute care: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Lay Khoon Lau, Nien Xiang Tou, Yew Yoong Ding, Shi Min Chua, Hoon Woon Audrey Chia, Kar Yen Jane Ho, Russell Kuok Leong Yoong, Seok Mei Lim, Ying Hui Dina Ee, Weng Kin Lai, Rui Ping Marian Lee, Bing Kit Chan, Poh Choo Chan, Shu Yi Lydia Au, Michelle Jessica Pereira, Mei Ying Melanie Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOlder adults often experience reduced mobilisation during hospitalisation, contributing to functional decline and increased risk of adverse health outcomes. Early mobility interventions in acute care settings are therefore critical to preventing functional loss. This study explored healthcare professionals’ experiences and perceptions of the feasibility and acceptability of implementing a multicomponent, mobility-focused intervention - Help, Optimise and Mobilise Elders (H.O.M.E).MethodsThis is a convergent mixed-methods study in which semi-structured interviews were conducted with a purposive sampling of 18 healthcare professionals involved in the intervention. An inductive approach and thematic content analysis were employed. Quantitative surveys were administered to the 25 implementers to measure the acceptability and feasibility of the intervention. The data on fidelity, specifically adherence and dosage to the intervention was also collected.ResultsThe quantitative survey shows that the H.O.M.E intervention was generally acceptable, although preferences for specific components differed across professional groups. Feasibility was rated lower than acceptability. Three overarching themes were identified from the qualitative data: (a) additional workload and responsibilities; (b) enhanced professional experience; and (c) challenges arising from patients. Although the intervention improved care workflows by enhancing nurses’ understanding of patients’ conditions and engagement levels, its implementation feasibility was constrained by patient resistance, competing clinical duties, staffing limitations and variations in intervention exposure.ConclusionThe quantitative and qualitative data were largely convergent, with the exception of nurses’ perceptions regarding the feasibility of implementing frequent mobilisation. While the H.O.M.E intervention was recognised as beneficial for patients, its implementation was constrained by operational challenges. Adaptation of workforce could be a viable option to overcome some of these challenges. Additionally, identifying specific patient subgroups most likely to benefit from group therapy could help optimise implementation outcomes.Trial RegistrationThis study is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05484063).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07955-0.Keywords: Mobility-focused, Multicomponent, Implementation, Acceptability, Feasibility

Introduction

Functional decline associated with hospitalisation is a common phenomenon [1], particularly for those with cognitive impairment or malnutrition [2]. It affects about 30% of hospitalised older adults [3] and could occur as early as the first day of admission [4]. The effects of functional decline are not limited to a reduced ability to perform activities of daily living and instrumental activities of daily living [1] but also include increased risks of institutionalisation [3] and mortality [1, 3] among older adults upon discharged. Missed ambulation in hospitalised adults results in prolonged immobility, rapid declines in walking ability within two days, and multiple adverse physiological effects across body systems [5]. Hence, this highlights the importance of maintaining physical activity during hospitalisation [6].

Early inpatient mobilisation, for example, has demonstrated benefits in reducing iatrogenic complications and improving physical function [5, 7]. Beyond this, it also reduced depressive symptoms and yielded positive effects on the mood of older adults; thus, improving their quality of life [5]. Studies have also reported a reduction in length of stay [5, 8] for patients who received early mobilisation, which could in turn positively impact the healthcare resources to cater to the needs of the rising ageing population [9].

However, reducing the risk of delirium, an acute neuropsychiatric syndrome affecting a considerable proportion of hospitalised older adults, requires more than physical activity alone. Evidence increasingly supports the use of multicomponent interventions, within which cognitive stimulation or engagement presents a key therapeutic element [10–12].

Eat Walk Engage is a ward-based hospital programme designed to enhance the multidisciplinary implementation of age-friendly care principles, namely adequate nutrition and hydration, early and regular mobilisation, and meaningful cognitive and social engagement, with the aim of reducing hospital-associated complications among older inpatients [13, 14]. However, implementing this approach presents several challenges, such as blurring of professional roles in an interdisciplinary environment [14] and risk-averse practice due to fear of patient falls [1, 14–16]. In addition, there is often limited staff commitment to cognitive-stimulation activities, such as providing newspapers, magazines, or crossword and sudoku puzzles, because these tasks are not always perceived as therapeutic interventions but rather as housekeeping or diversionary duties [14].

Art therapy, a non-pharmacological intervention, has been recognised as a form of cognitive engagement that can contribute to the prevention of ill health, the promotion of wellbeing and the support of illness management and treatment across the lifespan [12, 17, 18]. Nonetheless, its implementation is not without challenges. Commonly reported barriers include limitations in staff competency, as well as constraints related to time and physical space in the hospital setting [19]. Training new facilitators to undertake the roles such as conducting art therapy introduces additional complexities, including staff turnover, maintaining programme momentum, demonstrating tangible outcomes and convincing stakeholders of the value of facilitator-led interventions in sustaining implementation [13].

In Singapore, public hospital wards in subsidised or general classes are relatively dense, typically accommodating six to eight beds per room, which means that the physical space available per patient is often constrained. Furthermore, Singapore’s nurse-to-population ratio stands at 8.3 nurses per 1,000 population [20], lower than the OECD average of 9.2 per 1,000 [21]. These contextual and cultural differences suggest that the transferability of multicomponent interventions, such as those incorporating cognitive or creative engagement to Asian settings like Singapore, requires careful evaluation [22]. Additionally, the incorporation of art activity within multicomponent programmes for older adults in acute care remains relatively underexplored. The evaluation of such implementation efforts therefore serves as an important preparatory step toward potential scale-up across acute healthcare systems.

Hence, this mixed-method study aimed to evaluate the implementation of a multicomponent, mobility-focused intervention – Help, Optimise and Mobilise Elders (H.O.M.E) for older adults in an acute care setting in Singapore. Specifically, this study explored healthcare professionals’ experiences as well as their perceptions of the acceptability and feasibility of implementing the intervention. This is part of a Type II hybrid effectiveness-implementation study where the results on the effectiveness and implementation cost will be reported separately.

Methods

Setting and participants

The H.O.M.E intervention was implemented in an acute geriatric ward of Ng Teng Fong General Hospital, a public hospital in Singapore, from August 2022 to August 2023. Only patients aged 75 years or older were admitted to this ward. A quasi-experimental study design was adopted. Two designated cubicles of the ward were assigned to the intervention and control arms respectively and each cubicle consists of 12 beds. Newly admitted patients were allocated a bed in the ward by an independent bed management unit of the hospital. The patients assigned to the beds in the two cubicles of the intervention and control arms were assessed for their suitability to take part in this study by their respective medical consultants and subsequently invited to take part in this study. The exclusion criteria for the patients were vancomycin-resistant enterococcus status, requiring droplet or airborne precaution, critically ill or haemodynamically unstable, non-ambulant patients, those with advanced dementia (FAST 7 dementia) or fulfilling direct admission to another subspecialty unit.

A total of 81 patients (participants) were recruited (42 intervention; 39 control). Seventy-six patients (94%) completed the post-intervention assessments (38 per group). During hospitalisation, four intervention patients (9.5%) and one control patient (2.6%) did not complete the intervention. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants (patients and implementers) prior to enrolment. For patients with diagnosed dementia or mild cognitive impairment, informed consent was obtained from their legally authorised representative. In addition, assent was obtained from patients with mild cognitive impairment when possible. All consent procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study obtained ethics approval from the Singapore National Healthcare Group Domain Specific Review Board (No.: 2021/01147).

H.O.M.E intervention and implementation strategies

The H.O.M.E intervention (NCT05484063, 1 August 2022) was developed by a multidisciplinary team consists of geriatricians, nurses, physiotherapists, an occupational therapist and an art therapist. It consisted of pre-mobilisation assessments, namely GERI Care Bundle and the multicomponent interventions (frequent mobilisation, group art activity and physiotherapy). The GERI Care Bundle adopted a structured, multidisciplinary approach for systematic assessment of patients aged 75 years and above. Upon admission, nurses performed systematic screening for factors affecting mobility and overall function, including postural blood pressure, pain, nutrition, mentation and sensory impairments, while physiotherapists concurrently assessed patients’ mobility status. Standardised tools were used to evaluate key conditions such as delirium (Confusion Assessment Method) [23], depression (Patient Health Questionnaire-2) [24], falls risk (Morse Fall Scale) [25], nutrition status (3-Minute Nutritional Screening Tool) [26], and pressure injury risk (Braden Scale) [27], alongside assessments of urinary function and orthostatic hypotension.

The mobility intervention included components of patient mobilisation and group therapy. The former involved mobilisation of patients (at least three times a day) [8] performed largely by nurses. The group therapy consisted of weekly group physiotherapy and group art activity sessions, held on different weekdays, and championed by physiotherapists and nurses respectively. Group therapy was incorporated in the H.O.M.E intervention to promote mental well-being and social interaction among patients [28, 29]. The contents of the art activity were co-developed by both the art therapist and occupational therapist. Subsequently, training was given by the therapists to a few designated nurses to conduct the weekly art activity. Upper extremity exercises constituted the first 10 min of the art session. The H.O.M.E intervention was given to the intervention group throughout their hospitalisation while both groups received usual clinical care.

The implementation strategies for the H.O.M.E intervention included change physical structure and equipment, audit and provide feedback, revise professional roles and organise clinician implementation team meetings [30]. An area of the ward was designated as a patient lounge and set up with tables and chairs to facilitate group art activity. A physiotherapist designed a mobility board, which was installed on the wall at each patient’s bedside. Patients’ functional and ambulation status, including walking aid requirements were documented during assessments. Nurses or physiotherapists recorded each patient’s ambulation on the mobility board, while the attending doctor documented the total number of mobilisations in the patient’s case notes the following day.

A nurse champion led training for nurses on implementing the GERI Care Bundle. Selected nurses conducted audits and monitored these assessments. Physiotherapists provided training for nurses on patient mobilisation, while designated nurses were trained by art and occupational therapists to run weekly art sessions. Initially, nurses shadowed the therapists during sessions and gradually took over the facilitation. Weekly implementation team meetings were held to reflect on progress, share lessons learned and support ongoing professional learning. The implementation outcomes examined were fidelity (specifically adherence and dosage), acceptability and feasibility of the intervention, and implementation costs [31].

Study design

We adopted a convergent mixed-method study design [32], using predominantly qualitative methods. The quantitative data examined implementers’ acceptability and feasibility of the intervention and fidelity to the intervention. The qualitative data allowed us to explore the credibility and reasons behind the quantitative findings. The reporting and triangulation of findings from both quantitative and qualitative data were presented using the narrative weaving approach [32].

Qualitative study design and analysis

Eighteen healthcare professionals including doctors, nurses and physiotherapists involved in the H.O.M.E intervention participated in the semi-structured interviews, which were guided by interview questions (see Supplementary Table 1) but with flexibility to refocus questions for more information by interviewers [33]. The interviews were conducted either in-person in a private setting or virtually via a video conferencing platform. The interviews were conducted by two researchers (First and Second Authors), PhD holders with an additional formal training on qualitative methodology. No prior relationship was established between the researchers and implementers. All interviews were audio-recorded. Recruitment ceased when data saturation was reached, where sampling continued till no new information was heard during interviews [34].

The audio files were transcribed verbatim. This study is grounded in an interpretive approach [35]. In order to immerse in the data, the authors listened to the audio files and corrected for errors in the transcribed files. The transcribed files were coded by the two researchers, focusing on implementers’ interpretation and perceptions. The ideas expressed in a manifest were used to segment the data into coding units. In order to ensure reliability in the coding process, the two researchers separately assigned a code word or phrase that best described the meaning of the text segment [36] for three out of 18 transcribed files. A codebook was developed subsequently after discussion and calibration between the two researchers. The remaining transcribed files were coded using NVivo version R1 [37] by either researcher using the codebook as a guide. All the interviews were analysed inductively using thematic analysis [36]. Coding query was used to examine relationship between codes while matrix coding query was used to examine to what extent each profession has shared the similar or different experiences. We reported the study methods following the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research [38] (see Supplementary Table 2). Table 1 shows the demographic characteristics of the implementers. Qualitative interviews were not carried out for patients due to restrictions in place for healthcare setting during COVID-19 pandemic.

Quantitative study design and analysis

A total of 25 implementers were invited to take part in the quantitative surveys. The Acceptability of Intervention Measure (AIM) and Feasibility of Intervention Measure (FIM) were used to measure the implementers’ acceptability and perception of feasibility of the H.O.M.E intervention [39]. The questionnaires each consisted of four items per intervention component, on a Likert scale ranging from 1 to 5, 1 (completely disagree), 2 (disagree), 3 (neither agree nor disagree), 4 (agree) and 5 (completely agree). The results of the quantitative surveys were computed using Microsoft Excel. The mean and standard deviation scores for the acceptability and feasibility components were calculated.

A research staff member assisting with this study documented the dosage of group art and physiotherapy provided to each patient, along with adherence to the recommended mobilisation protocol of at least three daily sessions.

Qualitative data

For the qualitative component, findings are presented under three overarching themes identified from implementers’ responses. The first two themes examined implementers’ experiences of the H.O.M.E intervention, including both positive and negative aspects, followed by the theme relating to patient-related challenges encountered during implementation.

Additional workload and responsibilities

Implementers recognised mobilisation as important for preventing functional decline and perceived the H.O.M.E intervention as appropriately addressing hospital-associated physical deconditioning, including muscle wasting. In addition, it was suggested that the intervention could help prevent patients from developing the impression that they must remain confined to be or rely on diapers for voiding. Frequent mobilisation was also viewed as a means to enhance patients’ self-efficacy and potentially improve their mood. Nonetheless, despite recognising these benefits, nurses reported practical challenges that affected the consistent delivery of the intervention.

Operational and communication challenges

Approximately half of the nurses reported that the GERI Care Bundle was overly detailed and lengthy, making it time-consuming to complete given their current workload. Language barriers, particularly in cases where nurses were unable to understand the patient’s language or dialect, further complicated the assessment process. For patients who were unable to communicate, nurses were required to contact family members to obtain essential assessment information; however, these individuals were not always reachable.

"Sometimes got problems, because every time because for any people [sic], sometimes they come to the ward they cannot talk, then they cannot tell you anything, then so maybe the workload is quite is got increase one the workload [sic], because they cannot tell you. So you need to call for a family member [sic] ask for the information also." (Nurse8, < 10 years experience)

Competing clinical duties

Prioritisation of other pressing clinical tasks, such as medication administration, nursing procedures and accompanying patients for imaging, has reduced the capacity of nurses to fulfil the mobilisation role. This sentiment was shared by the majority of nurses. Taking time to coax patients to mobilise amid competing duties, including attending to patients requiring higher level of care, was described as an uphill task.

Resource barriers to implementation

Staffing constraint was the most common shared barrier by both nurses and physiotherapists and its association with competing duties was particularly emphasised by nurses (Fig. 1). A low nurse-to-patient ratio was identified as a barrier to frequent patient mobilisation, particularly due to competing demands on nurses’ time. Few nurses acknowledged that, when pressed for time, patients were transported using a commode chair rather than being assisted to walk.

Relationship between implementation challenges

Similarly, group therapy sessions were perceived as time-intensive, physiotherapists attributed the additional time and effort required, compared to conventional individual sessions, to factors such as difficulties patients have with following instructions and the need for preparatory work prior to conducting each session. Patient participation was also limited by scheduled clinical procedures, further constraining implementation.

In addition, group therapy requires substantial logistical preparation, as its feasibility is highly dependent on the number of patients deemed suitable for group activities. Additional staff resources is necessary, particularly for patients with a high risk of falls, making coordination and planning essential.

“I think the main challenge would probably be the manpower, because we do encourage these patients to mobilise daily at least three times a day. Their mobility has to be supervised and we know that the doctors are not there all the time because we have other patients as well. So ultimately, this main responsibility actually lies on our nurses who are equally as busy. So sometimes they are unable to meet the target because you know, they have let’s say a sick patient to tend to that day, or maybe one of their team members are down with MC so their manpower is short. So sometimes it’s quite limiting on their end, but I think most of the time it’s okay.” (Doctor4, <10 years experience)

Challenges arising from expanded professional roles

The requirement to assume new responsibilities such as organising and facilitating art sessions increased the nurses’ workload. Additionally, a few nurses expressed concerns regarding the repetitive nature of the art activities, which was partly attributed to limited expertise. Nurses reported that they often had to set aside routine nursing duties to conduct the art sessions, which they perceived as falling outside their professional scope. Some were required to work beyond their scheduled hours. As a result, achieving consensus and coordinated participation among all staff members emerged as a significant challenge. Some has raised the concern that treating medical condition should be the priority in an acute care setting instead of delivering this additional intervention.

“Yeah then this is like additional work for us, because previously we have not been doing this kind of activity. I think also not part of our job description one, but– yeah so it’s additional workload.” (Nurse6, <10 years experience)

Enhanced professional experience

Improved clinical workflow and care delivery

Despite the additional responsibilities, most implementers, especially the nurses reported that integrating the GERI Care Bundle improved clinical workflow, largely due to the structured assessment tools that guided care planning. One implementer, however, expressed a differing view, indicating that the bundle did not change workflow as similar assessments were already part of routine nursing practice.

Nonetheless, there was an agreement that the GERI Care Bundle enabled a more systematic and comprehensive assessment process, reducing the likelihood of missed evaluations. Nurses reported being better able to recognise delirium and delirium-related behaviours. The holistic nature of the assessment also provided a deeper understanding of patients, allowing nurses to deliver care that addressed not only physical conditions but also behavioural needs. As the implementers have observed

"I think because of the experience, because of the assessment. Yeah I think the Geri nurses are more alert on that, because of this assessment. We are more knowledgeable, experience on how to really see patients’ behaviour, if patients’ behaviour is still in the normal level or patients’ behaviour might have the tendency to like, change. I think this assessment also helps us to grow, compared to other nurses that are just taking care of the medical surgical cases only. They are more focused on the sickness but us Geri nurses we are more focused on the behaviour, then checking if what’s causing this behaviour." (Nurse5, < 10 years experience)

Enhanced professional autonomy

Enhanced knowledge contributed to improved self-efficacy among nurses, who reported initiating relevant patient assessments independently, rather than relying solely on physician direction. Regular clinician implementation team meetings also facilitated more collaborative discussions between doctors and nurses regarding patients’ conditions. Consequently, nurses perceived an enhancement in their professional autonomy, as noted by the senior nurses.

"It helps the nurse to be able to relate and apply what we learn in theory. It used to be whatever the doctors order then we carry out, and then the answer goes back to the doctor. So like the doctor order for this this this [sic], then we will perform and based on the result the doctors will look at the results and you know– but right now it’s kind of– we have a bit more autonomy. So we know what we are doing, before the doctor’s order there’s this set of things for us to perform such as all the screening. And then we roughly know the results and things like that, and then we work like– almost like on the same level." (Nurse1, ≥ 10 years experience)

Improved engagement with patients

Implementers perceived that group therapy enhanced patients’ overall well-being by providing physical, psychological and social benefits. They also reported that group physiotherapy promoted greater cognitive stimulation and social engagement compared with individual sessions. Approximately one-third of the implementers expressed improved engagement with patients, particularly through group therapy. Patients were generally observed to enjoy participating, with increased social interaction. The nurses, in particular rated art activity more favourably and reported positive experiences interacting with patients.

"So when I see the smile on their face then I feel satisfied. Because really boost the– how do I say? My relationship with my patients. And then I can see the other side of them. Because usually when they are sick they’re just in bed lying down there, so it’s good to see them like quite active, doing this kind of things." (Nurse3, < 10 years experience)

Challenges arising from patients

Patients’ conditions impeding implementation

Not all patients were suitable for the interventions. Physiotherapists (Fig. 1) also noted that the mobility intervention and group therapy may be inappropriate for medically unstable patients. They suggested that these interventions are more suitable for patients who primarily require functional maintenance. Concerned for patient safety, physiotherapists had to constantly monitor for sudden changes in the patients’ condition during the intervention. Patients were returned to bed if their conditions deteriorated. Implementers also had medical support available, such as an oxygen tank on standby during the group therapy sessions or daily walking.

Heterogeneity of patients’ functional status (Fig. 1), ranging from dependence to independence, further complicated implementation and raised concerns about unequal attention given to each patient, which could undermine the effectiveness of the therapy.

"If let’s say the participants recruited are very far off or very different from each other, then I would think the group therapy would be quite difficult to carry on. Then if let’s say for example like I mentioned earlier, if one is very independent and then the other one requires more assistance, and then we wouldn’t be able to conduct the group therapy as efficiently as we would like it to be. And not just our time wasted but also time for patients to do the group therapy, it’s also being affected." (Physiotherapist1, < 10 years experience)

Patient resistance and engagement barriers

Furthermore, not all patients responded positively to the interventions. Nurses commonly observed refusal to walk, with reasons including fear of falling, tiredness, and internalised ageist beliefs (for example perceiving themselves as too old to mobilise). Many patients had been admitted due to falls, which further contributed to reluctance. Some preferred using diapers or commode chairs instead of walking to the toilet.

Similarly, not all patients responded positively to the art activity; those who lacked confidence in their drawing abilities often required additional encouragement from the nursing staff. Nurses reported that considerable effort was needed to coax patient participation, which added to their workload.

Variability in intervention exposure

Some implementers reported that the extent of the intervention’s beneficial effects remains unclear and warrants further investigation. One concern raised was that the “dosage” of the intervention depended on the length of hospitalisation; some patients did not receive any group sessions due to a short hospital stay. These qualitative observations are also consistent with the reported adherence rates (Table 3).

Discussion

The H.O.M.E intervention adopted a novel approach by integrating group-based art and physiotherapy sessions alongside frequent patient mobilisation. This study examined the experiences of implementers towards its implementation in the acute care setting. Overall, the implementers held mixed perceptions of this mobility-focused intervention. While the intervention was generally well-received due to enhanced professional autonomy and clinical workflow, the fast-paced acute care setting and constraint resources have challenged the feasibility of H.O.M.E intervention. Barriers to the implementation identified could be categorised in three overarching domains for hospital-based interventions in the literature: system (workload, staffing issue), staff (role identity, competing duties) and intervention (fit for patients and availability of evidence) [40].

The acceptability and feasibility for mobilisation were rated lower compared to art activity by nurses in the quantitative survey. Particularly, implementation challenges regarding frequent mobilisation were the primary concerns of the nurses. Although the nurses did not explicitly rule out their responsibilities for patients’ mobilisation, the H.O.M.E intervention probably did not shift their mindset of patient mobilisation as their secondary roles. This corroborates with a previous study where nurses have a more negative attitudes towards mobilisation compared to physiotherapists [22]. Competency in mobilising patients was not a barrier shared by the nurses as in a previous study [22]. The major obstacle was additional efforts were needed to coax patients who were resistant to walk. Competing duties [15, 40] deterred nurses from implementation of frequent mobilisation, which requires greater time commitment. This was compounded by the staffing issue, which is not an uncommon challenge in acute care settings [40–42]. Furthermore, the COVID-19 pandemic worsened the staffing situation with more staff reporting sick [43]. By comparison, art activity was better received by nurses than frequent mobilisation. Interventions were tailored to individual patient conditions and preferences in this study. Nonetheless, concerns regarding the competency required to conduct art activity were noted, consistent with findings from previous studies [19].

Physiotherapists rated mobilisation as more acceptable and feasible than group therapy, which was corroborated by qualitative data highlighting implementation challenges for group exercise. This was in line with the notion that physiotherapists were ranked highest for the knowledge and attitude towards mobilisation [22]. Group physiotherapy intervention is a less common approach in an acute care setting compared to individual rehabilitation. Challenges arising from patients were the primary concerns for physiotherapists. Patients’ medical conditions coupled with their short hospital stay have also been identified as the barriers to recruitment for in-patient exercise programme in a previous study [44].

Although conducting on-going training for staff was the planned implementation strategy, only one-off structured training sessions were delivered at the start of the study due to time constraint. New staff were given on-the-job training by senior staff instead; thus, the purpose of the H.O.M.E intervention might not have been conveyed effectively. While the implementers observed improved social interaction of patients due to the intervention, longer-term outcomes could not be observed due to the short intervention period. Thus, the implementers emphasised the importance of effectively communicating to staff the goal and purpose of the H.O.M.E intervention, as well as the evidence [40, 41] of its longer-term benefits for patients.

Overall, the quantitative and qualitative findings were largely convergent. Quantitative data indicated that nurses generally reported higher overall ratings in the survey, although they rated the acceptability of the mobilisation intervention lower than physiotherapists, and both groups rated feasibility lower than acceptability (Table 2). These patterns were consistent with the qualitative findings, in which nurses frequently described challenges with mobilisation (Table 4). Some divergence was observed among nurses regarding the feasibility of frequent mobilisation. While this component received relatively high quantitative ratings, qualitative data highlighted notable concerns about its implementation. Nevertheless, as the survey responses were not anonymised, the relatively high quantitative ratings should be interpreted with caution, as the potential influence of socially desirable, lenient or acquiescent responding cannot be excluded [45].

Adherence to the recommended intervention ‘dose’ was variable, highlighting an important implementation challenge. Missing sessions often resulted not only from human factors, such as staff availability, patient resistance but also from unavoidable clinical procedures, such as imaging or treatments, that coincided with scheduled group sessions. These challenges were further compounded by the COVID-19 pandemic, particularly during the portion of the study corresponding to heightened restrictions, which affected staffing levels and patient flow, and complicated intervention delivery. This reflects a key tension in acute care where even well-designed interventions can be difficult to deliver consistently when both personnel and patients face unpredictable demands. Suboptimal adherence may limit the effectiveness of the intervention and influence staff perceptions of its feasibility and sustainability, underscoring the need for implementation strategies that consider both human and operational realities.

A major gap of this study was that qualitative experiences of patients were not sought due to the limited access to the acute care setting by researchers during COVID-19 pandemic. Nonetheless, a systematic review has documented that patients in the acute care setting were better reassured if they feel connected and build a reciprocal relationship with nurses as well as receiving person-centred care [46]. This is particularly crucial for older adults who tend to lose their sense of identity, especially among those with dementia during hospital admission [46]. It has been reported elsewhere that older adults enjoyed group sessions and were motivated and supported by their peers [47]. In this study, both the nurses and physiotherapists have also shared that the patients generally appreciated and enjoyed the group therapy. Reciprocally, nurses shared that they have better job satisfaction due to patients’ positive responses to art activity versus job autonomy as reported previously [48].

Approximately one third of implementers suggested assigning dedicated personnel to carry out the H.O.M.E intervention, particularly patient mobilisation, as a strategy to enhance feasibility and sustainability. They further indicated that these personnel need not be healthcare professionals if provided with basic training, citing examples such as basic care associates or volunteers. However, there were also concerns raised regarding patient safety if mobilisation were conducted by non-healthcare personnel. Further investigation is warranted regarding the effectiveness and implementation of volunteer involvement in supporting the mobilisation of older adults in acute care settings, as the current empirical evidence remains limited [49].

Future research may benefit from the use of anonymised surveys, particularly when evaluating work-related initiatives, to mitigate the influence of power dynamics and reduce socially desirable responding. More broadly, most implementers recommended increasing staffing levels to improve intervention sustainability. Additional staffing would better support implementers and potentially improve compliance with the intervention implementation. This is particularly important for ensuring patient safety during group therapy sessions. Implementers emphasised that improved staff buy-in is the key to enhancing implementation engagement and may facilitate the integration of the intervention into routine care. Refinement of the implementation strategy is also recommended, including the provision of regular, structured training, clarification and revision of professional roles through improved communication and education, recognition of achievements [50] and the generation of evidence on the long-term effectiveness of the intervention. In order to improve the adherence to the intervention dose, flexible scheduling, coordination with clinical procedures and adaptive strategies such as staggered session times are needed to optimise delivery. Future studies may also consider identifying specific subgroups that are most likely to benefit from group therapy.

Conclusion

The findings of our study contribute to the well of knowledge on the implementation challenges of a multicomponent mobility-focused intervention in an acute care setting. Future studies conducted in the similar setting should consider staffing issues (system), enhancement of professional role identity (staff) and suitability of patients, and ease of integration of the intervention into clinical care (intervention) in the implementation process.
