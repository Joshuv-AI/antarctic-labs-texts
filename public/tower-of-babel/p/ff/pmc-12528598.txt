Breaking Barriers: Advancing Equity and Support for Women in Plastic Surgery
Authors: Meeti Mehta, Ruvi Chauhan, Tara L. Huston, Paige L. Myers, Carolyn De La Cruz, Paris D. Butler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Summary:Despite recent modest increases, women remain underrepresented in the plastic surgery workforce, particularly in leadership roles. This disparity highlights the need for systemic, cultural, and professional changes to foster equity and support for women in the field. This article outlined key barriers faced by women in plastic surgery and provided actionable recommendations for improvement. Systemic changes are essential for increasing representation and leadership opportunities. Transparent recruitment practices, accountability in diversity efforts, and leadership development initiatives can help ensure gender parity at all levels. Flexible work environments and family-friendly policies, such as parental leave and competency-based training, are vital for retaining female residents and faculty. Cultural shifts, including addressing unconscious gender bias and redefining leadership perceptions, are critical to combatting discrimination and promoting diverse leadership styles. Mandatory bias training and robust reporting mechanisms for harassment are recommended to create an inclusive workplace culture. Professional support through mentorship, sponsorship, and networking opportunities is vital for fostering career development. Structured mentorship programs, peer networks, and sponsorship initiatives can empower women surgeons and promote their advancement. Institutions should also prioritize work-life integration by offering adaptable career pathways, childcare resources, and wellness programs to address burnout and support mental health. By implementing these strategies, programs and institutions can create a more equitable and supportive environment for women in plastic surgery, ultimately advancing diversity and inclusivity within the specialty.

Takeaways

Question: What systemic, cultural, and professional support barriers do women in plastic surgery face, and how can these barriers be addressed to advance equity and support?

Findings: This review highlights key barriers faced by women in plastic surgery, including implicit bias, limited leadership opportunities, and work-life integration challenges. It proposes actionable solutions such as institutional policies to promote gender equity, cultural shifts to address bias and discrimination, and enhanced mentorship, sponsorship, and career flexibility initiatives.

Meaning: Advancing equity for women in plastic surgery requires intentional systemic, cultural, and professional efforts to create an inclusive environment that supports their recruitment, retention, and leadership development.

INTRODUCTION

Plastic surgery has long been at the forefront of medical innovation, advancing techniques that restore form and function while profoundly improving patients’ lives. However, the specialty’s evolution has not been mirrored in the composition of its workforce. Women remain underrepresented in plastic surgery, particularly in leadership roles, despite constituting nearly 60% of all matched plastic surgery residents.1–5 This disparity reflects broader gender inequities within surgical fields and underscores the need for targeted efforts to achieve gender parity.

The importance of gender diversity in surgery extends well beyond the moral argument. Studies have shown that diverse teams enhance patient care, foster innovation, and improve workplace dynamics.6,7 Yet, female plastic surgeons face persistent barriers, including implicit biases, inequitable access to leadership opportunities, and challenges in balancing professional and personal responsibilities.8–11 These systemic and cultural challenges not only limit the growth of individual surgeons but also hinder the advancement of the specialty as a whole.

In recent years, initiatives at the institutional and societal levels have sought to address these disparities. Structured mentorship programs, family-friendly policies, and increased advocacy for women in surgical leadership have begun to make a difference.12,13 However, progress remains slow, and significant gaps persist in the recruitment, retention, and advancement of female plastic surgeons.14–18

This article seeks to document the progress made in supporting women in plastic surgery, identify ongoing challenges, and propose actionable recommendations to address barriers at the program level. By fostering systemic changes, promoting cultural shifts, and enhancing professional support, the field can move closer to a future where all surgeons have equitable opportunities to thrive and contribute to the specialty’s growth.

PAST AND PRESENT INITIATIVES

Significant progress has been made since Dr. Miranda Stewart, in the early 19th century, had to disguise herself as a man to practice surgery.16,19 Pioneers like Dr. Suzanne Noel, often recognized as the first female plastic surgeon, proved women’s capabilities in the field.20 The American College of Surgeons began recognizing female surgeons in 1931, and in 1948, Dr. Alma Dea Morani became the first female plastic surgeon admitted to the American Society of Plastic Surgeons (ASPS).19 The founding of the Association of Women Surgeons in 1981 and the Women Plastic Surgeons Forum in 1992 were pivotal in advocating for women in surgery. These initiatives helped foster mentorship, leadership roles, and networking within plastic surgery organizations.

Since then, 8 women have served as presidents of the Plastic Surgery Foundation and ASPS, supported by early mentorship and organizational efforts.21 Professional societies such as the ASPS and the American Council of Educators in Plastic Surgery continue to support initiatives such as the Women Plastic Surgeons Forum and the Women in Academic Plastic Surgery Committee. Programs such as the LIMITLESS Leaders Program and Women in Plastic Surgery Symposium provide opportunities for leadership development and networking. Efforts to increase inclusive excellence are exemplified by initiatives like Times Up PRS, Women of Color in Plastic Surgery, and the LIMITLESS docuseries, which empower underrepresented surgeons. These organizations also offer career development support through grants and scholarships, such as the Women’s Microsurgery Group Travel Scholarship, and the Plastic Surgery Foundation’s Diversity and Inclusion Grants.

As part of broader efforts to support female surgeons, recent initiatives have focused on improving work-life integration. In 2021, the American Board of Plastic Surgery implemented a leave policy allowing up to 12 weeks of leave during training without delaying graduation, a significant increase from the previous median of 4 weeks.22 The Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education also updated its policy to require lactation rooms and protected lactation time during work hours.

CURRENT BARRIERS TO GENDER EQUALITY IN PLASTIC SURGERY

Despite initiatives aimed at increasing gender equality, significant barriers remain. These challenges stem from 3 main obstacles women face during training and early careers: (1) systemic barriers, (2) cultural barriers, and (3) lack of support.

Systemic Barriers

Underrepresentation and Leadership

Women represent only 12.3% of academic plastic surgeons and are less likely to hold leadership positions, conduct research, or gain tenure compared with their male counterparts.23,24 Female representation decreases at each level of leadership: 12% are program directors, 8.7% are departmental chairs, no major journal has a female Editor-in-Chief, and only 14.8% of conference keynote speakers are women.24,25 Despite equal numbers of men and women pursuing academic careers, female surgeons’ career advancement is significantly slower, unrelated to factors such as age, institution, or research output.1,4,5

This underrepresentation perpetuates a cycle, as the lack of female role models in academic plastic surgery discourages women from entering the field. Studies show that female students are more likely to choose surgery and residency programs with higher female faculty representation, and exposure to successful female surgeons reduces concerns about entering male-dominated specialties.16 Representation is crucial, as it impacts both women’s entry into the field and their training experiences. For example, female surgeons often face more musculoskeletal issues due to poorly sized instruments, highlighting how underrepresentation affects their practice.26–28

Structural Challenges in Training and Practice

During training, residents work 40–80 hours/wk with limited flexibility. Until recently, the American Board of Plastic Surgery offered only 4 weeks of maternity leave, with an option for 2 additional weeks from vacation time, which often risked delayed graduation. Over 70% of residents with children before the leave policy change felt they lacked adequate parental leave. Barriers to having children include long work hours, training demands, and concerns about burdening coresidents. Despite policy changes, only 52.3% of residents felt their programs offered consistent support for family planning.29 In fact, a 2022 survey of plastic surgery program directors found that only 21.4% of institutions offered parental or fertility support.30

Gender Pay Gap

The gender pay gap in medicine is especially wide in procedural specialties, with male plastic surgeons earning over twice as much in industry payments as their female counterparts.14 The 2019 Doximity Physician Compensation Report highlighted an 11% wage gap in plastic surgery.31 Male plastic surgeons are also twice as likely to earn incomes more than $400K, despite similar work hours and practice profiles.32,33

Cultural Barriers

Bias and Discrimination

Traditional gender roles and bias remain significant barriers to the advancement of female surgeons. Many women report feeling unrecognized or excluded due to rigid gender stereotypes.17 Female surgeons are 10 times more likely than male surgeons to perceive gender discrimination, which affects hiring, promotions, and evaluations.34 For instance, reference letters for men highlight their accomplishments, whereas those for women focus more on effort and personal life.35 Implicit biases, such as assumptions about women’s commitment or capability, limit career opportunities.36 This is especially evident with the motherhood bias, where assumptions about a female surgeon’s family life may hinder her career advancement.37 For instance, female applicants are more likely than men to be asked prohibited questions about marital status and family planning during residency interviews, which often influences their program preferences.38,39

Women are also more likely to feel isolated from their peers, as career opportunities are not equally available.40 Even subtle gender bias can lower a woman’s sense of accomplishment and hinder her career progression, contributing to imposter syndrome.34,41 Additionally, women are five times more likely than men to experience overt sexism or harassment, which can undermine confidence and hinder both training and career growth.42,43

Biases are even more pronounced for women with intersectional identities, including women of color, women with disabilities, and nonheterosexual women. Women of color, in particular, face unique challenges due to the intersection of race and gender.44–47 Female physicians from racial/ethnic minorities experience a higher prevalence of microaggressions, contributing to greater burnout than their White counterparts.48 A recent ASPS survey found similar trends among non-White and nonheterosexual female plastic surgery residents.49 Commonly reported microaggressions include a lack of role models and authority figures of the same race, being perceived as a “foreigner” or “not a ‘true’ American,” and being unfairly labeled as aggressive or intimidating. Many also experience racial/ethnic sexualization.48 In the workplace, underrepresented women are disproportionately targeted by sexual harassment and racial discrimination, including from patients.47,50 These compounded challenges contribute to the higher attrition of women from underrepresented groups in academic medicine.51

Leadership Perception and Double Standards

Traditional gender roles shape perceptions of women as nurturing and dependent, and men as independent and action-oriented.52 Role congruity theory suggests that women leaders face prejudice when they act outside these norms. Women displaying assertiveness or leadership often face backlash, being labeled “bossy,” whereas men receive constructive feedback for improving skills.53 Anger in men is typically seen as circumstantial, but in women, it is viewed as a character flaw. Women navigate a tradeoff between being perceived as too masculine (respected but disliked) or too feminine (liked but not respected).54 This dynamic affects salary negotiations, where women are often seen as unlikable when advocating for themselves.55 Similarly, studies show that male applicants are rated as more competent and given higher starting salaries and more mentorship than identical female applicants.56 Women’s accomplishments are also more likely to be attributed to team efforts, hindering their career advancement.52

Personal and Professional Support Barriers

Mentorship and Sponsorship Gaps

The gender gap in academic plastic surgery leads to fewer mentors and role models for female surgeons. Although 90% of male plastic surgeons report being mentors, only 68% of women do, and female mentors are more likely to support female mentees.57,58 Only 78% of female junior faculty have mentors, compared with 91% of their male counterparts,59 and only 15% of female plastic surgeons have same-sex mentors, compared with 75% of men.15 Female residents also report fewer mentor relationships than men.40 Same-gender mentorship is crucial for women, as it addresses unique gender-specific challenges and fosters confidence.60 Female mentors help combat imposter syndrome and advocate for promotions and leadership roles.13 The lack of female-specific mentorship impairs career development and research productivity61 and is a key factor in women leaving academia.40

Work-life Integration Challenges

Female plastic surgeons, especially those with families, report poor work-life integration, with long hours, emergency call shifts, and the demands of reconstructive surgery.62 The disproportionate caregiving responsibilities women face limit time for research and practice development, hindering career advancement.63 Married female surgeons often juggle dual-income households.32 As a result, women are more likely to leave academic or institutional practice for family reasons,40 and family obligations influence retirement decisions.64 These challenges contribute to female surgeons seeking career paths offering better work-life balance,62 reducing the pool of talented surgeons in academia.65

Women also face personal life impacts, such as higher rates of single status, delayed marriage, and postponed childbearing.32,66 Female surgeons are more likely to see pregnancy as a career threat67 and experience higher rates of pregnancy complications and infertility compared with the general population.15,66 Female residents face stress over maternity leave, with many taking insufficient leave and feeling dissatisfied with the time off.68 Childcare and breastfeeding during residency are also major stressors, and postresidency, women may face difficulties balancing family and career prospects.66 These work-family conflicts contribute to higher burnout and stress, especially among trainees,69–71 who report feeling isolated and anxious about balancing personal and professional responsibilities.72–74

FUTURE RECOMMENDATIONS TO ADDRESS BARRIERS FOR WOMEN IN PLASTIC SURGERY

We recommend addressing barriers faced by women in plastic surgery through systemic changes, cultural shifts, and enhanced professional support.

Systemic Changes

Increasing Representation and Leadership Opportunities

Recognizing and highlighting the achievements of female plastic surgeons is crucial to inspire future leaders.13 Program committees, editorial boards, and professional societies must make an active effort to include and highlight female plastic surgeons as speakers, moderators, panelists, and leaders.16 Institutions can promote this visibility by establishing awards for excellence in mentorship, research, or clinical practice.

To increase institutional diversity, programs should be committed to establishing fair and transparent recruitment practices, including publicizing open positions12 and forming recruitment committees with female representation to ensure gender parity.75

Retaining female faculty members is important to promote their advancement to senior leadership roles, as most chairs and chiefs of plastic surgery are internal hires.76 Institutions should commit to diversity by creating independent committees to review policies and leadership opportunities and ensure alignment with the diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) goals. These committees would set targets for gender parity in key decision-making positions and support initiatives for female faculty advancement.15

Gender parity creates a positive feedback loop, with female-led programs implementing stronger interventions for female leaders.12 Programs can provide leadership training, mentorship, and shadowing opportunities for residents and early-career female surgeons while incorporating business, negotiation, and social media training into the curriculum to prepare young surgeons for practice. A “mini-MBA” program or elective business training could also benefit residents and junior faculty.14 Additionally, institutions can offer career advancement programs for female faculty focused on grant writing, public speaking, and media engagement. In particular, leadership development programs for women from underrepresented groups are essential to addressing this gap in representation.77 These changes require significant effort and resources, which should be supported by dedicated time and resources for faculty involvement in DEI projects, without affecting their academic or clinical productivity metrics.78

Improving Training and Practice Structures

Institutions should create flexible work environments to enhance satisfaction and quality of life for residents and faculty. Programs can offer flexible training schedules, allowing residents to adjust workloads or focus on research during pregnancy or other life transitions. Family-friendly policies, such as universal parental leave, should be included in all employment contracts.68 This would benefit parents agnostic of gender. These nontraditional schedules should not be seen as a lack of commitment, as parental leave and part-time work are essential for retention, as the cost of recruiting and training new faculty can be as high as 1.5 times the average salary.79,80

Residents opting for flexible schedules should not be required to make up missed call or lose board eligibility due to extended parental leave. To avoid resentment, programs should hire clinical associates for coverage rather than relying on residents.68 Competency-based graduation requirements, instead of time-based, can further promote flexibility.65

Women are found to be more likely to report being unaware of promotion criteria.81 Programs should ensure clear, equal promotion criteria and adopt fair pay policies, with transparent salary, bonus, and promotion expectations.13 To address the gender pay gap, factors such as clinical time, research productivity, teaching, and DEI efforts should be considered in promotion decisions.

Cultural Shifts

Combatting Bias and Discrimination

Education on unconscious gender bias is paramount to mitigating discrimination.82 In fact, increasing awareness of bias leads to more women being promoted.83 Studies show that many male surgeons do not recognize gender disparities, making implicit bias training vital for both men and women in leadership, faculty, and hiring roles.84 Institutions should implement upstander initiatives to foster meaningful communication, improve workplace culture, and empower all individuals—including men—to advocate for and support colleagues from underrepresented backgrounds, including those with intersectional identities.47 Programs should enforce a zero-tolerance policy for sexual harassment, with anonymous reporting mechanisms. Such policies must be upheld to prevent harassment and foster a supportive culture that encourages everyone to challenge misconduct.42,85

Reframing Leadership Perceptions

To combat the tradeoff that women face between success and likeability, institutions should promote diverse leadership styles, emphasizing traits like collaboration, empathy, and decisiveness over traditionally masculine traits.55,86 Faculty and staff should be trained to recognize and value these styles, reducing double standards. Programs should also encourage team-based leadership and educate on gender bias, evaluating leaders based on performance rather than subjective traits like warmth. Highlighting female leaders who exemplify diverse styles can increase visibility and challenge these implicit biases.

Enhancing Professional Support

Expanding Mentorship and Sponsorship

Increasing mentorship, especially with female mentors, is critical for supporting women in plastic surgery.87 As of 2020, only 10% of programs offered mentorship pairing junior and senior women surgeons.12 Structured mentorship programs have been shown to enhance diversity and improve faculty retention.88,89 Programs should create such mentorship programs and foster peer mentorship network for shared support. Offering incentives such as protected time and awards for mentorship can help institutionalize these efforts.89

The sponsorship of senior mentors advocating for mentees’ career advancement is equally as crucial as mentorship to promote the success of women surgeons.90–93 Although the importance of sponsorship grows, access decreases over time.94 Senior faculty should be proactive in sponsoring women for leadership roles and opportunities. To expand access to mentors and sponsors, institutions and organizations should support networking opportunities. Institutions can support networking through professional meetings and alumni networks, encouraging women to engage in identity-based and nonidentity-based societies that provide opportunities for networking, targeted mentorship, and sponsorship.18 Creating virtual platforms and promoting social media for informal networking can further enhance professional connection.12,95

Facilitating Work-life Integration

Institutions can attract and retain employees by promoting family-friendly policies.13 Programs should foster a supportive culture for pregnancy, breastfeeding, childcare, and family needs, offering flexible career paths, sabbaticals, reduced clinical loads, and part-time tenure options.13,68,82 Childcare resources, such as on-site facilities with extended hours and financial assistance, should be made available.68 Lactation facilities should be accessible, and clinical duties should include break times for lactation.12

To support fertility and family planning, programs can educate trainees about available resources, the impact of delayed childbearing, and options like oocyte cryopreservation. Health insurance should also cover fertility preservation and assisted reproductive technology.68

Finally, to combat burnout and mental health challenges, programs should provide mental health support, financial coaching, and stress management tools.96,97

CONCLUSIONS

The efforts to support women in plastic surgery have evolved over time, with notable progress in advancing gender equity and inclusivity within the field. This article highlighted the initiatives at the program level that have fostered a more supportive environment for women, including mentorship, policy changes, and the promotion of female role models. These initiatives, although impactful, reveal the ongoing need for continued focus on gender-related disparities, especially regarding leadership opportunities, work-life integration, and recognition. Although programmatic efforts to support women have fluctuated throughout history, we must continue to ride the tides of external influences with steadfast resolve. Moving forward, it is crucial to build on the momentum of these advancements and advocate for systemic changes that ensure equal opportunities for women, ultimately fostering inclusive excellence within the plastic surgery workforce.
