Recurrent Duplicated Middle Cerebral Artery Aneurysm Treated with Flow Diverter After Coil Embolization and Surgical Clipping, A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Natsuki Shozuhara, Yushiro Take, Hiroki Sato, Kaima Suzuki, Yoshiki Mochizuki, Daisuke Tanikawa, Akina Iwasaki, Yukie Nakayama, Takuma Maeda, Oudrhiri Mohammed Yassaad, Masataka Yoshimura, Satoshi Iihoshi, Shinya Kohyama, Hiroki Kurita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Duplicated middle cerebral artery (DMCA) aneurysms are rare, and their management can be challenging owing to their unique anatomical configuration and altered hemodynamics. We herein present a case of a large DMCA aneurysm that recurred 3 years after initial coil embolization. The patient underwent surgical clipping but experienced postoperative hemorrhage, which required the placement of a flow diverter as salvage treatment. This case highlights the potential for DMCA aneurysm recurrence and the importance of considering multiple treatment modalities.

Introduction

A duplicated middle cerebral artery (DMCA) is a rare anomaly caused by abnormal vascular development during embryonic stages. The DMCA is considered an anomalous early ramification of the early branch of the middle cerebral artery (MCA), which normally supplies the anterior temporal lobe. 1 Owing to the low incidence of this anomaly, large-scale studies on the probability or cause of cerebral aneurysm formation in this population have not been conducted. Interestingly, a systematic review reported that 52.9% of patients with aneurysms associated with the DMCA have other concurrent cerebrovascular malformations, suggesting that congenital disabilities in cerebrovascular development may play a role in the formation of DMCA aneurysms. 2

Coil embolization has only been used in a limited number of cases to treat DMCA aneurysms. However, to the best of our knowledge, there are still no published data on how to manage a DMCA aneurysm that recurs after initial endovascular treatment, leaving clinicians without evidence-based guidance for this challenging situation. Endovascular treatment of DMCA aneurysms must preserve the patency of the DMCA, which often originates at the aneurysm neck. Even when patency is maintained, altered hemodynamics in this rare vascular anomaly can still promote recurrence. 3 Addressing these challenges is crucial for improving patient outcomes and expanding the understanding of DMCA aneurysms within the neurosurgical and neurointerventional communities.

We present the first documented case of a large DMCA aneurysm that recurred 3 years after coil embolization, was surgically clipped, and rebled postoperatively, necessitating rescue flow-diverter stent placement. This report, therefore, aims to clarify the technical challenges and recurrence mechanisms unique to DMCA anatomy and to synthesize the available literature to propose practical retreatment strategies, thereby guiding clinicians who encounter this rare anomaly.

Case Presentation

A 72-year-old woman was incidentally found on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to have an aneurysm located at the bifurcation of the left internal carotid artery (ICA) and the DMCA (type B). 4 She had a history of mild cognitive impairment, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and a previous near-drowning incident, and had a modified Rankin scale (mRS) score of 3. The aneurysm measured 7 mm in height and 5 mm in neck width and had a maximum diameter of 10 mm. It was a saccular aneurysm with two blebs at the apex, directed posteroinferior to the ICA–DMCA bifurcation. The DMCA originated from the aneurysm neck ( Fig. 1 ). The DMCA supplied the anterior temporal lobe cortex. Surgical treatment was initially recommended because of the size, shape, and location of the aneurysms. However, after considering the patient's preferences and the family's wishes, the decision was made to proceed with endovascular coil embolization. The procedure was performed with balloon assistance to prevent coil protrusion into the ICA. The aneurysm was embolized with a neck remnant to maintain the patency of the DMCA ( Fig. 2 ). The patient was followed up with periodic MRI without any signs of recurrence. However, 3 years after the initial treatment, follow-up MRI revealed recurrence of the aneurysm ( Fig. 3 ). MRI performed 3 months later showed further enlargement of the aneurysm to 23 mm ( Fig. 4 ), indicating the need for further treatment. The mechanism of the aneurysm growth was suspected to be either thrombosis or vasa vasorum–mediated wall growth. Therefore, the treatment strategy was considered to proceed with surgical clipping with a high-flow bypass if needed.

The aneurysm was identified upon opening the Sylvian fissure ( Fig. 5 ). Indocyanine green (ICG) video angiography revealed antegrade filling of the aneurysm ( Fig. 6 ). Given the location of the arteries and the aneurysm neck, trapping and high-flow bypass are challenging. Therefore, neck clipping was attempted while preserving the DMCA. Using two clips, the aneurysm dome was successfully occluded, and the DMCA was preserved, as confirmed by intraoperative ICG video angiography and digital subtraction angiography (DSA; Fig. 7 ). The aneurysm wall was coagulated to prevent blood flow to the aneurysm via the vasa vasorum. The remaining portion of the aneurysm neck was wrapped, and surgery was performed ( Fig. 8 ). However, on postoperative day 7, the patient developed aphasia. Computed tomography (CT) revealed hemorrhaging around the surgical clip ( Fig. 9 ). Given the intraoperative findings and considering the family's decision, further surgical intervention was avoided, and a flow-diverter stent was placed. Dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) was initiated at a loading dose (aspirin 200 mg and clopidogrel 300 mg), and a flow-diverter stent was placed on the same day ( Fig. 10 ). Although no further hemorrhage or ischemic complications occurred, the patient required prolonged intubation and tracheostomy due to respiratory deterioration. Two weeks after the tracheostomy, the patient experienced bronchial obstruction due to a clot caused by a hemorrhage from the tracheostomy site. Hence, DAPT was suspended from the day of the event and restarted after 5 days. Considering the patient's status, we followed up on the patency of the MCA and ICA on MRI/A without DSA ( Fig. 11 ). Two months after the clipping surgery, the patient presented with hydrocephalus and underwent a lumbo-peritoneal shunt. Three months after the surgical clipping, the patient was transferred to a rehabilitation hospital with an mRS score of 4.

Discussion

We herein report the first case of a large aneurysm arising from a DMCA that recurred after a successful initial coil embolization. The aneurysm was subsequently treated with surgical clipping; however, the patient experienced postoperative hemorrhage, necessitating the placement of a flow diverter as a salvage treatment. This case highlights the unique challenges associated with managing DMCA aneurysms and the potential for recurrence, even after a seemingly successful endovascular treatment. This underscores the importance of considering multiple treatment modalities, including surgical and endovascular options, for the management of complex DMCA aneurysms.

The DMCA is a rare anatomical variant in which an additional vessel arises from the ICA adjacent to the anterior choroidal artery. Although Uchino et al reported a ruptured aneurysm originating from the DMCA trunk, most DMCA aneurysms originate from the bifurcation of the ICA and DMCA. 5 Abnormal vascular geometry, particularly a steep bifurcation from the parent artery, can disturb laminar flow and promote aneurysm formation and growth, as Kai et al reported in type B DMCA aneurysms, the subtype observed in our patient. 4 Generally, a sharp branch angle is likewise an established predictor of recurrence after coil embolization (PMID 29802164; 33677365). Similar hemodynamic stresses have been described in posterior communicating artery aneurysms that arise from the ICA, where a steep angle from the parent vessel correlates with aneurysm enlargement (PMID 38318545). Because the DMCA must be preserved, balloon-assisted coil embolization is most often selected, and we therefore used it as first-line therapy. However, in previous papers, three of the eight published DMCA cases achieved only partial occlusion, a situation that may be associated with recurrence ( Table 1 ). Partial filling is common when the coil mass must spare the DMCA origin, and in our patient, the artery originated directly from the aneurysm neck. This anatomical feature likely contributed to the observed recurrence.

The management of recurrent DMCA aneurysms after the initial endovascular treatment remains challenging, and there is no consensus on the optimal treatment strategy. To the best of our knowledge, only eight cases, including ours, of coil embolization for DMCA aneurysms have been reported, and no recurrence case ( Table 1 ). 3 6 7 8 9 10 11 In our case, surgical clipping was initially chosen as a salvage treatment after aneurysm recurrence, considering the potential role of the vasa vasorum in aneurysm regrowth. However, postoperative hemorrhage necessitated the placement of a flow diverter to secure the aneurysm because of the difficulty in performing the trapping and bypass. Flow-diverter placement has emerged as a promising treatment option for complex and recurrent aneurysms, particularly those with wide necks. 12 13 In addition to the minimally invasive nature of endovascular treatment, we selected this approach because flow diverters can preserve branch-vessel patency 14 and offer a streamlining effect. 15 Their capacity to reconstruct the parent artery and promote aneurysm thrombosis makes them attractive options for DMCA aneurysms, which often present with unique anatomical challenges. However, the use of flow diverters in DMCA aneurysms remains limited, especially in ruptured cases. Further studies are needed to establish their safety and efficacy. This case highlights the importance of considering multiple treatment modalities and customizing treatment approaches to get complete obliteration at the first session based on individual aneurysm characteristics and patient factors. Close follow-ups and a multidisciplinary approach are crucial for managing these complex aneurysms.

The potential role of the vasa vasorum in the growth and rupture of cerebral aneurysms has garnered increasing attention in recent years. The vasa vasorum is a small blood vessel that supplies the walls of larger vessels, including the cerebral arteries. In the present case, we hypothesized that the recurrent aneurysm growth after coil embolization might have been partially attributable to the presence of vasa vasorum. This hypothesis is supported by a study suggesting that the presence of the vasa vasorum in the aneurysm wall is associated with aneurysm growth and risk. 4 Korkmaz et al analyzed the ultrastructure and quantitative differences in the walls of unruptured and ruptured cerebral aneurysms. 16 Their results revealed few inflammatory cells in the vasa vasorum in unruptured aneurysms. However, in ruptured aneurysms, numerous inflammatory cells are found in the vasa vasorum that infiltrate the vessel wall. This may have also contributed to the instability of the aneurysm. Furthermore, a case report suggested that increased blood flow through the vasa vasorum can lead to wall remodeling and aneurysm growth even after successful occlusion of the aneurysm lumen. 17 In our case, the decision to perform surgical clipping and coagulation of the aneurysm wall was based on the assumption that obliteration of the potential vasa vasorum might help prevent further aneurysm growth and rupture. However, postoperative hemorrhage and the subsequent need for flow-diverter placement highlight the challenges in managing complex recurrent aneurysms, even when the vasa vasorum is targeted.

In our case, detecting the cause of the hemorrhage was challenging. The hemorrhage occurred 5 days after clipping. DSA and CT angiography revealed no extravasation or suspicious regions. There was no change in the filling of the enhanced agent into the aneurysm dome compared with the prehemorrhagic condition. Therefore, we can only discuss possible causes of bleeding after surgical clipping. An association between intra-aneurysmal thrombosis and bleeding has been reported. 18 In this case, most of the aneurysms were thrombosed, and we used ring clips to avoid a coil mass. Therefore, the recanalization occurred, and partial thrombosis may have induced bleeding due to the weakness of the aneurysm walls.

Our experience yields three practical points. First, clinicians should strive for definitive occlusion at the initial procedure, yet remain alert for delayed hemorrhage, because flow-diverter stenting can serve as an effective rescue when clipping or coiling is incomplete. Second, early postoperative angiography, complemented by vessel-wall MRI when available, may detect silent recurrence related to vasa-vasorum-driven wall fragility. Third, pooled multicenter data, enriched by computational flow modeling and histopathology, are needed to establish true recurrence rates, define the safety limits of flow diverters in small, duplicated vessels, and guide evidence-based follow-up and retreatment algorithms for this challenging lesion.

Conclusion

This case report presents a rare case of recurrent and ruptured DMCA aneurysm managed with multiple treatment modalities, including a flow-diverter stent, highlighting the unique challenges associated with these lesions. The complex anatomy and potential for recurrence should be considered when managing DMCA aneurysms. Our experience suggests the importance of considering multiple treatment modalities and customizing treatment approaches to get complete obliteration at the first session based on individual aneurysm characteristics and patient factors. The flow-diverter treatment is emerging as a promising option for the treatment of complex or recurrent aneurysms. Close follow-up and a multidisciplinary approach are crucial for managing DMCA aneurysms. Further research is needed to better understand the underlying mechanisms of aneurysm formation and growth in the context of DMCA and to identify optimal treatment strategies for these challenging lesions.
