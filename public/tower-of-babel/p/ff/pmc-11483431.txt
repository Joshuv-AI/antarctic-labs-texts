Determination of Calcium Isotopes Using Inductive Coupled Plasma–Tandem Mass Spectrometry with Ozone-Induced Oxidation
Authors: Daiki Asakawa, Yanbei Zhu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, we propose a new method for determination of calcium (Ca) isotopes using inductive coupled plasma–tandem mass spectrometry (ICP–MS/MS). The most abundant isotope of Ca, 40Ca, has a spectrum identical with that of argon (Ar), which is often used as the carrier gas in ICP–MS analysis. The detection capability for 40Ca is thus significantly reduced in the presence of 40Ar. To avoid interference from 40Ar during the 40Ca measurements, ozone (O3) was used as the reaction gas in the ICP–MS/MS analysis. The reaction of Ca+ with O3 in the collision cell preferentially produced CaO3+. In contrast, the reaction between Ar+ and O3 produced ArO+, which further reacted with the oxidized O3 to produce Ar+. Because Ar+ does not produced ArO3+ as the main product of the reaction with O3, the measurement of Ca+ as CaO3 via O3-induced oxidation resulted in considerably less interference from Ar+. The detailed mechanism of CaO3+ formation was investigated via quantum chemistry calculations by using density functional theory. The interaction between Ca+ and O3 yielded CaO3+, which immediately dissociated into either Ca+ and O3 or CaO+ and O2 because the pressure in the collision cell was approximately 1 Pa in the MS/MS experiments. Consequently, CaO3+ was formed by the consecutive reaction of Ca+ with O3, with CaO+ and CaO2+ as intermediates. The present method achieved the detection of less abundant Ca isotopes in certified reference materials such as 43Ca, 44Ca, 46Ca, and 48Ca, thereby allowing the determination of Ca isotopes with high sensitivity.

Calcium (Ca) is the sixth most abundant element on Earth1 and fifth most abundant in animals. Ca plays an important role in geological and biological processes. Because Ca consists of 20 protons, which are the magic numbers of nuclei, Ca isotopes are considerably more stable than the other elements. Consequently, Ca has 26 known isotopes, ranging from 35Ca to 60Ca.2 Among these, four isotopes, that is, 40Ca (96.941%), 42Ca (0.647%), 43Ca (0.135%), and 44Ca (2.086%), are known as stable isotopes. In addition, 0.004% of 46Ca and 0.187% of 48Ca exist as long-live radioactive isotopes. The half-life of 46Ca and 48Ca are 2.8 × 1015 and 4.3 × 1019 years, respectively.

Inductively coupled plasma–mass spectrometry (ICP–MS) is a powerful analytical tool for characterizing chemical elements and their isotopes with high sensitivity and sample throughput. However, the detection limit of an analyte significantly deteriorates when interfering ions with a similar mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) are present. In particular, the most abundant isotope of Ca, 40Ca, has a spectrum identical with that of argon (Ar), which is often used as the carrier gas in ICP–MS analysis. The most abundant Ar isotope is 40Ar, and the mass difference between 40Ca and 40Ar is only 0.20774 mDa. The required resolution of the mass spectrometer for separating 40Ca+ and 40Ar+ is approximately 200,000, which is unachievable using commercial instruments. Recently, the analysis of Ca isotopes has been demonstrated using cool plasma ICP–MS, which allows the selective generation of Ca+ and prevents the ionization of Ar.3 In contrast, the detection of less abundant Ca isotopes, such as 43Ca, 44Ca, 46Ca, 48Ca, and other trace radioisotopes, is hampered by the decrease in the plasma temperature in ICP–MS.

The most common approach for removing the interfering ions is the use of tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) with a collision cell.4−6 Ammonia (NH3), methane (CH4), and hydrogen (H2) are often used to remove the interference of Ar+,4,6 because the reaction of Ar+ with NH3, CH4, and H2 is reported to occur with a rate constant greater than 10–9 molecules–1 cm3 s–1.7−9 In contrast, Ca+ barely reacts with these gases, resulting in the improvement of the detection limit of 40Ca+ via ICP–MS/MS using NH3 and CH4 as the collision gases. In contrast, the radioactive isotope of potassium (K) with a long half-life 40K+ is also an interference ion for 40Ca+. Because the ionization energy of K is only 4.34 eV, NH3, CH4 and H2 cannot remove 40K+. Transmission of 40Ca+ and removal of 40Ar+ and 40K+ by collisions with the gases mentioned above are very difficult.

As an alternative approach using ICP–MS/MS, shifting of the m/z value of 40Ca+ can be achieved via the occurrence of a reaction in the collision cell. This method improves the detection limits of analytes, particularly in complex mixtures, by detecting specific product ions originating from the analyte ions undergoing reactions in the collision cell. Oxygen (O2) is a traditional reaction gas that provides the analyte oxide. However, the efficiency of formation of CaO+ by the reaction between Ca+ and O2 is relatively low.6 Nitrous oxide (N2O) is used as the more reactive gas as an alternative to O2.10−12 The reaction of Ca+ and N2O produces CaO+ with a rate constant of 5 × 10–11 molecules–1 cm3 s–1.13 N2O has been used as the reaction gas for the analysis of 40Ca+ using ICP–MS/MS.14

Similar to N2O, ozone (O3) is a notable oxidizing gas.15 The reaction of the analyte cation with O3 produces not only analyte monoxide, but also its multioxide.15 The number of oxygen atoms attached to the analyte cation by the reaction with O3 is dependent on the binding energy of the analyte oxide and oxygen atom.15 Recently, a method using O3 as the reaction gas has been developed to extend the applicability of ICP–MS/MS.16,17 O3 reacts with metal cations at the capture rate,15 strongly suggesting that it is an efficient oxidizing gas for shifting the m/z value of metal cations during MS/MS experiments. The rate constant for CaO+ production by the reaction of Ca+ with O3 was reported to be in the range of 1.6 × 10–10 to 4.8 × 10–10 molecules–1 cm3 s–1,18−20 which is approximately 3–10 times larger than that with N2O. These reports indicate that Ca+ is more reactive with O3 than with N2O. In this study, we demonstrate an improvement in the analytical sensitivity of Ca isotopes, including 40Ca, using ICP–MS/MS with O3 as the reaction gas. CaO3+ was preferentially produced by the reaction between Ca+ and O3. In contrast, Ar+ mainly produced ArO+ by reaction with O3. Consequently, the use of O3 as the reaction gas significantly reduced the interference of Ar+ in the analysis of Ca isotopes. In addition, we investigated the reaction mechanisms of Ca+ and O3 in detail by using density functional theory (DFT) calculations.

Experimental Section

Chemicals

A single-element Ca standard (1000 mg L–1, metrologically traceable to the International System of Units via the Japan Calibration Service System) and ultrapure-grade nitric acid (HNO3) were used to prepare the calibration standard. A test solution containing 0.1 mg L–1 Ca was prepared by diluting the Ca standard solution with water containing 0.3 mol L–1 of HNO3.

Multiple reference materials, coral (GSJ CRM Jcp1), giant clam (GSJ CRM JCt-1), dolomite (GSJ CRM JDo-1), and limestone (GSJ CRM JLs-1) were obtained from the Geological Survey of Japan and analyzed to evaluate the approach used in this study. Digestion of each sample was conducted by adding a mixed acid solution (5 mL, HNO3/HCl = 3:1, v/v) to 0.5 g of solid sample. This mixture of acid solution and solid sample was put in an oven set to 95 °C for 60 min. After digestion, pure water was added to obtain a 50 mL solution for each sample. Furthermore, simple dilution was conducted to each digested sample to obtain a final solution for measurement by ICP–MS/MS. The final solution contains approximately 0.3 mg L–1 of Ca in 0.3 mol L–1 of HNO3.

Instruments

All experiments were conducted using an ICP tandem mass spectrometer (Agilent 8800s, Agilent Technologies, CA, USA) with online generated O3 as the reaction gas. The concentration of O3 was approximately 10.5% (v/v) in the oxygen matrix. Introduction of O3 into the ICP tandem mass spectrometer was performed using the fourth cell line, where a 3-way valve permitted the switching between O3 and pure oxygen for their comparison as the cell gas. The operating conditions for the ICP tandem mass spectrometer were optimized based on the typical parameters used for the oxygen reaction.

Calculations

All electronic structure calculations were performed using the Gaussian 16 suite.21 The energies of the atomic cations were obtained using the ωB97X-D hybrid functional22 and the split-valence triple-ζ basis set, 6-311++G(2df) level of theory. The optimized geometries of the complexes of atomic cations and oxygen atoms were obtained using the ωB97X-D/6-311++G(2df) level of theory. The obtained geometries were characterized by using frequency calculations to determine the zero-point vibrational energy. To establish the energetics required for the reaction to occur, the transition state geometries were optimized at the ωB97X-D/6-311++G(2df-) level and confirmed by verifying the existence of an imaginary part of their vibrational frequencies. The connections among the transition states, reactants, and products were examined using intrinsic reaction coordinate analysis starting from the transition state geometry. The harmonic frequencies obtained by frequency analysis were used to determine the zero-point energy corrections.

Results and Discussion

Reaction with O2

First, a 0.3 mol L–1 aqueous HNO3 solution was analyzed by using ICP–MS/MS as the blank without Ca. The ion corresponding to the peak obtained at m/z 40 and O2 were used as the precursor ion and reaction gas, respectively, for the MS/MS analysis. The radio frequency (RF) power of the ICP ranged from 700 to 1400 W and was adjusted stepwise by 50 W. The precursor ion at m/z 40 corresponded to 40Ar+ under these experimental conditions. As shown in Figure 1a, the signal intensities were plotted as a function of the RF power of the ICP system for the product ions obtained at m/z 40, 56, 72, and 88, which corresponded to 40Ar+, 40Ar16O+, 40Ar16O2+, and 40Ar16O3+, respectively. The signal intensity increased with an increasing RF power for all selected product ions, indicating that the amount of Ar+ produced by ICP increased with an increasing RF power.

Subsequently, Ca was added to the 0.3 mol L–1 aqueous HNO3 solution at a final concentration of 0.1 mg L–1, which was analyzed by using the same procedure as that used for the blank experiment. Under these conditions, the ions corresponding to the peak obtained at m/z 40 were a mixture of 40Ar+ and 40Ca+. After the MS/MS analysis of the precursor ion with m/z 40, the signal intensities of the peaks of the product ions obtained at m/z 40, 56, 72, and 88 were plotted as a function of RF power, as shown in Figure 1b. The chemical compositions of the product ions were 40Ca16On+ or 40Ar16On+, with n = 0–3. The signal intensities of the peaks of the product ions plotted in Figure 1b were several orders of magnitude greater than those plotted in Figure 1a when the RF power of the ICP was below 900 W. These results indicate that the use of plasma with a lower RF power preferentially produces Ca+ and prevents the ionization of Ar because the ionization energy of Ca is 6.11 eV, which is considerably lower than that of Ar (15.76 eV). According to a recent study, ICP–MS with RF power below 600 W allows the selective generation of Ca+ and prevents the ionization of Ar.3 However, the signal intensity of the peak of the ion at m/z 40 was very weak when the RF power was below 700 W under the experimental conditions set in this study. Therefore, the RF power was set to 850 W unless otherwise noted. The corresponding condition resulted in a stronger signal for 40Ca+ than for 40Ar+.

Figure 1c shows the product ion spectrum of the precursor ion with m/z 40, with O2 as the reaction gas. The vertical axis represents the signal intensity ratio obtained from the MS/MS spectrum of 0.3 mol L–1 HNO3 with 0.1 mg L–1 Ca divided by that of the blank without Ca. Compared to the blank experiment, the addition of Ca significantly increased the signal intensities of the peaks of the product ions obtained at m/z 56 and 72, which correspond to 40Ca16O+ and 40Ca16O2+, respectively. The product ions originating from Ar+ and Ca+ after their reactions with O2 are shown in Figure 1d,e, respectively. Ar+ yielded 0.3% ArO+ (Figure 1d). In contrast, the reaction of Ca+ with O2 resulted in 7.4% CaO+ and 1.5% of CaO2+.

The reactions of Ar+ and Ca+ with the O2 were investigated via DFT calculations. Figure 1f illustrates the reaction pathway of Ar+ and O2 calculated using the ωB97X-D/6-311++G(2df) level of theory. The interaction between Ar+ and O2 produced intermediate complex ArO2+, which is more stable than the reactants by 3.59 eV. Although the association reaction of Ar+ and O2 is exothermic, ArO2+ was not obtained as the main product in the MS/MS experiments. Regarding the exothermic association reaction, the energy accumulated in product by the association is liberated by collision with a third body. The ion/molecule association reaction is usually observed at pressure of above 100 Pa.23 The pressure in the octopole collision cell is typically maintained at 1 Pa for the MS/MS experiments. Consequently, the association of O2 with Ar+ is unlikely to occur under these ICP–MS/MS experimental conditions. Consequently, ArO2+ dissociates into either Ar+ and O2 or ArO+ and O. The formation of ArO+ involves the cleavage of the O–O bond, which is endothermic (2.70 eV) compared to the reactants Ar+ and O2. The low yield of ArO+ from the reaction of Ar+ with O2 is possibly due to its high endothermicity.

The reaction between Ca+ and O2 is illustrated in Figure 1g. The binding between Ca+ and O2 produced the intermediate complex, CaOO+, which is more stable than the reactants by 1.55 eV. Here, we considered whether the ion corresponding to the peak obtained at m/z 72 (CaO2+) was produced by the association reaction of Ca+ with O2. According to a previous study, the reaction of Ca+ with O2 proceeds with the third-order rate constant of (1.6 ± 0.4) × 10–29 molecules–2 cm6 s–2, when N2 was used as the third body.19 Assuming that the pressure in the octopole collision cell is 1 Pa, the time scale of the association reaction is approximately 1 s. Because the residence time of the ions in the octopole collision cell is typically on the millisecond time scale, CaO2+ is unlikely to be formed by the reaction of Ca+ with O2. Consequently, CaOO+ dissociates into either Ca+ and O2 or CaO+ and O, similar to Ar+ and O2. The formation of CaO+ and O from Ca+ and O2 is an endothermic reaction (1.67 eV). Subsequently, CaO+ further reacts with O2 to produces CaO2+; the corresponding reaction pathway is illustrated in Figure 1h. The interaction between CaO+ and O2 weakens the Ca–O bonds. Consequently, the resultant CaO2+ has a cyclic structure, and O is produced via the transition state TS(CaO+–O2). The corresponding reaction is endothermic (1.13 eV). The low yields of CaO+ and CaO2+ from the reaction of Ca+ with O2 can be attributed to the endothermicity of the reaction.

Reaction with O3

In this section, we focus on the MS/MS analysis of the ion at m/z 40 with O3 as the reaction gas. Figure 2a,b show the signal intensities for the peaks of the product ions obtained at m/z 40, 56, 72, and 88 from the MS/MS analysis of the analyte solutions, that is, 0.3 mol L–1 of HNO3 without and with 0.1 mg L–1 of Ca, respectively. The RF power of the ICP system ranged from 700 to 1400 W and was adjusted stepwise by 50 W. As in the case of Figure 1, the comparsion of Figures 2a and 2b indicated that the preferential ionization of 40Ca was achieved when the RF power of the ICP was around 850 W. Consequently, the RF power was set as 850 W. Figure 2c shows the product ion spectrum of the precursor ion at m/z 40, with O3 as the reaction gas. The vertical axis represents the signal intensity ratio obtained from the MS/MS spectrum of 0.3 mol L–1 HNO3 with 0.1 mg L–1 Ca divided by that of the blank without Ca. The product ion spectrum clearly showed that the peak at m/z 88 corresponding to CaO3+ was selectively produced by the reaction between Ca+ and O3. In contrast, the peak at m/z 104 corresponding to CaO4+ was almost absent. The product ions originating from Ar+ and Ca+ owing to the reaction with O3 are shown in Figure 2d,e, respectively. ArO+ (2.2%), ArO2+ (0.8%), and ArO3+ (0.7%) were obtained from the reaction of Ar+ with O3. In contrast, the reaction of Ca+ with O3 resulted in CaO+ (7.0%), CaO2+ (3.9%), and CaO3+ (86.3%).

To demonstrate that O3 is a more efficient reaction gas than isotope O2, ICP–MS/MS experiments were conducted by changing the reaction gas from O2 to O3. The ion at m/z 40 was used as the precursor ion, and the signal intensities of the peaks of the product ions obtained at m/z 56, 72, and 88 were monitored (Figure 2f). The reaction gas was changed from O2 to O3 at 13 min. The signal intensity of the peak of the ion obtained at m/z 40 decreased as the reaction gas was changed from O2 to O3. In contrast, the signal intensity of the peak of the ion obtained at m/z 88, which corresponds to CaO3+, increased significantly by approximately 1000 times. These results clearly indicate that the O3 is an efficient reaction gas for the analysis of Ca using ICP–MS/MS.

Thereafter, reactions of Ar+ and Ca+ with O3 were investigated by using DFT calculations at the ωB97X-D/6-311++G(2df) level. Figure 3a illustrates the formation pathway of ArO+ via a reaction between Ar+ and O3. The interaction between Ar+ and O3 produced an intermediate complex, ArO3+, which is more stable than the reactants by 3.56 eV. Subsequently, ArO3+ dissociated into ArO+ and O2 via the formation of the transition state TS(ArO+–O2) and the intermediate complex ArO+–O2. The formation of ArO+ from Ar+ and O3 was exothermic (2.03 eV). Although the formation of ArO+ and O2 was exothermic, the yield of ArO+ was only 2.2%, when O3 was used as the reaction gas. To explain the low yield of ArO+ under the present experimental conditions, we examined whether ArO+ was lost during the reaction with O3. The energy diagram of the reaction between Ar+ and O3 is shown in Figure 3b. The interaction between Ar+ and O3 produced an intermediate complex, ArO4+, which then dissociated into Ar+ and two O2 molecules via formation of the transition state, TS[Ar+–(O2)2]. The corresponding reaction was exothermic and therefore occurred easily under the present experimental conditions. The low yield of ArO+ under these experimental conditions can be explained by the reaction of ArO+ with the reaction of O3 to produce Ar+.

Energy diagrams of the reactions of (a) Ar+, (b) ArO+, (c) Ca+, (d) CaO+, (e) CaO2+, and (f) CaO3+ with O3. The relative energies (eV) were calculated using DFT at the ωB97X-D/6-311++G(2df-) level, and include the zero-point vibrational energies.

The reaction between Ca+ and O3, which is the main focus of this study, mainly produced a peak at m/z 88 corresponding to CaO3+, in the ICP–MS/MS spectrum (Figure 2). As discussed above, ion–molecule association is unlikely to occur under the present ICP–MS/MS experimental conditions. Figure 3c illustrates the reaction pathway for the interaction between Ca+ and O3, which produced the intermediate complex CaO3+, and then dissociates into either Ca+ and O3 or CaO+ and O2. CaO3+ preferentially produces CaO+ and O2 because these products are formed via a pathway more stable than that for Ca+ and O3. O3 has been reported to react with Ca+ at the capture rate, producing CaO+.15 The resultant CaO+ is then converted into CaO2+ by further O3 addition and subsequent O2 loss (Figure 3d). Subsequently, CaO2+ undergoes O3 addition, accompanied by O2 loss, producing CaO3+ (Figure 3e). O3-induced oxidation of Ca+, CaO+, and CaO2+ is exothermic. Consequently, CaO3+ is formed by the consecutive reactions of Ca+ with O3.

CaO3+ can react with O3 and the expected product of this reaction is CaO4+. However, the peak at m/z 104, which corresponds to CaO4+, was almost absent in the ICP–MS/MS spectrum of Ca+, with the reaction gas being O3 (Figure 2c). To explain the absence of CaO4+ under the present experimental conditions, the reaction pathway for the interaction of CaO3+ with the molecule of O3 was investigated in detail. An energy diagram of the reaction is shown in Figure 3f. The interaction between CaO3+ and O3 produced CaO6+, which was then converted to CaO4+ via the loss of the O2 loss. Notably, this reaction is slightly endothermic, and the reaction rate is low compared to those for the formation of CaO+, CaO2+, and CaO3+. In addition, CaO4+ can dissociate to form CaO2+ and O2, as shown in Figure 3d. The transition state barrier for this dissociation reaction was 0.21 eV, and the products, CaO2+ and O2, are more stable than CaO4+ by 2.45 eV. Consequently, CaO4+ produced by the reaction of CaO3+ with O3 immediately decomposed into CaO2+ and O2. Thus, CaO3+ has the highest number of oxygen atoms and is the main product of the reaction between Ca+ and O3. The high yield of CaO3+ and the absence of the peak corresponding to CaO4+ can be understood by using DFT calculations.

As described above, 40K+ is also an interference ion for 40Ca+, K+ does not react with O3.16 Consequently, measuring 40Ca+ as 40CaO3 using MS/MS with O3 avoids interference from 40K+.

The use of N2O as the reaction gas for the MS/MS analysis of Ca+ selectively produces CaO+, according to previous studies14 Because Ca+ produces a higher yield of monoxide than Ar+, the detection limit of Ca can be improved using N2O as the reaction gas in MS/MS analysis. Nevertheless, Ar+ also produces ArO+ in MS/MS analysis using an oxidizing gas, suggesting that the analysis of Ca+ are slightly subject to the interference of Ar+ when N2O is used as the reaction gas. Therefore, the measurement of Ca+ as CaO3 via the O3-induced oxidation resulted in less interference from Ar+.

Application to Ca Concentration Analysis in Reference Materials

Ca concentrations in multiple certified reference materials were determined by using ICP–MS/MS with O3 as the reaction gas. The results are shown in Figure 4; in comparison to each certified value, they are shown as a solid line. For each certified reference material, the results observed for each calcium isotope agreed with the certified value in terms of the expanded uncertainty of the results and certified value. This can be explained by the fact that the application of O3 aided in the free-of-spectral-interference measurement of calcium isotopes, including 40Ca, which typically suffer from extreme spectral interference from 40Ar. The apparently larger expanded uncertainties for the observed values from the measurement of 46Ca can be attributed to its extremely low abundance (0.004%), with a signal intensity of approximately 2000 counts per second for a sample solution containing approximately 10 mg L–1 calcium. Notably, our method can analyze low-abundance calcium isotopes, that is, 43Ca, 46Ca, and 48Ca. In contrast, the detection of these trace Ca isotopes is hampered by recently reported method using cool plasma ICP–MS.3 The improvement of the detection limit in our method is due to the reaction with O3, which significantly improves the separation of spectral interferences, allowing for the measurement under higher temperature plasma conditions. Notably, the present study was conducted using a single detector ICP–MS/MS instrument, whose typical precision of measurement is approximately 0.5–3% (relative standard deviation). It is expected that highly precise measurements of calcium isotopes can be achieved using multicollector ICP–MS instrument with O3 as the reaction gas, which can cover each calcium isotope.

Conclusions

In this study, we examined the use of ICP–MS/MS, with the reaction gas being O3, for Ca analysis. The reaction between Ca+ and O3 preferentially produces CaO3+. In contrast, Ar+, which is the major interfering ion for Ca+, reacts with O3 to form ArO+. Therefore, measuring Ca+ as CaO3 using MS/MS with an oxidizing agent like O3 avoids interference from Ar+ and K+. This method allows the detection of less abundant Ca isotopes in certified reference materials such as 43Ca, 44Ca, 46Ca, and 48Ca.

Regarding the mechanism, the association reaction of Ca+ with O3 did not occur under the MS/MS experimental conditions at a pressure of approximately 1 Pa. Consequently, CaO3+ was formed by the consecutive reaction of Ca+ with O3 with CaO+ and CaO2+ as intermediates. In conclusion, O3 is one of the most effective oxidizing gases for ICP–MS/MS because it produces oxides of the precursor ions and O2.
