Exploring Staff Perspectives on the Feasibility of Integrating the Environmental Components of a Novel Intervention for Hydration in Long‐Term Care
Authors: Sophia Werden Abrams, Safura Syed, Ashwini Namasivayam‐MacDonald, Raksha Aravind, Kristina Devlin, Wen Liu, Christina Lengyel, Phyllis Gaspar, Janet Mentes, Susan E Slaughter, Minn N Yoon, Heather Keller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundLong‐term care (LTC) residents are prone to dehydration, which has many serious effects on health. A multicomponent intervention is required to mitigate dehydration in LTC; however, previous research has not explored the experiences of staff who deliver interventions. The purpose of this study was to explore staff perspectives on the feasibility of implementing the environmental components of a novel multicomponent hydration intervention.MethodsThe intervention was enacted over 4 weeks on an urban LTC unit in Ontario, Canada. The components were large drinking glasses (300 mL) and a purpose‐built beverage trolley. Observations of workflow by research assistants were conducted throughout the intervention. Personal support staff participated in post‐intervention semi‐structured interviews, framed using the Behaviour Change Wheel, to determine the perceived factors influencing implementation. Reflexive thematic analysis was used to generate qualitative themes from interview transcripts.ResultsEight staff members participated in post‐intervention interviews. Ages ranged from 20 to 49 years, with 1–25 years of experience in LTC. While some minor improvements were suggested, staff were accepting of the environmental strategies and integrated them into the workflow consistently. Four major themes were generated via qualitative analysis and categorised using the Behaviour Change Wheel: (1) optimising efficiency (physical opportunity, reflective motivation), (2) adapting to change (reflective motivation and social opportunity), (3) knowing the resident (reflective motivation, psychological capability) and (4) supporting hydration (physical opportunity, psychological capability, reflective motivation).Implications for PracticeThe results of this study indicate that LTC staff perceive the environmental components of a novel hydration intervention to be feasible and useful in supporting resident hydration.Keywords: dehydration, dehydration prevention protocol, environmental intervention, environmental modification, geriatric care, hydration, long‐term care, multicomponent intervention

Introduction

Dehydration due to low fluid intake is a high‐priority concern for older adults and those who care for them (Beck and Volkert 2023). There are many negative health effects associated with dehydration, including increased falls, acute kidney injury, urinary tract infections, delirium, as well as increased hospitalisation and mortality (Bennett et al. 2004; El‐Sharkawy et al. 2014, 2017; Hooper, Abdelhamid, Attreed, et al. 2015; Mentes and Culp 2003). Reports indicate that up to 38% of long‐term care (LTC) residents may be dehydrated as a result of many contributing factors (Marra et al. 2016; Mentes 2006; Parkinson et al. 2023). Dehydration can occur due to low fluid intake (hypertonic dehydration), or alternatively through water and electrolyte loss (isotonic dehydration) as a result of vomiting, diarrhoea, sweating, haemorrhaging or diuretic and laxative medications (Lacey et al. 2019; Puga et al. 2019). Older adults in LTC are susceptible to both forms of dehydration, which is exacerbated due to cognitive decline, incontinence, dysphagia, polypharmacy, mobility impairments and a reduced sensation of thirst (Hooper et al. 2014; Lacey et al. 2019; Leibovitz et al. 2007; Mentes 2006; Namasivayam and Steele 2015; Volkert et al. 2022; Werden Abrams et al. 2023).

Monitoring and assessing LTC residents for dehydration is a complex issue, as many methods are invasive or unreliable (Volkert et al. 2022). Less invasive preventative measures, such as increasing oral fluid intake, are often inadequate once the negative sequala of dehydration have been observed (Volkert et al. 2022). There are many proposed measures to assess dehydration; however, a growing body of research suggests that serum osmolality is the gold standard (Hooper, Abdelhamid, Ali, et al. 2015; Volkert et al. 2019, 2022). Previously suggested measures, such as skin turgor, urine colour, bioelectrical impedance and weight changes, are not consistently valid nor reliable in assessing dehydration in older adults (Fortes et al. 2015; Hooper, Abdelhamid, Attreed, et al. 2015; Hooper et al. 2016; Volkert et al. 2019). Unfortunately, serum osmolality measures require invasive blood draws and are not a practical means to continually assess the hydration status of each LTC resident (Fortes et al. 2015; Hooper, Abdelhamid, Ali, et al. 2015). While monitoring fluid intake cannot provide diagnostic confirmation of dehydration, it can be valuable to ensure the daily recommended minimum of 1.6–2 L (for women and men, respectively) of fluid are being consumed (Volkert et al. 2019); however, consistent fluid intake monitoring outside of the research contexts is not reliable (Jimoh et al. 2015). Until a valid and reliable non‐invasive assessment is readily available, preventative hydration strategies are essential to improve low‐intake dehydration in LTC residents.

Numerous factors contribute to low fluid intake; however, there are no single evidence‐based preventative interventions to reliably increase fluid intake and reduce rates of dehydration for LTC residents. Instead, a multicomponent hydration intervention is recommended (Bunn et al. 2015; Cook et al. 2019; Low et al. 2015; Volkert et al. 2019). The suggested components of a multicomponent intervention include: a variety of preferred beverages available at frequent intervals; adequate and timely staff assistance for residents requiring support for drinking and toileting; and, staff education on dehydration and the importance of fluid intake (Volkert et al. 2022). While there is a growing body of research outlining ideal strategies to improve fluid intake and reduce dehydration, there is a paucity of research that seeks to understand the experiences of the LTC staff who support resident fluid intake and hydration (Boström et al. 2012; Towers et al. 2020). The majority of LTC staff who provide food and beverages during mealtimes and nourishment are unregulated personal support workers (PSWs) and dietary aides. It is essential to understand the perspectives and motivations of these staff who are responsible for intervention implementation before strategies can be successfully adapted into their workflow. Otherwise, interventions may not be successfully sustained, and resources may be wasted (Slaughter et al. 2020). Frameworks, such as Michie's Behaviour Change Wheel, are essential for translating research into practice and can help contextualise the factors influencing behaviour and implementation of interventions (Low et al. 2015; Michie et al. 2011). The authors of the Behaviour Change Wheel posit that motivation, opportunity and capability are the core factors that can influence changes in behaviour (Michie et al. 2011). See Appendix A for a visual representation of the Behaviour Change Wheel. The purpose of this study was to explore staff perspectives on the perceived feasibility of integrating two environmental components (a purpose‐built beverage trolley and larger drinking glasses) of a novel multicomponent hydration intervention via observations and semi‐structured interviews, using the framework of the Behaviour Change Wheel.

Methods

Ethics approval for this study was granted by the University of Waterloo Ethics Board (#44201). Qualitative methods (observations and semi‐structured interviews) were conducted to understand the experiences of LTC staff who implemented the intervention. The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) Checklist was used to inform this study (Tong et al. 2007), see Appendix B.

Development of PROMOTE

A team of 12 multidisciplinary researchers from across North America in the fields of dietetics, nursing, gerontology and speech‐language pathology was assembled in 2019 to systematically develop a multicomponent hydration intervention. The final iteration of the intervention, titled PROMOTE, includes seven core evidence‐based strategies: Preferred cups and glasses, with the specific requirement that they be easy to hold and contain at least 250 mL of fluid; Reminders to drink, which include direct care staff meetings and huddles to discuss how to promote fluid intake; Offer fluid with other care routines, events and activities; Medication fluid offers, where the nursing team provides fluid with pills and encourages residents to drink; Offer fluids as a social opportunity, recognising that residents drink more with other residents, friends and family; Trolley of drink choices, where three or more beverages are provided to residents between meals; and Educate and encourage, which includes educational videos and posters for staff, residents and families.

The PROMOTE strategies are based on a compilation of evidence from published intervention research; consultations with experts on how to improve hydration in LTC (Ballesteros‐Pomar et al. 2020); a survey of LTC home providers to determine the acceptability of 20 evidence‐based strategies (Keller et al. 2022); a study to determine preferred drinking vessels of LTC residents (i.e., cups and glasses) (Yoon et al. 2022); and a provider review of the proposed multicomponent intervention (Wei et al. 2021). The study on the acceptability of 20 strategies consolidated a list of components believed to be potentially effective and feasible by LTC personnel. These strategies were confirmed by 25 personnel‐informants, who then provided details on how to tailor and introduce the components into LTC homes (Wei et al. 2021). The study on preferred drinking vessels helped to identify a proposed glass that was larger in volume, but still easy to hold (Yoon et al. 2022). During 2022, educational materials and supporting resources were developed. Branding for the PROMOTE intervention and materials was also completed at this time. The educational components of PROMOTE were then evaluated by key informants, and they were deemed to be feasible and useful (Keller et al. 2024). The original intention of this study was to implement all components of the PROMOTE intervention in a pilot study to assess the hydration status of residents pre‐ and post‐intervention; however, this was not possible. Firstly, there were limited funds to implement the full feasibility study. Conducting research in LTC can cost three times more than studies conducted in community settings (Lam et al. 2018). Secondly, the LTC home was recovering from the effects of the COVID‐19 pandemic and did not feel they could add a new process requiring significant staff behaviour changes. The LTC home chose to trial the two environmental components of PROMOTE, which are described in detail below.

Environmental Components

Two environmental components of the larger PROMOTE intervention were implemented: larger drinking glasses, which were deemed to be preferred by residents (Yoon et al. 2022), and a beverage trolley to deliver beverages between meals. The goals of these two components target various aspects of the seven core PROMOTE strategies. The LTC home already used a simple cart to deliver in‐between‐meal beverages. The purpose‐built beverage trolley that was trialled was larger and had special features (e.g., hydration logo could be put on the side, a built‐in cold drawer). As it was specific to fluid delivery, it was believed it might increase excitement among staff and residents by emphasising the importance of hydration as part of existing snack routines. Larger drinking glasses offered in the trolley also functioned to encourage hydration and offered an additional cup choice for residents. While these two environmental components do not encapsulate all strategies within the PROMOTE intervention, they do address key aspects identified to support hydration.

Larger Drinking Glasses

Our preliminary data identified that a lightweight plastic drinking glass with ridged sides was considered preferable by residents (Yoon et al. 2022). The larger 300 mL (Aladdin Temp‐Rite. Reusable Low‐Profile Tumbler 12 Oz., Clear (80 per Case) ‐ DMT212, n.d.), glass comfortably held 225 mL which was a 75 mL increase in volume, while maintaining a similar weight to the previous glasses (see Figure 1). Despite the potential for acceptability based on the study team's previous research, it was not known if all residents would be able handle the larger circumference and added weight of the increased volume, and if staff would fill the glass to its maximum. Further, it was unknown if the glass would fit with current routines (e.g., stackable storage) and meet dishwashing requirements.

Beverage Trolley

The research team collaborated with Burlodge to identify a rolling beverage trolley with pull‐out drawers for cups and glasses, as well as a surface for storing liquids that would enhance beverage delivery in the LTC home. Beverage trolleys have been used in previous LTC hydration interventions, but have not previously been integrated within a multicomponent hydration intervention (Robinson and Rosher 2002). The PROMOTE Burlodge trolley was customisable and included cooling thermal inserts as well as replaceable branding. In the picture below, the PROMOTE logo was used to customise this purpose‐built trolley (see Figure 2). The beverage trolley was stocked by dietary staff and used by PSWs to deliver fluids between meals to resident rooms. The feasibility of determining the manoeuvrability, storage and use of the trolley by regular staff was assessed as part of this study. Training on the use of the beverage trolley prior to intervention (i.e., operation of the directional caster wheels) was offered to LTC home staff by Burlodge; however, it was declined by the home. As this was a research study, no adaptations were made to the beverage trolley during the intervention phase. In a typical clinical setting, ongoing adaptations and training would be provided by the manufacturer based on the needs of the LTC home.

Burlodge beverage trolley.

Setting

The two environmental components of the PROMOTE intervention were tested over 4 weeks in early 2023 at a for‐profit LTC home in London, Ontario, Canada. The LTC home was selected based on its interest in improving the hydration of their residents. The intervention was implemented at one 32‐resident neighbourhood within a larger 192‐resident LTC home. The LTC home has a total of six neighbourhoods, including two secure neighbourhoods for residents living with dementia, each with 32 residents. The neighbourhood that implemented PROMOTE had residents with a mixture of diagnoses and was not a secure unit. Most residents had some physical impairments. As the focus of this study was on the experiences of staff, no resident‐specific data were collected. A typical daytime shift was staffed by two nursing staff members and four PSWs.

Observations of the Long‐Term Care Home Neighbourhood

Two research assistants (SWA and SS, both doctoral trainees and healthcare providers) completed 10 observations at the LTC home throughout the 4‐week intervention. Each research assistant made on‐site unplanned visits for 2–3 h for all weeks of the PROMOTE intervention. There was a minimum of one morning and one afternoon observation per week. Each research assistant completed observation sessions independently. Observations took place during meals and between meals when PSWs took the beverage trolley around to all residents. Observation tracking included: (1) whether three different drink choices were offered on the trolley; (2) whether thickened fluids were offered, and if so, whether more than one choice was available; (3) whether the new larger glasses were used at meals and on the trolley, and if so, whether the old smaller glasses were also used; (4) if drinking glasses were fully filled; and (5) if fluids were offered at recreation events. Research assistants also noted how staff interacted with the trolley, as well as any comments and opinions expressed by LTC staff during observations.

Qualitative Interviews and Analysis

All full‐time staff members in the neighbourhood were invited to participate in one‐on‐two semi‐structured interviews with the research assistants (one participant to two research assistants). Most staff in the neighbourhood were full‐time. Part‐time staff who only worked in the neighbourhood occasionally were not eligible to participate, as they were less familiar with the residents and routines. An email was sent by the LTC home to staff after the 4‐week testing period. Invitation notices were distributed at team meetings and team leads provided the information verbally during team huddles. Research assistants also informed staff of interviews while conducting observations. The interview guide (Appendix C) was developed iteratively by the PROMOTE team, after which it was reassessed using Michie et al.'s (2011) Behaviour Change Wheel to ensure that the topics of capability, opportunity and motivation were sufficiently captured. The Behaviour Change Wheel is an implementation science framework that was developed to encapsulate the critical components of 19 pre‐existing frameworks (Michie et al. 2011). The Behaviour Change Wheel can be used to support the implementation of interventions by identifying potential gaps in design and policy creation (Michie et al. 2011). The three core factors can be further subdivided as follows: psychological capability and physical capability; social opportunity and physical opportunity; automatic motivation and reflective motivation. These subdomains were used to categorise themes generated during the semi‐structured interviews.

Any staff member who consented to participate in an interview received a $10 gift card honorarium. Interviews took place in a private room within the LTC neighbourhood during shift changes to accommodate as many staff as possible. Interviews were conducted by research assistants (SWA and SS), both doctoral students and clinicians (speech‐language pathologist and dietitian, respectively) with expertise in LTC. Both interviewers conduct research and work clinically with older adults and LTC residents, and therefore both have a vested interest in the topic. All interviews were audio recorded on two devices. After all interviews were completed, they were transcribed and checked for accuracy by a third research assistant (RA) who removed all identifying information.

A reflexive thematic analysis, as described by Braun and Clarke (2019), was used to analyse the semi‐structured interview transcripts. A primarily inductive and data‐driven approach was used for open‐coding. The analysis process was completed recursively and iteratively using the six phases outlined by Braun and Clarke (2019). All interview transcripts were read through for a holistic familiarisation of the data. During the initial reading, memos, key concepts and initial patterns were noted independently by two researchers (SWA and HK). Memos consisted of thoughts each researcher had upon reviewing the thematic documents, as well as notes from meetings between the two researchers. While Braun and Clarke (2019) typically do not recommend that multiple researchers be involved in the coding process, they note that it is beneficial for early‐career researchers to share coding with more experienced researchers to support the reflexivity process. Therefore, coding was completed independently by one trainee researcher (SWA) and an experienced researcher (HK). Each transcript was open‐coded at a semantic and latent level where a code equated to a word or short phrase representative of a larger concept (Braun and Clarke 2019). The researchers met to discuss codes and how patterns of meaning could be made of the codes. Thematic documents that defined the codes and provided exemplar quotes were used to support this discussion and interpretation. Initial themes were iteratively generated based on patterns observed within the codes. Themes were defined as broader ideas that captured patterns observed across the transcripts (Braun and Clarke 2019). The initial themes were shared with the larger team for discussion, reflection and refinement. This team also included the other research assistant who was present during observations and interviews. Before finalising the themes, the criteria described by Braun and Clarke (2019) were reviewed to determine the boundaries, range, meaning and quality of each theme. All code and theme iterations, consolidations, changes and removals were tracked throughout the analysis process. The final iteration of the themes is included in the results.

Results

Observations

During the observations, the research assistants identified that there were consistently three beverage choices available on the trolley (during 9/10 observations, 90%). Beverages included water, milk, juice, tea and coffee. Having three beverages available is standard practice in LTC and is consistent with offerings prior to the integration of PROMOTE. Thickened fluids were only offered one time (10%), as no residents required liquid modification. Thickened liquids were initially offered as an artefact, as a resident requiring thickened beverages lived in the neighbourhood when the trolley was originally stocked. The new larger drinking glasses were used consistently (100%) and were typically fully filled (70%). Smaller drinking glasses were used in lieu of larger glasses on one occasion (10%) as the resident required a lid and on another occasion (10%) due to a lack of clean larger glasses. No recreation events occurred during the observation sessions because of continued concerns about illness outbreaks after the fall and Christmas season of 2022. Various advantages and limitations of the beverage trolley and larger drinking glasses were identified during the observations. The observed advantages of the beverage trolley included: ample space for preparing drinks and snacks; designated areas for organising napkins, utensils, condiments and dishes; and the ability to facilitate multiple people working together on the spacious worktop. It was also noted that having larger jugs or more than one jug for each beverage choice was useful when the supply ran out before all residents were served. Prior to the integration of PROMOTE, restocking of the trolley was required to serve all residents. While the new trolley did not increase beverage options, the trolley offered increased efficiency, as it eliminated the need for multiple trolleys and multiple trips. There were also some limitations of the drink trolley, which were expressed by the LTC home staff to the research assistants during observation sessions. The beverage trolley was larger than the historical cart used in the home, taking up more space and potentially impeding movement in and around the cart, hallways and dining areas. Some staff members had difficulty manoeuvring the trolley when fully loaded, which was likely due to the directional caster wheels on the cart being disengaged. Staff felt there was a lack of space for both solid and liquid garbage, and the bins added by staff were noted to fall off frequently. Overall, staff suggested some minor alterations to the trolley, such as improved access to various compartments on both sides, larger wheels to improve manoeuvrability and a protective barrier to prevent spills and items from falling off the trolley. Some residents were occasionally skipped during the room‐to‐room trolley rounds, reducing their access to beverages; however, it was not clear why these residents were not offered items.

The larger glasses had a positive reception from the staff. Lids for the home's current drinking glasses were compatible with the larger glasses, allowing for easy covering. Initially, staff used smaller glasses for residents who required lids on their glasses; however, once it was discovered that the lids fit on the larger glasses, the smaller glasses were no longer used. During snacks and mealtimes, the larger glasses were filled to approximately three‐quarters of their capacity, with a serving of approximately 225 mL. Similarly, staff filled the smaller cups to about three‐quarters full, at approximately 150 mL. Staff reported that residents were able to lift and handle the larger glasses comfortably. Staff provided beverages in residents' preferred personal cups if requested. They also gave multiple beverages to any residents who requested.

Interviews

Eight participants completed semi‐structured interviews. Seven interviews took place in person and one interview took place over the phone due to scheduling constraints. The interviews ranged in duration from eight to 13 min. While interviews were relatively short, data saturation was achieved and no new themes were generated by the final interview. Discussion of the perception and experience of adapting to and delivering the environmental components of PROMOTE was not overly complex and therefore did not result in lengthy interviews. This interview length is consistent with previous studies involving PSWs, with one study having an average interview time of 4 min (Stephens and Knight 2022). Staff time constraints are a known barrier to research in LTC, and if dedicated time and extra staffing were supported by managerial staff for the interviews, it is possible that they may have been longer (Law and Ashworth 2022). Typically, participants were interviewed while they were on shift at the LTC home. Seven of the participants were PSWs, and one was a recreation therapist. No nursing or managerial staff chose to participate. This is likely due to a lack of familiarity with the beverage trolley and drinking glasses. Further, PSWs had more direct interaction with the research staff and were able to develop a familiarity during the observations; positive relationships are a primary facilitator for research in LTC (Law and Ashworth 2022). The PSW role at this LTC home includes direct care, eating and drinking support, washroom assistance, meal delivery and offering beverages between meals. The recreation therapist role includes planning and leading daily activities for residents, as well as meal delivery and offering of beverages. See Table 2 for participant demographics.

Four themes pertaining to the implementation of the environmental components of the PROMOTE programme were generated, Behaviour Change Wheel categorisation is noted in brackets: (1) optimising efficiency (physical opportunity, reflective motivation); (2) adapting to change (reflective motivation, social opportunity); (3) knowing the resident (reflective motivation, psychological capability); and (4) supporting hydration (physical opportunity, psychological capability, reflective motivation).

Optimising Efficiency (Physical Opportunity, Reflective Motivation)

Participants identified both the beverage trolley and larger glasses as helpful in optimising efficiency of their workflow. Perceived improvements in efficiency were of high value to the participants. During workflow observations, staff made casual comments to researchers that they felt busy and overworked. These sentiments are also reflected during the semi‐structured interviews, with one PSW remarking, ‘It has everything I need, I don't find myself running back to the kitchenette to grab something’ (P5). Some participants expressed that the PROMOTE strategies were initially ‘another thing for [us] to do’ (P1), noting a preliminary feeling of burden towards adapting their workflow. After implementing the interventions, participants changed their opinion and were pleased with how the environmental interventions were integrated into their routines. For example, they ‘noticed a huge difference [in their] ability to serve the residents, like, efficiently’ (P4). Participants also reflected on how inadequate staffing affected residents, such as whether residents ‘on more of a thickened fluid actually get as much fluid intake as those that are on regular consistency’ (P8). They noted that those consuming thickened liquids often needed physical assistance from staff to drink (i.e., holding the cup or bringing the cup to their mouth). Additionally, while no residents were currently consuming thickened fluids, staff recalled that previous residents who required thickened fluids were often non‐verbal and therefore could not request drink preferences as easily as other residents.

Staff noted that the beverage trolley improved efficiency both in its ability to dispense a range of drink options and simultaneously collect items requiring disposal. For example, one participant noted, ‘If we were collecting something dirty, we don't have to walk all the way back to the kitchen to dispose of it and then continue on we just put it away and keep going’ (P7) to serve other residents. The beverage trolley had more space for supplies and multiple drink options compared to previously used trolleys, which had a smaller surface area and no storage drawers. Further, the drawers and storage on the trolley made it easy for staff to improve sanitation and infection control, ‘so it eliminates the kind of mess and potential like the infection control when we're placing those things [dirty dishes and garbage] away’ (P4). The trolley also prevented residents from accessing the contents of the cart, ‘[…] with our old carts […] residents could come by and touch anything’ (P1). Staff also felt that the trolley would work well to support future residents needing thickened drinks, as it would have sufficient space for a can of beverage thickener and multiple pre‐thickened drink options.

The larger glasses improved efficiency as staff were not required to ‘run back and forth to do a lot of top‐ups’ (P7), and residents typically required only ‘the one glass instead of giving [residents] two or three’ (P2) to meet fluid intake goals. PSW participants also found that the larger glasses were ‘a lot easier for [residents] to hold’ (P4), which improved efficiency by reducing the amount of physical staff support required. The recreation therapist, however, perceived that residents found the larger cups more difficult to hold, ‘but I did realize though, that some residents with smaller hands or with arthritis couldn't really grip the cup as easily as they could the other ones’ (P8). This finding reinforces the PROMOTE principle of preferred drinking glasses, choice and adapting to resident needs.

Adapting to Change (Reflective Motivation, Social Opportunity)

Participants commented on initial resistance in implementing the new environmental strategies, and some disliked the drink trolley initially, ‘like, at the beginning, I think we were kind of like, ‘oh my God, this thing is huge’ (P1). Another PSW remarked, ‘It took a little while for us to get used to [the trolley]’ (P6). There was a range in the ability of participants to adapt to the changes required to integrate the strategies. Participants reflected on being comfortable in the routines of their previously established workflow, ‘you've been doing things for so many years, you just become accustomed to the same thing’ (P5). During observations by the research team, participants verbally expressed resistance to changes during the initial stages; however, during the post‐intervention interviews, almost all feedback about the strategies was positive. Some suggestions for physical adaptations to the beverage trolley were suggested, but no participants expressed a dislike or unwillingness towards continuation of the two strategies.

Participants also perceived that the residents had difficulty adjusting to change, noting during the interviews that ‘they are set in their ways’ (P1). For example, some residents were resistant to the change in glasses and participants noted that certain residents explicitly told them, “Oh, you're changing my glasses at me now,’ right? – So no, ‘I want the old ones” (P3). Some participants also felt that the larger glasses were intimidating, ‘our residents who we already struggle with to drink are intimidated by it’ (P1). PSWs described adapting by offering smaller glasses to those who asked, ‘okay this person's not going to drink 300 [mL], I can give you a small glass’ (P5), or by ‘filling [glasses] up halfway’ (P1), ‘I would rather give them three of those large cups that have half portions than to give them one full one where they won't touch it’ (P1). Interestingly, this conflicts with participants' feedback of the larger glasses improving workflow efficiency as well as the observations that glasses were typically filled to three‐quarters.

Knowing the Resident (Reflective Motivation, Psychological Capability)

Personal support workers are well placed to support residents through times of transition, even minor environmental changes, such as the addition of a purpose‐built beverage trolley and larger cups. Getting to know residents was a prominent theme described during the interviews and was expressed as central to the PSW professional identity. Many participants articulated how necessary it was to get to know residents: ‘Learn your resident. That's the only way I can say it. Learn the people you're taking care of and go from there’ (P1). Participants expressed that verbal reminders provided encouragement and supported resident hydration. One PSW noted, ‘just push, push, push, that's what we do [laughs]’ (P3), many provided examples of their verbal cues, such as: ’it's really good, you should just try it I bet you you'll like it’ (P7). Knowing drink or glass preferences was also helpful: ‘We should know the residents enough to know what they can handle and which cup they can't handle’ (P6), ‘cause some like prune juice, some like tomato juice, diet juice’ (P3). Ultimately, participants felt tailored strategies beyond environmental adaptations were required to encourage hydration, ‘it's pretty different for every resident in order to get them drinking when they are struggling’ (P7), reinforcing the importance of the other PROMOTE strategies. Knowing residents also allowed staff to identify which residents required strategies beyond the environmental supports of the beverage trolley and larger glasses. For example, it was noted that residents requiring thickened fluids ‘need(ed) more people on the floor to help’ (P6) to provide physical assistance to drink. While participants felt it was essential to ‘know the resident’ by anticipating needs and preferences, this mindset may also limit resident autonomy and choice. For example, participants expressed that ‘if the resident's not going to take [the drink] and you know that ahead of time, why pour it?’ (P5).

Supporting Hydration (Physical Opportunity, Psychological Capability, Reflective Motivation)

Overall, participants perceived larger glasses and beverage trolley to be effective in supporting hydration. Some participants noted that hydration was a focus in their workflow even pre‐intervention, ‘I always say, you know, ‘hydration is key you need to drink a lot more” (P8). In particular, the ‘size of the glass’ (P4) was stated to ‘make all the difference’ (P4) in supporting residents in ‘getting more of an intake’ (P8) for fluids. The beverage trolley acted as a visual reminder and residents ‘flock[ed] towards it’ (P4) to explore the drink options of the day. These findings suggest that novel or rotating drink options, in addition to a standard set of favourites, may be useful for continued resident interest, ‘instead of us saying ‘oh, we have orange juice or apple juice in the cart’ they could actually see, […] a lot more people took more to drink” (P1). The visual component is crucial, because, as one PSW stated, if ‘you don't see it, you don't want it’ (P5). These comments highlight the benefits of a visually interesting environmental adaption, such as the beverage trolley or a visual menu, to support resident uptake of hydration‐promoting interventions.

Personal support staff in LTC, such as personal support workers, are essential for encouraging resident hydration, as they deliver beverages throughout the day, know resident preferences, and can assist them in adapting to environmental changes. Overall, the staff were able to adapt to the two environmental strategies and found they improved their ability to meet residents' needs.

Discussion

LTC staff perceive implementing environmental strategies as feasible and beneficial in supporting their ability to provide hydration support to residents. Interviews with staff to learn about their experience implementing the PROMOTE intervention generated four main themes: (1) optimising efficiency; (2) adapting to change; (3) knowing the resident; and (4) supporting hydration. These themes mapped on to all three core areas of the Behaviour Change Wheel (opportunity, capability, motivation), indicating staff motivation and potential for long‐term implementation. Observations by research assistants of staff implementing the strategies found that the beverage trolley and larger drinking glasses were used consistently. While staff expressed that some adaptations to the beverage trolley would be beneficial, they were pleased with the benefits it provided, such as the increased storage for more glasses and beverage offerings.

Adaptions and adjustments were not made to the beverage trolley during the intervention phase of the study; however, in a non‐research setting, these changes could be easily made through collaborations between the manufacturer (i.e., Burlodge) and the LTC home. While staff had suggestions for ways to improve the manoeuvrability and usability of the cart, all concerns could be addressed through alterations and accessories available through the manufacturer. For example, space for liquid and dry garbage can be accommodated using the manufacturer's accessories and is recommended in place of a bucket on the side shelf of the cart, which was used by the LTC home staff in this study and resulted in spills. Further, staff felt the cart was sometimes difficult to manoeuvre; however, this concern is easily mitigated through training and correct utilisation of directional caster wheels. Collaborations between manufacturers and LTC homes have the potential to result in unique beverage trolleys tailored to the needs of individual neighbourhoods, which can support increased beverage offerings and fluid intake.

While there is research outlining interventions to improve hydration in LTC (Bunn et al. 2015; Cook et al. 2019), there remains a gap in the uptake of these strategies. Frameworks and theories from the field of implementation science can guide strategies for successful adoption of new policies and interventions. Within the context of the present study, Michie et al.'s (2011) Behaviour Change Wheel provides a useful framework for considering individual sources of behaviour (capability, opportunity and motivation). Various factors may have contributed to staff perceptions of the feasibility of integrating the environmental components of the PROMOTE intervention. The larger drinking glasses and beverage trolley changed the environment of the LTC home, which increased the physical opportunity for changes in behaviour to occur, as evidenced by the themes Optimising Efficiency and Supporting Hydration. These findings indicate that even minor environmental restructuring may facilitate behaviour change. This is consistent with a large body of existing literature exploring how changes to the physical environmental in LTC can reduce responsive behaviours and improve the effectiveness of care delivery (Cook et al. 2024; Holt et al. 2021; Lee et al. 2021). Further, environmental changes have been shown to be important for other health‐promoting interventions in LTC, such as increased physical activity (Narsakka et al. 2022). Other multicomponent hydration interventions place a higher burden on LTC staff by requiring them to attend workshops or training sessions (e.g., Cook et al. 2024; McMichael et al. 2023). While training is valuable and can be effective at changing capability and motivation, it can also impede sustainability due to high staff turnover, which is common in LTC (Lam et al. 2018). The beverage trolley and larger drinking glasses are examples of choice architecture, which suggest, or ‘nudge’ individuals to make a particular choice by adapting the environment (Mertens et al. 2022). Creating choice architecture through environmental changes may improve intervention longevity as it has a reduced demand on cognitive processing for staff and has the opportunity for less variation between staff members (Mertens et al. 2022). As the beverage trolley and larger drinking glasses become a part of the LTC home routine, they will remain even during periods of high turnover. Participants also expressed that residents were sensitive to minor changes, which suggests that residents may require additional time and support from staff to ease transitions when implementing environmental changes, such as larger glasses. This initial friction to the changes created opportunities for staff to demonstrate their care and knowledge of residents (psychological capability) in adapting to change while strengthening their professional identities (reflective motivation). The new environmental components also changed the social and psychological behaviour of staff, as captured within the themes, Knowing Your Resident and Adapting to Change. Similar themes, such as the need for personalised care planning, knowing the resident and verbal reminders, were observed in a cluster randomised controlled study, which assessed the feasibility of a multicomponent hydration intervention, Think Drink (Cook et al. 2024). Further, the perceived improvements in efficiency reported by the staff may have functioned as a means for increasing reflective motivation and subsequent psychological capability. These evolving changes in LTC staff attitudes and behaviours indicate that a multicomponent hydration intervention has the potential to be sustained. Other recent multicomponent hydration interventions were also found to be feasible and accepted by staff, suggesting an increasing focus on hydration in LTC (Cook et al. 2024; Greene et al. 2018; Syed et al. 2024). Changes in staff perception and knowledge, however, do not always align with changes in behaviour and improved hydration (Greene et al. 2018). To bolster uptake, specific knowledge translation strategies may also be beneficial to integrate into routines and workflow.

Johnson and May's (2015) comprehensive analysis of systematic reviews offers practical guidance on knowledge translation strategies proven to effectively influence the behaviour of healthcare professionals in primary and secondary care settings. Of the 10 implementation interventions identified in their analysis, the most effective included patient‐mediated interventions, audit and feedback, education outreach visits and reminders. Patient‐mediated interventions, which might also be described as patient‐oriented research or co‐design, include any intervention developed in collaboration with patients or families (Fønhus et al. 2018; Johnson and May 2015). Research in LTC to support hydration might also include staff‐and‐patient‐mediated interventions to balance optimal care and practicality (Bruno et al. 2021). One such example is the Wet your Whistle with Water (W3) study, which was a three‐strategy intervention designed by members of the PROMOTE research team, based on staff recommendations of useful and feasible strategies (Syed et al. 2024). Auditing of practice was also used in the present study through observation and evaluation to determine uptake and use of the beverage trolley and new glasses. Audit and feedback in the LTC setting could be integrated as an observation of the target behaviours with follow‐up feedback and recommendations by the auditor (Johnson and May 2015). For example, a designated staff member could complete impromptu observations of workflow and provide structured feedback on providing choices or offering more drinks to residents. Reminders are another effective strategy which could be applied to staff for adapting workflow, and as noted by participants during semi‐structured interviews, can be effectively used to prompt residents and encourage new routines to increase fluid intake. A process evaluation exploring healthcare aides' perception of various knowledge translation strategies also found reminders to be a useful and acceptable strategy to change behaviour (Slaughter et al. 2018). However, when determining which knowledge translation strategies to implement, it is essential to consider the professional hierarchies and power dynamics that exist within each LTC home. The efficacy of knowledge translation strategies lies in their collaborative and interactional focus, distinguishing them from less effective approaches characterised by a diffuse focus, such as mass media or local opinion leaders (Johnson and May 2015; Low et al. 2015; Slaughter et al. 2018). Ultimately, the effectiveness of behaviour change is maximised when grounded in a coherent and clearly communicated vision for those adapting their actions; however, the impact of these behavioural changes is further strengthened when they are precipitated by structural and policy adjustments (Johnson and May 2015; Slaughter et al. 2018). In this study, participants recognised the importance of resident hydration as a clear and coherent motivation for adopting these environmental strategies; however, this perspective only includes a small subset of perspectives from PSWs.

Clinical Implications

Clinicians and LTC staff are essential for supporting hydration for residents, and all members of the care team can play an important role. Personal support staff and recreation therapists can integrate beverage offerings as part of their regular routines to naturally increase the opportunities for fluid intake throughout the day. Dehydration can have serious and life‐threatening consequences; therefore, it is essential for mitigation to be a top priority in the LTC setting. While integrating multicomponent interventions may feel like a daunting task, the amalgamation of small changes carried out as a team can potentially make lasting changes and improve the quality of life for LTC residents.

Limitations

There are limitations to this study that should be considered. Firstly, the interview guide was not piloted and the participants who completed semi‐structured interviews were aware that the goal of the environmental adaptations was to improve fluid intake. This may have contributed to a social desirability response bias when responding to interview questions. Future work in this area may consider the use of a social desirability scale (van 2020), or a priori training for interviewers on behaviours that may indicate social desirability tendencies and strategies to mitigate these responses (Bergen and Labonté 2020). Longitudinal objective outcome measures should also be used in future studies to assess long‐term implementation and effectiveness of the whole PROMOTE hydration intervention, including resident perspectives and clinically meaningful increases in fluid intake. Additionally, the intervention was only implemented at one LTC home in one neighbourhood where the residents were observed to have more independence and less cognitive impairment as compared with secure neighbourhoods. There may have been a selection bias for the neighbourhood that agreed to participate in this study. Many of the staff who participated noted that they believed resident hydration was important and was already a priority in their workflow; therefore, the beliefs and attitudes about the feasibility of the intervention may not be generalisable to other LTC homes. Leadership in the neighbourhood (e.g., nursing staff), although invited, were not involved in interviews and may have provided different perspectives on these environmental strategies.

Future Research

There are many avenues for future research exploring methods for improving hydration in LTC. This exploratory study indicates that future work should explore the implementation of other PROMOTE strategies in LTC homes with the goal of eventually trialling the full PROMOTE programme. Guidelines from the European Society for Clinical Nutrition (2022) suggest that interventions to prevent dehydration should be developed in collaboration with older adults, as well as LTC home management and staff. Participatory co‐design research with staff, residents and families has the potential to provide valuable insights into the priorities and preferences of those implementing and receiving the proposed interventions (Peryer et al. 2022).

Conclusion

Dehydration due to low fluid intake is prevalent in older adults in LTC and has many serious consequences. While the adverse effects of dehydration are well documented, there are limited interventions which have been successfully implemented and integrated into LTC staff workflow. LTC home staff perceive that it is feasible to implement the two environmental components of the PROMOTE hydration intervention. Participants perceived that the larger drinking glasses and drink trolley improved their ability to support resident hydration. LTC staff are crucial to delivering preventative hydration interventions, as they know resident preferences and can guide them when environments and routines are adapted to encourage fluid intake. Implementation science theories and frameworks may support changes in staff behaviour, which can inform future research to improve the sustainability and effectiveness of hydration interventions in LTC.
