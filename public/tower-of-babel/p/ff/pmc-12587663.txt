Association between leukocyte-derived composite indices and premature coronary artery disease: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Haodong Jiang, Yuntong liu, Li Che, Zhibo Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNumerous studies have demonstrated that leukocyte-derived composite indices (LDCIs) are closely associated with coronary artery disease (CAD). However, their relationship with premature coronary artery disease (PCAD) remains unclear. This research endeavors to elucidate the relationship between LDCIs and PCAD and to evaluate the predictive accuracy of different LDCIs for PCAD.MethodIn this cross-sectional study, 211 young and middle-aged adults, who underwent their first coronary angiography (CAG) at the Second Hospital of Jilin University between March 2023 and July 2023 were consecutively enrolled, among whom patients diagnosed with PCAD included both chronic coronary syndrome (CCS) and acute coronary syndrome (ACS). Demographic characteristics, laboratory test results, and CAG results were collected from the patients’ medical records. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) was calculated by dividing the neutrophil count by the lymphocyte count. the systemic inflammation response index (SIRI) was computed as the neutrophil count multiplied by the monocyte count divided by the lymphocyte count; and the lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR) was derived by dividing the lymphocyte count by the monocyte count. CAD was defined as having at least one major coronary artery with ≥ 50% stenosis on CAG. Multivariate logistic regression and restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis were employed to investigate the associations between the NLR, LMR, SIRI, and PCAD. The predictive value of each index for PCAD was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis.ResultsBased on the CAG results, individuals diagnosed with CAD were categorized into the PCAD group (n = 143), while those without CAD were categorized into the Control group (n = 68). When LDCIs were analyzed as continuous variables, both SIRI and NLR were identified as independent risk factors for PCAD (OR 1.980; 95% CI 1.168–3.892 and OR 1.416; 95% CI 1.066–2.038, respectively). When LDCIs were analyzed as categorical variables, the highest quartile of SIRI had a 3.481-fold increased risk of PCAD compared to the lowest quartile (OR 3.481; 95% CI 1.140-11.475), while the highest quartiles of the other LDCIs did not show a significant difference in risk compared to their lowest quartiles. In addition, SIRI was the best LDCI for predicting PCAD, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.710 (95% CI 0.635–0.784) and positive and negative predictive values of 0.835 and 0.490, respectively. Subgroup analyses showed that the association between SIRI and PCAD remained stable across different subgroups (P for interaction > 0.05); however, the association was more pronounced in females and in non-smokers than in males and smokers.ConclusionOur study found that certain LDCIs are significantly associated with PCAD, with SIRI demonstrating the best predictive performance. This finding may be useful in patients being considered for CAG to help stratify the need for the invasive procedure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05268-5.Keywords: Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, Leukocyte-derived composite indices, Platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio, Premature coronary artery disease, Lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio, Systemic inflammation response index

Background

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is among the foremost causes of mortality globally, significantly burdening global economies and public health systems [1–3]. Premature coronary artery disease (PCAD) is a specific subtype of CAD, characterized by its onset in men younger than 55 years and women younger than 65 years [4]. A large cohort study with a 10-year follow-up of PCAD patients revealed that 52.3% of patients experienced at least one major adverse cardiovascular event (MACE) during the follow-up period, and 20% died [5]. Although evidence suggests that PCAD has a better prognosis compared to mature CAD [6], the fact that PCAD often affects individuals who are key contributors to the workforce and family underscores the potential for a greater socioeconomic burden. Therefore, identifying high-risk individuals for PCAD among healthy populations is crucial.

In fact, research on reliable biomarkers for predicting the risk of CAD began several decades ago. Given that inflammation is crucial throughout the formation and progression of atherosclerosis, predicting CAD using inflammatory markers has been a focal point of research. Over decades of research, many novel inflammatory markers have been identified as associated with CAD and have demonstrated good predictive performance [7–10]. However, these novel inflammatory markers are not routinely tested in clinical practice, and their high costs limit their use in large-scale CAD screening in the community. Interestingly, some researchers have explored complete blood count parameters (CBC) and found that leukocyte-derived composite indices (LDCIs), such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR), and systemic inflammation response index (SIRI), provide better predictive value for cardiovascular-related diseases than individual leukocyte subtypes [11, 12]. Additionally, Li et al. found that LDCIs significantly outperformed traditional inflammatory markers like C-reactive protein in predicting chronic total occlusion and in-stent restenosis [13]. Therefore, the emergence of LDCIs offers the potential for large-scale use in community screening or clinical practice, particularly in resource-limited settings, presenting a promising application prospect.

Although recent research has shown that LDCIs are closely related to cardiovascular diseases [14–16], to the best of our knowledge, their association with PCAD has not yet been reported. Therefore, this study explores the relationship between LDCIs and PCAD, comparing the predictive efficacy of various LDCIs for PCAD, aiming to fill the research gap regarding LDCIs in the field of PCAD.

Method

This cross-sectional study consecutively enrolled 211 young and middle-aged adults who underwent their first coronary angiography (CAG) at the Second Hospital of Jilin University between March 2023 and July 2023, among whom patients diagnosed with PCAD included both chronic coronary syndrome (CCS) and acute coronary syndrome (ACS). The indications for CAG included the presence of clinical manifestations of myocardial ischemia together with supporting ancillary testing or imaging evidence of ischemia. The inclusion criteria were: (1) Patients aged over 18 years, with onset of CAD before 55 years in men and before 65 years in women. (2) first-time coronary angiography; (3) complete coronary angiography records and complete blood count results. The exclusion criteria were: (1) history of coronary revascularization; (2) diagnosed infectious diseases such as urinary tract infection or pulmonary infection; (3) chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, bronchiectasis, or bronchial asthma; (4) autoimmune diseases, severe hepatic/renal insufficiency; (5) malignancies or hematologic diseases; (6) thyroid diseases. This study complies with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Jilin University (2024 − 328).

Data collection and definition

Demographic information, laboratory test results, and CAG results were extracted from the patients’ medical records. The demographic information encompassed age, gender, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), body mass index (BMI), smoking history, drinking history, histories of hypertension, and diabetes. Laboratory tests included neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, monocyte count, platelet count, triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), and high-density lipoprotein (HDL). Blood samples were collected either on the day of hospital admission or the following morning after an overnight fast by trained nurses. The specimens were subsequently delivered to the hospital’s clinical laboratory, where analyses were performed by qualified laboratory personnel. TG and TC were determined using enzymatic methods, and lipoproteins were assessed using the turbidimetric method. CAG was conducted by two experienced interventional cardiologists, and the relevant information was obtained from the angiography records. CAD was defined as having at least one major coronary artery with ≥ 50% stenosis on CAG [17]. PCAD was described as CAD presenting in males under 55 years old and females under 65 years old [4]. The NLR was calculated by dividing the neutrophil count by the lymphocyte count; the SIRI was computed as the neutrophil count multiplied by the monocyte count divided by the lymphocyte count; and the LMR was derived by dividing the lymphocyte count by the monocyte count [18].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables with a normal distribution were described as mean ± standard deviation, whereas those with a skewed distribution were described using median and interquartile range. For comparisons between two groups, the t-test was employed for normally distributed variables, and the Mann-Whitney U test was utilized for skewed distributions. For comparisons among multiple groups, normally distributed variables were analyzed using analysis of variance (ANOVA), whereas non-normally distributed variables were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis H test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were conducted using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Variables with more than 10% missing values were excluded from the analysis.For missing values, mean imputation was applied (Figure S1) [19]. Logistic regression analyses were conducted to investigate the associations of NLR, LMR, and SIRI with PCAD. Covariates were selected based on existing literature and clinical expertise [20]. Three models were used to progressively adjust the number of covariates to explore the relationship between LDCIs and PCAD. Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age, gender, BMI, SBP, DBP, smoking, and drinking; and Model 3 further adjusted for history of hypertension, history of diabetes, and laboratory test results in addition to the variables in Model 2. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was used to examine the dose-response relationship between LDCIs and PCAD. The predictive abilities, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) of various LDCIs for PCAD were assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. The DeLong test was used to compare area under the curve (AUC) between the ROC curves. To assess the robustness of the associations, we performed subgroup analyses of LDCIs with the best diagnostic performance for PCAD. The likelihood ratio test was used to evaluate interactions between subgroups. All statistical tests were two-sided, with a P value of < 0.05 considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1.

Association between NLR and PCAD

When NLR was treated as a continuous variable, it exhibited a significant relationship with PCAD (Model 1: OR 1.419; 95% CI 1.124–1.899; Model 2: OR 1.497; 95% CI 1.126–2.113; Model 3: OR 1.416; 95% CI 1.066–2.038). To further investigate its association with PCAD when treated as a categorical variable, NLR was divided into quartiles: Q1 (NLR ≤ 1.718, N = 53), Q2 (1.718–2.153, N = 53), Q3 (2.153–2.983, N = 52), and Q4 (> 2.983, N = 53). Across models, no statistically significant differences in PCAD risk were observed among Q2, Q3, Q4, and the reference group Q1 (all p > 0.005) (Table 2). Trend analysis for each model also showed no statistical significance (all p > 0.005).

Association between LMR and PCAD

When LMR was analyzed as a continuous variable, it emerged as a protective factor against PCAD in the unadjusted model (OR 0.800; 95% CI 0.682–0.933). However, after adjusting for covariates, LMR was not significantly associated with PCAD (Model 2: OR 0.841; 95% CI 0.694–1.008; Model 3: OR 0.853; 95% CI 0.687–1.049). To further investigate its association with PCAD when treated as a categorical variable, LMR was divided into quartiles: Q1 (LMR ≤ 3.536, N = 53), Q2 (3.536–4.667, N = 53), Q3 (4.667–6.00.667.00, N = 56), and Q4 (> 6.00, N = 49). In Model 1, compared to the Q1 group, the risk of PCAD was significantly lower in the Q2, Q3, and Q4 groups, with higher LMR levels associated with a lower probability of developing PCAD (Q2: OR 0.214; 95% CI 0.077–0.541; Q3: OR 0.296; 95% CI 0.106–0.753; Q4: OR 0.221; 95% CI 0.078–0.565). In Model 2, compared to the Q1 group, the risk of PCAD was significantly lower in the Q2 and Q4 groups (Q2: OR 0.238; 95% CI 0.077–0.671; Q4: OR 0.265; 95% CI 0.084–0.769). However, in the fully adjusted Model 3, only the Q2 group had a significantly lower risk of PCAD compared to the Q1 group (OR 0.226; 95% CI 0.067–0.693), suggesting that the predictive power of LMR decreased after adjusting for multiple factors (Table 2). Additionally, trend analysis showed a significant statistical difference in Model 1 (p = 0.008), but not in Model 2 (p = 0.053) or Model 3 (p = 0.118).

Dose–response relationship between LDCIs and PCAD

We further plotted RCS curves, adjusted for all covariates, to examine the dose–response relationships between LDCIs and PCAD. The results showed that SIRI, NLR, and LMR were all linearly associated with the occurrence of PCAD. Specifically, the risk of PCAD increased with increasing SIRI and NLR levels, whereas it decreased with increasing LMR levels. We used the point corresponding to an OR of 1 as the reference point, which was 0.835 for SIRI, 2.150 for NLR, and 4.632 for LMR (Fig. 1).

Discussion

Main finding

In summary, we explored the associations between LDCIs and PCAD and drew the following conclusions: (1) When analyzing LDCIs as continuous variables, both NLR and SIRI were significantly associated with the occurrence of PCAD, whereas LMR was not associated with PCAD. (2) When analyzing LDCIs as categorical variables, higher SIRI levels were linked to a significantly increased risk of PCAD. However, NLR and LMR did not show significant risk differences for PCAD. (3) SIRI outperformed NLRand LMR in predicting PCAD.

The underlying mechanisms

Current perspectives on the mechanism of atherosclerosis suggest that stimulatory factors lead to vascular endothelial injury, lipid deposition beneath the intima, and subsequent oxidation of lipids, which induce inflammation and activate endothelial cells. Platelets in the blood bind to endothelial cells via their IIb/IIIa receptors, leading to increased endothelial cell activation and upregulation of vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1), which enhances the adhesion of monocytes and lymphocytes. Chemokines and cytokines drive monocytes and lymphocytes into the subendothelial space. Under the effect of macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF), monocytes develop into macrophages. These macrophages, through their scavenger receptors and Toll-like receptors, engulf oxidized lipids in the intima and produce large amounts of inflammatory mediators, promoting the formation of atherosclerotic plaques. Meanwhile, lymphocytes recognize antigens and become activated, further activating endothelial cells and other inflammatory cells, exacerbating the inflammatory response and advancing the development of atherosclerosis [21–23]. Certain patients with PCAD may exhibit abnormal levels of circulating inflammatory cytokines due to genetic polymorphisms, among which tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) is one example [24]. Studies have shown that TNF-α can directly activate monocytes, promoting their transformation into cholesterol ester–rich cells. This mechanism is associated with the upregulation of functional adhesion molecules and scavenger receptors. In addition to the aforementioned mechanisms, PCAD is a disease associated with genetic mutations, which may lead to abnormal inflammatory responses and consequently contribute to the development of coronary artery disease. Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) may play an important role in this process. Li et al., using the GEO database, identified NET-related genes and found that MMP9 is a key gene involved in NET formation [25]. Interestingly, studies have also reported that the MMP9 gene exhibits polymorphisms in patients with PCAD [26]. This suggests that the high infiltration of neutrophils observed in PCAD patients may be related to the abnormal expression of NET-associated genes. Moreover, increased MMP9 levels can degrade the anti-inflammatory protein Ninj1 (nerve injury-induced protein 1), whose loss under pathological conditions can promote monocyte recruitment by enhancing MAPK signaling and blocking the PI3K/Akt pathway in macrophages [27].

From the above mechanisms, it is evident that atherosclerosis in patients with PCAD is accompanied by a chronic inflammatory response involving multiple types of inflammatory cells. Therefore, elevated leukocytes in the blood may reflect local chronic inflammation. Additionally, since NLR and LMR only involve two types of inflammatory cells, they cannot fully capture the body’s inflammatory status. In contrast, SIRI combines the proportions of neutrophils, monocytes, and lymphocytes, providing a more comprehensive reflection of systemic inflammation. This helps address the limitations of traditional indices and offers greater predictive accuracy.

LDCIs and cardiovascular disease

Interestingly, this is not the first time that SIRI has been found to be superior to traditional LDCIs. Li et al. discovered that SIRI was associated with MACE in patients with acute coronary syndrome and that its predictive ability was significantly better than that of traditional LDCIs, such as NLR [14]. Additionally, SIRI has also been reported to be linked to the degree of coronary stenosis in ST-elevation myocardial infarction patients, with better predictive ability compared to NLR [28]. Furthermore, SIRI is not only related to coronary artery disease but has also been proven to be associated with various other cardiovascular diseases. In a cross-sectional study involving 20,000 subjects, Zhang et al. analyzed the differences in LDCIs between heart failure patients and non-heart failure patients, showing that SIRI, NLR, and MLR were significantly associated with the occurrence of heart failure, but they did not further compare the predictive effectiveness of these indices for heart failure [29]. Lin et al. discovered that elevated SIRI levels independently increased the risk of atrial fibrillation in ischemic stroke patients [30]. Based on these studies, we compared several LDCIs with PCAD and concluded that SIRI is the most effective LDCI for predicting PCAD, further demonstrating the significant importance and potential application of SIRI as an inflammatory marker in the assessment of cardiovascular diseases.

The significance of LDCIs in predicting PCAD

Studying the relationship between LDCIs and PCAD holds significant importance for community hospitals and resource-limited areas. Firstly, these indices are based on routine blood tests, which are low-cost and easily accessible, providing an economical and efficient screening tool. Secondly, in resource-limited settings, complex diagnostic and treatment procedures may not be feasible. LDCIs can help identify high-risk individuals early, enabling healthcare providers to implement appropriate interventions at the early stages of the disease, thereby improving patient outcomes. Lastly, LDCIs can assist health departments in better understanding the overall health status of the community, formulating and adjusting public health policies, and optimizing the allocation and use of medical resources.

Framingham Risk Score (FRS) and SCORE2 are currently among the most widely used tools for estimating the 10-year risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD), primarily incorporating variables such as age, sex, blood pressure, lipid profile, smoking status, and diabetes [31, 32]. However, previous reports suggest that most 10-year CVD risk prediction models perform suboptimally in older populations, underscoring the importance of age-stratified research [33, 34]. The SCORE2-OP model has been developed specifically for assessing 10-year CVD risk in individuals aged 70 years and older [35], yet validated tools for younger populations remain scarce, despite the trend toward an increasing incidence of CVD in younger individuals [36]. In our study, SIRI emerged as a LDCI for predicting PCAD. Notably, inflammation-related biomarkers have not been incorporated into traditional tools such as the FRS or SCORE2. Theoretically, SIRI could serve as an additional parameter integrated into existing PCAD risk assessment frameworks to generate a novel composite score, which might reclassify certain individuals from intermediate to high risk, thereby improving the early identification of PCAD.

Strengths and limitations

As far as we know, this is the inaugural study exploring the relationship between LDCIs and PCAD and comparing the predictive abilities of LDCIs. We found that SIRI is the most effective LDCI for predicting PCAD, which can aid community physicians or healthcare providers in resource-limited areas in diagnosing and screening for PCAD. However, several limitations should be noted. First, as a cross-sectional study, causality cannot be established. In addition, it is currently recognized that the pathogenesis of PCAD involves both genetic and non-genetic mechanisms. Gene mutations are considered the genetic mechanism of PCAD, whereas smoking, alcohol consumption, excessive psychological stress, physical inactivity, obesity, and opioid use are regarded as non-genetic risk factors. In our study, although we attempted to include as many of these risk factors as possible as covariates in the multivariable logistic regression analysis, due to the inherent limitations of a cross-sectional design, information on the presence of gene mutations, medication use, mental status, and physical activity habits could not be obtained, which may have affected the accuracy of our results. Second, Although the AUC of SIRI was higher than that of other LDCIs, its 95%CI was relatively wide, indicating only modest predictive accuracy. We speculate that this may be attributable to our relatively small sample size. Third, current studies have found that elevated interleukins, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, and TNF-α levels are significantly associated with PCAD. Although our study investigated the associations between common LDCIs, such as SIRI, NLR, and LMR, and PCAD, we did not compare their predictive performance with classical inflammatory markers or other LDCIs. In addition, our study may have included some patients in the acute phase of cardiovascular events, such as acute heart failure and acute myocardial infarction, which could lead to substantial short-term fluctuations in LDCI levels and potentially confound our conclusions. Fourth, although a luminal stenosis of > 50% in at least one major coronary artery on CAG is commonly used as a diagnostic criterion for CAD, the assessment of stenosis severity currently relies primarily on the visual estimation of the operator. Consequently, the reporting of stenosis degree is inherently subjective. This subjectivity may lead to inconsistent results in borderline cases with approximately 50% stenosis, depending on the individual judgment of different operators, which could, to some extent, influence our conclusions. Therefore, in future studies, we plan to adopt a threshold of > 70% stenosis in at least one major coronary artery as the grouping criterion, which may render our findings more robust and clinically meaningful..Finally, as our study was conducted at a single center, it remains unclear whether the cutoff value of SIRI is applicable to other hospitals or regions. Therefore, given these limitations, our conclusions require further external validation. In future studies, we aim to conduct large-scale, multicenter, prospective investigations, stratify PCAD patients into acute and stable cardiovascular phases, include a broader range of covariates and consider using 70% stenosis as the diagnostic threshold to further verify our findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study found that certain LDCIs are significantly associated with PCAD, with SIRI demonstrating the best predictive performance. This finding may be useful in patients being considered for CAG to help stratify the need for the invasive procedure.
