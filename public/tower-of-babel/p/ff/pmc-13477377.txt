Total Clinical Event Burden With Edoxaban Monotherapy Versus Dual Antithrombotic Therapy in Atrial Fibrillation and Stable Coronary Artery Disease: Insights From the EPIC‐CAD Trial
Authors: Sung‐Hwan Kim, Min Soo Cho, Soyoon Park, Young Choi, Jung‐Bok Lee, Duk‐Woo Park, Gi‐Byoung Nam, Yong‐Seog Oh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOral anticoagulation is essential for stroke prevention in atrial fibrillation, whereas antiplatelet therapy remains standard for coronary artery disease (CAD), raising concerns about bleeding when both are combined. The effect of these regimens on recurrent events, reflecting cumulative disease burden, has not been fully evaluated.MethodsThis post hoc analysis of the EPIC‐CAD (Edoxaban Versus Edoxaban With Antiplatelet Agent in Patients With Atrial Fibrillation and Chronic Stable Coronary Artery Disease) trial included 1040 patients with atrial fibrillation and stable CAD randomized to edoxaban monotherapy or edoxaban plus a single antiplatelet agent. The primary end point was the total number of net adverse clinical events at 12 months.ResultsOver a median follow‐up of 12.1 months, 155 total events occurred (123 first, 32 subsequent). Event rates were lower with edoxaban monotherapy than with dual therapy (9.4 versus 19.8 per 100 person‐years; incidence rate ratio, 0.47 [95% CI, 0.34–0.67]). Monotherapy reduced first events (incidence rate ratio, 0.46 [95% CI, 0.32–0.68]) and showed a lower cumulative hazard of total events (hazard ratio, 0.48 [95% CI, 0.32–0.71]; P<0.001). Bleeding was the predominant event type in both groups but occurred more often with dual therapy (85.7% versus 55.3%; P<0.001). Rates of ischemic events and death were similar between groups.ConclusionsEdoxaban monotherapy significantly reduced the overall burden of clinical events compared with dual antithrombotic therapy in patients with atrial fibrillation and stable CAD. Clinical Perspective

What Is New?

In patients with atrial fibrillation and stable coronary artery disease, a recurrent‐event analysis of the EPIC‐CAD (Edoxaban Versus Edoxaban With Antiplatelet Agent in Patients With Atrial Fibrillation and Chronic Stable Coronary Artery Disease) trial demonstrated that edoxaban monotherapy significantly reduced the total cumulative burden of net adverse clinical events, including both first and subsequent events, compared with dual antithrombotic therapy.

What Are the Clinical Implications?

For most patients with atrial fibrillation and stable coronary artery disease, long‐term oral anticoagulation alone is sufficient. Avoiding routine antiplatelet therapy significantly reduces bleeding without increasing ischemic risk.

Atrial fibrillation (AF) and coronary artery disease (CAD) are 2 of the most prevalent cardiovascular diseases, and their coexistence is increasingly common with the aging population. 1 Each condition independently confers significant morbidity and death, but when they occur together, therapeutic decisions become challenging. AF is associated with an ≈5‐fold increased risk of ischemic stroke, for which long‐term oral anticoagulation has been the standard therapeutic strategy. In contrast, CAD is a manifestation of atherosclerosis, where antiplatelet therapy has been the cornerstone of secondary prevention. Managing patients with both AF and stable CAD requires balancing the dual goals of stroke prevention and coronary event reduction, while minimizing the heightened risk of bleeding that results from combined therapy. The EPIC‐CAD (Edoxaban Versus Edoxaban With Antiplatelet Agent in Patients With Atrial Fibrillation and Chronic Stable Coronary Artery Disease) trial demonstrated that edoxaban monotherapy led to a lower risk of a composite of death from any cause, MI, stroke, systemic embolism, unplanned urgent revascularization, or major bleeding or clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding at 12 months than dual antithrombotic therapy. 2 However, the primary outcome was based on the time to first event, only capturing the occurrence of the first event. 3 , 4 , 5 The subsequent events pertaining to the patients are not considered, although they were clinically relevant, resulting in an underestimation of the true burden of disease. Analyses that include total events, encompassing recurrent occurrences, more accurately reflect the cumulative disease burden over time. 6 , 7 , 8 This analysis aimed to determine whether edoxaban monotherapy was associated with a lower risk of total clinical events.

Methods

The data supporting this article will be made available by the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Population

The design and results of the EPIC‐CAD trial (ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT03718559) have been published. 2 , 9 Briefly, EPIC‐CAD was a multicenter, open‐label, adjudicator‐masked, randomized trial comparing edoxaban monotherapy with dual antithrombotic therapy (edoxaban plus a single antiplatelet agent) in patients with AF and stable CAD. Eligible patients met the following inclusion criteria: age ≥18 years; any type of AF; a CHA2DS2‐VASc score ≥2; stable CAD, defined as a chronic coronary syndrome previously treated with percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or coronary artery bypass grafting at least 6 months before enrollment, an acute coronary syndrome previously treated with PCI or coronary artery bypass grafting at least 12 months before enrollment, or anatomically confirmed CAD (≥50% stenosis of a major epicardial coronary artery on cardiac catheterization or coronary computed tomographic angiography). Key exclusion criteria were contraindications for antithrombotic drugs; a history of intracranial hemorrhage, prosthetic heart valves, or moderate to severe mitral stenosis; and severe hepatic dysfunction or severe renal insufficiency. We also evaluated the bleeding risk at baseline as defined by the HAS‐BLED score. 10 The institutional review board approved the protocol and subsequent amendments at each participating site. All patients provided written informed consent before enrollment.

Participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive either monotherapy with standard‐dose edoxaban or dual antithrombotic therapy that included standard‐dose edoxaban plus a single antiplatelet agent (either aspirin or a P2Y12 inhibitor, at the discretion of the treating physician). Indications for dose adjustment of edoxaban to 30 mg once daily included a creatinine clearance of 15 to 50 mL/min, a body weight of ≤60 kg, and the use of certain P‐glycoprotein inhibitors. Randomization was performed using a web‐based interactive response system, stratified by participating site.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the total number of first and subsequent events, including death from any cause, bleeding, and ischemic events. The bleeding events were a composite of major bleeding including hemorrhagic stroke and clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding, as defined by the International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis. The ischemic events were a composite of ischemic stroke, systemic embolism, MI, or urgent revascularization. Prespecified definitions were used to assess each clinical outcome. Blinded adjudication of the end points was conducted by an independent clinical events committee.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables, reported as means±SDs, were compared using Student's t test. Categorical variables, presented as counts (percentages), were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate.

The primary statistical analysis for this study was performed on the intention‐to‐treat population, whereby participants were assigned to groups on the basis of the randomization results, regardless of later crossover or adherence. Study outcome incidence rates were reported per 100 person‐years. A Poisson regression model was used to estimate the incidence rates and rate ratios of total clinical events, comparing the edoxaban monotherapy and dual antithrombotic therapy groups.

The mean cumulative function was estimated using the nonparametric Nelson–Aalen method. An unadjusted Andersen–Gill model, including only the randomization group as an independent variable, was used to generate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals and visual inspection of log(−log) survival plots. The Andersen–Gill model is an extension of the standard Cox proportional hazards model, allowing for recurrent events, which assumes a common baseline hazard function that is independent of the number or order of events. 11 , 12

All reported P values were 2‐sided and were not adjusted for multiple comparisons. Statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software version 4.3.2 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria).

During a median follow‐up of 12.1 (interquartile range, 11.8–12.9) months, a total of 155 clinical events occurred, comprising 123 first events and 32 subsequent events. The incidence rates of total, first, second, and third events in the edoxaban monotherapy group were 9.4, 7.3, 1.5, and 0.5 events per 100 person‐years, respectively, compared with 19.8, 15.9, 3.0, and 0.9 events per 100 person‐years, respectively, in the dual antithrombotic therapy group.

The incidence rate ratio (IRR) for total events was significantly lower in the edoxaban monotherapy group (IRR, 0.47 [95% CI, 0.34–0.67]; P<0.001), particularly for first events (IRR, 0.46 [95% CI, 0.32–0.68]; P<0.001). However, the IRRs for the second (IRR, 0.50 [95% CI, 0.21–1.17]; P=0.109) and third events (IRR, 0.60 [95% CI, 0.14–2.50]; P=0.479) were not statistically significant. In a separate analysis stratified by the type of antiplatelet therapy, the benefit of edoxaban monotherapy remained consistent, with a significantly lower risk of the primary outcome compared with both aspirin‐based (IRR, 0.59 [95% CI, 0.40–0.87]; P=0.007) and clopidogrel‐based (IRR, 0.36 [95% CI, 0.24–0.53]; P<0.001) dual antithrombotic therapy.

In the Andersen–Gill extension of the Cox proportional hazards model, edoxaban monotherapy was associated with a 52% lower cumulative hazard of total events over the entire follow‐up period, including death, ischemic stroke, systemic embolism, MI, revascularization, major bleeding, and clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding (HR, 0.48 [95% CI, 0.32–0.71]; P<0.001; Figure 1).

Among the first events, bleeding was the most common in both groups, with a significantly higher proportion observed in the dual therapy group (55.3% in the edoxaban monotherapy group versus 85.7% in the dual antithrombotic therapy group; P<0.001) indicating that bleeding events are clinically more frequent than ischemic events, especially in the dual antithrombotic therapy group. Bleeding also remained the most frequent type of event for both the second and third events, although no significant differences were observed between the groups.

The incidence rate of overall death was 0.8 events per 100 person‐years in both groups, without a significant difference (IRR, 0.99 [95% CI, 0.25–3.96]; P=0.994). Death accounted for 6.5% of the first events (8/123) and none of the subsequent events (0/32). The incidence of total ischemic events was also not significantly different between groups (2.3 versus 2.4 events per 100 person‐years for the monotherapy and dual therapy groups, respectively; IRR, 1.08 [95% CI, 0.49–2.36]; P=0.853). In contrast, the rate of total bleeding events was significantly higher in the dual antithrombotic therapy group than in the monotherapy group (16.8 versus 6.2 events per 100 person‐years; IRR, 0.37 [95% CI, 0.25–0.55]; P<0.001).

Bleeding events occurred more frequently than ischemic events, regardless of the type of the first event. During the overall follow‐up, 122 bleeding events occurred among 99 patients; 80 patients experienced a single bleeding event, and 19 (19.2%) had multiple bleeding episodes (15 had 2 events, and 4 had 3 events). Among the 99 patients with at least 1 bleeding event, 6 (6.1%) also experienced a total of 8 ischemic events (5 repeat revascularizations, 2 spontaneous MIs, and 1 ischemic stroke). Regarding the temporal relationship, 5 of these ischemic events occurred before the bleeding episode, whereas 3 occurred subsequently. The incidence rate of any ischemic event was numerically higher than those without bleeding (2.9 versus 1.7 per 100 person‐years; IRR, 1.65 [95% CI, 0.48–5.62]; P=0.426). The patients with bleeding episodes were more likely to be receiving clopidogrel compared with those without bleeding events (17.1%, 36.2%, and 36.8% for patients with 0, 1, and ≥2 bleeding events, respectively; P<0.001; Table S1).

Discussion

The main findings of this post hoc analysis of the EPIC‐CAD trial were as follows: (1) Compared with dual antithrombotic therapy, edoxaban monotherapy was associated with a lower risk of total net adverse clinical events, encompassing both first and subsequent events; (2) the total number of bleeding events was substantially greater than that of ischemic events or deaths; and (3) the benefits of monotherapy were primarily driven by a reduction in total number of bleeding events, whereas the rates of total ischemic events and death were not different between the 2 groups.

While the main EPIC‐CAD trial previously established the efficacy and safety of edoxaban monotherapy on the basis of the time‐to‐first‐event analysis, the present recurrent‐event analysis extends those findings by capturing the cumulative incidence of both initial and subsequent events. This approach provides a more comprehensive assessment of the overall clinical burden experienced by patients during follow‐up. Importantly, our data demonstrated that bleeding events were more frequent than ischemic events in both treatment groups, with a particularly pronounced excess in the dual antithrombotic therapy group, underscoring the central role of bleeding as the predominant adverse outcome in this population. These results further extend the findings of prior pivotal trials evaluating antithrombotic strategies in patients with AF and concomitant CAD. 13 , 14

Historically, many patients with AF and CAD received both oral anticoagulation and antiplatelet therapy. 15 This approach was largely extrapolated from evidence obtained in high‐risk settings, where dual antiplatelet therapy reduces stent thrombosis and recurrent MI. However, in the context of stable CAD, defined as patients without recent acute coronary syndrome or PCI, the benefit of adding long‐term antiplatelet therapy to anticoagulation has been increasingly questioned. Dual antithrombotic therapy inevitably increases bleeding risk compared with single antithrombotic therapy. Major bleeding events, including gastrointestinal and intracranial hemorrhage, are strongly associated with death and often outweigh the potential benefits of dual therapy. 16 , 17 Several landmark studies established that dual therapy with a non–vitamin K oral anticoagulant plus a single antiplatelet agent is safer than traditional triple therapy in patients undergoing PCI. 14 , 18 , 19 , 20 However, those studies were conducted in the setting of recent acute coronary syndrome or PCI rather than stable CAD, and follow‐up was generally limited to the periprocedural period, when ischemic risk is highest. Patients with stable CAD represent a different clinical scenario in which the risk of recurrent coronary events diminishes over time, whereas the cumulative risk of bleeding from continued combination therapy increases.

Bleeding was the most common type of adverse event in patients with stable CAD and AF. While ischemic protection remains a priority in this population, in our data, bleeding accounted for more than half of first events in the monotherapy group and >85% in the dual therapy group, indicating that bleeding represents the primary determinant of long‐term morbidity. This finding is consistent with previous literature demonstrating that major bleeding events are associated with prolonged hospitalization, impaired quality of life, and increased subsequent death, sometimes surpassing the prognostic impact of ischemic events. 16 , 17 The bleeding events in our study were major or clinically relevant, emphasizing the substantial burden borne by both patients and the health care system. Notably, recurrent bleeding occurred in approximately one fifth of patients with an initial bleeding episode, illustrating the tendency for patients to experience multiple bleeding events once a first event occurs. In addition, a post hoc analysis of the AFIRE (Atrial Fibrillation and Ischemic Events With Rivaroxaban in Patients With Stable Coronary Artery Disease) trial demonstrated an increased risk of ischemic events following bleeding episodes. This association is likely mediated through multiple mechanisms, including the interruption of antithrombotic therapy, anemia‐related ischemia, hemodynamic instability, transfusion, and bleeding‐induced hypercoagulability. 21 Thus, bleeding represents a clinically meaningful event that not only worsens prognosis but may actively precipitate subsequent ischemic events, underscoring the critical importance of bleeding‐minimizing antithrombotic strategies. Taken together, these findings argue strongly in favor of strategies that minimize bleeding risk in patients with AF and stable CAD, even if this entails a modest trade‐off in ischemic protection.

The present analysis has several strengths. First, it was based on a randomized controlled trial with rigorous end point adjudication, thereby minimizing bias. Second, sufficient sample size and multicenter design increase the generalizability of the findings. Third, absence of specific restrictions on lesion characteristics or prior management in the eligibility criteria makes our results highly representative of real‐world practice, reinforcing the conclusion that monotherapy is suitable for most patients. Finally, the recurrent‐event analysis provides novel insights that complement the original trial results and more accurately reflect patient outcomes over time.

Our findings raise several important directions for future research. Longer‐term follow‐up studies are warranted to determine whether the benefits of edoxaban monotherapy persist beyond 1 year and to evaluate potential late ischemic events that may emerge over time. In addition, comparative effectiveness studies of different non–vitamin K oral anticoagulants used as monotherapy could help determine whether the benefits observed with edoxaban are consistent across agents. Further investigation into biomarkers or clinical predictors of bleeding versus ischemic risk may allow more individualized approaches to antithrombotic therapy.

Conclusions

This recurrent‐event analysis of the EPIC‐CAD trial demonstrates that edoxaban monotherapy significantly reduced the cumulative burden of net adverse clinical events compared with dual antithrombotic therapy in patients with AF and stable CAD. Bleeding complications occurred more frequently than ischemic events, particularly among those receiving dual antithrombotic therapy, underscoring the importance of minimizing bleeding risk in this population. These findings suggest that oral anticoagulation monotherapy can be considered a reasonable default strategy for patients with AF and stable CAD, while individual treatment decisions should remain personalized to account for specific ischemic risk features and patient preferences.
