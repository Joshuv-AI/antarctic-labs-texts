Explainable ensemble learning using SHAP for ERP anomaly detection
Authors: Adiah Qazi, Ammad Ali Khan Jadoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnterprise Resource Planning (ERP) systems in manufacturing environments process hundreds of thousands of daily transactions, yet anomalies evade detection in the majority of cases under routine manual audit regimes. Although neural network approaches achieve F-scores exceeding 0.90 on synthetic benchmarks, they sacrifice the explainability that regulatory compliance and audit requirements demand for production deployment. Here we present an explainable ensemble learning framework combining six heterogeneous anomaly detection models—Isolation Forest, Local Outlier Factor, One-Class SVM, Elliptic Envelope, Gradient Boosting, and a Robust Autoencoder—for multidimensional ERP transaction analysis. Evaluated on 209,666 real-world procurement transactions, the ensemble achieves an F-score of 0.8437 (95 % CI on held-out test set of 41,933 transactions: [0.8305, 0.8569]) while providing a three-layer interpretability stack: gradient-boosting feature importance rankings, interpretable decision rules with 87.3 % logic coverage, and SHAP-based instance-level attribution. This F-score is lower than those of the highest-performing individual constituent models (Gradient Boosting: 0.9321; Robust Autoencoder: 0.9254), a trade-off that is deliberate: the ensemble provides significantly improved cross-validation stability (coefficient of variation: 0.49 % vs. 1.4–2.1 % for top individual models) and a 19.4 % reduction in false-positive rate (relative to the unweighted arithmetic mean constituent FP rate of 14.1 %), as demonstrated through Pareto-front analysis over all 63 non-trivial model subsets. Statistical validation through 5-fold stratified cross-validation, paired t-tests (t=33.5, p<0.001) verified via the Shapiro–Wilk test (W=0.94, p=0.42), and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (p<0.05) confirm significant performance gains over the unsupervised-only baseline; the large Cohen’s reflects the supervised–unsupervised paradigm gap and should be interpreted accordingly. Generalizability sensitivity analyses across five heterogeneous ERP scenarios—varying anomaly prevalence, feature-distribution shift, module composition, and cross-industry correlation structure—confirm F-scores in the range [0.794, 0.862], supporting the framework’s transferability beyond the primary dataset. Deployment in a manufacturing environment demonstrates $4.1 million in fraud prevention value with a net benefit of $3.27 million (conservative ROI: 392%; an optimistic scenario including a speculative, empirically unvalidated deterrence multiplier projects 884 % ROI and is provided for illustrative purposes only) and regulatory compliance across SOX 404, the COSO framework, and ISO 27001.Keywords: ERP systems, Anomaly detection, Explainable AI, Ensemble learning, SHAP analysis, Fraud detectionSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) systems form the transactional backbone of modern manufacturing, orchestrating financial accounting, procurement, inventory management, supply chain coordination, and resource allocation within a single integrated platform. A typical mid-market facility generates 178 data attributes per transaction across hundreds of thousands of daily records1,2. Despite layered access controls and workflow safeguards, anomalies—ranging from inadvertent data-entry errors to sophisticated fraudulent schemes— persistently evade detection. The 2023 Association of Certified Fraud Examiners (ACFE) report estimates that business-process fraud costs organisations an average of $145,000 per incident, with a median detection lag exceeding 18 months3; corroborating evidence from KPMG’s global fraud survey further underscores the pervasiveness of enterprise fraud across sectors4.

In our analysis of 209,666 real-world ERP transactions, a comprehensive forensic audit (described in Section Ground-Truth Construction and Labelling Protocol) identified 12,682 anomalous transactions, representing an anomaly prevalence of 6.05 %. Critically, the organisation’s pre-existing routine manual audit programme—constrained by staff capacity, sampling-based audit design, and the absence of automated detection—had not flagged these transactions prior to the forensic engagement. We therefore characterise the pre-automation regime as having an approximately 85–93 % evasion rate for such anomalies (the exact figure depends on assumptions about prior partial detection); we use “evasion rate” throughout to mean the fraction of confirmed anomalies not detected under the pre-automation control regime, not the anomaly prevalence itself. The resulting exposure—approximately $4.1 million within a single organisation—provides a compelling motivation for automated, yet interpretable, anomaly detection5,6.

Enterprise IT infrastructure security provides important context for ERP anomaly detection. Research has demonstrated that analysing traffic patterns is essential for network optimisation and security monitoring within large-scale Data Centre Networks (DCN) that host ERP workloads7. The transition to cloud-based ERP has introduced complex new attack surfaces; recent frameworks have addressed these by applying standardised security evaluations based on NIST and ISO protocols to ensure system integrity8. Complementing infrastructure-level defences, the integration of Zero Trust Architecture (ZTA) has emerged as a critical mechanism for securing Oracle ERP Cloud environments through identity-centric protection and continuous monitoring9—an architectural philosophy that informs the continuous, transaction-level anomaly detection approach developed in this paper.

Data-driven anomaly detection in enterprise settings has progressed from purely statistical process monitoring to machine-learning-centric pipelines10,11. Wang et al.1 demonstrated the promise—and the interpretability limitations—of deep learning for smart-manufacturing analytics. In the narrower domain of industrial control-system security, Liu et al.5 proposed deep-learning-based intrusion detection, and Zhang et al.6 developed ensemble fault-detection methods. However, both lines of work focus on physical process telemetry rather than transactional ERP data, leaving a gap in enterprise system security. Process mining has emerged as a complementary lens: van der Aalst12 formalised the extraction of process models from event logs, and subsequent work applied these models to supply-chain and workflow anomaly detection13–15. Recent work in adjacent financial-risk domains has also demonstrated the value of integrating heterogeneous feature families for rare-event detection16,17, a principle that motivates the multi-category feature engineering adopted here. Despite these advances, the integration of explainable AI with process-aware anomaly detection in a production ERP context—particularly under regulatory audit constraints—remains under-explored18.

SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)19 has become one of the most widely adopted model-agnostic explanation frameworks. SHAP has been applied to fault diagnosis20, IoT anomaly detection18, and feature-selection stability analysis21. Antwarg et al.22 extended SHAP to explain autoencoder-based anomalies, while Marcílio and Eler23 assessed SHAP values as a feature-selection mechanism; Ponce-Bobadilla et al.24 recently provided practical implementation guidelines. The value of combining gradient-boosting classifiers with SHAP for interpretable risk prediction has been convincingly demonstrated across domains: Shen et al.25 showed that XGBoost with SHAP analysis provides not only accurate multi-class risk predictions but also transparent, actionable feature contributions that support decision-makers in high-stakes settings, emphasising that model interpretability and predictive accuracy are complementary rather than competing objectives. This principle directly informs the design of our three-layer explainability framework, where SHAP attribution explains individual transaction risk drivers and complements global feature rankings and surrogate rules to support traceable audit judgements (Sections "Layer 3: SHAP Decomposition" and "Divergence Between GB Importance and SHAP Rankings"). Broader XAI surveys26–28 have established evaluation taxonomies applicable to industrial deployments, with domain-specific extensions to healthcare29 and defence30,31 further refining interpretability criteria. A recent comprehensive benchmark evaluated 16 business-process anomaly detection methods32 and identified explainable anomaly detection and human-in-the-loop frameworks as the two highest-priority research directions. In adjacent domains, XGBoost- and LightGBM-based financial fraud detection systems commonly achieve F-scores of 0.91–0.95 on supervised benchmark datasets16; the unsupervised and semi-supervised nature of ERP anomaly detection distinguishes it from these fully supervised settings and explains the lower F-score ceiling. The combination of real ERP transactional data, multi-layer explainability spanning importance rankings, decision rules, and instance-level SHAP, and documented production deployment with ROI quantification represents a distinct contribution relative to prior work; however, we note that any individual property (e.g., SHAP explanation of autoencoders, surrogate decision trees) has antecedents in the literature, and the primary novelty lies in their joint application to ERP anomaly detection under regulatory constraints.

ERP anomaly detection poses three specific challenges: (i) multi-perspective dependencies across control-flow, resource, and attribute dimensions that univariate monitors cannot capture10; (ii) a high-dimensional sparse feature space (178 raw attributes) that creates the curse of dimensionality33; and (iii) severe class imbalance (6.05 % anomaly rate) with ground-truth scarcity that undermines supervised approaches20,34.

These challenges are not unique to ERP settings. In financial transaction networks, Lei et al.35 demonstrated that multi-scale graph learning with neighbourhood diffusion and temporal consistency constraints substantially improves detection stability under non-stationary conditions characterised by sparse labels, relational dependencies, and temporal drift. Although ERP transaction data does not naturally constitute an explicit transaction network, it shares the same structural difficulties: severe class imbalance, complex cross-document and cross-user dependencies (e.g., three-way purchase-order matching, shared login sessions), and dynamic behavioural patterns that shift with procurement cycles and supplier changes. This parallel motivates the use of heterogeneous model ensembles and stability-oriented design in our framework—covering density, boundary, tree, and reconstruction detection geometries—and the emphasis on cross-validation stability as a primary design criterion alongside peak F-score.

Guided by these challenges and the open priorities identified by Guan et al.32, this work pursues four research objectives:

RO1. Design a robust, heterogeneous ensemble architecture for ERP anomaly detection that maintains competitive accuracy on real-world transactional data while offering principled diversity guarantees across detection geometries.

RO2. Develop a multi-layer explainability framework that produces actionable, human-interpretable explanations at global, rule-based, and instance levels, serving diverse organisational stakeholders without requiring machine-learning expertise.

RO3. Establish the statistical reliability of the proposed framework through rigorous validation that satisfies the assumptions of the chosen tests, provides non-parametric alternatives, and quantifies practical effect sizes.

RO4. Bridge the research–practice gap by quantifying the financial return on investment of the deployed system and documenting its alignment with regulatory compliance frameworks relevant to manufacturing enterprises.

Research contributions

The four contributions of this paper map directly to the research objectives stated above.

RC1 RO1: Heterogeneous six-model ensemble with diversity analysis. We combine Isolation Forest, Local Outlier Factor, One-Class SVM, Elliptic Envelope, Gradient Boosting, and a Robust Autoencoder into an F-score-weighted ensemble achieving 0.8437 on 209,666 real-world ERP transactions. Each model is selected to cover a distinct detection geometry (density, boundary, tree, and reconstruction), yielding a mean pairwise Jaccard similarity of 0.38—confirming 62 % detection disagreement and validating the ensemble’s diversity. We provide a principled analysis (Section Per-Model Performance, Ensemble Analysis, and Pareto-Front Optimisation) of why the ensemble F-score is lower than those of certain individually evaluated models: the voting mechanism deliberately trades peak single-model accuracy for improved robustness (cross-validation coefficient of variation: 0.49 % vs. 1.4–2.1 % for individual models), reduced false-positive variance, and broader anomaly coverage. A Pareto-front analysis over all 63 non-trivial model subsets, a pairwise error correlation analysis, and a leave-one-model-out ablation (Section Per-Model Performance, Ensemble Analysis, and Pareto-Front Optimisation) formally justify this trade-off. Generalisation under heterogeneous ERP scenarios is assessed via systematic sensitivity analysis (Section Generalizability Sensitivity Analysis), confirming F-scores in the range [0.794, 0.862] across five simulated scenarios.

RC2 RO2: Three-layer explainability framework with effectiveness evaluation. We introduce a multi-stakeholder interpretability stack comprising (i) gradient-boosting feature importance, where the top five features explain 48.92 % of detection decisions (executive summary for plant managers); (ii) a surrogate decision tree (max_depth=5, 63 leaf nodes) that captures 87.3 % of ensemble logic in human-readable rules mappable to COSO control activities (compliance-officer level); and (iii) SHAP instance-level decompositions with confidence intervals that attribute each anomaly score to individual feature contributions (data-scientist level), following the principle established by Shen et al.25 that interpretable feature attribution supports actionable, traceable decision-making. We additionally present a structured expert assessment of explainability effectiveness (Section Explainability Framework Effectiveness) and discuss the expected divergence between gradient-boosting impurity importance and SHAP-based attribution (Section "Divergence Between GB Importance and SHAP Rankings").

RC3 RO3: Rigorous, assumption-verified statistical validation. We report 5-fold stratified cross-validation (F-score: 0.8444± 0.0041), paired t-tests (t=33.5, p<0.001) whose normality assumption is explicitly verified via the Shapiro–Wilk test (W=0.94, p=0.42), a non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank alternative (p=0.031), and effect-size quantification (Cohen’s ). This large effect size reflects the supervised ensemble versus unsupervised baseline comparison and should be interpreted in that specific context rather than as a general indicator of superiority. We additionally provide pairwise comparisons against the three highest-performing individual models.

RC4 RO4: Quantified production deployment with regulatory compliance. Deployment in a mid-market manufacturing environment demonstrates $4.1 M in fraud prevention value, a net benefit of $3.27 M, and a conservative ROI of 392 %. The implementation includes full compliance documentation for SOX 404, the COSO framework, and ISO 27001. A Monte Carlo sensitivity analysis (Section "ROI Sensitivity Analysis") quantifies the robustness of ROI estimates; the break-even confirmation rate (8.4 %) is well below the observed 28.2 %, supporting the financial viability of deployment across a range of parameter assumptions.

Results

Ensemble detection performance

The six-model ensemble was evaluated on a held-out test set of 41,933 transactions (20 % stratified split) from the 209,666-record dataset. Importantly, the train/test split was performed before any feature selection or preprocessing; all feature engineering steps (RFE, Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) filtering, Mann–Whitney selection) were applied exclusively within training folds during cross-validation (see Section Feature Engineering for details). Table 2 reports the primary performance metrics. Given the class-imbalanced nature of the data (6.05 % anomaly rate), we emphasise F-score, AUC-PR, and recall over accuracy, which is inflated by the majority class. The ensemble achieved an F-score of 0.8437 with precision 0.8856 and recall 0.8034, reflecting the design emphasis on precision (minimising false positives that waste audit resources) while maintaining recall above 80 %.

Error correlation analysis

To formally explain why combining high-performing individual models produces a lower ensemble F-score, we computed the pairwise error correlation matrix across the six models. The three highest-scoring models (GB, AE, EE) exhibit Pearson error correlations of , , and , indicating highly correlated error patterns. When errors are correlated, weighted averaging cannot cancel errors as effectively as with independent predictors. In contrast, the lower-performing models (IF, LOF, OCSVM) exhibit low error correlation with the top models (), contributing complementary detection patterns. The F-score-weighted voting assigns IF and LOF weights of 0.1427 and 0.1300 respectively, which dilute the high individual scores of GB, AE, and EE but capture 5,055 anomalies that no single model detects. This explains the 8–9 percentage-point F-score reduction: the ensemble sacrifices peak accuracy for detection breadth and stability. The ensemble false-positive rate (FP rate ) is 19.4 % lower than the unweighted arithmetic mean constituent FP rate (, where precisions are drawn from Table 3), confirming that the voting mechanism suppresses constituent false positives beyond what would be expected from any single model operating alone.

Leave-one-model-out ablation

Table 5 presents the leave-one-model-out ablation results. Removing any single model degrades at least one operationally critical metric: removing LOF reduces unique anomaly coverage by 12.3 %; removing IF raises the false-positive rate by 1.8 %; removing GB eliminates Layer 1 explainability. No five-model subset simultaneously matches the ensemble’s stability, coverage, and explainability.

Statistical significance, assumption verification, and comparison against high-performing individual models

Ensemble vs. isolation forest (unsupervised baseline)

We compared the ensemble against the best-performing purely unsupervised single model (Isolation Forest) using a paired t-test over 5-fold F-scores. Because the paired t-test assumes normally distributed differences, we first applied the Shapiro–Wilk test to the five fold-level differences , obtaining W=0.94 with p=0.42—providing no evidence to reject normality at α =0.05.

Explainability results

Layer 1: feature importance

The gradient-boosting model provides global feature importance via impurity-based gain. The top five features account for 48.92 % of total importance: LAST_UPDATE_LOGIN (11.3 %), NEW_COST (9.5 %), SHIPMENT_NUMBER (8.8 %), TRANSACTION_QUANTITY (8.5 %), and PRIOR_COST (8.3 %). These rankings reveal that user-session metadata and cost modifications are the strongest anomaly signals, followed by supply-chain sequence integrity and quantity discrepancies—consistent with ACFE fraud typologies3. Figure 3 presents the full top-15 feature ranking; features are colour-coded by category (financial, quantity, control-flow, resource, derived) to highlight that the top-five predictors span three distinct categories, confirming that the multi-category feature design captures complementary anomaly mechanisms. A surrogate decision tree (max_depth=5) fitted to gradient-boosting predictions yields 63 leaf nodes, reproducing 87.3 % of ensemble logic with 94.1 % fidelity. These rules map directly to COSO control activities and ISO 27001 requirements.

Layer 3: SHAP decomposition

SHAP values19 decompose each prediction into additive feature contributions. As an illustrative example, Transaction (94 % anomaly probability) decomposes as: base rate (+0.35), unauthorised user (+0.21), 289 % cost variance (+0.16), high equipment cost (+0.11), expedited mode (+0.08), high encumbrance (+0.07), authorised manager (-0.03, mitigating), legitimate procurement chain (-0.01, mitigating). This identifies the primary risk driver while crediting mitigating factors, enabling nuanced review decisions.

The role of SHAP in providing transparent, actionable feature contributions for decision-makers has been convincingly established across risk-prediction domains. Shen et al.25 demonstrated that combining XGBoost with SHAP analysis for multi-class risk classification produces not only accurate predictions but also interpretable attribution maps that enable domain experts to understand why a given instance is flagged, thereby improving confidence and uptake among practitioners. Our framework extends this principle to ERP anomaly detection: the SHAP decomposition at Layer 3 explains key risk drivers at the individual transaction level (e.g., the dominant role of cost variance and session identity in above), and its complementary relationship with Layer 1 global rankings and Layer 2 surrogate rules ensures that explanations are accessible across stakeholder groups—from forensic analysts to plant managers. This multi-level coverage directly supports the traceable audit trail required under SOX 404 and COSO.

Divergence between GB importance and SHAP rankings

The gradient-boosting impurity importance (Layer 1) and the SHAP-based attribution (Layer 3) produce different feature rankings: LAST_UPDATE_LOGIN and NEW_COST dominate impurity importance, while SERIAL#, PLANNING_TP_TYPE, and PRIOR_COST exhibit the highest mean absolute SHAP values. This divergence is expected and well documented in the literature19,21. Impurity-based importance is biased toward high-cardinality features (features with many unique values receive more split opportunities), while SHAP values provide theoretically grounded, game-theoretic attribution that accounts for feature interactions. Resource-identifier features such as SERIAL# and PLANNING_TP_TYPE have high cardinality, inflating their SHAP impact relative to impurity importance.

Rather than undermining the framework, this divergence strengthens the case for multi-layer explainability: impurity importance provides a computationally efficient global summary suitable for executive dashboards, while SHAP values offer more accurate, interaction-aware attribution for forensic analysis. Practitioners should use Layer 1 for rapid triage and Layer 3 for detailed investigation.

Explainability framework effectiveness

To assess whether the three-layer explainability framework meaningfully improves decision quality, we conducted a structured audit-decision experiment with 12 domain experts (six certified internal auditors and six procurement officers drawn from the manufacturing organisation’s compliance function). Experts were presented with 60 flagged transactions sampled stratified across risk tiers and asked to classify each as “confirm fraud,” “investigate further,” or “dismiss” under two conditions in a within-subject crossover design: (A) detection score only (no explanation); and (B) detection score plus all three layers of explanation. Ground-truth labels were available after each session for outcome assessment.

Discussion

This work demonstrates that competitive anomaly detection accuracy can coexist with comprehensive, multi-stakeholder explainability in production ERP environments. The four research objectives are addressed as follows.

Regarding RO1 (RC1), the six-model ensemble achieves an F-score of 0.8437 on real-world data. While individual supervised models achieve higher peak F-scores (GB: 0.9321), the Pareto-front analysis over 63 model subsets confirms that the full six-model ensemble is the unique Pareto-optimal configuration when F-score stability (CV: 0.49 %), false-positive rate, and anomaly coverage are optimised jointly. Importantly, the paired comparisons in Table 6 confirm that the ensemble is statistically significantly lower than GB, AE, and EE on peak F-score—an honest acknowledgement that the design explicitly accepts a lower F-score ceiling in exchange for operational robustness. Here, “robustness” is operationalised as stability across data partitions (low CV) and resistance to distributional shift, not as accuracy maximisation. The generalizability sensitivity analysis (Section Generalizability Sensitivity Analysis) further demonstrates that F-scores in the range [0.794, 0.862] are maintainable across five heterogeneous ERP scenarios, with ensemble CV consistently below that of constituent individual models even under perturbation. The parallel with non-stationary financial transaction network detection identified by Lei et al.35—where heterogeneous, stability-oriented ensemble design improves robustness under class imbalance and temporal drift—reinforces the theoretical motivation for our design choice.

Regarding RO2 (RC2), the three-layer explainability framework ensures that 100 % of flagged transactions carry actionable explanations. The expert assessment experiment (Section Explainability Framework Effectiveness) provides preliminary evidence that the three-layer XAI stack improves auditor classification accuracy by 14.9 percentage points and reduces decision time by 36 %, though this assessment was conducted with 12 experts from a single organisation and requires external validation. The SHAP decomposition layer, informed by the principle established by Shen et al.25 that interpretable feature attribution supports transparent and actionable decision-making in high-stakes settings, produces instance-level explanations that identify dominant risk drivers and mitigating factors for each flagged transaction, enabling auditors to make confident, evidence-based classification decisions. The documented divergence between impurity importance and SHAP rankings (Section "Divergence Between GB Importance and SHAP Rankings") is a feature, not a limitation.

Regarding RO3 (RC3), the dual parametric–non-parametric validation protocol addresses a common methodological weakness in applied anomaly detection studies. The large Cohen’s reflects the supervised-versus-unsupervised paradigm gap between the ensemble and the IF baseline, not the ensemble’s advantage over its strongest competitors—as Table 6 clarifies, the ensemble is statistically significantly worse than GB, AE, and EE on peak F-score, a finding we report transparently to support honest assessment of the design trade-offs.

Regarding RO4 (RC4), deployment demonstrates $3.27 M net benefit with documented compliance across SOX 404, COSO, and ISO 27001. All ROI figures in the main text and abstract refer to the conservative scenario (392 %); the 884 % optimistic-scenario ROI applies a speculative, empirically unvalidated 2× deterrence multiplier and is provided for illustrative purposes only. The Monte Carlo sensitivity analysis confirms positive ROI across 98.7 % of simulated parameter combinations, with a break-even confirmation rate of 8.4 %—well below the observed 28.2 %.

Limitations

Four principal limitations should be noted.

First, the model is trained on procurement and inventory transactions from a single legal entity operating multiple manufacturing plants. The generalizability sensitivity analysis in Section Generalizability Sensitivity Analysis demonstrates that the framework retains F-scores in the range [0.794, 0.862] across five heterogeneous simulated scenarios—varying anomaly prevalence, feature-distribution shift, module composition, and covariance structure— providing preliminary evidence of transferability. Nevertheless, direct validation on external datasets from other ERP modules (e.g., accounts payable, fixed assets, human resources), industries (e.g., retail, healthcare, utilities), or organisational structures (e.g., publicly traded, multi-national, public sector) is required before deployment generalisation can be claimed with confidence. Meta-learning approaches for multi-site adaptation and federated learning across manufacturing sites represent the most direct path to empirically validated cross-enterprise generalizability.

Second, a semi-supervised extension incorporating expert-labelled samples could improve F-scores to 0.92–0.94; the human-in-the-loop feedback protocol provides a pathway to collect ∼1,000–3,000 labelled samples from production flagging.

Third, the sequential prediction latency (∼150 ms per transaction in the deployed containerised system) exceeds real-time requirements (<10 ms); model parallelisation and ONNX optimisation represent tractable engineering improvements. The 27.30 s fold-level prediction time in Table 3 represents batched, parallelised inference across 4 threads for 41,933 transactions; the 150 ms figure applies to sequential, per-transaction processing in the deployed service, where transactions are scored independently through all six models in series. SHAP explanations are computed asynchronously and do not contribute to alerting latency.

Fourth, concept drift and adversarial adaptation: the framework is trained on nine months of historical data. Enterprise procurement patterns evolve with seasonal cycles, supplier changes, and regulatory shifts; moreover, adversaries may adapt behaviour once a detection system is deployed. This challenge parallels the non-stationary conditions studied by Lei et al.35 in financial transaction networks, where temporal consistency constraints were shown to improve detection stability under drift. In the current production deployment, the model is retrained quarterly using a sliding 12-month window, and distribution-shift monitoring (KL divergence on feature marginals) triggers ad hoc retraining when drift exceeds a predefined threshold. The F-score of 0.8437 reflects performance over the nine-month training period; maintaining this performance against emerging fraud types requires continuous monitoring and retraining. Formal integration of online drift detection (e.g., ADWIN36) and adversarial robustness testing represent important future work.

Future directions include semi-supervised enhancement with ∼10 % labelled data, online detection at transaction-posting time via probabilistic automata, meta-learning for multi-plant generalisation, causal counterfactual explanations, and federated learning across manufacturing sites.

Methods

Note on paper structure: This manuscript follows the Results-before-Methods convention adopted by Scientific Reports. All methods described below were designed and implemented prior to results collection; the ordering is editorial rather than procedural. Cross-references from Results to Methods sections guide readers who wish to consult methodological details alongside specific results.

Dataset

The primary dataset consists of 209,666 procurement and manufacturing transactions extracted over nine months from the production ERP system (SAP/Oracle-equivalent) of a mid-market, multi-plant manufacturing organisation (single legal entity). Each record contains 178 raw attributes: 52 resource identifiers, 41 financial values, 28 status indicators, and 57 system-metadata fields. The class distribution is heavily imbalanced: 196,984 normal transactions (93.95 %) and 12,682 flagged anomalies (6.05 %). Data-quality profiling revealed that 94 of 178 features (52.8 %) contained at least one missing entry, with an overall cell-level missingness rate of 0.83 %; additionally, 0.2 % of records were duplicates, 0.7 % contained invalid entries (infinities), and 2.1 % were statistical outliers beyond three standard deviations.

Ground-truth construction and labelling protocol

The 12,682 anomaly labels were established through a multi-stage audit process designed to ensure independence and reproducibility.

Stage 1: Initial flagging. An independent external forensic accounting firm applied rule-based transaction-monitoring filters (threshold alerts on cost variance, duplicate invoice detection, segregation-of-duties violations) to the full 209,666-record dataset, producing 18,412 candidate anomalies.

Stage 2: Expert adjudication. Two certified internal auditors (CIA-credentialled, >10 years ERP audit experience), blinded to each other’s assessments, independently reviewed each candidate. Transactions labelled anomalous by both auditors were accepted directly (10,891); disagreements (3,217) were escalated to a third senior auditor for tie-breaking, of whom 1,791 were confirmed anomalous, yielding the final 12,682 anomaly labels.

Stage 3: Inter-rater reliability. Cohen’s κ between the two primary auditors was 0.81 (95 % CI: [0.79, 0.83]), indicating “almost perfect” agreement per Landis and Koch37. The remaining candidates were labelled normal.

Independence from feature engineering. The domain-expert review in Feature Engineering Step 4 (Section Feature Engineering) was performed by a separate team—two senior auditors and one ERP architect who were not involved in the labelling process. Their role was limited to validating business-logic coherence and the absence of target leakage.

Post-deployment confirmation. The 28.2 % fraud confirmation rate reported in Section Business Impact is based on new transactions flagged during production deployment (post-training), reviewed by the same external forensic firm. None of the original labelled training data was included in this evaluation.

Class imbalance handling

The 93.95:6.05 normal-to-anomaly ratio constitutes severe class imbalance. We employed four complementary strategies throughout the pipeline to address this.

(i) Contamination parameter alignment. For models requiring a contamination parameter (Isolation Forest, LOF, Elliptic Envelope), we set contamination=0.10, above the observed 6.05 % rate to account for boundary uncertainty and reduce false-negative risk.

(ii) Imbalance-aware metrics. We report F-score, AUC-PR, and recall as primary metrics rather than accuracy, which is dominated by the majority class and inflated by correctly classifying normal transactions. Table 2 presents AUC-PR (0.8923) as the primary scalar metric for ranking under imbalance.

(iii) Stratified cross-validation.

All 5-fold cross-validation splits preserve the 6.05 %/93.95 % class ratio within each fold, ensuring that each validation fold reflects the true class distribution.

(iv) Threshold selection via precision–recall optimisation. The ensemble threshold τ =0.25 was selected by maximising the F-score on the validation partition of each cross-validation fold (not on the held-out test set), balancing precision and recall across class-imbalanced folds. At the high-confidence threshold τ =0.50 (used for test-set metrics in Table 2), the precision–recall trade-off was explicitly verified against the PR curve.

Note on resampling. Synthetic oversampling techniques (e.g., SMOTE) were considered but not employed because (a) they are incompatible with several unsupervised constituent models (IF, LOF, OCSVM) that require no explicit class labels during training, and (b) preliminary experiments on the training partition showed no F-score improvement when SMOTE was applied to the Gradient Boosting training data alone (+0.003 F-score, within cross-validation noise).

Feature engineering

The 178 raw attributes were reduced to 29 discriminative features through four stages. Critically, the 80/20 stratified train/test split was performed before any feature selection. All feature engineering steps described below were applied exclusively to the training partition (); the held-out test set () was sequestered and not used for any feature selection, threshold tuning, or model selection. Within cross-validation, feature selection was repeated independently on each training fold (nested design).

In the first stage, recursive feature elimination (RFE) using a gradient-boosting classifier removed the bottom 10 % of features iteratively until cross-validated F-score degraded by more than 1 %, yielding 48 candidates. The second stage removed multicollinear features (VIF >5); retained features exhibit mean VIF =1.84, max =3.3. The third stage retained only features with Mann–Whitney U test p<0.05 comparing anomaly and normal subpopulations within the training partition (all 29 passed). The fourth stage involved domain-expert review by two senior auditors and one ERP architect (independent from the labelling team; see Section Ground-Truth Construction and Labelling Protocol) to validate business-logic mapping and the absence of target leakage—specifically, confirming that no feature was a direct derivative of the anomaly label or post-hoc audit outcome. The final set retains 92.3 % of discriminative power (ablation-verified) across five categories: financial (8 features), quantity (5), control-flow (6), resource (6), and derived (4).

In designing the feature groups, we followed the principle—demonstrated in adjacent rare-event detection research16,17— that rare-event risk detection benefits from integrating heterogeneous signals rather than relying on a single transaction-attribute family. Accordingly, the feature groups capture complementary anomaly mechanisms: financial features detect cost manipulation, quantity features detect discrepancies between ordered and received goods, control-flow features detect authorisation bypasses, resource features detect user-level patterns consistent with account sharing or collusion, and derived features capture cross-field ratios that are individually normal but jointly anomalous.

Mann–Whitney U test assumptions under class imbalance. The Mann–Whitney U test is distribution-free and tests for stochastic dominance between two groups. Under the alternative hypothesis, the test is commonly interpreted as a location-shift test, which requires that the two distributions differ only in location (not shape). In the presence of severe class imbalance, this assumption warrants scrutiny. We verified it by inspecting quantile–quantile plots comparing anomaly and normal feature distributions for all 29 candidate features. For 24 of 29 features, the distributions exhibited a monotone-transformation relationship consistent with a location-shift interpretation. For the remaining 5 features (SERIAL#, PLANNING_TP_TYPE: bimodal anomaly distributions; COST_VARIANCE_PCT, BUDGET_VARIANCE, ENCUMBRANCE_AMOUNT: heavy-tailed anomaly distributions), the Mann–Whitney test remains a valid test of stochastic dominance—though not strictly of location shift—and the presence of shape differences was flagged to domain experts in Stage 4. All five features were retained after domain-expert review confirmed their business-logic validity, and ablation analysis showed that removing any of them degraded held-out F-score by 0.3–1.2 percentage points.

Data preprocessing

Preprocessing follows four stages applied independently within each training fold during cross-validation.

Infinite-value replacement. Infinite values (0.7 % of records) are replaced by the 99th-percentile value of each feature within the training fold. We verified that all 1,468 records with infinite values in the training partition were attributable to system-entry errors: (i) they arose exclusively in derived ratio features (COST_VARIANCE_PCT, QUANTITY_VARIANCE_PCT) as a result of division-by-zero when denominator fields were missing or zero-filled by the ERP system on incomplete records; (ii) cross-referencing with the ground-truth label distribution confirmed that none of the 1,468 infinite-valued records was labelled as anomalous; and (iii) the same records exhibited missing values in correlated fields (PRIOR_COST, ORDERED_QUANTITY), consistent with incomplete-entry artefacts. The 99th-percentile replacement therefore does not discard legitimate anomaly signals; it corrects system artefacts in a small subset of incomplete records.

Missing-value imputation. Missing values (0.83 % of total entries) are imputed with the training-fold median, chosen for robustness to right-skewed financial distributions (test–retest r=0.997).

Clipping. Features are clipped at the 1 st and 99th percentiles, preserving 99.8 % of known anomalies.

Ensemble architecture

The ensemble combines six models selected to cover complementary detection geometries.

Isolation Forest (IF) 38 identifies anomalies as instances requiring fewer random splits to isolate, efficiently detecting globally extreme values. Configuration: 100 trees, max_samples=256, contamination=0.10.

Local Outlier Factor (LOF) 39 computes local density relative to k-nearest neighbours, detecting contextual anomalies that appear normal globally but deviate locally. Configuration: k=20, Euclidean distance.

One-Class SVM (OCSVM) 40 maps data into kernel space and finds the smallest enclosing hypersphere, capturing non-linear decision boundaries. Configuration: RBF kernel, ν =0.10, gamma=’scale’.

Elliptic Envelope (EE) fits a robust Minimum Covariance Determinant estimator to model the multivariate Gaussian structure of normal transactions. Configuration: contamination=0.10.

Gradient Boosting (GB) captures complex non-linear feature interactions and provides native feature-importance scores forming Layer 1 of the explainability framework. Configuration: 100 stages, learning_rate=0.1, max_depth=5, subsample=0.8.

Robust Autoencoder (AE) learns a compressed latent representation () with BatchNormalisation and Dropout(0.2). Training minimises Huber loss (δ =1.35) via Adam () with early stopping (; halts at epoch 18). Two additional dense skip (residual) connections—one in the encoder path (29→ 16, 480 parameters) and one in the decoder path (16→ 29, 493 parameters)—enable gradient flow through the bottleneck. The complete model summary is provided in Supplementary Table S1, confirming 31,446 trainable parameters (32,694 total, including 1,248 non-trainable BatchNorm running statistics) across the ensemble of three independent autoencoders. High reconstruction error flags anomalies deviating from the learned normal manifold.

Explainability framework

Three complementary explanation mechanisms target different stakeholders. Layer 1 uses gradient-boosting impurity-based gain for global feature importance rankings. Layer 2 fits a surrogate decision tree (max_depth=5) to ensemble predictions, producing 63 human-readable leaf nodes covering 87.3 % of decision logic (fidelity measured on the held-out test set). Layer 3 computes SHAP values19 to decompose individual predictions into additive feature contributions via game-theoretic Shapley values, following the principle established by Shen et al.25 that transparent, actionable feature attribution supports decision-maker confidence and uptake. The expected divergence between Layers 1 and 3 is discussed in Section "Divergence Between GB Importance and SHAP Rankings". The computational cost of SHAP is reported in Table 8; in the deployed system, SHAP is computed asynchronously for flagged transactions only.

Statistical validation protocol

Performance is assessed via 5-fold stratified cross-validation. The ensemble is compared to (i) the best unsupervised baseline (Isolation Forest) using a paired t-test, with normality of paired differences verified by the Shapiro–Wilk test; and (ii) the three highest-performing individual models (GB, AE, EE) using the same paired t-test protocol (Table 6). A non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test provides confirmation without distributional assumptions for the IF comparison. Effect size is reported as Cohen’s 41, the mean difference divided by the standard deviation of the within-pair differences.

Ethical considerations and data protection

The dataset contains resource-identifier fields (CREATED_BY, LAST_UPDATED_BY) that constitute processing of personal data. All resource identifiers were pseudonymised via irreversible SHA-256 hashing before extraction from the production system; the mapping table was destroyed after extraction. Model training and evaluation were conducted on the pseudonymised data; no personally identifiable information (PII) is stored in, or recoverable from, the trained models or SHAP explanations. The deployed dashboard presents pseudonymised identifiers; de-pseudonymisation for investigative follow-up is performed by the organisation’s compliance office under existing internal audit authority, not by the ML system. These measures align with Pakistan’s Personal Data Protection Bill 2023 and are consistent with GDPR Article 89 provisions for scientific research. The study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of NUST Military College of Signals, Islamabad, Pakistan, and the organisation’s internal data governance committee. A waiver of individual informed consent was granted on the basis that all PII was irreversibly pseudonymised prior to data extraction. The expert-assessment experiment reported in Section Explainability Framework Effectiveness (12 domain experts, crossover design) was conducted under the same IRB approval, which covers research activities within the manufacturing organisation’s compliance function; participants provided verbal informed consent and were aware that their audit decisions were being recorded for research purposes.

Software and reproducibility

The software stack comprises scikit-learn 1.3.2, TensorFlow 2.14.0, SHAP 0.43.0, pandas 2.0.3, NumPy 1.24.3, and XGBoost 2.0.1. All experiments were conducted on a GPU workstation (NVIDIA A100 40 GB, 8-core AMD EPYC CPU, 64 GB RAM). The production deployment architecture routes transactions through a containerised prediction service (Docker/MLflow), a rule-based decision engine stratifying alerts by risk tier, and a dashboard presenting SHAP explanations to compliance officers. Per-transaction inference latency in the deployed system is ∼150 ms (sequential processing of all six models per transaction); SHAP explanations are computed asynchronously in daily batch mode for flagged transactions only (Table 8).
