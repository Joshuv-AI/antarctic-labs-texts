Differential effects of cognitive training and aerobic exercise on regional gray matter volume and inter-regional covariance in community-dwelling older adults
Authors: Lijuan Jiang, Xinyi Cao, Ting Li, Kai Wei, Shaohui Lin, Junjie Yang, Nannan Gu, Yang Hu, Chunbo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrevious studies have shown that both cognitive training and aerobic exercise benefit cognition and brain structure in healthy older adults, yet direct comparisons are limited. This study aimed to compare the effects of three months of cognitive training and aerobic exercise on regional gray matter volume and inter‑regional covariance in community‑dwelling older adults. Structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was conducted at baseline and 12-month follow-up. Repeated-measures analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) revealed nominally significant time-by-group interactions in the left posterior orbital gyrus (POrG), left posterior insula (PIns), left subcallosal area (SCA), and left transverse temporal gyrus (TTG), with only the left POrG remaining significant after false discovery rate (FDR) correction. At the 12-month follow-up, left POrG volume increased only in the aerobic exercise group, whereas the control group showed decreases in the left POrG, left PIns, and left SCA. Inter-regional covariance analyses revealed weaker covariance between volume changes in the left SCA and left TTG in the aerobic exercise group compared with controls, although this effect did not survive FDR correction, suggesting a lower degree of coordinated structural change between these regions. Overall, these findings suggest that both cognitive training and aerobic exercise are associated with attenuated age-related gray matter volume reduction, though they differ in the extent and coordination patterns of these volume changes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46631-6.Subject terms: Cognitive ageing, Alzheimer's disease

Introduction

Cognitive function declines with aging, manifesting as deterioration in working memory, processing speed, executive function, and attention1–3. Such impairments in older adults pose significant health challenges, resulting in considerable social and economic burdens4. In addition to cognitive decline, aging leads to a global reduction in brain volume, with pronounced decreases observed in the frontal and temporal lobes5. Consequently, in the context of an aging society, identifying strategies to prevent or delay age-related cognitive decline and brain atrophy has become a pressing priority6,7. Non-pharmacological interventions have emerged as a promising alternative to pharmacological therapies8. Evidence-based studies indicate that both cognitive training and physical exercise positively influence cognition and brain structure in healthy older adults9. However, studies directly comparing the effects of cognitive training and aerobic exercise on brain structure and behavior in healthy aging are still limited.

Research on older adults has shown that cognitive training can enhance cognitive function or slow cognitive decline10–12. For instance, our previous study demonstrated that the cognitive benefits of such interventions were sustained for up to one year after training concluded11. Similarly, the Advanced Cognitive Training for Independent and Vital Elderly (ACTIVE) study, which followed 2,832 healthy older adults over a 10-year period, reported that the effects of reasoning and speed-of-processing interventions were preserved in their respective cognitive domains12. In addition, evidence supports physical exercise as an effective strategy for mitigating cognitive decline in older adults13. Aerobic exercise, in particular, has been linked to improvements in several cognitive domains, including processing speed, attention, memory, and executive function14,15. Overall, both cognitive training and aerobic exercise have been shown to enhance cognitive function. Cognitive training might aim to enhance specific cognitive abilities and increase cognitive reserve, while aerobic exercise may improve physical health, potentially supporting cognitive function8.

Advancements in neuroimaging techniques have facilitated non-invasive observations of structural brain changes associated with normal aging, providing valuable insights into the neuroplasticity mechanisms underlying the effects of cognitive training and aerobic exercise. Both cognitive training and aerobic exercise have been shown to mitigate age-related reductions in gray matter volume16–18, possibly through different neuroprotective mechanisms8. Cognitive training enhances specific cognitive functions by promoting reorganization and strengthening of brain networks19, supporting the maintenance of volume in targeted brain regions20. In contrast, aerobic exercise improves cardiovascular health and cerebral blood flow21, influencing structural changes in multiple regions, including the prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, and cerebellum18,22,23. A systematic review by Intzandt et al. (2021)24 identified that cognitive training primarily improves white matter integrity, while exercise training, including aerobic and resistance types, tends to produce more pronounced macrostructural changes, such as volume-based brain alterations detectable via MRI in healthy older adults. Specifically, aerobic training appears to enhance or preserve gray matter volume, whereas resistance training has been associated with changes in white matter volume and may also influence functional brain activity, although the evidence for these effects is still emerging24,25. Both cognitive and exercise interventions are also associated with task-related changes in BOLD signals24. Moreover, there is growing interest in using structural covariance to investigate network-level changes in healthy aging and neurodegenerative diseases26,27. Structural covariance reflects coordinated volumetric changes between brain regions, which correspond to white matter connections and functional connectivity, providing unique insights into inter-regional relationships28,29. Despite these advances, the effects of cognitive training and aerobic exercise on gray matter volume and inter-regional covariance across multiple brain regions remain unclear, highlighting the need for further investigation.

In our previous study30, we directly compared the effects of cognitive training and aerobic exercise on resting-state functional connectivity in older adults, finding that both interventions modulate connectivity with the entorhinal cortex (EC) through different neural pathways, with changes in EC functional connectivity correlating with cognitive improvements. A similar study by Chapman et al. (2016)31 also compared these interventions, focusing on cerebral blood flow and cerebrovascular reactivity, and found that both interventions improve brain health in distinct ways. Building on these findings, we hypothesized that both cognitive training and aerobic exercise would mitigate age-related reductions in gray matter volume, with aerobic exercise potentially increasing it, but with different coordination patterns across brain regions. To explore this hypothesis across the brain, we analyzed 122 Gy matter regions of interest (ROIs) from the Neuromorphometrics atlas and compared alterations in regional gray matter volumes and inter-regional covariance between the cognitive training and aerobic exercise interventions from baseline to the 12-month follow-up. We further hypothesized that changes in gray matter volume would correlate with cognitive improvements and examined this relationship.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This study utilized an MRI sub-sample from a prior trial that examined the effects of cognitive training and aerobic exercise on the behavioral outcomes of healthy older adults. The original trial followed a prospective, assessor-blind, block-randomized, controlled, parallel design. It received ethical approval from the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Shanghai Mental Health Center (Approval number: 2013-40) and was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (http://www.chictr.org.cn) under Registration number ChiCTR-TRC-13004788. Recruitment for the MRI sub-sample involved sending advertisements to all participants from the original study. Those who expressed interest were assessed for MRI eligibility before being included in the sub-sample. We have previously published an article based on resting-state functional connectivity using this MRI sub-sample30. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Participants were recruited from the Jing’an District of Shanghai through a notice/broadcast disseminated by the local community service institute. The inclusion criteria required participants to be between the ages of 65 and 75, have completed at least one year of formal education, maintain normal functional abilities, and live independently in the community. Besides, they were required to have no impairments in hearing, vision, or communication. Participants had to achieve a score above 19 on the Chinese version of the Mini-Mental State Examination (CMMSE) for those with elementary education or a score above 24 for individuals with middle school education or higher (with a score range of 0 to 30, where higher scores reflect better cognitive function)32. Individuals with significant cognitive decline due to conditions such as Alzheimer’s disease, major neurological or psychiatric disorders (including stroke, depression, or schizophrenia), or active cancer requiring treatment were excluded from the study.

Figure 1 depicts the flow of participants throughout the study. A total of 539 individuals were initially evaluated for eligibility from November 2013 to September 2014, with 172 selected for the MRI sub-sample. At baseline, 47 participants in the cognitive training group, 49 in the aerobic exercise group, and 46 in the control group completed both cognitive assessments and MRI scans. During the intervention period, 4 participants withdrew consent, 5 experienced scheduling conflicts, and 4 were excluded due to pre-existing medical conditions. Between the end of the intervention and the 12-month follow-up, 5 withdrew consent, 6 experienced scheduling conflicts, 1 had recently undergone a scan, 9 refused scanning, and 8 were excluded due to abnormal MRI findings. At the 12-month follow-up, 100 participants had completed the post-intervention assessments and were included in the final analysis: 33 in the cognitive training group, 36 in the aerobic exercise group, and 31 in the control group (Fig. 1).

Interventions

Participants assigned to the intervention groups received either cognitive training or aerobic exercise. The cognitive training group participated in 24 one-hour group sessions conducted twice weekly over a 12-week period, as previously described in detail11. The cognitive training in this study targeted multiple domains, including memory (e.g., episodic memory, face-name associative memory, verbal paired associates learning, and semantically unrelated word lists), reasoning (e.g., series of figures, numbers, and words), problem-solving strategies (e.g., Tower of Hanoi), and visuospatial map reading skills. Implementation followed the structured manual from prior work, with two adjustments: the handcraft making was removed, and the health and physical exercise was replaced with finger and limb movements to prevent overlap with the elements present in aerobic exercise.

Aerobic exercise was conducted on a treadmill under supervision. Each session began with a 5-minute warm-up stretching routine, followed by brisk walking, and ended with a 5-minute cool-down period. Brisk walking duration started at 10 min and progressively increased by 5 min each week. By the 8th week, the duration reached 45 min and remained constant thereafter33,34. Participants wore heart rate monitors (Mio ALPHA, Mio Global, Physical Enterprises) to ensure their heart rate stayed within the target zones: 50–60% of the maximum heart rate reserve during the first 8 weeks, and 60–75% during weeks 9 through 1234. Additionally, a portable automated external defibrillator (AED) and emergency medications were readily available in the training area. The same schedule (twice weekly for 12 weeks) was used for both interventions.

Participants in the control group did not receive any intervention but attended the same health-education lectures as the intervention groups, thus serving as a passive control condition. During the first 3 months, all three groups attended monthly lectures on healthy living (e.g., common cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, osteoporosis, and geriatric anxiety and depression). Thereafter, lectures were held every 6 weeks through the 12-month follow-up. Each lecture lasted 75–90 min, with roughly 60 min dedicated to invited presentations, followed by 15 to 30 min of group discussions and interactive activities.

Cognitive measurement

To assess the impact of the intervention on cognitive function, the Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status (RBANS)35 was administered at baseline and at 12 months post-intervention. This assessment has shown strong reliability and validity in Chinese community-dwelling older adults36. The RBANS comprises 12 subtests that evaluate five cognitive domains: Immediate Memory (list learning and story memory), Visuospatial/Constructional (figure copying and line orientation), Language (picture naming and semantic fluency), Attention (digit span and coding), and Delayed Memory (list recall, list recognition, story memory and figure recall). Also, it yields an overall cognitive function score. All cognitive evaluations were conducted by trained research assistants, adhering to the standardized procedures outlined in the manual.

MRI acquisition

MRI scans were conducted at both baseline and 12 months post-intervention using a Siemens 3.0 Tesla Magnetom Verio scanner (Siemens Medical, Erlangen, Germany) at the Shanghai Mental Health Center. Foam pads and earplugs were used to minimize head movement and reduce scanner noise. Structural imaging was performed with a sagittal magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE) 3D T1-weighted sequence, using the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 1900 ms, echo time (TE) = 3.43 ms, flip angle (FA) = 9°, field of view (FOV) = 240 × 240 mm2, acquisition matrix = 256 × 256, number of slices = 160, slice thickness = 1 mm, and voxel size = 0.9 × 0.9 × 1.0 mm3. The total scanning time was 5 min. An experienced neuroradiologist reviewed all MRI images to identify and exclude artifacts, signs of stroke, brain tumors, or other abnormalities.

Image processing and quality control

T1-weighted structural images were processed using the Computational Anatomy Toolbox (CAT12, Version 12.6; http://www.neuro.uni-jena.de/cat/) in conjunction with SPM12 (Wellcome Trust Centre for Neuroimaging, London, UK; http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/)37. All longitudinal images underwent processing following the CAT12 longitudinal pipeline. Initially, raw images were converted from DICOM to NIFTI format. For each participant, baseline and follow-up T1-weighted images were aligned, and bias field correction was applied. These aligned images were then segmented to generate probabilistic maps of gray matter, white matter and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). A mean image of the aligned scans was used to derive the spatial normalization parameters for transforming the images from native space to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space via the DARTEL algorithm38. Total intracranial volume (TIV) was calculated as the sum of gray matter, white matter, and CSF volumes. Finally, image quality scores were generated in CAT12 based on resolution, intensity bias, and noise level, and any images with a quality score lower than ‘B-’ were excluded from further analysis.

Gray matter volume and inter-regional covariance quantification

The Neuromorphometrics parcellation atlas (http://www.neuromorphometrics.com/), which is available by default in CAT12, was transformed into each participant’s native space using the spatial normalization parameters derived earlier. Using the probabilistic gray matter map and the transformed parcellation atlas for each participant, the gray matter volume for each ROI was calculated by multiplying the mean gray matter proportion within the ROI by the total volume of the corresponding ROI mask. Gray matter volumes were extracted for 122 ROIs, excluding 20 regions that are not relevant to cortical or subcortical gray matter morphology (e.g., ventricles, brainstem, CSF, and white matter).

To evaluate inter-regional covariance among ROIs, we first calculated the change in gray matter volume for each ROI between baseline and follow-up scans (i.e., 12-month follow-up volume minus baseline volume). Spearman correlation coefficients were computed across participants to quantify the covariance of volume changes between every pair of ROIs with each group. These ROI-level gray matter volume and inter-regional covariance measures were then used for subsequent statistical analyses.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Version 20.0 (IBM Corporation, Somer, NY, USA), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Demographic characteristics across the three groups were compared using Chi-square tests for categorical variables and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables. Baseline cognitive performance across the three groups was analyzed using general linear models adjusted for age, sex, and years of education.

The primary outcomes of this study were changes in regional gray matter volume and inter‑regional covariance. To examine the effects of the intervention on RBANS domain index scores and 122 ROI-based gray matter volumes, repeated-measures analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed. Intervention type (cognitive training, aerobic exercise, or control) was entered as a between-subjects factor, and time (baseline and 12-month follow-up) as a within-subject factor. Age, sex, education, or baseline TIV were added as confounding covariates. Post-hoc within-group comparisons were performed using paired t-tests. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the false discovery rate (FDR) approach39, with the significance threshold set at q < 0.05. The relative annual volume change rate (Supplementary Method 1) was calculated only for ROIs with nominally significant time-by-group interaction effects.

For inter-regional covariance, the statistical analysis was restricted to ROIs that exhibited nominally significant time-by-group interaction effects. The secondary outcome was the correlation between changes in cognitive performance and gray matter volume. Partial correlations were calculated separately for each group between changes in RBANS domain index scores (12-month follow-up minus baseline) and changes in ROI-based gray matter volumes, while controlling for age, sex, education, and baseline TIV. All correlation results were corrected for multiple comparisons using the FDR adjustment.

Intervention effect on cognitive measures

No significant time-by-group interactions and main effects of group were observed for any RBANS measures (Table 2). In contrast, significant main effects of time were identified for the RBANS total score (F = 6.074, p = 0.016, q = 0.048) and the immediate memory index score (F = 6.389, p = 0.013, q = 0.048). Post-hoc paired t-tests confirmed significant improvements from baseline to the 12-month follow-up in both the RBANS total score (t = 7.258, p < 0.001) and the immediate memory index (t = 10.084, p < 0.001) across all groups, with these improvements remaining significant after FDR correction (q < 0.05). These findings indicate a significant overall improvement in these cognitive measures over time, irrespective of group assignment.

Intervention effect on regional gray matter volumes

As shown in Fig. 2, ANCOVA revealed nominally significant time-by-group interactions for gray matter volumes in four ROIs: left posterior orbital gyrus (POrG; F = 9.123, p < 0.001), left posterior insula (PIns; F = 3.382, p = 0.038), left subcallosal area (SCA; F = 3.347, p = 0.039), and left transverse temporal gyrus (TTG; F = 4.795, p = 0.010). After FDR correction, only the left POrG remained statistically significant (q = 0.024). Post-hoc paired t-tests further showed a nominal increase in left POrG volume in the aerobic exercise group before correction (t = 2.151, p = 0.038), which did not survive FDR correction (q = 0.570), indicating that the within-group change should be interpreted with caution. No significant changes were observed in the cognitive training group for any of the four regions (all p > 0.05). In the control group, significant decreases were found in the left POrG (t = −3.962, p < 0.001, q = 0.001), and left PIns (t = −3.654, p < 0.001, q = 0.003), while a nominal decrease in the left SCA (t = −2.184, p = 0.037) did not remain significant after FDR correction (q = 0.111) (Fig. 2). To further quantify these changes, relative annual volume change rates for the four ROIs were calculated (Table S1). The control group exhibited the greatest decline in gray matter volume, followed by the cognitive training group, while the aerobic exercise group showed the smallest decline.

Inter-regional covariance results

Inter-regional covariance analysis focused on the four ROIs (left POrG, left PIns, left SCA, and left TTG) exhibiting nominally significant time-by-group interactions. Between-group comparisons revealed a significant positive correlation between left SCA and left TTG in the control group (q = 0.046), whereas a non-significant negative correlation was observed in the aerobic exercise group (q = 0.791) (Fig. 3D). Associations between changes in cognitive measures and gray matter volumes

We investigated the relationships between changes in RBANS domain index scores and gray matter volumes in the four ROIs, with adjustments for age, sex, education, and baseline TIV in each of the three groups. As shown in Table S3, no significant correlations were observed between changes in cognitive measures and gray matter volume in any group after FDR correction (q > 0.05).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the effects of cognitive training and aerobic exercise on cognitive performance, regional gray matter volumes, and their inter-regional covariance in a sample of healthy older adults (aged 65–75 years). We observed nominally significant time-by-group interaction in the left POrG, PIns, SCA, and TTG, with only the left POrG remaining significant after FDR correction. This interaction revealed an increase in left POrG gray matter volume in the aerobic exercise group, while the control group showed decreases in left POrG, left PIns, and left SCA. Compared to the control group, the aerobic exercise group exhibited lower inter-regional covariance between volume changes in the left SCA and left TTG, suggesting attenuated coordinated structural variation between these regions in the aerobic exercise group. Together, these findings highlight the differential effects of cognitive training and aerobic exercise on brain gray matter volume and inter-regional covariance in healthy aging adults and provide insights into potential interventions for enhancing brain health in this population. However, although a hypothesis was defined for this study, the imaging analyses were broad and exploratory, which increases the risk of type I errors.

Aging is associated with cognitive decline and atrophy in multiple brain regions, with the earliest changes typically occurring in the prefrontal cortex, followed by the medial temporal lobe, parietal cortex, and cerebellum40. In this study, after 3 months of intervention, only the RBANS total score and immediate memory index score improved over time, though no significant group differences were observed. This may be due to the participants’ relatively high cognitive reserve, as the average education level was 12 years, leading to a ceiling effect. Alternatively, structural brain changes, such as gray matter volume, may precede measurable cognitive improvements, as suggested by previous research41. Importantly, we did not find evidence that changes in the four ROIs were directly associated with changes in the RBANS domain index scores, suggesting that any structural benefits observed may not translate into measurable cognitive gains over the course of one year in this high-functioning sample.

Our study found a significant time-by-group interaction effect on the left POrG, with increased volume in the aerobic exercise group, no change in the cognitive training group, and a decrease in the control group at the 12-month follow-up. The posterior orbital gyrus (POrG), a region within the orbital frontal cortex, is involved in several higher-order executive functions, including reward prediction, emotional regulation, and decision-making42. Previous studies have shown that aerobic exercise increases gray matter volume, particularly in regions such as the hippocampus22 and prefrontal cortex43. For instance, Erickson et al. (2011)22 observed a roughly 2% increase in anterior hippocampal volume after one year of aerobic training. However, many studies have focused on larger, functionally well-defined brain regions such as the hippocampus and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), while smaller regions, such as the POrG, remain less studied. Our research found a 0.62% annual increase in the left POrG volume following aerobic exercise, suggesting this region may also benefit from such intervention. The mechanisms by which exercise enhances brain volume include increased cerebral blood flow, elevated neurotrophic growth factors, and reduced neuroinflammation23. In contrast, the left POrG volume in the cognitive training group showed only a slight annual decline of −0.06%, which may reflect more subtle changes in white matter microstructure24. Meanwhile, our control group exhibited a 1.9% annual decline in left POrG volume, similar to the 1.72% decline reported for the bilateral orbital-frontal cortex in the 3 C-MRI cohort44, supporting the idea that control group trajectories reflect normative aging. However, due to differences in ROI definition and methodology, caution is advised when comparing these results. Although our study observed preliminary cognitive and brain volume changes, the 3-month intervention duration was insufficient to fully capture these effects. Structural changes typically require longer intervention periods to accumulate enough neuroplasticity24. Existing evidence suggests that exercise interventions need to last at least 16 weeks, and ideally 6 to 12 months or longer, to observe significant changes in brain structure24,45. Given the limited understanding of optimal exercise and cognitive training dosages, future studies should extend intervention durations to further explore their effects on brain structure. Overall, by employing ROI-based analyses, our study provides compelling evidence of structural changes in gray matter and suggests that both aerobic exercise and cognitive training may offer protective effects against age-related brain atrophy.

In the inter-regional covariance analysis, we found significantly stronger covariance between volume changes in the left POrG and left TTG only in the aerobic exercise group. However, this finding is exploratory and requires replication. The mechanisms underlying structural covariance are complex and may involve factors such as white matter connectivity or synchronized neuronal activity46,47. The transverse temporal gyrus (TTG), a key component of the auditory cortex, is involved in auditory processing and language comprehension48, while the orbital frontal cortex plays a role in various brain functions through its connections to other areas49. Previous animal studies have suggested that the orbital frontal cortex is linked to the auditory regions of the superior temporal gyrus50, which may be relevant for processing affective vocalizations51. Anatomically, the orbital frontal cortex is connected to the anterior temporal lobe via long-range white matter fibers, specifically the uncinate fasciculus (UF)52. Diffusion tensor imaging studies have shown that aging-related reductions in UF integrity are associated with cognitive decline53. The enhanced coupling between the left POrG and left TTG in the aerobic exercise group may be related to white matter pathways, though further investigation is needed. Overall, these findings suggest that aerobic exercise may help preserve gray matter volume and enhance network-level structural coordination associated with cognitive functions in older adults.

Additionally, we observed weaker covariance between the volume changes in the left SCA and left TTG in the aerobic exercise group compared to the control group. This result is also exploratory and requires replication. The subcallosal area (SCA), a small region in the medial frontal cortex located along the path of the UF54, is involved in emotion regulation and is linked to affective disorders55. The weaker covariance between the left SCA and left TTG in the aerobic exercise group may reflect attenuated coordinated structural variations between these regions. Given the lack of FDR-corrected significance, this interpretation should be viewed with cautious. Based on these findings, aerobic exercise and cognitive training may help delay cognitive decline by influencing structural coordination between brain regions, but further research is necessary. Our structural covariance results should be viewed primarily as exploratory, providing hypotheses about network-level changes rather than definitive evidence.

This study has several limitations: (1) The moderate sample size (n = 100) may have limited the statistical power to detect small effects, including those in sex-specific subgroup analyses. The sample was restricted to older adults aged 65 to 75 years, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to younger populations. Including a younger control group would help ascertain whether the effects of cognitive training and aerobic exercise are specific to older adults. Future research should seek to replicate these findings across a broader age range and in multiple centers. (2) Imaging data were not acquired immediately following the 12 weeks interventions, precluding assessment of short-term effects on gray matter volume and preventing us from distinguishing short-term structural changes from longer-term maintenance effects. In addition, we did not include biomarker indicators such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), neurochemicals, and cerebral blood flow, limiting a comprehensive understanding of the underlying mechanisms of these interventions. (3) some inter-regional covariance results did not remain significant after FDR correction. These findings are therefore reported as exploratory and should be interpreted with caution. Future studies with larger sample sizes and more comprehensive designs, including intermediate post-intervention imaging time points and multimodal biomarkers, are needed to further elucidate short- and long-term effects on cognition and neuroplasticity.

Conclusion

In summary, this study suggests that both 12-week cognitive training and aerobic exercise interventions are linked to a delay in the reduction of left POrG gray matter volume, although they vary in the degree and coordination patterns of these volume changes. Understanding the neural mechanisms underlying these interventions may contribute to the development of more effective strategies for preventing or delaying Alzheimer’s disease.
