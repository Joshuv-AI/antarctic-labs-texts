Background

Plant diseases cause an estimated annual loss of 10–15% of global crop production, resulting in significant economic losses of up to hundreds of billions of dollars [1]. Pathogenic fungi cause around 70–80% of plant diseases [2]. Fusarium oxysporum (FO) is one of the most devastating soilborne fungal pathogens, producing serious diseases in nearly all commercially important vegetable, fruit, and forest plants, resulting in substantial global losses [3]. F. oxysporum produces several mycotoxins and secondary metabolites harmful to plants and animals [4–9]. It can persist in soil for a long time through the decay of plant litter and is transmitted via soil, animals, and farm equipment. Once established, its eradication from the soil is challenging [10, 11]. The control of FO diseases has always been a research hotspot in plant protection. FO has several pathogenic strains that are difficult to control by conventional strategies [11]. FO produces crescent-shaped multicellular conidia that play a key role in its spread to healthy plants [12]. Hence, inhibiting conidiation can significantly restrict the spread of FO and control its diseases.

Steroids are secondary metabolites widely present in plants, animals, and microorganisms, and are closely related to growth, development, reproduction, and other life processes. Steroids are considered good antifungal agents [13]. Trichoderma viridae produces viridins, a series of steroid-like antibiotics that inhibit spore germination of Botrytis allii, Colletotrichum lini, and Fusarium caeruleum [14]. Steroids inhibited germination of conidia in Penicillium paneum [15]. Pregnan-type steroids such as caudatin and qingyangshengenin showed good antifungal activity [16].

The steroid pregnenolone (Preg) is known to restrict the growth of fungi by inhibiting conidiation in Trichoderma citrinoviride and Fusarium graminearum [17]. However, Preg is produced in living organisms, including fungi, as an essential endogenous steroid that works as a precursor to several hormones, including estrogen, progesterone, testosterone, and glucocorticoids [18]. Research has shown that pregnenolone and its metabolic byproducts have positive effects on the brain, improving learning and memory, alleviating depressive disorders, and modulating brain function. Neuroinflammatory illnesses have been found to have lower levels of pregnenolone, highlighting its role in neuroprotection and neuroregeneration [19]. Recent studies have shown that pregnenolone derivatives have shown potential efficacy in inhibiting certain cancer cells in animals, but the mechanism of action remains unclear [20]. Pregnenolone inhibits conidiation in T. citrinoviride in liquid culture [17], indicating that Preg regulates conidiation in fungi. However, the mechanism underlying Preg-mediated control of conidiation in fungi remains unclear. Here, we investigated the effects of Preg on conidiation in F. oxysporum and identified key Preg-responsive genes through high-throughput transcriptomic analysis. Furthermore, by generating knockout strains, we established the roles of key genes involved in Preg-mediated regulation of FO conidiation via functional genomic analysis. This study aims to elucidate the mechanistic control of pregnenolone-mediated regulation of conidiation in phytopathogenic fungi.

Methods

Fungal strains and culture conditions

F. oxysporum (strain LZ51) was used in the study. The FO LZ51 was isolated from infected tomato plants and identified through ITS sequencing (Internal transcribed spacer) (Supplementary File S1). The fungi were grown in pure cultures on potato dextrose agar (PDA) at 28 °C.

Determination of the number of conidia

Fungi were grown on PDA plates at 28 °C and subsequently in 250 mL conical flasks containing 100 mL PD broth supplemented with final concentrations of 10 mg/L, 50 mg/L, and 100 mg/L pregnenolone at 28 °C for 5 days. Pregnenolone was dissolved in 0.1% DMSO as the solubility in H2O was low. The control flasks did not contain any Preg. The experiment was set up with five replications. Next, the cultures were filtered through 4 layers of muslin cloth. The filtrates were centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatants were discarded. The fungal spore pellets were washed with autoclaved distilled water, briefly centrifuged, and then resuspended in 5 mL of autoclaved distilled water. The conidia were counted under a compound microscope using a hemocytometer. Each replication of each treatment was counted five times.

Transcriptomic analysis

After determining the inhibitory impact of Preg on conidiation via spore counts, 10 mg/L was found to be the optimal Preg concentration, with a significant effect on sporulation. Therefore, this concentration was used for transcriptomic analysis. For this purpose, F. oxysporum broth cultures were supplemented with 10 mg/L Preg, as described above, in triplicate. Control flasks did not contain Preg. Cultures were grown at 28 °C for 72 h, then centrifuged at 6000 rpm and 4 °C for 10 min in 50 mL RNase-free, sterile centrifuge tubes. The supernatant was re-suspended in 2 mL of RNA-BE-Locker A solution on ice for 20 min. Total RNA was extracted by using Trizol. Shenzhen BGI Co., Ltd. performed RNA sequencing using the BGI Multiomics System (https://biosys.bgi.com). The raw RNA-seq data generated and analyzed during this study have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under the BioProject accession number PRJNA1428133.

Bioinformatics workflow

The low-quality (More than 20% of the bases are lower than 10) reads were filtered, and reads with adaptors and reads with unknown bases (N bases more than 5%) were also removed to obtain the clean reads. The clean reads were then mapped onto the reference genome (JGI_Fusox2) using Bowtie2 and the gene expression levels were calculated using RSEM, followed by novel gene prediction (using StringTie), SNP &INDEL calling and gene-splicing detection (using rMATS with a false discovery rate FDR < = 0.05). Finally, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified between samples using DESeq2 with a P-value < = 0.05, followed by cluster analysis and functional annotation. The clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2. Pearson correlation between all samples was calculated using cor, hierarchical clustering was performed using hclust, PCA was performed on all samples using princomp, and the diagrams were drawn using the ggplot2 function in R. GO annotation and KEGG pathway analysis were also performed.

Fusarium oxysporum genomic DNA extraction

Five discs (5 mm in diameter) were punched out of 5-day-old FO cultures and inoculated into conical flasks containing 100 mL PDB at 28 °C. The culture was filtered through four layers of muslin cloth after three days. The mycelium was rinsed with sterile distilled water 3–4 times and crushed in liquid nitrogen. Genomic DNA was extracted using CTAB. The crushed mycelium was resuspended in 5 mL of CTAB (preheated to 65 °C) in a water bath at 65 °C for 2 h. The solution was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 15 min, and the supernatant was transferred to fresh tubes containing 50 µL of 2 mol/L DTT, which were then mixed well. An equal volume of phenol: chloroform: isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1, v/v/v) was added, gently mixed, and allowed to stand for 5 min. The tubes were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 15 min, and the supernatant was transferred to a new centrifuge tube. Equal volumes of chloroform and isoamyl alcohol (24:1, v/v) were added, mixed by inversion, allowed to stand for 5 min, and then centrifuged at 4 °C and 4000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was transferred to a new centrifuge tube. Isoamyl alcohol was added, mixed by inversion, and allowed to stand at 20 °C for 1 h. The mixture was then centrifuged at 4 °C and 12,000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was discarded. DNA pellets were washed three times with 70% ethanol and then resuspended in 50 µL of ddH2O. The presence of genomic DNA was confirmed via agarose gel electrophoresis.

Construction of gene knockout constructs

The genomic DNA sequence (GCA_000149955.1 ASM14995v2) of Fusarium oxysporum strain LZ51 were downloaded from the NCBI database. The genomic DNA sequence of Fusarium oxysporum was analyzed using SnapGene software, and the location and sequence of the knockout gene were determined based on gene annotation. F. oxysporum genes were knocked out via homologous recombination. A specific region of approximately 800 bp upstream and downstream of the target genes was selected as the upstream and downstream homology arms (Figure S7). The upstream and downstream homology arms were amplified from genomic DNA using the gene-UP-F/-UP-R and DW-F/DW-R primer pairs (Table S3). These primer pairs contained a 20-bp sequence complementary to the hygromycin resistance gene. Primers HYG-F and HYG-R were designed to amplify the hygromycin resistance gene from the pFC332 plasmid. All DNA fragments were combined via Fusion PCR, run on an agarose gel, purified, and the sequences were confirmed.

Preparation of F. oxysporum protoplasts

Five-day-old F. oxysporum broth cultures were grown as described above. Fungal cultures were filtered through four layers of sterile gauze, and the resultant suspension was centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min. The pelleted conidia were resuspended in sterile water to achieve a concentration of approximately 6 × 107 cells/mL. Then, in a 250 mL conical flask containing 100 mL of PDB medium, 1 mL of the spore suspension was inoculated and incubated for 36 h at 28 °C at 150 rpm. The young mycelium was collected by filtering it through a single layer of sterile Miracloth and washed three to four times with 1.2 M KCl. Next, 5 mL of enzymatic hydrolysate and 5 mL of sterile 1.2 M KCl were added to 0.2 g of mycelium, and the mixture was gently mixed at 30 °C and 90 rpm for 3–4 h. A single-layer magic filter cloth (Calbiochem, DEU, China) was used to filter and collect the enzymatic hydrolysate. The hydrolysate was centrifuged at 4 °C and 1000 g for 5 min, and 10 mL of STC Buffer was added to resuspend the protoplasts at a concentration of approximately 4 × 107 cells/mL.

Protoplast transformation

Fusarium oxysporum protoplasts (800µL) were carefully mixed with 200 µL of PTC buffer (composition) in a 15 mL tube. Next, 5–10 µg of target gene knockout DNA constructs were added and allowed to stand on ice for 30 min and then at room temperature for 20 min. Next, 5 mL of TB3 liquid medium (3 g Yeast extract, 3 g acid-hydrolyzed casein, 200 g sucrose) containing 50 mg/L ampicillin was added, and the mixture was incubated at 30 °C at 90 rpm for 12–16 h. The cultured protoplasts were then centrifuged at 1000 g for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The protoplasts were mixed with TB3 medium containing 50 mg/L hygromycin and spread on PDA medium at 28 ℃ for 3–5 days.

Screening and validation of transformants

Successfully transformed FO protoplasts were visible as small colonies on PDA plates. At least 6 transformants were selected for every gene knockout. These transformants were transferred to PDA plates containing a high concentration of hygromycin (100 mg/L) for rescreening. The transformants that grew normally were selected for genotyping. For this purpose, the mycelium was collected by scraping the surface of the colony, and the hyphae were cleaved with Lysis solution (0.2 M NaOH, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100. TFB1: rubidium chloride 100 mM, manganese dichloride 50 mM, potassium acetate 30 mM, calcium chloride 10 mM, 15% glycerol, pH adjusted to 5.8 with acetic acid 0.2%). The genomic DNA was extracted, and the corresponding primers for each gene (Table S3) were used for PCR amplification. PCR products were detected by agarose gel electrophoresis to confirm the expected band size.

Determination of ergosterol biosynthesis

Fungus was cultured on a PDA plate at 28 °C for 5 days. A 5 mm mycelial disc was then transferred to 100 mL PDB (with/without pregnenolone) and incubated for another 5 days. The harvested mycelia were washed, dried, and ground into a powder. The ground mycelia (0.1 g) were added to 10mL of alcoholic potassium hydroxide solution and incubated at 90 °C for 4 h. After cooling, sterols were extracted by adding 5 mL of ether and 5 mL of deionized water, followed by centrifugation. The upper organic phase was collected. Ergosterol was quantified using HPLC with a C18 column, a methanol mobile phase, and UV detection at 281 nm, column temperature of 30 ℃, and detection for 20 min. The ergosterol content was calculated by comparing the sample peak to a standard curve prepared under the same conditions.

Molecular docking

CB-Dock2 was used to detect the binding interaction of proteins and pregnenolone (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/index.php). The interaction was depicted in a 3D structure using PyMOL [21]. A widely used technique called homology modeling uses homologous proteins with known structures to estimate a protein’s three-dimensional structure. The general web tool SWISS-MODEL (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/) was utilized to predict the target protein models. The structure of the ligand (pregnenolone) was made by ChemDraw (http://www.perkinelmer.co.uk/category/chemdraw) (RRID: SCR_016768). TMHMM v2.0 ( https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/service.php? DeepTMHMM-1.0) was used for the prediction of transmembrane (TM) helices in proteins using a hidden Markov model (HMM).

Isothermal Titration Calorimetry (ITC) and Microscale Thermophoresis (MST)

Protein expression

ERG2 and EBP genes were cloned into pET-28a (+) protein expression vector with N-terminal 6xHis-tag and transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) competent cells by Synbio Technologies using the heat shock method. The expressed proteins were sent to Bio-lab (Wuhan, China). ERG2 and EBP GFP-tagged proteins were synthesized in E. coli BL21 (DE3) by Synbio Technologies (Suzhou, China). The expressed proteins were sent to Bio-lab (Wuhan, China) for Microscale Thermophoresis (MST) analysis. This allowed for the investigation of protein interactions with pregnenolone.

Protein expression was induced by using 0.1 mM IPTG at 22 °C for 4 h on a shaker at 220 rpm. Target proteins were visualized on the SDS-PAGE. Protein samples were treated with denaturing buffer (8 M urea), and the denatured protein was purified using nickel column affinity chromatography and eluted with different concentrations of imidazole. Protein renaturation was performed via dialysis using a renaturation solution with gradually decreasing urea concentrations from 2 M urea, 1 M urea, 0.5 M urea, 0.2 M urea, and finally against buffer. The renaturation buffer gradually replaces the denaturant solution. The re-natured protein samples were centrifuged and then run on SDS-PAGE.

Results

Pregnenolone inhibits sporulation of Fusarium oxysporum

Previous studies have found that the steroid pregnenolone can inhibit the conidia of Trichoderma citrinoviride and Fusarium graminearum in liquid culture [17]. Although the inhibition effect was very attractive, its inhibition mechanism remains unclear. In the current study, we used pregnenolone to investigate its inhibitory effect on F. oxysporum and to analyze the regulatory pathway and target of its inhibition of F. oxysporum conidiation. Our results demonstrated that Preg is an effective sporulation inhibitor of F. oxysporum. Specifically, treatment with 10 mg/L Preg reduced conidiation by more than 35% compared to the untreated control.

The solubility of Preg in water is only about 200 ng/L at 20 °C [17]. Applying pregnenolone dissolved in water resulted in uneven distribution in the medium, which eventually caused F. oxysporum colony growth in patches (Fig. S4). Therefore, the effects on sporulation may not be reliable, as shown in Fig. 1(a). Preg is hydrophobic and therefore cannot readily dissolve in water. Similarly, when Preg was added directly to the media at 10 mg/L, 50 mg/L, and 100 mg/L, it significantly reduced spore counts, as shown in Fig. 1(b). Data showed a 35%, 54%, and 75% reduction in sporulation at 10 mg/L, 50 mg/L, and 100 mg/L Preg, respectively (Fig. 1(b)). However, the effects were not limited to sporulation alone, as overall colony growth was also significantly reduced (Fig. 1(c)). The mycelium at the edges of the F. oxysporum colony in the control plates was dense, compact, and uniform. On the other hand, the mycelium of the F. oxysporum colony in plates supplemented with 50 mg/L pregnenolone was loose and scattered. Whereas the mycelium in plates supplemented with 100 mg/L pregnenolone was even more loose and scattered (Fig. 1(c)).

When the Preg was dissolved in DMSO, it significantly enhanced the inhibitory effect of Preg on FO sporulation. DMSO, as a co-solvent, improved the solubility of pregnenolone in the medium, further enhancing its inhibitory effects on sporulation (Fig. 1(d) and Fig. S2) and the overall growth of the colony (Fig. S3). Data showed an average 71% reduction in colony diameter by 50 mg/L Preg treatment (Fig. S3). The inhibition of FO sporulation by the different concentrations of Preg dissolved in DMSO was determined. Data indicated a 40%, 60%, and 80% reduction in sporulation by the 10 mg/L, 50 mg/L, and 100 mg/L Preg treatment (Fig. 1(e)).

Fusarium oxysporum produces two morphologically distinct types of asexual spores, differentiated by their size and cell number, namely microconidia (small, single-celled) and macroconidia (crescent-shaped, larger than microconidia and multicelled). Results indicated significant Preg-mediated inhibition of both micro and macroconidia, with inhibition rates of 72% and 55%, respectively, at 100 mg/L Preg (Fig. 1(f)). Interestingly, our results also showed that Preg significantly affected the growth of FO mycelium in a solid medium. In contrast, its effect on mycelium biomass in a submerged broth culture was not statistically different, as shown in Fig. 1(g). Together, these results underscore a broad-spectrum inhibitory potential of Preg against FO sporulation, making it a promising candidate for further development as a fungicide for preventing disease dissemination in agricultural settings.

RNA Seq analysis

Transcriptomic analysis was performed on 8 samples. These included 4 samples each of control (Control 1–4) and four samples of F. oxysporum treated with pregnenolone 10 mg/L (Preg 1–4). Following the analysis of high-quality reads, the data indicated that sequencing generated approximately 6.17 Gb of bases per sample. Furthermore, a total of 18,506 genes were identified, with 18,285 known genes and 227 novel genes. In addition, 2,075 novel transcripts were identified, comprising 1,247 previously unknown splicing events for known genes, 227 novel coding transcripts without any known features, and the remaining 601 were long noncoding RNAs, as shown in Table S1. To reflect the gene expression correlations between samples, we calculated Pearson correlation coefficients for all gene expression levels and presented them as a heatmap (Fig. 2(a)). The correlation coefficient in the pregnenolone group is higher than 89.7%, and for the control, it is higher than 85%, which indicates that these data can be used for subsequent analysis. From a total of 18,506 genes, 16,669 were expressed in both sample groups. However, 822 DEGs were unique only to the control samples, whereas 1015 were unique to Preg-treated FO samples (Fig. S5). Most upregulated and downregulated genes are shown in the supplementary file Table S2.

DEGs were identified using the DESeq2 algorithm by comparing gene expression levels between samples or groups. Comparing the control and pregnenolone-treated groups. A total of 3,993 differentially expressed genes were identified, among which more than 2,058 were upregulated, while approximately 1,935 were downregulated (Fig. 2(b)). KEGG enrichment analysis was performed on differentially expressed genes identified from the transcriptome. DEGs were mainly enriched in five metabolic pathways: Steroid biosynthesis, ABC Transporters, Lysine degradation, C5-branched dibasic acid metabolism, Penicillin, and cephalosporin biosynthesis (Fig. 2(c)). Five DEGs enriched in the steroid biosynthesis pathway (Fig. S6, Red box) were selected for further study, as pregnenolone may affect the steroid biosynthesis pathway of F. oxysporum during its regulation of spore production.

Additionally, several antifungal agents and fungicides are known to interfere with the ergosterol biosynthesis pathway [22].Pregnenolone is a steroid, and we hypothesized that it might exert its effect by interfering with the fungal steroid (ergosterol) biosynthesis pathway.

Identification of steroid pathway genes

DEG enrichment analysis revealed five upregulated genes in the steroid biosynthesis pathway (Fig. S6, Red box). Interestingly, all five genes appear to be membrane-localized. The genes include FDFT1 (encoding the enzyme Farnesyl-Diphosphate Farnesyltransferase 1), WOR2 (white-opaque regulator 2), EBP (Cholestenol Delta-isomerase), ERG2 (C-8sterol isomerase), and ERG4 (Table 1).

Steroid biosynthesis pathway regulates sporulation in F. oxysporum

In contrast to WT F. oxysporum, the knockout mutants ΔERG4, ΔERG2, ΔFDFT1, ΔEBP, and ΔWOR2 exhibited a substantial decrease in mycelial growth as shown in Fig. 3a. This finding highlights the importance of these genes in the growth and development of Fusarium oxysporum.

Furthermore, the sporulation of ΔERG4, ΔERG2, ΔFDFT1, ΔEBP, and ΔWOR2 mutant strains was found to be pregnenolone-insensitive compared to the WT strain, where sporulation was reduced by 80% (Fig. 3(b)). The results showed that pregnenolone lost its regulatory effect on conidiation following ΔERG4, ΔERG2, ΔFDFT1, ΔEBP, and ΔWOR2 knockouts, indicating that the deletion of these genes led to the loss of pregnenolone targets, and it can be speculated that these gene products may be pregnenolone targets. Since light and dark periods are thought to significantly affect sporulation in fungi, especially under artificial culture conditions [23]. However, WT F. oxysporum exhibited only a slight difference in conidiation between light (12 h L/12 h D) and dark conditions (continuous darkness for 5 days). The ΔERG2 and ΔWOR2 mutants exhibited effective sporulation under normal light but had considerably lower conidia count in constant darkness (Fig. 3(c)). This suggests that these genes regulate light-dependent sporulation. In contrast, the ΔERG4 and ΔEBP mutant strains exhibited light-insensitive sporulation, with no significant difference in spore counts under either light condition. In contrast, ΔFDFT1 showed a slight difference in conidiation under both conditions (Fig. 3(c)). Measurements using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) revealed a notable decrease in ergosterol concentration in the mutant FO strains compared to the wild-type strains. The results indicated that ergosterol content was markedly lower in the mutants ΔERG4, ΔERG2, ΔFDFT1, and ΔWOR2 (Fig. 3(d)). Additionally, since EBP is not directly involved in ergosterol biosynthesis, its deletion did not significantly impact ergosterol levels. These results imply that ERG4, ERG2, FDFT1, and WOR2 are essential for ergosterol biosynthesis.

Both mutant and wild-type strains were treated with ergosterol to assess their capacity to utilize external sterols. High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis showed no notable differences in ergosterol levels between the ergosterol-treated and control groups (Fig. 3(e)). Furthermore, the addition of exogenous ergosterol did not affect conidiation in either strain. These results suggest that Fusarium oxysporum may be unable to take up external sterols.

Prediction of the interaction of ERG2 and EBP proteins with pregnenolone

Molecular docking is a computational technique that predicts how ligands, or tiny molecules, will interact with a target protein’s binding site [24–26]. Molecular docking studies revealed that ERG2 and EBP proteins have high predicted binding affinity with pregnenolone as compared to other genes. We obtained affinity scores and docked sizes from docking analysis, showing a noticeably greater binding affinity (represented by low scores) between pregnenolone and the ERG2 and EBP proteins. Cavity 2 was found to have the highest binding affinity for ERG2, as indicated by the lowest score of -7.6 kcal/mol. On the other hand, cavity 1 exhibited the highest binding affinity for EBP, with a docking score of -8.5 kcal/mol. The highest binding affinities were − 7.6 kcal/mol (cavity 2) and − 8.5 kcal/mol (cavity 1) for ERG2 and EBP, respectively, as shown in Table 2. Figure 4(a, b) provides a cartoon presentation of the proteins and pregnenolone. Based on these results, we selected ERG2 and EBP proteins for further analysis.

ERG2 and EBP proteins interact with pregnenolone

ERG2 and EBP proteins were expressed in E. coli BL21 (Fig. S11). As transmembrane proteins, most of the expressed proteins were insoluble (Fig. S11(a, b)), and pure proteins could not be isolated either by direct purification from soluble fractions or by denaturing the inclusion bodies and then renaturing them (Fig. S11(c-f)). Therefore, the interaction of Preg with ERG2 and EBP was determined using Microscale Thermophoresis (MST). MST does not necessarily require purified proteins; instead, it relies on a fluorescent tag, such as GFP, to detect binding events in unpurified samples. For this purpose, GFP-tagged ERG2 and EBP proteins, representing 94 kDa and 55 kDa, were expressed in E. coli (Fig. S11(g)). In MST analysis, the fraction of a target molecule that is currently bound to a ligand at a specific concentration is represented by ‘fraction bound,’ which is inversely related to the binding affinity (KD) [27]. KD values below 50 µM indicate binding between a ligand (Preg) and the target molecules ERG2-GFP and EBP-GFP. At a fixed concentration of ERG2-GFP and EBP-GFP, and pregnenolone concentrations varying between 0.000763µM to 25 µM, it was observed that at a ligand concentration of 1E− 4, the bound fraction values are 1.0, 0.6, and 0.0 for ERG2-GFP, EBP-GFP, and GFP-tag, respectively; however, at a ligand concentration of 1E− 9, the fraction bound is 0.00 for each of ERG2-GFP, EBP-GFP, and GFP-tag, as shown in Fig. 4(c).

The higher the binding affinity (i.e., the lower the KD), the more significant the fraction bound at a given ligand concentration, meaning a higher proportion of the target molecule will be complexed with the ligand when the affinity is more potent [28]. Hence, the order of binding affinity with increasing fraction bound is EBP-GFP > ERG2-GFP > GFP-tag at a given ligand (Preg) concentration. The results showed that the KD value of EBP-GFP is 0.15 µM, indicating that EBP has a high binding affinity for pregnenolone. In contrast, ERG2-GFP has a medium binding affinity, having a KD value of 16.3 µM (Table S4). These results demonstrated that pregnenolone can bind to ERG2 and EBP, with EBP showing a higher binding affinity. These findings validate the roles of ERG2 and EBP as molecular targets of pregnenolone, suggesting their involvement in inhibiting fungal conidiation.

Discussion

Fungi are among the dominant causal agents of plant diseases, resulting in an annual estimated loss of 10–15% of the world’s major crops, amounting to billions of dollars [1]. Of these diseases, 70–80% are caused by pathogenic fungi. Fungal spores are crucial for infection, fungal dissemination, and propagation [29]. Inhibiting spore production in pathogenic fungi is crucial for disease prevention and control, ultimately enhancing crop yield and quality. Fungal diseases are the most critical limiting factor in food production and are difficult to control [11]. Farmers continue to use synthetic fungicides to control fungal diseases. There are several other reasons to completely stop or minimize the use of synthetic chemicals, aside from their negative environmental impact. An efficient conidiation inhibitor can effectively control the spread of fungal diseases [30].

Steroids are known to influence the development of fungi. Trichoderma viride produces viridins, a series of steroid-like antibiotics that inhibit spore germination of Botrytis allii, Colletotrichum lini, and Fusarium caeruleum at concentrations of 3–6 ng/mL [14]. Steroidal alkaloids, also known as glycoalkaloids, are secondary metabolites found in numerous solanaceous plants that can be used against insects, viruses, bacteria, and fungi. Two major steroidal alkaloids of the potato, α-chaconine and α-solanine, have been used as an antifungal agent against pathogenic fungi [31]. Steroids inhibit the growth of pathogenic fungi Botrytis cinerea [32].

Moreover, Pregnan-type steroids such as caudatin (1) and qingyangshengenin show good antifungal activity against the phytopathogenic fungus Sclerotinia sclerotiorum by inhibiting its growth [33]. Brassinosteroids play an essential role in defense responses to pathogens by activating various defense-related enzymes and ROS-scavenging enzymes. It reduced the severity of Fusarium head blight caused by Fusarium culmorum [34–37]. Research has shown that pregnenolone and its metabolic byproducts have positive effects on the brain, improving learning and memory, alleviating depressive disorders, and modulating brain function. Neuroinflammatory illnesses have been found to have lower levels of pregnenolone, highlighting its role in neuroprotection and neuroregeneration [19]. Recent studies have shown that pregnenolone derivatives have shown potential efficacy in inhibiting certain cancer cells in animals, but the mechanism of action remains unclear [20]. Pregnenolone inhibited the conidiation in Trichoderma citrinoviride. However, the conditions, modes of action of pregnenolone, and the mechanism of its inhibition were not clear [17]. The present study recommended pregnenolone as an antifungal agent against F. oxysporum. Ergosterol (31) and 3,5,9-trihydroxyergosta-7,22-dien-6-one (32), isolated from Trichoderma sp. YM 311,505 exhibited substantial antifungal activities against Pyricularia oryzae, Candida albicans, Aspergillus niger, and Alternaria alternata [38].

Regarding Fusarium oxysporum, potassium phosphonate has been shown to inhibit microconidial production [39]. However, in our case, pregnenolone inhibited both the micro and macroconidia of F. oxysporum. Similarly, by analyzing the effect of pregnenolone on the growth of F. oxysporum, it was found that pregnenolone had no effect on mycelial biomass in submerged culture, but it significantly affected mycelium growth on solid media. Previous studies have reported that chitosan can be used as an antifungal agent, as it inhibits the sporulation and growth of F. oxysporum f.sp. cubense race 4 (FocR4) and F. solani [40, 41]. Similarly, saponin and nano-chitin whiskers significantly inhibited the conidiation and mycelial growth of Fusarium species [42, 43].

The ergosterol biosynthetic pathway plays a crucial role in sexual reproduction, particularly during sporulation, and is the primary target of antifungal drugs [44]. The present study showed that pregnenolone affects the steroid biosynthesis pathway of F. oxysporum during its regulation of spore production. Our results are in agreement with those of other researchers who have described azole targeting of ergosterol biosynthesis genes in fungi. Azoles mainly target ERG2, ERG5, ERG6, and other key genes of the ergosterol pathway [45, 46]. A previous study also observed that fungi upregulate genes in the ergosterol biosynthesis pathway when exposed to antifungal agents [47]. In the present study, based on transcriptome data analysis, 5 up-regulated genes in the steroid biosynthesis pathway were selected as candidate genes for the pregnenolone target protein.

The present study also observed that the deletion of ERG4, ERG2, FDFT1, EBP, and WOR2 genes in the ergosterol biosynthesis pathway led to a significant decrease in the conidia yield of the knockout lines as compared to the wild type, indicating that these genes were functional genes affecting the production of conidia in Fusarium oxysporum. Our results are consistent with those of other researchers, who found that deletion of ERG4A, ERG4B, or ERG4C inhibited sporulation in Penicillium expansum [48]. Our results also investigated the mycelial growth of the knockout strains and found that deletion of the ERG4, ERG2, FDFT1, EBP, and WOR2 genes not only reduced conidiation but also significantly reduced mycelial growth. Earlier reports support the finding where the deletion of ERG4 in Fusarium graminearum produced abnormal conidia and also reduced its conidiation and mycelial growth [48].

The present study also suggested that Fusarium oxysporum cannot take up exogenous sterols, as the addition of ergosterol did not affect conidiation in mutant and wild-type strains. These findings align with previous studies that reported Fusarium graminearum cannot absorb exogenous ergosterol [49, 50]. The current study also observed a significant reduction in ergosterol concentration in mutant strains compared to the wild-type strains. These findings strongly suggested that these genes play critical roles in ergosterol biosynthesis. Similar findings were reported by previous researchers who demonstrated that ergosterol biosynthesis was blocked in the ERG4 deletion mutant [48]. We also demonstrated that pregnenolone lost its regulatory function with respect to the number of conidia produced by these knockout strains, indicating that the deletion of these genes led to the loss of pregnenolone targets. These results are consistent with a previous report showing that antifungal agents target ergosterol biosynthesis genes in fungi. Specifically, azoles target the sterol 14-demethylase genes ERG2, ERG5, ERG6, and ERG24, which are involved in the ergosterol biosynthesis pathway [51].

The molecular docking study revealed that both ERG2 and EBP proteins exhibit the highest binding affinity to pregnenolone, -7.6 and − 8.5 kcal/mol, respectively. These findings are consistent with previous research indicating that Cobicistat exhibits a strong binding affinity of -8.6 kcal/mol at multiple binding sites on MAPK14 [52]. Similarly, 3-phenylphenol exhibited a strong binding affinity (-6.7 kcal/mol) to the pathogenic Sge1 protein of Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cubense [53]. MST analysis also demonstrated that pregnenolone binds to both ERG2 and EBP, with EBP showing a higher binding affinity. These findings validate the roles of ERG2 and EBP as molecular targets of pregnenolone, suggesting their involvement in the inhibition of conidiation in fungi. Our results are in agreement with previous work, which reported that EBP is a high-affinity drug-binding protein [54]. The identification of ERG2 and EBP as molecular targets of pregnenolone offers a promising foundation for the development of novel antifungal agents.

Conclusion

This study observed that pregnenolone is an effective conidiation inhibitor and can be utilized as an environmentally friendly chemical to control the development of F. oxysporum. Furthermore, RNA-seq analysis identified (5) upregulated genes in the steroid biosynthesis pathway, suggesting that pregnenolone regulates conidia production through this pathway. The study also observed that the deletion of genes in the steroid biosynthesis pathway led to a significant decrease in the conidia yield of the knockout lines. Moreover, the findings also demonstrated that pregnenolone lost its regulatory function on the number of conidia produced by ERG4, ERG2, FDFT1, EBP, and WOR2 knockout strains, indicating that the deletion of these genes led to the loss of pregnenolone targets. The investigations of this work also demonstrated that pregnenolone exhibits specific binding to ERG2 and EBP. These findings validate ERG2 and EBP as molecular targets of pregnenolone, suggesting their roles in inhibiting fungal conidiation.
