A Patient Bill of Rights and Responsibilities for Wound Prevention and Care in Canada: Grounded in Stories and Guided by Co‐Design and Community‐Based Participatory Principles
Authors: Idevania G. Costa, Catherine Philips, Kathleen Stevens, Catherine Schoales, Irmajean Bajnok, Mariam Botros, Robert Strachan, Samantha Santorelli, Pilar Camargo‐Plazas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis project co‐created a Patient Bill of Rights and Responsibilities in Wound Prevention and Care grounded in the lived experiences of patients and care partners. Guided by co‐design and community‐based participatory research principles, digital stories from the ‘Our Voices, Our Stories’ initiative, publicly hosted on the Wounds Canada website, were analysed using thematic and interpretive narrative approaches informed by social constructivism. Initial analysis identified nine themes, later expanded through iterative team‐based analysis to capture recurring challenges and systemic barriers in wound care. These informed 36 preliminary statements (18 rights, 18 responsibilities), which were refined into a draft framework of 16 rights and 16 responsibilities. The co‐creation process engaged diverse participants including individuals with lived experience, care partners, clinicians, and policymakers through three virtual workshops (May–July 2025). Through facilitated discussions and collaboration, the framework was refined to a final set of 12 rights and 12 responsibilities (n = 24), with over 88% agreement among participants. This framework offers a plain‐language, equity‐oriented approach that positions patients, care partners, and providers as partners in care. This framework serves as an educational and advocacy tool to strengthen person‐centred wound care, enhance communication and continuity, and inform practice, education, and policy development.

Introduction

Chronic wounds represent a significant health challenge, with profound personal and economic consequences. These hard‐to‐heal wounds, including diabetic foot ulcers, pressure injuries, and venous leg ulcers, can lead to serious complications such as infection and amputation and even death. As a result, hard‐to‐heal wounds contribute to increased healthcare utilisation and rising healthcare expenditures. Globally, wound care expenditures reached USD148.65 billion in 2022, underscoring the massive economic burden [1, 2]. In Canada, the cost of wound care is estimated to be more than $13 billion [3]. The psychosocial toll is substantial, including social isolation, stigma, economic hardship, and psychological distress including depression and anxiety. Families and caregivers are also impacted and often experience decreased quality of life, frustration, guilt, and loss of ability to work [4]. Despite advances in wound care, patients in Canada continue to face barriers, including long waits for specialist appointments, high out‐of‐pocket costs for supplies, pain, mobility issues, and gaps in continuity of care. These challenges are compounded by fragmented services across acute, rehabilitation, community, and long‐term care sectors [5, 6, 7].

Against this backdrop, a Patient Bill of Rights and Responsibilities (PBoRR) offers a tool to guide equitable care, advocacy, and system improvement [8]. The World Health Organisation (WHO)'s Patient Safety Rights, Charter positions patient rights as foundational to dignity, equity, and quality of care, and includes the right to timely, appropriate, and effective services delivered by competent providers; access to understandable information and education; and supported decision‐making [9]. The Parliament of Canada similarly describes a bill of rights as a formal declaration of core principles and entitlements in healthcare [10]. Patient rights frameworks in healthcare tend to be broad by design, [9] and this generality often comes at a cost [11]. For example, they rarely account for the particular challenges of chronic wound prevention and management, including how patients and care partners (i.e., care partner in this project refers to family members and linked support person) navigate fragmented systems [12]. Most frameworks also stop at rights, with little acknowledgment that care is a relational and reciprocal process involving shared responsibilities [11, 13]. There is growing recognition that co‐designed, patient‐informed approaches produce tools that are more relevant, equitable, and actionable in practice [14, 15, 16]. Unfortunately, wound care has been notably slow to follow; contextually grounded resources that translate the lived experience of people managing chronic wounds into practical guidance for care, education, and policy remain scarce. This study responds to that gap through the co‐creation of a Patient Bill of Rights and Responsibilities in Wound Prevention and Care. Building on these principles, we define this bill of rights and responsibilities for people living with, or at risk of, chronic wounds as a set of plain‐language commitments reflecting patient and care partner rights and complementary responsibilities. The bill of rights and responsibilities specifies: (a) what patients can reasonably expect from healthcare services, including safe, timely, effective, respectful, culturally safe, trauma‐ and violence‐informed, evidence‐based care; clear communication and transparency; prevention of deterioration through early referral; continuity and coordination of care across settings; and (b) responsibilities such as sharing relevant information, monitoring and reporting changes, participating in care plans, and seeking help promptly.

Despite increasing recognition of patient rights in healthcare, individuals living with, or at risk of, chronic wounds frequently encounter fragmented services, inconsistent access to specialised care, and limited involvement in decisions that affect their treatment and daily lives [1, 17]. These challenges are particularly evident, where inequities in access to wound prevention, assessment, and treatment persist [18]. Qualitative research has highlighted that people living with chronic wounds often experience frustration, isolation, and difficulties coordinating care across multiple providers and settings [19, 20, 21, 22]. There remains a critical need for a wound prevention and care–specific framework that articulates the rights and responsibilities of patients and care partners while guiding healthcare providers and systems towards more equitable, person‐centred care.

This bill of rights and responsibilities is not a legal instrument or a clinical guideline; rather, it is an equity‐oriented, co‐created tool grounded in the lived experiences of patients and their care partners. Unlike many generic patient charters typically developed by experts and administrators, the framework was created inductively through a bottom‐up approach, drawing directly on the narratives of storytellers who contributed to the Our Voices, Our Stories initiative [19, 23]. This initiative brought together narratives from individuals living with chronic wounds and their care partners, creating a unique platform for them to voice their needs, concerns, and challenges as they navigate daily life and complex health care systems. In doing so, it not only amplified lived experiences but also positioned them as a central source of knowledge to inform more equitable, person‐centred care. As a result, the framework is designed to educate and support individuals at risk of or living with wounds in navigating daily life and the healthcare system, but also to foster dialogue, support shared decision‐making, reduce preventable harm, and align clinical practices with person‐centred values.

This bill of rights and responsibilities is a dynamic document intended to evolve with ongoing use and feedback. It outlines the specific rights and accompanying responsibilities that individuals should know, understand, and exercise across care settings. The framework emphasises the importance of quality care, self‐determination, respect, and dignity in wound prevention and management. For example, individuals have the right to timely referral to preventive and specialised wound care services. By knowing and understanding their rights, patients are better equipped to advocate for themselves within a healthcare system that is often fragmented and difficult to navigate. At the same time, care partners, patient advocates, and healthcare providers play an important role in supporting and upholding these rights while recognising patients' active participation in care decisions and healing processes. Research shows that care partners want to be involved in wound healing, be informed and educated to achieve optimal care and outcomes [22]. Additionally, the inclusion of patient responsibilities alongside rights is intentional and emphasises a patient–care partner–provider partnership model in which each party has a vital and distinct role in achieving optimal care and outcomes. Through this lens, the framework aims to advance collaboration while equipping patients and care partners to engage and advocate for themselves. This paper outlines a multi‐phase creation process that led to the development of the first Patient Bill of Rights and Responsibilities in Wound Prevention and Care in Canada.

Methodology

This project is grounded in lived experiences and guided by co‐design and community‐based participatory principles [24, 25, 26, 27]. Co‐design, defined as designing with, rather than for, people, is recognised as a transformative and community‐centred approach that actively engages community members in shaping solutions that affect their lives [27]. As an equity‐oriented approach, co‐design challenges traditional biomedical, provider‐led models by elevating lived experience, redistributing decision‐making power, and fostering collaboration.

The project was further guided by principles commonly associated with community‐based participatory approaches, which emphasise equitable participation, shared accountability, and respectful, reciprocal relationships in which contributors and facilitators learn from one another while sharing power, resources, and recognition for outcomes [25, 28]. Together, co‐design and participatory principles provided a foundation for engaging a diverse range of community partners in the co‐creation of the framework. This approach also supported the meaningful inclusion of perspectives from equity‐deserving groups while fostering collaborative knowledge creation [29].

Ethical Considerations

The Research Ethics Board (REB) at Lakehead University reviewed this initiative and determined that it did not constitute a research study requiring REB approval. The REB therefore issued a determination letter permitting the dissemination of information describing the phases involved in the co‐creation and dissemination of the framework. All feedback gathered during workshops and surveys was voluntary and anonymous. Any illustrative quotes included in this manuscript have been de‐identified to protect the confidentiality of contributors.

Reflexivity Statement

Reflexivity in this study was approached not as a discrete section, but as an ongoing and collective practice embedded throughout the research process. Consistent with this orientation, reflexivity was enacted as a continuous and shared process across the team, contributing to the study's trustworthiness and confirmability. It involved critical reflection on how the research team's positionality, prior expertise, and relationships with partners could shape the interpretation of participants' stories. These reflections were integrated into iterative team discussions where assumptions and emerging interpretations were made explicit, critically examined, and, where necessary, revised, ensuring that multiple perspectives were considered and no single viewpoint dominated the analysis.

Reflexivity directly informed the analytic process, influencing which themes were prioritised, how rights and responsibilities were articulated, and which interpretations were reconsidered or set aside. The return of the draft framework to storytellers in Phase 4 further extended reflexivity beyond the research team, positioning participants' feedback as essential in validating and refining interpretations. Rather than existing alongside trustworthiness strategies, reflexive practices were integral to them. For instance, credibility was strengthened through ongoing reflexive engagement during analysis, while confirmability was supported through transparent documentation of how positionality informed interpretive decisions (Table 1). In this way, reflexivity was enacted across all six phases of the study, contributing to the overall rigour and integrity of the research.

Participants' Involvement and Roles Across Project Phases

The development of the bill of rights and responsibilities followed a multi‐phase, participatory co‐creation process designed to ensure that the framework was grounded in lived experience, informed by clinical and system‐level expertise, and refined through iterative engagement. This framework was developed through six phases: Phase 1: Thematic Analysis; Phase 2: Community Engagement Workshops; Phase 3: Building Consensus; Phase 4: Validation by Storytellers; Phase 5: Knowledge Mobilisation Preview; and Phase 6: National Dissemination Strategy.

Different participants' groups contributed across these multi‐phase processes. The project team led Phase 1 by conducting the thematic analysis and generating the initial framework. Community members—including individuals with lived experience, care partners, healthcare professionals, and system‐level decision‐makers—were primarily involved in Phases 2 and 3, where they contributed to refining, expanding, and building consensus. Storytellers, whose narratives informed the initial analysis, were engaged in Phase 4 to validate the emerging framework. Subsequent phases focused on broader feedback, refinement, and dissemination, drawing on continued engagement with participant groups and wound association partners.

Project Team

The project team consisted of six researchers with diverse expertise relevant to wound prevention and care, health equity, and participatory approaches. Four members were co‐investigators on the original project, funded by the Social Sciences and Humanity Council of Canada, while additional collaborators were invited to contribute complementary expertise. Team members were affiliated with post‐secondary institutions and community organisations, including representation from Wounds Canada.

Collectively, the team brought expertise in wound prevention and care, social determinants of health, health equity and inclusion, social justice, community‐engaged approaches, and participatory methodologies.

In Phase 1 (Thematic Analysis), the team conducted an interpretive analysis of publicly available digital narratives (n = 14) from individuals living with chronic and traumatic wounds (e.g., pressure injuries, diabetic foot ulcers, venous leg ulcers, burn injuries, and skin tears). Through iterative coding, discussion, and reflexive interpretation, the team identified key themes that informed the initial draft of rights and responsibilities [30]. This draft served as the foundation for subsequent co‐creation phases.

Community Engagement and Recruitment

The framework was developed through an inclusive, community‐engaged process involving a diverse group of participants who contributed primarily to Phase 2 (Community Engagement Workshops) and Phase 3 (Building Consensus). These included: (a) individuals with lived experience of wounds, care partners, and patient advocates; (b) healthcare professionals with experience in wound prevention and care; and (c) policymakers and health administrators. Participants were invited to contribute perspectives relevant to wound prevention and care.

Potential contributors were identified through existing professional networks, patient advocacy groups, and organisations involved in wound prevention and care. Email invitations were sent in March of 2025 to a total of 43 individuals outlining the purpose of this initiative, expectations for engagement, and the opportunity to contribute to three virtual co‐creation workshops. Participants were also invited to provide feedback through short surveys (Phase 3) and to engage in facilitated virtual discussions.

A total of 26 participants contributed to the co‐creation process, including individuals with lived experience of wounds and/or care partners (n = 7), policymakers and health administrators (n = 8), and healthcare professionals (n = 11) with a diversity of experience (e.g., family physician, nurse practitioner, wound care consultant, advanced foot care nurse).

Digital Storytellers

To ensure that the framework remained grounded in lived experience, individuals (n = 10) who had previously contributed digital stories to the Our Voices, Our Stories initiative were electronically invited to participate in Phase 4 (Validation Phase). The invitation included a summary of the co‐creation process, a draft of the bill of rights and responsibilities, an information and consent form, and a link to a short, anonymous online survey.

In this phase, storytellers were invited to review the consensus version of the emerging framework and provide feedback on its authenticity, relevance, and alignment with their lived experiences. Their role was to validate the extent to which the framework accurately reflected the realities, needs, and priorities expressed through their stories, as well as to identify any areas requiring clarification, refinement, or further emphasis. This validation step is consistent with qualitative and participatory research approaches that prioritise returning findings to participants to enhance credibility, authenticity, and trustworthiness.

The Multi‐Phase Co‐Creation Process

Building on real digital stories from the Our Voices, Our Stories initiative, the development of the bill of rights' framework followed a multi‐phase co‐creation process consisting of (Figure 1): [1] thematic analysis of digital stories; [2] community engagement workshops; [3] consensus building; [4] validation by storytellers; [5] knowledge mobilisation preview; and [6] national dissemination strategy.

Using reflexive thematic analysis, [30] informed by narrative approaches to understanding lived experience, [33] the research team examined the digital stories to identify key themes. These included recurring patterns and insights related to the struggles, challenges, and systemic barriers experienced by individuals living with wounds.

The themes emerged in this phase informed the initial framework of rights and responsibilities.

Through this collaborative process, a total of 18 themes were identified. These themes were then operationalised in formal statements representing patient rights and corresponding responsibilities. This process resulted in an initial framework consisting of 18 rights and 18 corresponding responsibilities' statements (n = 36 statements).

Following thematic synthesis and further review by the first author, overlapping or conceptually similar statements were consolidated to improve clarity. This refinement resulted in a framework consisting of 16 rights and 16 corresponding responsibility statements (n = 32 statements).

Phase 2: Community Engagement Workshops

Following thematic analysis, three virtual workshops were conducted between May and July of 2025. Each workshop lasted between 90 and 120 min and was essential to support meaningful community engagement. During the sessions, participants engaged in facilitated discussions and small‐group dialogue to reflect on the emerging rights and responsibilities statements and share perspectives on their relevance, clarity, and applicability.

The workshops served as a collaborative platform to integrate diverse perspectives and ensure that the bill of rights and responsibilities reflected the priorities and experiences of individuals living with wounds, care partners, healthcare providers, and policy stakeholders. Phase 3: Consensus Building

Participants who attended the workshops were invited to complete an anonymous online survey to provide additional feedback on the overall emerging framework and support the consensus‐building process. The survey enabled participants to rate their agreement with each patient right and corresponding responsibility using a five‐point Likert scale. Open‐text fields were also included to capture additional suggestions for refinement.

Of the 26 participants invited, 19 completed the survey (73%). Among respondents, 20% identified as patients or care partners and 80% as healthcare providers or policymakers. Agreement levels across the statements ranged from 68.42% to 100%, with an overall average of 88.94%. Overall, 28 of the 32 statements (87%) achieved agreement above the predefined 80% threshold, indicating strong consensus.

Based on survey feedback, statements with agreement below 80%, as well as those identified as repetitive or unclear, were further refined. A professional editor subsequently reviewed the statements to enhance clarity, consistency, and plain‐language readability. This process resulted in a refined framework, in which overlapping statements and redundancies were addressed, yielding a final set of 12 rights and 12 corresponding responsibilities.

Co‐Creation Outcomes: The Patient Bill of Rights and Responsibilities Framework

The multi‐phase co‐creation process resulted in the final framework consisting of 12 rights and 12 responsibilities (n = 24 statements). These were derived from thematic analysis and iteratively refined through community engagement workshops, participants' feedback, and consensus‐building activities.

Conceptually, these 12 rights define an integrated, patient‐centred framework for wound prevention and care that moves beyond episodic treatment towards a coordinated, systems‐level approach. They emphasise timely access, early risk identification, and evidence‐based care, while also prioritising person‐centred practices. Patients are positioned as active partners through clear communication and shared decision‐making. The framework also recognises pain management, psychosocial, and trauma‐informed care as essential components of wound prevention and care. Finally, these rights situate health within a wider socio‐economic context by highlighting the importance of care navigation, wound prevention, management, and financial stability, reflecting a holistic and equitable framework.

The complete framework is publicly available in both English and French through the Wounds Canada website (Supporting Information), supporting accessibility across Canada's official languages [34].

Phase 4: Validation by Storytellers

To ensure that the framework accurately reflected the experiences that informed its development, the framework was shared with individuals who had contributed digital stories to the Our Voices, Our Stories initiative. Email invitations were sent to all storytellers outlining the purpose of the validation step, summarising the co‐creation process, and included a link to the draft document, an information and consent form, and an invitation to complete a short anonymous online survey.

Six storytellers (n = 60%) responded and completed the survey. Participants were asked to provide feedback on the clarity, relevance, and usefulness of the framework, and whether the rights and responsibilities statements reflected their experiences navigating daily life and the healthcare system.

Overall, responses indicated that the framework was perceived as a clear and useful document that accurately reflected the experiences, values, and needs of individuals at risk of, or living with, wounds. This validation step further supported the credibility and relevance of the framework prior to broader dissemination.

Phase 5: Knowledge Mobilisation Preview

In September 2025, the final framework was presented to National Wound Community Think Tank, an action‐oriented working group convened by Wounds Canada that brings together clinicians, patient advocates, educators, researchers, industry partners, and other stakeholders from across Canada [35]. During this session, participants engaged in facilitated dialogue to share perspectives regarding the framework and to discuss potential strategies for its dissemination. Insights from these discussions contributed to planning subsequent knowledge mobilisation strategies.

The framework and its co‐creation process were also presented at the Wounds Canada National Conference, held 2–4 October 2025, in Toronto, Ontario. The oral presentation, titled [‘Grounded in Stories, Driven by Data: Co‐Creating a Patient Bill of Rights and Responsibilities to Transform Wound Prevention and Care in Canada,’], introduced attendees (n = over 400) to the development process and the emerging framework. The session included facilitated dialogue exploring potential applications of the bill of rights and responsibilities for advancing compassionate, equitable, and responsive wound prevention and care across Canada.

In addition, the co‐creation process and final framework were presented as a poster during the conference Poster Café session. The poster received both the 2025 People's Choice Award and the 2025 Judge's Choice Award. These recognitions reflect strong engagement from the wound care community and highlight the perceived relevance and potential impact of the PBoRR‐WPC in advancing equity‐informed, person‐centred wound prevention and care.

Phase 6: National Dissemination, Early Uptake and Impact

In partnership with Wounds Canada, a multi‐pronged national dissemination strategy was developed to build awareness of the bill of rights and responsibilities as a foundation for early uptake and future implementation. Designed to support, rather than prescribe, application, the strategy enables flexible, context‐specific engagement across care settings.

Dissemination includes coordinated, multi‐channel approaches such as website integration, social media campaigns, webinars, conference presentations, and point‐of‐care distribution through print and digital materials, alongside integration into professional education programmes preparing healthcare professionals for wound prevention and care management.

Since its launch on 26 February 2026, the framework has achieved broad national reach through Wounds Canada platforms and networks (+18 000 social media followers). The launch webinar engaged 460 registrants, supported by targeted e‐blasts (> 20 000 reach each). To date, the resource has reached 3762 viewers, with 533 English and 103 French downloads, indicating strong early uptake across patient, caregiver, and healthcare communities.

Early uptake is further reflected in formal recognition and endorsement by key healthcare organisations, including Covenant Health (Edmonton, Alberta, Canada) and the Canadian Association of Foot Care Nurses (CAFCN), as well as integration into their patient education resources. The first author was also invited to present this work at the CAFCN national conference (22–24 May 2026), reflecting its growing influence on practice across Canada.

The impact of this work also extends to education and capacity building. The framework is being integrated into healthcare curricula at Canadian universities (e.g., Lakehead University) and into a national educational programming at Wounds Canada Institute, supporting the training of future healthcare professionals in equity‐oriented, person‐centred wound care.

A Living Framework: Ongoing Refinement and Evaluation

The framework is intended to evolve over time as a dynamic, living resource. It is currently hosted on the Wounds Canada website and through EQUIP@Action, a health equity research hub at Lakehead University, that supports participatory research and knowledge mobilisation initiatives. The framework remains open to ongoing input from patients, care partners, clinicians, and other community members.

Feedback will be collected continuously and considered alongside emerging evidence and new patient stories to inform future refinements. While updates will occur periodically, the process is intentionally flexible to ensure responsiveness without reliance on fixed timelines. Updates will be guided by community members' input, ongoing narrative analysis, and evolving priorities in wound prevention and care, supporting the continued development of the framework towards a patient‐informed charter capable of influencing practice and policy.

Evaluation is conceptualised as an evolving process, consistent with principles from knowledge translation and implementation science [36, 37]. Initial evaluation efforts will focus on dissemination and uptake, including indicators such as website downloads, participation in educational and community initiatives, and engagement across dissemination activities. Over time, evaluation will expand to examine the influence of the bill of rights and responsibilities practice, education, and health systems. Drawing on the Donabedian model, [38] the framework is intended to inform structures (e.g., organisational policies), processes (e.g., care delivery and patient engagement), and outcomes (e.g., patient experience and equity) across micro, meso, and macro levels.

Discussion

This initiative underscores the value of co‐creating a patient bill of rights and responsibilities in wound care and prevention grounded in lived experience. Patient and care partner narratives revealed systemic shortcomings, including disparities in access to services, gaps in information sharing, fragmented wound care pathways, and communication breakdowns. These gaps were not only logistical but also relational, rooted in disempowerment, mistrust, and structural inequities [20]. These findings align with broader literature showing that marginalised populations disproportionately experience barriers to wound prevention and treatment due to social determinants of health, systemic bias, and inequitable access to specialised services [1, 37]. These challenges are further compounded by the fragmented organisation of wound care across healthcare sectors in Canada [7].

The framework responds to these challenges by offering a contextually grounded framework to support equitable, person‐centred wound care. Importantly, it extends beyond traditional rights‐based charters by pairing each patient right with a corresponding responsibility. This approach promotes a shared‐care philosophy that recognises patients, care partners, and healthcare providers as collaborators. Such a framework reflects core principles of person‐ and family‐centred care, including reciprocity, partnership, and shared accountability [39, 40]. These principles are particularly relevant in wound care, where chronicity, stigma, and complex care trajectories require strong system navigation, continuity, and coordination across providers and settings [41, 42, 43] When such supports are lacking, individuals may experience delays, preventable complications, and poorer outcomes, including avoidable amputations, particularly in rural and remote communities [44, 45, 46].

The framework has strong potential as a knowledge translation tool across practice, education, and policy. In clinical practice, it may be used to raise awareness through accessible formats such as posters, pamphlets, or digital resources. By articulating shared values, rights, and responsibilities, the framework can clarify roles among providers, patients, and care partners, supporting collaborative care planning and shared decision‐making. It may also support advocacy for appropriate resources and services.

In educational contexts, the patient bill of rights and responsibilities may be integrated into undergraduate and graduate curricula, as well as continuing professional development. Its use within interprofessional education can foster shared, values‐based learning across disciplines, supporting a common understanding of roles and collaborative care [9].

Beyond its practical application, the co‐creation process demonstrates the value of co‐design and narrative‐informed approaches in developing tools responsive to real‐world health system challenges. By positioning patients, care partners, providers, and policymakers as partners, the process reflects equity‐oriented and patient‐engaged principles, supporting a more balanced distribution of power and shared decision‐making [15, 16, 20].

From a knowledge translation perspective, the bill of rights and responsibilities framework also offers opportunities for advocacy and system‐level change. It may support policy dialogue by articulating patient‐informed priorities in wound care. In practice settings, both active and passive strategies may be used, such as displaying the framework in clinical environments to support awareness.

In the Canadian context, this work aligns with broader commitments to reconciliation and to strengthening health systems that include the voices of those most affected by inequities [47, 48].

Overall, this initiative highlights the importance of collaborative approaches that centre lived experience in developing health system tools. By drawing on patient and care partner narratives, the framework highlights systemic challenges while offering a practical framework to support advocacy, accountability, and equitable care. By pairing rights with responsibilities, it promotes a shared‐care ethos among patients, care partners, and providers.

Strengths and Limitations

A key strength of this initiative is its grounding in the lived experiences of individuals affected by wounds and their care partners, captured through participatory digital storytelling within the Our Voices, Our Stories initiative. By centering patient voices, the patient bill of rights and responsibilities reflects priorities, challenges, and system‐level gaps identified directly by those navigating wound care. The study is further strengthened by its rigorous, multi‐phase co‐creation process, which engaged diverse stakeholders across iterative stages of analysis, dialogue, and consensus building.

The framework also represents an innovative approach to knowledge mobilisation by translating qualitative insights into a practical tool for education, clinical practice, and advocacy. The inclusion of both rights and corresponding responsibilities is a novel contribution, supporting a relational and person‐centred approach that emphasises shared accountability.

A limitation is the relatively small number of storytellers involved in the validation phase, reflecting the size of the original digital storytelling cohort. However, smaller samples are common in participatory research requiring sustained engagement. Additional validation across broader populations and diverse healthcare settings would further strengthen the generalisability and applicability of the framework.

Conclusion

The Patient Bill of Rights and Responsibilities in Wound Prevention and Care reflects a shared vision for compassionate, equitable, and responsive wound prevention and care that prioritises human dignity and social justice. This initiative demonstrates how a narrative‐informed, participatory co‐creation process can generate a meaningful and representative Patient Bill of Rights and Responsibilities grounded in the voices of individuals living with and caring for chronic wounds.

Beyond documenting these experiences, the framework offers a practical framework that functions as both an educational resource and an advocacy tool. It can support patients, care partners, healthcare providers, and decision‐makers in understanding person‐centred values and promoting accountability for respectful and equitable care. Through its integration into education, clinical practice, and policy dialogue, the framework has the potential to strengthen practice standards, foster cultural change within wound care systems, and inform the development of more responsive services.

Importantly, this work contributes to a growing body of evidence supporting co‐design and participatory approaches as essential strategies for developing health system tools that are both contextually relevant and grounded in lived experience. While developed within the Canadian context, the principles underpinning the PBORR‐WPC may be transferable to other settings seeking to advance equity‐oriented and person‐centred care.

Ultimately, the patient bill of rights and responsibilities invites ongoing dialogue among patients, care partners, clinicians, and policymakers to co‐create systems of care with individuals rather than for them. As a living framework, its continued evolution through dissemination, engagement, and evaluation will be critical to sustaining its relevance and impact. By centering lived experience and shared responsibility, the framework provides a foundation for strengthening patient‐centred accountability and advancing more equitable wound care systems.
