Implementation and evaluation of an insulin use manual in the public health system of a Peruvian Region: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Cecilia Anza-Ramirez, Karen Bonilla-Aguilar, Joaquín Carrasco-Zavala, José A Diaz-RG, Janeth Tenorio-Mucha, Jessica Hanae Zafra-Tanaka, Dulce Moran, Jessica Faustor Sanchez, Angela Guillermina Roncal Vergara, Maria Lazo-Porras

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetes is a major public health issue, especially in low-and middle- income countries (LMICs) where barriers to care are common. Despite insulin’s critical role, access remains limited due to market concentration, weak policies, poor availability, and lack of trained staff. This study evaluated the implementation of an insulin use manual in Tumbes, Peru, focusing on healthcare workers training and systemic barriers.MethodsThis was a two-phase descriptive mixed-methods study conducted in Tumbes, Peru. In the first phase (March–June 2023), a virtual course on insulin use followed by an on-site workshop was implemented for healthcare workers, with quantitative assessments of knowledge and satisfaction. In the second phase (October 2023), qualitative interviews were carried out with these healthcare workers as well as patients and caregivers to evaluate both the implementation process and the local health system using the Rapid Assessment Protocol for Insulin Access, Medical Research Council for Process Evaluation, and the World Health Organization (WHO)’s Six Building Blocks framework. Data were analyzed with descriptive statistics and thematic coding.ResultsThe virtual course registered 193 participants, but only 44 completed all units, the median knowledge median scores rose from 15.3 to 19.1). In the on-site workshop, 28 participants attended. Median scores increased from 14 (IQR 10–16) at baseline to 15 (IQR 10–16) in the post-test. Feedback highlighted the course’s flexibility and practical relevance, though participants suggested more hands-on components and patient education tools. Interviews revealed systemic barriers across WHO’s Six Building Blocks framework, including limited training and confidence among healthcare workers in insulin management, shortages of insulin and related supplies at the primary care level, weak health information systems without proper tracking of insulin users; high out-of-pocket costs that undermine adherence, inadequate patient education, and insufficient leadership and supervision to ensure consistent diabetes care.ConclusionsThis study shows that while targeted training can improve healthcare workers’ knowledge on insulin use, systemic gaps continue to hinder quality diabetes care. Background

By 2050, diabetes prevalence is projected to rise more sharply in regions such as Latin America and Africa [1]. Approximately 30% of those with type 2 diabetes and all people with type 1 diabetes need insulin for their treatment and for this reason they face more difficulties [2]. Despite the relevance of insulin in the management of diabetes mellitus, previous studies show limited access to insulin [2, 3]. This limited access results from a complex chain of factors: absence of game-changing innovations, multinationals supplying 95% of the insulin worldwide, lack of policies for selection, procurement and pricing of insulin, poor availability in public and private pharmacies [4].

Insulin treatment without insurance coverage and barriers in prescription, including the shortage of trained professionals in diabetes diagnosis and insulin use, are key challenges in low and middle-income countries (LMICs) [5]. In order to focus efforts in improving diabetes management in a country, different approaches can be boosted; having national standards to train people that use insulin is one of them, as well as, provide training to healthcare workers in insulin application and, above all, in how to teach patients to apply their own insulin dosage [6]. For these reasons, an Insulin Use Manual was developed in Peru by the Ministry of Health [4]. The manual was developed primarily as a training and reference resource for healthcare workers involved in diabetes care at different levels of the health system. It provides standardized guidance on administration techniques, glucose monitoring, hypoglycemia management, and patient education strategies to support the safe and effective use of insulin in routine clinical practice. Its implementation is carried out at the regional level through the DIRESAs (Regional Health Directorates), which are decentralized administrative bodies of the Ministry of Health responsible for organizing and supervising health services.

It is important to evaluate the implementation of this type of guideline. Implementation is highly context-dependent, and existing studies have mostly been conducted in specific settings such as capital cities or urban areas [7]. It is also necessary to assess the implementation of clinical practice guidelines, protocols, or manuals through formative evaluation, as understanding the context and identifying barriers is key for successful implementation [8, 9]. So far, previous studies conducted in LMICs to understand the health system response to people with diabetes have revealed some barriers such as (i) scarce information at different levels of the health system about diabetes; (ii) insufficient financial resources; and (iii) insufficient human resources trained in diabetes management [10, 11]. These findings are similar to a previous study conducted in Peru [5]. Previous studies in LMICs have mostly evaluated diabetes care in general, often in single urban settings, with limited focus on insulin-specific implementation, workforce training in insulin use, and health-system readiness for sustained adoption [5, 7, 10, 11]. Although the Insulin Use Manual exists in Peru, evidence on its implementation in decentralized regions is still limited; this study addresses that gap by integrating training results with a structured assessment of system-level barriers and facilitators to insulin use.

In LMICs, it is essential to generate data from diverse settings due to the high degrees of inequalities between states or regions [12, 13]. The different “realities” can lead to different actions in accordance with the findings to plan better implementation of clinical practice guidelines, protocols or manuals and strategies in health [7]. Therefore, this study had two objectives. The primary objective was to conduct a formative evaluation of the implementation of the Insulin Use Manual training strategy in Tumbes, focusing on reach, knowledge change, acceptability, fidelity, and adoption among healthcare workers. The secondary objective was to identify barriers and facilitators related to insulin access and diabetes care delivery at primary and secondary levels from the perspectives of healthcare workers, patients, and caregivers. This pilot study in Tumbes was designed to inform future national implementation.

Materials and methods

Background

The “Addressing the Challenges and Constraints of Insulin Sources and Supply” (ACCISS) project, in collaboration with the Directorate of Non-Communicable Diseases (DENOT, for its Spanish acronym) of the Ministry of Health of Peru (MINSA, for its Spanish acronym), has developed strategies to improve the knowledge, use, and application of insulin in the country. As part of its activities between 2018 and 2021, the project team developed an Insulin Use Manual which ended with the implementation of the first edition of an online course [14]. Later, in 2023, the ACCISS team in collaboration with the Regional Health Directorate of Tumbes (DIRESA-Tumbes), implemented the second edition of the course, consisting of a virtual training and an on-site workshop. The present study focuses on this second edition.

Study design

A descriptive mixed-methods study was carried out in two phases. The first phase, conducted from March to June 2023, consisted of the implementation of the previously described course in Tumbes, a semi-urban coastal region in northern Peru, where 45.1% of people aged 15 years or older were reported to have at least one comorbidity, including obesity, diabetes mellitus, and/or hypertension [15]. The course had two components: a virtual training delivered asynchronously through Moodle and an on-site practical workshop. Quantitative evaluations were performed through pre–post tests and surveys. The second phase (October 2023) focused on evaluating both the implementation process of the course and the health system response at the primary and secondary levels. This phase used qualitative methodologies through structured interviews. No formal a priori sample size or power calculation was performed because this was a pragmatic formative implementation study; enrollment depended on operational feasibility and participation of eligible healthcare workers in the region.

Participants

The first phase consisted of the virtual course and the on-site workshop. Inclusion criteria for healthcare workers were being affiliated with DIRESA-Tumbes, belonging to an eligible professional category (physicians, nurses, nursing technicians, obstetricians, nutritionists, or laboratorians), and providing informed consent. Exclusion criteria were duplicate registration, residence/work outside Tumbes, or not meeting professional eligibility criteria. For implementation analyses, completion of the virtual course was defined as completion of all four units with submission of all unit assessments in Moodle; completion status was verified through platform activity logs. Completion of the on-site workshop was defined as attendance plus completion of pre- and post-workshop assessments. In the second phase, eligible participants were healthcare workers who completed the first phase, as well as patients with diabetes currently using insulin and their primary caregivers at primary and secondary care levels. Participants were recruited using convenience sampling among those involved in the implementation process. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with healthcare workers, patients, and caregivers. Interviews continued until thematic saturation was reached, meaning that no substantially new themes emerged from additional interviews. Data triangulation was achieved by comparing perspectives from healthcare workers, patients, and caregivers. Interviews lasted approximately 30–45 min and were conducted by trained members of the research team with prior experience in qualitative interviewing and diabetes research.

Study procedures

First phase: implementation

Recruitment for the first phase took place between December 2022 and February 2023. Participants were invited through several dissemination channels, including DIRESA-Tumbes institutional communications, WhatsApp groups used by healthcare workers, and paid advertisements on Facebook. Enrollment closed on March 5, 2023.

The virtual course ran from March 13 to May 21, 2023, through Moodle, with four units covering: (1) generalities of diabetes and insulin (2), subcutaneous injection technique (3), hypoglycemia management, and (4) glucose monitoring. Each unit included videos, slides, and evaluations at the beginning and end. To progress, participants had to pass the evaluation of the previous unit. The final grade was obtained by averaging the four-unit scores. Participants were followed up through personal and group messages via WhatsApp.

Upon completion of the course, participants were invited to an on-site workshop. Two versions were held: one for completers (June 9, with morning and afternoon schedules) and another for non-completers (June 8, in the afternoon). The sessions included practical training on insulin regimens, application techniques, and glucose self-monitoring, with pre–post evaluations. The information provided during the course and workshop, as well as the dates of these are presented in Supplementary Material 01.

Second phase: evaluation

In October 2023, the second phase was conducted through interviews. Eligible participants included only healthcare workers who had successfully completed the first phase, as well as patients using insulin and their caregivers. Participants were recruited through convenience sampling, primarily referred by primary care healthcare workers. For process evaluation, interviews with healthcare workers were restricted to those who had completed the virtual course and attended the on-site workshop; healthcare workers who did not complete training were not interviewed.

Patient inclusion criteria required current insulin treatment and prior diabetes education. Four guides were developed: three based on the Rapid Assessment Protocol for Insulin Access (RAPIA) [16] protocol to assess the health system, and one based on the Medical Research Council (MRC) [17] framework for process evaluation to evaluate the implementation.

Interviews were conducted mostly on-site, audio-recorded with participants’ permission, and transcribed verbatim in Spanish. Transcripts were anonymized by removing personal identifiers and assigning participant codes prior to analysis. The de-identified transcripts were analyzed thematically.

Confidentiality was ensured throughout the process. Audio recordings and transcripts were stored in password-protected files with access restricted to the research team, and only anonymized excerpts were used for reporting.

Variables and outcomes

Study outcomes were defined according to the two prespecified objectives. For the primary objective, outcomes were implementation indicators of the training strategy (virtual course and on-site workshop): reach (proportion enrolled and completing each component), knowledge acquisition (pre–post scores), and implementation process measures (acceptability, fidelity, and adoption). Knowledge was assessed with unit-specific multiple-choice tests derived from the Insulin Use Manual and mapped to predefined learning objectives for each course unit, using a 0–20 grading scale. Before implementation, items were reviewed by the research team for content alignment and clarity. Because these tests were designed for programmatic training evaluation, a formal psychometric validation study (e.g., Cronbach’s alpha or item-discrimination analysis) was not performed. For the secondary objective, outcomes were barriers and facilitators in the health system response to insulin use and diabetes care, assessed four months after the intervention was implemented through RAPIA-based interviews and analyzed across WHO Six Building Blocks domains.

Secondary outcomes included participant satisfaction, measured through a short questionnaire at the end of the course, and sociodemographic characteristics of participants. Barriers, facilitators, adaptations, and feedback were also documented through qualitative interviews.

Data analysis

Sociodemographic information was presented as absolute and relative frequencies, with age summarized using medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). Pre–post test results were summarized as paired changes at both unit and course levels using medians and interquartile ranges. Changes in score were described using median paired differences. Participants with incomplete course units or missing assessments were excluded from paired analyses. Satisfaction responses were expressed as percentages. Statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Qualitative data were analyzed using ATLAS.ti. software (version 23). An initial coding framework was developed based on the study objectives and the WHO’s Six Building Blocks framework (deductive approach), and was iteratively refined to incorporate emerging themes from data (inductive approach). Coding was performed by members of the research team, and discrepancies were discussed and resolved through consensus. Findings were organized into two analytical domains: (1) Implementation process of the virtual course and on-site workshop, including acceptability, fidelity, and adoption; (2) Health system at the primary and secondary level of care. The first part of the information was presented by addressing the motivation for enrollment, content provided, course implementation and strengths and limitations. The second part was analysed using the WHO’s Six Building Blocks framework: service delivery, workforce, information systems, access to medicines, financing, and governance [18]. Triangulation across participants groups was used to enhance the credibility of the findings.

Ethical issues

Informed consent was obtained from the participants who agreed to participate in the course, workshop, interviews and authorized the use of their information. Both the study protocol and the informed consent were approved by the ethics committee of the Universidad Peruana Cayetano Heredia (UPCH) (Approval No. 209805, September 30, 2022). Feedback and satisfaction survey

At the end of the virtual course, 40 participants completed the feedback and satisfaction survey. Questions were asked regarding learning achievements, course content, learning resources, correspondence of evaluations, impact of the intervention on their learning, new skills acquisition, usability of the platform and overall perception of the course. Regarding all of these, between 90 and 100% of the participants had positive responses. Specifically, 100% considered that the course met their expectations and that they would recommend it to others.

First part of the second phase: analysis of the process of implementation of the intervention

Motivation for enrollment and content provided

Participants cited the high incidence of diabetes in their communities as a major reason for joining the course. This highlighted the urgent need to improve their skills and knowledge to manage diabetes effectively. During the qualitative interviews, participants reported learning about the course through WhatsApp groups, emails from health authorities, and professional organizations. One primary care healthcare worker remarked, “There are so many patients who come in with diabetes complications that we need to be prepared to manage better here rather than referring everyone to the hospital”. Another nurse from the Urgency Medical Attention System (SAMU) expressed, “I always try to keep learning because every day brings new knowledge”. Most participants found the course content relevant, referring to the technique to apply insulin, a participant expressed: “…we always applied it horizontally…they told us that it had to be vertical, but we have to use the “tiny syringes”. That was a novelty for me”. Nevertheless, participants also suggested improvements, such as including more clinical cases, tailoring information by specialty, and adding visual aids like flipcharts to facilitate their understanding and practical application.

Course implementation

The virtual course format was appreciated for its flexibility. It allowed participants to complete it from home without significantly interfering with their daily schedules. In the words of some healthcare worker, “We manage our time flexibly, studying after shifts or on weekends”. However, challenges emerged as the course was frequently considered a secondary activity, making it difficult for those working in health centers to balance it with their clinical responsibilities. Some SAMU healthcare workers adapted by completing the course during downtime between emergency calls, as mentioned by one participant, “We reviewed the course during free moments between calls”. Additionally, several participants felt that getting quicker answers to questions during the course could further improve their learning experience.

Strengths and limitations

Both strengths and limitations of the course were identified by the feedback received. Participants noted that simplifying concepts and focusing on practical applications impacted their work settings. To strengthen their knowledge, many suggested adding more hands-on components, especially through clinical case simulations. Scheduling classes outside of holidays and periods of peak healthcare demand was also suggested to improve accessibility and reduce conflicts with health campaigns. About formatting/presentation, one nurse emphasized, “Larger fonts and clearer visuals would make it easier to follow for everyone”. Additionally, participants recommended the use of flipcharts and other visual aids to facilitate patient education on insulin use, especially in areas with lower literacy levels.

The second part of the second phase of the study identifies major challenges in each of the six topics of the WHO’s Six Building Blocks framework. Participants reported barriers related to service delivery, health workforce capacity, health information systems, access to medicines, financing, and leadership/governance. These challenges affected the organization and delivery of diabetes care, particularly for patients requiring insulin treatment. Service delivery

Diabetes care in terms of service delivery experiences significant limitations not only with respect to quality but also towards accessibility. There is a severe deficiency on the primary health care (PHC) level when it comes down to patients who use insulin. This originates from lack of training in health personnel and scarce supplies for insulin use, often translating to referrals to the regional hospitals. This in turn places an extra burden on patients, as they are requested to wait more for their attention at the hospital in comparison to the PHC. Healthcare workers from PHC centers stated, “because we don’t always have insulin available, patients have to bring their own, and not all health personnel are trained to administer it” (Primary healthcare worker), revealing the system’s gaps in meeting diabetes management needs.

Problems remain at the hospital level including overcrowded clinics and no customized education programs to assist with diabetes management. Patients often relay a combination of stories tales of individualized care on one hand, and for the majority: anecdotes about impoverished service delivery that does not do much to facilitate their health, such as insufficient supply chains, availability of medicines or lack of appropriate and empathic manners from the health workers. This disconnect affects patient satisfaction, as they feel “education is rushed and inadequate, especially on insulin application, diet, and physical activity”, highlighting a need for structured training and resources dedicated to diabetes care.

Regarding patient education, patients highlight the lack of initiatives by establishments to reinforce education in self-management. It is reported that this is due to limited time for care and lack of specialized environments for education/application and materials. This leads to patient education being quick and unpremeditated, leading to patient dissatisfaction. Patients report insufficient education for their needs, especially regarding insulin application, diet and physical activity. Likewise, with situations such as the belief that continued insulin use is associated with complications, cultural beliefs also pose a challenge in providing education. Participants frequently reported limited training opportunities and insufficient resources to support diabetes education.

Health workforce

The health workforce (doctors and nurses) at various levels of experience from general medicine practitioners to specialist, exhibited poor knowledge in insulin prescription and administration. Participants reported gaps in training related to insulin prescription and administration. Patients with diabetes who use insulin cannot rely on their physicians to treat complex blood glucose levels which highlights the desperate need for improved education and training, especially in a theoretical-practical on-site modality. In addition, care processes and health personnel oversight offer alternative strategies to improve diabetes outcomes. Regarding patient care of people with diabetes, there is a lack of supervision of staff specifically for this function. But today’s failure to have the necessary interdisciplinary approach and training restricts healthcare workers’ capability of delivering quality care. These training gaps should be addressed and increased because interdisciplinary collaboration may have a potential large impact on patient outcomes.

Health information systems

Efficient health information management is a requirement for effective monitoring and treatment of people with type 2 diabetes. The present system, which lacks electronic health records, makes data collection, management, preservation and organization challenging, thereby adversely affecting the continuum as well as the quality of care. The lack of consistent and complete patient records makes the longitudinal follow-up of individuals difficult. Also, there is no specific registry of patients with type 2 diabetes and their management, which makes it harder to determine the outcomes of the interventions. This includes knowledge of the type of diabetes, use of insulin and health establishment of care. Indeed, quite a few healthcare workers face challenges concerning accessibility and refreshing patient records. This ultimately leads to gaps in the process, limiting opportunities for timely interventions. In addition, the lack of registration specificity for patients who use insulin complicates targeted interventions, as noted by a health worker who said, “there’s no category for patients who use insulin in the official records” (Hospital-based healthcare worker) Enhanced data and information systems would similarly help healthcare workers deliver connected care based on timely, accurate records.

Access to essential medicines

The availability of essential medicines, including insulin, in resource-poor environments is limited across all levels of care. The lack of insulin in health clinics, which leads patients to travel long distances there and back, causing additional costs and making people less likely to adhere to the treatment. Even in hospitals, where insulin is generally more accessible, shortages of supplies like specific syringes can still occur. Moreover, staff supervision is minimal, which compounds these limitations, as noted in patient complaints about inconsistency in the quality of care. Participants also described perceived disrespectful treatment during healthcare encounters, such as dismissive attitudes from healthcare workers. One patient described this experience as, “some nurses are dismissive, saying I should learn to inject myself already”. There is also a lack of monitoring devices, such as glucometers, and their use is not well known. These gaps can lead to major treatment delays and ineffective diabetes management. Participants described frequent shortages of insulin and related supplies, which they reported as barriers to treatment adherence.

Financing

The high cost of diabetes treatment is a big cause for concern among these patients. In case of shortages at the primary care level, these patients are referred to regional hospitals, which adds further costs to transportation and care. When one adds those expenses to the cost of buying insulin and other supplies, often not completely covered by the Health Insurances, the costs become unaffordable for many patients. A patient remarked, “it becomes unaffordable when I have to buy everything myself” (Patient with diabetes), emphasizing how financial barriers limit treatment adherence. In a nutshell, these economic barriers of cost can easily translate into poor treatment adherence and subsequently worse health outcomes. Participants described financial barriers related to transportation costs, insulin purchases, and other treatment-related expenses.

Leadership/governance

Efficient governance and leadership are also necessary to facilitate the implementation of educational programs, to ensure health personnel roles in diabetes care. Currently, there isn’t much formal training on insulin management, and healthcare staff supervision is often inadequate. In addition, there is limited community involvement by health establishments on an extramurally basis. Supervision by the regional health authorities (DIRESA) was reported to occur infrequently, generally only once or twice a year and focused on specific indicators. This limited frequency of monitoring leaves many operational and quality-of-care issues insufficiently addressed. This gap in leadership and governance leads to inconsistencies on care quality, which frequently results in poor patient outcomes. Participants reported limited supervision and coordination from regional health authorities. Finally, improving the coordination and supervision of healthcare personnel is necessary to guarantee that protocolized care is applied, assuring standardized quality in patient management.

Discussion

Main results

This study identified some positive aspects of the training provided to the region but at the same time the remaining barriers in the health system that hamper the implementation of the training intervention based on the Insulin Use Manual. When considered together, the quantitative and qualitative findings provide a more complete picture of the implementation process. The quantitative results allowed us to evaluate participation in the training intervention and changes in knowledge among healthcare workers, while the qualitative interviews helped explain the contextual factors that influence how insulin is managed in routine practice. This integration of findings highlights that although training can improve knowledge, broader health system barriers—such as limited supplies, insufficient supervision, and weak information systems—continue to affect the effective implementation of diabetes care. The training was completed by a small percentage of participants that enrolled to the virtual course. At the same time, the results suggest that the impact of the course should be interpreted cautiously. Although participants who completed the virtual course showed improvements in their scores, these findings cannot be interpreted as definitive evidence of effectiveness given the small number of completers and the absence of inferential analysis. The voluntary and asynchronous training formats, where competing professional responsibilities and limited time may affect participation and course completion. These results also contribute to understanding how educational interventions on insulin use can be implemented within the public health system. While the training helped strengthen knowledge among participating healthcare workers, the qualitative findings reveal contextual barriers that influence how these practices are applied in routine care, including limited availability of supplies, insufficient supervision, and gaps in patient education. The knowledge score of the participants improved in the before-after evaluation and the feedback to the workshop in insulin use was positive. Some aspects highlighted in the feedback to enrich the training was the use of clinical cases simulation, more hand-on components and resources for education to patients. The aspects of the health system that are perceived as barriers are workforce capacity, supply availability, poor information system and more funding to manage diabetes properly. Given the observed attrition, these findings should be interpreted primarily as evidence among participants who remained engaged with the training.

Comparison with others studies

The knowledge about diabetes and about insulin use is poor in healthcare workers [19–21]. Training healthcare workers is important and needed because they will explain to the patients about the procedure. A survey applied in Saudi Arabia to patients about insulin injection technique showed that only 36% demonstrated an appropriate technique. Also, those patients that received training from diabetes educators reported a better score in comparison to those trained by other professionals or none training [22]. Another study in Egypt conducted in pharmacists showed that less than 13% of them had adequate knowledge about insulin and they reported online courses and workshops as their preferred method to receive training [20]. This evidence resonated with our findings highlighted in the health system assessment such as the need to improve the skills of the workforce.

Whereas in other countries the training for healthcare workers is well organized and online options are available [23, 24], there is a need in other low-and middle- income countries for that type of resource. In the literature some experiences in training of healthcare workers have been identified, one study in Tanzania trained mid-level healthcare workers in NCDs in Tanzania, the training was during 5 days and included diabetes, hypertension, sickle cell disease and rheumatic fever. The topics were based on the WHO Package of Essential Noncommunicable (PEN) Disease Interventions, as well as the evaluation, the scores improved by almost 4 points. However, the percentage of participants with adequate training in diabetes did not improve after the training [25]. Another study in training about diabetic retinopathy was conducted in Nepal, they delivered three sessions during 8 months to promote referral of patients for retinal screening, the evaluation showed that knowledge improved between those that received the sessions and the referral change from 50 to 95% [26]. Also, a study in Myanmar showed the effect of training healthcare workers in using digital tools for diabetes control and follow-up. The training was conducted during three days and it was focused on explaining two apps, one about diabetes guidelines from the country and a diabetes registry. The intervention group improved in knowledge and performance. Also, they identify that the intervention was cost-effective. All the presented studies showed improvement in healthcare workers’ knowledge and/or performance in LMICs, but they were mostly on-site training and they were not focused on insulin use.

Studies about insulin use had been reported in the UK, a study explored the workload of district nurses (healthcare workers that provide services to older people in diagnosis, treatment and medication for diabetes). 91% perceived that their workload has increased over the last 2 years and it is related to more insulin use, more referrals and lack of willingness to self-administer insulin [27]. Also, another intervention in the UK conducted with 6 months of follow-up provided support to district nurses by community diabetes specialist nurses with a focus on insulin use. They found a reduction in hypoglycemic and hyperglycemic events, as well as number of visits and use of acute hospital services with cost savings [28]. These studies from HIC highlight the need for better collaboration and training of healthcare workers to provide education and care to diverse populations with diabetes in need of specific care for insulin use. The current study proposed an approach to provide training in insulin use in LMIC. However, some aspects identified in the health system evaluation have to improve in order to achieve a high-quality care for people with diabetes.

Relevance for public health

Building capacity of healthcare workers is complicated due to high turnover in primary healthcare centers and for this reason virtual courses have the potential to support training because they can be replicated easily and scaled-up to other regions. However, in the case of insulin use and glucose measurement, practical sessions need to be added in order to acquire the skills and competences. Healthcare workers in Peru and other low- and middle- income countries lack a standardized continuous training program as well as protocols and regulations in this matter. The model proposed in this study is an opportunity that can be followed in LMIC to close the gaps in terms of training in diabetes care.

The findings of this study show that although the training intervention based on the Insulin Use Manual was helpful to provide healthcare workers with tools to deliver better care, issues related with the health system jeopardize the implementation. Decision makers and stakeholders need to work in other aspects related to access to insulin such as distribution of insulin to health facilities at the primary care level to improve its availability. Also, another aspect to be improved is related to access to care and service delivery for people with diabetes.

Based on the analysis across the WHO Six Building Blocks framework, these findings suggest several practical implications for strengthening diabetes care and the implementation of insulin training programs. Strengthening workforce capacity through periodic training that combines theoretical and hands-on components could help improve confidence in insulin prescription and administration. Ensuring a more consistent supply of insulin and related materials at the primary care level would reduce barriers to appropriate treatment. In addition, strengthening information systems to better identify and follow patients receiving insulin therapy could support more effective monitoring of diabetes care. Finally, reinforcing supervision and coordination between regional health authorities and health facilities may help support the sustained implementation of training initiatives and improve the quality of diabetes management.

Limitations and strengths

This study included a small region in Peru, this limited the representativeness of the findings to the country, but it is a good example of what can be achieved because we include a comprehensive evaluation of the implementation of the course and the evaluation of health system indicators. Recruitment relied on open dissemination through institutional networks and social media platforms (e.g., WhatsApp and Facebook), resulting in a non-probabilistic sample. This may have over-represented healthcare workers with greater digital access or higher motivation for training, introducing potential selection bias. Many healthcare workers registered in the course, but a small percentage began the course and completed it. Even when we have the support of the Regional Health Directorate, the participation in the virtual course and workshop was outside the working hours; this was a limitation for many participants but at the same time it shows the commitment of healthcare workers in terms of acquiring new knowledge and improving their skills.

Another limitation is the potential selection bias in the satisfaction survey, as only 40 participants completed it. This subgroup may not fully represent the views of all participants, and responses may have been biased toward those with greater motivation or more positive perceptions of the intervention. Additionally, although the study was originally designed to apply both the course and health system evaluations to the same healthcare workers and their patients, in practice the groups interviewed were different. Most patients were not under the care of the trained healthcare workers, which limited the possibility of linking perspectives directly. Furthermore, because formal psychometric reliability analyses were not conducted for the knowledge tests, improvements in pre–post scores should be interpreted as indicative of educational gains within this implementation context. Also, the relatively small number of participants who completed the course limited the possibility of conducting inferential statistical analyses, and findings should therefore be interpreted as descriptive and exploratory.

Despite these limitations, many of the challenges and facilitators identified are likely to be shared by other regions and countries with similar health system structures. Furthermore, the formative, multi-methods approach applied here can be replicated by researchers and decision-makers to inform the design and adaptation of future implementation strategies. Future research should evaluate the effectiveness of similar training interventions using larger samples and more structured implementation strategies, including blended learning approaches and stronger integration with routine health system supervision.

Conclusions

This study shows that specific training in insulin use was associated with improvements in knowledge among participating healthcare workers, even in resource-limited settings. Nevertheless, the low course completion rate and only modest improvements in evaluation scores suggest that voluntary virtual formats may face important challenges in sustaining engagement and achieving larger gains. In addition, qualitative findings revealed persistent structural barriers (such as poor availability of insulin at the primary care level, lack of ongoing supervision and training, poor information systems and high out-of-pocket costs) that hinder the effective implementation of diabetes management protocols. These gaps, identified in the WHO’s Six Building Blocks framework of the health system, highlight the need for a comprehensive strategy that combines continuous education with system strengthening. To support the sustainable implementation of insulin use in LMICs, it is essential to invest in infrastructure, human talent development, governance and equitable access to essential medicines.
