Changes in Frailty, Depression, and Loneliness among Stroke Survivors and Their Spouses
Authors: Niamh Rennie, Katie I. Gallacher, Terence J. Quinn, Peter Hanlon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionStroke often substantially impacts a person’s physical, psychological, and social wellbeing. The impact on carers and family can also be considerable, but is less often quantified. We aimed to measure changes in physical, psychological, and social health status (assessed using frailty, depression, and loneliness) before and following a stroke among survivors and their spouses compared to age- and sex-matched controls who had not experienced a stroke themselves or within their household.MethodsWe used data from three population longitudinal ageing surveys (Health and Retirement Survey [HRS], USA; Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe [SHARE]; and the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing [ELSA]). Participants aged ≥50 were surveyed at 2-yearly intervals along with their household. We identified people who had survived a stroke occurring after their initial visit (and so had data collected both pre- and post-stroke) as well as their spouses. Stroke participants were included regardless of whether they had a spouse or not. We also identified age-, sex-, and survey wave-matched controls and their spouses. We assessed frailty using a cumulative deficit frailty index, depression using the Centre for Epidemiological Studies Depression (CES-D) Scale or the Euro-Depression Scale, and loneliness using a single question (“do you feel lonely?”) – each measured at both time points. We assessed change in frailty and depression scores using (generalised) linear mixed regression models (with a logistic link function for loneliness) adjusted for age and sex.ResultsThere were 5,399 participants experiencing stroke and 3,013 spouses, 16,197 matched controls, and 8,249 control spouses. People with stroke had higher baseline (pre-stroke) levels of frailty, depression, and loneliness than matched controls. The average change in frailty index values following a stroke was greater than controls (3.17 deficits, 95% confidence interval [95% CI]: 3.03–3.31). Spouses of stroke survivors did not show significant changes in frailty. Both stroke survivors and their spouses, on average, showed increases in depression scores which were greater than controls (0.18 standard deviations increase [95% CI: 0.14–0.21] for stroke survivors versus controls and 0.12 [95% CI: 0.07–0.17] for spouses versus control spouses). The odds of becoming lonely were greatest for spouses of stroke survivors compared to control spouses (odds ratio 5.07 [95% CI: 2.74–9.41]); however, the changes in loneliness among stroke survivors were not significantly different from controls (1.35 [95% CI: 0.95–1.91]).ConclusionStroke does not just affect the individual, but it also affects families. The psychosocial sequalae of stroke should be proactively managed, with family actively involved in post-stroke support strategies.

Introduction

The physical sequelae of stroke are well known, but the psychosocial impacts of stroke on the individual and their support network are less well understood. Physical, psychological, and social wellbeing after a stroke are inextricably linked, with each potentially impacting the others and all contributing to critical impacts on quality of life and stroke recovery [1–3]. These holistic effects of stroke occur via a range of mechanisms, including direct physical impairments following stroke (e.g., movement, swallowing, coordination), the psychological impact of the stroke and its sequelae, the potential need for increased assistance with activities, increased frailty, and the impact of each of these on social functioning and connectedness [2, 4–6]. This can place increasing demands on the families of stroke survivors to provide assistance and support rehabilitation, who may themselves experience physical, psychological, and social sequalae as a result of caregiving duties [7, 8]. A recent national research priority setting exercise in the UK (the James Lind Alliance) identified assessment and management of the psychosocial impact of stroke and improving support for unpaid caregivers as research priorities [9].

Stroke research has tended to focus on hyperacute, acute, and rehabilitation strategies to improve stroke-related disability such as walking impairments or hand functioning. However, there has been growing interest in how the concept of frailty relates to stroke [5]. Frailty is “a decline in functioning across multiple physiological systems, accompanied by increased vulnerability to stressors associated with increased mortality, hospitalisation, falls, and admission to long-term care” [10]. Frailty is both a risk factor and a potential consequence of stroke, and frailty among stroke survivors is associated with higher rates of mortality, hospital admission, and recurrent stroke [11, 12]. Depression following a stroke is also common, with between a fifth to a third of stroke survivors experiencing depressive symptoms. Post-stroke depression is associated with poorer prognostic outcomes, increased rates of mortality, and poorer quality of life [13]. Frailty is closely associated with, but distinct from, social vulnerability – of which one important aspect is loneliness [13]. Stroke survivors experience elevated levels of loneliness following stroke compared with healthy individuals, but this has been under-researched [14]. Loneliness and depression are associated with increased risk of developing frailty [13, 15]. Previous studies have suggested strong links between loneliness, depression, and increased risk of frailty and stroke [13–16].

Long-term health conditions can impact the physical, psychological, and social wellbeing of spouses or unpaid caregivers, yet this has rarely been quantified in stroke [17, 18]. Spousal caregiving in older adults has been associated with accelerated frailty progression [19]. It is estimated that over 60% of stroke survivors require support from a paid or unpaid caregiver [20]. Previous research has shown that 87% of unpaid caregivers report one or more negative emotions such as stress, anxiety, or apprehension since becoming a caregiver [20]. Unpaid caring can have negative impacts on quality of life such as financial instability, role changes, independence loss, strained relationships, and mental health decline [21]. Stroke survivors experience varying circumstances and levels of functioning post-stroke. It has been previously observed that caregivers of stroke survivors who experience greater disability or emotional difficulties following their stroke and, caregivers who spend long periods of time caring with no respite, experience greater carer burden [21]. This study aimed to quantify changes in frailty, depression, and loneliness before and after stroke in both stroke survivors and their spouses, and to compare these changes to age/sex-matched controls who had not experienced a stroke and their spouses.

Method

Study Design and Participants

This is an observational study drawing upon data from three harmonised surveys of ageing: Health and Retirement Survey (HRS) from the USA [22], Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE) [23], and the English Longitudinal Survey of Ageing (ELSA) [24]. These surveys collect data from sampled households at 2-yearly intervals including individuals aged 50 and over.

For this study, we identified individuals who experienced a stroke after their initial assessment wave (so as to identify individuals for which data were available both before and after stroke). Stroke was identified either from self-reported survey data or from the cause of death data for individuals who had died during follow-up. We excluded individuals who reported having had a stroke at the first wave at which they were included (and therefore did not have any pre-stroke measures available). As data were collected from all eligible individuals within the household, we then identified the spouses of people who had experienced a stroke from their pre-stroke assessment wave. For the purposes of these analyses, it was assumed that the cohabiting spouse would assume the role of primary unpaid caregiver. We acknowledge that this may not be the case (e.g., the stroke survivor may require little additional care, may have a primary caregiver who is not their spouse, or may themselves provide care for a spouse who has their own care needs). As such, our analysis assesses spousal impact of stroke, rather than explicitly caregiver impact. People who experienced a stroke who did not have a spouse, or for whom spouse data were not available, were still included in the study.

For a comparison group, we identified three control participants for every participant who had experienced a stroke during follow-up. Controls were randomly sampled without replacement from eligible participants after excluding stroke survivors and matching on dataset, sampling wave, age (within a 3-year window), and sex. The matching strategy was intended to identify an age-, sex-, and time-comparable reference group rather than to achieve full covariate balance. After identifying the control group (age-sex-matched to stroke survivors), we then identified the spouses of the control participants (excluding any participants who had either experienced a stroke, their spouses, or participants who had already been selected as controls).

For all eligible participants, we assessed two time points. For participants experiencing a stroke, this was the wave immediately before the stroke occurred, and the first wave in which the stroke was reported (or in which they were recorded as having died from a stroke). This gives a 2-year window in which the stroke could have occurred. We did not have data on the precise timing of the stroke, meaning that the time interval between the stroke and the post-stroke measures could be any interval up to 2 years and will have varied between individuals. Data on spouses were taken from these same time points. Data on controls and their spouses were taken from the wave matched to the pre-stroke wave and the following wave (2 years later).

Measures

Age and sex data were taken from the first time point (pre-stroke or the first time point for matched controls). Frailty was assessed using the cumulative deficit frailty index (FI). The FI is a measure that quantifies frailty by counting the number of “health deficits” an individual has, divided by the total number of items that have been measured, giving an overall score between 0 and 1. Higher values indicate a greater degree of frailty. Deficits include chronic conditions, symptoms, signs, and functional limitations, selected from the items available within a given dataset providing they meet specified criteria (increasing prevalence with age, not being ubiquitous within the population of interest, and being associated with poor health). For this study, we used a 41-item FI using variables that were available and harmonised between all three of the included surveys, which we had constructed for a previous analysis of frailty in stroke survivors [11]. Frailty was modelled as a numerical variable. For visualising changes in frailty, we also categorised as robust (FI <0.12), mild (FI 0.12 to <0.24), moderate (FI 0.24 to <0.36), and severe (FI ≥0.36).

Depression was measured using validated symptom scales embedded within each of the respective surveys: a shortened version on the Centre for Epidemiological Studies Depression (CES-D) scale [25] in HRS and ELSA and the Euro-Depression (Euro-D) scale in SHARE [26]. These scales contained eight and twelve items, respectively. For each instrument, responses were scored according to standard procedures and summed to create a total symptom count. Because the instruments differ in length and item composition, raw scores are not directly comparable across surveys. To facilitate harmonised analyses, depression scores were therefore standardised within each questionnaire by dividing individual scores by the cohort-specific standard deviation. These standardised scores represent relative depressive symptom burden (in SD units) and allow comparison of changes over time and differences between groups across datasets, without assuming equivalence of absolute scale thresholds.

Loneliness was assessed by a single question. Participants were asked whether they felt lonely and answered “yes”/“no.” For this outcome, data were only available from HRS and ELSA as loneliness was not asked as part of the core SHARE survey in all waves.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline participant demographics were summarised for age, sex, frailty, depression, and loneliness. These measures were taken from the pre-stroke values to assess the baseline levels for the participants.

To assess the effect of stroke on the FI and on depression scores, we used linear mixed-effects models. A random-effect for each participant to account for repeated observations in the same individual was used, and a fixed-effect to assess the association between covariates (stroke status [stroke/spouse/control], time point, age, and sex) and the outcome. To assess how changes in the outcome over time varied between stroke survivors, their spouses, and the control participants, we fitted an interaction term between stroke status and time point. For loneliness, in which the outcome was a binary variable, we used generalised linear mixed-effects model with a logistic link function. This allowed a logistic regression model to be fitted for loneliness status with a random effect accounting for repeated observations within individuals. As the estimate of interest was the change over time within individuals, models were limited to those participants with complete data at both time points for the outcome of interest (i.e., complete case analysis). Matched controls were used as the reference category for the effect on stroke survivors. To assess the impact on spouses of stroke survivors, we reran the models with control spouses as the reference category.

Discussion

We observed important effects of stroke on levels of loneliness, depression, and frailty for stroke survivors and their spouses. Stroke survivors experienced increased frailty and depression post-stroke compared to pre-stroke, but similar results were not identified for loneliness. However, stroke survivors were more likely to report loneliness and depression pre-stroke compared to the non-stroke population. Spouses of stroke survivors were considerably more likely to experience loneliness and depression after their spouse had a stroke, but frailty was not increased. These findings highlight that experiencing stroke, or caring for someone who has experienced stroke, impacts physical, mental, and social wellbeing. A holistic approach to rehabilitation is therefore essential when addressing the rehabilitation of an individual who has experienced a stroke and the collateral impact on their spouse to allow a comprehensive recovery.

Other studies which examined frailty post-stroke have shown that it tends to be more common in stroke survivors than in the general population (consistent with our findings) [27]. Pre-stroke frailty is also highly prevalent – with a pooled prevalence of 24.6% in a systematic review of 14 studies – and is associated with greater risk of adverse outcomes including mortality and disability post-stroke [12]. Consistent with the broader literature, our study showed a high prevalence of frailty in those who went on to experience a stroke, with a deterioration in frailty following the stroke itself [28]. The changes in frailty over time in all groups (affected and unaffected by stroke) reflect the dynamic nature of frailty more generally and are consistent with others’ observations that frailty may improve as well as worsen (although improvement in the group experiencing a stroke was rare within the timescale of our study) [25].

We are not aware of any previous studies that had examined frailty in stroke caregivers or spouses. There is emerging evidence in older people that increased frailty progression can be linked to higher intensity caregiving by cohabiting spouses and that this reduces the spouse’s time for respite and recovery [19, 26]. Spouses may also experience a psychological impact from caregiver burden, financial strain, and concern for their partner’s health and wellbeing [29]. Furthermore, they too are at increased risk of social isolation and loneliness due to reduced opportunities for socialising and self-care [30].

Depression increased in both stroke survivors and their spouses post-stroke, and people who had a stroke were more likely to report depression pre-stroke than matched controls. Previous research shows that depression among stroke survivors is associated with reduced odds of returning to work, lower quality of life, lower physical functioning, cognitive impairment, and with higher mortality [31]. A systematic review of twelve studies also described a consistently high prevalence of depressive symptoms (40%) and anxiety symptoms (21%) among caregivers of stroke survivors, although depression was lower among spousal and child caregivers than others [32].

Another interesting finding was that spouses of those experiencing a stroke were more likely to become lonely, but not stroke survivors. We are not aware of any previous quantitative studies that measured loneliness in spouses of stroke survivors. While our analyses do not allow definitive analysis of the mechanisms for this, there are several possible explanations. Stroke survivors may experience increased professional and social contact and a change of social expectations after stroke. Spouses may simultaneously face reduced social participation, caregiving burden, and loss of reciprocal companionship, increasing their risk of loneliness. Our findings resonate with a range of studies that have explored the experience of spouses and caregivers, who often experience difficulties around the changing dynamic of the relationship, conflicting roles, and often describe a lack of available support [33]. One large-scale study in the UK found an association between stroke and loneliness, despite accounting for objective measures of social isolation such as living alone [34]. Our findings are consistent with this previous work, but also indicate that higher levels of loneliness pre-stroke may account for at least some of this phenomenon. Our findings reflect the wider literature on the experience of caregiving for stroke survivors and highlight the need for better support for caregivers and spouses at each point of their partners’ rehabilitation. Furthermore, findings emphasise that along with practical and training support for people supporting caregivers, professionals should be alert to the development of social isolation and its impact on both stroke survivors and their support networks.

As well as the changes in frailty, depression, and loneliness described, our study also demonstrated that people who went on to experience a stroke already had higher levels of frailty, depression, and loneliness than similar aged people from the same time period and countries. They also had higher degrees of baseline comorbidity, risk factors such as smoking, and (on average) relatively lower socio-economic position. This reflects well-established epidemiological risk factors for stroke, but is important in this context both individual and structural factors such as socio-economic deprivation may influence not only the likelihood of experiencing frailty, depression, or loneliness, but the means by which individuals and families can respond to these types of adversity. For example, socio-economic inequalities among stroke survivors may influence their ability to access additional care; lower health literacy may negatively influence ability to access or engage with psychological therapies (particularly where these are self-guided). Our findings should therefore be interpreted within the wider context in which people experience stroke, and the individual and structural challenges that they face.

Strengths of this study include a large sample size increasing the precision of results. Furthermore, it draws on data from multiple different contexts across multiple different countries from different years. A strength of this study was the availability of data both before and after the occurrence of a stroke. However, due to the design of the surveys and the nature of our secondary analysis, we were unable to specify when in the intervening 2-year period the stroke had occurred. Our findings may therefore be skewed towards chronic (rather than acute) effects of stroke although we are unable to disentangle these within the available data. A limitation of this study is that for assessment of loneliness variable, data from the SHARE survey were unavailable. For the depression variable, access was only available to the shortened version of the CES-D/Euro-D scores available. Therefore, this study may not represent the full spectrum of depressive symptoms and, thus, underestimate the number of participants with depressive illness. Furthermore, these shortened questionnaires lack established threshold for minimal clinically important differences. Despite this, we would highlight that the differences between stroke survivors or their spouses and controls in terms of depression score change were small in magnitude (<0.2 standard deviations). Loneliness was assessed using a single yes/no question, due to the nature of the question asked, which is a relatively narrow sub-question within validated loneliness scales. Furthermore, it only assesses explicit rather than implicit loneliness. Our loneliness analysis was also limited to the UK and USA, as this question was not consistently asked in SHARE, which may limit the generalisability of these findings. The FI used in the surveys (HRS, ELSA, SHARE) was designed to be harmonised and comparable across all three datasets, but this reduced the total number of variables that could be included. Finally, while the inclusion of data on spouses is a strength, we lacked data on whether the spouse was the main caregiver or on the impact on other members of a survivor’s household or support network who may also be impacted by the occurrence of a stroke.

Our findings reinforce the need for holistic stroke rehabilitation that incorporates physical, mental, and social wellbeing for stroke survivors and their families. Patients with complex support needs require complex and comprehensive support. Investigation into interventions preventing or managing poor mental health in stroke survivors and their spouses would allow provision of optimal, evidence-based, and comprehensive stroke rehabilitation services. Furthermore, investigation into better support for caregivers and spouses of stroke survivors could reduce feelings of loneliness. To address these issues, increased funding into stroke research, services, and support for caregivers is necessary to make progress.

In conclusion, stroke survivors often experience declines in physical, psychological, and social wellbeing, and their spouses may be even more likely to experience a decline in social wellbeing. Support for stroke survivors should address physical, psychological, and social health as well as assessing and supporting the needs of spouses and caregivers. Stroke survivors and their families have complex needs and require a multifaceted approach to rehabilitation to ensure that each stroke survivor receives comprehensive rehabilitation.
