Middle Fossa Craniotomy With Hydroxyapatite Cement as a Structural Repair for Noniatrogenic Cranial Cerebrospinal Fluid Leaks: A Case Series
Authors: Kelly Shaftel, Abdullah Al Tekreeti, Kristen Angster, Laura Brainard, Karam Asmaro, Jack Rock
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVES:Cranial cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leaks due to tegmen defects can be repaired through transmastoid or transcranial surgical approaches with different repair substrates, including autologous grafts or nonimmunogenic cements. Here, we describe a case series of patients who underwent middle fossa craniotomy for repair of noniatrogenic cranial CSF leak with the use of hydroxyapatite (HA) bone cement.METHODS:A retrospective analysis of all patients who underwent craniotomy with HA cement for repair of noniatrogenic cranial CSF leak at Henry Ford Hospital in Detroit between January 1, 2013, and September 30, 2024. Patient demographic features, presenting symptoms, location of cranial defect, comorbid conditions, and the outcomes of hospital length-of-stay and leak recurrence within 3-6 month follow-up were collected.RESULTS:HA cement middle fossa craniotomy repair of noniatrogenic cranial CSF leak was performed in 15 patients (11 women [73%] and 4 men [27%]). The median age was 56 years (range, 29-77), and the mean body mass index was 35.7 ± 8.8 kg/m2. Most patients presented with hearing loss (n = 12; 80%) and/or encephalocele (n = 12; 80%), and more than half (n = 8; 53%) had otorrhea. There were 8 patients (53%) with obstructive sleep apnea, and only 3 (20%) had idiopathic intracerebral hypertension. Defects were located within various cranial regions, with most patients (n = 9; 60%) having a defect in the tegman tympani. The median hospital length of stay was 3 days (range, 1-28), and no patients had leak recurrence within the follow-up period.CONCLUSION:Using HA bone cement for middle fossa craniotomy to repair cranial CSF leaks due to tegmen defects led to durable repair with no leak recurrence, suggesting that this approach may be highly effective for temporal bone repair.

Cranial cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leaks can occur due to various causes, including maxillofacial trauma, erosive malignancies, or spontaneous defects in the cranial floor. Importantly, these leaks can lead to progressive thinning of the cranial table and potentially result in CSF fistula formation or meningoencephalic herniation, which can significantly increase the risk of meningitis.1-3 When these leaks arise in the middle cranial fossa, they often manifest as otorrhea accompanied by conductive hearing loss.3,4 Although conservative nonsurgical therapies, such as acetazolamide to decrease CSF production, may have some benefit for CSF leaks, surgical intervention remains the gold standard method for definitive leak closure.

Surgical repair of CSF leaks typically involves a combined transmastoid and transcranial approach, often with middle fossa craniotomy.3-6 Historically, the first materials used to directly seal CSF leaks were autologous grafts; however, success rates varied with this approach.7,8 For example, 1 study of 44 patients who underwent CSF leak repair with autologous temporalis fascia grafts showed that while 82% of the patients had their presenting otorrhea resolved, 4 patients experienced infection or wound dehiscence that required further surgery. Thus, alternative materials for direct leak closure have been used, including hydroxyapatite (HA) cement, which shares the nonimmunogenic characteristic of autologous grafts.3,7 In fact, HA cement has long been a preferred material for multiple otolaryngology procedures, including for reconstructive middle ear repair and the creation of preformed ossicular prostheses. Importantly, HA cement has also been used successfully in temporal bone craniotomy, with low postoperative complication rates. The rapid hardening and biocompatibility of HA cement make this material a versatile and safe compound for otologic repair, craniotomy, and other reparative surgical procedures.9-11

Because HA cement has been used successfully in temporal bone craniotomies for various neuro-otologic indications, our institution has been using this material for the surgical repair of CSF leaks in the middle cranial fossa. Here, we present a case series of 15 patients with CSF leaks from tegmen defects who underwent a middle fossa approach for leak repair with the use of HA bone cement. Our aim was to comprehensively describe the varied clinical features of patients who had cranial CSF leaks repaired with HA cement, including their range of presenting symptoms, as well as some key outcomes, such as postoperative hospital length-of-stay and rate of leak recurrence. Overall, our series highlights the advantages of HA cement for repairing tegmen defects and strongly suggests that this approach is well-suited for repairing noniatrogenic cranial CSF leaks.

METHODS

Patient Selection

We conducted a retrospective, descriptive case series of all patients who underwent open craniotomy with the HA cement approach at Henry Ford Hospital (Detroit) between January 1, 2013, and September 30, 2024, for repair of noniatrogenic CSF leaks caused by tegmen defects. Patients who had CSF leaks that had resulted from surgery for tumor removal were excluded. CSF leak diagnoses were confirmed via presence of persistent clear otorrhea and/or rhinorrhea (with and without beta-2 transferrin fluid sample positivity) and a tegmen defect diagnosed on high-resolution, thin-cut temporal bone computed tomography (CT). Tegmen defects and the presence of associated encephaloceles were evaluated via preoperative MRI or CT imaging in all patients before surgery, and all defects were easily identified during surgery before repair. All patients underwent a process of consent before undergoing the neurosurgical procedures described. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Henry Ford Health, and the need for patient consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study (IRB #18472). This case series was developed in alignment with the Preferred Reporting of Case Series in Surgery Guidelines.12

The following data were manually extracted from the medical record: age, sex, body mass index (BMI), presenting symptoms, type of leak (acquired, spontaneous, or congenital), location of defect, comorbidities (obstructive sleep apnea and idiopathic intracerebral hypertension), postoperative complications (if noted), postoperative hospital length-of-stay, and presence of recurrent leak at follow-up clinical visits 3 to 6 months after surgery. Idiopathic intracerebral hypertension was diagnosed based on clinical suspicion in patients presenting with symptoms (headache, tinnitus) supported by ophthalmologic examination for papilledema and neuroimaging findings, with diagnosis confirmed by lumbar puncture demonstrating elevated opening pressure. MRI and CT imaging findings were also reviewed for ancillary clinical details. Data were compiled with descriptive statistics as counts and percentages for categorical data and mean with SD or median and range for continuous data.

Operative Technique

All patients underwent a standard temporal or middle fossa craniotomy to access the tegmen region of the petrous bone. In brief, the craniotomy provided the extradural exposure needed to identify the CSF leak origin site and to remove encephalocele when present. Once the defect site was located and cleared of meningocele and/or encephalocele, a piece of gelfoam was placed in the defect to provide a barrier between the ossicles and the subsequent HA cement graft (OtoMimix; Olympus) to prevent ossicles from being fixated by the graft. The HA cement was placed into the defect while wet, molded to the level of adjacent bone, and allowed to harden (approximately 6 minutes), thus effectively sealing the CSF fistula. Dural graft matrix (DuraGen, Integra LifeSciences) was subsequently placed over the dural defect, and tenting sutures were then placed in the dura before closing the craniotomy site. For 11 patients with elevated intracranial pressure, a lumbar drain was intraoperatively placed to allow controlled CSF drainage and reduce intracranial pressure, thus allowing placement and setting of the HA cement. This approach was needed due to the high rate of idiopathic intracerebral hypertension in this subset of patients. The drains were removed at the end of surgery. Detailed photographic images illustrating the use of HA cement are available in 2 previous reports of this procedure.3,13

RESULTS

There were 15 patients who underwent middle fossa craniotomy for repair of a noniatrogenic CSF leak secondary to a tegmen defect within the petrous bone, including 11 women (73%) and 4 men (27%). Ages ranged from 29 to 77 years, with a median of 56 years. The mean BMI was 35.7 ± 8.8 kg/m2.There were 5 patients (33%) with an acquired leak, 9 (60%) with a spontaneous leak, and 1 (7%) with a congenital leak due to semicircular canal dehiscence (SSCD). All 9 patients who had a spontaneous CSF leak were female, and they had a mean BMI of 36.6 kg/m2. Of these 9 patients, 7 had an elevated BMI>30 kg/m2, which is considered within the class 1 or higher obesity range. Patients presented with a range of signs and symptoms, including 12 patients (80%) who had an encephalocele identified on preoperative imaging. The most common symptoms were hearing loss in 12 (80%) patients and otorrhea in 8 (53%) patients. There were 8 patients (53%) with obstructive sleep apnea, and of those individuals, 3 patients were also found to have idiopathic intracerebral hypertension (Table 1).

The postoperative median hospital length-of-stay was 3 days (range, 1-28). One patient experienced a 28-day hospital stay after seizure and occipital stroke complicated by a secondary pneumonia infection. However, these complications were determined to be caused by preexisting conditions and not the HA cement or the surgical procedure. There were 2 patients who had previously undergone transmastoid CSF leak repair but required the current procedure due to recurrent otorrhea and diminished hearing. All 15 patients showed no signs of recurrent leak at 3-to-6-month follow-up (Table 1). In addition, no postoperative infections were observed within the follow-up period.

Patient-specific details are presented in Table 2. Tegmen defects were seen in a range of locations, with tegmen tympani defect being the most common, as seen in 10 patients (67%). However, these defects were often associated with other surrounding dehiscence and were multifocal in nature. Of the 3 patients (20%) who had defects involving the superior semicircular canal, all 3 had hearing loss. There were 5 patients (33%), 3 male and 2 female, who had an acquired leak, 4 being due to long-standing, chronic otitis media and 1 caused by nonsurgical trauma. Although 12 patients had presented with hearing loss and 12 had encephalocele on imaging, the 2 symptoms were not necessarily seen together in the same patients. There were 9 patients (60%) who presented with both hearing loss and encephalocele, whereas 3 patients had encephalocele with no hearing loss, and 3 had hearing loss with no evidence of encephalocele. The youngest patient was a 29-year-old man who had a congenital defect with SSCD, and he was the only patient who reported experiencing vertigo and sensitivity to sound. The oldest patient was a 77-year-old woman who had an acquired leak due to otitis media (Table 2).

Illustrative Case

A 55-year-old woman with a medical history of rectal cancer status post-chemoradiation presented with right ear fullness. Physical examination showed acute maxillary sinusitis. She was initially treated with once-daily azithromycin for 14 days; however, symptoms persisted and upon return to the clinic for further evaluation 5 months later, she was treated with another 7-day course of oral amoxicillin plus ciprofloxacin ear drops, also without resolution of symptoms. Not only did the ear fullness remain but she also started developing progressive hearing loss and recurrent popping and crackling sensations in the ear. Acoustic MRI showed a large dehiscence of the tegmen with meningoencephalocele extending inferiorly to the level of the semicircular canals (Figure 1). She underwent open craniotomy via middle fossa approach for extradural removal of the encephalocele and repair of the tegmen defect and CSF leak with the HA cement technique (Figure 2). The patient was discharged 2 days after surgery. The middle ear effusion was much improved, with no evidence of recurrent leak at a 3-month follow-up visit in otolaryngology.

DISCUSSION

In this case series of 15 patients who underwent craniotomy with HA cement-based repair of noniatrogenic CSF leak, we described the main clinical features of a group of patients who all had successful procedures with no leak recurrence within 3 to 6 months of surgery. Notably, our patients presented with a range of symptoms, mostly otorrhea and hearing loss, and only 1 patient had serious postoperative complications, which were due to preexisting conditions. Although most of our patients had defects within the tegmen tympani, 20% of the patients, interestingly, had defects involving SSCD. This is a rarer defect; however, our findings corroborate a previous study that showed 21% of patients with SSCD had presented with an isolated CSF leak.14 Overall, our institutional experience shows that using HA cement to repair cranial CSF leaks in a range of patients is not only feasible, but leads to beneficial outcomes with a minimal need for hospital stay.

The development of biocompatible, rapid annealing, and safe materials has expanded the surgical options for CSF leak repair.3,10,11,15 Although autologous grafts offer a surgical approach that minimizes the inflammatory response, grafts have been associated with a nontrivial risk of infection.8 However, HA cement, developed in the 1980s, offers a novel, nonimmunogenic material that can be molded into a properly sized plug for CSF leak repair.16 The original preparation had some ease-of-use advantages, such as requiring water to form the paste; but these early forms had slow setting times of 20 to 60 minutes, meaning that the integrity of the structure could be compromised if the surgical field was not completely dry.15-17 Later, a fast-setting HA cement was formulated, offering rapid setting times of 5-8 minutes and reducing many risks that might lead to the need for repeat surgery.15,16 Subsequently, sodium citrate was added to the formulation to improve the ease of use, shorten the setting time, and increase lattice strength.18 Overall, these longitudinal improvements have collectively resulted in a new material for repairing middle fossa erosions.

In this case series, we investigated the clinical trajectories of patients who underwent craniotomy CSF leak repair with an HA and sodium citrate cement mix and only a 6-minute setting time (name brand OtoMimix). This HA cement led to a 100% success rate at 3-6-month follow-up visits, and the procedures led to no direct complications. Similarly, in a retrospective study examining surgical repair of tegmen CSF leaks, none of the 24 patients who underwent surgery with an injectable sodium citrate formulated HA cement (HydroSet, Stryker) had any postoperative complications or short-term recurrent leaks, whereas 2 of the 21 patients who underwent surgery without HA cement had a recurrent leak.13 The effectiveness of HA cement for preventing subsequent leaks may be due to the porous nature of the matrix, which allows for bone ingrowth within the HA cement lattice.10,16

Other HA cement formulations and non-HA cements that have been used in ossicular reconstruction include carbon-based components, such as polymethylmethacrylate, cyanoacrylates, and glass ionomers.10 However, the chemical properties of some of these substrates can pose challenges, particularly due to their high exothermic polymerization reactions. For example, reactions in formulations containing polymethylmethacrylate may reach upward of 110 °C, which could contribute to complications such as a poor osseointegration, proinflammatory activation, and toxicity.9,17 In 1 animal study comparing HA against a methyl-methacrylate control, all animals undergoing methylmethacrylate repair showed evidence of foreign body giant-cell formation and fibrous encapsulation of implants.18 These potential complications associated with polymethylmethacrylate make these formulations suboptimal for cranial bone repair.

However, in addition to the quality of the biocompatible polymer, the surgical approach is also an important consideration for successful CSF leak repair. One study that evaluated the middle fossa craniotomy with HA cement approach to repair CSF leaks suggested that this procedure offers a wider exploration of the cranial table than the transmastoid approach and may provide a more complete treatment of the thinning bone without increasing the risk of hearing loss.13 In addition, most of the patients in the study had numerous sites of dehiscence located in areas that the transmastoid approach could not effectively reach, highlighting another benefit to middle fossa craniotomy.13 In our study, 3 patients presented with dehiscence outside of the working field of a transmastoid approach; however, this was not a limitation, as we performed only middle fossa craniotomy. We note that a combined middle fossa craniotomy and transmastoid approach could theoretically provide more thorough inspection of the cranial floor. One study that evaluated patients who had tegmen defects repaired with either single or a combined approach found no significant differences in hospital length-of-stay or postoperative CSF fistula formation between the 3 surgical strategies; although the highest frequency of postoperative CSF fistulae were seen in patients who had the combined approach.3 Overall, few studies have deeply explored the benefits and risks of the singular vs combined approaches; therefore, patients should be evaluated on a case-by-case basis and offered the surgical approach that would provide the best access to the location of the defect.

HA cement has also been used for craniotomy repair procedures following retrosigmoid and translabyrjnthine approaches, with favorable outcomes and minimal complications. For instance, 1 case series describing several suboccipital retrosigmoid cranioplasties reconstructed with HA cement reported only 1 wound infection attributed to a dural substitute and no postoperative CSF leaks, supporting the safety and feasibility of this approach.19 Similarly, in translabyrinthine approaches for vestibular schwannomas, HA cement cranioplasty has shown low complication rates of approximately 3.8% for CSF leak and about 5.8% for infection, representing a viable alternative to traditional abdominal fat graft closure while avoiding donor-site morbidity associated with fat harvesting.20 In fact, a recent matched cohort study showed that patients undergoing HA cement closure after translabyrinthine craniotomy had significantly lower postoperative pain scores, reduced opioid requirements, and shorter hospital stays than patients who had fat graft cranioplasty, with similarly low rates of CSF leakage between groups.21

Notably, the increasing prevalence of spontaneous CSF leaks has been linked to rising rates of obesity, particularly in female patients, with the leaks potentially being caused by the idiopathic intracerebral hypertension associated with higher BMI.5,22,23 Elevated intracranial pressure is thought to contribute to cranial bone erosion and ectopic displacement of the meninges, thus leading to CSF leak.3,13,22 In addition, obstructive sleep apnea associated with elevated BMI may also lead to increased intracerebral pressure that could lead to CSF leak.3,5,13,22,23 Interestingly, an ophthalmologic study showed that patients with intracerebral hypertension and associated spontaneous CSF leak did not have associated papilledema, likely due to the nature of the CSF flow diversion through the tegmen defect.21 The study showed significantly higher intracranial pressure measurements after CSF leak repair, which can lead to future papilledema if the intracranial hemorrhage is not subsequently addressed.24 In our study, over half of our patients had obstructive sleep apnea, and several female patients had elevated BMI and intracerebral hypertension, supporting the association between these conditions and risk of cranial CSF leak. Larger studies using reliable real-world datasets might shed more light on the connection between these conditions, which would be helpful for developing risk stratification tools for patients who might be at risk of spontaneous CSF leaks. This study suggests that HA cement is highly effective for repairing noniatrogenic skull base CSF leaks. Our patients who had HA bone cement skull base reconstruction had minimal hospital stays and, in our experience, no recurrence of leaks.

Importantly, no patients in our study developed postoperative infection, and this complication has been reported infrequently even in larger cohort studies, which have noted infection rates of about 6% and rates of wound dehiscence with CSF leak at approximately 2%.8,17 However, the risk of infection has been attributed more to the invasive nature of the procedure rather than to the HA cement being a nidus of infection.17

Limitations

This case series primary purpose is to demonstrate the effectiveness of HA cement for tegmen defect CSF repair; however, it there are some limitations worth noting. This series only includes 15 patients, making it a small sample size. Furthermore, given the retrospective nature of the study, there wasn't a consistent or standardized follow-up protocol. While this case series does suggest a reliable option for skull base CSF leak repair, further research with a prospective study would be necessary to validate this data.

CONCLUSION

Middle fossa craniotomy with HA cement has been successfully used at our institution for over a decade for repairing noniatrogenic cranial CSF leaks due to tegmen defects in patients of various ages presenting with a range of symptoms—most prominently hearing loss and otorrhea. In our study, no patients experienced recurrent leaks, most patients had a very short postoperative hospital length of stay, and no surgically related complications were encountered, highlighting the effectiveness and utility of craniotomy with HA cement for temporal bone repair.
