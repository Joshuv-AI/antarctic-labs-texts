Host tree availability shapes potential distribution of a target epiphytic moss species more than direct climate effects
Authors: Adrian Wysocki, Sylwia Wierzcholska, Jarosław Proćków, Kamil Konowalik
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractClimate change significantly impacts the distribution of woody plants, indirectly influencing the dynamics of entire ecosystems. Understanding species' varied responses to the environment and their reliance on biotic interactions is crucial for predicting the global changes' impact on woodland biodiversity. Our study focusses on Dicranum viride, a moss of conservation priority, and its dependence on specific phorophytes (host trees). Using species distribution modelling (SDM) techniques, we initially modelled its distribution using climate-only variables. As a novel approach, we also modelled the distribution of the main phorophyte species and incorporated them into D. viride SDM alongside climate data. Finally, we analysed the overlap of climatic and geographic niches between the epiphyte and the phorophytes. Inclusion of biotic interactions significantly improved model performance, with phorophyte availability emerging as the primary predictor. This underscores the significance of epiphyte-phorophyte interactions, supported by substantial niche overlap. Predictions indicate a potential decline in the suitability of most of the current areas for D. viride, with noticeable shifts towards the northern regions of Europe. Our study underscores the importance of incorporating biotic interactions into SDMs, especially for dependent organisms. Understanding such connections is essential to implement successful conservation strategies and adapt forest management practices to environmental changes.Keywords: Bryophytes, Biotic interactions, Climate change, Forest management, Nature conservation, Species distribution modelSubject terms: Ecological modelling, Climate-change ecology, Forest ecology, Conservation biology, Plant ecology

Introduction

Ongoing climate changes are initiating a series of new processes and phenomena influencing the distribution of woody plants1–3. Increased summer temperatures, prolonged drought periods, and declining groundwater levels are recognised as the most important factors that affect the distribution of plants and their communities4,5. Trees, establishing epiphytic habitats and facilitating the development of dependent organisms, constitute a particularly important role in shaping landscape biodiversity6–10. In the temperate climate zone, dynamic alterations in the distribution of native tree species are observed as a result of climate change, along with changes in land use11. Many tree species are experiencing the consequences of prolonged droughts and are struggling to adapt to changing climate12–14. Assessing the potential availability of forest habitats in Europe in response to climate change underscores the documented adverse effects on ecologically and economically significant tree species15–18. Therefore, assessing tree distribution under the impact of climate change is becoming an excellent tool in forest management and nature conservation1,19–22.

Epiphytic bryophytes, a significant group influencing forest biodiversity, are fundamental to the flora of mature forests23–25. Due to their physiological dependence on water regimes, bryophytes are prone to changes in drought frequency, shifts in air humidity, and alterations in water availability, making them particularly susceptible to the impacts of climate change26,27. Despite their physiological adaptations and effective dispersal mechanisms, bryophytes may still face challenges in adapting to changing climate conditions28,29. Recent studies using species distribution models (SDMs) have provided valuable insight into how climate change could affect the distribution of epiphytic bryophytes. These models suggest that shifts in climate variables, such as temperature and precipitation, could lead to significant changes in habitats suitable for these species30–32. Projections indicate potential northward migrations and the contraction of suitable habitats in current ranges29,31,33–35. This highlights the importance of integrating climate change projections into conservation strategies for epiphytic bryophytes, allowing a better understanding of future habitat shifts and identification of potential refuges or high-risk areas36.

Climate-induced changes impacting tree species significantly affect dependent epiphytes37,38. Therefore, predicting the distribution of native phorophytes (living trees hosting epiphytes) and epiphytic bryophytes dependent on their presence is a novel challenge in determining the future suitable habitats of this ecologically significant group of plants. Understanding the interplay between phorophytes and epiphytic bryophytes under changing climatic conditions is crucial for conservation of biodiversity. Exploring these complex ecological relationships and dependencies can lead to more accurate predictions of their distribution patterns. Recent advances in predictive modelling techniques, enhanced by incorporating biotic interactions, have shown promising results39–44. These models consider not only climatic variables but also ecological interactions that govern the presence and distribution of target species. This holistic approach can contribute to more effective conservation strategies and a better understanding of the dynamics within this unique ecological group of plants.

Among European epiphytic bryophytes, Dicranum viride (Sull. & Lesq.) Lindb. (Dicranaceae), a target species sensu Kiehl et al.45, is of significant importance for conservation efforts. Protected under the Bern Convention since 1979 (listed in Annex I on September 19, 1979), and later included in the EU Habitats Directive in 1992 (listed in Annex II of Council Directive 92/43/EEC), it is also categorised as “V” (Vulnerable) in the “Red List of European Bryophytes”46. In Europe, this moss predominantly inhabits near-natural deciduous and mixed forests within the temperate climate zone, showing a preference for beech, oak-hornbeam and riparian forests47–49. Dicranum viride typically grows at the base of living deciduous trees, particularly Fagus sylvatica and Carpinus betulus6,10,50. It benefits from the stable microclimate provided by microhabitats, especially at the trunks of large-diameter trees10,47,51–53. Despite its microhabitat requirements, forest management practices such as the preservation of veteran trees, regional connectivity, and continuous forest stands contribute to its presence10,54–57. Although D. viride has global ecological importance in biodiversity conservation and forest management, comprehensive knowledge about the primary factors that influence its distribution remains scarce and limited58.

Our research puts forth three hypotheses. First, we hypothesise that the potential distribution of D. viride in Europe will change in the future, showing noticeable range shifts toward northern regions. Second, we hypothesise that models that integrate climate data and the phorophyte availability outperform those that depend solely on climate. Third, we hypothesise that the availability of tree phorophytes is the most significant predictor of the occurrence of D. viride, supported by the high overlap of their niches.

Materials and methods

Target species

Dicranum viride is an epiphytic moss that thrives in subcontinental mountainous regions and is prevalent in areas in the northern hemisphere, spanning central and northern Europe, the Caucasus, northern and eastern Asia, as well as eastern parts of North America54,59,60. This moss primarily propagates asexually through leaf fragments, resulting in a limited dispersal capacity53. This life strategy demonstrates its adaptability, as D. viride employs various strategies to inhabit the available niches10. Protection of this moss species is particularly challenging57, as evidenced by its unfavourable conservation status in most regions of Europe61. The high conservation value of D. viride, along with its role as an indicator of mature forests and transformations in nemoral landscapes, have led to its recognition as a target species.

Occurrence data

We used a combined dataset from various sources to gather information on the European distribution of D. viride. These sources include the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF) database (www.gbif.org), a database of D. viride distributions in the Czech Republic (provided by Julie Jandová), as well as our own observations and data from the relevant literature (Table S1).

After conducting a comprehensive analysis of the collected data, we summarised the information pertaining to the phorophyte species upon which D. viride was observed. Out of 1,553 recorded observations, 772 contained accurate species-level information. From these accurate species-level occurrences, we identified tree species with occurrence counts above average as preferred by D. viride. The average occurrence count was calculated based on the total number of species-level accurate phorophyte records available divided by the number of tree species (Table S2). The selected phorophytes, Fagus sylvatica L., Fraxinus excelsior L., Tilia cordata Mill., and Carpinus betulus L., were chosen because their occurrence counts were above average and they had the highest number of occurrences with D. viride, which were 384 (50%), 108 (14%), 104 (13%) and 57 (7%) records, respectively. We acquired the occurrence data for each species from the GBIF database (www.gbif.org). These specific tree species were considered to describe their potential impact on the distribution of the studied epiphytic moss on the European continent.

Prior to their utilisation in the species distribution modelling the occurrences of each species underwent a three-stage filtering process. In the first stage, considering the high resolution of the selected climatic layers (30 arc seconds, ~ 1 km), only locations with accuracy greater than the raster resolution were used. In the second stage, to prevent model overfitting and environmental bias, occurrences within a single raster cell were consolidated into one point22,62. In the final stage, environmental filtering was implemented, a method proven more effective than geographical filtering63–65. For this purpose, we used the algorithm provided by Varela et al., which is available at https://github.com/SaraVarela/envSample. We utilised Principal Component Analysis (PCA) maps generated from pre-selected environmental variables (see Subsection Climatological data) as filters and reduced all species presence data points that fall within one unit of PCA1 and PCA2 to one randomly selected data point. Background data, comprising all data points from the European continent, was generated by converting pixels from the raster layer that represent the study region into points (each point being the centroid of a cell) using the ‘Raster to Point’ tool in QGIS66. The generated data was then subject to environmental filtering. The final dataset comprised 10,325 records documenting species occurrences (Fig. 1a). These records were compiled based on the specific count of observations for each species: D. viride—327 (Fig. 1b), C. betulus—1743 (Fig. 1c), F. sylvatica—2666 (Fig. 1d), F. excelsior—3233 (Fig. 1e), and T. cordata—2356 (Fig. 1f).

Distribution of the final dataset (a) of species occurrences used to model the distribution of D. viride (b) and four tree phorophyte species—C. betulus (c), F. sylvatica (d), F. excelsior (e) and T. cordata (f). The maps were projected using the WGS 84/Pseudo-Mercator (EPSG: 3857) coordinate system and generated using QGIS version 3.22.666.

Climatological data

We utilised the CHELSA V2.1 global dataset67, which provides climatic variables with a horizontal resolution of 30 arc seconds (~ 1 km). The dataset is available at www.envidat.ch. To prepare the climatic variables for species distribution modelling, we followed a three-staged filtering process as described. Firstly (1), we only considered variables that have corresponding data available for future scenarios. Subsequently (2), the raster values were assigned to the species presence points for each of the remaining variables. Any variable with missing values (NA values) that accounted for ≥ 5% of the data was then excluded from the analysis. Finally (3), Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to ensure independence and reduce multicollinearity among variables. We removed variables with a correlation coefficient |r| ≥ 0.8068–71, resulting in the selection of 14 variables for further species distribution modelling. The selected variables include: bio2—mean diurnal air temperature range (mean diurnal range of temperatures averaged over 1 year), bio3—isothermality (ratio of diurnal variation to annual variation in temperatures), bio7—annual range of air temperature (the difference between the maximum temperature of warmest month and the minimum temperature of coldest month), bio8—mean daily mean air temperatures of the wettest quarter (the wettest quarter of the year is determined), bio9—mean daily mean air temperatures of the driest quarter (the driest quarter of the year is determined), bio10—mean daily mean air temperatures of the warmest quarter (the warmest quarter of the year is determined), bio12—annual precipitation amount (accumulated precipitation amount over 1 year), bio15—precipitation seasonality (the coefficient of variation is the standard deviation of the monthly precipitation estimates expressed as a percentage of the mean of those estimates (i.e. the annual mean)), bio18—mean monthly precipitation amount of the warmest quarter (the warmest quarter of the year is determined), gddlgd5—last growing degree day above 5 °C (last day of the year above 5 °C), gddlgd10—last growing degree day above 10 °C (last day of the year above 10 °C), gsl—growing season length (length of the growing season based on TREELIM72), gsp—accumulated precipitation amount on growing season days (precipitation sum accumulated on all days during the growing season based on TREELIM72), and gst—and mean temperature of the growing season (mean temperature of all growing season days based on TREELIM72).

To perform projections in future scenarios, we used downscaled Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) climatology datasets from CHELSA V2.167. For such studies, General Circulation Models (GCMs) are essential73. We selected the GFDL-ESM4 Earth system model provided by the Geophysical Fluid Dynamics Laboratory, particularly because it encompasses atmospheric and ecosystem dynamics, as well as ocean and land physics74, and it has the highest selection priority following the Intersectoral Impact Model Intercomparion Project (ISIMIP)75. We focussed on a set of previously selected variables that were deemed relevant for our study. The time span for our analysis extended from 2071 to 2100, allowing us to examine long-term projections. Within this time frame, we considered three distinct Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSP) scenarios, each representing different trajectories of future anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions and radiative forcing. These included SSP126, a scenario envisioning a sustainable future with low greenhouse gas emissions; SSP370, a moderate scenario with unchecked emissions leading to significant environmental changes; and SSP585, a high-emission pathway predicting substantial global warming. These scenarios are analogous to the Representative Concentration Pathways (RCPs) 2.6, 7, and 8.5, respectively. By using these SSP scenarios, our aim was to encompass a range of potential future climate conditions and explore their implications for the distribution of D. viride.

Species distribution modelling

Modelling procedure

To analyse the current and future distribution of the studied species, we employed an ensemble modelling approach using the BIOMOD2 package76. Since the modelling algorithms require both presence and absence data, the BIOMOD2::BIOMOD_FormatingData() function was utilised to designate 10,000 randomly selected, previously filtered background points as pseudo-absences77. Considering the stochastic nature of generating pseudo-absences, this procedure was repeated three times to mitigate potential sample bias78. Prior to selection, points with one or more missing values for explanatory variables were removed from the analysis.

We used BIOMOD2::BIOMOD_Modeling() to calibrate and evaluate modelling techniques. We implemented nine different algorithms, including: Generalized Linear Model (GLM)79, Generalized Additive Models (GAM)80, Generalized Boosted Models (GBM)81, Classification Tree Analysis (CTA)82, Flexible Discriminant Analysis (FDA)83, Artificial Neural Networks (ANN)84, Maximum Entropy (MaxEnt)85, Random Forest (RF)86 and Surface Response Envelope (SRE)87. Each algorithm was configured with its default modelling options. We randomly divided the occurrence data into two distinct subsets: 80% for model calibration and 20% for testing. To account for randomness in the performance of the model, we conducted five evaluation runs for each model. Cross-validation was employed to compute the Receiver Operator Curve (ROC) and the True Skill Statistic (TSS) as evaluation metrics for each algorithm, where greater metric values signified enhanced model performance77,78,88. We ran 100 permutations to estimate the importance of variables for each model.

Employing the BIOMOD2::BIOMOD_Projection() function, we projected the calibrated models, resulting in a collective of 135 models (generated from three replicate pseudo-absence datasets, nine algorithms, and five rounds of evaluation). To evaluate the set of generated models and create a unified ensemble prediction, we employed the BIOMOD2::BIOMOD_EnsembleModeling() function. The ensemble model was comprised solely of the best performing individual modelling outputs, each with a TSS value exceeding 0.7, given the TSS’s resistance to validation set size and species prevalence77,78,89. We employed the weighted mean approach to combine the individual models into an ensemble, benefitting from the ability to omit underperforming algorithms and substantially enhance the overall performance of the ensemble model, compared to other methods such as mean, median, and committee averaging90,91. In the final stage, we employed the BIOMOD2::BIOMOD_EnsembleForecasting() function to construct an ensemble species projection across space and time.

This sequential procedure was repeated for each time period combination, encompassing both the present and the future (2071–2100), while considering the three SSP scenarios.

Modelling approach

To analyse the distribution patterns of D. viride under present and future climatic conditions, we implemented a comprehensive approach that combines climatic data and the availability of phorophytes. Initially, we created projections for the four studied phorophyte species (following the procedure outlined in Subsection Modelling procedure; Figs. S1–S4). After this step, we averaged the predictions of these tree species under both present and future conditions to develop unified models, using the ‘Raster calculator’ tool in QGIS66. As a result, we generated the phorophyte availability layers for D. viride across each of the examined scenarios: present conditions and three distinct future scenarios (SSP126, SSP370, and SSP585). Subsequently, we performed modelling for D. viride, utilising both climatic data and the phorophyte availability, to elucidate the impact of phorophyte availability on the predictions for D. viride. Additionally, we conducted a test projection with individual trees (Fig. S5), without combining them into a single layer. We compared the result of this projection with the outcome of the projection in which the trees were combined, measuring their similarity through Pearson’s correlation. A remarkably high correlation coefficient (|r| = 0.96) indicates that both models are consistent in predicting the habitat suitability for the studied species.

The unified models account for the collective impact of various tree species, thus offering a more comprehensive and robust representation of phorophyte availability for D. viride. This approach effectively minimises the influence of outliers or individual tree species and ensures a more balanced and reliable result. In connection with the above and given the similarity observed in the projections (unified phorophyte model and individual trees), further analyses were conducted for models incorporating joint phorophyte availability, as well as those considering only climatic data. To assess the differences in the performance of the models produced through these two approaches, we employed ROC and TSS data obtained by using the BIOMOD2::get_evaluations() function. We utilised the shapiro.test function to assess data distribution and, owing to its non-normal distribution, employed a Wilcoxon test to ascertain the significance of the differences. The outcomes of the resulting Wilcoxon test were visualised using the ggpubr::ggboxplot() function92. Additionally, we employed the BIOMOD2::get_variables_importance() function to derive importance values for each variable. Subsequently, these values were standardised to correspond to their percentage contribution in the construction of the final ensemble model.

Spatial analyses

Species range change

We used the BIOMOD2::BIOMOD_RangeSize() function to assess potential future changes in the geographical distribution of D. viride. This function applies a threshold (here, 393) derived from model evaluation metrics with maximum specificity and sensitivity. Pixels from SDM outputs are then categorised as suitable habitats (values above the threshold) or unsuitable habitats (values below the threshold). By comparing the number of areas occupied (pixels) in the current ensemble distribution model with future projections, we obtained precise pixel counts for four distinct categories of regions: areas where the range of the species will be lost (currently suitable but will become unsuitable in the future), areas where the distribution of species will remain unchanged (areas that will retain their suitability or unsuitability in both present and future climatic conditions), and areas where the range of the species will be gained (currently unsuitable but will become suitable in the future); assigning each pixel one of four values (− 2 for predicted loss, − 1 for stability, 0 for unoccupied, and 1 for predicted gain in the future). To visualise these range shifts, we generated maps using the rasterVis::levelplot() function93.

Niche overlap

To assess the niche overlap of D. viride and four studied tree phorophytes, we employed two approaches: climatological niche overlap according to94 and geographical niche overlap as proposed by95. To determine the degree of niche overlap, we utilised Schoener’s D statistic (which evaluates the extent of overlap between niches of the species, considering both shared and unique niches) and Hellinger’s I statistic (which quantifies the proportion of overlap between niches of the species). These metrics range from 0 (indicating no similarity) to 1 (indicating high similarity), providing a quantification of the overlap between species niches and offering insights into potential niche similarity or divergence. The combination of climatological and geographical niche overlap analyses facilitates a comprehensive understanding of the ecological interactions and spatial relationships between the studied species. Furthermore, it provides valuable insights into potential co-existence mechanisms and responses to changing environmental conditions.

We analysed the overlap of the climatic niches to determine the extent to which D. viride and the four tree phorophytes studied share their ecological niches under current climate conditions. We used previously selected climatological data for the species distribution modelling, along with species presence data, and generated background points. We processed these datasets using the raster package96 and conducted PCA on the stack of climatological files. To determine the appropriate number of PCA-derived maps (i.e., PC axes), we applied the Kaiser-Guttman criterion, which relies on the mean of all eigenvalues. Specifically, we selected only those PC axes with eigenvalues that exceeded this calculated mean. Subsequently, we employed the ENMTools package97 to calculate the niche equivalency test95, measuring the degree of similarity between the climatological niches occupied by the studied species. We applied the ENMTools::enmtools.ecospat.id() function that computes the climatological niche overlap expressed by statistics I and D. To visualise the results, we utilised the ggfortify::autoplot() function98 to create a plot that illustrates the occurrence of the studied species and the background points in the PCA space derived from climate data.

We conducted a geographical niche overlap analysis to assess the spatial convergence of distribution ranges between the studied species. For this analysis, we used the dismo package99, which facilitated the assessment of the geographical niche overlap between D. viride and four species of phorophytes under present and future conditions (represented by the three SSP scenarios). To quantify the overlap, we utilised the dismo::nicheOverlap() function. This function enabled the calculation of the niche overlap between the studied species, using I and D statistics. To visualise the results, we utilised species distribution models, previously prepared as raster files, for all species studied under present and future conditions. Using the raster package, we quantified the similarity between the distribution models of the studied species under specific conditions by calculating the product of values at the corresponding pixels within two raster layers compared. Subsequently, these similarity values were rescaled to a 0–100 percentage scale, facilitating their visualisation and interpretation. The resulting plot, which illustrates the spatial distribution of model similarity values among the studied species, was generated using the rasterVis::levelplot() function93. This approach can be effectively employed to visualise and analyse spatial patterns of geographical niche overlap among the studied species, providing insights into areas where these species may co-exist or share similar ecological conditions.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software100. All maps were created using QGIS66.

Results

Assessment of the performance of species distribution models and determination of influential variables

The models for D. viride, developed by incorporating both climatic data and phorophyte availability, consistently outperformed models based solely on climatic data in almost every one of the nine algorithms employed (Fig. 2; Table S3). ROC values showed a significant increase in all algorithms, except for FDA and SRE. Similarly, an improvement was observed in TSS values across all algorithms, except for SRE (Fig. 2; Table S3). These substantial differences in the evaluation metrics underscore the importance of incorporating phorophyte availability data, which significantly enhances prediction accuracy. Therefore, only models based on climatic data and availability of phorophytes were further analysed.

Most algorithms demonstrated excellent predictive capabilities, evidenced by mean ROC and TSS values exceeding 0.9 (Fig. 2; Table S4). Among these algorithms, the ones that demonstrated the best performance were RF (ROC = 0.998 (SD = 0.001); TSS = 0.964 (SD = 0.009), GLM (ROC = 0.996 (SD = 0.003); TSS = 0.964 (SD = 0.015)) and GBM (ROC = 0.996 (SD = 0.003); TSS = 0.960 (SD = 0.017)) (Fig. 3a; Table S4). The SRE algorithm showed comparatively lower accuracy relative to the rest of the algorithms, with a mean ROC of 0.775 (SD = 0.038) and a mean TSS of 0.550 (SD = 0.076) (Fig. 2; Table S4). The utilised ensemble approach ensures that greater weight is given to models with higher accuracy in the final predictions, enhancing the overall predictive power of the models of D. viride.

Among the 15 variables used in the projection of the ensemble model, the greatest importance in the distribution of D. viride was attributed to the availability of phorophyte, accounting for 57.25% of the influence (Fig. 3). Temperature-related climatic variables, including the annual range of air temperature (5.76%), the mean temperature of the growing season (4.10%) and the mean daily air temperatures of the warmest quarter (3.88%), also played a significant role. Furthermore, the length of the growing season contributed 3.92% to the predictive power of the model (Fig. 3).

Current and future potential distribution of D. viride and potential changes in its range

The current potential distribution of D. viride in Europe is primarily focussed in the central part of the continent, excluding the Pannonian Basin, and extends to the southern part of the Fennoscandia (Fig. 4a). Particularly suitable habitats for this species are found in mainland mountainous areas such as the Pyrenees, the Massif Central, the Apennines, the Alps, the Carpathians, and the Caucasus Mountains (Fig. 4a). A similar level of habitat suitability is also observed in the mountainous regions of the British Isles (Fig. 4a). Conversely, areas potentially unsuitable for D. viride include the northern and eastern parts of the continent, as well as the southern part of the Balkan Peninsula and almost all of the Iberian Peninsula.

Potential habitat suitability for D. viride in Europe under current conditions (a) and three future SSP scenarios: SSP126 (b), SSP370 (c), and SSP585 (d). The maps were projected using the WGS 84/Pseudo-Mercator (EPSG: 3857) coordinate system and generated using R version 4.2.1100 and QGIS version 3.22.666.

In all examined climate change scenarios, D. viride is projected to lose a significant proportion of its currently suitable areas, specifically 47.87%, 49.74% and 48.95% under SSP126, SSP370 and SSP585 pathways, respectively. Under scenarios with more pessimistic shared socioeconomic pathways, considerable northward and north-eastward shifts in its habitat optimum are anticipated, potentially creating new areas supportive of the species' persistence, with gains of 55.03%, 51.73% and 50.27% in new suitable areas under SSP126, SSP370, and SSP585 pathways, respectively (Fig. 4b–d). These climatic changes are expected to render most areas previously suitable, particularly in central Europe and the southern Nordic countries, unsuitable for the species (Fig. 4b–d). Furthermore, climate changes will likely lead to the fragmentation of potential habitats in central Europe, with the most suitable regions for its persistence becoming mainly limited to mountainous areas, including the Pyrenees, the Alps, the Carpathians, and the Caucasus Mountains (Fig. 4b–d).

Despite noticeable potential changes in the species distribution range and the potential northward and north-eastward shifts in its habitat optimum (Fig. 4a–d), future projections for D. viride range change (understood as the difference between potential gains and losses in suitable areas) are not pessimistic. Considering each of the three SSP scenarios, this species is projected to ultimately benefit in terms of the size of its potential range, albeit slightly (Fig. 5a–c). Under the SSP126 scenario (Fig. 5a), this gain is projected to be the largest, reaching 7.16%. However, for the SSP370 (Fig. 5b) and SSP585 (Fig. 5c) scenarios, the anticipated gain will be marginal, at 1.99% and 1.32%, respectively.

Climatic and geographical niche overlap with phorophytes

The extent of the overlap of the climatic niche between D. viride and its associated tree phorophytes, namely C. betulus, F. sylvatica, F. excelsior and T. cordata, showed varying proportions of shared and unique climatic niches (Table 1). Dicranum viride exhibited moderate overlap with C. betulus (I = 0.543, D = 0.291). In comparison, greater overlap was observed with F. sylvatica (I = 0.622, D = 0.383) and F. excelsior (I = 0.658, D = 0.424), while the highest degree of overlap was found with T. cordata (I = 0.750, D = 0.486). The PCA analysis results indicated that the first two principal components (PCs) accounted for 68.45% of the overall variance (Fig. 6). On the PC1 axis, the most influential climatic variables were temperature-related (last growing degree day above 10 °C (gddlgd10), isothermality (bio3), mean daily mean air temperatures of the driest quarter (bio9)) and growing season length (gsl) (Fig. 6; Table S5). A similar pattern emerged on the PC2 axis, where temperature-related variables (mean temperature of the growing season (gst) and mean daily mean air temperatures of the wettest quarter (bio8)), along with the precipitation seasonality (bio12), had the greatest influence (Fig. 6; Table S5).

Discussion

Depending on the scale of climate change resulting from anthropogenic radiative forcing, the range of D. viride is projected to shift noticeably toward the northern regions of the European continent. A similar pattern has been noted among other bryophytes (e.g.,77,101). The anticipated decline in the vast majority of currently suitable areas for this endangered epiphyte is alarming. Our analysis suggests that future refuges for this species in central Europe will likely be confined to mountainous regions. This shift reflects the findings of Steinbauer et al.102, who noted an increase in species richness at higher elevations as a rapid response of plants to climate change. This trend highlights the profound impact of global warming on plant communities, emphasising the urgent need for policy and conservation action.

Among the climate-related variables influencing this epiphyte, temperature-associated factors are the most significant. This significance can be linked to the ecophysiological and biological traits of bryophytes, especially their poikilohydric nature103. Poikilohydry, an adaptation to fluctuating water availability, involves alternating between a hydrated, photosynthetically active state and a desiccated, inactive phase104. Consequently, there is a heightened sensitivity to warm temperatures in temperate bryoflora, increasing susceptibility to thermal damage, especially when hydrated105. Furthermore, He et al.32 found that temperate bryophytes have lower optimal temperatures and reduced heat tolerance compared to angiosperms, indicating their increased vulnerability to climatic changes.

However, our analyses have yielded some rather intriguing insights. Although the immediate impact of climate on D. viride is undeniable, its significance appears to diminish in comparison to the changes in the distribution of phorophyte species, which are crucial for the epiphyte survival.

Habitat specialisation is increasingly recognised as a risk factor for species extinction across various groups of organisms106–110. Specialist species are notably vulnerable to climate change, primarily due to their limited ecological niches and strong affinity for specific habitats111. This vulnerability is exemplified by Dicranum viride, a woodland-specialist species closely associated with specific tree species. This moderately acidophilic epiphyte prefers deciduous trees6,47, particularly favouring smooth-barked species such as F. sylvatica and C. betulus10,48,52, although it can also grow on rough-barked trees58,112. Our studies indicate that, on a continental scale in Europe, D. viride occurs most frequently on F. sylvatica, C. betulus, F. excelsior, and T. cordata, consistent with these preferences. However, beyond the identity of the tree species, other factors also influence the presence of this epiphyte. Large-diameter and veteran trees are known to be important for the establishment of D. viride, as they provide suitable microhabitats, such as bark irregularities and specific bryophyte assemblages, that facilitate its presence10,51,56. Our analyses highlight its strong dependence on the phorophyte species it inhabits, emerging as a key factor in its predictive models. This vital epiphyte-host tree relationship, also noted in other moss species (e.g.,113–116), is mirrored in the significant geographic and climatic niche overlap between D. viride and its host trees. It reveals the critical nature of the distribution of this endangered epiphyte being closely related to specific phorophyte species.

The inclusion of biotic interactions in species distribution modelling, particularly significant associations such as with host plants39,43 or preferred habitat types117,118, is a critical factor, as underscored by previous research. This approach has been validated for other epiphytic organisms, including lichens20,22. Consistent with this view, our findings indicate that incorporating potential host tree availability considerably enhanced the accuracy and predictive power of our models.

The integration of biotic data with climatic factors in modelling epiphytic mosses is key to capturing the essential relationship between epiphytes and their host trees, an element not fully captured by relying solely on climatic variables. Furthermore, projecting future tree distribution acts as a valuable proxy in estimating the potential habitats of the species being studied. Particularly for woodland-specialist species, including this factor is vital to thoroughly assess how environmental changes may influence their future distribution patterns. However, it should not be neglected that the distributions of phorophyte species may also be directly affected by climate change1,119.

Trees, with their substantial role in ecological processes and resource supply, represent a fundamental component of forest ecosystems that require effective management strategies in the context of climate change120,121. Shifts in latitudinal distribution of key forest-forming tree species in the Northern Hemisphere1,122,123 may result in significant range changes or even the gradual loss of entire ecosystems reliant on specific tree species124. Possible scenarios include the transformation of alpine and tundra regions into forests, and the conversion of southern European forests into scrublands due to environmental pressures124. Such dramatic transformations will have extensive consequences for dependent organisms, including epiphytes (e.g.,116,125–127). As a woodland specialist, D. viride is poised to face significant distribution shifts due to these changes. The direct impact of climate combined with the shifts in the range of its phorophyte species is likely to amplify the effects of climate change on its distribution across Europe.

The vital ecological roles of target species like D. viride make them key to conservation efforts focussed on preserving biodiversity. Therefore, effective conservation planning is crucial, involving the identification of potential future refuges and areas of vulnerability for those species. Conventional conservation methods often focus on preserving current habitats, neglecting possible environmental changes, which highlights the need for adaptive nature conservation management plans in response to climate change128,129. Conservation strategies should not only safeguard current habitats but also consider and adapt to anticipated future habitat changes128,130. In forest management, where human impact is significant, this approach becomes especially vital. It enables the prediction of dynamics for woodland-specialist species like D. viride and identifies critical habitats requiring protection. Moreover, it guides forest managers on tree selection and composition for crucial habitats, emphasising the role of strategic forest management in species conservation. Consequently, this proactive approach is essential for developing effective conservation strategies to mitigate the effects of future climate change on epiphytic bryophytes, with D. viride as a great illustrative example.

As our study highlights, the distribution of D. viride is primarily dependent on the potential distribution of its phorophytes. However, there are limitations that restrict a full understanding of D. viride distribution dynamics. While we can predict range changes for specific phorophytes under future environmental scenarios, the impact of potential land use changes on them remains uncertain. The role of forest management is particularly crucial here, as epiphytes depend on moving between living trees as “stepping stones” for survival131. The effectiveness of ecological corridors, featuring trees of suitable age, is also pivotal for the dispersal of epiphytes132–134. Furthermore, we cannot foresee new biotic interactions that might affect both the epiphyte and its host trees. Lastly, our models, though robust, are based on the assumption that the climatic niche of the species will not change, overlooking possible acclimatisation or adaptation in the future.

Conclusions

The projected shifts in the distribution of D. viride are influenced by more than just climatic factors; they stem from a complex interplay of environmental variables, particularly climate change and the distribution of its phorophyte species. This intricate relationship underscores the importance of adopting a holistic approach in understanding and tackling the effects of climate change on both specific species and entire ecosystems. In our efforts to mitigate climate change’s impacts and preserve biodiversity, it is essential to account for not only the direct consequences of these changes but also the broader ecological context, including the biotic interactions among dependent organisms. Our study sheds light on how endangered epiphytes like D. viride might respond to climate change, underlining the urgency to address these environmental challenges. This insight underscores the necessity for proactive conservation strategies that safeguard not only the target species but also their vital habitats amid ongoing environmental transformations.
