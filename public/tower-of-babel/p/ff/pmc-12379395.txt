Development and evaluation of a convolutional neural network model for sex prediction using cephalometric radiographs and cranial photographs
Authors: Vitria Wuri Handayani, Mieke Sylvia Margareth Amiatun Ruth, Riries Rulaningtyas, Muhammad Rasyad Caesarardhi, Bayu Azra Yudhantorro, Ahmad Yudianto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurately determining sex using features like facial bone profiles and teeth is crucial for identifying unknown victims. Lateral cephalometric radiographs effectively depict the lateral cranial structure, aiding the development of computational identification models.ObjectiveThis study develops and evaluates a sex prediction model using cephalometric radiographs with several convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures. The primary goal is to evaluate the model’s performance on standardized radiographic data and real-world cranial photographs to simulate forensic applications.MethodsSix CNN architectures—VGG16, VGG19, MobileNetV2, ResNet50V2, InceptionV3, and InceptionResNetV2—were employed to train and validate 340 cephalometric images of Indonesian individuals aged 18 to 40 years. The data were divided into training (70%), validation (15%), and testing (15%) subsets. Data augmentation was implemented to mitigate class imbalance. Additionally, a set of 40 cranial images from anatomical specimens was employed to evaluate the model’s generalizability. Model performance metrics included accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score.ResultsCNN models were trained and evaluated on 340 cephalometric images (255 females and 85 males). VGG19 and ResNet50V2 achieved high F1-scores of 95% (females) and 83% (males), respectively, using cephalometric data, highlighting their strong class-specific performance. Although the overall accuracy exceeded 90%, the F1-score better reflected model performance in this imbalanced dataset. In contrast, performance notably decreased with cranial photographs, particularly when classifying female samples. That is, while InceptionResNetV2 achieved the highest F1-score for cranial photographs (62%), misclassification of females remained significant. Confusion matrices and per-class metrics further revealed persistent issues related to data imbalance and generalization across imaging modalities.ConclusionsBasic CNN models perform well on standardized cephalometric images but less effectively on photographic cranial images, indicating a domain shift between image types that limits generalizability. Improving real-world forensic performance will require further optimization and more diverse training data.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Cephalometric radiology, Cranium, CNN, Sex identification, Forensic odontology

Introduction

Deceased individuals are identified on-site by experts in forensic medicine and odontology [1, 2]. Forensic dentists primarily specialize in identifying human remains [3], using precise and validated methods that continue to advance with forensic science [4–6]. Determining the sex of unidentified corpses aids investigators in establishing an individual’s identity [4, 7, 8].

Cephalometric radiographs are two-dimensional (2D) images widely used in dental and orthodontic practice [9, 10] for standardized lateral views of craniofacial structures [11]. Owing to their low cost, accessibility, and standardized acquisition protocols, cephalometric radiographs are practical for forensic applications—particularly in postmortem identification—when advanced imaging technologies are unavailable. In contrast, three-dimensional (3D) modalities, such as computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), offer superior anatomical details; however, they are significantly more expensive and resource-intensive [12]. Hence, cephalometric radiography remains a cost-effective alternative tool for assessing skeletal sex dimorphism, especially in developing nations.

Computational methods that model human cognitive abilities are expanding forensic science, offering effective tools to strengthen scientific practices and enhance the professional competence of forensic examiners [6, 13]. Innovative AI applications have been proposed in various fields, including sex estimation [14–17]. However, most of these studies relied on single-modality imaging, limiting their generalizability. For instance, Khazaei et al. (2022) evaluated DenseNet, ResNet, and VGG models using 1.476 lateral cephalometric radiographs of Iranian adults (794 women, 682 men; aged 18–49). Their results showed that DenseNet121 attained the highest accuracy of 90% for sex classification, underscoring the effectiveness of transfer learning [18].

This study employs dual-modality imaging by training CNN-based automated sex prediction models on cephalometric radiographs and assessing their generalizability on lateral cranial images. The primary objective is to assess the robustness of multiple CNN architectures when transitioning from controlled medical imaging to photographic input in a simulated real-world forensic identification context.

Methods

Research design and model development

The study had two phases (Fig. 1). First, CNN models were trained on lateral cephalometric radiographs to classify sex by preprocessing the images, extracting key visual features with CNN layers, flattening the features into vectors, and feeding them into fully connected (dense) layers with dropout to mitigate overfitting. The final output layer performed the sex classification task (Fig. 1A).

Convolutional neural network (CNN) transfer learning. (A) Initial phase of CNN training workflow for sex classification from cephalometric images. (B) Second phase of CNN transfer workflow for sex classification from cranium lateral photographs

Second, transfer learning was applied using pre-trained weights from the cephalometric CNN model, without retraining, to evaluate the performance of cranial photographic images (Fig. 1B).

Model development and preprocessing were performed in Python using the TensorFlow framework. All procedures were conducted on a desktop computer with an AMD Ryzen™ 5-7535HS CPU and a 4 GB NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3050 GPU. Six CNN architectures were assessed: VGG16, VGG19, MobileNetV2, ResNet50V2, InceptionV3, and InceptionResNetV2.

All cephalometric images were resized to 224 × 224 pixels. The dataset was randomly divided into training (70%), validation (15%), and testing (15%) subsets. To address class imbalance and improve generalizability, data augmentation—including random rotations (up to 90°), zoom (range 0.25), width and height shifts (up to 25%), shear (range 0.15), brightness variation (0.5–2.0), vertical and horizontal flips, and nearest fill mode—was applied to the training set (Fig. 2).

Image processing and augmentation. (A) X-ray cephalometry; (B) augmented images

All CNN models were trained using the Adam optimizer (learning rate = 1e-4), categorical cross-entropy loss function, and dropout (rate = 0.5) for model reproducibility and stability. Early stopping with a patience of 10 epochs based on validation loss was implemented, and learning rate scheduling was applied to adjust the learning rate when a plateau occurred to prevent overfitting. Each model was trained for a maximum of 100 epochs with a batch size of 32.

Data collection and sample characteristics

A total of 340 lateral cephalometric radiographs were retrospectively collected from the Dental and Oral Hospital, Faculty of Dental Medicine, Universitas Airlangga, Indonesia. All participants were Indonesian males and females aged between 18 and 40 years. All radiography data were anonymized, and the ethics committee waived Informed Consent in compliance with institutional and national regulations. To evaluate models of real cranial structures, 40 lateral cranial photographs were obtained from cadaveric specimens at the Anatomy Laboratory, Faculty of Medicine, Universitas Airlangga. The sex of all specimens was confirmed based on anatomical records.

Data analysis

All CNN models were trained using categorical cross-entropy loss and evaluated using standard performance metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. The final trained CNN models were tested on a cranial lateral photograph dataset to assess their generalizability and robustness in real-world forensic scenarios.

When tested on cranial photographs, the models exhibited varying classification performances (Table 3). VGG16, VGG19, and MobileNetV2 achieved F1-scores of 57% for male classification but failed to classify female subjects (0%; weighted F1-score: 23%). ResNet50V2 improved slightly with F1-scores of 8% for males and 56% for females (weighted F1-score: 27%, macro average: 32%). InceptionV3 demonstrated a more balanced performance, yielding F1-scores of 64% for males and 40% for females (weighted F1-score: 54%, macro average: 52%). InceptionResNetV2 achieved the highest performance, with an F1-score of 74% for males and 35% for females (weighted F1-score: 58%, macro average: 54%). Hence, male classification decreased markedly because of the class distribution shift.

Discussion

Sex classification using cephalometric radiographs

The CNNs were tailored to the number of labels in the cephalogram images, which were classified as “male” (M) or “female” (F) based on sex characteristics. Sex estimation is a primary step in identification, preceding age or ethnicity [19]. This study focused on sex classification among Indonesian adults. Similarly, Aurizanti et al. (2017) defined sexual dimorphism in craniofacial linear measurements among Indonesian adults aged 20–40 years, using digital lateral cephalometric radiographs [20]. Lateral cephalometry is an essential tool for orthodontic diagnosis, treatment planning, and forensic odontology [21, 22]. Specifically, it reveals facial and dental structures—potentially associated with misalignment—as well as cranial and facial developmental patterns that are critical in forensic odontology [21, 22].

We previously reported that automatic analysis software can gather cephalometric measurements that nearly meet clinical standards [23]. In particular, the VGG19-based CNN cephalometric model demonstrates higher accuracy than VGG16 [23], but its performance declines because of sex differences in samples. Moreover, large datasets are required to train CNNs effectively and mitigate overfitting and non-convergence issues associated with deep learning systems [24–26]. Accordingly, in the current study, the cephalometric sample size was expanded, and additional CNN models were employed—MobileNetV2, ResNet50V2, InceptionV3, and InceptionV2, in addition to the VGG16 and VGG19—to improve results.

Female F1-scores were consistently higher for all models: VGG16 scored 94% for females and 93% for males, whereas InceptionResNetV2 exhibited greater disparity with 90% for females and 64% for males. These differences reflect the model’s sensitivity to class distribution. The F1-score, as a harmonic mean of precision and recall, is particularly sensitive to label imbalance and class skew performance [27–29], offering a more balanced assessment than accuracy, which can be distorted by majority-class performance. Thus, the F1-score crucially affects the minority class—in this case, males. It is also influenced by class asymmetry, depending on which class is defined as positive [27, 30, 31]. That is, bias toward the positive class, which is closely linked to the true positive rate, increases the F1-score [27]. Conversely, prioritizing the negative class decreases the F1-score, even if the data or class distribution remains unchanged [27]. The F1-score ranges from 0 (no precision or recall) to 1 (perfect precision and recall scores) [27]. Despite sex-based F1-score variations, the overall cephalometric model accuracy values remained consistently high.

Generalizability to cranial photographs

After developing effective models with cephalometric data, we proceeded to the second stage of the study to evaluate the models using lateral cranial photographs. This assessed how well the models trained on radiographs generalized to varied, less standardized image inputs. Table 3 provides a concise review of the performance of each CNN architecture across five parameters pertinent to sex identification. The evaluated model architectures (VGG16, VGG19, MobileNetV2, ResNet50V2, InceptionV3, and InceptionResNetV2) are well-established in computer vision and have demonstrated strong performance in large-scale image classification tasks [29, 32–35].

The performance declined markedly when tested on cranial photographs: VGG16, VGG19, MobileNetV2, and ResNet50V2 each scored 40% F1; InceptionV3 reached 55%, and InceptionResNetV2 achieved 62%. Although InceptionResNetV2 demonstrated superior performance relative to the other models, none reached the 80% F1-score benchmark, which is considered acceptable for practical applications. These results underscore the challenges in transferring learning from highly standardized radiographic data to more het244-246erogeneous photographic modalities and highlight the need for further optimization or domain adaptation. Subsequent research could be enhanced by employing domain adversarial neural networks (DANN) or fine-tuning with a limited set of labelled cranial images to address the modality disparity.

Comparative model analysis

CNNs have transformed image analysis through deep hierarchical architectures, and they remain integral to computer vision tasks, including applications in medical imaging [34, 36]. In particular, VGG16, VGG19, MobileNetV2, ResNet50V2, InceptionV3, and InceptionResNetV2 have specific architectural characteristics that influence their performance in different image detection tasks, such as cephalometric sex prediction and their generalizability to cranial images.

VGGNet, initially developed to reduce parameter complexity and enhance training efficiency, is available in multiple versions like VGG16 and VGG19, which are distinguished by having 16 and 19 layers, respectively [37]. In this study, both models demonstrated identical generalization performance on cranial images, with each achieving an F1-score of 40%. Further analysis revealed that the models exhibited high recall for males but 0% precision for females, reflecting a significant imbalance in classification performance. These findings illustrate challenges in model generalization to less-structured image modalities and emphasize issues related to data imbalance.

MobileNet is a lightweight CNN designed for efficient image classification [38]. Depthwise convolution reduces the parameter count, making deep neural networks lightweight [39]. MobileNet was TensorFlow’s first mobile computer vision model [40]; these models aim to achieve excellent accuracy while considering the constrained resources for on-device or embedded applications [40]. In the current study, MobileNetV2 achieved comparable results to VGG16, VGG19, and ResNet50V2, with an overall F1-score of 40%. However, its classification pattern was reversed, with higher precision for females and 0% for males, indicating data imbalance and robustness issues.

The ResNet50V2 architecture addresses vanishing gradients by incorporating bypass links [41]. The residual blocks of ResNet are utilized iteratively across the network, enhancing its efficacy despite the increased architectural complexity. Although ResNet offers greater accuracy, it is reportedly more prone to overfitting [41]. The current study achieved relatively good results on the cephalometric data; however, the F1-score dropped to 40% on the cranial photographs. Moreover, while precisions of 50% and 39% were obtained for males and females, respectively, the recall for males was extremely low (4%), leading to poor overall generalization. This reflects the importance of considering both precision and recall in imbalanced datasets.

Inception expands the network space in which the optimal networks are selected during training [32, 42]. Every inception module captures important characteristics on several levels: 5 × 5 convolutions gather global information, 3 × 3 convolutional layers capture scattered features [43], and max-pooling extracts low-level properties. These characteristics are merged before passing to the next layer [44, 45]. Using cranial images, an F1-score of 55% was obtained, with precision rates of 62% for males and 43% for females, reflecting a relatively better ability to generalize unstructured photographic inputs.

InceptionResNetV2, integrating the advantages of residual connections and Inception modules, outperformed all other models with a 62% F1-score on cranial images. It maintained the best tradeoff between precision and recall between sexes, suggesting stronger generalizability and resilience to image variability.

Comparison with existing literature

The results of the current study were slightly lower than those of Matsuda et al. (2020), who reported 100% accuracy using periapical index evaluation via orthopantomography [32]. Nonetheless, our findings align with our previous results, in which VGG16 and VGG19 achieved 89% and 93% accuracy, respectively [23]. Meanwhile, Shah et al. (2023) reported exceptional CNN performance in non-medical domains (agricultural), with InceptionV3, VGG16, VGG19, and ResNet50 achieving accuracies above 98% [33]. Although these results confirm the efficacy of CNNs in image classification, their direct translation to forensic cranial identification tasks requires further adaptation.

Previous research in dental imaging highlights that image standardization and anatomical consistency are key for reliable CNN performance [46]. For instance, deep learning models perform better with the high structural regularity of panoramic and intraoral radiographs compared to the variability in cranial photographic data. Other forensic odontology studies have shown that sex classification accuracy is highly dependent on the anatomical region analyzed, with mandibular and cranial base landmarks providing more robust indicators than soft-tissue features [47]. Additionally, data balancing techniques, such as Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) or class weighting, improve sex-based data imbalances [48, 49] and per-class metrics, particularly for minority classes [50]. The current study extends this work by revealing a performance gap between the radiographic and photographic domains, highlighting the need for tailored preprocessing and model adaptation strategies in forensic imaging.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the dataset exhibited a sex imbalance, particularly within the cranial image set (24 males vs. 16 females), which may have distorted model predictions. Although data augmentation was applied, this imbalance remained a critical issue affecting model robustness. Future research should consider implementing data balancing techniques, such as SMOTE, and class weighting strategies, or increasing the quantity and diversity of skull images to improve generalizability.

Second, CNN performance was likely affected by the lack of standardization in the photographic images, including variations in angle, lighting, and background. These inconsistencies may have introduced noise, influencing feature extraction.

Third, while six CNN architectures were evaluated, methods such as cross-validation, hyperparameter tuning, and ensemble techniques were not applied, which might improve model stability and performance.

Fourth, no domain adaptation methods were employed to address the modality gap between radiographs and photographic data. Future studies should explore domain adaptation strategies, such as fine-tuning with real-world photographic data, adversarial learning, or multimodal learning.

Finally, the study population only included Indonesian adults, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings to other ethnic and demographic groups.

Conclusions

CNNs performed well on structured radiographic data but exhibited diminished generalizability with photographic cranial images. This disparity underscores the significance of domain-specific features and suggests that strategies like domain adaptation, synthetic oversampling, and multimodal learning are needed to improve generalizability.
