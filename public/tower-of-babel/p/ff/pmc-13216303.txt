An ethics-informed computable audit framework for monitoring misdiagnosis risk in AI-assisted diagnosis
Authors: Yue Li, MengYu Yuan, Yujing Yang, Jia Fu, Yi Xin, Chu Jie Duan, Jun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiagnostic AI can misclassify under distribution shift and subgroup imbalance; governance signals are rarely computable at deploy time. We target deployed diagnostic decision-support systems that perform binary classification and output a continuous risk score (preferably a calibrated probability). The audit layer ingests deploy-time streams including features X, model score S, subgroup tag g, and clinician action a, while outcome labels Y may arrive later via adjudication or follow-up. We define a unit-scaled Misdiagnosis Risk Index (MRI-AI) aggregating shift, fairness, calibration, and human–AI interaction; implement a streaming sentinel with starter bands and stop rules; and log signals and actions in an accountability ledger. A minimal simulation emulates device/site drift and imbalance. Outcomes include deploy-time trigger behavior from label-free indicators and delayed updates of label-dependent metrics (overall/worst-group AUC/FPR, ECE/Brier), as well as trigger rate, top-decile error share, and decision-curve net benefit. Using a controlled, scenario-based synthetic stress-test suite (designed to evaluate the audit/monitoring layer rather than to claim clinical performance of a particular diagnostic model), we report both predictive metrics (overall and worst-group AUC/FPR, ECE, and Brier) and alert-centric endpoints (window-level alert rate, time-to-first-trigger, and persistence). The results show that alert burden remains low under stable conditions and increases in a graded and interpretable manner with shift type and severity, supporting scenario-dependent monitoring and risk-tiered governance actions. Temperature scaling improves calibration while preserving rank-based decision behavior, and subgroup disparities remain explicitly auditable. A computable audit layer—MRI-AI + streaming sentinel + ledger—turns fairness and transparency into actionable controls for diagnostic decision support, enabling auditable monitoring and risk-tiered interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46652-1.Keywords: Artificial intelligence (AI) diagnostics, Misdiagnosis risk, AI policy and regulation, Patient safety and trust, Ethical responsibility

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) has begun to reshape diagnostic workflows across oncology, cardiology, and radiology, with machine-learning models frequently reaching expert-level performance in development or benchmark settings1–4. Yet the translation to routine clinical use remains uneven. When models face populations, devices, or workflows that differ from their development conditions, performance often degrades and failure patterns shift in ways that are difficult to detect and correct in time5–9. Real-world deployments have reported such drift under temporal shocks and site/device heterogeneity, motivating both methodological work on drift detection and regulatory emphasis on measuring AI-enabled medical-device performance in real-world use. These practical gaps-rather than the lack of sophisticated algorithms-now limit the safe and equitable use of AI in diagnostics.

Misdiagnosis, understood as the failure to accurately identify or communicate a patient’s condition, acquires new contours in AI-supported care7. Data under-representation can yield subgroup performance gaps; opaque model behavior complicates error tracing and clinician trust; and human-AI interaction issues (automation bias, over-reliance, and unclear handoffs) impede timely correction of AI errors8–12. Prior work has catalogued these concerns and offered high-level recommendations, but clinical stakeholders still lack computable definitions, deploy-time triggers, and auditable processes that translate principles into day-to-day decisions.

This study addresses that gap by proposing a computable audit framework for reducing misdiagnosis risk in AI-driven diagnostics. Rather than adding yet another predictive model, we operationalize risk monitoring and governance into measurable components that can be implemented, evaluated, and audited within clinical workflows. Table 1 summarizes the failure modes we target and their operational signals; in contrast to prior taxonomies, we explicitly include calibration/uncertainty as a first-class source of diagnostic error alongside data shift, fairness gaps, and human-AI interaction.

Contributions. This work makes three concrete contributions: (1) a Misdiagnosis Risk Index (MRI-AI) that aggregates data shift, subgroup fairness gaps, calibration/uncertainty, and human–AI interaction into a single, optimizable score; (2) an operational governance layer unifying an online drift-and-bias sentinel (with explicit trigger bands), a risk-tiered explainability budget (with latency tracking), and an accountability ledger for deploy-time logging and audit; and (3) a minimal, reproducible stress-test protocol and reporting template for controlled data shift and subgroup imbalance. In this manuscript, the protocol is used to demonstrate end-to-end computability of the audit layer and to characterize alert behavior under predefined scenarios; prospective real-world evaluations are required to establish deployment performance and operational burden.

Throughout the manuscript, we use data shift as an umbrella term for differences between development and deployment. When needed, we distinguish domain shift (changes in population/site/device/workflow context) from distribution shift (changes in the data-generating distribution, including covariate shift, label/prevalence shift, and concept/conditional shift). Workflow-aligned risk instrumentation (mapping to Fig. 1)

This section describes how the audit layer is instrumented across the workflow in Fig. 1 so that monitoring signals, triggers, and downstream actions are computable at deploy time and recorded as auditable artifacts.

In data collection (Fig. 1, left panel), ingestion refers to the operational step where incoming deployment data are received, validated, and standardized before use. During ingestion, the system computes PSI on a small set of deploy-time variables (e.g., key demographics and device/site metadata) against development baselines, and logs missingness and acquisition artifacts that can confound monitoring. When ingestion-time checks breach starter bands, the event is recorded in the ledger and surfaced before retraining resumes or model updates are approved.

In model training and release (Fig. 1, second panel), the release package stored in the ledger includes model versioning, calibration method/parameters, operating threshold(s), grouped performance summaries (e.g., AUC/F1 by subgroup), calibration (ECE/Brier), and robustness checks under controlled synthetic shifts (e.g., subgroup re-weighting or perturbations). These artifacts provide the context needed to interpret later sentinel alerts and to support accountable updates.

In diagnostic application (Fig. 1, third panel), an online drift-and-bias sentinel runs stratified checks on rolling windows, updating shift indicators (PSI and score-distribution drift), subgroup disparity indicators (worst-group gaps), and calibration/uncertainty metrics when labels are available. At the case level, MRI-AI is computed to prioritize attention and allocate a risk-tiered explainability budget: low-risk cases receive lightweight explanations, while high-risk or flagged-group cases receive higher-fidelity explanations and additional review. Predictions, thresholds, explanations, and clinician actions (e.g., overrides) are logged to the ledger.

In feedback and optimization (Fig. 1, right panel), sentinel triggers and ledger analytics inform bounded actions such as recalibration, threshold adjustment, temporary holds, or retraining with refreshed data. Double-sign review denotes a two-person verification step for high-risk cases, where a second qualified clinician reviews the case before action is taken; assignments and outcomes are recorded in the ledger for auditability.

Governance and accountability: what must be logged

To make decisions auditable, the deployment should maintain an accountability ledger with: timestamp; site and device; model version and calibration; data digest (hashed summary); subgroup tags; key metrics (PSI, ΔFPR, ECE, MRI-AI); triggers and actions taken; reviewer ID, override and latency; and eventual clinical outcome when available. A minimal JSON schema is provided in the Methods/Appendix and is referenced by the sentinel at runtime.

Incident response, audit trail, and “stop rules”

We bind ethical requirements to time-bound actions and verifiable records via the accountability ledger described in Section “Failure modes and operational risk signals in AI-based diagnosis”. In deployment, the sentinel continuously evaluates rolling indicators—population-level shift (e.g., PSI), worst-group performance gaps (e.g.,ΔFPR, ΔAUC), calibration error (ECE)25,26, and the per-case MRI-AI score. When any statistic exceeds its pre-specified band, a trigger event is automatically written to the ledger with timestamp, model/version, site or subgroup, window ID, metric and value, band exceeded, and the proposed mitigation.

Default actions. (1) Fairness deviation (e.g., worst-group ΔFPR ≥ 0.05/0.10 or ΔAUC ≥ 0.03/0.05): enable dual-clinician sign-off for the affected subgroup, notify clinical leadership, and schedule fairness-aware recalibration or threshold retuning27. (2) Distribution shift (PSI ≥ 0.10/0.25): freeze model updates, sample an audit set, and consider data refresh or re-weighting. (3) Miscalibration (ECE ≥ 0.03/0.06): Perform temperature scaling (post-hoc probability calibration) and verify on a holdout stream23. (4) High-risk cases (MRI-AI in the highest-risk quantile): escalate explanations (e.g., SHAP/LIME), request senior review, and, when warranted, place a temporary hold.

Service levels and audit trail. Triage occurs within 24 h of trigger logging; a mitigation proposal is due within 72 h; and a root-cause analysis within 14 days, with corrective actions tracked to closure. Clinician overrides and their latencies are recorded in the ledger for quality improvement28.

Stop rules. If two independent triggers persist across K consecutive batches at the site level, clinical leadership temporarily suspends automated recommendations for the affected pathway and reverts to human-only decisions until recalibration or an updated model passes verification. Unless otherwise specified, we set K = 3 consecutive batches. Any off-label use is explicitly flagged in the ledger and routed to oversight-committee review.

Patient rights and informed consent-instrumented, not just declared

We translate patient rights and informed consent into auditable artifacts that are coupled to the deployment workflow. Patients are informed that an AI decision-support tool is used and that clinicians remain ultimately responsible for decisions29. They are told the model’s intended use, major limitations, and any known subgroup performance caveats. They are also notified that continuous monitoring (the sentinel) is active and that they retain a right to human review or a second opinion. Data use, protection, and logging practices are disclosed, including de-identification, retention periods, and access control. Where required by law or institutional policy, patients are offered an option to opt out of secondary use of their data for model improvement.

Operationally, a consent record is stored as a structured ledger entry linked to the clinical case. This record captures the timestamp, the version of the consent text presented, the language, acceptance versus opt-out status, the clinician witness (or workflow attestation), and a pointer to the deployed model version used for that case. In addition, we provide a fact sheet for the monitored diagnostic model (not for the audit layer) to clinicians and make it available to patients on request. This fact sheet summarizes the model’s intended use, validation populations, key subgroup performance, calibration status, operating threshold(s), and the current monitoring policy settings (e.g., starter bands and stop-rule parameters) so that alerts and mitigation actions can be interpreted in context.

Explanations follow a risk-tiered policy keyed to MRI-AI. Low-risk cases receive lightweight explanations, whereas high-risk cases or cases from flagged subgroups receive higher-fidelity explanations and senior review. The ledger records the explanation type and generation latency as part of human–AI interaction telemetry. To support equity, consent materials use plain language and translated versions when needed. The system monitors consent-completion rates and opt-out patterns by subgroup and issues governance alerts when disparities emerge. Clinical leadership reviews periodic summaries of overrides, response delays, and trigger distributions to detect and remediate disparate impact30,31.

Transparency and explainability for reducing AI misdiagnosis

This section connects transparency and explainability requirements to concrete, policy-controlled audit-layer mechanisms, including tiered explanations, latency constraints, and ledger logging .

From principle to measurable transparency

Transparency improves safety only when it is concrete, testable, and available at the time of decision. We therefore treat transparency as an operational control tied to three artifacts. First, a model fact sheet accompanies each deployment and states intended use, validation populations, subgroup performance, calibration status, and current trigger bands; the sheet is versioned and referenced in the accountability ledger32–34. Second, explanations are delivered under a risk-tiered policy linked to the MRI-AI score: low-risk cases receive fast, low-overhead attributions, whereas high-risk or flagged subgroups receive higher-fidelity explanations plus counterfactuals and senior review. Third, transparency is measured with explicit service and quality metrics: explanation latency, determinism across runs, stability to small input changes, and clinician uptake (how often explanations inform overrides). These quantities are logged per case so that teams can audit whether transparency actually reduces misdiagnosis risk rather than adding cognitive load.

Explainability in practice: tools, policy, and metrics

Different data modalities require different tools, but the policy that controls them is unified. For image models, gradient-based methods (e.g., integrated gradients, Grad-CAM variants) give fast localization suitable for triage35; for tabular or multi-modal risk models, additive methods (e.g., SHAP families) provide consistent feature attributions for review and protocol design36; for borderline cases, contrastive or counterfactual explanations help clinicians reason about plausible alternatives37. To make these tools clinically usable, we pre-compute light-weight caches where possible, cap wall-clock time for heavy explanations, and fall back to a summary if the cap is exceeded. The sentinel selects the explanation tier using the current MRI-AI percentile and subgroup tags, and the ledger records explanation type, parameters, and latency.

Explainability quality is itself evaluated. We report (i) fidelity/infidelity-how well an explanation predicts the model’s own output under local perturbations; (ii) stability-how much the explanation changes under small, clinically irrelevant input changes; (iii) localization/face validity for imaging tasks via expert review; and (iv) operational impact-the rate at which explanations precede safe overrides or help catch near-misses. These metrics are summarized alongside calibration (ECE/Brier) so that leadership can see whether more explanation actually translates into fewer diagnostic errors, faster corrections, or fairer outcomes38.

Patient communication and ethical integration

Transparency is incomplete unless clinicians can communicate model reasoning and limits in plain language and document that communication. We therefore pair explanations with patient-facing summaries that state the tool’s role, its known limitations, and the right to a human second opinion37. The consent record in the ledger stores the version of the text shown, language, acceptance or opt-out where applicable, and a pointer to the model version used. When subgroup caveats apply, the summary explicitly flags them and routes the case to the higher explanation tier. To reduce burden, the interface provides concise visual markers and short templated sentences that clinicians can adapt; the system tracks completion and comprehension proxies and alerts governance if disparities emerge across patient groups. In this way, explainability supports autonomy and accountability without disrupting clinical flow, and its effects are observable in the same telemetry that monitors model risk.

Recommendations and future directions for improving AI diagnostic systems

This section evaluates the end-to-end audit layer under controlled synthetic deployment scenarios, reporting both monitoring indicators and alert-centric endpoints that characterize trigger burden and governance behavior.

Technical and ethical strategies to reduce misdiagnosis

Reducing misdiagnosis requires controls that are measurable at deploy time rather than generic best-practice lists. We therefore couple technical rigor with ethical safeguards through three implementation tracks38. First, data curation and drift gating: training and validation must document subgroup composition and compute pre-deployment drift baselines; at deployment, the sentinel monitors PSI and related shift signals and writes them to the ledger, preventing silent degradation before model updates are approved. Second, calibration, uncertainty, and explainability as explicit, auditable components of the deployment workflow: models are shipped with a documented calibration method (e.g., temperature scaling or isotonic), uncertainty telemetry (ECE/Brier and, when feasible, conformal set size), and a risk-tiered explainability policy tied to the MRI-AI percentile so that high-risk or flagged-group cases automatically receive higher-fidelity explanations and senior review. Third, evaluation that matches the field setting: beyond held-out test sets, sites should run the minimal stress-test protocol from this paper, including distribution re-weighting and subgroup-imbalance scenarios; and report overall and worst-group metrics, calibration, MRI-AI, and sentinel trigger characteristics. These summaries, versioned with code/configs, become part of the ledgered release package. By design, each safeguard has an auditable metric and a trigger band, turning “fairness” and “transparency” into enforceable operational targets rather than aspirations.

Triggered escalation protocol and auditability

Responsibility in deployment should be operationalized via a RACI mapping across the lifecycle (see Section “Role allocation and duty of care (RACI mapping)” and Table 3) and enforced through time-bound procedures39. In this section we focus on the escalation logic that follows sentinel breaches. Specifically, triggers emitted by the sentinel (e.g., ΔFPR, PSI, ECE, high MRI-AI) start a documented response timeline: triage within 24 h, a mitigation plan within 72 h, and a root-cause analysis within 14 days, with actions, owners, and outcomes recorded as timestamped ledger entries. If two independent triggers persist across K consecutive windows at the site level, the affected pathway can be placed into a safety mode in which automated recommendations are suspended and human-only decisions resume until recalibration or an updated model passes verification. Regulators and oversight committees can build on this structure by requiring periodic audit exports from the ledger and by recognizing shared-liability models that align institutional duties, clinical decision-making, and vendor update responsibilities in AI-assisted care..

Advancing ethical standards and policy implementation

Ethical principles become effective only when they are implemented as artifacts that patients and auditors can see40. Prior to use, patients receive a plain-language model fact sheet stating intended use, limitations, and known subgroup caveats; the consent record (with language, timestamp, opt-out where applicable, and model version) is stored alongside the case in the ledger. During care, the interface provides concise, risk-tiered explanations; explanation type and latency are logged to quantify human-AI interaction cost and to ensure that transparency improves safety rather than adding noise. Post-deployment, governance dashboards summarize trigger distributions, override rates, delays, and consent completion by subgroup to surface disparate impact; remediation is tracked to closure in the same ledger. Simulation study: synthetic shift and subgroup imbalance

We conducted a scenario-based stress test to evaluate the audit framework under stable conditions and under multiple types and severities of distribution shift. We simulated a binary outcome and a binary subgroup label using a Gaussian-mixture data generator with shared covariance. We evaluated the following deployment scenarios: S0 (no shift), where deployment matches development; S1–S3 (covariate/feature drift) with graded severity (mild, moderate, strong) implemented via controlled mean shifts and additive acquisition noise on a pre-specified subset of features; S4 (subgroup-mixture shift) implemented by changing the deployment proportion of subgroup A versus subgroup B while keeping conditional feature distributions unchanged; and S5 (label/prevalence shift, when labels are available) implemented by changing the positive-class prevalence while keeping feature distributions conditional on the outcome unchanged. The full scenario definitions and parameterization are reported in Supplementary Table S1 and Table S2.

During streaming deployment, indicators were computed on rolling windows (window size W = 400 cases; N = 20 windows). For each window, we computed shift indicators (PSI on salient features and score-distribution drift, measured via KL divergence between score histograms), subgroup disparity (worst-group differences in false positive rate and AUC relative to the overall population at an operating threshold fixed on development), and calibration/uncertainty metrics (ECE and Brier). In addition to these model-performance indicators, we evaluated alert-centric endpoints for the streaming sentinel: window-level alert rate (fraction of windows triggering at least one band breach), time-to-first-trigger (number of windows until the first trigger), and persistence (maximum consecutive triggering windows relative to the stop rule). A window is marked as breached if at least one monitored indicator exceeds its corresponding starter band. Starter bands and stop-rule parameters are treated as policy settings and are reported together with alert behavior. The end-to-end protocol, including window generation, triggering logic, stop-rule actions, and ledger logging, is specified in Supplementary Algorithm S1.

We compared pre- and post-recalibration (temperature scaling) and summarized the overall trigger rate together with an override simulation in which cases in the top MRI-AI quantile were routed to dual-clinician sign-off. Unless otherwise stated, results are reported as descriptive averages over 10 random seeds; no hypothesis testing is performed. Where bracketed intervals are shown, they summarize empirical run-to-run variability (2.5th–97.5th percentiles across repeated runs) rather than inferential confidence intervals.

Decision-analytic evaluation. We evaluated clinical usefulness using decision curve analysis (DCA). For decision thresholds , net benefit (NB) was computed as ,and compared against treat-all and treat-none policies. We report NB at for the model before and after temperature scaling, alongside the two default policies.

Future directions

This study is designed as a computable, scenario-based stress test of an audit layer rather than a clinical validation of a specific diagnostic model. The current evidence is therefore limited by the use of controlled synthetic streams, which are valuable for isolating mechanisms (triggering, persistence, stop-rule behavior, and ledger logging) but cannot fully represent the heterogeneity of real deployment environments, including evolving case mix, site-specific workflows, and clinician adaptation over time. A second limitation is that some monitoring signals rely on delayed or incomplete outcome labels. While the framework accommodates label-availability policies and can operate on feature drift and score-distribution drift without labels, timely detection of label/prevalence shift and concept/conditional shift remains more challenging when ground truth is delayed, and the operational impact of such delays should be quantified in prospective studies. A third limitation is false-alarm burden: any sentinel can over-trigger if bands are too aggressive, if the window size is too small, or if correlated indicators are combined without accounting for dependence. In practice, starter bands, persistence-based stop rules, optional cool-down periods, and site-specific calibration of thresholds and weights are necessary to balance responsiveness against workload, and the optimal policy will vary by clinical context and available response capacity.

Future work should therefore prioritize prospective, multi-site evaluations that measure not only predictive performance but also operational endpoints such as alert rate, time-to-correction, override quality, and equity outcomes under real clinical workflows. Methodologically, research is needed on fast, risk-aware explainability that scales to streaming settings, and on online fairness calibration and dynamic thresholding that adapt under drift without erasing clinically meaningful subgroup differences. In parallel, federated and privacy-preserving auditing should be developed so that sites can share drift and disparity summaries (and potentially policy outcomes) without exposing raw patient data. Finally, governance-focused studies should compare alternative trigger-band designs, stop-rule and escalation policies, and consent/communication designs, linking these operational choices to measurable reductions in misdiagnosis risk and clinician burden in practice.

Conclusion

This paper reframes trustworthy AI in diagnostics as an operational problem and proposes a computable audit layer to reduce misdiagnosis risk. The framework defines a Misdiagnosis Risk Index (MRI-AI) that aggregates data shift, subgroup fairness gaps, calibration/uncertainty, and human–AI interaction signals; implements an online drift-and-bias sentinel with explicit trigger bands and stop rules; and specifies an accountability ledger that standardizes deploy-time logging, consent records, explanation telemetry, and incident timelines. We further provide a minimal, reproducible, scenario-based stress-test protocol (including a no-shift control and multiple shift types and severities) to characterize monitoring behavior in rolling-window deployment, reporting both predictive metrics (overall and worst-group performance and calibration) and alert-centric endpoints (window-level alert rate, time-to-first-trigger, and persistence). Together, these elements move transparency and fairness from principles to enforceable process, enabling auditable monitoring, risk-tiered interventions, and clearer duty allocation across developers, institutions, and clinicians. The current evidence is based on controlled synthetic scenarios and policy-parameterized trigger thresholds that require local tuning; prospective, multi-site evaluations are necessary to quantify real-world false-alarm burden, label-delay effects, workflow impact, and clinical outcomes.
