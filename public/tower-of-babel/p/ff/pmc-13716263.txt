Role of therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) in the management of anti-glomerular basement membrane (anti-GBM) disease: A case series from tertiary-care hospital in India
Authors: Samruddhi Pawar, Aseem Kumar Tiwari, Sunil Golia, Geet Aggarwal, Shubham Gupta, Gunjan Bhardwaj, Shyam Bihari Bansal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
INTRODUCTION:Anti-glomerular basement membrane (anti-GBM) disease is an uncommon, autoimmune disease in which IgG antibodies bind to intrinsic antigens distributed along the length of glomerular basement membrane. It is characterized by rapid and progressive loss of renal function. The rationale of management is to suppress renal inflammation and production of autoantibodies (with immunosuppression), as well as removal of circulating pathogenic autoantibodies (with therapeutic plasma exchange [TPE]). However, there is a paucity of data on the role of TPE in the management of anti-GBM cases worldwide.MATERIALS AND METHODS:Three consecutive anti-GBM patients were admitted with common complaints of high-grade fever, chills, hematuria, and low urine output. In view of decreased renal function and strong suspicion of anti-GBM disease, the treatment protocol was initiated in accordance with the international Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes guidelines.RESULTS:All three patients had crescentic glomerulonephritis (crescents in more >80% glomeruli) on renal biopsy. Management was initiated with TPE and immunosuppression (methylprednisolone) and cytotoxic drug (cyclophosphamide). TPE was done until the anti-GBM antibody titer dropped to <10. Case 1 underwent TPE daily (12 sessions), and case 2 and case 3 underwent 14 and 9 sessions, respectively, of TPE alternating with dialysis. Renal function improved in cases 1 and 2, and routine dialysis was discontinued. Case 3 showed improvement initially until TPE was done; however, the disease progressed to end-stage renal disease, and the patient remains dialysis dependent. None of the patients had adverse reactions during TPE.CONCLUSION:TPE is an effective and safe conjunct in the treatment of anti-GBM disease as shown by complete reversal in two cases and partial response in the third patient.

Introduction

Anti-glomerular basement membrane (GBM) disease is an uncommon, autoimmune glomerular disease with an incidence of approximately 0.5–1.8 cases per million per year in Asian and European populations.[1] It is responsible for 1%–5% of all kinds of glomerulonephritis. GBM antigen is a component of the noncollagenous domain (NC1) of the α3 chain of type IV collagen that is critical for maintenance of GBM suprastructure. Anti-GBM disease is caused by IgG antibodies, predominantly IgG1 and IgG3 subtypes, which bind to intrinsic antigens distributed along the entire length of the GBM.[2]

Anti-GBM disease mostly presents as an isolated kidney disease, a type of rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis (RPGN). Anti-GBM antibodies may cross-react with pulmonary basement membrane in the lung alveoli, resulting in simultaneous lung and kidney lesions (Goodpasture syndrome).[3] Most patients (80%–90%) present clinically with features of RPGN such as rapid onset of oliguria or anuria, hematuria with RBC casts in urine, proteinuria, hypertension, and edema. It may lead to acute renal failure and end-stage renal disease in most cases.[45]

According to the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines, all patients presenting with suspected RPGN should be tested for the presence of anti-GBM antibodies without delay. A high-resolution computed tomography scan is performed to rule out pulmonary hemorrhage. Serological testing is performed for anti-GBM antibodies, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies, and antinuclear antibodies (ANAs) at presentation. Renal biopsy with immunofluorescence should be performed within 24 h of admission.[6]

The rationale of management in anti-GBM disease is to suppress renal inflammation, remove circulating pathogenic autoantibodies (with plasma exchange), and suppress further production of autoantibodies (with immunosuppression). This aims to prevent ongoing renal damage, but it is unable to reverse already established chronic renal damage.[6]

Early diagnosis and management with therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) and immunosuppression has been established to improve patient survival. Two parameters have emerged as reliable predictors of adverse outcome: The fraction of crescentic glomeruli in biopsy specimens and anuria or a serum creatinine level greater than 5 mg/dL (442 µmol/L) at diagnosis.[5]

The American Society for Apheresis (ASFA) guidelines suggest that the prognosis of classical anti-GBM disease is strongly correlated to prompt initiation of therapy. Treatment includes the combination of TPE, cyclophosphamide, and corticosteroids for rapid clearance of antibodies and prevention of further antibody production. In most patients undergoing TPE and immunosuppression, anti-GBM antibodies fall to undetectable levels within 2 weeks; thus, the minimum course of TPE should be 10–20 days. It is generally recommended that the treatment begin daily for 2–3 weeks or until the anti-GBM level is undetectable.[7]

There is a paucity of data on the management of anti-GBM cases worldwide. Therefore, we would like to share our experience of managing three consecutive anti-GBM disease patients at our institute with TPE and immunosuppression.

Cases

Clinical presentation

Case 1

A 32-year-old male presented with a history of fever of 1-week duration. Upon evaluation for fever, the patient was incidentally detected to have microscopic hematuria on urine microscopy and deranged renal function tests. Renal biopsy revealed anti-GBM disease with cellular crescents and an initial anti-GBM titre of 359.

Case 2

A 32-year-old male presented with complaints of fever followed by decreased urine output and passage of dark colored urine. The presence of crescentic glomerulonephritis with necrotizing lesions on renal biopsy along with deranged renal function tests and an anti-GBM titer of 100 confirmed the diagnosis.

Case 3

A 53-year-old male was admitted outside with a history of high-grade fever and hematuria; urine culture had detected Escherichia coli growth, for which the patient was started on intravenous meropenem (anti-bacterial). Renal biopsy done proved anti-GBM disease, and the patient was then referred to our institute for further management having received pulse steroid therapy, intravenous cyclophosphamide, and hemodialysis for deranged renal function tests.

Investigations done at the time of admission and renal biopsy findings of all three patients are summarized in Table 1. There was no evidence of diffuse alveolar hemorrhage on computerized tomography scan of the thorax in either of the patients. All three patients were negative for ANA and antineutrophilic cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) and had crescentic glomerulonephritis on renal biopsy with 3+ IgG3 linear staining on immunofluorescence characteristic of anti-GBM disease.

Management

Management was initiated in accordance with KDIGO and 2023 ASFA guidelines. Management in all three cases included TPE along with immunosuppression with intravenous methylprednisolone and cytotoxic drug (cyclophosphamide).

Therapeutic plasma exchange

TPE was carried out using a Comtec continuous-flow blood-cell separator (Fresenius Kabi). Each patient had a nontunneled catheter inserted which enabled TPE to be undertaken. In each TPE session, 1–1.5 volumes of total plasma volume were exchanged with approximately four liters of the patient’s plasma being exchanged for 5% albumin solution as replacement fluid. However, when TPE was carried out within 72 h of a renal biopsy, 500 ml of fresh-frozen plasma was added as replacement fluid, toward the end of the procedure to restore clotting factors. TPE was initiated immediately upon admission and continued until the anti-GBM antibody titer dropped to <10.

Clinical course and implications

Assessment of renal function was done by serial monitoring of laboratory parameters (renal function tests and anti-GBM antibody titers) and daily urine output. Case 1

The anti-GBM titer level was 359 on admission. Three doses of pulse methylprednisolone and the first dose of cyclophosphamide 500 mg along with antiviral and antibacterial prophylaxis were started upon admission. In view of nonoliguric disease with cellular crescents, the patient was started on TPE via a right internal jugular vein nontunneled catheter. After doing TPE daily for 5 days, the repeat anti-GBM titer was found to be 79. Daily TPE was continued, and the patient was started on oral cyclophosphamide at 125 mg per day. As shown in Figure 2, the anti-GBM titer progressively decreased further with continuation of TPE to 17 after 8 sessions and 12 after 10 sessions. A total of 12 sessions of TPE were done, after which the anti-GBM titer was found to be <5. There was a significant improvement in renal function, evidenced by the decreasing serum creatinine and increasing urine output [Figure 1a], suggesting a positive response to TPE and immunosuppressive therapy. The serum creatinine was stable in the range of 5–5.5, and the patient remained nonoliguric through the course of admission. The medications were tolerated well and did not have any cytopenias during the hospital stay. The patient was discharged in a nonoliguric state with a serum creatinine of 4.4 mg/dl in a hemodynamically stable state on maintenance immunosuppressive therapy.

Case 2

The patient had a creatinine level of 11.1 mg/dl on admission and thus was started on hemodialysis alternating with TPE. Medical management involved pulse steroid therapy with 3 doses of 500 mg of methylprednisolone, followed by oral prednisolone and oral cyclophosphamide. Despite an initial decline in urine output and serum creatinine, both parameters improved significantly over time, and the patient became symptomatically better. As shown in Figure 1b, the fluctuating yet overall decreasing serum creatinine levels indicate a slow but steady recovery of renal function, demonstrating the benefits of sustained TPE and immunosuppressive therapy. The anti-GBM titer decreased from 100 on admission to 60 after 5 sessions of TPE and eventually decreased to <5 after 14 sessions of TPE alternating with hemodialysis [Figure 2]. The patient developed thrombocytopenia during hospital stay, most likely drug-induced which improved later. He also received 2 packed red blood cell unit transfusions during hospital stay for low hemoglobin levels. Hemodialysis was discontinued, and the patient was discharged in a stable condition with a serum creatinine level of 3.8 mg/dl.

Case 3

The patient was referred to our institute and was hemodialysis dependent on presentation. On admission, the anti-GBM titer was 74, and a review of the renal biopsy block revealed cellular crescents in all glomeruli along with features of diffuse acute tubular injury, 10%–15% tubular atrophy, and interstitial fibrosis changes. He underwent nine sessions of TPE alternating with hemodialysis, and the anti-GBM titer decreased to 28 after 5 sessions of TPE and further to <5 after 9 sessions. TPE was then discontinued, but the patient continued to have renal dysfunction with fluid overload features, in view of which maintenance hemodialysis was continued. As shown in Figure 1c, this patient exhibited the most fluctuation and the least improvement in serum creatinine levels, indicating ongoing renal impairment. Despite initial improvements in urine output, the patient’s renal function remained compromised, as evidenced by the less significant decrease in serum creatinine and the continued need for dialysis. He developed thrombocytopenia during hospital stay, for which one unit of single-donor apheresis platelets was transfused. The patient was hemodynamically stable but hemodialysis dependent at discharge in view of nonrecovery of renal function (serum creatinine 8.6 mg/dl).

Discussion

The three cases presented illustrate the clinical manifestations, diagnosis, and management of anti-GBM disease. This uncommon autoimmune disease is characterized by the presence of circulating anti-GBM antibodies, which target the glomerular and alveolar basement membranes, leading to RPGN and, in some cases, pulmonary hemorrhage.

All three patients presented with high-grade fever, chills, hematuria, and reduced urine output, consistent with the acute presentation of anti-GBM disease. Laboratory investigations revealed elevated serum creatinine and blood urea levels, indicating renal impairment, and urinalysis showed significant hematuria and proteinuria. The diagnosis was confirmed by renal biopsy, which revealed crescentic glomerulonephritis and strong linear staining for IgG3 on immunofluorescence, hallmark features of anti-GBM disease.[1] Additionally, the presence of elevated anti-GBM antibody titers further supported the diagnosis.

Management of anti-GBM disease requires prompt initiation of immunosuppressive therapy and plasmapheresis (TPE) to remove circulating antibodies and mitigate renal and pulmonary damage. Current ASFA guidelines (2023) recommend daily TPE with albumin or fresh frozen plasma as replacement fluid, if diffuse alveolar hemorrhage is present, for a minimum of 10–20 days and until resolution of active glomerular and/or pulmonary injury. In accordance with KDIGO guidelines, all three patients received intravenous methylprednisolone and cyclophosphamide, along with TPE using 5% albumin as replacement fluid.[7]

Case 1 responded well to daily TPE sessions, with significant improvement in renal function and no requirement for hemodialysis. Case 2 also showed improvement, leading to the discontinuation of routine hemodialysis. However, case 3, who was hemodialysis-dependent at presentation, showed only temporary improvement and ultimately progressed to end-stage renal disease, necessitating continued hemodialysis.

The outcomes observed in these cases were consistent with the variable prognosis of anti-GBM disease reported in the literature. McAdoo et al. noted that timely diagnosis and aggressive treatment are critical for improving renal outcomes, particularly in patients without significant chronic damage at presentation. However, patients presenting with severe renal impairment or who are hemodialysis-dependent, like case 3, often have a poorer prognosis despite intensive therapy.[4]

The management approach in these cases adhered closely to the KDIGO guidelines, which recommend the use of TPE and immunosuppressive therapy as the standard of care for anti-GBM disease. The use of daily TPE sessions until a significant reduction in antibody titers was achieved aligns with the recommended treatment protocol aimed at reducing antibody-mediated damage.

Published information on anti-GBM disease from the Indian subcontinent is scarce. Despite the modest number of patients, this series offers important clinical insights and adds to the body of knowledge. In our experience, earlier initiation of TPE (as in case 1) correlated with better renal outcomes, reaffirming that the timing of TPE may significantly influence prognosis – particularly in settings where diagnostic and treatment delays are common. The variation in biopsy findings and renal recovery further underscores the importance of histopathological assessment in predicting response. Notably, partial renal recovery was observed in one dialysis-dependent patient, suggesting that aggressive therapy may be beneficial even in advanced disease. Our series also reflects real-world limitations frequently encountered in resource-constrained settings, including delays in initiating TPE, financial barriers, and limited access to immunosuppressive therapy.

A few Indian studies have previously provided valuable insights into the clinicopathological spectrum and outcome predictors in anti-GBM disease, particularly highlighting the association between dialysis dependence at presentation, extensive crescent formation on biopsy, and poor renal survival.[89101112] Building on this foundation, the current case series offers a complementary perspective by focusing on the role of TPE as a key component of disease management. Monitoring daily changes in serum creatinine and urine output in relation to each TPE session allows for a dynamic assessment of patient response to therapy. Such real-time clinical tracking may aid transfusion medicine and apheresis professionals in optimizing the timing and delivery of TPE, especially in resource-limited settings where early intervention can significantly influence renal outcomes. Incorporation of daily clinical parameters into treatment protocols should be considered to enhance personalized patient care.

Conclusion

TPE is an effective and safe conjunct in the treatment of anti-GBM disease along with immunosuppression, as shown by complete reversal in two cases and partial response in the third patient in this series. Continued adherence to established guidelines and further research into optimizing treatment strategies are essential for improving the prognosis of this challenging disease.
