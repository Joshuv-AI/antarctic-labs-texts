Whole-genome profiling of antimicrobial resistance and virulence determinants in extensively-drug resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa isolates causing ventilator associated pneumonia in Egypt
Authors: Sabah M Alkhawagah, Shaimaa Elattar, Asmaa A Elmadbouly, Shimaa A Abdel Salam, Fatma A Ahmed, Hala Sabry Alsaedy, Ahmed F Gad, Hossam I Gebeer, Mohamed G Seadawy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmong critically ill ICU patients under prolonged mechanical ventilation, Pseudomonas aeruginosa is one of the most common cause of ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), with antimicrobial pressure leading to emergence of multidrug, extensively drug and pandrug-resistant (PDR) strains. In Egypt, very little genomic data exist on P. aeruginosa associated with VAP. This study aimed at characterizing the antimicrobial resistance (AMR) determinants, virulence repertoire, MGEs, and sequence types of two highly drug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa isolates, including one pandrug-resistant colistin-resistant isolate and one extensively drug-resistant colistin-susceptible isolate from respiratory tract of Egyptian ICU patients suffering from VAP. The two isolates were identified conventionally and confirmed to the species level using MALDI-TOF MS. Antibiotic susceptibility was assessed using the VITEK-2 Compact system and the broth microdilution method. Genome analysis was performed using PATRIC, ResFinder, CARD, and Mobile Element Finder. For both isolates, resistance was found to all antibiotics routinely tested, however, one isolate had high-level colistin resistance (MIC > 64 µg/mL), while the other isolate was still colistin susceptible. Whole-genome sequencing identified two rare sequence types, ST2023 and ST2685, both 6.5–7.6 Mb in size with a 66% GC content. The presence of 21 MGEs in the SRR36105565 genome shows that it has high genomic flexibility, including a broader resistome than other strains, such as blaVIM-2 and OXA variants, aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes, crpP, and disinfectant-resistance markers. Both isolates retained large virulence determinants including Type III and Type VI secretion systems, alginate regulation genes, quorum-sensing networks, and siderophore biosynthesis clusters. It also represents one of the first genomic studies of VAP associated PDR P. aeruginosa from Egypt. The combination of widespread AMR with intact virulence supports the potential value of future genomic surveillance efforts and improved antimicrobial stewardship in local ICUs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-13952-w.Keywords: Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), Pandrug-resistant (PDR) strains, ICU infections, Colistin resistance, Whole-genome sequencing

Introduction

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a rod-shaped aerobic Gram-negative opportunistic pathogen that can adapt to a wide variety of ecological niches probably because of its large genome, metabolic versatility, and complex regulatory systems. This allows it to be an important contributor to health care-associated infections, especially in severely ill or immunocompromised persons [11]. As estimated within the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2019 study, P. aeruginosa is one of the six most common bacterial pathogens linked to disease and mortality, the second most common pathogen associated with hospital and community acquired pneumonia, and the fifth major cause of infection-associated deaths around the world [6]. Outside the context of respiratory infections, it is often associated with catheter-related urinary tract infections (CAUTIs), catheter-related bloodstream infections (CRBSIs), and surgical site infections [18]. Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a significant global health threat due to the rise in antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa is an urgent threat according to the World Health Organization which has identified carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa as a critical priority pathogen to focus on for developing new therapeutic and diagnostic strategies. Based on the resistance to any drug class, clinical isolates may be either multidrug-resistant (MDR), extensively drug-resistant (XDR), or pandrug-resistant (PDR, i.e., resistant to all antimicrobial agents that are tested as part of routine clinical care) [8]. This disturbing resistance profile arises from a constellation of intrinsic mechanisms including efflux pumps, reduced outer membrane permeation and inducible β-lactamases and a number of acquired mechanisms such as carbapenemases, aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes and horizontal acquisition of resistance genes [9]. The P. aeruginosa genome, which is 5.5 to 7 Mb, is composed of a highly conserved core genome and a diverse accessory genome, supporting complex interbacterial communication and horizontal gene transfer [10]. Such genomic plasticity augments its survival in the severe clinical environment, persistence under antimicrobial pressure, and virulence capacity. Previous genomic studies have reported the emergence of high-risk clones in ICUs with increased resistance and adaptability was described in multiple genomic studies supporting the use of whole-genome sequencing (WGS) for surveillance and outbreak investigations [4]. However, the present study was designed as a preliminary genomic characterization of two highly drug-resistant isolates rather than an epidemiological investigation. Despite the global knowledge of P. aeruginosa as VAP-associated organism, limited data are available for the genomic diversity and AMR determinants of VAP-associated isolates in Egypt. In support of the need for genomic profiling to identify new virulence and resistance determinants, a WGS-based study on a VAP isolate showed this feature [1]. But detailed genomic analysis of local PDR isolates is still rare. The global problem of drug-resistant P. aeruginosa, compounded by the increasing incidence of such infections among ICU patients, necessitates a detailed analysis of the mobilome, AMR determinants, and virulence potential of two PDR P. aeruginosa strains isolated from lung biopsies of Egyptian VAP patients. It is one of the preliminary genomic studies of VAP-related PDR isolates in Egypt. This preliminary genomic characterization may provide useful baseline data for future surveillance-oriented studies and expanded genomic monitoring efforts in ICU-associated infections.

Methods

Ethical approval

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Military Medical Academy, Cairo, Egypt (Approval No. 17-2025).

Study design and participants

This prospective cross-sectional study included two highly drug-resistant P. aeruginosa isolates recovered from ICU patients with ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), later classified according to standardized MDR/XDR/PDR definitions based on phenotypic susceptibility profiles at Al-Zahraa University Hospital between November 2024 and May 2025. Clinical and demographic data were collected directly from medical records.

Bacterial isolation and identification

Endotracheal aspirate (ETA) samples were cultured on blood agar, chocolate agar, and MacConkey agar (Oxoid, UK) and incubated aerobically at 35 ± 2 °C for 24 h, with chocolate agar plates incubated under 5% CO₂. Identification was based on colony morphology, Gram staining, and conventional biochemical reactions. Final confirmation was performed using MALDI-TOF MS (LT2 Plus, Scientific Analysis Instruments, UK).

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Susceptibility to β-lactams, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, and colistin was assessed using the VITEK-2 Compact automated system. Colistin MIC values were confirmed via broth microdilution according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) 2024 guidelines (bioMérieux, France). All isolates were preserved in 20% glycerol stocks at − 80 °C.

Multidrug-resistant (MDR), extensively drug-resistant (XDR), and pandrug-resistant (PDR) phenotypes were defined according to standardized international criteria. MDR was defined as non-susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories, XDR as non-susceptibility to at least one agent in all but two or fewer antimicrobial categories, and PDR as non-susceptibility to all agents in all antimicrobial categories tested. Final classification was based on phenotypic susceptibility testing, including broth microdilution confirmation of colistin MICs.

Complete AST profiles and interpretive criteria are summarized in Supplementary Table S2.

DNA extraction and whole-genome sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA concentration and purity were evaluated using the Qubit™ dsDNA HS Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer, respectively. Library preparation was performed using the Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) Native Barcoding Kit 24 V14, with 400 ng input DNA. Sequencing was conducted on a PromethION Flow Cell (R10.4.1) using MinKNOW v24.06.5. Basecalling was performed using the high-accuracy ONT basecalling model implemented within MinKNOW v24.06.5. Reads with Q-scores ≥ 10 were retained for downstream analysis. Sequencing quality metrics demonstrated high-quality long-read datasets for both isolates. SRR36105565 generated 1,350,500 reads totaling 6.86 Gb with an N50 of 8,103 bp and mean genome depth of 752×, while SRR36105564 generated 121,177 reads totaling 741 Mb with an N50 of 15,398 bp and mean depth of 100×. Genome coverage exceeded 96% for both isolates, with Q30 values above 84%, supporting robust downstream assembly and comparative genomic analyses. Alignment statistics were generated using Minimap2 against the Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO1 reference genome. Detailed sequencing quality and assembly statistics are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Genome assembly was performed using the PATRIC bacterial genome assembly workflow. Assembly improvement/polishing was performed within the same workflow prior to downstream annotation. Species identity and possible contamination were assessed using genome-based comparison against Pseudomonas aeruginosa reference genomes, tetranucleotide correlation analysis, and Minimap2 alignment to the P. aeruginosa PAO1 reference genome.

Genome assembly, annotation, and AMR profiling

Genome assembly and annotation were performed using the PATRIC platform, incorporating RASTtk for functional annotation. Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes were detected using ResFinder as well as PATRIC’s curated AMR gene database, enabling prediction of resistance pathways and functional annotation.

Genomic similarity between the two isolates was calculated by using tetranucleotide correlation analysis (TETRA) method via JSpeciesWS server. Genome annotation was performed using the PATRIC platform with the RASTtk pipeline. Antimicrobial resistance genes were identified using ResFinder and the CARD Resistance Gene Identifier (RGI) pipeline with default thresholds. Virulence-associated genes were screened using ABRicate with the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB) applying ≥ 90% nucleotide identity and ≥ 80% coverage thresholds. MLST analysis was performed using the PubMLST Pseudomonas aeruginosa scheme based on seven housekeeping genes. Mobile genetic elements were identified using MobileElementFinder with curated reference databases.

AMR determinants were categorized into acquired resistance genes, intrinsic chromosomal resistance determinants, efflux-associated systems, and regulatory loci to avoid overinterpretation of genotype–phenotype associations.

Phylogenetic analysis

Phylogenetic placement was inferred using Mash/MinHash to identify the closest reference and representative genomes. Protein sequences belonging to PATRIC global protein families (PGFams) were aligned using MUSCLE, and nucleotide sequences were mapped to the protein alignment. A combined matrix of amino acid and nucleotide alignments was constructed and analyzed using RAxML with 100 bootstrap replicates.

Mobile genetic elements (MGE) identification

Mobile Element Finder (CGE server) was used to identify mobile genetic elements and assess their genomic context and potential association with AMR determinants. The database contained 4,452 curated MGE entries.

Multilocus sequence typing (MLST)

MLST was performed using the standard seven housekeeping genes (acsA, aroE, guaA, mutL, nuoD, ppsA, and trpE), enabling sequence type assignment for each isolate.

Detection of virulence genes

Virulence-associated genes were identified using ABRicate on the Galaxy platform with the Virulence Factors Database (VFDB). Predictions were filtered using ≥ 90% nucleotide identity and ≥ 80% coverage thresholds. Outputs were functionally categorized into adherence, secretion systems, toxins, biofilm formation, and iron acquisition pathways.

Antimicrobial resistance profile

Both isolates showed resistance to most routinely tested antimicrobial classes. However, broth microdilution confirmed divergent colistin susceptibility: SRR36105565 was highly colistin-resistant (MIC > 64 µg/mL), whereas SRR36105564 remained colistin-susceptible (MIC = 2 µg/mL), these phenotypic resistance profiles supported classification of SRR36105565 as a colistin-resistant pandrug-resistant (PDR) isolate and SRR36105564 as an XDR/near-PDR isolate (Supplementary Table S2). Accordingly, SRR36105565 was classified as PDR, while SRR36105564 was classified as XDR/near-PDR rather than PDR. No plasmid-mediated mcr genes were detected in either isolate. Screening of known chromosomal colistin-resistance–associated loci was beyond the scope of the current study. Detailed antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) profiles and MIC interpretations are summarized in Supplementary Table S2.

Whole-genome sequencing and MLST

Genome features

Genome assemblies showed excellent sequencing depth and high-quality metrics. The genome sequence data were submitted in NCBI under the BioProject ID PRJNA1364343. The accession no. of the two isolates are SRR36105565 and SRR36105564. Detailed sequencing quality statistics are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

SRR36105565 is ~ 7,619,648 bp, distributed across 4 contigs with GC content 65.47%, and SRR36105564 is ~ 6,539,855 bp distributed across 1 contig with a GC content 66.30%.

Sequencing quality metrics indicated high-quality long-read datasets for both isolates. SRR36105565 generated 1,350,500 reads totaling 6.86 Gb with an N50 of 8,103 bp and mean genome depth of 752×, while SRR36105564 generated 121,177 reads totaling 741 Mb with an N50 of 15,398 bp and mean depth of 100×. Genome coverage exceeded 96% for both isolates, with Q30 values above 84%, supporting robust downstream assembly and comparative genomic analyses.

Tetranucleotide correlation analysis showed a high genomic similarity between two isolates, TETRA = 0.99679, confirming their classification within the same species (Pseudomonas aeruginosa). Subsystem annotations identified genes related to AMR, virulence, metabolic pathways, and regulatory networks. Visual genome maps are illustrated in Fig. 1.

Antimicrobial resistance genes (AMR)

AMR profiling identified a combination of acquired resistance genes, intrinsic chromosomal resistance determinants, and multiple efflux-associated loci.

The detected genes in SRR36105565 were β-lactamases including blaVIM-2, OXA-10, OXA-1284, PDC-62, Aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes including ant(2'')-Ia, aac(6’)-Ib3, aadA1, aph(3’)-Ia, Folate pathway resistance including sul1, dfrA16, Quinolone resistance including crpP, and Multiple efflux pumps (Mex, Opr, and Tri systems), and the detected genes in SRR36105564 were β-lactamases including blaPAO, blaOXA-395, Aminoglycoside resistance including aph(3’)-IIb, Fosfomycin resistance including fosA, and xtensive multidrug efflux pumps (MexF, MexGHI, MexN, MexQ).K-mer based prediction of antimicrobial resistance determinants was carried out using the CARD platform. It allows the reliable identification of resistance-related genetic signatures from metagenomic contigs, complete assemblies and individual genomes.CARD analysis revealed putative sources and mechanisms of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes as well as relevant antimicrobial classes for the SRR36105565 and SRR36105564 strains. The perfect AMR finidings identified by CARD are summarized in (Fig. 3; Table 3), and (Fig. 3; Table 4) respectively as these represent genes with 100% sequence match, and strict AMR findings with high confidence homologs but not identical totally with the resistance genes were summarized separately in supplementary file 1. In P. aeruginosa SRR36105565 and SRR36105564, comparative AMR profiling identified marked drug class differences in resistance determinants. Figure 4 shows that SRR36105564 showed a broader distribution of detected resistance-associated loci, particularly involving multidrug efflux-associated systems and regulatory determinants. By contrast, SRR36105565 showed a narrower resistome with fewer gene counts among most of the drug classes. Comparative AMR profiling identified differences in the distribution of resistance-associated loci between the two isolates. SRR36105564 showed a broader distribution of detected resistance-associated determinants, particularly involving multidrug efflux-associated systems and regulatory loci, whereas SRR36105565 carried a broader repertoire of acquired β-lactamases and aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes. These differences may reflect variation in accessory resistance determinants and genomic resistance-associated content between the two isolates. However, the presence of intrinsic resistance-associated loci and efflux systems alone should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of phenotypic resistance without functional or transcriptional validation.

Comparative genomic and phenotypic features of SRR36105565 and SRR36105564

Although both strains displayed highly drug-resistant phenotypes, only SRR36105565 fulfilled phenotypic criteria for pandrug resistance, whereas SRR36105564 was classified as XDR/near-PDR due to retained colistin susceptibility. SRR36105565 carried a broader repertoire of β-lactamases and aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes and harbored more mobile genetic elements than SRR36105564. In contrast, SRR36105564 retained phenotypic susceptibility to colistin despite a rich efflux pump landscape. Both genomes encoded extensive virulence determinants, including flagellar and Type IV pili systems, alginate/biofilm regulators, quorum-sensing networks, phenazine and siderophore biosynthesis clusters, and Type VI secretion systems, underscoring the convergence of high-level antimicrobial resistance and preserved pathogenic potential in ventilator-associated pneumonia (Table 6). Discussion

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a model pathogen in translational science. P. aeruginosa is among the most challenging global health problems in the 21st century because of the capacity of this organism to adjust and thrive, its intrinsic mechanisms of resistance, and its ability to gain new determinants under the selective pressure of antimicrobial agents. An increase of MDR, XDR and PDR P. aeruginosa amongst ICU patients has recently been reported, associated with increased mortality, longer hospital stays and limited treatment options [8, 16]. The two isolates analyzed in this study, both recovered from fatal VAP cases, exhibited highly drug-resistant phenotypes, including one pandrug-resistant colistin-resistant isolate and one extensively drug-resistant colistin-susceptible isolate, highlighting the urgent threat of high-level resistant lineages circulating in Egyptian ICUs. One important clinical observation was the disparate colistin resistance of SRR36105565 (high-level resistant) and the SRR36105564 (susceptible). Although colistin is still a last-line agent, resistance to colistin due to lipid A modifications, pmrAB mutations, and regulatory changes in phoPQ systems is rising globally [7, 15]. The absence of plasmid-borne mcr genes may suggest the possibility of chromosomally mediated colistin resistance mechanisms in SRR36105565, although variant-level analysis of known regulatory loci was not performed in the current study, as suggested by more recent genomic studies indicating that colistin-resistance in P. aeruginosa is primarily non-plasmid-mediated and is frequently associated with adaptive mutations [13]. However, variant-level analysis of known polymyxin-resistance loci, including pmrAB, phoPQ, parRS, arn operon genes, and lipid A modification pathways, was not performed in the current study. Therefore, the precise molecular basis of colistin resistance remains to be further investigated. ST2023 and ST2685 are uncommon and underrepresented sequence types in currently available genomic databases (unlikely in clonal complexes with respect to globally well-known high-risk clones ST111, ST175, or ST235 [10]. Under studied and emerging STs have been also frequently reported from low- and middle-income countries, where genomic surveillance is scarce [4]. While the identification of these unusual STs here supports the potential utility of expanded genomic surveillance approaches for improving characterization of highly drug-resistant ICU-associated isolates in Egypt, monitoring the introduction of and spread of high-risk clones and, ultimately, supporting future surveillance-based investigations of ICU-associated resistant strains in Egypt. Various β-lactamases were identified from SRR36105565 by AMR profiling, including blaVIM-2 (a common treatment failure-associated carbapenemase) and other OXA variants. Several high-throughput whole-genome sequencing studies have shown that the presence of VIM metallo-β-lactamases and OXA enzymes can synergistically enhance the level of resistance to carbapenem [5, 12]. SRR36105564 although having less number of β-lactamases, had an holding of fosA, aph(3’’)-IIb, and also had a many operons for efflux pump responsible for multidrug resistence. The presence of multiple efflux-associated loci may contribute to reduced antimicrobial susceptibility, although gene expression analysis was not performed in the current study, when combined with downregulation of porins and enzymatic inactivation [3, 9]. However, genomic plasticity analyses indicated a much wider variety of mobile genetic elements (MGEs) in SRR36105565 than in SRR36105564. SRR36105565 carried a higher number of detected mobile genetic elements compared with SRR36105564, suggesting greater genomic plasticity within this isolate [17]. First, both isolates completely lack plasmids which is consistent with previously reported notions on P. aeruginosa, showing a preferential use of chromosomal integration instead of plasmid-mediated resistance when compared to Enterobacterales [14]. In contrast, key virulence gene clusters were retained in both isolates, such as Type III and Type VI secretion systems; alginate biosynthesis regulators (algU, mucA–D, algD); quorum-sensing networks (las, rhl); phenazine biosynthesis loci; and siderophore systems (pyochelin, pyoverdine). Recent genomic studies have demonstrated that this high-level resistance is not associated with within-host virulence cost, so for VAP, bacteria with high-level resistance and high virulence may coexist leading to significant disease [2, 11]. Such an observation is clinically relevant as the preserved virulence in PDR strains may indicate preservation of pathogenic potential despite extensive antimicrobial resistance. VAP isolates from PDR P. aeruginosa in this study represent a preliminary whole-genome characterization of highly drug-resistant VAP-associated P. aeruginosa isolates from Egypt. Measures to slow the increasing burden of drug-resistant P. aeruginosa infections include strengthening national genomic surveillance, integrating WGS into routine diagnostic workflowsm and enhancing antimicrobial stewardship, particularly in intensive care units.

This study has several limitations, including the small number of isolates analyzed, the absence of transcriptomic validation of efflux-associated determinants, and the lack of variant-level analysis of chromosomal polymyxin-resistance loci. Therefore, the identified genomic determinants should be interpreted as resistance-associated signatures rather than definitive predictors of phenotypic resistance. A limitation of the current study is the absence of variant-level analysis for chromosomal colistin-resistance determinants, which would be required to suggest the molecular mechanisms underlying the resistant phenotype observed in SRR36105565. The phylogenetic analysis in the current study was intended primarily for broad genomic contextualization and species-level comparison rather than detailed epidemiological reconstruction.

Conclusion

We describe here the genomic and phenotypic characterization of two highly drug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa isolates, comprising one PDR colistin-resistant isolate and one XDR/near-PDR colistin-susceptible isolate from Egyptian ICU patients with Ventilator Associated Pneumonia. Despite the absence of plasmids both isolates harbored extensive AMR determinants, diverse virulence factors and active mobile genetic elements. The identification of unique sequence types (ST2023 and ST2685) further highlights the evolving genotypic diversity of this organism in Egypt. Altogether, these results underline the critical need for routine whole-genome sequencing, enhanced antimicrobial stewardship and infection control in critical-care wards. The establishment of expanded local genomic databases may support earlier detection and monitoring of emerging high-risk clones in ICU settin, direct therapy against them, and ultimately minimise the rising burden of multidrug- and pandrug-resistant P. aeruginosa.
