In Utero Electroporation Uncovers an Early-Differentiating Subset of Dentate Gyrus Progenitors
Authors: Hiroshi M Shinohara, Tokiharu Takahashi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe dentate gyrus of the hippocampus develops through complex cellular migrations and differentiations, which have been primarily characterized using genetic lineage tracing approaches. Through systematic application of in utero electroporation across developmental stages, we found that labeling was most effective at embryonic day 12.5 (E12.5), as earlier stages resulted in embryonic lethality while later stages showed markedly reduced efficiency. To directly compare these cells with genetically-defined progenitor populations, we established a novel dual-visualization system, combining electroporation with transgenic reporter mice (Gfap-GFP). This approach revealed marked differences in developmental trajectories: Gfap-GFP+ cells maintain undifferentiated neural stem/progenitor characteristics with persistent Sox2 expression, while E12.5-labeled cells predominantly differentiate into Prox1-positive granule cells by E18.5. These early-labeled cells display characteristic migration patterns, with 60.9% differentiating into Prox1-positive granule cells compared to only 22.8% of Gfap-GFP+ cells (P < 0.001), exclusively following an outside-in trajectory to establish the initial framework of the granule cell layer, without reaching the tertiary dentate matrix. In contrast, Gfap-GFP+ cells populate the tertiary dentate matrix and serve as a sustained progenitor reservoir. Molecular marker analysis reveals sequential expression of Sox2, Tbr2, and Prox1, demonstrating progressive differentiation during migration. Our findings identify an early-differentiating subset of dentate progenitors with accelerated neurogenic progression, revealing previously unrecognized temporal and functional heterogeneity in dentate development. This study demonstrates how stage-specific in utero electroporation can complement genetic approaches by uncovering progenitor subsets with rapid differentiation kinetics, providing new insights into the cellular diversity that shapes hippocampal structure and function.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10571-025-01616-3.Keywords: Dentate gyrus, In utero hippocampal electroporation, Dentate progenitor cells, Granule cells, Dentate migration

Introduction

The hippocampus plays fundamental roles in learning, memory formation, and emotional processing as a key component of the limbic system. Its complex structure, comprising the hippocampal pyramidal cells layer of the cornu ammonius (CA1, CA2, and CA3 regions) and the granule cells layer (GCL) of the dentate gyrus (DG), develops through intricate cellular migrations and differentiations. Among these regions, the subgranular zone of the DG is particularly notable as a unique neurogenic niche where neurogenesis continues throughout life (Altman 1963; Seki and Arai 1993; Alvarez-Buylla and Lim 2004; Zhao et al. 2008). During development, undifferentiated cells migrate from the dentate notch (DN) through the dentate migratory stream (DMS) to establish the GCL (Li and Pleasure 2014; Hayashi et al. 2015), although the precise behaviors and properties of these migrating progenitors remain to be fully elucidated.

The developmental process of the DG follows a remarkably unique and complex trajectory, involving cell migration along pathways that differ significantly from those of pyramidal cells in the neocortex and CA1 (Kitazawa et al. 2014). Previous research (Altman and Bayer 1990a, 1990b; Li et al. 2009; Seki et al. 2014) has shown that neural progenitors that later differentiate into granule cells (GCs) in the DG exhibit a unique behavioral pattern, migrating between three distinct proliferative matrices: the primary dentate proliferative matrix (PDM) in the ventricular zone, the secondary dentate proliferative matrix (SDM) in the limbus, and the tertiary dentate proliferative matrix (TDM) in the hilus, thereby changing their proliferative zones (as illustrated in Fig. 1A). The spatial organization of these matrices follows a specific pattern: the PDM resides in the neuroepithelium superior to the fimbria, adjacent to the DN. Following initial proliferation in the PDM, progenitor cells migrate above the cortical hem to the fimbrio-dentate junction (FDJ) along the pial membrane. Subsequently, these cells proceed along the pial membrane toward the prospective DG via the DMS. During this process, neural progenitor cells undergo proliferation and establish the SDM in the region beneath the hippocampal fissure extending to the pial membrane at the DG limbus (Li and Pleasure 2014). These SDM-derived neural progenitors generate GCs in the outer shell of the GCL, while their TDM counterparts, which form in the hilus, give rise to GCs in the inner core of the GCL.

Our current understanding of these complex migratory patterns mainly comes from studies using transgenic reporter mice, particularly those expressing fluorescent proteins under specific promoters. For example, mice with the Gfap promoter-driven reporter have shown that neural stem/progenitor cells emerge at E14.5 and contribute significantly to DG formation (Seki et al. 2014). Although genetic approaches, particularly Cre-lox-based lineage tracing with tamoxifen-inducible control (Li et al. 2009; Hodge et al. 2013; Berg et al. 2019), have provided valuable insights, they are inherently limited by their dependence on pre-selected promoters of known genetic markers. This reliance on specific molecular markers potentially overlooks progenitor populations that lack expression of these canonical genes, particularly during earlier developmental stages when regulatory networks may be distinct. Complementary approaches that can label and track cells independently of specific gene expression would help eliminate potential biases and provide a more comprehensive understanding of DG development.

Direct labeling approaches, particularly in utero electroporation, have served as a pioneering method in developmental neurobiology since their introduction in the early 2000s. This technique first revealed crucial insights into neocortical development, including characterization of the inside-out migration pattern and other fundamental developmental processes (Fukuchi-Shimogori and Grove 2001; Saito and Nakatsuji 2001; Tabata and Nakajima 2001, 2003; Hatanaka et al. 2004; Mizutani and Saito 2005; Tabata et al. 2009; Sekine et al. 2011). A particular strength of this technique lies in its ability to enable precise spatiotemporal targeting along the ventricular surface at defined developmental stages while providing unbiased labeling of progenitors regardless of their molecular characteristics. Moreover, the sparse labeling pattern achieved through electroporation allows detailed observation of individual cell morphology and migration trajectories at single-cell resolution, providing an excellent complement to genetic and retroviral approaches.

While in utero electroporation has proven instrumental in studying hippocampal pyramidal neuron development (Kitazawa et al. 2014; Nakahira and Yuasa 2005; Navarro-Quiroga et al. 2007; Ako et al. 2011), its application to DG development remains relatively unexplored despite its early demonstration (Nakahira and Yuasa 2005; Meyer and Morris 2009; Choe et al. 2013; Zhang et al. 2023; Caramello et al. 2021). Given the complex nature of DG development and the distinct advantages of each methodology, the integration of diverse experimental approaches (genetic, viral, and electroporation-based) will be crucial for building a comprehensive understanding of DG development.

In this study, we set out to perform systematic analysis of DG development using in utero electroporation, examining multiple embryonic stages from E11.5 onward. While technical limitations restricted reproducible labeling to E12.5, this approach enabled immediate visualization of an early progenitor population through targeted cellular labeling during a critical period of DG development. By establishing a novel dual-labeling system that combines direct cell labeling with transgenic Gfap-GFP reporter mice, we were able to directly compare, within the same animal, the behavior of electroporated cells with the well-characterized Gfap-expressing progenitors. This comparative approach revealed a previously unrecognized subset that diverges from the Gfap-expressing dentate progenitors and may represent an earlier wave of dentate development not adequately captured by current genetic models. These labeled cells displayed unique characteristic features: exclusively populating the outer GCL without reaching the TDM and showing greater propensity for differentiation into GCs compared to Gfap-expressing progenitors that maintain an undifferentiated state. Our findings, using complementary transient and stable labeling approaches, reveal previously unrecognized temporal and functional heterogeneity among dentate progenitors, with early-labeled progenitors potentially establishing the structural scaffold of this critical hippocampal structure.

Materials and Methods

Animals

C57BL/6J mice and Gfap-GFP transgenic mice were used for in utero electroporation experiments. C57BL/6J mice were obtained from Charles River Laboratories Japan, Inc (Yokohama, Japan). The Gfap-GFP mice on a C57BL/6NCr background express green fluorescent protein (GFP) under the control of the mouse glial fibrillary acidic protein (Gfap) promoter and have been described previously (Suzuki et al. 2003). All mice were maintained under standard conditions with a 12-h light/dark cycle in the animal care facilities of Tokyo Medical University. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees of Tokyo Medical University and were performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publication No. 80–23, revised 1996). Efforts were made to minimize the number of animals used and their suffering. For timed mating, the day of vaginal plug detection was defined as embryonic day 0.5 (E0.5).

In Utero Electroporation

In utero electroporation of mouse embryos was performed as previously described (Shinohara et al. 2013), with modifications. The pCAG-mRFP construct (a gift from Dr. Masanori Uchikawa at Osaka University) was used to visualize neuronal progenitors and neurons by red fluorescent protein (RFP) expression, though signal intensity may decrease through successive cell divisions. Briefly, timed pregnant mice at E11.5 (n = 8), E12.5 (n = 91), E14.5 (n = 140), and E15.5 (n = 14) were anesthetized and the uterine horns were exposed by laparotomy. The plasmid solution containing the pCAG-mRFP construct (1 µg/µl) with 0.1% Fast Green was injected into the left lateral ventricle of embryos (7.29 ± 1.95 embryos/dam; range 0–10 embryos) using a glass micropipette. Square electrical pulses (30 V, 50 ms duration, four pulses) were then applied to the embryos (Fig. 1B) using a tweezer-type electrode (CUY650P3, BEX, Japan) connected to an electroporator (CUY21 Vivo-SQ, BEX). For targeting the dentate neuroepithelium, the electrodes were specifically positioned with the anode placed medially and the cathode laterally relative to the developing hippocampus (Fig. 1B). Both the electrical parameters and electrode configuration were optimized through preliminary experiments to achieve efficient transfection of dentate progenitors while minimizing tissue damage. After electroporation, the uterine horns were repositioned and the abdominal cavity was closed. Electroporated embryos were harvested and fixed at various developmental stages including E13.5, E14.5, E15.5, E17.5, and E18.5 for further analysis.

For Tol2-mediated gene transfer experiments, three plasmids were co-electroporated at the following concentrations: pT2k-pCAG-loxP-DsRed2-loxP-EGFP (1 μg/μl), pCAG-T2TP (0.5 μg/μl, generously provided by Dr. Yoshiko Takahashi, Kyoto University; Yokota et al. 2011), and pGfap-Cre (0.5 μg/μl, a gift from Dr. Seiji Shioda, Shonan University of Medical Sciences). Embryos were electroporated at E12.5, then harvested and fixed at E14.5, and E18.5 for subsequent analysis.

Immunofluorescence Analysis of Cell Differentiation Markers

Cell differentiation states were analyzed by immunofluorescence staining as previously described (Shinohara et al. 2013) with minor modifications. Antibodies were selected based on their specificity and previous validation in similar developmental studies of the DG. At embryonic stages E13.5, E14.5, E15.5, E17.5, and E18.5, pregnant dams were cervically euthanized and embryos were sequentially removed from the uterus. Mouse embryos were transcardially perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; volume: 2–3 ml/embryo; injection speed < 30 μl/sec) in phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.4), and the cortex primordium was dissected and further fixed in 4% PFA for 30 min at room temperature (RT). Fixed samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C in 20% sucrose in PBS, embedded in Tissue-TEK (OCT, Sakura Finetek, Japan), and frozen in isopentane cooled by liquid nitrogen.

DG tissues were cryosectioned perpendicular to the septotemporal axis of the hippocampus using a cryostat (CM1850, Leica Biosystems, Wetzlar, Germany) to preserve the processes of dentate neural progenitor cells. The sections were then immersed in a blocking solution containing 2% bovine serum albumin in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Triton X-100 (TBST, Sigma, St. Louis, MO) for 1 h at RT.

Sections were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, washed with TBST, and then incubated with the appropriate fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at RT. For biotinylated secondary antibodies, sections were further incubated with streptavidin Alexa Fluor 405 conjugate for 1 h at RT. The primary and secondary antibodies and fluorescent conjugates used are listed in Table S1. Confocal imaging was performed using confocal microscope (LSM700 and LSM900, Carl Zeiss, Germany) and the acquired images were processed using the Zeiss LSM Image Browser and ZEN software (Carl Zeiss) to adjust brightness and contrast and to generate composite images.

Quantitative Analysis and Statistics

For quantitative analysis, sections processed for immunofluorescence staining were imaged using LSM700/900 confocal microscopes with a × 20 objective lens. Position-matched sections from three or more brains at each embryonic stage were selected to ensure reproducibility, and cell counting was performed manually. For all marker combinations, cells were classified as positive based on nuclear (Sox2, Tbr2, Prox1) or cytoplasmic (GFP, EGFP, RFP) fluorescence intensity exceeding background levels by > 2 standard deviations. Ambiguous cells (< 5% of total) were excluded from analysis. Histograms represent average quantification from the indicated number of biological replicates (n ≥ 3). For regional analysis (Fig. 2F–I), the DG primordium was divided into three areas: “VS” located near the ventricular surface, “Fimbria-FDJ” extending from the upper part of the fimbria to the fimbriodentate junction (FDJ), and “DG” representing the dentate gyrus proper (Fig. 2E). At E13.5, since the DG region is still in the early stages of formation and differentiated Prox1+ cells are not yet present at this developmental stage, analysis was limited to VS and Fimbria-FDJ areas (Fig. 2F–H), and E13.5 data were not included in the Prox1+ cell analysis (Fig. 2I). For the Tol2 experiments (Fig. 5), cell counts were obtained from four embryos (n = 4 biological replicates), yielding a total of 119 cells in the DG: 76 DsRed2+ (Gfap-inactive) and 43 EGFP+ (Gfap-active) cells, indicating that 36.1% of E12.5-labeled cells activated the Gfap promoter by E18.5.

Results

Early Migration and Differentiation of E12.5 Dentate Progenitors

To study the development of dentate neural progenitors (DNPs), we performed in utero electroporation targeting the DN region using the pCAG-mRFP construct, which drives ubiquitous expression regardless of cell fate in targeted cells (Fig. 1B). Following previously established protocol (Nakahira and Yuasa 2005), we systematically examined developmental stages from E11.5 onward, the earliest stage at which the DN hippocampus region can be targeted by this method. Electroporation at E11.5 resulted in poor embryonic survival; in contrast, E12.5 consistently yielded viable embryos with reproducible DNP labeling. Critical to this success was the optimization of electrode positioning, with the cathode placed laterally and the anode medially relative to the developing hippocampus (Fig. 1B), a configuration that differed from standard cortical electroporation protocols (Tabata and Nakajima 2001, 2003; Shinohara et al. 2013) and proved essential for efficient targeting of the dentate neuroepithelium. While electroporation from E14.5 successfully labeled cells in different hippocampal regions, it showed dramatically reduced efficiency in specifically targeting DNPs, with only occasional sporadic success (Supplementary Fig. 1). This stage-dependent efficiency suggests a critical developmental window for DNP labeling by this method. Therefore, we focused our detailed analysis on DNPs labeled at E12.5, where we achieved reproducible results that enabled us to track their migratory behavior and fate. For molecular marker analysis, we used Sox2 to identify undifferentiated neural stem/progenitor cells and Tbr2 to detect committed neural precursors to specify the differentiation status of the labeled cells. We focused our analysis on RFP-positive cells maintaining detectable expression levels throughout development, noting that rapidly proliferating cells likely lost detectable RFP signal through episomal dilution, potentially biasing our analysis toward more differentiated populations. The in utero electroporation at E12.5 combined with these molecular markers allowed us to identify an early-differentiating subset with enhanced neurogenic propensity.

One day post-labeling (E12.5– > E13.5), RFP+ cells were distributed in the ventricular zone (VZ) and displayed undifferentiated characteristics, as evidenced by Sox2 expression (Fig. 1C, D1–4, white arrows). These cells had a bipolar morphology. Some RFP+ cells, which had migrated towards the pial surface, did not express Sox2 but instead expressed Tbr2, a marker of intermediate neuronal precursors (Fig. 1D1–4, green arrowheads). These partially differentiated neural precursors exhibited a multipolar morphology.

Two days post-labeling (E12.5– > E14.5), the RFP+ cells on the ventricular surface (VS) side remained Sox2+ and undifferentiated (Fig. 1E, F1–4, white arrows). In contrast, RFP+ cells on the pial side expressed Tbr2, indicating their progression towards a neuronal progenitor state (Fig. 1E, G1–4, green arrowheads).

This pattern persisted three days post-labeling (E12.5– > E15.5). RFP+ cells on the VS side retained a bipolar morphology and continued to express Sox2 (Fig. 1H, I1–4, white arrows), maintaining their undifferentiated state. At the same time, RFP+ cells that had migrated towards the pial surface continued to express Tbr2, further confirming their commitment to a neuronal fate (Fig. 1H, J1–4, green arrowheads).

These results demonstrate that some E12.5-labeled neural progenitor cells differentiated into neuronal progenitors as they migrated towards the pial surface, while others remained in an undifferentiated state on the VS side.

Maturation of E12.5-Labeled Progenitors into Granule Cells

To determine whether E12.5-labeled DNPs can progress to terminal differentiation and acquire granule cell identity, we examined the expression of granule cell markers at later developmental stages. We performed immunostaining at E15.5 (E12.5– > E15.5) for Prox1, a homeodomain transcription factor essential for GC identity and a marker of GCs. As shown in Fig. 2A1, RFP+ cells were observed extending from the VS to the emerging DG primordium. The RFP+ cells in the marginal zone were Tbr2-positive (Fig. 2A2–5, green arrowheads) and also expressed Prox1, indicating a more advanced differentiation state. In contrast, RFP+ cells in the hilus were Prox1-positive but Tbr2-negative (Fig. 2A2–5, green arrows), indicating their further differentiation into GCs.

Five days post-labeling (E12.5– > E17.5), RFP+ cells along the hippocampal fissure (HF) and pial surface remained Tbr2-positive (Fig. 2B1, B2–5, white arrowheads). RFP+ cells along the medial side of the HF expressed Prox1 (Fig. 2B1, B2–5, green arrows), suggesting their progression towards GC identity. These Prox1+/RFP+ cells were positioned more medially than the Tbr2+/RFP+ cells. This spatial organization shows that E12.5-labeled cells differentiate in a distinctive pattern, with cells migrating from the outer regions toward more medial positions as they mature.

Six days post-labeling (E12.5– > E18.5), RFP+ cells in the hilus, rather than just below the HF, were Prox1-positive but Tbr2-negative (Fig. 2C1; C2-5 green arrows). This Prox1+/Tbr2− GC phenotype persisted at both 5 and 6 days post-labeling. Notably, these Prox1+/RFP+ GCs also expressed the mature neuronal marker NeuN (Fig. 2D1, D2–5, white arrows). The co-expression of Prox1 and NeuN confirms the acquisition of mature granule cell identity, representing the terminal stage in the differentiation process of E12.5-labeled progenitors.

To complement these qualitative observations, we analyzed the spatial distribution of electroporated cells across three anatomically defined subregions: the VS, FDJ, and DG (Fig. 2E–I). This analysis revealed a clear temporal shift in cell localization from the VS and Fimbria–FDJ regions at earlier developmental stages toward the DG region at later stages. This pattern was observed for total RFP+ cells, Sox2+/RFP+ neural stem/progenitor cells, and Tbr2+/RFP+ intermediate progenitor cells (Fig. 2F–H). Notably, Prox1+/RFP+ GCs were predominantly localized in the DG region at E15.5 and E17.5, with minimal presence in the VS or Fimbria–FDJ regions (Fig. 2I). These distribution patterns (Fig. 2F–I, n = 3–7 animals per timepoint) confirm that E12.5-labeled progenitors progressively migrate toward and differentiate within the developing DG, where they acquire mature GC identity.

These results demonstrate that DNPs labeled at E12.5 are capable of differentiating into mature GCs within a relatively short developmental window, highlighting the rapid differentiation potential of this early progenitor subset.

Comparative Analysis of E12.5-Labeled and Gfap-GFP+ Progenitor Subpopulations

DNPs have been characterized using various approaches, including classical methods and transgenic reporter lines (Altman and Bayer 1990a, 1990b; Li et al. 2009; Seki et al. 2014; Hodge et al. 2013; Berg et al. 2019). These different studies have consistently revealed similar migratory behaviors and differentiation trajectories of DG progenitors. To evaluate how our electroporation-based labeling method at E12.5 relates to these established approaches and to potentially identify progenitor populations that may have been unrecognized by existing genetic labeling methods, we compared our labeled cells with Gfap-GFP+ progenitors that emerge at E14.5, representing one of the well-characterized genetic marking systems.

We introduced the pCAG-mRFP construct into the DG primordium of Gfap-GFP mice at E12.5 and analyzed the labeled cells at different time points. Two days post-labeling (E12.5– > E14.5), RFP+ cells displayed an undifferentiated Sox2-positive phenotype within the VZ (Fig. 3A1–5, B1–5) and Tbr2-positive neuronal precursor phenotype on the pial side (Fig. 3A1–5, C1–5, white arrowheads), consistent with our earlier observations in wild-type mice. At E14.5, Gfap-GFP+ cells observed were distributed throughout the VZ up to the pial membrane (Fig. 3A2, B1–5, orange arrows; C1–5, orange arrowheads). This suggests that while E12.5-labeled progenitors were already migrating and differentiating by E14.5, Gfap-GFP+ cells emerging at this stage still populated the entire DG primordium.

At this early timepoint (E14.5), we observed minimal overlap between the two subsets in transient transfection experiment, as Gfap-GFP+ cells were barely labeled by the RFP reporter after in utero electroporation at E12.5 (Fig. 3A1, A5, B1, 2, orange arrows; C1, 2, orange arrowheads). The Gfap-GFP+ cells located on the VS side at E14.5 were Sox2-positive but Tbr2-negative, indicating that they remained undifferentiated (Fig. 3B1–5, orange arrowheads).

Consistent with the observation in Fig. 1, the E12.5-labeled RFP+ cells that had migrated to the pial membrane side displayed a Tbr2-positive, Sox2-negative neuronal progenitor phenotype (Fig. 3C1–5, white arrowheads). Interestingly, a similar distribution and marker profile was also observed for Gfap-GFP+ cells at this E14.5 time point, with Gfap-GFP+ cells located on the pial membrane side also exhibiting Tbr2 positivity and Sox2 negativity (Fig. 3C1–5, orange arrowheads). These findings suggest that both subsets initially follow similar migratory routes, though their differentiation kinetics later diverge.

Three days post-labeling (E12.5– > E15.5), both RFP+ and Gfap-GFP+ cells were distributed within the developing DG region (Fig. 3D1–5) (Seki et al. 2014). Only a few undifferentiated RFP+ cells, characterized by Sox2 positivity and Tbr2 negativity, were found within the hilus (Fig. 3E1–5, white arrow). In contrast, undifferentiated Gfap-GFP+ cells with a Sox2+/Tbr2− profile were considerably more abundant in the hilus (Fig. 3E1–5, orange arrows).

At the same developmental stage, Tbr2-positive, Sox2-negative RFP+ cells were arranged in a V-shaped pattern along the pial membrane and prospective HF (Fig. 3E1–5, white arrowheads), indicating a more differentiated neuronal progenitor state. Notably, the Gfap-GFP+ cells showed a similar distribution, with Sox2−/Tbr2+ subpopulation lining up along the pial membrane and HF (Fig. 3E1–5, orange arrowheads).

To confirm whether these progenitor subpopulations differentiated into GCs, immunostaining was performed with the Prox1 antibody (Fig. 3F). The differentiation profiles of the two subsets showed clear differences at this stage. RFP+ cells on the pial side exhibited weak Prox1 expression while retaining Tbr2 positivity (Fig. 3F1, F2–5, white arrows), suggesting an intermediate state of differentiation. In contrast, RFP+ cells along the HF strongly expressed Prox1 (Fig. 3F2–5, white arrowheads), indicating progression towards a GC identity.

Gfap-GFP+ cells near the FDJ expressed Tbr2 but were negative for Prox1 (Fig. 3F1, F2–5, orange arrows), indicating an intermediate progenitor state. However, only a minute subgroup of Gfap-GFP+ cells on the pial side showed Tbr2 expression along with weak Prox1 immunoreactivity (Fig. 3F2–5, orange arrowheads), suggesting that they had progressed further towards a GC identity.

These comparative analyses demonstrate divergent developmental trajectories for the two progenitor components. While both E12.5-labeled and Gfap-GFP+ cells can generate GCs, they differ significantly in their differentiation kinetics. Specifically, E12.5-labeled cells exhibited more advanced GC differentiation, as evidenced by extremely robust Prox1 expression along the HF. In contrast, Gfap-GFP+ cells appeared to be slightly delayed in their differentiation trajectory, with a significant proportion retaining an intermediate progenitor phenotype near the FDJ.

Late-Stage Developmental Divergence of E12.5-Labeled and Gfap-GFP+ Subsets

To determine whether the differentiation differences observed at E15.5 persist and potentially become more pronounced at later developmental stages, we analyzed both cell subsets at E17.5 (5 days post-labeling) and E18.5 (6 days post-labeling).

At E17.5, both subpopulations showed similar positioning of Tbr2-positive neuronal progenitors just below the HF and along the pial membrane (Fig. 4A, B1–5, white arrowheads indicate the progenitors derived from E12.5-labeled cells and orange arrowheads from Gfap-GFP+ cells, respectively). Undifferentiated Sox2-positive cells were found within the hilus in both subsets, with a notable prevalence among Gfap-GFP+ cells (Fig. 4A, B1–5, white and orange arrows, respectively). Furthermore, Prox1 immunostaining (Fig. 4C–J) revealed differential spatial patterns of GC differentiation between the two subsets. E12.5-derived Prox1+/RFP+ cells were observed within the hilus (Fig. 4C, D1–5, white arrows), while Prox1+/Gfap-GFP+ cells were predominantly located on the pial membrane side (Fig. 4D1–5, orange arrows).

By E18.5, the spatial segregation between the two subsets became more pronounced. E12.5-derived GCs were preferentially localized within the molecular layer, particularly on the HF side (Fig. 4F1–5, white arrows). On the pial membrane side, these RFP+ GCs were not only found in the densely packed GCL, but also extended into the hilus region (Fig. 4E, G1–5, H, I1–5, white arrows). A fraction of undifferentiated Sox2-positive cells derived from E12.5 labeling were still present within the hilus (Fig. 4G1–5, white arrowheads).

In contrast to this distribution pattern, GCs derived from the Gfap-GFP+ subpopulation were mainly distributed along the hilar side or within the hilus of the densely packed GCL (dashed line) (Fig. 4F1–5, G1–5, I1–5, D1–5, orange arrows). Notably, undifferentiated cells persisted not only in the hilus but also within the densely packed GC region (Fig. 4F1–5, G1–5, orange arrowheads).

These observations highlight key differences in the migratory patterns and differentiation states of the two progenitor subsets. E12.5-labeled cells showed more advanced differentiation and integration into the molecular layer, while a substantial fraction of Gfap-GFP+ cells remained undifferentiated within the GCL itself.

Both E12.5-labeled RFP+ cell-derived and Gfap-GFP+ cell-derived Tbr2+ neuronal progenitors were present within the molecular layer at this stage (Fig. 4I1–5, white and orange arrowheads, respectively). To quantitatively assess the differentiation status of both subsets, we analyzed the percentage of RFP+ and Gfap-GFP+ cells expressing Sox2 (undifferentiated) and Prox1 (GC) markers within the DG primordium at E18.5. This analysis revealed statistically significant differences: 60.9% ± 3.7% (n = 3) of E12.5-derived RFP+ cells had differentiated into Prox1+ GCs, versus only 22.8% ± 3.7% of Gfap-GFP+ cells (P < 0.001, Fig. 4K). Conversely, 63.5% ± 6.9% of Gfap-GFP+ cells maintained an undifferentiated Sox2+ state (Fig. 4K). Importantly, RFP+/GFP+ double-positive cells comprised only a small fraction (8.4 ± 2.7%) of the total Gfap-GFP+ population, with 3.4% maintaining Sox2 expression, 3.2% expressing Prox1, and 1.8% expressing neither marker.

These quantitative data provide further evidence that E12.5-labeled progenitors and Gfap-GFP+ cells exhibit divergent behaviors during later stages of DG development. While the majority of E12.5-derived cells had progressed to a GC fate, a significant proportion of the Gfap-GFP+ subpopulation maintained an undifferentiated progenitor state, retaining their stem-like properties even as the GCL was being established. The contrasting behaviors of E12.5-labeled cells and Gfap-GFP+ cells—with the former predominantly generating GCs and the latter maintaining an undifferentiated state—establish the existence of two progenitor subpopulations with divergent developmental programs in the developing DG.

Distinct Cell Fate Trajectories Between Gfap-Inactive and Gfap-Active Progenitors Labeled at E12.5

To stably trace cell fate decisions independent of proliferation dynamics, we employed a Tol2 transposon-based genomic integration system combined with Cre/loxP-mediated lineage tracing. This strategy enables permanent labeling independent regardless of division rates. We electroporated E12.5 DG primordia with a reporter construct (pT2k-pCAG-loxP-DsRed2-loxP-EGFP) that initially expresses DsRed2 but switches to EGFP expression upon Gfap promoter activation via Cre recombinase (Fig. 5A). This system permits retrospective identification of cells based on their Gfap promoter activity status.

At E14.5, two days post-electroporation, we identified two separate groups along the ventricular surface: DsRed2+/EGFP− cells representing Gfap-inactive progenitors (Fig. 5B, white arrowheads) and EGFP+ cells representing Gfap-active progenitors (Fig. 5B, orange arrowheads). At this early timepoint, these EGFP+ cells retained DsRed2 protein from pre-recombination expression, as expected given the perdurance of the fluorescent protein. Both subsets expressed Sox2, confirming their undifferentiated progenitor identity at this stage (Fig. 5B4). Among cells that had migrated to the pial side, both DsRed2+ cells (Fig. 5B5, white arrows) and EGFP+ cells (Fig. 5B5, orange arrows) expressed Tbr2, indicating progression toward intermediate progenitor fate. By E18.5, six days post-labeling, both cell subsets had populated the developing granule cell layer (Fig. 5C). Strikingly, DsRed2+ cells (Gfap-inactive) predominantly differentiated into Prox1+ granule neurons (Fig. 5C, white arrows). In contrast, EGFP+ cells (Gfap-active) displayed heterogeneous fates: while some differentiated into granule neurons (Fig. 5C, orange arrows), others maintained an undifferentiated Sox2+ state (Fig. 5C, orange arrowheads).

Among the E12.5-electroporated cells identified in the DG at E18.5 (n = 4 animals), we counted 76 DsRed2+ cells (Gfap-inactive) and 43 EGFP+ cells (Gfap-active), indicating that 36.1% of E12.5-labeled cells present in the DG had activated the Gfap promoter by E18.5. This proportion is higher than the 8.4% overlap observed in our transient transfection experiments (Fig. 4K), likely reflecting the stable genomic integration that prevents signal dilution through cell division. Quantitative analysis revealed significant difference in differentiation propensity between these subsets. DsRed2+ cells showed a higher proportion of Prox1+ neuronal differentiation compared to EGFP+ cells (52.8% ± 1.6% vs 31.5% ± 5.0%, P = 0.0288) (Fig. 5D). Conversely, EGFP+ cells exhibited a trend toward maintaining Sox2 expression, though this did not reach statistical significance (24.2% ± 7.5% vs 14.1% ± 1.5%, P = 0.0597). These findings, consistent with our observations using Gfap-GFP mice (Fig. 4), demonstrate that E12.5 DG progenitors comprise heterogeneous subsets with different differentiation trajectories: Gfap-inactive progenitors preferentially undergo neuronal differentiation, while Gfap-active progenitors retain greater stem/progenitor potential.

Discussion

Stage-Specific Labeling by In Utero Electroporation Enables Direct Visualization of Early Dentate Progenitors

While transgenic reporter mice have been instrumental in revealing the molecular identity and lineage relationships of dentate progenitors (Li et al. 2009; Seki et al. 2014; Hodge et al. 2013; Berg et al. 2019; Caramello et al. 2021), direct visualization and tracking of early progenitors from their point of origin has remained challenging. In utero electroporation, which enables precise spatiotemporal targeting of ventricular progenitors and visualization of individual cell migration at single-cell resolution, has been successfully applied to studies of hippocampal pyramidal neurons (Kitazawa et al. 2014; Nakahira and Yuasa 2005; Navarro-Quiroga et al. 2007; Ako et al. 2011). However, its systematic application to early DG development has remained limited, explaining the paucity of electroporation studies targeting the developing DG in the current literature.

We performed comprehensive stage-dependent analysis using in utero electroporation from E11.5 onward to establish optimal conditions for targeting dentate progenitors. E12.5 provided the most consistent and reproducible labeling in our system, with our optimized electrode configuration—specifically positioned relative to the developing hippocampus (Fig. 1B)—enabling reliable targeting of the dentate neuroepithelium. Electroporation efficiency declined substantially from E14.5 onward in our hands, despite previous reports (Nakahira and Yuasa 2005; Meyer and Morris 2009; Choe et al. 2013; Zhang et al. 2023; Caramello et al. 2021), and electroporation at E11.5 resulted in poor embryonic survival. These findings contribute to establishing technical parameters for DG electroporation and likely reflect the dynamic morphological changes of the developing DN region, with E12.5 representing an optimal balance between tissue accessibility and embryonic viability for our analytical approach.

Despite these technical constraints, successful labeling at E12.5 proved particularly informative, as the sparse labeling and precise tracking provided by electroporation allowed us to follow the dynamic behavior of individual dentate progenitor cells. This approach revealed distinct patterns of progenitor migration and differentiation that enhance our understanding of early DG development, as illustrated in Fig. 6.

Developmental Trajectory of E12.5-Labeled Dentate Progenitors

E12.5-labeled cells exhibited a characteristic migration pattern from the DN to their final positions in the developing DG. As demonstrated in Fig. 6A, their migratory behavior followed a specific sequence (red arrows): First, these progenitors migrated from the DN towards the FDJ at the pial membrane. They then distributed just below the pial membrane and the HF, forming the SDM at the prospective margins of the DG. Finally, these cells differentiated into GCs, establishing the initial framework of the GCL. Critically, as shown in Fig. 6A, unlike Gfap-GFP+ cells that eventually populate the TDM in the hilus, E12.5-labeled cells were never observed in the TDM, providing compelling evidence that E12.5-labeled and Gfap-GFP+ cells follow different migration pathways.

This process followed a distinctive outside-in pattern, with cells progressively populating the GCL from the molecular layer side inwards towards the hilus (Fig. 6A, red region). The temporal sequence we observed—with E12.5-labeled cells occupying outer positions and later-emerging Gfap-GFP+ cells settling in more inner regions—provides a developmental explanation for the laminar organization of the adult DG, where earlier-born cells predominantly occupy outer positions in the GCL (Mathews et al. 2010). Our direct visualization of E12.5-labeled progenitors thus provides cellular-level evidence for how this architectural principle is established during embryonic development.

While previous studies using Nestin and Gfap reporter mice have suggested that subpial progenitors contribute to GC generation during migration to the hilus (Li et al. 2009; Seki et al. 2014), our work provides the first direct visualization of this process with single-cell clarity, revealing a continuous series of cell movements originating from the DN at E12.5. The combination of precise spatiotemporal targeting and sparse labeling enabled by electroporation has allowed us to track individual cell behavior during this critical developmental period, providing new insights into the cellular dynamics of DG formation. Importantly, our observations revealed that not all E12.5-labeled cells follow identical developmental trajectories, prompting us to examine their heterogeneous differentiation behaviors more closely.

Functional Heterogeneity within Early Dentate Gyrus Progenitors

This heterogeneity became evident when we analyzed the fate of cells labeled at E12.5, as we identified subsets with markedly different outcomes. While some labeled cells exhibited diverse behaviors or became undetectable due to signal dilution through proliferation, a particular subset maintained detectable RFP expression and showed unique properties. This early-differentiating subset demonstrated temporal and functional heterogeneity from the later-emerging Gfap-expressing progenitors, as illustrated in Fig. 6B. Our quantitative analyses at E18.5 demonstrated the distinct differentiation propensity of E12.5 progenitors within the DG. Using the Tol2 transposon and Cre/loxP systems for stable genomic labeling—which overcome signal dilution and marker expression changes—52.8% of E12.5-labeled cells differentiated into Prox1+ GCs, compared to only 31.5% of Gfap-GFP+ cells (P = 0.0288, Fig. 5D). This finding was consistent with our initial observations, where 60.9% of E12.5-labeled RFP+ cells versus 22.8% of Gfap-GFP+ cells became Prox1+ (Fig. 4K).

The E12.5-labeled cells exhibited rapid migration and gave rise to the initial GCL of the DG (red dots in Fig. 6B right panel), while a substantial proportion of the later-born Gfap-GFP+ cells maintained an undifferentiated state (dark green dots in Fig. 6B middle panel), suggesting their role in expanding the progenitor pool for subsequent GCL growth. To address potential signal dilution through proliferation and loss of Gfap expression during differentiation, we employed complementary genetic labeling strategies—the Tol2 transposon for stable genomic integration and Cre/loxP-mediated lineage tracing systems—which confirmed our findings (Fig. 5). Although the full heterogeneity of E12.5 progenitors may not be entirely captured due to variable electroporation efficiency, the identification of this differentiation-prone subset lacking Gfap expression represents a significant finding. The spatial distribution observed in Fig. 6B, together with quantitative analyses (Fig. 2F–I, 4K, 5D) demonstrates that E12.5-labeled RFP+ cells predominantly differentiate into Prox1+ GCs, while few maintain a Sox2+ progenitor state. This striking pattern reveals a functional divergence between early and late progenitors, reflecting a precisely regulated developmental program.

These divergent behaviors align with established migration routes for dentate progenitors. In the first route, the PDM in the ventricular zone near the DN continues along the pial membrane to form the SDM just below the HF, which then differentiates into GCs, while a subset of progenitors reaches the future hilus to form the TDM (Li and Pleasure 2014; Hayashi et al. 2015; Seki et al. 2014; Hodge et al. 2013). This ‘outside-in’ pattern, as illustrated in Fig. 6A, demonstrates that E12.5 progenitors exclusively contribute to the outer GCL (red) without reaching the TDM, in contrast to the later Gfap-GFP+ subset (green) that establishes the TDM and displays a more pronounced inside component (Li et al. 2009; Rakic and Nowakowski 1981; Altman and Bayer 1990c; Noguchi et al. 2019). Notably, some Gfap-GFP+ cells take a direct route toward the hilus, contributing to TDM formation—a second migration pathway absent in E12.5-labeled cells (Fig. 6A).

The relationship between E12.5-labeled cells and Gfap expression warrants further clarification based on our complementary experimental approaches. Our Tol2/Cre-loxP experiments revealed that 36.1% (43/119) of E12.5-labeled cells present in the DG had activated the Gfap promoter by E18.5, indicating that a subset of these early progenitors does express Gfap during development. This proportion is notably higher than the 8.4% overlap observed with transient transfection (Fig. 4K), a discrepancy that likely reflects key technical differences: episomal plasmids undergo dilution through rapid proliferation in Gfap-expressing progenitors, whereas the Tol2 system provides stable genomic integration that permanently marks all cells with any history of Gfap promoter activation. Additionally, the transient nature of Gfap expression during differentiation may result in lower overlap when assessed at single timepoints. Crucially, despite this technical consideration, approximately 64% of E12.5-labeled cells detected in the DG never activate the Gfap promoter and maintain significantly enhanced neuronal differentiation (52.8% vs 31.5% Prox1+, P = 0.0288), supporting our conclusion that early-labeled progenitors comprise a functionally heterogeneous population with an early-differentiating subset.

Our findings are consistent with previous reports that the SPB (Fig. 1A) develops before the IPB, with the SPB outer shell forming between E13.5 and P0.5, while the IPB outer shell emerges after E16.5 (Altman and Bayer 1990a, 1990b; Li et al. 2009; Nelson et al. 2020). E12.5 progenitors likely contribute to this initial SPB formation, as evidenced by their early migration pattern (Fig. 6B).

Understanding the developmental origins and lineage relationships of dentate progenitors has relied heavily on transgenic approaches, with Nestin-Cre systems revealing progenitor emergence at E14.5 (Li et al. 2009; Hodge et al. 2013) and Hopx-CreERT2 demonstrating labeling as early as E10.5 (Berg et al. 2019). While invaluable for identifying specific NSC-derived lineages, these methods are constrained by their dependence on predetermined promoter activities. Electroporation offers a complementary approach, directly targeting cells at the DN at precise developmental timepoints, regardless of gene expression profiles.

Our in utero electroporation at E12.5 has uncovered a previously uncharacterized early-differentiating subset of dentate progenitors. By providing a spatiotemporal snapshot of DN progenitors, this technique revealed their accelerated differentiation trajectory. The preponderance of RFP+/Prox1+ cells over RFP+/Sox2+ cells (Figs. 4K, 5D, 6B) underscores the specific properties of this E12.5 subset. This subset likely represents progenitors that exit the cell cycle early and commit to neuronal differentiation, as evidenced by maintained RFP expression despite episomal dilution in dividing cells. Although current technical limitations restricted our analysis to E12.5, these findings demonstrate how direct labeling complements genetic approaches in dissecting dentate complexity. Future refinements in electroporation methodology for different developmental stages will reveal how progenitor migration patterns and differentiation timing vary across development, potentially uncovering additional progenitor subpopulations and their contribution to the distinct bladed architecture of the DG. Such advances will provide a comprehensive spatiotemporal framework for understanding how this complex brain structure is formed.

In conclusion, our in utero electroporation at E12.5, validated through stable genomic integration systems, has identified an early-differentiating subset of dentate progenitors. This subset, captured by direct labeling but potentially underrepresented by genetic approaches, rapidly generates the initial GCs that establish the foundation of the DG.

Our findings demonstrate how combining complementary methodological approaches—direct electroporation and genetic lineage tracing—reveals previously unrecognized heterogeneity in dentate development, providing new insights into the cellular mechanisms that shape this critical hippocampal structure.
