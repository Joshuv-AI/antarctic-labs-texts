Early VV-ECMO enabling immunosuppressive therapy in severe diffuse alveolar hemorrhage due to ANCA-associated vasculitis
Authors: Ye Ji Jung, Sunjin Ryu, Young Kim, Kye Chul Shin, Seongho Jo, Seong Gyu Yoon, Jung Tae Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Microscopic polyangiitis (MPA), a subtype of ANCA-associated vasculitis (AAV), is a small-vessel necrotizing vasculitis frequently accompanied by pulmonary capillaritis and rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis. Diffuse alveolar hemorrhage (DAH) represents one of the most catastrophic manifestations of AAV and can precipitate abrupt respiratory failure, often associated with high mortality despite prompt intensive care. In fulminant presentations, the pace of hypoxemic deterioration frequently exceeds the onset of therapeutic benefit from immunosuppressive therapy, posing substantial challenges in early clinical decision-making.

High-dose glucocorticoids combined with cyclophosphamide remain the cornerstone of induction therapy for severe AAV. However, many patients with DAH develop life-threatening hypoxemia before immunosuppression becomes effective. Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) may provide temporary gas-exchange support in refractory respiratory failure, yet its use in DAH remains controversial because of concerns regarding anticoagulation-associated bleeding. Guidance on the optimal sequencing and intensity of ECMO and immunosuppression in vasculitis-related DAH is limited, and available evidence is largely confined to isolated case reports.

Here, we report a case of MPO-ANCA–positive MPA presenting with fulminant DAH and rapidly progressive renal dysfunction that was managed with venovenous ECMO (VV-ECMO) support, high-dose glucocorticoids, and early cyclophosphamide induction. Improvement in oxygenation and renal function occurred in parallel, allowing successful liberation from ECMO and avoidance of dialysis. This case suggests that, in carefully selected patients, VV-ECMO may function as a supportive bridge to definitive immunosuppression rather than as a contraindicated intervention, and that timely induction therapy may help limit multiorgan injury in life-threatening AAV.

Results

The patient presented with rapidly progressive hypoxemic respiratory failure accompanied by massive hemoptysis. Initial chest computed tomography demonstrated diffuse bilateral ground-glass opacities and consolidation with scattered centrilobular nodules, consistent with diffuse alveolar hemorrhage (DAH). Despite invasive mechanical ventilation, oxygenation continued to deteriorate, and venovenous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV-ECMO) was initiated on May 2 for refractory hypoxemia.

ECMO configuration

Veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV-ECMO) was initiated via right femoral venous drainage and right internal jugular venous return. Initial blood flow was maintained at 3.8–4.5 L/min, with sweep gas flow adjusted according to serial arterial blood gas analysis.

Cannulation was performed under real-time ultrasound guidance given the high hemorrhagic risk. A heparin-bonded circuit was used to mitigate contact activation. No cannulation-related complications occurred.

Anticoagulation strategy

Systemic anticoagulation was modified due to active diffuse alveolar hemorrhage.

At cannulation, a single bolus of 1000 IU unfractionated heparin was administered. VV-ECMO was then maintained without systemic anticoagulation for the first 12 hours to permit stabilization of airway bleeding.

During this heparin-free interval, close circuit monitoring was performed, including hourly visual inspection for fibrin deposition and serial assessment of plasma-free hemoglobin, lactate dehydrogenase, fibrinogen levels, and circuit pressures.

Following a marked reduction in endotracheal bleeding, low-intensity heparin infusion was initiated, targeting an activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) of 40–50 seconds, lower than conventional ECMO targets.

No oxygenator exchange, circuit thrombosis, major bleeding progression, or thromboembolic events occurred during support. VV-ECMO was successfully discontinued after 11 days.

Pulmonary response

After initiation of VV-ECMO, oxygenation stabilized with a progressive reduction in fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) requirements. FiO2 decreased from 58% immediately prior to ECMO initiation to 33% during ECMO support, and further to 13% at the time of ECMO removal on May 13 (Fig. 1). Arterial oxygenation improved in parallel, allowing gradual reduction of ventilatory support. MPO-ANCA was strongly positive (>134 IU/mL), while respiratory viral and bacterial polymerase chain reaction panels were negative.

High-dose methylprednisolone pulse therapy (1 g/day for 3 days) was initiated on May 1, followed by intravenous cyclophosphamide (CYC) 500 mg on May 3. After initiation of CYC, FiO2 requirements declined and ECMO weaning was initiated within 11 days (Fig. 1). Mechanical ventilation was subsequently discontinued, and pulmonary hemorrhage resolved. No thrombotic complications occurred despite minimized anticoagulation during VV-ECMO support.

Serial chest imaging demonstrated gradual resolution of bilateral pulmonary infiltrates during ECMO support and following cyclophosphamide induction (Fig. 2).

Inflammatory response

Inflammatory markers improved in parallel with respiratory recovery. Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP) decreased from 120 mm/hr and 14 mg/dL at presentation to 13 mm/hr and 5 mg/dL by May 4, with further reductions observed during the course of induction therapy (Fig. 3).

Trends in inflammatory markers during induction therapy. Serial erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP) values demonstrating progressive reduction following pulse glucocorticoids and cyclophosphamide.

Renal response

Renal function initially deteriorated, with serum creatinine increasing from 3.19 mg/dL on May 2 to a peak of 5.07 mg/dL on May 6 despite supportive management. Severe proteinuria persisted, with a urine protein-to-creatinine ratio (UPCR) of 4.7 g/g. Following the second dose of cyclophosphamide administered on May 20, renal function gradually improved without the need for renal replacement therapy, reaching a serum creatinine level of 1.73 mg/dL and UPCR of 1.1 g/g by May 29 (Fig. 4).

Renal function trajectory during induction therapy. Serial changes in serum creatinine and urine protein-to-creatinine ratio (UPCR) demonstrating gradual renal recovery without the need for dialysis.

Safety

No episodes of neutropenia, serious infection, thrombotic events, or hemorrhagic complications were observed during induction therapy.

Discussion

Diffuse alveolar hemorrhage (DAH) represents one of the most catastrophic manifestations of ANCA-associated vasculitis (AAV), particularly when respiratory failure progresses despite mechanical ventilation [[1], [2], [3]]. In fulminant cases, the pace of hypoxemia often exceeds the onset of therapeutic effect from immunosuppression, creating a critical management dilemma regarding the timing of definitive therapy. This report demonstrates that venovenous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV-ECMO), with carefully minimized anticoagulation, can provide sufficient cardiopulmonary support to permit early cytotoxic induction rather than delaying it. In our patient, a brief heparin-free interval followed by low-intensity anticoagulation allowed stabilization of active hemorrhage without circuit thrombosis or oxygenator failure.

Pulmonary hemorrhage has traditionally been regarded as a relative contraindication to ECMO; however, emerging evidence suggests that individualized anticoagulation strategies may mitigate hemorrhagic risk in selected patients [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. In this case, ECMO was initiated to prevent hypoxic organ injury while immunosuppressive therapy took effect. Oxygenation improved rapidly following cyclophosphamide (CYC) administration, with a parallel and sustained reduction in FiO2 requirements. The temporal association between CYC initiation and respiratory improvement suggests that ECMO functioned as a bridge to immunologic control rather than a salvage therapy after immunosuppression failure (Fig. 1) [5,7,8]. These findings support the concept that stabilization of gas exchange in catastrophic AAV is not the ultimate therapeutic endpoint, but a prerequisite that enables timely disease-directed therapy.

A second key observation was the parallel improvement of pulmonary and renal trajectories. DAH and rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis represent shared manifestations of systemic capillaritis, and their synchronous resolution has been linked to common immunopathogenic pathways involving neutrophil extracellular trap formation and complement activation [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. In this patient, serum creatinine and urine protein-to-creatinine ratio declined following induction therapy (Fig. 3, Fig. 4), in parallel with normalization of inflammatory markers (Fig. 2). This coordinated recovery supports a model of systemic endothelial healing rather than isolated organ-specific improvement. Notably, dialysis was avoided despite creatinine levels exceeding 5 mg/dL, consistent with emerging data indicating that prompt induction therapy may preserve renal function even in patients who otherwise meet criteria for renal replacement therapy consideration [13,14].

This therapeutic trajectory contrasts with traditional escalation paradigms in which ECMO is reserved only after failure of immunosuppression. By inverting this sequence, ECMO was used proactively to enable, rather than postpone, cytotoxic therapy. Recent literature increasingly characterizes DAH in AAV as an immunologic emergency rather than a purely respiratory one [15], and this case provides clinical confirmation of that framework by demonstrating rapid multiorgan recovery following timely induction therapy.

Histopathologic confirmation is frequently impractical in fulminant AAV due to profound hypoxia or bleeding risk. In accordance with contemporary classification and diagnostic frameworks, the diagnosis in this patient was established based on DAH, nephritic-range proteinuria, rapidly worsening renal function, and strongly positive MPO-ANCA serology [[16], [17], [18]]. This case underscores that delaying treatment to obtain tissue confirmation may be detrimental in life-threatening AAV and highlights the importance of decisive clinical judgment when biopsy is not feasible, consistent with recent EULAR recommendations [8]. Collectively, these findings suggest that in catastrophic MPO-AAV complicated by DAH, respiratory failure should not delay cytotoxic induction therapy, and VV-ECMO may serve as a critical bridge enabling timely immunosuppression while preventing hypoxic organ injury.

Conclusion

Fulminant MPO-ANCA–associated vasculitis with diffuse alveolar hemorrhage requires prompt immunosuppression, yet tissue diagnosis and conventional respiratory management may be unachievable in the setting of life-threatening hypoxemia. In this case, VV-ECMO with minimized anticoagulation safely stabilized gas exchange and enabled timely cyclophosphamide induction, resulting in rapid improvement of pulmonary hemorrhage, systemic inflammation, and renal dysfunction without the need for dialysis. These findings suggest that ECMO should not be considered an absolute contraindication in pulmonary hemorrhage; in carefully selected patients, it may serve as an effective supportive bridge until immunosuppressive therapy becomes effective. Early induction therapy supported by ECMO may therefore optimize pulmonary and multiorgan outcomes in catastrophic ANCA-associated vasculitis.
