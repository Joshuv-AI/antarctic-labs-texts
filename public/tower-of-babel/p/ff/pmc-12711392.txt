The nonamer code for RAG-mediated recombination in vivo
Authors: Wessal Hanout-Chuiko, Sunil Pachakar, Rawan Foqara, Hadas Sibony-Benyamini, Shira Peretz, Nurit Edri, Moshe Dessau, Yaakov Maman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractV(D)J recombination, essential for adaptive immunity, is initiated by RAG1/2, which recognizes recombination signal sequences (RSSs) flanking gene segments at antigen receptor loci. RSSs comprise conserved heptamer and nonamer motifs separated by a 12/23-base spacer. While the first three heptamer nucleotides (5′-CAC) are strictly conserved, RSS promiscuity enables RAG “off-target” activity at RSS-like sequences, termed “cryptic RSSs” (cRSSs), contributing to chromosomal deletions and lymphoid tumorigenesis. Notably, the nonamer exhibits substantial sequence variability across physiological RSSs. In addition, many cRSSs lack a discernible nonamer-like sequence, limiting the ability to predict RAG targets based on RSS consensus. Using a high-throughput approach, we characterized here the nonamer properties supporting RAG-mediated recombination. While the consensus nonamer (5′-ACAAAAACC) exhibited strong functionality, numerous sequences significantly different from it promoted a high recombination rate. These functional nonamers balance two, somewhat opposing, features: affinity for the RAG nonamer-binding domain—primarily via a shared purine–A/T–purine motif at positions 4–6—and nucleosome repulsion. Moreover, nonamers of genomic cRSSs mimic canonical nonamers mainly through nucleosome-repelling sequences. This study provides a model for both physiological RAG activity and its off-target effects, enhancing our understanding of immune repertoire formation and the genetic basis of lymphoid cancers.

Introduction

The capacity of the adaptive immune system to recognize and eliminate specific antigens is largely driven by B and T lymphocytes that generate a vast diversity of immunoglobulins (Ig) and T-cell receptors (TCR), respectively, collectively termed antigen receptors (AgRs) [1–4]. The initial diversity of AgRs arises by the V(D)J recombination process, through which the epitope recognition site is generated from the rearrangement of variable (V), diversity (D), and joining (J) gene segments comprising the germline AgR loci [1–4]. This process occurs during lymphocyte development in the primary lymphoid organs— the thymus for T cells and the bone marrow for B cells—ensuring a broad repertoire of antigens [1, 5, 6].

The process of V(D)J recombination is mediated by the recombination-activating gene (RAG) complex, which consists of two subunits: the catalytic subunit, RAG1, responsible for DNA binding and cleavage and the regulatory subunit, RAG2, which is essential for RAG activity in vivo, plays a role in its interaction with chromatin and in DNA binding [3, 4, 7–11]. Mechanisms that underlie RAG1/2 localization and recruitment to the AgR gene segments comprise different elements, both in DNA sequence and at chromatin levels [3, 4, 8, 9, 12, 13]. At the chromatin level, the localization of the RAG complex is enabled by interactions with posttranslational modifications of histone tails, with the most established interaction being RAG2 interaction with trimethylated lysine 4 on histone H3 (H3K4me3), a mark associated with active promoters [3, 7–9, 12]. At the DNA level, the RAG complex recognizes sequences flanking the V, D, and J segments termed recombination signal sequences (RSSs) that serve as the targets for RAG1-mediated DNA cleavage [1, 14, 15].

RSSs consist of a conserved heptamer at their 5′ end (consensus 5′-CACAGTG), which is the site of RAG1-mediated cleavage, and a partially conserved nonamer at their 3′ end (consensus 5′-ACAAAAACC), which serves as the binding site for RAG1. Between them resides a poorly conserved “spacer” sequence of either 12 ± 1 or 23 ± 1 base pairs in length, composing 12RSSs or 23RSSs, respectively [1, 4, 14].

During recombination, RAG1 recognizes a single RSS (“single complex”—SC), forms a single-strand break at RSS junction, and then interacts with a partner RSS either by utilizing cohesin-mediated loop extrusion [13, 16] or through diffusion [17] to form a “paired complex” (PC). Upon PC formation, double-strand breaks are introduced at both RSS junctions, and the resulting DNA coding and signal ends are joined through the non-homologous end-joining repair pathway [1, 18]. This process is restricted by the “12/23 rule,” which ensures that efficient rearrangement occurs between a 12 RSS and a 23RSS, thereby maintaining productive rearrangements of the AgR loci [15, 19].

The high level of sequence variability of the RSS, with only the first three nucleotides of the heptamer (5′-CAC) being perfectly conserved, enables millions of potential RAG target sites genome-wide, termed cryptic RSSs (cRSSs) [9, 20]. Breakage at a subset of these cRSSs is a hallmark of RAG-mediated genome instability and tumorigenesis [9, 21–24].

The role of RAG-mediated cleavage at cRSSs in tumorigenesis of lymphoid cancers is supported by a growing body of evidence [21, 23–27]. An outstanding example of such a role is the ETV6-RUNX1 B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL), which is characterized by in utero ETV6-RUNX1 fusion and found in 25% of B-ALL patients. Strikingly, cRSS signature has been observed in ∼40% of recurrent interstitial deletions in ETV6-RUNX1-positive B-ALL patients, marking RAG as a central driver of ETV6-RUNX1 B-ALL [24]. RAG-mediated chromosomal deletions are involved in the tumorigenesis of other lymphoid cancers such as T cell ALL, B cell non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma [27, 28], and cutaneous T cell lymphoma [29].

The traditional metrics for assessing RSS functionality (i.e., its tendency to undergo RAG1-mediated cleavage) are based on its similarity to the RSS consensus sequence, generated from the alignment of bona fide RSSs (bRSSs) across all AgR loci [30, 31]. Several similarity-based approaches have been developed to predict RSS functionality. For example, position weight matrices (PWMs), which assess sequence similarity by summing up the average similarity at each position, have been used to predict potential functional RSSs [24, 32]. A more complex computational approach—RSS information content (RIC)—has improved the sequence-based prediction of measured recombination efficiencies in vitro by taking into account the mutual information between different RSS positions [31, 33–35].

The in vivo DNA sequence preference of RAG is more complex and less predictable. bRSSs exhibit significant sequence variability, with AgR loci harboring distinct consensus bRSS sequences, and some, like TCRβ bRSSs, are considered poor-quality by the traditional “conservation-based” predictions [36]. This limited RAG target site predictability is even more pronounced in predicting functional cRSSs that often show only minimal sequence similarity to consensus bRSSs, with the majority of cRSSs completely lacking discernible nonamer-like sequences [13, 24, 37].

Thus, given the diverse nature of RSSs, and particularly nonamer sequences, current knowledge remains insufficient to accurately infer RSS functionality by the extent of similarity to bRSS consensus.

The interaction between RAG1 and RSS is enabled through the nonamer binding domain (NBD) of RAG1 located at the N-terminus of its catalytic core region, which recognizes and binds to the nonamer motif of the RSS [38, 39]. The NBD forms a symmetrical homodimer that interacts with two nonamer molecules. This interaction is essential for the formation of the synaptic complex required for V(D)J recombination [38–41].

Within the NBD, sequence-specific interactions are primarily mediated by the GGRPR motif (residues 389–393). In particular, arginine 391 (R391) [4, 39] interacts with positions 5 and 6 of the bottom strand of the nonamer (T5T6 in the consensus sequence), while R402 contacts position 2 on the same strand (G2 in the consensus) [39]. In addition to these base-specific contacts, non-specific interactions between other NBD residues and the phosphodiester backbone further contribute to the overall binding [4, 39].

In addition to directly facilitating RAG-mediated cleavage (through heptamer) and RAG binding (through nonamer), the RSS sequence may be driven by other forces indirectly contributing to RAG functionality in vivo [42, 43]. Previous studies have demonstrated that nucleosome occupancy at RSSs significantly hinders RAG-mediated cleavage [44]. Whether and how RSS sequences impact nucleosome positioning remains debated. Early in vitro studies suggested that RSSs attract nucleosomes in a nonamer-dependent manner, adding an extra layer of protection against off-target activity [44–46]. Conversely, other studies have reported weak or no effect of RSSs on nucleosome positioning [47]. More recent mapping of nucleosome positioning in lymphocytes revealed that nucleosomes are positioned immediately upstream of RSSs, leaving them accessible to RAG-mediated cleavage [43]. While sequence-dependent nucleosome positioning has not been proposed in this case, this observation aligns with the “nonamer first” model [3], proposing that the nonamer serves as the initial contact point for RAG1 binding, with nucleosome positioning creating a regulatory barrier that must be overcome before recombination can proceed [44]. Notably, the A-tract characteristic of the canonical nonamer is associated with low nucleosome occupancy [48–50], supporting its role in nucleosome repulsion and RSS accessibility.

In the current study, we constructed a high-throughput RSS functionality assay system to systematically characterize the DNA code of functional nonamers. In this system, a diverse library of 23RSSs with randomized nonamers was integrated into the genome of vAbl mouse pre-B cells in the context of a recombination construct containing a bona fide 12RSS and the randomized 23RSS [51]. Recombined and unrecombined constructs were then sequenced and analyzed to build a statistical model for nonamer functionality.

As expected, consensus-like nonamers were among the most functional sequences. Surprisingly, however, we identified a highly functional class of nonamers that significantly deviates from the canonical criteria. Further investigation revealed that functional nonamers—while hardly sharing direct sequence similarity—share two common features: strong NBD binding, often associated with a purine–A/T–purine (RWR) motif at positions 4–6 of the top strand, and a nucleosome-repelling tendency. Interestingly, while these two features negatively correlate, our data points toward a model by which bona fide nonamers were evolved to reside within a “sweet spot” that possesses both features simultaneously, while cRSSs—which were not co-evolved with RAG1 and its NBD—mimic bona fide nonamers mostly through their nucleosome repulsion capacity.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and treatment

Abelson murine leukemia virus-transformed (vAbl) pre-B cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 15% fetal calf serum (FBS; Sigma), 2 μM l-glutamine, sodium pyruvate, MEM non-essential amino acids (NEAA), penicillin (100 U/ml), streptomycin (100 μg/ml) (Gibco), and 50 μM 2-mercaptoethanol. Cells were incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2. For G1 cell cycle arrest and subsequent RAG1 expression, cells were treated with Abl kinase inhibitor 3 µM Imatinib (STI-571, Novartis) for 72 h [52].

Nonamer library cloning

Recombination substrates containing a 23RSS with a randomized nonamer (Supplementary Table S1) were constructed using the pMG-INV plasmid as a backbone [53], generously contributed by Prof. David Schatz. This plasmid harbors consensus 12/23RSS pairs in a head-to-tail orientation, flanking an inverted GFP gene [51]. Oligonucleotides containing 23RSS with a fully randomized nonamer (Supplementary Table S1) were ordered from Integrated DNA Technologies. These randomized oligonucleotides were polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-amplified using primers containing NotI and SacII restriction sites for subsequent cloning. The amplified fragments were cloned into the pMG-INV plasmid, replacing the native consensus 23RSS sequence. The integration of the randomized 23RSS was validated by Sanger sequencing using the ddPCR InvGFP_FWD primer (Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Fig. S1A).

Retroviral integration

The pMG-INV plasmid harboring the nonamer library was introduced into vAbl cells by retroviral integration. Retroviruses were produced by transfecting HEK-Phoenix cells, which stably express gag/pol, using PolyJet™ In Vitro DNA Transfection Reagent (SignaGen Laboratories). Cells were seeded 24 h before transfection at a density of ~2 × 106 cells per 10-cm dish. Upon reaching ∼70% confluency, cells were transfected with 6 μg of the retroviral vector and 2 μg of VSV-G envelope plasmid (Addgene), along with 24 μl of PolyJet™, following the manufacturer’s protocol. Twenty-four hours post-transfection, the culture medium was replaced, and retroviral supernatants were collected 48 h later. The supernatants were passed through a 0.45 μm syringe filter to remove cellular debris. For infection, 5 × 106 vAbl cells were incubated with the filtered viral supernatant for 72 h at 37°C in 5% CO₂ to allow for efficient transduction.

Immunostaining

To isolate plasmid-integrated cells, cells were labelled with VioletFluor-450-conjugated Thy1.2 antibody (1:100, Abcam) in 100 µl of ice-cold FACS buffer (1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, 2% BSA, 1× PBS) for 30 min on ice, protected from light. Next, cells were washed with FACS buffer and centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min at 4°C. The resulting cell pellet was resuspended in 1–2 ml of ice-cold FACS buffer. Prior to sorting, cells were filtered through a 40-µm mesh to remove aggregates. Thy1.2-positive cells were sorted using a BD Aria Cell Sorter (BD Biosciences). Live, single cells were gated based on forward and side scatter profiles, followed by selection of Thy1.2-positive events. Post-sort Thy1.2 enrichment was assessed by reanalyzing a subset of sorted cells (Supplementary Fig. S1B).

Genomic DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 5 × 106 harvested cells were resuspended in 200 µl of PBS and mixed with 20 µl of proteinase K. Subsequently, 200 µl of Buffer ATL was added, and the mixture was incubated at 56°C for 10 min. Following incubation, 200 µl of 96%–100% ethanol was added, and the lysate was transferred to a DNeasy spin column. The column was washed sequentially with 500 µl of Buffer AW1 and 500 µl of Buffer AW2. Finally, DNA was eluted in 200 µl of Buffer AE.

Nonamer library preparation and sequencing

Fragments containing the 12/23 RSSs at pre- and post-recombination orientations (Fig. 1A) were selectively amplified by PCR using Q5® High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (New England BioLabs). To enhance amplification specificity, nested PCR was conducted first using outer primers for “recombination” (“Outer-FWD-REC” and “Outer-RVS-REC”) and “no recombination” (“Outer-FWD-NOREC” and “Outer-RVS-NOREC”) orientations (Supplementary Table S1). Next, the resulting PCR products were gel-purified, using the NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Clean–up kit (MACHEREY-NAGEL), and were subjected to a second round of amplification using nested primers for both “post-recombination” (“Nested-I5a-FWD-UMI-REC,” “Nested-I7a-RVS-REC”) and “pre-recombination” (“Nested-I5a-FWD-UMI-NOREC,” “Nested-I7a-RVS-NOREC”) orientations (Supplementary Table S1). The nested primers contain Illumina adapters I5a and I7a and 3-digit unique molecular identifiers to account for amplification bias. Final PCR products were gel-extracted, and the final PCR product libraries were sequenced using Illumina MiSeq [MiSeq Reagent Kit v3 (150-cycle PE), Illumina]. Base calling was used to convert raw sequencing signals into FASTQ-formatted read files. The same approach was conducted for the plasmid library before integration to account for bias during plasmid preparation.

RSS library analysis

NGS libraries from both post-recombination and pre-recombination samples were processed by filtering raw reads for quality (Q > 30) and removing adapter sequences. For each replicate, “pre-integration,” “pre-recombination,” and “post-recombination” sequences were isolated based on their unique sequences (“CACAGTGGTAGTACT” for pre-recombination/pre-integration and “CACTGTGCACAGTG” for post-recombination; Heptamer sequences are highlighted). For each library set, nonamer functionality scores were calculated by first determining the frequency of each nonamer sequence, defined as the number of reads containing that nonamer, divided by the total number of reads in the respective library. The functionality score was then defined as the average log ratio of each unique nonamer frequency in the “post-recombination” library relative to its frequency in the corresponding “pre-recombination” library. To eliminate potential sequence bias introduced during plasmid preparation, nonamers with multiple reads in the “pre-integration” library were excluded from downstream analysis.

Nonamer functionality prediction

A sequence-based predictive model for nonamer functionality was performed using XGBoost implementation in R. The regression model employed gradient boosting with a squared error objective function and optimized hyperparameters (eta = 0.1, max_depth = 6, min_child_weight = 1, and sampling rates of 0.8). Training was performed over a maximum of 100 rounds, with early stopping triggered after 10 non-improving iterations to prevent overfitting. As input, the 11,201 nonamers obtained from the library were encoded as 36-element Boolean vectors, with each nucleotide position represented by four binary digits—“1” indicating the presence of a specific nucleotide (ordered as A, C, G, and T) and “0” for the others. Library-based functionality scores were used as the dependent variable for prediction. The trained model was subsequently used to predict functionality scores for all 262 144 possible nonamer combinations.

NBD–nonamer binding prediction

To predict NBD–nonamer binding affinity for various nonamer sequences, we performed in silico mutagenesis on two high-resolution RAG–RSS structures, using FoldX (version 5.1)[54]. The first structure is of the full-length RAG1/2 complex bound to 12/23 RSS pairs (PDB ID: 5ZDZ; resolution <3 Å). As the native spacer sequences in this structure differed from those used in our library, we first modified the 12 and 23RSS sequences within the model to match our experimental constructs (12RSS: CACAGTGCTACAGACTGGAACAAAAACA; 23RSS: CACAGTGGTAGTACTCCACTGTCTGGCTGTACAAAAACA), thereby minimizing confounding effects from nonamer-flanking sequences on RSS functionality. The second structure contained isolated NBD, bound to a nonamer (PDB ID: 3GNA; resolution <3 Å). In both structures, the nonamer region was systematically altered to represent each of the 11,201 nonamers obtained from our library, and the corresponding changes in free energy (ΔΔG) relative to the canonical sequence were computed by applying the BuildModel function in FoldX. The resulting ΔΔG values from the two structures were averaged and converted to normalized binding-strength scores by subtracting each value from 1. These scores, along with the corresponding nonamer sequences, were used to train an NBD–nonamer binding prediction model using XGBoost, following the same approach described above. In parallel, models constructed from each structure separately were compared to assess reproducibility. Hydrogen bond formation patterns in bona fide and mutated nonamers were analyzed using the PyMOL tool (version 3.1.0), which provides detailed visualization of protein–DNA interactions and hydrogen-bond networks. To analyze individual interactions within a structure, structural data were extracted from the PDB file using bio3d package in R [55]. The strength of each hydrogen bond was defined as e−D, where D represents the distance (in Å) between the corresponding donor and acceptor atoms. The interaction strength between a protein residue and DNA base was then calculated as the sum over all hydrogen bonds between them. A distance threshold of 3.8 Å was applied to define hydrogen-bond formation.

RIC score calculation

RIC scores were calculated for all nonamer sequences using an in-house script in R, based on the previously described RIC model by Cowell et al. (2002) [33] using mouse bona fide RSSs as a reference.

Nonamer nucleosome occupancy scoring

Nucleosome occupancy score (NucOc) of a nonamer was defined as the average nucleosome occupancy signal around all genomic locations with this specific nonamer sequence. NucOc scores were obtained as follows: First, genomic locations of each of the 11,201 nonamers obtained from the nonamer library were mapped to the mouse genome (mm10 build). For each mapped site, nucleosome occupancy was assessed within a 200-bp window using published MNase-seq data from mouse pre-B cells (GSE53460 [56]). Subsequently, for each nonamer, an experimental nucleosome score was calculated by averaging the reads per kilobase per million mapped reads across all its genomic occurrences. Finally, these experimental scores were used to train an XGBoost regression model—following the same approach described above—to predict sequence-based nucleosome positioning scores across all 262,144 possible nonamer combinations.

Statistical analysis

Statistical tests throughout the study were performed using R version 4.1.2 (http://www.r-project.org). The statistical tests are reported in the figure legend and main text. In comparisons where T-test was applied, the Shapiro–Wilk test was used to determine data normality for the compared variables. Area under ROC curve (AUC) was calculated using the R package pROC [57].

Genome alignment

ChIP-seq reads were aligned to the mouse genome (GRCm38p2/mm10) using Bowtie (version 1.1.2) with the options: –best–all –strata –l 50, allowing 2 mismatches, and discarded reads with multiple alignments (-n 2 –m 1). SAMtools was used to convert the SAM output file to a BAM file.

Results

Unbiased recombination assay identifies non-canonical functional nonamers

The natural diversity of nonamers in bona fide RSSs, and to a greater extent in cRSSs, calls for an unbiased method to assess the effect of nonamer sequence on RSS functionality. For this purpose, we utilized a retroviral recombination substrate containing a GFP gene flanked by a 12/23RSS pair, in which the nonamer of the 23RSS was randomized (Supplementary Fig. S1A). The substrate was randomly integrated into a vAbl-transformed pre-B cell line (vAbl), wherein RAG can be inducibly expressed in the context of cell cycle arrest by treating cells with the kinase inhibitor STI-571 [52]. Next, cells with plasmid integration were sorted using the integration marker Thy1.2 (Supplementary Fig. S1B; see the “Materials and methods” section). Following 72 h of STI-571 treatment, DNA was extracted, and pre- and post-recombination sequences were obtained and sequenced (Fig. 1A).

Eleven thousand two hundred one unique sequences that appeared in two independent biological replicates were used in the analysis. For each sequence, a nonamer functionality score (hereinafter referred to as “NF score”) was defined as the log ratio between its frequency in the post-recombination and pre-recombination libraries (see the “Materials and methods” section).

Overall, the NF score was in agreement with the RIC score (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. S1C)—calculated for each nonamer, along with the fixed heptamer and spacer used in the experiment (see the “Materials and methods” section). Specifically, among the top 115 nonamers with scores >2 in both replicates (“highly functional nonamers”), 52 had RIC scores above −45 and strongly resembled the consensus nonamer (“ACAAAAACC”). Surprisingly, the remaining showed non-canonical sequences [consensus of (“NNNRWRWCT”)] and did not significantly differ from non-functional nonamers in their RIC scores (Fig. 1B and C). To rule out PCR amplification bias, we employed a 3-digit barcode at the 5′ end of the reads and used non-redundant reads to calculate the log ratio of canonical, non-canonical, and non-functional nonamers across the different libraries—confirming that the observed sequence distributions reflected true biological differences (Supplementary Fig. S1D).

To further explore the DNA code underlying nonamer functionality, we utilized the NF score derived from the experimental data to build a nonamer-based predictive model for RSS functionality (see the “Materials and methods” section). The model performed well in predicting RAG1-mediated recombination by sequence on an independent dataset (AUC = 0.88; Supplementary Fig. S1E). Importantly, the predicted NF scores strongly correlated with the experimental scores for the 11,201 library sequences (Fig. 1D). Examining the sequences of the 1000 nonamers with the top predicted scores (Supplementary Table S2) out of all possible nonamers (49 = 262 144) revealed that 733 resembled the canonical consensus nonamer (RIC > −45), whereas 267 substantially differed from it (Fig. 1E). Taken together, our unbiased nonamer screen revealed that while canonical nonamers stand out in their functionality, there is another class, comparably functional, that significantly deviates from the canonical sequence.

Non-canonical functional nonamers retain NBD binding properties

We next aimed to identify the shared features among the diverse functional nonamers. Our hypothesis was that the non-canonical functional nonamers—despite being significantly different from canonical nonamers at the sequence level—retain NBD binding properties.

To test this hypothesis, we performed in silico mutagenesis using high-resolution structures of the RAG1–DNA complex (Fig. 2A). For this analysis, we focused on the nonamer from the 23RSS of a structure containing the core region of RAG1 bound to nicked bona fide 12/23RSS pairs (PDB ID: 5ZDZ) [60] and an isolated nonamer from a structure of NBD dimer bound to a pair of consensus nonamers (PDB ID: 3GNA) [39] (see the “Materials and methods” section). In each structure, we replaced the canonical nonamer with each of the 11,201 nonamer sequences from the library and estimated the resulting changes in free energy (ΔΔG) using the FoldX tool. We then applied the XGBoost algorithm to predict ΔΔG values for all possible nonamers. Finally, we defined a binding score ranging from 0 to 1 by normalizing the predicted ΔΔG. Importantly, models from both structures performed well in ΔΔG prediction (Supplementary Fig. S2A and D). Moreover, quantifying the binding scores of the weakest and strongest binders (1st and 100th percentiles, respectively) from one structure in the other revealed strong agreement in distinguishing strong and weak binders between the two structures. (Supplementary Fig. S2B and E). An additional comparison of the 12RSS and 23RSS nonamers within the 5ZDZ structure yielded similarly strong agreement (Supplementary Fig. S2G and H).

Consistent with our hypothesis, both canonical and non-canonical functional nonamers demonstrated high binding scores compared to non-functional nonamers (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. S2C, F, and I).

To obtain the structural basis for nonamer functionality preservation in non-canonical sequences, we analyzed the site-specific interactions of nonamers with the NBD obtained from the predicted structures. To pinpoint sequence-specific contacts within the NBD, we calculated the information content (IC) of nonamers contributing to position-specific interactions across residues 390–410 of RAG1. A high IC value indicates strong base-specific interactions. In agreement with previous studies, arginine 391 (R391) and R402 exhibited clear base-specific contacts [39]. Unexpectedly, histidine 406 (H406)—not previously recognized as a critical nonamer-interacting residue—also showed a notably high IC (Fig. 2C).

Next, we examined the sequence preference of each of these three residues. Of note, the NBD makes interactions with the bottom strand of the nonamer (3′-TGTTTTTGG; Fig. 2A). The canonical T5 and T6 that have been reported to make base-specific interactions with R391 [39] show high contribution to R391-mediated interactions (Fig. 2D). In addition, a non-canonical cytosine at position 6 (C6) and—to a lesser extent—adenine at position 5 (A5) showed high contribution to R391-mediated interactions (Fig. 2D). Further examination of the structure revealed that the binding of both the canonical T6 and the non-canonical C6 is enabled by the O2 atoms of these pyrimidines that are hydrogen-bonded with the main chain amide of R391 (Supplementary Fig. S3A), as previously reported [39]. In contrast, adenine at this position (A6)—which poorly contributes to R391-mediated interactions and confers poor functionality—lacks the oxygen atom and does not form a hydrogen bond with R391 (Supplementary Fig. S3A and Fig. 2D). In the 5th nonamer position, both T5 (canonical) and A5 (non-canonical) interact with the ε-amino and guanidino groups of R391, while locating guanine at this position (G5) is predicted to result in steric hindrance with R391 due to the N2 atom of the former [39] and loss of this bonding (Supplementary Fig. S3B and Fig. 2D). Interestingly, this sequence-dependent R391 positioning also impacts the interactions at position 4. Specifically, the presence of C or G at position 5 abolishes the interaction of R391 with any base at position 4. In contrast, when a preferable base (A/T) is located at position 5, the identity of the base at position 4, matters, and C/T makes stronger interactions than A/G (Supplementary Fig. S3C and D and Fig. 2D).

Another key interaction is mediated by bonding between R402 and G2 [39]. Interestingly, both non-canonical bases - C2 that confers high functionality, and A2 that correlates with low functionality are predicted to lose their interaction with R402 (Fig. 2D) due to steric clash mediated by their N4 and N6 atoms, respectively (Fig. 2E and Supplementary Fig. S3E). However, the conformational change mediated by this clash enables a novel hydrogen bond at H406 with C2 and T2 but not with A2 (non-functional; Fig. 2D and E and Supplementary Fig. S4A). Moreover, comparing R402 and H406 among the different structures revealed that H406 interactions are only formed in structures where R402 interactions are low or absent (Fig. 2F). Thus, our model proposes that replacement of G with C/T at position 2 loses the interaction with R402 but gains compensatory interaction with H406. Supporting this idea, in silico mutagenesis predicts that the H406A mutant (Supplementary Fig. S4B) loses the alternative C/T interaction, while the canonical G2–R402 interaction remains intact (Supplementary Fig. S4C). Together, our data suggest that the functionality of functional nonamers with non-canonical sequences is enabled by retaining the interactions with the NBD.

To test whether this diverse DNA code of nonamer functionality supports RAG1 binding across the genome, we sought to compare RAG1 signal from published ChIP-seq data around canonical, non-canonical, and non-functional nonamers. Importantly, RAG1 binding to chromatin consists of at least two components: interaction with H3K4me3 through the PHD domain of RAG2 and direct interaction with DNA [7–9, 61] (Fig. 2G). Thus, we reasoned that DNA-mediated binding can be obtained by quantifying the ratio between RAG1 and H3K4me3 signals at the same genomic regions.

Comparing the RAG1/H3K4me3 ratio around representative functional (canonical and non-canonical) and non-functional nonamers (10 from each; Supplementary Fig. S4D) revealed significant RAG1 enrichment around both types of functional nonamers (Fig. 2H and I).

In conclusion, functional nonamers—both canonical and non-canonical—share the capacity for interaction with the NBD.

Functional nonamer sequences impose balance between NBD binding and nucleosome repulsion

The data above demonstrate the importance of retaining NBD binding to nonamer functionality. However, quantifying nonamer functionality with respect to binding strength (Fig. 3A) revealed that most nonamers that are predicted to be strong NBD binders are not functional (Fig. 3A, right bar, and Fig. 3B). In other words, while NBD binding is required for nonamer functionality in vivo, it is not sufficient.

As noted above, nucleosome positioning at the RSS provides a regulatory mechanism for recombination, and it has been proposed that the nonamer plays a role in overcoming this barrier. We therefore hypothesized that the nucleosome binding tendency of a nonamer sequence might affect RSS functionality in vivo.

To test this hypothesis, we defined a "nucleosome occupancy (NucOc) score" for a nonamer (Fig. 3C) as the average nucleosome occupancy signal around all genomic locations with this nonamer sequence. Specifically, for each unique nonamer sequence, nucleosome enrichment at 200-bp surrounding all its genomic occurrences was calculated by quantifying signal from published MNase-seq data from mouse pre-B cells (see the “Materials and methods” section for details) [56]. Nucleosome occupancy levels of the 11,201 unique sequences from the nonamer library were calculated (Fig. 3C). Following that, a statistical model was constructed to predict nucleosome occupancy based on nonamer sequence (see the “Materials and methods” section; Supplementary Fig. S5A). Based on the model, each nonamer received a nucleosome occupancy (“NucOc”) score, reflecting its tendency to occupy nucleosomes. Interestingly, among nonamers with high NBD binding scores, canonical and non-canonical functional nonamers showed significantly lower NucOc scores compared to non-functional nonamers (Fig. 3D).

This suggests that the nonamer DNA code for RSS functionality should support both strong NBD binding capacity and nucleosome repulsion tendency. To further test this, we examined the localization of functional nonamers within a two-dimensional space defined by NucOc scores and NBD binding scores. Our analysis revealed that overall, a high NucOc score is associated with a high NBD binding score (Fig. 3E). However, the distribution of the top 1000 most functional nonamers, based on their NF score—defined as a “functionality gate”—delineates a “sweet spot" of low nucleosome positioning and—at the same time—high NBD binding (Fig. 3E; black circles).

To quantitatively assess the distinctness of the functional nonamers with respect to NBD binding and nucleosome positioning, we divided nonamers into 10 quantiles for each parameter and quantified the enrichment of functional nonamers within each quantile. Strikingly, the top 1000 nonamers were increasingly enriched with higher NBD binding quantiles and lower NucOc score quantiles (Fig. 3F, Supplementary Table S2, and Supplementary Fig.D–F). Similarly, bona fide RSSs from the AgR loci of both mouse and human were localized at the “functionality gate” and showed a similar pattern of enrichment in quantiles (Fig. 3E and G and Supplementary Fig. S5B and C, respectively). These two characteristics can be clearly observed in the cumulative signal at bona fide 12RSSs and 23RSSs (Fig. 3H top and bottom, respectively).

Taken together, these results demonstrate that the nonamer DNA code that supports RSS functionality balances between NBD binding and nucleosome repulsion.

cRSSs’ nonamers mimic bona fide nonamers by supporting low nucleosome positioning

The data above suggest that physiological RSSs have evolutionarily converged to possess both NBD binding capacity and nucleosome repulsion.

We next sought to explore the characteristics of non-physiological cRSSs where off-target RAG-mediated recombination has been documented. Specifically, we examined two datasets: The first contains 107 cRSSs identified through a high-throughput genome-wide translocation screen assay in vAbl mouse pre-B cells (“vAbl cRSSs”) [13], and the second set consists of 129 cancer-associated cRSSs found in chromosomal deletions of childhood B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (“B-ALL cRSSs”) (Fig. 4A) [24].

We compared bona fide RSSs, functional cRSSs, and non-functional cRSSs (defined as genomic sequences with strong heptamers but no detectable RAG-mediated recombination) across three metrics: NF score, NBD binding score, and NucOc score. As expected, both vAbl and B-ALL cRSSs exhibited NF scores lower than those of bona fide RSSs but higher than those of non-functional cRSSs. In contrast, their NBD binding scores closely resembled those of non-functional cRSSs, with modest or insignificant differences (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Fig. S6A). However, both functional cRSS datasets showed NucOc scores similar to bona fide RSSs and substantially lower than non-functional cRSSs (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Fig. S6B), indicating that nucleosome repulsion, rather than NBD binding, primarily drives cRSS functionality.

This conclusion was further supported by enrichment analyses of cRSS-derived nonamers across quantiles of NBD binding and NucOc scores (Fig. 4C). While enrichment modestly increased with higher binding scores, a stronger effect was observed with decreasing NucOc scores. Quantitative comparison of the relative contributions of these two parameters across nonamer categories revealed that cRSS-derived nonamers indeed rely more heavily on nucleosome repulsion, whereas bona fide RSSs depend more on NBD binding. Interestingly, functional nonamers identified in our library screen exhibit a balanced contribution of both features (Fig. 4D).

To investigate the impact of nucleosome positioning on the extent of RAG-mediated DNA cleavage, we analyzed published END-seq data, which maps DNA breaks genome-wide at nucleotide resolution. Specifically, we aggregated signals from four END-seq experiments performed in vAbl cells where DNA repair was compromised due to Ligase-4 knockout [62]. The cumulative END-seq signal was quantified across vAbl cRSSs, which were divided into four quantiles based on their predicted nucleosome positioning. Notably, cRSSs with reduced nucleosome occupancy exhibited increased DNA cleavage, reinforcing the link between nonamer-mediated nucleosome repulsion and cRSS functionality (Fig. 4E).

In summary, this study portrays a model (Fig. 4F) in which the nonamer code is shaped by two key features—binding affinity to the RAG1 NBD and the ability to repel nucleosomes. While bona fide RSSs integrate both features, nonamers of cRSSs appear to mimic this signature primarily through low nucleosome occupancy.

Discussion

In this study, we utilized high-throughput screening to investigate RAG-mediated recombination, aiming to decode the sequence rules of the nonamer portion of the RSS. Our findings revealed that while the canonical nonamer is the most efficient, a surprising diversity of nonamers can still support effective RAG-mediated cleavage in vivo. Further analysis identified two key characteristics of these functional nonamers: (i) strong binding to the NBD and (ii) tendency for nucleosome repulsion.

The sequence promiscuity of the RSS, with only the first trinucleotide of the heptamer—5′-CAC—being perfectly conserved, has intrigued researchers seeking to decipher the underlying properties of the RSS DNA code. It has long been recognized that RAG-RSS recognition is governed by a combination of complex interactions rather than strict sequence specificity [60, 63].

In this context, Hoolehan et al. systematically analyzed the sequence specificity of the last four bases of the heptamer (along with the first two spacer bases) and found that functionality is driven by the recognition of a specific purine/pyrimidine motif [63].

Such a balance between sequence flexibility and the conservation of higher-order features is more notable in the poorly conserved spacer. For example, the distribution of CA/TG dinucleotide distribution in the spacer is proposed to support the bending and kinking of the RSS in its complex with RAG and might underlie the favorable pairing between 12/23RSS partners [60, 64]. A similar effect on the local DNA structure is attributed to the polypurine tract of the spacer [60].

The sequence specificity of RAG1, mediated through its NBD, in recognizing the nonamer has been extensively studied but remains partially uncertain. An early in vitro study demonstrated that certain substitutions within the nonamer, particularly in the A-tract at positions 5–7, significantly reduce RAG binding [65]. This finding was later supported by structural studies, which indicated that the NBD’s GGRPR motif extensively interacts with this nonamer portion [39, 60].

However, some bona fide RSSs flanking functional AgR genes deviate from this consensus sequence, even in the most conserved positions [33], and cRSSs at off-target sites often share no—or only minimal—similarity with the canonical nonamer [13, 24].

Two complementary explanations may account for this discrepancy in the nonamer sequence. First, like the heptamer and spacer, the nonamer might possess higher-order conserved features that are maintained despite sequence variability. Second, in a chromatin context, the nonamer might play additional roles beyond serving as a direct RAG binding site, contributing to RAG activity in vivo. One such role is the proposed nonamer-mediated nucleosome positioning, enabling accessibility of RAG to the DNA. Although still speculative, the A/T tract of the nonamer is expected to support nucleosome breathing [48–50].

Here, we show that the nonamer code that supports RAG-mediated cleavage in vivo extends significantly beyond the canonical sequence, even in the most conserved positions. Specifically, we show that the YWY motif on the bottom strand (RWR on the top strand) in positions 4–6 strongly supports nonamer functionality. This was further supported by analysis of predicted structures obtained from in silico mutagenesis approach, showing that base replacements that follow this motif retain the hydrogen bond interactions of the consensus 3′-TTT trinucleotide with R391. Similarly, our prediction shows that the R402 interaction is strongly restricted to the consensus guanine at position 2 and that locating a non-consensus base at this position results in a steric clash with R402. Interestingly, this loss of interaction has been shown to be accompanied by de novo interaction with H406 due to a conformational change that brought it closer to the nonamer base at this position. While these predictions, derived from structural data, are in line with our functionality assay, experimental structural data should be used to directly validate them.

Our analysis further demonstrates that functional nonamers—in addition to exhibiting high affinity for NBD—have tendency for nucleosome repulsion. This is particularly interesting considering that NBD binding and nucleosome occupancy often correlate, making conflict between the ability of a nonamer to bind RAG efficiently and its availability for RAG binding in vivo. Functional nonamers—and bona fide nonamers within them—seem to achieve a “sweet spot,” balancing strong NBD binding with nucleosome repulsion.

Another important insight from this study is the characterization of cRSSs where minimal “off-target” RAG activity has been observed. We demonstrate that, despite lacking sequence similarity to bona fide RSSs, cRSSs somewhat mimic the nonamer feature of RSSs by exhibiting a tendency for nucleosome repulsion. This demonstrates how sequences not evolved to bind NBD can still support RAG activity by adopting common characteristics.

It remains unclear whether nucleosome positioning alone can fully account for the poor functionality of the majority of high-affinity nonamers. It is plausible that excessively strong binding exerts an inhibitory effect on the recombination process. In line with this, Dai et al. [66] demonstrated that increasing the stability of the RAG complex has an inhibitory effect on cleavage, suggesting that RAG binding to RSS should be flexible enough to support RAG-mediated cleavage. Similarly, Banerjee et al. [67] showed that a pair of Db RSSs supports highly efficient synapsis but poorly supports cleavage, further emphasizing that the strength of binding does not directly infer cleavage efficiency. Future in vitro experiments using isolated RAG and RSS substrates should be conducted to assess the functionality of these sequences in nucleosome-free DNA.

Notably, our nonamer screening assay focused on the nonamer in the context of 23RSS, and we cannot rule out the possibility that the syntax of the 12RSS nonamer might show some differences. Nevertheless, the close agreement in NBD binding capacity from our structural analyses—whether with standalone nonamers or with those from 12RSS and 23RSS—suggests that while subtle variations may exist, the overall nonamer syntax is largely common across RSSs.

While this study advanced our understanding of the linguistic rules underlying RSS functionality, important gaps remain in our understanding of the specificity of RAG activity to the AgR loci and, to a lesser extent, its off-target sites. Addressing this requires consideration of sequence-independent chromatin determinants that play a role in RAG targeting. The most notable of these is the histone modification H3K4me3 that recruits RAG1/2 to chromatin through interaction with the PHD domain of RAG2 [7]. This interaction is essential for RAG activity and occurs both at the “recombination centers” within the AgR loci and at off-target sites [8]. However, given that RAG1 occupies only portions of H3K4me3 [9], other, yet unidentified, factors must also contribute. Intriguingly, RAG1 can promote ubiquitination of histone 3 (H3) via its RING domain [68]. Such ubiquitination is known to facilitate RAG-mediated recombination [69], though its role in the genome-wide RAG targeting is not known. A comprehensive model that integrates both sequence specificity of RAG and its chromatin reading and writing capacities is therefore needed to fully decode the multilayered language of RAG targeting.
