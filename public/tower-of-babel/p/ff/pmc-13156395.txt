The OsRALF5-FLR1 Module Regulates Rice Root Development and Provides a Target for Studying Cell–Cell Communication
Authors: Weiyu Xiao, Naibin Zhang, Dongxu Wen, Yinyao Qi, Junjie Xing, Yu Peng, Long Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRice root system architecture, particularly primary root length, is a crucial agronomic trait that significantly impacts water absorption, nutrient uptake, and drought tolerance. The identification of plant ligand-receptor pairs provides a foundational dataset for studying cell communication in root. However, current understanding of root-based ligand-receptor regulatory modules in rice remains limited. We identified FERONIA-Like Receptor 1 (FLR1), a member of the FERONIA-like receptor (FLR) family of receptor-like kinases (RLKs), as a vital regulator of rice root development. FLR1 governs primary root length by modulating cell division within the root apical meristem (RAM). Using an interaction screening system, we identified multiple Rapid Alkalinization Factor (RALF) peptides, which are secreted small signaling molecules, that interact with FLR1, with OsRALF5 emerging as a key functional ligand. In FLR1 promoter-edited mutants, the co-abundance of OsRALF5 and FLR1 mRNAs showed a strong positive correlation with root length. In situ hybridization and single-cell transcriptomics revealed a striking co-expression of FLR1 and OsRALF5 in the epidermis, cortex, and stele. By mediating cell-cell communication across root tissues, the OsRALF5-FLR1 module coordinates growth and maintains the stem cell niche and RAM homeostasis processes that are fundamental for root patterning. Collectively, this study strongly implicates the OsRALF5-FLR1 module in the regulation of rice root length and supports its potential as a model for studying root-related cell communication through quantitative analysis of ligand-receptor mRNA expression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12284-026-00904-w.Keywords: Receptor like kinase FLR1, OsRALF5, Rice root length, Cell–cell communication

Introduction

The root system of plants serves as the primary organ for absorbing nutrients and water, which is crucial for maintaining plant survival. As a crop with a fibrous root system, rice includes primary roots, lateral roots, and adventitious roots. Its root architecture is one of the important agronomic traits determining rice yield, particularly since root length dictates the contact area between rice and soil. Deep root length enhances penetration ability to absorb water and nitrogen from deep soil layers, alleviating competition for surface nutrients (Chen et al. 2022). Under drought stress, rice with longer roots can access deep soil moisture, mitigating water deficit stress (Kim et al. 2020), which is crucial for enhancing adaptation and ensuring stable yields.

Root development relies on root apical meristem (RAM) maintenance. Based on stem cell positions in the RAM, they can be divided into proximal and distal stem cells. Proximal stem cells, located above the quiescent center, continuously divide, and cells exiting the stem cell niche eventually differentiate into the maturation zone, with root length significantly influenced by proximal stem cells (Zhu et al. 2020). Therefore, deciphering the regulatory mechanisms of root apical stem cell activity forms the cellular basis for understanding root system establishment.

Plant receptor-like protein kinases (RLKs) located on the plasma membrane act as initial sensors for such signaling networks. By perceiving signals from small peptides, RLKs mediate intercellular communication during root growth, development, and stress responses, dynamically regulating root architecture (Zhu et al. 2020). In Arabidopsis, the peptide ROOT MERISTEM GROWTH FACTOR 1 (RGF1) and its receptors RGF1 RECEPTORS (RGFRs)/RGF1 INSENSITIVES (RGIs) enhance transcription factor PLETHORA 2 (PLT2) stability by altering reactive oxygen species distribution in root tips, promoting mitotic activity (Shinohara et al. 2016; Yamada et al. 2019), and quintuple rgi mutant exhibits extremely short root (Ou et al. 2016). Additionally, the CLAVATA3/ESR-RELATED 14 (CLE40)/CLAVATA3 (CLV3)-ARABIDOPSIS CRINKLY 4 (ACR4) pathway restricts transcription factor (WUS-RELATED HOMEOBOX 5) WOX5 expression to regulate root meristem development (Stahl et al. 2009, 2013).

While these pathways establish the fundamental framework for root development, specialized signaling modules have also evolved to coordinate root architecture with fluctuating environments. Several modules provide successful precedents: under phosphate deficiency, the CLAVATA3/ESR-RELATED 14 (CLE14)–CLAVATA2 (CLV2)/PEP1 RECEPTOR 2 (PEPR2) module remodels root architecture by regulating SHR/SCR and auxin pathways (Gutiérrez et al., 2017). Similarly, the C-TERMINALLY ENCODED PEPTIDE (CEP)–CEP RECEPTOR (CEPR) module coordinates nitrogen acquisition with root morphology to adapt to nutrient fluctuations (Tabata et al. 2014). Beyond nutrient regulation, the Rapid Alkalinization Factor 1 (RALF1)–FERONIA (FER) module influences root elongation under stress conditions by modulating RNA alternative splicing and phase separation (Wang et al. 2020; Xu et al. 2024b). These diverse functional examples underscore the value of dissecting ligand–receptor interactions in crops for integrating environmental cues and regulating root plasticity.

As peptides represent a promising class of bioagents for agricultural applications (Zhang et al. 2023), identifying ligand–receptor pathways governing root development in rice could facilitate the development of peptide-based strategies for enhancing nutrient uptake and stress tolerance. The rice genome encodes over 1,100 receptor kinases, far exceeding the 600 in Arabidopsis thaliana (Liu et al. 2024). However, the mechanisms underlying the formation and regulation of roots length remain poorly understood, with only a few genes or regulatory modules reported, such as ROOT LENGTH REGULATOR 4 (OsRLR4) (Sun et al. 2022) and CALCINEURIN B-LIKE PROTEINS (OsCBL)-CBL-INTERACTING PROTEIN KINASE 3 (OsCIPK3) (Yu et al. 2025). Therefore, studies on root-related regulatory genes or pathways in rice could provide targets for improving nutrient uptake and stress resistance.

Cell-cell communication is a fundamental biological process enabling information transfer and functional coordination through chemical signals, physical contact, or structural linkage (Su et al. 2024). Advances in artificial intelligence and single-cell genomics have provided a foundation for studying cell communication. Modeling cell communication facilitates the simulation of intercellular/intracellular biological functions and creates a basis for synthetic cell design (Bunne et al. 2024). However, plant cell communication research faces several challenges: (1) The lack of ligand-receptor pairs with defined functional associations in plants hinders the development of virtual plant cell models. (2) Establishing cell communication networks relies on multi-omics data, particularly transcriptomic gene expression profiles, yet the correlation between root membrane-localized ligand/receptor expression levels and specific cellular behaviors remains uncertain. Constructing root function-based ligand-receptor modules will provide data support for future root cell communication studies.

The Arabidopsis FER receptor kinase regulates diverse developmental processes, including cell elongation and fertility. In rice, its homologs form the FERONIA-like receptor (FLR) family. Among them, FERONIA-like receptor 1 (FLR1) and FERONIA-like receptor 2 (FLR2) are the closest homologs to FER and have been functionally characterized in rice. Loss-of-function mutants flr1 and flr2 exhibit pleiotropic phenotypes, including semi-dwarf stature and reduced tillering, with flr1 specifically displaying severe male sterility due to defective pollen development (Li et al. 2016). Another member, FERONIA-like receptor 15 (FLR15), has been implicated in regulating grain size, the flr15 mutants exhibited a severe dwarf phenotype (Wang et al. 2021). This study focuses on rice roots and identifies the receptor kinase FLR1 as a regulator of root development. Using an interaction screening system, multiple OsRALF ligands interacting with FLR1 were identified. Genetic validation confirmed that the OsRALF5-FLR1 module mediates root development regulation in rice. By generating FLR1 promoter-edited lines with varying expression levels, we demonstrated a strong correlation between OsRALF5 and FLR1 expression in roots, providing a peptide-based tool for rice root modulation. These findings not only establish a foundation for plant root cell communication research but also highlight the potential of peptide-receptor expression levels as a predictor of root phenotypic traits.

Methods

Plant Materials and Growth Conditions

In this study, the wild-type (WT) backgrounds for our genetic material using the Nipponbare (Oryza sativa L. ssp. japonica), Dongjin (Oryza sativa L. ssp. j aponica), and Hwayoung (Oryza sativa L. ssp. japonica), respectively. Specifically, the flr1 mutant and its controls were in the DJ background, while the flr2 mutant and its controls were in a comparable japonica background. These flr1 and flr2 lines are previously described loss-of-function transferred DNA (T-DNA) insertion mutants (Li et al. 2016). The twelve flr mutant lines used in this study, which are in the Nipponbare (NIP) background, were developed in prior research (Yang et al. 2020). The FLR1-overexpressing (FLR1-OE) transgenic lines used in this study were generated as previously described (Wang et al. 2021). Briefly, the full-length FLR1 coding sequence lacking the stop codon was cloned into the pCAMBIA1300 vector and introduced into rice embryogenic calli via Agrobacterium tumefaciens (strain EHA105)-mediated transformation. Additionally, the osralf mutant lines (osralf5, osralf20, osralf23, osralf27, osralf28, osralf43) were newly generated in the NIP background using the CRISPR/Cas9 system, targeting specific exons to create frameshift mutations and disrupt gene function. The specific mutation types (e.g., nucleotide deletions/insertions), locations within the gene, and the resulting impact on the coding sequence for each mutant are detailed in Table S1 and have been validated by sequencing. The plants were cultivated for 14 days in a constant-temperature incubator set at 26 °C with 60% to 70% relative humidity under simulated 16-hour light and 8-hour dark cycles.

Vector Construction and Rice Transformation

The CRISPR/Cas9 system utilized in this study was executed following the established pYLCRISPR/Cas9 multiplex genome-editing protocol (Ma et al. 2015). A dual-target knockout strategy employing two distinct promoters, OsU6a and OsU6b, was implemented to drive the expression of different single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs), ensuring efficient and simultaneous editing of the target genes. For reporter construction, a 1.5-kb promoter fragment upstream of FLR1 was PCR amplified from NIP genomic DNA using Phanta Flash Super-Fidelity DNA Polymerase. The amplified FLR1 promoter was subsequently cloned into the multiple cloning site of pCAMBIA1301, generating a transcriptional fusion with the β-glucuronidase (GUS) reporter gene. All recombinant vectors were introduced into NIP embryogenic calli through Agrobacterium tumefaciens (strain EHA105)-mediated transformation. Plant genetic transformation services were commercially obtained from Wuhan Boyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Primer sequences for vector construction are detailed in Table S2.

Analysis of Root Length Phenotypes of flrs and osralfs Mutants

To characterize the functional contributions of these genes to rice root system architecture, surface-sterilized seeds (20–30 per genotype) were imbibed in sterile water and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Seedlings exhibiting radicle emergence (≥ 1 mm) were transferred to growth chambers maintained at 28 °C 8/16 h photoperiod. Following 14 days of hydroponic growth, seedlings were harvested for analysis. Primary root length was measured using ImageJ software from digital images. Three biological replicates (n ≥ 15 seedlings per genotype) were evaluated. Statistical comparisons were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test).

EdU Staining

5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) Staining was performed as previously described (Hao et al. 2023). Two millimeters of root tips of 7-day-old rice seedlings were incubated in 1 mL deionized water with 10 µM EdU for 2 h. The root tips were then fixed in a freshly prepared solution containing 3.7% (v/v) paraformaldehyde and 1% (v/v) Triton-X 100 in 1 × PBS for 1 h, followed by two washes with 3% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA) in 1 × PBS. Subsequently, the seedlings were incubated with 100 ml of Click-iT reaction mixture (from Abbkine Click-iT Cell Proliferation EdU Image Kit (Green Fluorescence), KTA2030, China; composed of 76 ml deionized water, 10 ml of 10 × reaction buffer, 4 ml of copper reagent, 0.2 ml of AbFluor 488 azide, and 10 ml of 10 × reducing agent) for 1 h at room temperature, protected from light. After staining, the seedlings were washed once with 3% (w/v) BSA in 1 × PBS and then stored in 1 × PBS in the dark until imaging. GFP fluorescence was excited using the 488 nm spectral line and detected at 465–505 nm The fluorescence signals were visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope.

Prediction of Genomic cis-Regulatory Elements

The 2000-bp genomic sequence upstream of FLR1 was retrieved as its putative promoter region from the Rice Genome Annotation Project database (https://rice.uga.edu/). The FLR1 promoter sequence was subsequently submitted to the PlantCARE (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) platform for systematic prediction and annotation of cis-acting regulatory elements according to previous study (Guo et al. 2024).

Molecular Cloning and Vector Construction

The construction of the signal peptide-CLuc-FLR1 vector was performed following previously described methods (Liu et al. 2021a). Briefly, to construct the signal peptide-CLuc-FLR1 vector, the FLR1 protein sequence was retrieved from the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/), and the presence of N-terminal signal peptide was predicted using the SignalP-6.0 online tool. The coding sequences for the FLR1 signal peptide (residues 1–22), CLuc, and the FLR1 cytoplasmic domain deletion fragment (residues 23–459) were amplified by PCR and cloned into the 35S promoter-driven pCAMBIA1300 vector using the ClonExpress II One-Step Cloning Kit. For the OsRALF-NLuc vector construction, the coding sequences of OsRALF and NLuc were amplified by PCR and cloned into the 35S promoter-driven pCAMBIA1300 vector using the aforementioned method. All other constructs were similarly assembled using homologous recombination-based cloning strategies.

Luciferase Complementation Assay

The screening vectors OsRALF-NLuc and signal peptide-CLuc-FLR1 were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. Bacterial suspensions were spread onto LB agar plates supplemented with kanamycin and rifampicin (Kana/Rif LB plates) and incubated at 28 °C. Single colonies were inoculated into liquid LB medium for overnight culture. Freshly grown bacterial cells were resuspended in infiltration buffer to OD600 = 1.0, premixed, and incubated at room temperature for 1–3 h. Four-week-old Nicotiana benthamiana plants were selected for infiltration, with three leaves from independent plants injected per experimental group to serve as biological replicates. Infiltrated plants were covered with black plastic bags for 24 h of dark treatment, followed by 24–48 h of light incubation. After protein expression, leaf discs were excised, coated with 10 µL luciferin substrate, and subjected to a 5-minute dark adaptation. Luciferase activity was detected using an in vivo fluorescence imaging system to evaluate OsRALF-FLR1 protein interactions.

GUS Assays

Histochemical GUS staining in T1-generation pFLR1::GUS transgenic plants was performed following established protocol (Liu et al. 2022). Briefly, appropriate quantities of pFLR1::GUS tissues were immersed in GUS staining solution (Solarbio, G3061, China) and incubated at 37℃ for 24 h. Active GUS expression in plant tissues manifested as blue coloration at enzymatic activity sites. Post-staining procedures involved chlorophyll removal through 70% ethanol immersion for 3-hour intervals, repeated until negative regions achieved complete discoloration. For thorough chlorophyll elimination, decolorization steps were reiterated until pigment-free status. Ethanol-preserved specimens were analyzed via conventional light microscopy, displaying GUS-positive blue signals against whitened backgrounds. Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were prepared and imaged using a digital slide scanning system (3Dhistech Pannoramic MIDI, Hungary) for visualization and documentation.

RNA Extraction and qRT-PCR

For RNA isolation from plant tissue, total RNA was extracted using a Plant Total RNA Extraction Kit (TIANGEN, DP419, China). cDNA synthesis followed established protocols from the RevertAid Reverse Transcriptase Kit (Thermo Scientific, K1622, USA) as referenced in prior research (Zeng et al. 2020). Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT‒PCR) analyses were conducted with SYBR I Premix ExTaq-based qRT‒PCR (Takara Bio, RR067A, Japan) for transcriptional profiling. Triplicate biological samples were examined, with expression normalization achieved through comparative ΔCt methodology. Intergroup statistical significance was assessed via single-factor variance analysis. The OsActin was used as the housekeeping gene in all qPCR analyses. Amplification primers are provided in Table S2.

Expression Profiling of OsRALF Peptides Across Rice Tissues

Expression levels of all rice OsRALF small peptides in different tissues were retrieved from the Rice Expression Database (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/red/index). The acquired expression data (FPKM) were subsequently visualized using TBtools software.

Pull-Down Assay

Pull-down assay was performed as previously described (Wang et al. 2020). The expression of recombinant proteins, including GST-FLR1-ECD and His-TF-OsRALF5, was induced with IPTG in E. coli BL21. Following purification, the His-TF-OsRALF5 protein was incubated overnight at 4 °C with recombinant GST-FLR1-ECD protein or GST control protein in the presence of GST beads. After incubation, the beads were washed three times with washing buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, and 10 mM MgCl₂). The bound proteins were released by boiling in a 2× SDS sample buffer and subjected to 10% SDS‒PAGE, and analyzed by immunoblotting using anti-His (ABclonal, AE003, China) and anti-GST (ABclonal, AE001, China) antibodies.

Bimolecular Fluorescence Complementation Assay

To generate bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) constructs, the coding sequence of OsRALF5 was cloned and inserted the pE3449 vector to produce OsRALF5-cVenus, while the coding region of FLR1 was cloned and inserted into the pE3308 vector to generate FLR1-nVenus. The resulting constructs were then transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. Agrobacterium tumefaciens strains containing the pE3449 and pE3308 constructs were co-infiltrated into the leaves of N. benthamiana. Leaf discs were harvested from the infiltrated area at 2 days post-infiltration and subjected to confocal laser-scanning microscopy. Venus fluorescence was excited using the 488 nm spectral line and detected at 465–505 nm. The fluorescence signals were visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope.

Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization

Fourteen-day-old NIP plant roots were collected, rapidly fixed in FAA solution, and processed through sequential dehydration, paraffin infiltration, and embedding. The Cy3-labeled probe for FLR1 and the FITC-labeled probe for OsRALF5 were directly synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Tissue sections underwent pretreatment and hybridization following established protocols (Zhang et al. 2021). Fluorescent signals were captured using a Nikon Eclipse 80i fluorescence confocal microscopy system, and subsequent image analysis and overlay were performed using CaseViewer 2.4.

Analysis of FLR1 Single-Cell Expression Data

The single-cell expression data of FLR1 was retrieved and visualized through the PlantscRNAdb website (http://ibi.zju.edu.cn/plantscrnadb) (Chen et al. 2021) and the RCAC website (https://www.elabcaas.cn/rcar/index.html) (Liu et al. 2021b).

Results

Root Length Analysis of FLR Family Mutants

To investigate the functional role of receptors in regulating rice root development, we generated and analyzed mutants of FLR family genes (Li et al. 2016; Yang et al. 2020). Through comprehensive phenotypic analysis of loss-of-function mutants, we observed that flr1, flr2, and flr15 mutants displayed significantly reduced primary root lengths compared to WT plants at 14 days post-germination. Specifically, the primary root lengths of flr1, flr2, cas9-flr15-20, and cas9-flr15-45 were reduced by approximately 52%, 43%, 57%, and 54%, respectively, relative to their corresponding WT (Fig. S1A–D). These findings suggest that specific FLR family members play crucial roles in modulating root development in rice. Consistent with previous reports (Li et al. 2016), we observed that the shoot lengths of flr1 and flr2 mutants were significantly reduced compared to the WT (Fig. S1A). Based on the pronounced root phenotype observed in flr1 mutants, we selected FLR1 for further functional investigation. We generated FLR1-OE transgenic lines in the NIP background. Consistent with our hypothesis, FLR1 overexpression resulted in a significant enhancement of root length, with transgenic plants exhibiting approximately 25% longer roots compared to WT controls (Fig. 1A, B). We further investigated RAM activity in WT, flr1, and FLR1-OE using the EdU staining. Both confocal imaging and quantification of EdU-labeled cells revealed a significant reduction in the signal of EdU-labeled cells in the flr1 mutant compared to WT, while the signal of EdU-labeled cells in FLR1-OE was markedly increased (Fig. 1C, D). These results indicate that FLR1 regulates RAM cell division, thereby influencing root length in rice.

Expression Pattern of FLR1 in Root

To investigate the molecular mechanism by which FLR1 regulates root elongation in rice, we analyzed its expression patterns using qRT‒PCR and GUS staining. qRT‒PCR revealed that FLR1 mRNA is expressed at varying levels in roots, stems, and leaf of NIP seedlings, with significantly higher expression in roots compared to stems and leaves (Fig. 2A). Spatial expression analysis of FLR1 in root tissues was further performed using GUS staining. The strongest GUS signals were detected in the root tip, with high expression specifically observed in the root meristematic zone, crown root initiation sites, and crown root meristems (Fig. 2B-F). Notably, expression was particularly prominent in the cortex/endodermal primary tissues of the RAM (Fig. 2G). These results indicate that FLR1 is highly expressed in regions of active cell division, highlighting its critical role in root development.

Ligand Screening of FLR1 and Analysis of its Expression Levels Across Different Tissues

Previous studies have reported that the Rapid Alkalinization Factor (RALF) small peptide family in rice comprises 41 members (Kim et al. 2023). To identify potential ligand molecules that co-regulate root development with FLR1, we first analyzed the expression profiles of rice OsRALF family members with Rice Expression Database data. The OsRALF family displayed distinct expression profiles, including both ubiquitous (e.g., OsRALF4/6/8/26/28) and tissue-enriched (e.g., OsRALF25 in roots) members (Fig. S2). Given that FLR1 regulates root elongation and is highly expressed in roots, we prioritized OsRALF ligands that are also expressed in root tissues. We identified 36 OsRALF genes with reported root expression from the Rice Expression Database (Table S3). Due to technical challenges caused by the high local GC content in certain members CDS regions which hindered successful amplification, we eventually obtained 26 OsRALF peptides. These were subsequently utilized in a high-throughput luciferase complementation assay (LCA) to screen for interactions between FLR1 and the 26 OsRALF peptides (Fig. 3A). The results identified six OsRALF peptides, including OsRALF5, OsRALF20, OsRALF23, OsRALF27, OsRALF28, and OsRALF43, that interact with FLR1-ECD, suggesting their potential role as candidate ligands in FLR1-mediated signaling (Fig. 3A, B; Fig. S3). To validate their functional in root development, we generated knockout mutants of osralf5, osralf20, osralf23, osralf27, osralf28, and osralf43 in the NIP genetic background and conducted phenotypic analyses of root length (Fig. S4A-F). Phenotypic analysis showed that both osralf5 and osralf20 mutants had significantly shorter primary roots, and consistent with this trend, the shoots of osralf5, osralf20, and osralf23 mutants were also markedly shorter than those of the WT (Fig. 3C, D). In addition, we also performed qRT‒PCR analysis on six FLR1-interacting OsRALF genes to further delineate their expression levels in the root, stem, and leaf of NIP. The results demonstrated that OsRALF43 exhibited the highest expression levels in root, stem, and leaf, whereas OsRALF5 showed the next highest expression, with particularly high expression in roots (Fig. 3E–G). These findings suggest that OsRALF5 and OsRALF20, acting as FLR1-interacting partners through the receptor-ligand signaling pathway, may play critical roles in regulating root development in rice.

The OsRALF5-FLR1 Module Affects the Root Length of Rice Via its Expression Level

The high expression levels of FLR1 in roots, combined with the short-root phenotype observed in flr1 mutants, suggest that the mRNA expression levels of FLR1 may regulate root length in rice. To investigate the impact of FLR1 expression levels on rice root length, we employed a tiling deletion strategy targeting the cis-regulatory regions of FLR1 to generate mutant lines with varying FLR1 expression levels (Guo et al. 2024). Within the 1000-bp promoter region upstream of the TSS, 189 sites meeting the PAM sequence requirements of the Cas9 system were identified, and four short segments containing key cis-acting elements were subjected to targeted deletion (Fig. 4A). Sequencing validation of promoters in different mutant lines revealed that Targets T1T2 produced large deletions of 40 bp and 37 bp, effectively removing the CAAT box; 92 bp and 90 bp deletions at Targets T3T4 eliminated the TATA box; 104 bp and 84 bp deletions at Targets T5T6 disrupted the CAT box; And target T7T8 successfully deleted approximately 10 bases, but failure to effectively knockout the MYB-binding site (Fig. 4A). After successful construction the promoter-editing materials, root length was analyzed in T1 generation homozygous mutants. The results indicated that the root length in the T1T2 transgenic plants was significantly shorter than that in the WT, while there were no significant differences between the other edited materials and the WT (Fig. 4B, C). To explore the relationship between ligand-receptor expression levels and root length, FLR1 and its ligands OsRALF5/OsRALF20 expression were examined in FLR1 promoter-editing plants. Results showed significant downregulation of FLR1 expression in T1T2 transgenic plants (Fig. 4D). The expression level of OsRALF5 showed a significant increase in both T3T4-1 and T5T6-1 transgenic plants, whereas OsRALF20 expression was significantly downregulated in all materials except T5T6-2 line (Fig. 4E, F). Although only T1T2-1 and T1T2-2 displayed significantly shorter roots than WT, FLR1 expression levels showed a strong positive correlation with root length (R2 = 0.79, Fig. 4G). Further analysis revealed a moderate correlation between OsRALF5 expression and root length in FLR1 promoter-editing materials (R2 = 0.44, Fig. 4H), while OsRALF20 expression showed no significant association with root length (R2 = 0.17; Fig. 4I). In addition, we conducted separate correlation analyses between the expression levels of OsRALF5/OsRALF20 and FLR1. Consistent with the correlation patterns observed in roots, OsRALF5 exhibited a significant positive correlation with FLR1 (R2 = 0.59; Fig. S5A), whereas no significant correlation was detected between OsRALF20 and FLR1 expression levels (R2 = 0.06; Fig. S5B). These findings demonstrated that FLR1 and OsRALF5 exhibit highly correlated mRNA expression patterns in roots and collectively regulate rice root length.

OsRALF5-FLR1 Module is Involved in Root Cell–Cell Communication

Ligand-receptor interactions mediate signal transduction, thereby regulating cell–cell communication and executing biological functions (Armingol et al. 2024). To elucidate the functional relationship between FLR1 and OsRALF5, we performed a series of complementary experiments. Therefore, we further validated the interaction between FLR1 and OsRALF5 through in vitro pull-down assays (Fig. 5A). The GST-tagged extracellular domain (ECD) of FLR1 (GST-FLR1-ECD) was able to specifically pull down His-tagged OsRALF5 (His-TF-OsRALF5), whereas the GST control did not, demonstrating a direct interaction between OsRALF5 and FLR1-ECD in vitro (Fig. 5A). To validate this interaction in a living cellular context, we employed a BiFC assay in N. benthamiana leaves. Co-expression of FLR1-nVenus and OsRALF5-cVenus reconstituted Venus fluorescence at the cell periphery, whereas control combinations showed no signal (Fig. 5B). This result confirms that FLR1 and OsRALF5 interact in vivo. A functional signaling module requires not only molecular interaction but also spatial co-expression. We therefore performed fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) to map their expression patterns in rice roots. Transverse sections revealed that both FLR1 and OsRALF5 mRNAs were predominantly localized in the epidermis, cortex, and stele (Fig. 5C, F). Longitudinal sections further showed their expression in the root cap, stem cell niche, and epidermis of the root tip and elongation zone (Fig. 5D, E, G, H). Critically, FLR1 and OsRALF5 exhibit strikingly overlapping expression patterns across these distinct cell layers (Liu et al. 2021b; Chen et al. 2021). To clearly define the spatial context of these distinct cell populations, schematic representations of rice root cross-sectional and longitudinal anatomy are provided (Fig. 5I, J). Mapping the single-cell data onto these anatomical structures confirmed that, consistent with our FISH results, FLR1 expression was prominently enriched in cortical, stele, and epidermal cell clusters (Fig. 5K–M). In conclusion, the biochemical interaction and overlapping spatial expression patterns of OsRALF5 and FLR1 suggest a possible role for this ligand-receptor pair in mediating signaling between root cell layers. These findings propose a model in which the OsRALF5-FLR1 module may act as a signaling system influencing root development, providing a basis for further functional validation of cell-to-cell communication in this context.

Discussion

The receptor kinase FER regulates numerous processes of root growth, such as root hair development and root penetration ability (Xu et al. 2024a; Duan et al. 2010). Meanwhile, its ligand molecule RALF1 has the ability to inhibit root elongation. Studies have shown that the RALF1-FER module inhibits the elongation of primary roots (Du et al. 2016). However, it remains unclear how the homologous genes of FER in rice regulate root development. Here, we found that both FLR1 and FLR2, the two genes most homologous to FER in rice, promote root development in rice, indicating functional differences from FER in Arabidopsis. Previous studies have shown that FLR1 and FLR2 jointly regulate plant height in rice (Li et al. 2016; Pu et al. 2017). In terms of root length regulation, FLR1 and FLR2 also exhibit synergistic functions. GUS staining experiments confirmed that FLR1 is highly expressed in roots, particularly in root tips and cortical cells (Fig. 3C). Several receptor kinases regulate root length by modulating cell division in the meristematic zone (Stahl et al. 2009, 2013), suggesting that FLR1 may primarily influence rice root length through cell division. Future research will focus on identifying downstream genes through which FLR1 and FLR2 mediate root cell division.

There are many cell types in root cells, with the development of single-cell sequencing techniques, good technical means have been provided for studying cell communication between different cells. However, the establishment of cell communication mainly relies on the interaction between ligand-receptor pairs and the expression abundance of ligands and receptors in single-cell sequencing. Firstly, plant cells lack a complete ligand-receptor relationship (Xu et al. 2022). Meanwhile, it is unclear whether the mRNA expression levels of ligands and receptors regulating the same signaling pathway are correlated. In this study, we used the OsRALF5-FLR1 module to clearly demonstrate that the mRNA expression levels of ligands and receptors are significantly correlated. After more ligand-receptor pairs in roots are identified in the future, the communication processes between different cells in roots will be established. Therefore, this work lays a methodological foundation for constructing rice ligand-receptor networks in the future and exploring intercellular communication through quantitative expression analysis of ligand-receptor pairs.

The receptor kinase FLR1 is a multi-trait regulatory gene, and multiple ligand molecules have been found to interact with it. However, effective methods for establishing the link between different ligands and receptors in specific phenotypes are still lacking. In this study, we verified that OsRALF5 acts as the primary ligand molecule of FLR1 to affect root length development (Fig. 4D-I). While the genetic evidence in this study supports the role of the OsRALF5-FLR1 module in regulating root length, the conclusion would be further strengthened by more rigorous genetic analyses, such as generating double mutants or overexpressing OsRALF5 in the flr1 mutant background. Nevertheless, the integrated approach applied here, combining interaction screening with phenotypic validation in receptor expression variants, may serve as a generalizable strategy for linking other receptors and ligands to specific phenotypes.
