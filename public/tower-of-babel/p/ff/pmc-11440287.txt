Biodesign: Engineering an aortic endograft explantation tool
Authors: Grayson S Pitcher
Published: 2024

Open surgical repair can be a definitive treatment for failed endovascular aneurysm repair. However, open graft explantation is associated with increased morbidity and mortality secondary to the technical challenges of this complex procedure. In the Journal of Vascular Surgery: Cases, Innovations, and Techniques, Hatami et al present a novel functional explantation tool to assist with open graft explantation.1

One technical consideration is the removal of a well-incorporated endograft with active suprarenal fixation where hooked barbs can result in significant endothelial damage and hemorrhage. A limited number of techniques have been reported to help free the incorporated graft in the perirenal aorta. The ‘modified syringe technique’ is a method where a large bore syringe is cut and shaved to make a smooth cylinder that is then inserted over the endograft until the suprarenal stents are covered, and where the syringe-graft assembly can be removed together.2 Another technique involves using a disposable proctoscope, which also has a smooth cylinder and facilitates resheathing into the proctoscope in a similar fashion to the ‘modified syringe technique.’3 However, both techniques are limited by their uniform circumference design, where insertion may result in a “snowplow” effect secondary to shear stress between the device and aorta. Snagging can also occur at the junctional fabric zones between individual stents as the proximal edge of the device inserts over the caudal aspect of the endograft. Other techniques include cutting the suprarenal struts and leaving the struts and barbs in situ, and manual pressure application to the proximal graft with directional force to disengage the barbed hooks from the aorta.4

Hatami et al have presented these technical considerations through the focus of mechanical engineering biodesign framework to develop a functional explantation device to decrease aortic wall damage. Their proposed solution is a flexible cylinder with an interior lumenal taper to reduce graft hook diameter and mitigate endothelial tearing from the hooks. A further component of their design includes integrated ball bearings to reduce shear stress on the aortic endothelium. The tapered geometric design enables a caudally oriented inward radial force to help collapse the endograft and is an upgrade to the linear cylinder. Therefore, the tapered design should decrease the risk of the caudal endograft struts catching on the cranial aspect of the explantation device and allow for easier capture of the top stents while reducing the manual forces exerted from the surgeon, which would otherwise translate to shear stress on the aortic endothelium. The device does not entirely solve the issue of well-incorporated suprarenal barbs, and some cranial force of the device and graft may be necessary to displace these barbs. However, the device is a promising enhancement to an existing technique for an otherwise highly morbid operation.
