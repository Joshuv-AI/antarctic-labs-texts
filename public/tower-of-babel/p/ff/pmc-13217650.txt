If you build it who will come? Widening access through a place-based Rural Training Stream to address local medical workforce shortages
Authors: Lara Fuller, Jessica Beattie, Laura Gray, Matthew McGrail
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe establishment of end-to-end rural medical education provides an opportunity to embed place-based strategies to address rural workforce disparities. Deakin University established a Rural (medical) Training Stream (RTS) in 2022, prioritising applicants from the school’s rural footprint in Western Victoria. From 2024, the RTS was extended to a rural end-to-end program (RTS E2E) utilising blended learning to enable students to remain living in their rural communities. This study evaluates the impact of these programs on widening access for under-represented populations and implications for regional workforce development.MethodsCross-sectional admissions data (2020-2025) collected at application to Deakin’s. Doctor of Medicine (MD) course were examined according to five entry cohorts: 2020-21 pre-RTS General, 2022-23 General, 2022-23 RTS, 2024-25 General, 2024-25 RTS E2E. Student demographic variables examined included gender, age, rural background location, residence at course application and undergraduate degree. Descriptive statistics, Fisher’s exact test and the Fisher-Freeman-Halton test were used to analyse cohort differences. ResultsData for 783 students were included in the analysis. Prior to the RTS (2020-21), 5% (n = 12) of MD students were from the rural footprint. This increased to 15% (n = 37) across both cohorts in 2022-23. In 2024-25, 100% of RTS E2E students were from the rural footprint, increasing their proportion in the MD cohort to 28% (n = 77). There were significantly more female students in the RTS E2E (77%) than the RTS (55%) or the General cohorts (range 45–48%). Significantly more students aged over 29 were in the RTS E2E (69%), than the RTS (20%) or General cohorts (range 13–24%). More students entering the RTS E2E had undergraduate health professional degrees (48%) than the RTS (15%) or General cohorts (range 18–25%).ConclusionThe RTS E2E program has successfully widened access for a distinct cohort of students from the rural footprint, predominantly mature-aged, female, health professionals returning to study. With consideration of tailored strategies to support success, there is an opportunity to strengthen students’ existing connections to their rural communities during their rural E2E MD and retain them as medical practitioners for the region, with the depth of understanding of their communities required for locally contextualised, patient-centred care. Background

Socially accountable approaches to medical education and workforce development recognise the priority to develop a local workforce that reflects and meets the needs of the communities in which training is embedded [1–3]. Strategies employed to achieve these aims include prioritising the recruitment of students from communities that have the greatest health needs and implementing measures to widen access for under-represented population groups [4–7]. In Australia, rural communities are a priority group in recognition of the maldistribution of the health workforce in these areas, particularly in primary care [8, 9].

Rural background (RB) students are widely acknowledged to be an under-represented group in medicine, experiencing intersecting forms of disadvantage that impact on achieving medical school entry, including educational access and lower socioeconomic status [10, 11]. The shortage of medical practitioners in rural areas compounds this, with a lack of visibility impacting on career aspirations [11]. They are also a group that are most likely to work as rural practitioners upon graduation [12–15], hence measures aiming to redress rural workforce shortages often include strategies for the recruitment of more RB students [10, 15, 16].

The Rural Health Multidisciplinary Training (RHMT) program that provides funding support for Australia’s Rural Clinical Schools (RCSs) defines RB as having lived for five years consecutively or ten years cumulatively in rural Australia (with ‘rural’ currently defined by the Modified Monash (MM) model as MM2-7) [17, 18]. Medical programs with RCSs are required to select a minimum quota of 25% RB students annually, using this definition [18]. As entry into medical school occurs on a national scale and is highly competitive, RB students may apply widely, commonly resulting in intra- and inter-state relocation to take up places. Consequently, students from the local rural region may be greatly under-represented in their local medical school. Furthermore, following graduation, there is often a redistribution of RB students, with a lack of retention in the local training area [19, 20].

An increasing number of rural end-to-end medical programs are being established in Australia, building on the existing national RCS program to provide greater opportunities for entire completion of medical courses in rural locations [21]. Such programs employ a comprehensive approach to rural medical education, aligning recruitment, training and evaluation plans with the aims of the program [22, 23]. There is an opportunity to strengthen this further with the adoption of place-based approaches, with proactive recruitment strategies and a program delivery model designed to meet the unique contextual needs of local communities [16, 24]. Prioritising the recruitment of local rural students is an evidence-based strategy to enhance local workforce development, as those who both originate from and train in the region are far more likely to stay and work there after graduation [20]. Furthermore, an intentional focus on local recruitment has the potential to increase the admission of students who more closely reflect, and better understand, the healthcare context and needs of their community [16].

Deakin’s School of Medicine is a four-year graduate entry Doctor of Medicine (MD), with RCSs established under the RHMT program in Western Victoria. With an area of 79,843 square kilometres, the Western Victoria Primary Healthcare Network (PHN) comprises approximately one third of the state of Victoria [25]. The population of 708,573 (2023) is concentrated in the Greater Geelong and Ballarat Local Government Areas, and the medical workforce maldistribution mirrors this, with 62% of general practitioners in the PHN located in these larger centres in 2023 [26]. In response to these critical workforce shortages, a Rural Training Stream (RTS) was developed in Deakin’s MD, adopting a place-based approach to growing our own workforce, through prioritising local student recruitment and training them in place. Key elements of the RTS design included geographic tiered selection, adapted admission criteria and redesign of curriculum delivery.

Geographic tiered selection

A formal description of Deakin’s ‘rural footprint’ in Western Victoria was adopted in 2019, inclusive of small, medium and large rural towns, remote communities and specific regional centres within the Western Victorian Primary Healthcare Network (MM2-6) [27]. Commencing in 2022, applicants who met the RHMT RB definition for locations in the rural footprint (Tier 1) were prioritised for entry to the 30 MD RTS places annually, ahead of applicants from other parts of rural Victoria (Tier 2) and rural Australia (Tier 3).

Adapted admission criteria

Community consultation in the rural footprint identified barriers to medical school entry for local applicants and informed changes to the admission requirements for the RTS, which were introduced incrementally (Fig. 1). To increase the selection of students with a meaningful connection to a rural community and personal traits and aspirations aligned with the goals of the RTS, a written application was introduced to the existing MD admission requirements. From 2024, the requirement for completion of the Graduate Medical School Admissions Test (GAMSAT) was waived for rural footprint applicants. This was based on limited evidence of its effectiveness in predicting medical school performance and its potential contribution to perpetuating underrepresentation in medicine amongst specific groups, due to the expense involved, time typically required for preparation, and complex English language requirements [28–30]. The 10-year recency of undergraduate study rule was simultaneously waived for footprint applicants, designed to increase access for local professionals who had been working in a rural community for an extended period.

Redesign of curriculum delivery

For the 2022 and 2023 RTS cohorts, the two pre-clinical years of the MD were required to be completed at Deakin’s Waurn Ponds campus (Geelong, MM1), following which RTS students were allocated via preferences to one of the three RCS for their two years of clinical training [23]. From 2024, receipt of additional commonwealth government funding enabled the commencement of the RTS end-to-end rural program (RTS E2E). Delivery of the MD pre-clinical years for RTS E2E students now occurs through blended online and face-to-face learning, allowing many students to remain living in their home rural communities.

A program logic model (PLM) has been developed for the RTS program to align research and evaluation plans with the place-based goals of the program (Supplementary file 1) [23]. A short-term objective of the RTS included in the PLM is to recruit rural footprint residents to the program, based on the assumption that there is a pool of suitably qualified applicants within the footprint who have a desire to undertake rural medical training through the RTS. This study aims to evaluate the impact of the MD RTS and RTS E2E programs on widening access for underrepresented populations and considers the implications for regional workforce development.

Methods

This cross-sectional study provides an analysis of six years of admission data (2020–2025) collected by the Graduate Entry Medical Schools Admissions System (GEMSAS) at application to Deakin’s MD course. These data do not include students entering through direct application to the school via the Indigenous Entry Stream or international student entry, which constitute up to 15% of the total cohort. De-identified applicant demographic data were provided to the research team for analysis and examined according to five distinct entry cohorts:

Descriptive statistics have been used to describe differences in demographic variables between the cohorts. Differences between three cohorts were of primary interest, defined as (A), (C) and (E) above. Statistical comparisons were performed for the following variables: gender (male, female), age categories (< 29, ≥ 29), rural background tier (footprint, other rural Victoria, other rural Australia), residence at course application (MM1, MM2, MM3-5) and undergraduate degree (science, humanities, health professional). Due to small cell counts, Fisher’s exact test was used for 2 × 2 comparisons and Fisher-Freeman-Halton test for 3 × 2 and 3 × 3 comparisons, with significance assigned to p < 0.05. The crosstabs function in SPSS 30 statistical software was used for all analyses.

Age

There were significantly more students aged 29 and over in the RTS E2E cohort (69%), compared with either the RTS cohort (20%, p < 0.001) or the pre-RTS cohort (13%, p < 0.001).

Rural background location

In 2020-21, only a small proportion of the MD cohort’s 29% (n = 74) RB students were from Deakin’s rural footprint (n = 12, 16%), with most RB students being from other parts of rural Victoria (n = 33, 45%) and rural Australia (n = 29, 39%) (Fig. 2). The RTS cohort included significantly more RB students from the rural footprint (n = 34, 52%; p < 0.001), which further increased to 100% in the RTS E2E cohort (n = 61, p < 0.001). This increase in admission of RB footprint students was accompanied by an increase in the number and proportion of RB students entering the MD overall in each paired year group, from 29% (5% footprint) in 2020-21 to 44% (15% footprint) in 2022-23 and 46% (28% footprint) in 2024-25.

Residential location of MD students at course entry

There were significant differences in the intrastate (Victorian) residential location of students at course entry between the pre-RTS and RTS (p < 0.001), with non-significant further improvements in the RTS E2E cohort. Specifically, a higher proportion of students entering the RTS (68%) and RTS E2E (74%) were current Victorian rural residents (MM2-5) at the time of course entry compared with the pre-RTS cohort (10%). A difference observed between the RTS E2E and the RTS was an increase in the number of students residing in smaller rural communities (MM3-5) compared with regional centres (MM2) (RTS: MM 3–5 n = 17, 26%; RTS E2E: MM 3–5 n = 27, 44%). Additionally, a higher proportion of RTS E2E students were also current residents in the rural footprint at the time of course admission (72%) compared to RTS students (38%) (Fig. 3).

Undergraduate degrees

There were significantly more students with undergraduate health professional degrees entering the RTS E2E (48%), compared with either the RTS (15%; p < 0.001) or pre-RTS cohort (18%; p = 0.002). Science-based undergraduate degrees dominated every other cohort (A to D, range 65–77%).

Discussion

The paucity of students entering the MD course with a RB from the local footprint prior to the commencement of the RTS, is striking (2020-21: n = 12, 5%). This demonstrates that prevailing approaches to increasing the admission of RB students to medical courses, through quotas and bonuses, are insufficient to facilitate increased entry of local rural applicants. The introduction of dedicated places for local applicants through the initial RTS iteration went some way towards addressing this, increasing the entry of applicants with RB in the rural footprint to 52% of the RTS and n = 37 in that time period. However, further significant adjustments to admission requirements, together with distributed rural program delivery, removing the requirement for relocation to Geelong, were the key to filling the RTS E2E stream with 100% local students, boosting the count to n = 77 when combined with the general cohort.

An assumption inherent in the establishment of the RTS E2E, explicit in the PLM, was that there were suitably qualified residents in the rural footprint who would take up the opportunity to undertake medical training close to their home [23]. It is noteworthy that in the RTS E2E cohort, most students (72%) were current residents in the rural footprint at the time of course commencement. As a graduate entry course, there was some concern that a number of RB applicants may have previously relocated from the rural footprint to complete their undergraduate studies, due to limited access to tertiary education opportunities in rural and remote communities [31]. The high uptake of the RTS E2E program by current residents, with a particular increase in MM3-5 residents (44%), indicates that the distributed model of rural MD delivery has effectively widened access for current footprint residents, particularly those living outside of regional centres.

The RTS was developed in close consultation with rural communities, with accessibility for rural residents, particularly those with existing professional and caring responsibilities, intentionally incorporated in the design. The uptake of the RTS E2E by a cohort including a higher proportion of mature-aged, female, health professionals, demonstrates the appetite for post-graduate medical training that exists amongst this demographic in the rural footprint and the significant uptake that can occur when institutional barriers to participation are removed [7]. The waiver of the 10-year recency of undergraduate study and GAMSAT requirements were undoubtedly enabling factors for this group, who are more likely than ‘traditional’ medical students to have had professional and personal responsibilities impacting on their capacity to meet these criteria [7, 10, 32].

Another key element in effectively widening access for this demographic was the course delivery redesign for the RTS E2E, with blended learning and strategically located rural learning campuses in MM3 and 4 locations enabling many students to remain living in their home communities whilst undertaking their pre-clinical studies. Anecdotally, this was a game-changer for this cohort, who would not have considered studying medicine to be a possibility under previous models where relocation was required. This finding aligns with experiences reported by other international health professional education programs, that establishing distributed learning sites is effective in attracting local ‘place bound’ rural residents [16].

The implications of course commencement by a cohort of predominantly female students, of whom 69% are at least 29 years of age, are worth considering. Mature-aged women likely have strong existing ties to their rural communities, with established families, jobs, community involvement and supports, and a desire to contribute to the economic and social development of their communities [32, 33]. Such strong place attachment is crucially important in rural workforce retention, suggesting that ‘growing our own’ in place through the RTS, will lead to these graduates remaining in place [34–36]. Furthermore, the literature describes the life experience, motivation, resilience and commitment that mature aged students bring to their studies that enable success [32, 37, 38]. There is evidence that students from lower socioeconomic circumstances, women, those first in family to attend university, and older students are more likely to pursue general practice as a career choice, one of the priority workforce needs in the rural footprint [39, 40]. There is a natural synergy between widening access strategies and social accountability, ensuring that not only are there sufficient health professionals available in rural areas, but also that these individuals have the depth of understanding of their communities required for the delivery of locally contextualised patient-centred care [6, 41].

Widening participation requires going beyond simply ‘opening the door’ to supporting long-term student success through tailored measures for diverse student cohorts [6, 37]. Mature age student cohorts have specific needs and challenges relating to employment, caring responsibilities and financial pressures, requiring flexible program delivery and academic and financial support [32, 33, 37, 42]. In the context of the RTS E2E, assistance with academic re-entry is an important consideration, with the removal of both the GAMSAT and 10-year recency of study requirement for rural footprint applicants meaning that commencing students may not have engaged in tertiary studies for a significant time. Previous research has demonstrated that although RB students may enter medical courses with lower academic scores or entry examination results, they demonstrate improvement in their academic standing and achieve similar results after admittance to medical programs [43–45]. Future evaluation of rural E2E students’ academic progress will be important to determine whether this trend continues.

The RTS E2E program design has disrupted the previous predominance of science undergraduate degrees (65–77%), with almost half (48%) of the E2E cohort possessing a health professional qualification. Deakin’s own research has demonstrated that medical students with health professional undergraduate degrees attain lower scores on the GAMSAT than students with science undergraduate degrees, yet demonstrate consistently superior performance across a range of areas, throughout the medical course [30]. Health-background students have been over-represented in the top 10% performing students, with a very low non-progression rate [30].

The impact on the rural workforce of attracting existing health professionals into graduate rural MD programs does, however, require careful monitoring. Rural communities require interprofessional healthcare teams to deliver primary care, and experience workforce shortages in nursing and allied health professions, as well as in medicine [46, 47]. It is critical to ensure that training strategies enhance overall capacity for rural healthcare delivery, rather than inadvertently weakening one element whilst strengthening another. Alongside the establishment of the RTS in 2022, a Pathways to Health Careers program was established in the rural footprint, under the banner of the School’s Damion Drapac Centre for Equity in Health Professions Education [48]. The Pathways program aims to increase visibility and foster aspirations for a wide range of health professional careers amongst secondary students in the region. This not only aims to redress local health professional shortages but provides clear and relevant entry pathways to the graduate MD RTS E2E.

Future research will evaluate the impact of the provision of four years of rural training-in-place on graduate retention and on medical workforce shortages in the region [23]. Deakin’s published graduate workforce outcomes (2023 data) for the rural footprint identify many of the key variables associated with graduates practicing in the rural footprint and provide a baseline for future evaluation inclusive of graduates of the RTS and RTS E2E [19]. Work is also underway to accurately describe the population’s demographic profile and the medical workforce status of Deakin’s bespoke rural footprint. This will enable assessment of the effectiveness of the comprehensive RTS E2E program design in recruiting a student cohort reflective of these communities and to evaluate graduate workforce outcomes with reference to the priority workforce needs of the region.

Limitations

The data available for this research was limited to applicants entering the MD course through GEMSAS - the general stream and RTS. As such, students entering through direct application to the school, through the Indigenous Entry Stream and international student entry stream, are not included in this demographic profile. This paper presents early outcomes based on small numbers, with ongoing research required to evaluate the longer-term impact of the RTS E2E program. It is possible that the first two years of RTS E2E data include a degree of ‘pioneering cohort’ effect, with the removal of previous admission requirements attracting an existing pool of eligible applicants in the footprint, who may not be reflective of future cohorts [49]. However, the Pathways to Health Careers program aims to increase aspiration for health professional careers and develop an ongoing pool of future local applicants. Conclusions drawn regarding the effect of admission and course delivery changes on attracting this distinctive student cohort have drawn on existing literature, inference and anecdotal information [16]. Future qualitative research is warranted to explore these assertions and add nuance to our understanding of the relative contributions of various aspects of program design to effectively widening access and participation.

Conclusions

The results of this study demonstrate the substantial impact that comprehensive place-based program design, inclusive of adaptations to admission requirements and course delivery, can have on determining who can access the program. The RTS E2E program has effectively widened access for a distinct cohort of students from the rural footprint, predominantly mature-aged, female, health professionals returning to study. With consideration of tailored strategies to support success, there is an opportunity to strengthen students’ existing connections to their rural communities during their four-year E2E MD. This will support the school’s mission to grow medical practitioners for the region who have the depth of understanding of their communities required for locally contextualised patient-centred care.
