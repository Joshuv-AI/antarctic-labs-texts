Published: 2025

Introduction

Ginkgo flavonol glycosides are crucial active constituents of Ginkgo biloba exhibiting significant pharmacological effects in improving blood circulation, antioxidant activity, and neuroprotection,with extensive applications across multiple fields including the food industry [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Currently, solvent extraction remains the predominant method for flavonol glycoside isolation, but this approach suffers from low efficiency, prolonged processing cycles, high energy consumption, and challenges in solvent recovery [5]. Emerging techniques such as enzyme-assisted Ultrasonic extraction [6], deep eutectic solventsand ultrasound-cold isostatic pressing synergy have been explored for flavonoid extraction [[6], [7], [8]]. Nevertheless, these methods retain inherent limitations such as high costs, stringent conditions, or complex parameter settings [9,10]. Consequently, developing a greener and more efficient novel extraction methodology is critically important for ginkgo flavonol glycoside utilization.

Supramolecular solvents (SUPRAS) constitute nanostructured liquids formed through self-assembly and coalescence of amphiphilic compounds in colloidal suspensions. Their efficacy stems from synergistic non-covalent molecular interactions enabling selective enrichment and targeted extraction of bioactive compounds, offering environmental benignity and safety advantages widely applicable in botanical extraction [11]. Leite demonstrated that an octanoic acid–ethanol-water SUPRAS system achieved efficient β-carotene and total phenolic extraction from Buriti fruits, yielding extracts with superior antioxidant activity surpassing BHT standards, Despite these advances, limitations persist including restricted amphiphile selection range, monolithic compositional structures, and challenging solvent recovery [[12], [13], [14]]. Natural deep eutectic solvents (NADES), derived from natural metabolites (organic acids, sugars, amino acids, choline derivatives), form room-temperature liquid systems via hydrogen bond donors (HBD)/acceptors (HBA) interactions. NADES characteristics encompass low toxicity, biodegradability, high solubility, and ecological-compatibility [15]. Cai et al. innovatively integrated NADES with SUPRAS properties, developing the NADES-SUPRAS/EqS solvent system using fatty acid-based NADES, ethanol, and water as anti-solvents for rutin, chlorogenic acid, and isoquercetin extraction [16]. This system outperformed conventional solvents (water, ethanol, 70 % ethanol, methanol) by factors of 7.604×, 2.352×, 1.141×, and 1.384 × respectively [17]. While demonstrating design flexibility and extraction efficiency, current research primarily focuses on physicochemical property impacts, lacking molecular-level investigation into formation mechanisms and target-specific extraction principles.

The NADES-SUPRAS/EqS systems hold potential for green extraction, their formation and extraction mechanisms remain poorly understood. This study developed fatty alcohol-based NADES combined with tetrahydrofuran and water as anti-solvents to construct a novel green NADES-SUPRAS/EqS system, and incorporated ultrasonic assistance for extracting ginkgo flavonol glycosides from Ginkgo biloba leaves, boosting mass transfer and speeding up solvent penetration into plant tissues. Single-factor experiments tuned extraction parameters to raise efficiency. To tackle the mechanism gaps, confocal laser scanning microscopy characterized the solvent supramolecular structures, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analyzed plant tissue micromorphological changes pre- and post-extraction, and density functional theory (DFT) calculations clarified NADES-SUPRAS/EqS formation. Solvent recyclability was also assessed.

Materials and methods

Materials

Ginkgo leaf powder was derived from Ginkgo biloba leaves collected in Sichuan Province. The material underwent air-drying, crushing, sieving (60 – 80 mesh), and was subsequently stored in lightproof sealed bottles. Reagents used: n-octanol, n-nonanol, n-decanol, n-caprylic acid, n-pelargonic acid, n-capric acid (all purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.), tetrahydrofuran (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.), water, n-heptanol, isoamyl alcohol (Tianjin Damao Chemical Reagent Factory), methanol (Comio Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.), acetic acid (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.), dichloromethane (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.), isorhamnetin, quercetin, kaempferol (Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.).

Preparation procedure of the solvent

NADES was dissolved in tetrahydrofuran to form micelles, followed by the addition of water. The solution was then mixed at room temperature for approximately 1 min using a vortex oscillator (Huxi Industrial Co., Ltd.). Finally, the phases were separated by centrifugation to obtain the NADES-SUPRAS/EqS solution. The upper layer, enriched with the organic phase, was identified as the SUPRAS phase, whereas the lower layer, containing primarily water, was designated as the EqS phase. The solution must be freshly prepared and used immediately [18].

Extraction procedure of the solvent

Precisely weigh a defined quantity of Ginkgo leaf powder and an intact two-phase NADES-SUPRAS/EqS solvent according to the predetermined solid-to-liquid ratio, The mixture was vortexed on a vortex shaker for 1 min to ensure uniform mixing. Place the mixture in an ultrasonic extraction apparatus (GuTe Ultrasonic Equipment Co., Ltd.) and subject it to ultrasonic extraction at 160 W for 15 min. Upon completion of extraction, centrifuge the mixture at 10,000 rpm for 10 min using a centrifuge (Pingfan Instrument Co., Ltd.), The clear solution was separated to obtain the extract solution.

Determination of components

The upper SUPRAS phase and lower EqS phase were collected separately, and acid hydrolysis was performed following the procedure outlined in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia. Hydrolysis conditions: incubation in a water bath at 70 ℃ for 1.5 h. After hydrolysis completion, methanol was added to adjust the final volume to 5 mL, yielding the test solution [19]. The content of flavonol glycosides in the test solution was determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) under the following conditions: Chromatographic column: maintained at 36 ℃, Mobile phase: 0.4 % phosphoric acid aqueous solution-methanol (50:50, v/v), Flow rate: 1.0 mL/min, Injection volume: 10 μL, Detection wavelength: 360 nm. Single-factor experiment method

In this study, the single-factor experiment method was employed to investigate the effects of ultrasonic power, extraction temperature, solid–liquid ratio, and ultrasonic time on the extraction efficiency of Ginkgo biloba flavonoids. Specifically, the ultrasonic power was tested within the range of 120 – 360 W (set at 5 levels), the extraction temperature within 25 – 60 ℃ (set at 5 levels), the solid–liquid ratio was tested within 10 – 80 mg/mL (set at 5 levels), and the ultrasonic time was tested within 1 – 20 min. A stepwise optimization strategy was adopted for the experiment: firstly, the optimal ultrasonic power was determined via single-factor experiments. under this optimal power, the experiment was further conducted to screen for the optimal extraction temperature. Subsequently, the effect of solid–liquid ratio was examined under the combined conditions of the already determined optimal ultrasonic power and extraction temperature, so as to determine its optimal value. Finally, the optimization of ultrasonic time was completed under the optimal conditions of the aforementioned three factors, and its optimal parameter was confirmed.

Solvent recovery

The extracted solution was divided into two distinct phases: the SUPRAS phase and the EqS phase, which were subjected to phase separation.Given the boiling point of tetrahydrofuran (THF) at 61 ℃, the EqS phase underwent selective distillation to recover THF. Flavonoids within the EqS phase were ultimately dispersed in water for recovery. When water was added to the SUPRAS phase, THF dissolved into the aqueous medium, leaving flavonol glycosides predominantly in the water-insoluble phase. Following liquid–liquid separation, the introduction of an anti-solvent to the water-insoluble phase facilitated precipitation of the flavonoid compounds.

Characterization experiments

SEM experiment

Collect samples including untreated Ginkgo powder, Ginkgo powder extracted with 60 % ethanol, Ginkgo powder extracted with NADES-SUPRAS/EqS solvent, and Ginkgo powder subjected to ultrasound-assisted NADES-SUPRAS/EqS solvent extraction. After filtration, each sample underwent sequential treatments: natural air-drying, washing with ultrapure water, and oven-drying at 60 ℃ for 5 h. The processed samples were sputter-coated with a conductive metal layer and was subsequently examined under a scanning electron microscope (SEM) to characterize their surface morphology [24].

Optical microscopy experiment

500 μL of NADES-SUPRAS/EqS solvent was pipetted onto a clean glass slide, observed under an inverted microscope, and images were recorded.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy experiment

A 500 μL of the NADES-SUPRAS/EqS solvent was aspirated, followed by the addition of 5 μL lipophilic Nile Red staining solution (0.02 wt%, dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide). The mixture was then incubated in the dark at 25 °C for 30 min to ensure complete staining. Subsequently, 15 μL of the stained sample was pipetted onto a pre-cleaned glass slide, gently covered with a No. 1.5 thickness coverslip, and examined using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica SP8, Germany) with Nile Red fluorescence detected at excitation/emission wavelengths of 488/535 nm.

Statistical analysis

Solvent Screening and Single-Factor Experiments were conducted in triplicate following a completely randomized experimental design. Statistical analyses of the obtained data were performed using SPSS Statistics 24.0 software. The Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to assess data normality. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to identify any significant differences among groups (p < 0.05).

Results and Discussion

Screening of solvents

The screening results of NADES-SUPRAS/EqS solvents are illustrated in Fig. 1. As shown in panel Fig. 1A, the solvent prepared using n-Heptanol as the fundamental component exhibited superior performance. Further refinement of the volumetric ratio between NADES and THF (Fig. 1B) identified an optimal proportion of 1:5 (v/v) for enhanced extraction efficiency. Subsequently, the composition ratio of the NADES solvent was optimized (Fig. 1C), revealing that a formulation with n-Heptanol: acetic acid: isoamyl alcohol at 2:1:1 (v/v/v) delivered the highest extraction efficiency. Additionally, investigation into the influence of water content within the NADES-SUPRAS/EqS solvent on extraction efficacy, Fig. 1D confirmed that 50 % water content yielded the most favorable outcomes. Collectively, these findings established the optimal compositional parameters for the NADES-SUPRAS/EqS solvent system. The final solvent composition was determined to be 8.3 vol% NADES/41.7 vol% THF/50 vol% H2O.

Single-Factor experimental optimization

The effect of ultrasonic power on the extraction efficiency of ginkgo flavonol glycosides, is depicted in Fig. 2A. As shown, the extraction yield increased with rising power from 120 W to 160 W, whereas a significant decline was observed beyond 160 W [25]. This phenomenon can be attributed to the following mechanisms: insufficient power failed to disrupt plant cell walls adequately, impeding intracellular flavonoid release, elevated power enhanced cell wall disruption, facilitating flavonoid dissolution. however, excessively high power induced degradation of already dissolved flavonoids, thereby reducing the overall extraction yield [26].

Investigation of the effects of ultrasonic power (A), solid-to-liquid ratio (B), extraction temperature (C), and extraction time (D) on extraction yield.

Fig. 2B illustrates the impact of solid-to-liquid ratio on the extraction efficiency of ginkgo flavonol glycosides. Within the tested range of 10–––80 mg/mL, the extraction yield reached its maximum at 40 mg/mL, corresponding to a dynamic equilibrium of mass transfer effects. Below this ratio, excess solvent diluted the raw material concentration, diminishing effective interfacial contact between the solvent and substrate while reducing the driving force for mass transfer. Conversely, ratios exceeding 40 mg/mL led to material compaction, compressed contact interfaces, and increased mass transfer resistance. The optimal solid-to-liquid ratio of 40 mg/mL achieved balanced solvent utilization efficiency and mass transfer dynamics, thereby maximizing the extraction yield [27].

The effect of extraction temperature on the efficiency of ginkgo flavonol glycoside extraction, as shown in Fig. 2C, reveals that within the tested range of 25–––60 ℃, the extraction yield peaked at 30 ℃. This optimal temperature achieved a balanced trade-off between extraction efficiency and energy consumption, as higher temperatures compromised flavonoid solubility. Conversely, temperatures exceeding 30 ℃ triggered thermal degradation of the thermolabile ginkgo flavonol glycosides. Notably, the NADES-SUPRAS/EqS solvent system maintained maximal extraction yield at 30 ℃ while energy conservation: below 30 ℃, reduced molecular thermal motion weakened interactions between Ginkgo powdignificantly reducing energy consumption, highlighting its operational superiority for sustainable extraction processes [28].

The impact of extraction duration on the efficiency of ginkgo flavonol glycoside extraction efficiency, as illustrated in Fig. 2D. With the increase of extraction time, the extraction yield of ginkgo flavonol glycoside increased significantly. When the extraction time was 15 min, the maximum extraction yield of ginkgo flavonol glycoside was 8.025 mg/g. However, when the extraction time was further extended, the yield of ginkgo flavonol glycoside decreased instead. The reason for this phenomenon was that ultrasound not only enhanced the dissolution of flavonoids but also led to their degradation. [29].

In summary, under the same ultrasonic conditions, when a 60 % ethanol solvent was used for extraction, the final yield was only 6.148 mg/g, which was significantly lower than that of the NADES − SUPRAS/EqS system. Compared with traditional extraction methods without ultrasonic assistance, in the traditional extraction process, this process took 12 h to achieve an extraction efficiency comparable to what was obtained in just 15 min under ultrasonic assistance with a 60 % ethanol system. The underlying mechanism was essentially attributed to ultrasound − enhanced mass transfer coupled with the high permeability of the NADES solvent, which synergistically accelerated the dissolution of target compounds [30]. The system attained maximal extraction efficiency at 15 min, representing a 30.5 % yield improvement, and an exponential reduction in time cost was observed. Study on extraction mechanism

SEM analysis of raw materials

The microstructural evolution of Ginkgo leaf powder was characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Fig. 3). Comparative analysis revealed: untreated samples exhibited smooth surfaces without notable pores or fissures (Fig. 3A). Post-ethanol extraction, surface roughness marginally increased yet porous structures remained indistinct (Fig. 3B). NADES-SUPRAS/EqS solvent extraction induced significant surface roughening and enhanced porosity (Fig. 3C), confirming its cell wall disruption capability [31]. ultrasound-assisted NADES-SUPRAS/EqS extraction further intensified cell wall disintegration, exposing the fibrous network architecture (Fig. 3D). These microstructural alterations demonstrate that NADES-SUPRAS/EqS solvents possess superior cell wall disruption potential compared to conventional solvents, likely constituting the structural basis for their extraction efficiency advantage. Furthermore, ultrasonic-assisted extraction not only enhances mass transfer dynamics but also exerts synergistic effects with NADES-SUPRAS/EqS solvents during cell wall deconstruction [32].

Morphological analysis of the solvent

As shown in Fig. 4, the observation of dense vesicular structures under an optical microscope confirmed micelle formation (Fig. 4A) [33]. Following staining with the lipophilic Nile Red dye, confocal microscopic imaging revealed intense red fluorescence within the vesicle lumen, while no fluorescence signal was detected externally, validating the normal micellar architecture of the SUPRAS phase characterized by a “hydrophilic shell-hydrophobic core” structure (Fig. 4B, C). This structural feature aligns with previously reported enrichment of ginkgo flavonol glycosides (lipophilic components) in the SUPRAS phase. The internal hydrophobic normal micellar configuration observed in this solvent system provides critical evidence for its efficacy in efficiently extracting Ginkgo Flavonoids.

FTIR analysis of the solvent

To investigate the formation mechanism of the NADES-SUPRAS/EqS solvent system, this study measured Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra of isoamyl alcohol, THF, n-heptanol, and the NADES-SUPRAS/EqS solvent within the wavenumber range of 400–––4000 cm−1 (Fig. 5) [26]. Key characteristic vibrational bands were identified as follows: O-H stretching at 3459 cm−1, C-H stretching at 2965 cm−1, C-O stretching at 1088 cm−1, O-H bending at 1593 cm−1, C-H bending at 1362 cm−1, and (CH2)n framework vibrations at 770 cm−1·THF exhibited distinct absorption peaks at 3420 cm−1 (O-H), 2965 cm−1 (C-H), and 1088 cm−1 (C-O). While the NADES-SUPRAS/EqS solvent displayed analogous characteristic peaks, its O-H stretching band showed significant broadening and intensification. This spectral evolution indicated enhanced hydrogen bonding networks among components, driving close packing of fatty alcohol molecules to facilitate normal micelle assembly. Concurrently, attenuated intensities in C-H stretching, C-O stretching, and (CH2)n vibrations corroborated confinement of long-chain fatty alcohol hydroxyl groups within the hydrophilic core [34].

Analysis of computational chemistry results

Analysis of ESP computational results

Electrostatic potential [35] (ESP) calculations were employed to predict intramolecular reactive sites by mapping surface charge distributions: blue and red regions depicted positive/negative electrostatic potentials, respectively, with color intensity corresponding to potential strength. This property leveraged strong electrostatic complementarity between oppositely charged regions to identify potential binding sites [35,36]. The NADES-SUPRAS/EqS solvent system (Fig. 6E), composed of acetic acid, n-heptanol, and isoamyl alcohol, adopted an energetically favorable configuration stabilized by a tripartite hydrogen-bonding network: hydroxyl H of n-heptanol → carboxyl O (acetic acid), carboxyl H of acetic acid → hydroxyl O (isoamyl alcohol), and hydroxyl H of isoamyl alcohol → hydroxyl O (n-heptanol). ESP analysis revealed that electron-rich oxygen atoms (red, negative ESP) in n-heptanol within the optimized conformation exhibited charge complementarity with electron-deficient hydrogen atoms (blue, positive ESP) in rutin (Fig. 6D), indicating non-covalent binding propensity. Geometric optimization confirmed that the complex (Fig. 6I) possessed the lowest binding energy, validating its stability advantage. Comparative evaluation between the experimental data of supramolecular systems composed of monomeric amphiphilic substances (with hydroxyl/carboxyl groups) and that of NADES-SUPRAS/EqS solvent systems (Fig. 6F − H) elucidated the efficient extraction mechanism: although both systems contained hydrogen bonds meeting geometric criteria (bond length: 1.0–3.5 Å, bond angle: 90° − 180°) formed between –OH/N–H donors and electronegative N/O/Cl acceptors [37], the NADES-SUPRAS/EqS solvent formed four hydrogen bonds (compared to one per individual component) with shortened bond lengths confirming enhanced stability. The extended hydrogen-bonding network further increased binding site density.

AIM analysis

Topological analysis via Atoms in Molecules (AIM) enabled quantitative characterization of non-covalent interactions by translating microscopic bonding features into macroscopically observable parameters [38]. This methodology elucidated the binding characteristics between the NADES-SUPRAS/EqS solvent and rutin through bond critical point (BCP) analysis, evaluating electron density (ρ), kinetic energy density (G), potential energy density (V), Laplacian of electron density (∇2ρ), and total energy density (H). Interaction types were determined based on ∇2ρ and H(BCP): elevated ρ values signify strong interactions, with ∇2ρ > 0 indicative of non-covalent bonds (e.g., hydrogen bonds, van der Waals forces) and ∇2ρ < 0 denoting covalent character. Data from Table 3 demonstrated that ∇2ρ > 0 across all systems, confirming exclusively non-covalent interactions between the four solvents and rutin, predominantly governed by hydrogen bonding and van der Waals forces.

Solvent regeneration performance

Following centrifugation of the extracted solution, liquid–liquid phase separation was performed to obtain the NADES-SUPRAS phase and the EqS phase (Fig. 10). The EqS phase, primarily composed of tetrahydrofuran (THF) and water, enabled low-energy recovery of THF through distillation, while retaining target extracts in the aqueous phase. Upon adding an equal volume of water to the NADES-SUPRAS phase followed by centrifugation, THF dissolved into a newly formed EqS phase. The residual NADES-SUPRAS phase, termed the water-insoluble phase, transferred 24.6 % of flavonoids to this new EqS phase, which underwent synchronous distillation with the original EqS phase. Subsequently, an equal volume of dichloromethane was added to the water-insoluble phase. After centrifugation, acid hydrolysis of the supernatant revealed only 8.74 % residual flavonoids. As illustrated in (Fig. 11), this process concentrated 69.9 % of flavonoids in the aqueous phase, precipitated and recovered 21.4 % of flavonoids, achieving a total recovery rate of 91.3 %. As shown in Fig. 12, it is noteworthy that after four cycles, the solvent extraction efficiency rate remained at 91.68 % of its peak value.

Conclusion

This study developed the NADES-SUPRAS/EqS solvent system for efficient and green extraction of Ginkgo Flavonol Glycosides, demonstrating markedly enhanced extraction efficiency compared to Supramolecular solvents. Optimal process parameters identified via single-factor experiments included a solvent composition of 8.3 vol% NADES/41.7 vol% THF/50 vol% H2O, ultrasonic power of 160 W, sonication time of 15 min, solid-to-liquid ratio of 40 mg/mL, and temperature of 30 ℃, yielding an extraction efficiency of 8.025 mg/g for Ginkgo Flavonol Glycosides. SEM showed the solvent effectively broke down Ginkgo leaf cell walls. optical/confocal microscopy confirmed its normal micellar vesicular structure with hydrophobic interior, hydrophilic exterior. DFT studies found significantly stronger hydrogen bonding and van der Waals interactions with rutin than single-component systems, indicating strong affinity for flavonoids as the key to better extraction efficiency. For recovery, the EqS phase distillation enriched flavonol glycosides in the aqueous phase, followed by dichloromethane precipitation from the upper phase, achieving a total recovery rate of 91.3 %. Recovered THF was reusable, confirming the system sustainability and simplicity, with greater industrial potential than conventional methods. This study offers a reference framework of ultrasonic-assisted extraction for plant-derived polyphenolic bioactive compounds.
