Achieving over 90% Faradaic Efficiency in Cyclohexanone Oxime Electrosynthesis Using the Cu–Mo Dual-Site Catalyst
Authors: Runyao Zhao, Yiding Wang, Jiaju Fu, Fengtao Zhang, Linzi Wen, Yanfei Zhao, Bo Guan, Buxing Han, Zhimin Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoupling with the nitrate electroreduction reaction (NitRR), the electrosynthesis of cyclohexanone oxime (CHO, the vital feedstock in the nylon-6 industry) from cyclohexanone provides a promising alternative to the traditional energy consumption process. However, it still suffers from low efficiency because selective production of *NH2OH intermediate from NitRR under large current densities is challenging. We here report a Cu1MoOx/nitrogen-doped carbon (NC) electrocatalyst with high-density Cu–Mo dual sites for NitRR to selectively produce and stabilize *NH2OH, with the subsequent cyclohexanone oximation achieving the highest CHO Faradaic efficiency of 94.5% and a yield rate of 3.0 mol g–1 h–1 at an industrially relevant current density of 0.5 A cm–2. Furthermore, in situ characterizations evidenced that the Cu–Mo dual sites in Cu1MoOx/NC effectively inhibited hydrodeoxygenation of hydroxyl-containing intermediates of NitRR, selectively producing *NH2OH and thus achieving cyclohexanone oximation with high efficiency. This work provides a high-performance catalyst for CHO electrosynthesis from nitrogenous waste, showing promising application potential in industrial production of CHO.

Introduction

It is projected that the global annual production of polyamide nylon-6 will reach 8.9 million tons by 2024, indicating a further increase in the demand for cyclohexanone oxime (CHO), the principal precursor for caprolactam production in the nylon industry.1,2 Traditional industrial CHO production mainly relies on the oximation of cyclohexanone with hydroxylamines (e.g., hydroxylamine sulfate or in situ generated hydroxylamine from ammonia oxidation with H2O2),3,4 suffering from high energy consumption, high industrial pollution, and high cost of noble metals.4 The renewable energy-driven electrosynthesis of CHO has been achieved by cyclohexanone oximation with in situ generated hydroxylamine intermediates (*NH2OH) from the electroreduction of nitrate/NOx wastes, providing a promising and alternative approach for CHO production with significance for the sustainable development of the nylon industry.5−10 However, the competitive formation of ammonia from nitrate electroreduction significantly impedes the selectivity of CHO, so viable strategies to promote nitrate-to-*NH2OH for the CHO electrosynthesis is urgently demanded.11,12

In the past few years, the catalytic nitrate electroreduction reaction (NitRR) has been intensely investigated.6,7 As illustrated in Figure 1a, nitrate can be first reduced into *NOH intermediate with several proton-coupled electron-transfer and hydrodeoxygenation steps over transition metal-based electrocatalysts (e.g., Fe and Cu).13−18 To achieve the *NH2OH species for CHO electrosynthesis, further hydrodeoxygenation steps to ammonia should be prohibited. However, such active intermediates, including *NOH, *NHOH, and *NH2OH, are more favorably converted into ammonia subsequently rather than reacting with cyclohexanone in the reported process, thus leading to the poor selectivity toward CHO.19−22 Therefore, the electrocatalysts are highly required to be capable of catalyzing NitRR to selectively generate *NH2OH and suppress its further hydrodeoxygenation to NH3.23 To date, several catalysts have been reported for CHO electrosynthesis via the NitRR.8,9,12 For example, the Fe catalyst prepared by electrical explosion achieved a CHO yield rate of 55.9 g h–1 gcat–1 and a Faradaic efficiency (FE) of 20%;15 the Zn–Cu alloy catalyst11 afforded 27% FECHO at 100 mA cm–2. Although such progress has been made, the FE of CHO still remains very low, leading to insufficient energy efficiency and hindering industrial-level applications of the CHO electrosynthesis.

Herein, we report a nitrogen-doped carbon (NC)-supported Cu–Mo dual-site catalyst (Cu1MoOx/NC) for precisely regulating the NitRR pathway to selectively generate *NH2OH, which can achieve the subsequent cyclohexanone oximation to CHO. The as-described Cu1MoOx/NC catalyst possessed abundant atomically dispersed Cu with a loading of 1.15 wt % to form high-density Cu–Mo dual sites on the NC support, which displayed an excellent performance for CHO production with a FE of 94.5% and a high yield rate of 3.0 mol g–1 h–1 at an industrially relevant current density of 0.5 A cm–2. Furthermore, in situ Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy and differential electrochemical mass spectrometry (DEMS) revealed that the Cu–Mo dual sites in Cu1MoOx/NC synergistically inhibited the hydrodeoxygenation of hydroxyl-containing intermediates of NitRR and promoted the generation of *NH2OH, thus achieving electrosynthesis of CHO with high efficiency. This work provides a high-performance electrocatalyst for CHO electroproduction via NitRR and subsequent cyclohexanone oximation, which affords an alternative route for the synthesis of CHO and may have promising application potential.

Results and Discussion

Density functional theory (DFT) calculation was first conducted to fast-screen appropriate structures of electrocatalysts for NitRR. Considering that carbon-supported MoOx clusters showed high activity for NitRR and C–N coupling,10 different Mo–M (M = Fe, Co, Ni, and Cu) dual-site structures were constructed, and the Gibbs free energy changes of hydrodeoxygenation (ΔGHDO) for *NOH, *NHOH, and *NH2OH over these as-designed Mo–M sites were calculated (Figure S1). As illustrated in Figure 1b, the ΔGHDO of *NOH over Mo–Mo sites (i.e., MoOx/C) is the largest negative value, suggesting that hydrodeoxygenation of *NOH is preferred rather than hydrogenation to *NHOH and *NH2OH. Conversely, when doped with a second element, the ΔGHDO of *NOH is obviously increased, following the order Mo–Cu > Mo–Ni > Mo–Co > Mo–Fe. This suggests that the as-constructed Mo–M dual metal sites are unfavorable to hydrodeoxygenation of *NOH compared to Mo–Mo site. In particular, the Cu–Mo site exhibits the highest ΔGHDO values for all three hydroxyl-containing intermediates, indicating that this catalyst has the possibility to afford and stabilize *NH2OH from NitRR. In addition, the more negative Bader charge of the Mo atom at the Cu–Mo site than at the Mo–Mo site (Figure 1c) suggests that the Cu d electrons are transferred to Mo, resulting in a decreased valence state of Mo, thus impacting the hydrodeoxygenation of NitRR intermediates (e.g., *NOH).

Based on the above DFT calculation results, the NC-supported Cu–Mo dual-site catalyst (Cu1MoOx/NC) was constructed, along with atomically dispersed Cu and MoOx clusters. For comparison, the NC-supported MoOx clusters (MoOx/NC) and atomically dispersed Cu (Cu1/NC) were also synthesized (see details in the Supporting Information). As shown in Figure 2a,b, the aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (AC-HAADF-STEM) images show that the as-synthesized Cu1MoOx/NC catalyst exhibits a nanoflower-shaped morphology with a diameter of approximately 500 nm. The MoOx clusters with size <2 nm and atomically dispersed Cu were uniformly dispersed on the NC support. Extracted line intensity profiles along with the selected areas show the isolated arrangement of Cu and Mo atoms with a distance of 0.9 nm (Figure 2c), evidencing the presence of abundant Cu–Mo dual sites. Further, the Cu and Mo elemental distribution of Cu1MoOx/NC is also confirmed by electron energy loss spectroscopy-mapping (EELS-mapping, Figure S2). Further, the corresponding energy-dispersive spectroscopy (HRTEM-EDS, Figure 2d) confirms the uniform dispersion of Cu and Mo atoms in Cu1MoOx/NC. The findings presented above indicate the successful construction of the Cu–Mo dual-site catalyst.

For the MoOx/NC catalyst, amorphous MoOx clusters with an average size of 1.1 nm (Figures 2e and S3) were observed, providing the Mo–Mo sites. Inductively coupled plasma–mass spectrometry analysis showed that the loading of Mo in MoOx/NC was 13.6 wt % and those of Cu and Mo in Cu1MoOx/NC were 1.15 and 11.5 wt %, respectively, implying abundant Cu–Mo dual sites in Cu1MoOx/NC (Supporting Information Table S3).

X-ray diffraction (XRD) and selected area electron diffraction analysis indicate that no crystalline Cu or Mo species are present in these two samples (Figures 2f and S4), excluding the formation of large particles. The detailed chemical and electronic states of the as-prepared catalysts were systematically explored with spectral analyses. High-resolution X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyses were first conducted to observe the Mo and Cu chemical states in the as-described catalysts (Figures 3a,b). The 0.5 eV blueshift of Mo 3d binding energy (BE) for Cu1MoOx/NC compared to that of MoOx/NC indicates an overall decrease in the Mo-oxidation state, suggesting the strong interaction between Cu and Mo atoms with the electron transfer from the Cu to Mo site. Correspondingly, the presence of CuII species with a BE of 934.1 eV is observed from the Cu 2p3/2 spectrum of Cu1MoOx/NC compared with that of Cu1/NC (Figure 3b). By deconvoluting the Cu 2p XPS signal, the proportion of CuII species was estimated to be ∼15%, which may result from coordination with the O element of MoOx.

The Raman spectrum of Cu1MoOx/NC shows a decreased Mo–O signal compared to that of MoOx/NC (Figure 3c), which might be attributed to the formation of Cu–Mo sites that destroy the MoOx structure. Further, synchrotron X-ray near-edge structure (XANES) and EXAFS analyses provide the detailed structural information on Cu and Mo sites in Cu1MoOx/NC. As shown in Figure 3d,e, the Mo sites exhibit a lower oxidation state than standard MoVI with one path at 1.76 Å (a coordination number of 5.5) obtained from the fitting of Fourier transform k3-weighted R space (Figure S5 and Supporting Information Table S1), while MoOx/NC exhibits a slightly higher Mo-oxidation state than Cu1MoOx/NC with two O-coordinated paths at 1.52 and 1.86 Å (coordination numbers of 3.6 and 1.5), respectively. This suggests that the presence of Cu sites significantly impacts the coordination environment of Mo. In addition, the absence of long-distance Mo–Mo paths in the wavelet transforms of the Mo K-edge EXAFS spectra of MoOx/NC and Cu1MoOx/NC (Figure 3f) demonstrates the ultrasmall size of the MoOx clusters. For the Cu sites in Cu1MoOx/NC, the Cu K-edge XANES and EXAFS analyses reveal that the Cu atoms exist in a valence state between +1∼+2 (Figure 3g) and are primarily distributed as individual atoms (Figure 3h,i). The fitted first shells of 1.94 and 1.85 Å with the coordination numbers of 3.0 and 0.80 correspond to the O and N coordination structures, respectively, compared with the fitted Cu–N3 structure in Cu1/NC (Figures 3i and S6 and Supporting Information Table S3), which indicates the presence of Cu–Mo sites. The above characterization data demonstrate that the as-designed Cu–Mo dual-site catalyst was successfully obtained.

The electrocatalytic performances of Cu1MoOx/NC and MoOx/NC for CHO electrosynthesis were examined under ambient conditions using an aqueous solution of KOH (0.5 M) and KNO3 (0.5 M) as the catholyte-containing cyclohexanone (0.1 M) and using a flow cell equipped with a three-electrode system (see details in the Supporting Information).

As shown in Figure 4a,b, the increased current density was observed on the LSV curves performed over Cu1MoOx/NC than MoOx/NC, indicating a preferred NitRR activity of the Cu1MoOx/NC catalyst. The products were analyzed according to the reported methods: ammonia was determined using the Nessler reagent method and proton nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H NMR) analysis (Figure S7), while CHO was determined and quantified by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS) and 1H NMR, respectively (Figures 4c and S8).10 In addition, validation using isotopically labeled K15NO3 instead of K14NO3 confirmed that nitrate was the nitrogen source for CHO formation (Figure S9). The current-dependent product distributions and corresponding CHO yield rates are illustrated in Figure 4d,e. As expected, Cu1MoOx/NC displayed high performance for the electrosynthesis of CHO via NitRR, achieving FECHO > 90% in a wide current density range of 0.2–0.5 A cm–2 (Figures 4d and S10) with the highest FECHO of 94.5% and a production rate of 3.0 mol g –1h–1 at 0.5 A cm–2, remarkably suppressing NH4+ generation. In contrast, MoOx/NC afforded NH4+ as the main product in the tested current density range, identical to the reported results.15,24 These findings indicate that the unique structure of Cu1MoOx/NC with Cu–Mo dual sites is responsible for the NitRR-to-*NH2OH process with high selectivity, thus promoting the electrosynthesis of CHO. We tested the performances of Cu1MoOx/NC in the cyclohexanone concentration range of 0.05–0.2 M, and the results are shown in Figure S11. In the tested cyclohexanone concentration ranges, FECHO kept high values, even at a concentration of 0.05 M, FECHO was still as high as 85.2%. Furthermore, the electrochemically active areas (ECSAs) of Cu1MoOx/NC and MoOx/NC were determined to be 824.2 and 706.9 cm2, respectively (Figure S13). The comparable data of these two catalysts indicate that ECSA is not the main reason for the changes in their catalytic performances. The time-dependent single-pass conversion efficiency (SPCE) of cyclohexanone and full-cell voltages without iR correction are given in Figure 4f. The SPCE of cyclohexanone reached 47.2% at a full-cell voltage of 3.5 V and a current density of 0.5 A cm–2, indicating a high energy efficiency of CHO electrosynthesis over Cu1MoOx/NC. The durability of this catalyst was evaluated at an industrially relevant current density of 0.2 A cm–2,25 and the system kept an approximately 3.0 V with FECHO exceeding 90.0% after 60 h continuous test (Figure 4g). No obvious changes were observed for the used Cu1MoOx/NC by EXAFS, XRD, AC-HADDF-STEM, and ICP analysis (Figures S14–S16 and Table S4), demonstrating the excellent durability of the resulting Cu1MoOx/NC catalyst. Figure 4h shows the overall effect of Cu1MoOx/NC in the electrosynthesis of CHO, which is superior to the recently reported24 (Supporting Information Table S4) in FE and yield.

To explore the CHO formation pathways over the Cu1MoOx/NC catalyst, in situ DEMS and FTIR analyses were performed to identify the intermediates of CHO electrosynthesis. In the in situ DEMS as shown in Figures 5a and S17a, the signals with mass-to-charge ratios of 30, 17 and 33 are observed at 0.2 and 0.4 A cm–2 for Cu1MoOx/NC, corresponding to *NO, *NH3, and *NH2OH species, respectively.

Study on the mechanism of CHO electrosynthesis. (a,b) In situ DEMS analysis on the electrosynthesis of CHO over Cu1MoOx/NC and MoOx/NC at −0.4 A cm–2, respectively. (c,d) In situ FTIR spectra of the reaction performed over Cu1MoOx/NC with potential adjusted between +0.3 and −0.5 V in electrolyte-containing 0.5 M KOH and 0.5 M KNO3 and in electrolyte-containing 0.5 M KOH, 0.5 M KNO3, and 0.1 M acetone, respectively. The background spectrum was collected at open-circuit potential, and the others were acquired at 80 mV intervals. (e) Schematic illustration of NitRR and oximation reaction over Cu1MoOx/NC and MoOx/NC.

In addition, the intensity of the signals for *NH2OH increases with applied potentials and remains unchanged with time, confirming the formation and accumulation of *NH2OH species during NitRR. In contrast, the DEMS spectra collected for MoOx/NC (Figures 5b and S17b) exhibit intense signals ascribing to *NO and *NH3 species, with a fragile signal to *NH2OH, suggesting the fast hydrodeoxygenation of hydroxyl-containing species over MoOx/NC. Similarly, the multireflection in situ FTIR spectra (Figure S18) also support the presence of the *NH2OH intermediate over Cu1MoOx/NC (Figures 5c,d and S19).

The enhanced and diminished bands are labeled with up and down arrows, respectively. The peaks around 965, 1125, and 1612 cm–1 could be attributed to *NH2, *NH2OH, and δHOH,26,27 respectively, while those at 1340 and 1390 cm–1 could be attributed to *NO3–, and the peaks at 1036 and 1203 cm–1 belong to *NO2–.28 As the applied potentials decrease from +0.3 to −0.5 V, reverse peaks of *NO3– and δHOH appear in both cases using Cu1MoOx/NC and MoOx/NC catalysts (Figures 5c and S19a), indicating rapid consumption of NO3– and H2O. Notably, a distinct peak of *NH2OH appears with the overpotential increase in the case using Cu1MoOx/NC, while hardly being observed for MoOx/NC, suggesting that the Cu–Mo dual sites of Cu1MoOx/NC should be the key to generating the active *NH2OH intermediate, agreeing well with the in situ DEMS results.

Correspondingly, using acetone as a probe, in situ FTIR analysis on the electrosynthesis of oxime via NitRR was carried out. In the FTIR spectra, the intensity of the bands ascribed to *NH2OH increase with the decreased potentials and then kept hardly changed at low potentials (Figures 5d and S19b). This suggests that the in situ generated *NH2OH species could be rapidly consumed by acetone at the electrode surface, balancing its surface coverage on the electrode. The band at 1740 cm–1 is attributed to carbonyl C=O of acetone, adsorbed by the electrode. The appearance of the peak around 1534 cm–1 ascribed to the characteristic C=N bond signifies the occurrence of the oximation reaction of acetone with *NH2OH. In contrast, in the FTIR spectra collected for MoOx/NC, the signal assigned to the C=N bond is very weak (Figure S19b), indicating the hindered oximation process. The in situ FTIR experiments provide solid evidence for the enrichment of *NH2OH on the surface of Cu1MoOx/NC, further supporting the inhibition of the hydrodeoxygenation of this intermediate. Therefore, two possible reaction pathways of NitRR over Cu1MoOx/NC and MoOx/NC are proposed with different intermediates as illustrated in Figure 5e.

In line with the DFT computational results, the cooperation of atomically dispersed Cu and Mo sites in Cu1MoOx/NC effectively increases the energy barrier for the hydrodeoxygenation of hydroxyl-containing intermediates, thus directing the NitRR pathway toward *NH2OH.

However, over MoOx/NC, the hydrodeoxygenation of NitRR intermediates preferentially takes place, resulting in NitRR-to-NH3, thus inhibiting the production of CHO. More importantly, the Cu1MoOx/NC catalyst is also effective for the electrochemical oximation of other ketones/aldehydes, affording a series of oximes in FEs >80% (Figure S20), further confirming that the key step of the electrochemical oximation process should be the NitRR-to-*NH2OH process, which thus expends the product diversity of the NitRR-coupled electrosynthesis. Furthermore, we calculated the energy barriers for the generation of the key intermediate *NH2OH on the Mo–Mo and Cu–Mo sites. As shown in Figure S21a, the energy barrier for the generation of *NH2OH at the Cu–Mo site is 0.34 eV lower than that at the Mo–Mo site, thus accelerating the generation of *NH2OH. We also calculated the free Gibbs energy changes in the adsorption/desorption process of *NH2OH at these two sites. As shown in Figure S21b, the energy barrier for the desorption of *NH2OH from the Cu–Mo site is 0.33 eV lower than that from the Mo–Mo site, which suggests that hydroxylamine is conducive to desorption from the Cu–Mo dual site. These calculation results indicate that the Cu–Mo site is a potentially effective site for the electrosynthesis of CHO, further verifying the experimental results.

Conclusions

In summary, based on a DFT calculation guide, we successfully developed a Cu–Mo dual-site catalyst for precisely regulating NitRR to the *NH2OH intermediate, thus achieving high-efficiency electrosynthesis of CHO from waste NOx. The as-designed Cu1MoOx/NC catalyst with abundant Cu–Mo sites could effectively prohibit hydrodeoxygenation of NitRR intermediates, achieving an unprecedented FECHO of 94.5% at an industrially relevant current density of 0.5 A cm–2. Systematic characterizations further evidenced the selective conversion of nitrate to *NH2OH over the Cu–Mo sites, effectively reducing the production of unwanted ammonia byproducts, thus improving the energy efficiency and economic viability of the technique. We established a simple technoeconomic analysis model (Figure S22), and the results are shown in Figure S23. The high FECHO of Cu1MoOx/NC significantly increases the potential benefits of CHO electrosynthesis. Additionally, the versatility of this system is proven through its successful application in synthesizing various oximes from basic ketones and aldehydes, significantly expanding its utility in oxime synthesis.
