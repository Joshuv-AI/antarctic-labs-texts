O-Linked N-Acetylglucosamine Epitope H Expression in Placentas of Pregnancies Complicated With Fetal Growth Restriction: A Case-Control Immunochemistry Study
Authors: AGAPI KARKABOUNA, ATHINA A. SAMARA, KONSTANTINA ZACHAROULI, MARIA IOANNOU, ANTONIOS GARAS, ALEXANDROS DAPONTE, SOTIRIOS SOTIRIOU
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Epitope H contains an O-linked N-acetylglucosamine (O-GlcNAc) residue in a specific conformation or environment recognized by mouse IgM monoclonal antibody H (1). As described by Arvanitis in 2001 (2), this antibody stains two bands with relative molecular mass of 209×10−3 and 62×10−3 in lysates of cultured rat astrocytes and recognizes epitope H consisting of an O-GlcNAc and neighboring amino acids. O-GlcNAc modification and protein phosphorylation often exhibit an inverse relationship. In this respect, O-GlcNAc can alter the availability of hydroxyl residues for phosphorylation, potentially influencing signal transduction pathways (3,4). Since its discovery, numerous studies (5-9) have reported that O-GlcNAcylation contributes to many cellular functions, involving signaling, protein localization and stability, transcription, chromatin remodeling, mitochondrial function, and cell survival. It is now clear that cells are sensitive to changes in O-GlcNAc homeostasis, in part, due to altered nutritional availability and metabolization, affecting cellular functions (10).

Pregnancy is characterized by a variety of physiological changes in both the maternal and fetal systems, provoking several adaptations in cell signaling, and immunological alterations (11). Moreover, data from several studies (12-15) suggest that O-GlcNAcylation regulates several processes related to implantation, stem cell differentiation, development of the fetus, invasion of the trophoblast, remodeling of the uterine arteries, and placentation. However, it may play an important role even before implantation, since there is evidence that increased O-GlcNAc levels during oocyte maturation have a negative impact on fertilization (16).

Fetal growth restriction (FGR) is a major and common complication of pregnancy, affecting approximately 1 in 10 pregnancies. FGR is divided into early-onset when diagnosed before the 32nd week of gestation and late-onset when presented after the 32nd week. A broadly accepted definition of FGR based on the Delphi international consensus, includes an estimated fetal weight or abdominal circumference below the 3rd percentile of given reference ranges or below the 10th percentile combined with uterine artery Doppler pulsativity index (PI) >95th percentile or umbilical artery PI >95th percentile (17). FGR is associated with a variety of maternal, fetal and placental factors that affect fetal growth. The main pathophysiological changes of placental-related FGR are a result of the failure of the remodeling of the uterine spiral arteries supplying the placenta during early pregnancy. The resultant malperfusion induces cell stress within the placental tissues, leading to selective suppression of protein synthesis and reduced cell proliferation (18).

FGR is most commonly associated with placental insufficiency (19). FGR has also been linked to other placental abnormalities, such as the implantation of the velamentous cord, circumvallate placenta, and a single umbilical artery (20).

During pregnancy, placental insufficiency is frequently identified through a process of exclusion because it cannot be directly evaluated (21). After all other possible causes of FGR have been ruled out, including fetal genetic or structural abnormalities, infections, maternal substance use or certain medications, placental insufficiency is usually blamed, especially if abnormal Doppler velocimetry is seen in the umbilical or maternal uterine arteries (22).

Transporting vital nutrients including glucose and amino acids through the placenta is necessary for a healthy baby's growth and development. Numerous glucose transporters located in the placenta allow glucose to reach the developing fetus (23). Furthermore, a variety of signaling channels work together to carefully coordinate nutrient intake in response to fetal demand, and O-GlcNacylation is a known nutrient-sensing route implicated in this process. Interestingly, studies have linked small term newborns and poor fetal growth to arterial hypertension throughout pregnancy (24). The impact of the O-GlcNacylation pathway on placental nutritional status and fetal development in this condition is as yet unclear. The importance of studying the expression of O-GlcNAc epitope H in the placenta is based on its potential link with the failure of the fetus to achieve its genetically determined growth potential (23,24). Consequently, understanding the way in which the expression of the epitope H of O-GlcNac modifies the placenta may shed light on the mechanisms contributing to the FGR.

As the placenta holds the key to fetal growth, the detection of biomarkers associated with FGR is essential. The aim of the present study was to compare the expression of O-GlcNAc epitope H in placental tissues of pregnancies complicated with FGR and gestations with normal growth.

Materials and Methods

Approval for the study was provided by the committee of our university (13/14-07-2021) and the research complied with the Helsinki Declaration. Informed consent was obtained from all individuals included in this study.

Postpartum samples from chorionic villi from pregnancies were used in the present study. Fifteen samples from pregnancies complicated with FGR and 15 control samples from pregnancies with normal growth, matched for other maternal factors (age, parity, body mass index and medical history).

Tissue samples underwent a fixation process using a 10% neutral-buffered formaldehyde solution for 24 h. Subsequently, they were dehydrated through a series of ethanolic solutions with increasing concentration (80%, 95%, and 100%) followed by 100% xylene. Finally, the samples were embedded in paraffin wax. Slices (4-µm-thick) were used for hematoxylin-eosin staining and immunohistochemistry.

After deparaffinization and blocking of endogeneous peroxidase activity by immersing the sections in 3% H2O2 in Tris-saline buffer (pH 7.6), the sections were incubated in 10% normal rabbit serum in buffer for 30 min to inhibit the non-specific binding of antibodies. Then the sections were incubated for 2 h at room temperature in undiluted supernatant containing mouse monoclonal antibody H, which is not commercially available and was produced at the University of Chicago (25). After washing for 3×10 min in buffer, the sections were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated rabbit anti-mouse antibody (POIGI-DAKO, Glostrup, Denmark) diluted 1:50 in buffer for 1 h. After washing for 3×10 min, the color was developed by incubating the sections in diaminobenzidine-H2O2 in buffer for 8 min. Then, after washing, counterstaining in hematoxylin and dehydration, the sections were covered with permount for microscopic examination. For the negative control sections, the primary antibody was omitted.

The expression of epitope H was categorized into five levels: Negative: no cells stained; very low: <5% of cells stained; low: 5-30% of cells stained; moderate: 30-75% of cells stained; and high: 75-100% of cells stained. Two researchers (M.I and K.Z) independently and blindly evaluated three different slides from each sample.

Statistical calculations were performed in SPSS v.25 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Chi-square test was used to determine whether a statistically significant difference existed between the two groups regarding epitope-H expression. A value of p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

In total, 30 pregnant women were included in the present study. Specifically, 15 women for each group were enrolled: 15 gestations with normal intrauterine growth and 15 with FGR, matched for all other variants.

Figure 1 and Figure 2 depict the immunochemical expression of epitope H In the FGR group. Epitope H was highly expressed in the cells of chorionic villi in 12 samples (80%) and moderately expressed in three samples (20%). On the other hand, in the control group (Figure 3 and Figure 4) very low expression was observed in five samples (33%), low expression in another three (20%), moderate in six samples (40%) and high expression in only one sample (7%). When the expression of epitope H was compared between the two groups, the proportion of FGR pregnancies with high expression of epitope H pregnancies was statistically significant higher (p<0.001).

Discussion

O-GlcNacylation plays an important role in regulating placental function and fetal well-being during pregnancy (11). Many conditions, including hyperglycemia and hypertension, contribute to dysregulation of this modulator, which, in turn, can result in serious adverse events for both mother and the developing fetus (26). The objective of the present study was to investigate the expression of O-GlcNAc epitope H in chorionic villi tissues from pregnancies complicated with FGR and those with normal growth. Our hypothesis that placentas of pregnancies complicated by FGR highly express O-GlcNAc epitope H was based on the effect of O-GlcNAcylation in fetal growth patterns. In the FGR group, epitope H was found to be overexpressed in 80% of the samples and moderately expressed in 20% of the samples, while 33% of the samples in the control group showed very low expression, 20% showed low expression, 40% showed moderate expression and only one sample (7%) showed high expression. Epitope H was expressed at statistically significant higher levels in FGR pregnancies than in controls (p<0.001).

Abnormal O-GlcNacylation is also associated with hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, particularly pre-eclampsia. In pre-eclampsia, the placentation process is disrupted, resulting in shallow cytotrophoblast invasion and inadequate uteroplacental blood flow (27). O-GlcNacylation of specific transcription factors, such as specificity protein 1 (SP1), has been shown to promote the expression of aquaporin-3 (AQP3), which is vital for trophoblast function. Reduced expression of AQP3 and O-GlcNacylation levels in the placenta have been documented in models of severe hypertension during pregnancy (28). Moreover, hypoxia-inducible factor-1 alpha, a key regulator of placental vascular development, is affected by O-GlcNAc cycling, with increased levels observed in placentas from women with pre-eclampsia (14). O-GlcNacylation is involved in trophoblastic differentiation and invasion, which are critical for successful implantation and placentation. O-GlcNacylation of the histone variant H2A is required for trophoblast differentiation and is abundantly expressed during early placental development (11,29).

O-GlcNAc modification appears to enhance the cellular stress response by promoting expression of heat-shock proteins (HSPs), particularly HSP72, through the modulation of signaling pathways involving glycogen synthase kinase 3 and heat shock factor 1 (30). Moreover, O-GlcNAc modifications of HSP27 significantly reshape its interaction network, enhancing its capacity to collaborate with co-chaperones such as BCL-2-associated athanogene 3 and influencing its oligomeric interactions with other small HSPs, which may have critical implications for cellular stress management and the prevention of protein aggregation (31).

During pregnancy, O-GlcNacylation acts as a nutrient sensor, with its levels being influenced by the availability of glucose and other nutrients through the hexosamine biosynthetic pathway (31). In the context of placentation, O-GlcNacylation modifies proteins that are essential for placental development and function. In an experimental study with rats, Dos Passos Jr. (22) suggested that decreased protein O-GlcNAcylation in the placenta, often observed in hypertensive pregnancies, can lead to placental dysfunction. This dysfunction is associated with impaired fetal growth, as the placenta is responsible for nutrient exchange between the mother and the fetus. Moreover, Palin (32) reported changes in O-GlcNAcylation of proteins involved in clathrin-mediated endocytosis in placentas from pregnancies with pre-gestational diabetes, suggesting that nutrient transfer may be altered in a way that could impair fetal growth. The human placental O-GlcNAcome provides a resource to aid further investigation of molecular mechanisms governing placental nutrient sensing.

A major limitation of the present study is the limited number of cases included in the analysis. This drawback is a result of the high cost of the antibody used for the immunochemical study that unfortunately restricted the analysis of additional placental samples. However, our study provides valuable preliminary insights and introduces the novel concept of investigating epitope H of O-GlcNAc in the context of FGR. The main strength of the study is based on the fact that all cases were carefully selected to represent early-onset FGR, in strict accordance with the International Society of Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology guidelines (17), in order to limit selection bias. Our research team is currently designing a larger follow-up study to validate and expand upon these findings, that will allow more robust conclusions in the future.

In conclusion, although preliminary findings regarding the expression of O-GlcNAc epitope H in chorionic villi samples of pregnancies complicated by FGR are promising, further studies are expected to contribute to a better understanding of the role of O-GlcNacylation in FGR pathogenesis and its clinical significance. Thus, a joint effort combining basic and clinical research is required in order to minimize the unfavorable effects of FGR.
