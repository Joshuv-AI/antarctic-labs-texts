Deep learning for survival prediction in triple-negative breast cancer: development and validation in real-world cohorts
Authors: Yiyue Xu, Butuo Li, Bing Zou, Bingjie Fan, Shijiang Wang, Jinming Yu, Taotao Dong, Linlin Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTriple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is an aggressive and heterogeneous disease, highlighting the need for better patient stratification to guide treatment. We developed a deep learning-based survival model and an individualized prognosis system using data from 37,818 TNBC patients in the SEER database (split into training [65%], validation [17.5%], and test [17.5%] sets). The survival model, built using the pysurvival algorithm, achieved strong performance (C-index: 0.824 in validation set, 0.816 in test set), outperforming traditional methods (CPH: 0.781 and 0.785; RSH: 0.779 and 0.766). External validation on a real-world cohort confirmed its robustness (C-index: 0.758). Our individualized prognosis system also showed higher predictive accuracy than traditional AJCC-TNM staging (AUC 0.821 vs. 0.771). These tools improve TNBC prognosis assessment, enable better patient stratification, and provide clinicians with significant treatment recommendations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-16331-8.Keywords: Triple-negative breast cancer, Deep learning, Prognosis model, Survival prediction systemSubject terms: Breast cancer, Cancer models

Introduction

Breast cancer is confirmed as the most commonly diagnosed cancer worldwide in 2020, and represents a heterogeneous disease comprising multiple subtypes1. Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is defined as one subtype in which all estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor-2 (Her-2) are not expressed, which limits the treatment options of endocrinotherapy and targeted therapy2. TNBC accounts for 15–20% of all breast cancers diagnosed in the USA and is associated with approximately 50% of breast cancer deaths3. Because of its high mortality and poor prognosis, a powerful mortality risk prediction system is important for decision-making and treatment strategies in TNBC4. Traditionally, the American Joint Committee on Cancer/International Union for Cancer Control (AJCC/UICC) Tumor, Node, Metastases (TNM) staging system is used to stratify patients based on tumor size, number of positive lymph nodes, and distant metastasis. However, due to the absence of distinction between different molecular sub-types of breast cancer, the AJCC-TNM staging system cannot provide sufficient prognostic information in TNBC5. Therefore, there is a great need to develop a novel prognostic system as well as a new prognosis prediction model.

There are several studies about new prognosis systems for TNBC, but almost all currently available studies in the field use the Cox proportional hazards regression model (CPH) for survival analysis6–9. These approaches focus on statistically significant factors and therefore much information is ignored. What’s more, these models can only identify the linear relationship between variables and survival based on linearity assumptions, which may not be suitable for the complicated structure in real-world scenarios.

A deep learning network is a mainstream technique in artificial intelligence-based machining learning studies that can automatically extract intricate and nonlinear associations from the raw data using high-throughput algorithms10. With the advent of the era of artificial intelligence, the adaptive nonlinear dynamic system formed by deep learning through enriched and complete connections offers technical safeguards for big data analysis and has been successfully applied in breast cancer imaging diagnosis11relapse prediction12and survival prediction. For example, Vidhi et al. assembled a neighborhood component analysis to achieve 94% accuracy in breast cancer prognosis13. Deep learning algorithms and other methods were compared in another study. Deep learning achieved the highest accuracy among the various approaches, which repeatedly demonstrated its reliability and feasibility14. However, these studies neither conducted separate analyses for this highly heterogeneous TNBC subtype nor developed individualized prognostic systems, or only used data from the database. There is a lack of studies utilizing deep learning for prognosis prediction and the construction of novel prognostic systems in TNBC.

In this study, we innovatively employed deep learning methods to develop a survival prediction model using data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database. The model’s predictive accuracy was compared with conventional CPH and Random Survival Forest (RSF) models to evaluate the advantages of this deep learning approach. In addition, a novel individualized prognostic system based on the deep learning neural network model was developed and compared with the traditional clinical AJCC-TNM staging system. To further validate the robustness in different geographies, the deep learning model was tested in a Chinese real-life clinical dataset.

Methods

Patient identification and selection

As the largest population-based cancer registry, the SEER database collects demographic and clinicopathology data from approximately 34.6% of the U.S. population. This study was based on SEER data of TNBC patients confirmed by pathological examinations and immunochemistry from 2010 to 2016. Patients with primary breast cancer were identified according to the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, Third Edition (ICD-O-3) pathology codes (C50.0, C50.1, C50.2, C50.3, C50.4, C50.5, C50.6, C50.8, and C50.9). Then TNBC patients were selected based on BREAST SUBTYPE with the selection code 4 Triple Negative.

Eventually, 206 variables of 37,818 patients were included in the study. The mean follow-up time was 34 months and the longest follow-up duration was 84 months. We submitted an agreement form to the website (https://seer.cancer.gov/) and accessed the SEER database. The SEER database is open for research purposes and no informed patient consent was needed because cancer is a reportable disease in every state of the USA. Similarly, this study does not require ethics approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB).

With the institutional review board approval from Shandong Cancer Hospital, we selected 164 TNBC patients who were pathologically confirmed between November 2010 and May 2012. The age of patients ranged from 28 to 74 years with a median age of 49 years. These patients were quite distinct from the patients in the SEER database. Patients with multiple primary tumors, incomplete survival details, or missing staging data were excluded from the analysis in both SEER and real-life datasets.

Data collection

Based on clinical experience and literature, we selected 20 potential prognostic parameters for further statistical analysis. Then correlation matrix was used to evaluate the correlation between the parameters, and those with high correlation were removed. We deleted the stage variable due to the high correlation with T staging, N staging, and M staging as seen in Fig. 1 (the correlation coefficient was 0.75). Final variables included in the study include the age at diagnosis, patient sex, marital status at diagnosis, the month of diagnosis, the year of diagnosis, race, Spanish, T staging, N staging, M staging, primary site, histologic type, grade, laterality, total number of tumors, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, surgery, and normalized Cost-of-living Index. Outcome data is survival time and death status. Finally, the entire data was randomly divided into a training set (65%), a validation set (17.5%), and a test set (17.5%) using the hold-out method.

Correlation matrix of included variables. The correlation coefficient indicates strength of association, with higher absolute values representing stronger correlations. Positive values denote positive relationships, while negative values indicate inverse relationships.

Variables were collected using standardized questionnaires in the real-life dataset. The Cost-of-Living Index variable was replaced by the Consumer Price Index (CPI) commonly used in China. Other than that, all the remaining variables were the same as the SEER dataset.

Survival prediction models

Pysurvival is a deep learning algorithm based on the Neural Multitask Logistic Regression Model (N-MTLR) first developed by S. Fotso in 2018, which contains a hierarchical structure with a fully connected layer and a single output node to calculate the survival risks15. Briefly, this model converts the patient’s follow-up time into a series of time points. When an event occurs in the patient, the corresponding time is labeled 1 and the remaining time points are recorded as 0. If the patient was lost to follow-up, all time points after the loss of follow-up were denoted as 1. The tabular data obtained from SEER were primarily preprocessed using STATA (see Supplementary information). Subsequent deep survival analysis was performed using the open-source pysurvival package (developed by S. Fotso et al.), publicly available at https://www.pysurvival.io/. The model was implemented with the following optimized parameters after extensive tuning: weight initialization via glorot_uniform, Adam optimizer with a learning rate (lr) of 1e-3, ReLU activation function, and a network architecture consisting of 4 hidden layers with 50 units each. Regularization was achieved through L2 regularization (l2_reg = 1e-2), L2 smoothing (l2_smooth = 1e-2), and dropout (rate = 0.2). The model was trained for 2000 epochs.

To compare the deep learning model with other methods, we used CPH and RSF as the benchmarks. The age at diagnosis, total number of tumors, and normalized cost-of-living index were defined as continuous variables. Patient sex, marital status at diagnosis, the month of diagnosis, the year of diagnosis, race, Spanish, T staging, N staging, M staging, primary site, histologic type, grade, laterality, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and surgery were defined as categorical variables. Variables that were proved statistically significant (p < 0.1) in the univariate Cox regression analysis were incorporated into the multivariate analysis. The CPH model included variables that remained statistically significant (p < 0.05) in multivariate analysis.

Being an ensemble machine learning method proposed by Leo, Random Survival Forest built hundreds of decision trees by using all collected variables16. The result is output by aggregating the decisions of these decision trees through a majority vote. The RSH model assessed variable predictiveness with the variable importance (VIMP) measure, which was obtained by measuring the decrease in accuracy of the survival prediction with the Mean Squarer Error17. Higher VIMP indicated the predictive capacity of that variable was higher.

The survival models were trained with the training set and applied to the validation set for prediction. Then, independently validations were carried out in the test set to further validate the model. The applicability of the proposed model in the real world was evaluated in a real-life dataset. The concordance index (C-index) and integrated Brier score (IBS) were employed to evaluate the model performances. C-index represents the matching degree between the predicted results and the actual results. The Brier score measures the difference between the predicted probability and the true outcomes, which is calculated by the average squared distances between the model-predicted survival probability and the actually observed survival status. 0 means the prediction is completely correct, and 1 means completely incorrect.

Individualized prognostic system and survival curves

Based on the deep survival model, a new prognostic system was developed to individualize the prognosis evaluation of TNBC patients. In the test set, Kaplan-Meier curves of the traditional clinical AJCC-TNM staging system and the new prognostic system were drawn respectively. The log-rank test was used to compare the survival curves and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The receiver operating characteristics (ROC) curve and the area under the curve (AUC) were used to assess the prediction accuracy of the traditional clinical staging system and the new prognostic system. A Delong test was performed to compare the prognostic performance between the models.

All experiments were conducted on a Inspur NF5280M5 server featuring dual Intel Xeon Gold 6248R CPUs (48 cores/96 threads total) and 384GB DDR4 RAM. The software stack included Ubuntu 20.04 LTS, Python 3.8, and PyTorch 1.12 with CUDA 11.6 and cuDNN 8.4. The deep learning modules were built in the Pytorch framework and other statistical analyses were performed using STATA software (2013 StataCorp LP, 13.1).

Deep survival prediction model

This study used an N-MTLR to process the whole data to develop a deep survival prediction model. N-MTLR represents the patient’s survival data as a series of variables and estimates the probability that an outcome event occurs within each time interval.

We chose the model with the best stability and performance. Our deep neural network consisted of an input layer, four hidden layers with 50 neurons in each layer, and an output layer. ReLU activation, L2 regularization, and dropout techniques were used to prevent overfitting. The grid search method automatically found the optimal hyperparameters of this model. Finally, the initial method was Glorot uniform, the optimizer of choice was Adam, the learning rate was 1e-3, l2 regularization was 1e-2, l2 smooth was 1e-2, and the dropout rate was 0.2. The training curve was plotted to depict the loss function decrease for the training set as the weights of the neural network were tuned over each epoch. The value of the loss function decreased from 45561.82 to 25149.28 after 2000 iterations (see Supplementary Fig. S1 online). In the training set, the C-index was 0.824, the IBS was 0.09 (Fig. 2a), the median absolute error was 5.052, and the mean absolute error was 11.315 (see Supplementary Fig. S2a online). In the validation set, the C-index was 0.824, the IBS was 0.09 (Fig. 2b), the median absolute error was 5.261, and the mean absolute error was 6.714 (see Supplementary Fig. S2b online). In the test set, the C-index was 0.816, the IBS was 0.09 (Fig. 2c), the median absolute error was 5.079, and the mean absolute error was 6.875 (see Supplementary Fig. S2c online). The calibration curve showed that the predicted curve was basically within the confidence interval of the actual curve (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Individualized prognosis system based on a deep neural network model

Then, we stratified patients into six classes based on the predicted risk and established a new survival outcome prediction system using our deep learning survival model (Fig. 4a). Kaplan-Meier curves stratified by the new prognostic system demonstrated statistically significant between-group differences (log-rank test, p < 0.001). Patients with lower-stage disease demonstrated significantly reduced mortality risk compared to those with advanced-stage disease. (Fig. 4b). Univariable Cox regression analysis revealed the mortality of new staging 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 patients was 2.6, 4.8, 14.1, 30.1, and 118.0 times higher than those patients with new staging 1 patients ( Supplementary Table S1). The ROC curve was also plotted and the AUC was 0.821 (Fig. 4c).

The new prognostic system based on deep learning. (a) Patients were stratified into six classes based on the predicted risk scores. (b) Kaplan-Meier curves of patients staged with the new staging system. (c) The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve of the new staging system.

To contrast the traditional clinical staging, A Kaplan-Meier curve based on the AJCC-TNM staging system was also drawn (log-rank test, p < 0.001) (Fig. 5a). The Kaplan-Meier curves revealed substantially attenuated stratification compared to our novel prognostic system (Fig. 4b vs. 5a). The mortality risk of patients in stages IB, IIA, IIB, IIIA, IIIB, IIIC, and IV was 1.5, 1.9, 3.2, 5.2, 9.4, 9.8, and 28.4 times higher than stage IA patients, respectively ( Supplementary Table S2). The AUC of the ROC curve was 0.771 (Fig. 5b). The Delong test comparing the novel prognostic system with the TNM staging system revealed a statistically significant AUC difference of 0.017 (95% CI 0.015–0.018; p < 0.001), which proved that the performance of the new prognostic system was better than traditional clinical staging in TNBC patients.

Discussion

TNBC is the subtype of breast cancer with the worst prognosis due to its high invasiveness, high recurrence rate, and insensitivity to HER-2-targeting treatments and anti-hormone therapies18. Previous studies found that TNBC is more aggressive than HR + and/or HER2 + disease in each stage regardless of tumor grade, patient age, or race8. Most of the previous research on TNBC patients’ survival outcome prediction used conventional statistical methods, which have not yielded satisfactory results19. Shi et al. brought albumin and lymphocyte into their models, and the established nomogram was slightly better than AJCC. But nomogram was just a visualization method of Cox regression, therefore its analytical power was still limited20. Deep learning is a cutting-edge approach that has proved its power and utility for cancer prognostication in recent years. For example, Yu et al. found that analyzing lymphocytes through deep learning can predict TNBC recurrence12. Another study used a convolutional neural network to predict distant breast cancer recurrence and obtained an AUC of over 0.8821. Yet, the utility of deep learning in TNBC patients is largely unknown. Our research team first reported a deep learning model based on clinicopathological features of big data for survival outcome prediction in TNBC patients at the 2021 American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) meeting and achieved a C-index of 0.799 in the validation set22. This study further used the ReLU function, L2 regularization, and dropout techniques to optimize the model and the C-index in the verification set enhanced to 0.824. The robustness of the model is further verified in the real-world datasets.

The multivariate Cox regression analysis showed that the age at diagnosis, marital status, grade, T staging, N staging, M staging, total number of tumors, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, surgery, and normalized cost-of-living index were identified as independent risk factors for outcomes in TNBC patients. Previous studies have also analyzed some of these parameters. For example, Lin et al. used Cox regression analysis and found that the number of positive lymph nodes and histological grade were independent prognostic factors23. Yang et al. concluded that tumor size and node status were predictive of the outcomes of TNBC patients24. Another research revealed that nodal status, tumor size, radiotherapy, and surgery were the most useful prognostic parameters25. Pathological factors including poor differentiation, advanced T/N/M stages, and multiple tumor lesions contribute to increased tumor burden and consequently poorer prognosis. Therapeutic interventions (chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and surgery) may alter the tumor microenvironment, yielding both therapeutic benefits and adverse effects. Younger patients typically demonstrate better performance status and consequently longer survival. Married patients showed superior outcomes compared to unmarried individuals, potentially attributable to enhanced caregiving and psychosocial support. Higher standardized cost-of-living index correlated with improved survival, likely reflecting superior economic conditions facilitating better treatment access and social support. Compared with previous studies, our study had a larger sample size and more powerful statistical tools, and thus more reliable results have been achieved in this study.

In this research, the deep learning model obtains a better C-index (0.824) than the traditional Cox regression model (0.781) and RF model (0.779). The performance of the new prediction system based on deep neural networks is better than the commonly used clinical staging (AUC 0.821 vs. 0.771). The significant Delong test confirmed a statistically significant difference between the two prognostic staging systems. Given the particularly poor prognosis of TNBC among breast cancer subtypes, the conventional TNM staging system (designed for all breast cancers) may have limitations in stratifying TNBC patients with varying risks. The prognostic system developed in this study specifically for TNBC patients can better capture the heterogeneity within this population. Besides, deep learning is an artificial intelligence technique that automatically extracts information and can directly incorporate raw data into the model. Therefore, compared with traditional statistical methods, deep neural networks can analyze more parameters and fully extract potential prognostic factors26,27. In addition, neural networks can capture the nonlinear relationship between parameters and survival, thereby improving the performance of the staging system28.

This study included 37,818 TNBC cases from SEER to establish a novel deep learning model, and the test set proved that this model is stable. As an external validation set, the independent real-life dataset proved the robustness of the proposed model in complex, real scenarios and its applicability in TNBC patients of different ethnicities. The analyzed data incorporated clinical parameters, treatment content, the effects of surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. In this study, we proved that deep learning could play an important role in the TNBC patient prognosis. This approach can accurately distinguish patients with different risks of death to assist clinical management of individual patients. First, it can inform the patient’s survival individually29. Secondly, precise stratification helps the choice of treatment plan. High-risk patients should adopt more aggressive treatment strategies, such as dose-intensive chemotherapy when tolerated, while low-risk patients can be spared from overtreatment. As well, it also helps to reduce sample bias in clinical trials and increase sample representativeness.

The present study also has several limitations. Firstly, deep learning algorithms are complicated to perform and cannot be expressed by simple equations, which restricts its development as a mainstream method. The clinical deployment of deep learning models requires hospitals to possess appropriate computational infrastructure, while the application of these methods for patient risk analysis presents notable challenges for clinicians. Secondly, deep learning does not provide an interpretation of the results because it is hard to explain the calculation processes. This is why deep learning approaches are called black-boxes. Finally, the SEER-derived data may contain inherent selection biases common to cancer registry datasets, and the real-world dataset was conducted exclusively using Han Chinese female patient data, therefore the potential impacts of sex, race, and Spanish remain unassessed.

Conclusions

In summary, this study used a population-based database to develop a deep learning-based survival prediction model which showed better prediction efficacy than conventional methods in TNBC. Furthermore, we established a new prognostic system with a better performance in stratifying mortality risk. By processing patient data through deep learning model and evaluating the presence of high-risk factors, this framework generates mortality risk predictions that may provide clinicians and researchers with a more precise analytical tool for treatment strategy development and research applications.
