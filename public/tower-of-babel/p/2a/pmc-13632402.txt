NSUN2-mediated m5C modification of E2F1 promotes lung adenocarcinoma progression via the RAD54L signaling axis
Authors: Yue Li, Chen Chen, HaiLin Liu, ZhenFa Zhang, ChangLi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality, often driven by the hyperactivation of cell cycle and DNA repair pathways. While genomic alterations in these pathways are well-documented, the role of RNA epigenetics in orchestrating these oncogenic signals remains poorly understood. Here, we identify a hierarchical signaling axis driven by the RNA methyltransferase NSUN2 that fuels LUAD progression. We demonstrate that NSUN2 is frequently amplified and overexpressed in LUAD, where it correlates with advanced disease stage and poor patient survival. Mechanistically, NSUN2 catalyzes 5-methylcytosine (m5C) modification of the E2F1 3′-untranslated region (3′-UTR). This modification, recognized by the reader protein YBX1, enhances the translational efficiency of E2F1 transcripts. The resulting accumulation of E2F1 transcriptionally activates the DNA translocase RAD54L, which we redefine as a proactive oncogenic effector essential for cell proliferation, invasion, and in vivo tumorigenesis. Integrated multi-omics and rescue experiments confirm that the NSUN2-mediated translational induction of E2F1 is a prerequisite for RAD54L-driven malignancy. Our findings uncover a novel epitranscriptomic-transcriptional regulatory cascade, establishing the NSUN2-E2F1-RAD54L axis as a critical driver of LUAD and a promising target for therapeutic intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12931-026-03757-5.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally, with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) constituting the most prevalent histological subtype [1]. A significant proportion of LUAD cases are driven by activating mutations in the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) [2]. While the advent of targeted therapies, particularly EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), has revolutionized the clinical management of these patients, the inevitable emergence of acquired resistance severely limits long-term survival [3]. The mechanisms driving tumor progression and therapeutic evasion extend far beyond secondary genomic mutations; they encompass profound transcriptomic reprogramming, dysregulation of cell cycle checkpoints, and the hyperactivation of DNA damage repair pathways [4–6]. Consequently, deciphering the molecular mechanisms that enable LUAD cells to bypass physiological growth restraints and maintain genomic stability under high replication stress is imperative for identifying novel therapeutic vulnerabilities.

Over the past decade, epitranscriptomics—the study of dynamic and reversible RNA modifications—has emerged as a critical layer of post-transcriptional gene regulation [7, 8]. Among over a hundred known RNA modifications, 5-methylcytosine (m5C) is highly conserved and plays a pivotal role in regulating mRNA export, translation, and stability [9, 10]. The deposition of m5C is primarily catalyzed by the RNA methyltransferase NSUN2 (the “writer”), while its biological outcomes are dictated by specific RNA-binding proteins (the “readers”), such as YBX1 and ALYREF [11, 12]. Recent pan-cancer genomic analyses have highlighted the frequent dysregulation of m5C regulators in human malignancies. NSUN2, in particular, has been implicated in the pathogenesis of various cancers, where it promotes proliferation, metastasis, and chemoresistance [13–15]. However, the precise epitranscriptomic landscape dictated by NSUN2 in LUAD, and specifically how m5C modifications interface with critical cell cycle and genomic stability networks, remains poorly understood.

The E2F family of transcription factors, particularly E2F1, acts as the master gatekeeper of the G1/S transition, orchestrating the expression of genes essential for DNA replication and cell cycle progression [16, 17]. In lung cancer, E2F1 is frequently hyperactive, a phenomenon classically attributed to the loss of the upstream retinoblastoma (Rb) tumor suppressor pathway [18]. Yet, emerging evidence suggests that E2F1 abundance is also subject to intricate post-transcriptional controls. Whether RNA epigenetic modifications, such as m5C, directly modulate E2F1 expression to sustain its oncogenic signaling in LUAD is a critical unresolved question.

Downstream of cell cycle entry, aggressively proliferating cancer cells must manage significant replication stress. RAD54L, a DNA-dependent ATPase and a core component of the homologous recombination (HR) repair machinery, is classically recognized for its role in repairing DNA double-strand breaks [19, 20]. While historically viewed strictly as a reactive caretaker of genomic integrity, emerging functional screens hint that RAD54L might possess uncharacterized, proactive roles in tumor growth and progression. We surmised that RAD54L might be actively reprogrammed by upstream oncogenic signals to fuel LUAD aggressiveness, rather than merely acting as a passive repair enzyme.

Based on these critical knowledge gaps, we propose the scientific hypothesis that genomic amplification of the RNA methyltransferase NSUN2 drives LUAD progression by establishing an aberrant m5C epitranscriptomic landscape, which in turn hyperactivates an oncogenic E2F1-RAD54L signaling axis. Specifically, we hypothesize that NSUN2-mediated m5C deposition on the E2F1 transcript enhances its translational efficiency, leading to a surge in E2F1 protein that directly transcriptionally activates RAD54L, thereby uncoupling cell cycle progression and DNA repair from normal physiological restraints.

The primary objective of this research is to systematically map and functionally characterize the NSUN2-E2F1-RAD54L axis in LUAD. To achieve this, we employed an integrated approach combining clinical cohort analyses, transcriptome-wide m5C mapping (RNA-BisSeq), and multi-omics profiling (RIP-seq, RNA-seq). Through rigorous in vitro and in vivo rescue experiments, we aimed to dissect the mechanistic dependency of E2F1 translation on NSUN2/YBX1-mediated m5C modification, delineate the direct transcriptional control of RAD54L by E2F1, and redefine the biological role of RAD54L as a critical downstream effector of tumorigenesis. Ultimately, this study seeks to uncover a novel epitranscriptomic-transcriptional regulatory cascade, offering new prognostic biomarkers and potential therapeutic targets for the clinical management of advanced LUAD.

Methods

Clinical specimen collection and processing

Primary EGFR-mutant lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) tissues and adjacent normal tissues were obtained from patients at the Tianjin Medical University Cancer Institute and Hospital (Supplementary Table S1). Strict exclusion criteria were applied: no prior surgery, chemoradiotherapy, or immunotherapy within six months of enrollment. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Tianjin Medical University Cancer Institute and Hospital and were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Tissue microarray and IHC quantification

Tissue microarrays (TMA) were constructed using formal-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) blocks from the 62 LUAD patients. IHC staining for E2F1 and RAD54L was performed as previously described. Staining intensity was graded as: 0 (negative), 1 (weak), 2 (moderate), or 3 (strong). The percentage of positive cells was scored as: 0 (0%), 1 (1–25%), 2 (26–50%), 3 (51–75%), and 4 (76–100%). The final IHC score (0–12) was the product of the intensity and percentage scores. Samples were stratified into “High” and “Low” expression groups based on the median score.

Cell culture

The human EGFR-mutant LUAD cell lines H1975, HCC2279, and H1650 were procured from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). These cell lines were maintained under 37 °C within a humidified incubator supplemented with 5% CO2, where they were cultivated in RPMI-1640 medium enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco).

Lentiviral transduction and stable cell line generation

For stable knockdown of RAD54L and NSUN2, short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) were cloned into the pLKO.1 vector. For overexpression, the human RAD54L, E2F1, and NSUN2 coding sequences were amplified and inserted into the pLenti-puro or pLHCX vectors. Lentiviral particles were produced in HEK-293T cells using the psPAX2 and pMD2.G packaging system. LUAD cell lines (H1650, HCC2279, H1975) were infected with viral supernatant containing 8 µg/mL polybrene. Stable clones were selected with 2 µg/mL puromycin or 200 µg/mL hygromycin for 7–10 days. The knockdown/overexpression efficiency was validated by RT-qPCR and western blotting.

RNA immunoprecipitation-PCR (RIP-PCR)

RIP was performed as described in the general methods. Briefly, cell lysates were incubated with 5 µg of anti-NSUN2 antibody or rabbit IgG. After washing and proteinase K treatment, the co-precipitated RNA was extracted. The enrichment of specific candidate mRNAs was quantified by RT-qPCR and calculated as a fold enrichment relative to the IgG control.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

ChIP assays were performed using the SimpleChIP® Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Cell Signaling Technology). Briefly, LUAD cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature and quenched with glycine. Chromatin was fragmented to 200–500 bp using micrococcal nuclease followed by brief sonication. Immunoprecipitation was performed overnight at 4 °C with 2 µg of anti-E2F1 antibody (Abcam) or normal rabbit IgG as a negative control. DNA was eluted, and the cross-linking was reversed. The enrichment of the RAD54L promoter region was quantified by RT-qPCR using specific primers (F: 5’-GGCAGTGAGAGGAGGAAAG-3’, R: 5’-GGTCGCCTTAGTCCACTTG-3’). Data were normalized to the 2% input DNA.

mRNA stability (Half-life) assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates and allowed to reach 70% confluence. New transcription was inhibited by adding 5 µg/mL Actinomycin D (Sigma). Total RNA was harvested at 0, 2, 4, and 6 h post-treatment. E2F1 mRNA levels were quantified by RT-qPCR and normalized to GAPDH. The mRNA half-life was determined using non-linear regression (one-phase decay analysis) in GraphPad Prism.

m5C-RNA immunoprecipitation (m5C-RIP)

Total RNA was extracted and mRNA was purified using the PolyATtract mRNA Purification Kit (Promega). mRNA was fragmented into ~ 200 nt pieces using RNA Fragmentation Reagents (Ambion). Fragmented RNA was incubated with 5 µg of anti-m5C antibody (Abcam) or mouse IgG in IP buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% NP-40) for 4 h at 4 °C, followed by incubation with Protein G magnetic beads (Invitrogen) for 2 h. After extensive washing, the methylated RNA was eluted and recovered using TRIzol. The enrichment of E2F1 transcripts was determined by RT-qPCR and normalized to the input.

RNA dot blot

Purified mRNA was denatured at 65 °C for 5 min and spotted onto an Amersham Hybond-N+ membrane. After UV crosslinking (150 mJ/cm²), the membrane was blocked in 5% non-fat milk and incubated with an anti-m5C antibody (1:1000, Abcam) overnight. Following incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, signals were detected using ECL. To ensure equal loading, membranes were counterstained with 0.02% methylene blue in 0.3 M sodium acetate (pH 5.2).

Plasmid construction and site-directed mutagenesis

The wild-type (WT) 3′-UTR of E2F1 was amplified and inserted into the pGL3-control vector downstream of the firefly luciferase gene. The m5C-site mutant (MUT) 3′-UTR was generated by replacing the identified m5C-modified cytosine with adenine using the QuikChange II Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Agilent). FLAG-tagged NSUN2-WT and the catalytic-inactive mutant (C271A/C321A) were cloned into the pcDNA3.1 vector.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The human RAD54L promoter region (containing the predicted E2F1 binding sites) was amplified and cloned into the pGL3-Basic vector (Promega) to generate the pGL3-RAD54L-Luc reporter. H1650 cells were seeded in 24-well plates and co-transfected with either the overexpression plasmid (oeE2F1) or shRNA plasmids (shE2F1) along with the pGL3-RAD54L-Luc and a pRL-TK Renilla luciferase vector (internal control) using Lipofectamine 3000. After 48 h, luciferase activities were measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega). Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity.

H1650 cells were seeded in 24-well plates and co-transfected with 100 ng of pGL3-E2F1-3′UTR (WT or MUT), 10 ng of pRL-TK (Renilla control), and 200 ng of NSUN2 expression plasmids or shRNA constructs. After 48 h, luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega). Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla activity for each sample.

Animal models

All animal experiments received authorization from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Tianjin Medical University Cancer Institute and Hospital (Tianjin, China) and complied with the institutional ethical review board standards for animal research. During the tumorigenesis assay, H1650 cells (1 × 106) subjected to shRNA transduction or carrying empty vectors were prepared as a suspension in 100 µl PBS, which was administered into the right axillae of 6-week-old nude mice. Tumor expansion was evaluated, and tumor volumes were determined every 3 days starting from day 7 following cell inoculation. The mice underwent euthanasia after 3 weeks, whereupon xenograft masses were recorded.

In vivo tumorigenesis rescue assay

Xenograft Sect. (5 μm) were stained with H&E for structural overview or processed for IHC using antibodies against Ki-67 (1:200, Abcam) and cleaved Caspase-3 (1:200, Cell Signaling Technology). Relative staining intensity and the percentage of positive cells were quantified using ImageJ software by three blinded observers. The proliferation index (Ki-67) and apoptotic index (Caspase-3) were calculated across five random high-power fields per section.

Statistical analyses

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± SD. Two-tailed Student’s t-tests were used for pairwise comparisons, and one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was applied for multiple-group analyses. Survival differences were assessed by the log-rank test. A p-value below 0.05 was interpreted as indicating a statistically meaningful or highly significant difference.

Results

RAD54L promotes LUAD cell proliferation and tumorigenesis

To elucidate the oncogenic potential of RAD54L in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), we performed loss-of-function studies using H1975, HCC2279, and H1650 cell lines, which harbor characteristic EGFR mutations. Successful silencing of RAD54L was achieved via two independent shRNAs (Supplementary Fig. 1). CCK-8 assays revealed that RAD54L knockdown significantly attenuated the proliferation rates of all three LUAD cell lines compared to non-targeting controls (Fig. 1A-C). Consistent with these results, colony formation assays demonstrated that RAD54L deficiency markedly reduced the long-term growth and clonogenic capacity of LUAD cells (Fig. 1D). Given that aggressive proliferation is often accompanied by increased motility, we examined the impact of RAD54L on cell migration and invasion. Transwell assays showed that depletion of RAD54L led to a substantial decrease in the number of migrated and invaded cells in all tested lines (Fig. 1E).

To validate these in vitro findings in a physiological context, we employed a subcutaneous xenograft model using H1650 cells. RAD54L-deficient cells exhibited significantly impaired tumor-forming ability compared to control cells (Fig. 1F). From day 13 post-inoculation, tumors in the RAD54L-sh group grew at a significantly slower rate, resulting in a marked reduction in both tumor volume and final tumor weight at the experimental endpoint (Fig. 1G, H).

Furthermore, we performed immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis on the harvested xenograft tissues to assess the cellular mechanisms underlying tumor suppression. RAD54L-silenced tumors exhibited a significant decrease in the percentage of Ki-67-positive cells, indicating reduced in vivo proliferative activity (Fig. 1I). Simultaneously, we observed a robust increase in cleaved Caspase-3 staining in the RAD54L-sh group, suggesting that RAD54L depletion triggers programmed cell death in LUAD tumors (Fig. 1J). Taken together, these data establish RAD54L as a critical driver of LUAD progression and a potential therapeutic target.

E2F1-mediated induction of RAD54L correlates with disease progression and poor prognosis in LUAD

To determine the clinical relevance of the E2F1-RAD54L axis, we performed transcriptomic profiling on our clinical cohort of LUAD patients. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering of DNA repair-related genes identified a distinct module that was significantly enriched in patients with advanced tumor grade and higher clinical T-stage (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Fig. 2). Within this module, RAD54L exhibited the highest correlation with E2F1 expression (R = 0.901), ranking it as the top co-expressed candidate among several critical DNA repair factors including RAD51, BRCA1, and BLM (Fig. 2A).

We next evaluated the prognostic significance of these findings. Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed that patients with high RAD54L expression had significantly shorter OS and PFS compared to those with low expression (Fig. 2B). To assess whether RAD54L serves as an independent prognostic marker, we performed a multivariate Cox regression analysis. After adjusting for age, gender, and AJCC stage, high RAD54L expression remained a robust independent risk factor for poor OS (HR > 10, P < 0.001; Fig. 2C). Consistent with these findings, high E2F1 expression was also significantly associated with reduced OS and PFS in the same cohort (Fig. 2E).

To validate these transcriptomic associations at the protein level, we performed IHC on tissue microarrays. Both E2F1 and RAD54L protein levels were markedly elevated in tumor tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 2D). Notably, the staining intensity for both proteins increased progressively from early (T1/T2) to advanced (T3/T4) pathological stages (Fig. 2D). Finally, Pearson correlation analysis confirmed a significant positive linear relationship between E2F1 and RAD54L mRNA levels in our patient samples (r = 0.56, P < 0.001; Fig. 2F). Collectively, these clinical data demonstrate that the E2F1-RAD54L axis is significantly upregulated during LUAD progression and serves as a critical determinant of poor patient outcomes.

E2F1 acts as a direct transcriptional activator of RAD54L in LUAD

Having observed a strong clinical correlation between E2F1 and RAD54L, we investigated whether E2F1 directly modulates RAD54L expression. Silencing of E2F1 using two independent shRNAs (shE2F1-1 and shE2F1-2) resulted in a robust decrease in RAD54L protein levels across H1650, HCC2279, and H1975 cell lines (Fig. 3A). RT-qPCR analysis confirmed that this downregulation occurred at the mRNA level, indicating a transcript-dependent mechanism (Fig. 3B). To further validate this regulatory relationship, we performed gain-of-function experiments. Stable overexpression of E2F1 (oeE2F1) led to a significant induction of both RAD54L protein and mRNA expression in all three LUAD cell lines (Fig. 3C, D).

To determine if this regulation is mediated through direct physical interaction, we performed Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays. In H1650, HCC2279, and H1975 cells, an anti-E2F1 antibody specifically enriched the RAD54L promoter region compared to the control IgG, demonstrating that E2F1 physically binds to the regulatory region of RAD54L in a cellular context (Fig. 3E).

Finally, we utilized dual-luciferase reporter assays to assess the functional impact of this binding. In H1650 cells, overexpression of E2F1 significantly enhanced the luciferase activity driven by the RAD54L promoter (Fig. 3F, left). Conversely, knockdown of E2F1 markedly suppressed the promoter-driven reporter activity (Fig. 3F, right). Collectively, these findings provide definitive evidence that E2F1 serves as a direct transcriptional activator of RAD54L in LUAD, establishing the E2F1-RAD54L axis as a key regulatory component in these cells.

RAD54L is a critical downstream effector of E2F1-mediated LUAD progression

To determine whether E2F1 exerts its oncogenic effects through the regulation of RAD54L, we performed a series of rescue experiments in H1650 and H1975 LUAD cells. Overexpression of E2F1 (oeE2F1) significantly accelerated cell proliferation, as evidenced by CCK-8 assays (Fig. 4A). However, the E2F1-induced increase in cell viability was markedly attenuated by the simultaneous knockdown of RAD54L (Fig. 4A). Similar results were observed in colony formation assays, where RAD54L depletion significantly reduced the clonogenic capacity gained by E2F1 overexpression (Fig. 4B).

We further examined the impact of this axis on the metastatic potential of LUAD cells. While oeE2F1 strongly enhanced the migratory and invasive capabilities of H1650 and H1975 cells in Transwell assays, these effects were largely neutralized by RAD54L silencing (Fig. 4C, D). These in vitro data suggest that RAD54L is essential for E2F1-mediated cell growth and motility.

The functional significance of the E2F1-RAD54L axis was further validated in vivo using a subcutaneous xenograft model. As expected, E2F1 overexpression led to a rapid increase in tumor volume and weight (Fig. 4E-G). Conversely, knockdown of RAD54L resulted in minimal tumor development. Importantly, the tumorigenic drive provided by E2F1 was significantly impaired in the absence of RAD54L, with the shRAD54L+oeE2F1 group exhibiting significantly smaller and lighter tumors than the shNC+oeE2F1 group (Fig. 4E-G).

Histological analysis of the xenograft tissues provided further mechanistic insight. IHC staining revealed that E2F1 overexpression increased the proliferation marker Ki-67 and decreased the apoptosis marker cleaved Caspase-3 (Fig. 4H, I). Silencing of RAD54L reversed these patterns, leading to a substantial reduction in the Ki-67-positive proliferative index and a robust induction of Caspase-3-mediated apoptosis, even in the presence of elevated E2F1 (Fig. 4H, I). Collectively, these findings establish that RAD54L is an indispensable downstream mediator of the E2F1-driven oncogenic program in LUAD, acting by both promoting proliferation and inhibiting programmed cell death.

To identify key epigenetic regulators in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), we first screened a panel of m5C writers, readers, and erasers using TCGA datasets. Among these, the RNA methyltransferase NSUN2 emerged as the most frequently altered regulator, exhibiting mRNA upregulation in 17% of LUAD cases and 38% across a pan-cancer cohort (Fig. 5A, B). Further analysis of lung cancer subtypes revealed that NSUN2 genetic alterations—predominantly genomic amplifications—were significantly more frequent in LUAD compared to lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) or small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) (Fig. 5C).

We found a robust dose-dependent relationship between NSUN2 copy number and transcript abundance, with amplified cases showing the highest mRNA levels (Fig. 5D). Pearson correlation analysis confirmed a significant positive association between NSUN2 genomic copy number and mRNA expression (r = 0.62, P < 0.001; Fig. 5E), suggesting that genomic amplification is a primary driver of its overexpression. Collectively, these results establish NSUN2 as a clinically relevant oncogene in LUAD, driven by genomic amplification and associated with aggressive disease progression.

We first characterized the cellular role of NSUN2 as an m5C methyltransferase in LUAD. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that NSUN2 is predominantly localized within the nuclei of H1650 cells, a localization consistent with its role in processing precursor mRNA transcripts (Fig. 6A). Stable knockdown of NSUN2 resulted in a marked reduction in nuclear fluorescence intensity, validating our depletion models (Fig. 6A).

To determine if NSUN2 modulates the global m5C status of the LUAD transcriptome, we performed biochemical dot blot assays. Silencing of NSUN2 led to a substantial decrease in global m5C deposition on mRNA, while ectopic overexpression of NSUN2 (oeNSUN2) significantly increased total m5C levels (Fig. 6B). These results indicate that NSUN2 is the principal enzyme responsible for global mRNA m5C modification in these cells.

To map these modifications at higher resolution, we employed transcriptome-wide RNA-Bisulfite sequencing (RNA-BisSeq). Quantitatively, NSUN2 knockdown caused a significant shift in the distribution of methylation levels, with the median m5C level per site dropping from approximately 38% to 18% (Fig. 6C). Metagene mapping showed that m5C sites in LUAD cells are primarily concentrated within the coding sequences (CDS) and the 3′ untranslated regions (3′ UTR) of mRNA transcripts (Fig. 6D). Importantly, NSUN2 depletion resulted in a consistent reduction of methylation density across all transcript regions without significantly altering the overall distribution pattern, suggesting that NSUN2 targets are distributed throughout the transcriptome (Fig. 6D).

Finally, we performed functional annotation of the m5C-modified transcripts identified in LUAD cells. Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway analyses revealed that methylated transcripts were significantly enriched in biological processes essential for tumor maintenance, including cell cycle control, DNA repair, and RNA processing (Fig. 6E). These data establish that NSUN2-mediated m5C modification preferentially targets transcripts involved in genome stability and cell cycle progression, providing a molecular bridge between the m5C epitranscriptome and the E2F1-RAD54L axis.

This multi-omics approach yielded 164 high-confidence transcripts that were m5C-modified, bound by NSUN2, and sensitive to NSUN2 depletion (Fig. 7A). Within this set, we identified a core signature of 37 genes consistently regulated across H1650 cells, with the oncogenic transcription factor E2F1 emerging as a top-ranked candidate (Fig. 7B).

We first sought to determine whether the regulation of the E2F1-RAD54L axis depends on the catalytic activity of NSUN2. Ectopic expression of wild-type NSUN2 (NSUN2-WT) significantly induced the mRNA levels of both E2F1 and RAD54L (Fig. 7C). In contrast, a catalytically inactive NSUN2 mutant (NSUN2-MUT) failed to elicit a similar response, demonstrating that the m5C methyltransferase activity of NSUN2 is essential for the positive regulation of this axis (Fig. 7C). Consistently, siRNA-mediated knockdown of NSUN2 resulted in a concomitant reduction of E2F1 mRNA across H1650 cells (Fig. 7D).

We next investigated the mechanism by which NSUN2 maintains E2F1 mRNA abundance. Given that many m5C-modified RNAs exhibit altered stability, we performed Actinomycin D-based half-life assays. Surprisingly, the decay kinetics of E2F1 mRNA were nearly identical between the control and NSUN2-knockdown groups in H1650 cells (Fig. 7E). These results indicate that NSUN2 does not significantly affect the stability or degradation of E2F1 mRNA, suggesting that the observed reduction in steady-state abundance likely stems from altered transcriptional efficiency or nuclear processing.

Finally, we validated the physical interaction between NSUN2 and its putative targets. RIP-PCR confirmed that NSUN2 specifically binds to E2F1 mRNA, as well as several other candidate transcripts identified in our multi-omics screen (Fig. 7F, G). Among all tested candidates, E2F1 showed one of the highest levels of enrichment in anti-NSUN2 immunoprecipitates (Fig. 7G). Collectively, these findings establish that NSUN2 directly binds and maintains the steady-state mRNA levels of E2F1 in a methyltransferase-dependent manner, identifying E2F1 as the primary epigenetic effector of NSUN2 in LUAD.

NSUN2-mediated m5C modification of the 3′-UTR is required for E2F1 translation

To determine if NSUN2 directly catalyzes the m5C modification of E2F1 mRNA, we performed m5C-RIP-qPCR. We observed significant m5C enrichment on E2F1 transcripts in H1650 cells, which was markedly reduced upon NSUN2 knockdown (Fig. 8A), confirming that NSUN2 is the primary methyltransferase responsible for E2F1 m5C deposition. We next assessed the relationship between NSUN2 catalytic activity and its substrate binding. RIP-qPCR analysis demonstrated that while wild-type NSUN2 (NSUN2-WT) robustly bound to E2F1 mRNA, the catalytic-inactive NSUN2 mutant (NSUN2-MUT; C271A/C321A) exhibited significantly diminished, though not entirely abolished, binding affinity (Fig. 8B). This suggests that while NSUN2 can associate with the E2F1 transcript, its stable interaction and subsequent modification are dependent on its catalytic residues.

To establish the functional consequence of this modification, we utilized luciferase reporters fused to the WT or m5C-site mutant E2F1 3′-UTR. Overexpression of NSUN2-WT significantly enhanced the luciferase activity of the WT 3′-UTR reporter but had no effect on the mutant 3′-UTR reporter (Fig. 8C). Importantly, the NSUN2-MUT protein failed to activate either the WT or the mutant reporter, demonstrating that the catalytic activity of NSUN2 is indispensable for this regulatory effect (Fig. 8C).

Furthermore, we performed loss-of-function studies to confirm these findings. Silencing of NSUN2 resulted in a significant decrease in the luciferase activity of the WT E2F1 3′-UTR reporter (Fig. 8D). In contrast, the activity of the m5C-site mutant reporter was unaffected by NSUN2 depletion (Fig. 8D). These data provide definitive evidence that NSUN2 regulates E2F1 expression through an m5C-dependent mechanism specifically targeting the 3′-UTR. Given our earlier observation that E2F1 mRNA stability was unaffected by NSUN2 (see Fig. 7D), these findings collectively indicate that the NSUN2-mediated m5C modification primarily enhances the translational efficiency of the E2F1 transcript in LUAD.

The m5C writer NSUN2 and reader YBX1 are required for E2F1 recruitment to the RAD54L promoter

Having established that E2F1 transcriptionally activates RAD54L, we explored whether this process is regulated by the m5C RNA methylation machinery. We first assessed the impact of the m5C writer, NSUN2, and the known m5C reader, YBX1, on RAD54L mRNA expression. RT-qPCR analysis in H1650 cells revealed that silencing of either NSUN2 or YBX1 led to a significant reduction in RAD54L transcript levels, comparable to the effect observed with E2F1 knockdown (Fig. 8E). These results suggest that the m5C machinery is essential for the E2F1-mediated transcription of RAD54L.

To test the hypothesis that m5C modification facilitates the physical recruitment of E2F1 to its target promoter, we performed ChIP-qPCR assays under conditions of NSUN2 or YBX1 deficiency. In control cells (shNC), E2F1 exhibited robust binding to the RAD54L promoter region (Fig. 8F). Strikingly, depletion of the m5C writer NSUN2 almost entirely abolished E2F1 occupancy at the RAD54L promoter, with enrichment levels dropping to near-basal IgG levels (Fig. 8F, left). Similarly, knockdown of the m5C reader YBX1 significantly impaired E2F1 binding to the same promoter region (Fig. 8F, right). Collectively, these data provide mechanistic evidence that the m5C regulatory axis (NSUN2 and YBX1) is a prerequisite for the effective recruitment of E2F1 to the RAD54L locus, thereby bridging epigenetic RNA modification with transcriptional control in LUAD.

Discussion

The epitranscriptomic regulation of gene expression has emerged as a fundamental driver of cancer pathogenesis. Among the various RNA modifications, m5C remains relatively undercharacterized compared to m6A, particularly regarding its target specificity and functional consequences in specific tumor contexts. In this study, we elucidate a novel oncogenic axis in LUAD, demonstrating that the m5C RNA methyltransferase NSUN2 drives tumor progression by hypermethylating the 3′-UTR of E2F1 mRNA (Fig. 9). This modification, recognized by the reader protein YBX1, enhances E2F1 translational efficiency, which subsequently hyperactivates the transcription of the DNA repair factor RAD54L.

Our identification of NSUN2 as a genomically amplified oncogene in LUAD aligns with and expands upon previous literature. Consistent with our findings, earlier studies have reported that NSUN2 is overexpressed in various malignancies, where it promotes proliferation and metastasis [11, 21–23]. However, our study highlights critical differences and novelties. While prior research frequently attributed NSUN2 upregulation to transcriptional transactivation (e.g., by c-Myc) or post-transcriptional stabilization [24, 25], our pan-cancer and LUAD-specific TCGA analyses establish that somatic gene amplification is the primary driver of NSUN2 overexpression in lung cancer. Furthermore, while previous mapping of m5C landscapes has primarily been conducted in standard cell lines, our transcriptome-wide RNA-BisSeq specifically profiles EGFR-mutant LUAD cells. We observed that NSUN2-dependent m5C sites are highly enriched in transcripts governing the cell cycle and homologous recombination, providing a specific functional context for NSUN2 in driving genomic instability in lung cancer.

A major conceptual advance of this study lies in unraveling the epitranscriptomic regulation of the transcription factor E2F1. E2F1 is canonically known to be regulated at the post-translational level, predominantly through its sequestration by the unphosphorylated retinoblastoma (Rb) protein, or via ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation [26, 27]. Here, we introduce a fundamentally distinct, RNA-centric layer of E2F1 regulation. We discovered that NSUN2 deposits m5C modifications on the 3′-UTR of E2F1 mRNA. Interestingly, contrary to previous reports suggesting that m5C generally stabilizes mRNAs (such as p21 or HDGF in other cancer models) [28, 29], our Actinomycin D decay assays unequivocally demonstrated that NSUN2 depletion does not alter E2F1 mRNA half-life. Instead, dual-luciferase reporter assays using wild-type and catalytic-dead NSUN2 mutants revealed that m5C modification significantly enhances the translational efficiency of E2F1. This difference underscores the context-dependent nature of m5C: rather than serving universally as an mRNA stabilizer, m5C in the 3′-UTR can act as a direct translational enhancer, heavily dependent on the recruitment of specific reader proteins such as YBX1.

Furthermore, we mapped the downstream functional consequences of this hyperactive E2F1 signaling to the DNA repair gene RAD54L. RAD54L is a well-known core component of the homologous recombination (HR) repair pathway, functioning as a motor protein that facilitates DNA strand exchange [20, 30]. While previous genomic studies have identified RAD54L mutations in selected cancers, its specific transcriptional regulation and dependency in LUAD remained opaque. Our ChIP-qPCR and luciferase reporter data establish RAD54L as a direct, transcriptionally activated target of E2F1. Importantly, our in vivo and in vitro rescue experiments (epistasis analysis) provide striking evidence that E2F1-induced tumorigenesis is almost entirely abrogated in the absence of RAD54L. This differs from the classical paradigm wherein E2F1 drives oncogenesis solely through G1/S phase cyclins [17, 31]; instead, in EGFR-mutant LUAD, E2F1 heavily relies on upregulating HR machinery (RAD54L) to manage replication stress and prevent apoptosis, thereby driving tumor growth.

The primary innovation of this study is the delineation of a continuous, multi-tiered molecular axis connecting RNA epigenetics (m5C) directly to DNA repair mechanisms (HR). We pinpointed the exact catalytic dependency of NSUN2 in this process and functionally mapped the specific m5C locus on the E2F1 3′-UTR, defining a new translational regulatory paradigm for this classic oncogene. Despite these advances, our study has certain limitations. First, while we confirmed the role of the YBX1 reader protein in facilitating E2F1 recruitment, the precise structural mechanism by which YBX1 promotes ribosome assembly or translation initiation on the E2F1 transcript requires further biochemical characterization via techniques such as cryo-EM or Ribo-seq. Second, our in vivo models relied on subcutaneous xenografts; orthotopic or genetically engineered mouse models (GEMMs) with intact immune systems would be necessary to fully evaluate how the NSUN2-E2F1-RAD54L axis influences the tumor microenvironment and metastasis. Finally, we focused predominantly on EGFR-mutant LUAD; it remains to be determined whether this m5C axis operates similarly in KRAS-driven or ALK-rearranged lung cancers.

Conclusion

In summary, this study uncovers a novel epitranscriptomic mechanism fueling lung adenocarcinoma progression. We demonstrate that genomic amplification of the RNA methyltransferase NSUN2 leads to the aberrant m5C modification of E2F1 mRNA at its 3′-UTR. This epitranscriptomic mark does not alter transcript stability but instead drives robust translational amplification via the reader protein YBX1. The resultant accumulation of E2F1 protein hyperactivates the transcription of the DNA repair factor RAD54L, empowering LUAD cells to sustain unchecked proliferation and evade apoptosis. By bridging RNA m5C methylation with the transcriptional control of DNA homologous recombination, our findings highlight the NSUN2-E2F1-RAD54L axis as a critical vulnerability in LUAD, offering a strong rationale for developing epitranscriptomic-targeted therapies for advanced lung cancer.
