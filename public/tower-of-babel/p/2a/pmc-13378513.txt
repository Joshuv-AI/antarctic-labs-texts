Cervical Collar in Adult Trauma: Evidence, Controversies, and Clinical Decision-Making
Authors: Saied Besharaty, Shahab Sheikhalishahi, Hesameddin Jafarinasab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCervical collars have traditionally been applied routinely in adult trauma to reduce the risk of secondary cervical spinal cord injury. Increasing evidence, however, supports selective spinal motion restriction and earlier collar removal when instability has been adequately excluded. This narrative review evaluates current evidence on collar application, avoidance, clearance, and continued use after confirmed cervical fracture. A structured literature search was conducted across major biomedical databases and guideline sources, with the synthesis guided by the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles. In blunt trauma, collar use should be based on clinical risk, examination reliability, mechanism of injury, and phase of care. Routine collaring is generally unsupported in isolated penetrating trauma and may delay time-critical management. In evaluable patients, validated decision rules and high-quality computed tomography (CT) support early clearance when appropriate. In adults with impaired consciousness, collar removal after a negative high-quality CT should be individualized according to neurological findings, clinical context, examination reliability, institutional protocols, and the presence or absence of radio-clinical discrepancy. After fracture confirmation, continued collar use should be individualized according to stability, frailty, treatment goals, and tolerance. Collar-related harms should be incorporated into repeated reassessment. The proposed Four-Domain Trauma Collar Framework organizes these decisions across application, clearance, continuation, and de-escalation but remains a conceptual, unvalidated model.

Introduction

Cervical spine injury is relatively uncommon after trauma, yet its consequences may be severe. Delayed recognition of clinically important instability or inadequate early stabilization can result in neurological deterioration and long-term disability. Contemporary epidemiological evidence indicates that traumatic spinal cord injury remains a substantial individual and societal burden, with cervical-level injuries contributing disproportionately to functional impairment.1,2 Within this context, cervical collars became embedded in prehospital and emergency trauma care as a default intervention. Their intended role was to reduce secondary spinal cord injury until clinically significant cervical spine instability could be excluded.3,4 However, the widespread routine use of cervical collars has increasingly exceeded the strength of the supporting evidence.

A central conceptual limitation is the frequent conflation of cervical collar use with spinal immobilization. Modern trauma care increasingly favors the broader concept of spinal motion restriction, which more accurately describes the achievable clinical objective.3,5 Rigid collars may reduce cervical motion, but they do not produce complete immobilization. Their clinical value depends on patient characteristics, injury mechanism, phase of care, and competing risks.3,5,6 This distinction has practical implications. The relevant question is no longer whether all trauma patients should be immobilized. Rather, it is whether a specific patient requires collar-based motion restriction for a defined indication and a limited duration. Recent implementation studies further suggest that selective immobilization protocols require training, audit, and system-level reinforcement to achieve consistent practice change.7,8

Recent evidence has reinforced this reassessment. Evidence syntheses in blunt trauma have not demonstrated a clear reduction in neurological deterioration, spinal injury, or mortality with routine prehospital or emergency department immobilization. In contrast, collar use has been associated with pain, discomfort, and device-related complications.3 Danish evidence- and consensus-based guidance issued a weak recommendation against routine rigid-collar use in ABCDE-stable adult trauma patients.5 In penetrating trauma, the evidence is more consistent. The Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma (EAST) recommends against routine spinal immobilization in adult penetrating trauma because neurological benefit has not been demonstrated and mortality may be increased.9,10

Uncertainty also persists after arrival to definitive care. In awake and evaluable blunt trauma patients, validated clinical decision rules, selective clearance strategies, and modern CT-based pathways have enabled more targeted imaging and earlier collar removal.7,11–14 In adults with impaired consciousness after blunt trauma, the key debate concerns when a negative high-quality CT can support collar removal without adjunct MRI. This decision should account for CT adequacy, neurological findings, clinical context, examination reliability, institutional protocols, and any radio-clinical discrepancy, while balancing the risk of missed instability against the consequences of low-value imaging and prolonged immobilization.15–18 After a cervical fracture is confirmed, collar use becomes a treatment decision rather than a clearance measure. The optimal strategy and duration remain poorly standardized, particularly in older adults.19–22 Collar-related harms further complicate management.23,24 Emerging artificial intelligence (AI) may improve fracture detection and workflow triage but is not validated for collar prescription or removal.25 This review therefore examines when a cervical collar should be applied, avoided, removed, or continued in adult trauma care. The review is intended for emergency physicians, trauma surgeons, spine and orthopedic surgeons, neurosurgeons, critical care clinicians, and prehospital care providers involved in adult trauma management.

Materials and Methods

This narrative review examined the role of cervical collars in adult trauma care, focusing on collar application, avoidance, clearance, continued use after confirmed cervical fracture, collar-related harms, and emerging decision-support technologies. The six SANRA domains were addressed by explaining the clinical importance of the topic and stating explicit review objectives; describing the literature search and source-selection approach; supporting major statements with appropriate references; distinguishing evidence-supported conclusions from interpretive or conceptual propositions; and presenting the evidence in relation to clinically meaningful outcomes, including neurological safety, collar clearance, treatment burden, functional outcomes, and collar-related harms. This article was not designed or reported as a formal systematic review or meta-analysis.26,27

A structured literature search was performed in MEDLINE/PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science Core Collection, and relevant guideline repositories and professional society websites. Searches combined terms related to cervical spine trauma, cervical collars, spinal immobilization, spinal motion restriction, prehospital care, emergency department and intensive care unit clearance, computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), obtunded trauma patients, penetrating trauma, cervical fractures, odontoid fractures, nonoperative management, collar complications, artificial intelligence (AI), and machine learning (ML). No strict publication-date restriction was applied, although greater emphasis was placed on recent guidelines, systematic or scoping reviews, prospective or large observational studies, and clinically relevant evidence from approximately the last 10–12 years. Earlier landmark studies were included selectively when they provided essential historical, diagnostic, or conceptual context.

Sources were eligible if they were English-language publications involving adult or predominantly adult populations and directly addressed cervical collar application, spinal motion restriction, cervical spine clearance, confirmed cervical fracture management, collar-related harms, or AI-assisted cervical trauma assessment. Eligible source types included guidelines, consensus statements, evidence syntheses, diagnostic studies, and relevant prospective or retrospective clinical studies. Pediatric-only studies, noncervical spinal conditions without separable cervical data, AI studies unrelated to cervical trauma, duplicate reports, and conference abstracts lacking sufficient methodological or outcome information were excluded. Nontraumatic or chronic neck-pain populations were excluded from the primary trauma-focused synthesis; selected studies were retained only when used to contextualize rehabilitation-related outcomes or future research priorities.

Records were screened by title, abstract, and, when necessary, full text. Evidence was synthesized narratively across predefined domains: prehospital blunt trauma, penetrating trauma, collar clearance in evaluable and obtunded patients, MRI after negative high-quality CT, collar treatment after confirmed cervical fracture, geriatric odontoid fractures, collar-related complications, and AI or ML applications in cervical trauma assessment. Methodological appraisal was informal and was used to guide interpretation rather than to generate formal study-level ratings or certainty-of-evidence grading. Formal duplicate screening, protocol registration, risk-of-bias scoring, and PRISMA-style study accounting were not undertaken. Key studies and guideline documents informing each thematic domain are summarized as an evidence map in Supplementary Table S1.

Major guideline, consensus, and best-practice positions relevant to cervical collar application, avoidance, and clearance in adult trauma care are summarized in Table 1. To distinguish clinically different questions, the narrative synthesis was organized into six controversy domains: (a) routine versus selective collar application after blunt trauma; (b) routine versus selective use after penetrating trauma; (c) clinical and CT-based clearance in awake and evaluable adults; (d) CT-alone versus selective CT-plus-MRI clearance in adults with impaired consciousness; (e) continued collar use as treatment after a confirmed cervical fracture; and (f) management of odontoid fractures in older adults. These domains represent different phases and populations and should not be conflated: evidence concerning initial collar application does not necessarily determine subsequent clearance or treatment decisions. Prehospital Blunt Trauma: Selective Spinal Motion Restriction

Rationale and Evolving Terminology

Cervical collar application has long been a visible component of prehospital trauma care. Historically, patients with blunt trauma were often managed under a precautionary model of presumed cervical spine instability. Rigid collar placement was combined with other immobilization measures to reduce the theoretical risk of secondary neurological injury during extrication, transport, and early emergency department evaluation.3,5,28,29 This approach was simple and operationally attractive. However, its widespread adoption preceded robust evidence showing improved neurological or survival outcomes from routine immobilization in unselected blunt trauma populations.3,4,32,33

A key limitation of the traditional paradigm is the assumption that collar placement is equivalent to spinal immobilization. Contemporary trauma care increasingly favors spinal motion restriction, which better reflects the achievable clinical goal.5,28,29,34 A rigid collar alone does not fully immobilize the cervical spine. Its effect also varies according to collar fit, adjunctive devices, patient movement, transport conditions, and provider technique.35,36 The clinical objective is therefore not absolute immobilization. It is selective, proportionate motion restriction for patients with credible risk of unstable cervical spine injury.

Evidence Base and Selective Application

The evidentiary basis for universal collar application in blunt trauma remains limited. Evidence syntheses have not demonstrated a consistent reduction in neurological deterioration, missed unstable injury, or mortality attributable to routine prehospital or early emergency department immobilization.3,4,32 At the same time, collar placement is not a neutral intervention. Reported burdens include pain, discomfort, respiratory restriction, impaired airway access, increased intracranial pressure in susceptible patients, pressure-related injury, and delayed clearance.3,23,36

Accordingly, modern recommendations have moved away from indiscriminate immobilization. Current practice increasingly supports selective spinal motion restriction based on clinical risk, examination reliability, mechanism of injury, and extrication context.5,28,29,34 Implementation remains challenging. Scoping and observational data suggest that protocol adoption depends on training, decision-support clarity, provider confidence, documentation, and system-level audit.8,37,38

Risk stratification is central to this approach. Low-risk blunt trauma patients are typically alert, cooperative, hemodynamically stable, and clinically examinable. They have no midline cervical pain or tenderness, focal neurological signs, intoxication, altered mental status, distracting injury, or visible spinal deformity.7,11–13,39 In such patients, routine collar placement may offer limited marginal benefit while exposing many patients to avoidable discomfort and downstream imaging or immobilization cascades.3,5,23,36 High-risk patients include those with impaired consciousness, intoxication, unreliable examination, neurological symptoms or deficits, midline spinal tenderness, major distracting injuries, concerning high-energy mechanisms, or extrication circumstances in which uncontrolled spinal motion is likely.7,40–45 In these patients, spinal motion restriction, including a correctly fitted cervical collar when feasible, remains clinically defensible until unstable injury is excluded.

Although the National Emergency X-Radiography Utilization Study (NEXUS) criteria and the Canadian C-Spine Rule (CCR) were developed for imaging decisions in alert and stable trauma patients, their underlying logic supports selective risk assessment.11–13,45 Both instruments have demonstrated high sensitivity for identifying clinically important cervical spine injury when applied to appropriately selected, alert, and clinically evaluable blunt trauma populations.11–13 Prehospital adaptations of clinical clearance and selective immobilization protocols have similarly aimed to reduce unnecessary immobilization while preserving safety for patients with plausible cervical spine injury.7,42–45

Remaining Uncertainties

Important uncertainties remain. Available studies differ in case mix, provider training, immobilization protocols, collar type, adjunctive devices, outcome definitions, and follow-up methods.3,4,8,32,37,38 Neurological deterioration directly attributable to prehospital cervical motion is rare and difficult to measure. Many studies therefore rely on process outcomes rather than patient-centered endpoints. Future research should separate collar use from broader spinal motion restriction, stratify patients by clinical risk, and evaluate harms as well as benefits.

Penetrating Trauma: Limited Role for Routine Collar Use

In contrast to blunt trauma, isolated penetrating trauma is a setting in which routine cervical collar application is difficult to justify. The immediate threats are usually hemorrhage, airway compromise, and rapid physiological deterioration rather than occult mechanically unstable cervical spine injury.5,9,10,46–48 Collar placement may therefore distract from time-critical priorities. It may also complicate airway access, delay transport or operative hemorrhage control, and provide little plausible neurological benefit for most patients.9,10,28,49

Available evidence suggests that unstable cervical spine injury after isolated penetrating trauma is uncommon, particularly in neurologically intact patients and after stab wounds. In penetrating neck trauma cohorts, unstable injury has been rare and is usually accompanied by neurological deficit, altered mental status, or a high-risk ballistic trajectory.50–52 Large database and trauma-center analyses have also associated prehospital spinal immobilization with increased mortality in penetrating trauma, while reporting very low rates of potentially preventable neurological deterioration.10,53 These data informed EAST guidance, which recommends against routine spinal immobilization or spinal motion restriction in adult penetrating trauma.9,54 Scandinavian evidence- and consensus-based guidance also supports a selective rather than routine approach to spinal stabilization in adult trauma patients.5

This position does not imply that collar use is never appropriate. Selective motion restriction may be considered when there is overt neurological deficit, depressed consciousness, inability to participate in examination, direct spinal pain or deformity, or a missile or stab trajectory that raises specific concern for vertebral column disruption.5,9,36,52,55 Even in these scenarios, collar placement should not supersede airway management, hemorrhage control, resuscitation, or rapid transfer to definitive care. In isolated penetrating trauma, the default approach should therefore shift from routine collaring to context-dependent motion restriction when clinical features suggest a credible risk of unstable spinal injury.

Cervical Collar Clearance in Awake and Evaluable Patients

Clinical Assessment and Decision Rules

In awake, alert, and clinically evaluable blunt trauma patients, cervical collar clearance should begin with a structured neurological and cervical spine examination. Reflexive continuation of immobilization should be avoided when the clinical risk is low. An evaluable patient can reliably report pain, cooperate with examination, and perform active cervical range of motion when appropriate. The clinical objective is to identify the small subset with clinically important cervical spine injury while reducing unnecessary imaging, prolonged collar use, and immobilization-related morbidity.30,56

Validated clinical decision instruments support selective clearance. The NEXUS criteria and CCR were developed to identify low-risk blunt trauma patients in whom clinically important cervical spine injury can be excluded without imaging.11–13,57 Although these tools differ in structure, both depend on a reliable examination. Low-risk assessment requires absence of midline cervical tenderness, neurological deficit, intoxication, altered mental status, and distracting injury.39,58 CCR also incorporates age, mechanism, low-risk factors, and active neck rotation. This links clearance to both risk profile and functional examination.13,39,58

CT-Based Clearance

Current best-practice guidance supports collar removal without imaging in an awake, asymptomatic adult trauma patient with a normal neurological examination, no high-risk mechanism, free cervical motion, and no neck tenderness.30 When imaging is indicated, CT is the primary modality for adult blunt cervical spine evaluation. It has largely replaced plain radiography because of superior diagnostic performance and workflow reliability.14,31,59,60 Large prospective trauma data indicate that a negative high-quality CT scan has a very high negative predictive value for clinically significant cervical spine injury.59–61 Persistent focal neurological abnormality, inadequate CT quality, or radio-clinical discrepancy should prompt specialist input and selective MRI rather than routine prolonged immobilization.14,31,62

Persistent Pain After Negative CT

Persistent neck pain after a negative CT remains a frequent practical dilemma. Available evidence does not support routine prolonged rigid-collar use solely for pain in an otherwise neurologically intact, evaluable patient with negative high-quality CT imaging. Pain should instead be interpreted in clinical context. Persistent midline pain may warrant reassessment, analgesia, documentation of neurological status, and individualized follow-up. It should not automatically mandate continued immobilization, flexion-extension radiographs, or MRI when neurological findings, concerning CT features, and radio-clinical discordance are absent.14,31,62,63 In evaluable patients, collar clearance is therefore best conceptualized as a risk-stratified process that integrates clinical decision rules, CT when indicated, and selective escalation when clinical or radiological concern persists.

Cervical Spine Clearance in Obtunded Patients: CT Alone versus Adjunct MRI

The Clearance Dilemma

Cervical spine clearance in obtunded blunt trauma patients remains a debated area of trauma collar management. The clinical examination is incomplete or unreliable. Unlike awake patients, obtunded individuals cannot consistently report pain, paresthesia, or midline tenderness. Active range-of-motion assessment is also not feasible. Clinicians must therefore balance two competing risks. The first is the low probability of occult unstable injury after high-quality CT. The second is the potential harm of missed instability, unnecessary MRI, and prolonged collar immobilization.15–18

CT-Alone Clearance

The CT-alone strategy is based on the high diagnostic performance of modern multidetector CT for clinically significant osseous and unstable cervical spine injuries. Evidence syntheses and prospective trauma data support a very high negative predictive value when CT is technically adequate and interpreted by experienced radiologists.15,16,59,64,65 EAST conditionally recommends collar removal after a negative high-quality cervical spine CT alone in adults with impaired consciousness after blunt trauma.15 However, this conditional recommendation should not be interpreted as indicating that a negative CT automatically permits collar removal in every case. The decision should incorporate CT quality and technical adequacy, neurological findings, the broader clinical context, examination reliability, institutional protocols, and the presence or absence of radio-clinical discrepancy.31 The rationale is not that CT identifies every soft-tissue abnormality, but that clinically important unstable injuries requiring intervention are rarely missed when modern multidetector CT is technically adequate and concordant with the clinical and neurological findings.15,16,66

Adjunct MRI and Residual Uncertainty

The CT-plus-MRI strategy reflects a more conservative approach to residual diagnostic uncertainty. MRI may detect ligamentous edema, disc injury, epidural hematoma, spinal cord abnormality, and other soft-tissue findings that are occult on CT.62,67–69 However, the clinical significance of many MRI-only findings remains uncertain. MRI frequently identifies stable or nonspecific abnormalities that do not change management. It may also increase collar duration, consultations, delayed clearance, and resource use.17,68,69 Recent data in obtunded low-energy trauma similarly suggest that adjunct MRI after negative CT rarely identifies injuries requiring surgery, while increasing intensive care and ventilator days.66

This concern has generated the concept of low-value findings in cervical spine clearance. Adjunct MRI may identify abnormalities that appear radiologically relevant but are not mechanically unstable In critically injured patients, MRI also introduces logistical risks. These include transport away from monitored environments, prolonged supine positioning, limited airway access, compatibility issues, cost, and delays in mobilization or nursing care.70,71

Pragmatic Interpretation

Institutional variability persists because definitions of obtundation differ, CT quality varies, and tolerance for missed injury differs across trauma, spine, radiology, and critical care teams.14–18 Newer meta-analytic evidence has also challenged overly simple CT-only conclusions by showing that MRI may detect additional injuries after negative CT, although treatment-changing findings are less common.18,71 A pragmatic approach is therefore appropriate. A negative high-quality CT may support collar removal in appropriately selected adults with impaired consciousness, but it should not be treated as an automatic stand-alone authorization for clearance. The decision should integrate CT adequacy, neurological findings, the clinical context, examination reliability, and institutional protocols. Continued immobilization, specialist consultation, repeat imaging, or MRI should be considered when CT is inadequate or equivocal, focal neurological abnormalities persist, ligamentous instability is suspected, or radio-clinical discrepancy remains.

Collar Treatment After Confirmed Cervical Fracture

From Clearance to Treatment

Once a cervical fracture has been confirmed, the clinical question changes. The issue is no longer whether the collar can be removed, but whether external immobilization is an appropriate treatment strategy. Collar continuation after diagnosis is therefore a therapeutic decision rather than a clearance measure. It may aim to support fracture healing, maintain alignment, reduce pain, or provide temporary protection while stability is assessed. The appropriateness of this strategy depends on fracture morphology, mechanical stability, neurological status, ligamentous integrity, age, frailty, immobilization tolerance, and surgical feasibility.19,72–76

Stability, Treatment Intent, and Fracture Pattern

Nonoperative collar treatment is most appropriate for fractures judged to be mechanically stable or acceptably stable after specialist assessment. Examples include selected nondisplaced or minimally displaced upper cervical fractures, certain axis body fractures, stable Hangman’s fractures, isolated nondisplaced unilateral facet fractures, and some minor subaxial compression or posterior element injuries without neurological deficit or radiological instability.77–82 In contrast, fracture-dislocations, translational injuries, progressive deformity, marked displacement, neurological compression, or clear posterior ligamentous complex disruption usually require operative stabilization or more rigid external fixation rather than collar treatment alone.73–75,79

The intended role of the collar is often incompletely defined. In some patients, it is prescribed as a stabilizing orthosis to limit motion across the fracture site. In others, it functions mainly as an analgesic support and behavioral reminder while biological healing or fibrous stabilization occurs.19–21,72,83–85 This distinction is especially important in older adults. In this group, radiographic union may be less important than function, comfort, avoidance of neurological deterioration, and minimization of immobilization-related harm.22,86 Odontoid fracture studies show that nonunion is common after collar treatment in geriatric patients. Stable fibrous nonunion may still be acceptable in selected patients when pain is low, function is preserved, and dynamic instability is absent.85

Duration and Reassessment

Duration of collar use remains poorly standardized. Protocols vary by fracture type, institution, surgeon preference, imaging schedule, and patient tolerance.19,72 In nonoperatively managed cervical fractures, orthotic clearance has been reported at a mean of approximately 70 days after discharge. Follow-up imaging was frequently obtained, although later imaging often had limited influence on management decisions.72 In geriatric type II odontoid fracture cohorts, rigid collar protocols of approximately 12 weeks are commonly reported. However, union rates may remain low, and functional outcomes may still be acceptable in selected patients.85,86

Current evidence highlights the central limitation of this field. Most immobilization protocols are supported by retrospective, heterogeneous, and variably reported studies.72 Collar type, duration, adherence, imaging endpoints, functional outcomes, and adverse events are inconsistently described. Duration should therefore not be treated as a fixed ritual. A more defensible approach is individualized reassessment based on fracture pattern, stability, neurological status, frailty, tolerance, early radiographic behavior, and the balance between expected mechanical benefit and immobilization-related harm.19,23,72 Common fracture scenarios in which collar continuation may be considered after confirmed cervical fracture are summarized in Table 2. Ongoing Collar Use After Confirmed Cervical Fracture

Odontoid Fractures in Older Adults: Union, Function, and Collar Tolerance

Clinical Context and Competing Risks

Odontoid fractures in older adults represent a useful model for the broader uncertainty surrounding post-traumatic cervical collar use. These injuries are common in geriatric trauma, often follow low-energy falls, and occur in patients with limited physiological reserve. Bone quality, comorbidity burden, cognitive status, baseline function, and frailty may be more prognostically important than fracture morphology alone.83,87–90 Type II odontoid fractures are particularly controversial. They are anatomically prone to nonunion, yet the clinical relevance of radiographic nonunion in older adults remains uncertain.91 Management must therefore balance mechanical stability, expected healing, functional independence, treatment tolerance, and competing mortality risks.

Halo, Hard Collar, and Soft Collar Treatment

The comparison between halo immobilization and hard cervical collar treatment illustrates this tension. Halo fixation provides greater external restriction than a collar. Its use in older adults is limited by morbidity, pin complications, pulmonary compromise, dysphagia, reduced mobility, and poor tolerance.20,23,92 Comparative evidence suggests that failure rates may be similar in common geriatric type II fracture scenarios, while halo treatment is associated with higher complication rates.20 This supports a restrained role for halo use in frail patients, especially when the main goal is radiographic union rather than neurological preservation.

The distinction between hard and soft collars is also clinically relevant. Rigid collars have traditionally been used to promote bony union. However, geriatric cohorts show high nonunion rates despite hard-collar treatment, often without corresponding neurological decline or unacceptable functional outcome.21,84,85,93 Comparative data on hard versus soft collars have not demonstrated a clear mortality advantage after adjustment for age, and nonunion remains common in both groups.21 More recent multi-institutional data also support fibrous nonunion as an acceptable outcome in selected conservatively managed patients, provided that dynamic instability and neurological deterioration are absent.94

Union versus Patient-Centered Outcome

A central unresolved question is whether union should remain the primary endpoint. Traditional algorithms often favored surgery or more rigid immobilization when osseous union was the dominant goal.95 More recent evidence suggests that fracture union and clinical outcome may be weakly associated in older adults. Carefully selected patients with fibrous nonunion, near-anatomic alignment, no dynamic instability, and a normal neurological examination may do well without further intervention.94 Prospective comparative data also suggest broadly similar clinical outcomes between surgical and conservative treatment, and support prioritizing function and quality of life over radiographic union alone.22 Newer meta-analytic data continue to show higher fusion after surgery, but this must be weighed against complications, length of stay, frailty, and patient goals.86

The Duration of External Neck Stabilisation (DENS) trial protocol further illustrates the uncertainty in this field. Even hard collar versus no collar is being tested in older or frail adults, with quality of life rather than union as the primary endpoint.96 In this population, collar prescription should be individualized according to stability, neurological status, frailty, life expectancy, dysphagia or skin-risk burden, and patient priorities.

Collar-Related Complications and Practical Implications

Pressure injury is among the most visible and preventable harms. Risk increases with prolonged wear time, critical illness, immobility, mechanical ventilation, impaired perfusion, poor nutrition, and reduced ability to report discomfort.97,98 Collar design and fit influence interface pressure, skin hydration, humidity, and comfort, supporting careful sizing, skin protection, and device-specific collar care.99 In ICU populations, these risks are amplified by sedation, vasopressor use, delayed clearance, and high nursing dependency.100,101 Dysphagia and respiratory complications are especially relevant in older adults and patients with traumatic brain injury or cervical spine injury. Collar restriction may impair swallowing mechanics and increase aspiration or respiratory risk.24,102 In geriatric trauma cohorts, collar placement has been associated with higher rates of dysphagia and respiratory failure, supporting swallow assessment when collar use is prolonged or when frailty, neurological impairment, or aspiration risk is present.24

Raised ICP is a distinct concern in patients with traumatic brain injury. Physiological studies suggest that rigid collar application can increase cerebrospinal fluid pressure, ICP, optic nerve sheath diameter, or internal jugular venous dimensions, plausibly through impaired venous outflow.103–106 Although clinical relevance varies by baseline intracranial compliance, unnecessary collar persistence should be avoided once cervical instability has been reasonably excluded. Airway access and functional recovery are also practical concerns. Semi-rigid and rigid collars reduce mouth opening and may worsen intubating conditions, so emergency airway management may require partial collar removal with manual in-line stabilization.107,108 In frail older patients, prolonged collar use may also contribute to discomfort, delirium, immobility, poor sleep, and reduced rehabilitation participation. More immobilization should not be assumed to be safer; collar use should remain indication-specific, time-limited, and frequently reassessed.

Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning in Cervical Trauma Assessment

AI and ML are emerging adjuncts in cervical trauma assessment. Their current role remains diagnostic rather than prescriptive. Most studies focus on automated detection or localization of cervical spine fractures on CT. Others evaluate radiographic fracture detection, fracture classification, or broader musculoskeletal fracture algorithms.25,109 These tools are clinically attractive because cervical trauma imaging is time-sensitive, fractures may be subtle, and delayed recognition can affect clearance, consultation, and immobilization pathways.110–115

Current evidence supports AI mainly as a triage, flagging, or concurrent-reader tool. It should not replace expert radiological interpretation or clinical judgment.25,109 Cervical spine–specific studies report promising diagnostic performance, but sensitivity, specificity, false-positive burden, and detection of clinically unstable injuries vary across datasets, model architectures, imaging modalities, and reference standards. Challenge-based and external-performance studies further emphasize that model accuracy may change when algorithms are tested outside the development environment.116 Broader fracture-detection evidence also supports potential diagnostic value, while highlighting persistent concerns about external validation and generalizability.109

These limitations reflect wider translational challenges in medical imaging AI. Important barriers include dataset shift, spectrum bias, incomplete prospective evaluation, workflow integration, unclear accountability, and uncertain effects on patient-centered outcomes.117–119 Therefore, AI should not currently be viewed as a collar-prescription or collar-clearance engine. A positive or negative algorithmic output does not determine whether a collar should be applied, removed, continued, or de-escalated. Collar decisions require integration of imaging findings with neurological status, mechanism of injury, examination reliability, fracture stability, frailty, treatment goals, and tolerance of immobilization.120–122

Future systems may combine AI-assisted fracture detection with validated clinical decision rules, CT quality metrics, injury-stability classification, frailty-aware risk prediction, and harm monitoring. Before such systems can influence collar pathways, they will require external validation, prospective clinical evaluation, transparent reporting, and evidence that they reduce missed injuries or unnecessary immobilization without increasing downstream harm.123–125

The 4D Trauma Collar Framework: A Practical Conceptual Model

The 4D Trauma Collar Framework is proposed as a conceptual decision pathway for organizing cervical collar use in adult trauma care. It is not intended to function as a validated algorithm, formal guideline, or substitute for local trauma protocols. Rather, it structures collar-related decisions into four recurring steps: whether to apply, clear, continue, or de-escalate the collar (Figure 2). The 4D Trauma Collar Framework: a conceptual decision pathway for adult trauma care. The framework organizes cervical collar decision-making into four recurring steps: deciding whether to apply, clear, continue, or de-escalate the collar. It is intended to support indication-specific, time-limited, and harm-aware clinical reasoning. It should not be interpreted as a validated algorithm, formal guideline, or substitute for local trauma protocols, specialist judgment, or individualized patient care.

Existing decision pathways address narrower, phase-specific clinical questions. The NEXUS criteria and Canadian C-Spine Rule identify alert and clinically evaluable blunt trauma patients who may not require imaging, whereas CT-centered protocols, EAST guidance, and Western Trauma Association evidence primarily inform cervical spine clearance after imaging in evaluable or obtunded patients.11–13,15,30,31 These approaches do not generally extend to whether a collar should subsequently be continued as fracture treatment or de-escalated because of device-related harms.23 The additive purpose of the 4D framework is therefore longitudinal and organizational: it links initial collar application, diagnostic clearance, therapeutic continuation, and harm-triggered de-escalation across the continuum of trauma care. It is intended to complement, rather than replace, validated clinical decision rules, imaging pathways, specialist assessment, or local institutional protocols.

D1: Decide to Apply

Refers to selective collar use when unstable cervical spine injury is plausible, particularly in blunt trauma patients with unreliable examination, neurological deficit, midline tenderness, deformity, or high-risk mechanism. In isolated penetrating trauma, routine collaring should generally be avoided unless neurological deficit, inability to examine the patient, or trajectory-specific concern suggests possible spinal injury.7,9

D2: Decide to Clear

Emphasizes timely collar removal when clinically important instability has been excluded. In low-risk evaluable patients, clinical clearance may be sufficient. When imaging is indicated, high-quality CT is usually the primary test, while MRI should be reserved for neurological deficit, radio-clinical discrepancy, inadequate CT, or suspected ligamentous instability.11,12,15,16,31,59,62

D3: Decide to Continue

After a cervical fracture is confirmed, collar continuation becomes a treatment decision rather than a clearance measure. Ongoing use should be individualized according to fracture morphology, presumed stability, neurological status, nonoperative treatment intent, frailty, adherence, and tolerance. Duration should not be dictated by habit or fixed timelines alone.19–22

D4: Decide to De-Escalate

Continued immobilization should be reassessed when harms begin to outweigh expected benefit. Dysphagia, pressure injury, delirium or frailty burden, raised intracranial pressure concerns, poor adherence, and limited functional benefit should prompt reconsideration of collar type, duration, or discontinuation.21–23 The 4D framework therefore reframes collar use as selective, time-limited, and harm-aware, while remaining explicitly conceptual rather than prescriptive.

Discussion

Evidence Supported by Current Literature

Current evidence supports a more selective approach to cervical collar use in adult trauma care. Routine collaring after blunt trauma has not shown consistent evidence of reducing neurological deterioration, missed unstable injury, or mortality. Contemporary guidance instead favors selective spinal motion restriction based on clinical risk, examination reliability, and mechanism of injury.3,5,34 In penetrating trauma, the evidence is more consistent. Routine immobilization is generally discouraged because neurological benefit has not been demonstrated and time-critical priorities may be delayed.9

In awake and evaluable blunt trauma patients, validated clinical decision rules and CT-based strategies support early collar removal in appropriately low-risk patients.11,12,31,59 In obtunded patients, high-quality CT has a high negative predictive value for clinically significant unstable injury. MRI remains useful when neurological deficit, radio-clinical discrepancy, inadequate CT, or suspected ligamentous instability is present.15,16,18,62 After a cervical fracture is confirmed, collar use becomes a treatment decision. Evidence on fracture-specific device type and duration remains heterogeneous and insufficiently standardized.19 This uncertainty must be weighed against known collar-related harms, including pressure injury, dysphagia, respiratory compromise, and increased intracranial pressure.23,24

Interpretive Synthesis

The central synthesis of this review is not that cervical collars are obsolete. Rather, collars should be viewed as indication-specific, time-limited interventions. Their value depends on the phase of care and the clinical objective. In early trauma care, the objective is temporary motion restriction until unstable injury is excluded. In confirmed fracture care, the objective may shift toward maintaining alignment, reducing pain, or supporting nonoperative treatment. In both settings, the expected benefit should be reassessed against patient-specific harm.

This interpretation is particularly important in older adults with odontoid fractures. Radiographic union, functional outcome, mortality, comfort, dysphagia, and skin integrity may not align.21,22,96 A rigid collar may increase radiographic or mechanical reassurance, but it may also increase treatment burden in frail patients. Similar caution applies to artificial intelligence. AI may improve fracture detection and triage, but it remains a diagnostic adjunct. It should not determine collar application, clearance, continuation, or de-escalation without clinical integration.25,122,125

Future Recommendations and Conceptual Framework

Future research should move beyond the binary question of whether collars are useful. Studies should test indication-specific and harm-aware strategies. In prehospital blunt trauma, pragmatic trials and implementation studies should compare routine collaring with selective spinal motion restriction. Patient-centered outcomes should be prioritized over immobilization rates alone.3,5,8 Comparative studies of CT-only and selective CT-plus-MRI clearance pathways should evaluate missed instability, treatment-changing findings, collar duration, complications, resource use, and patient-centered outcomes.15–18 In confirmed cervical fractures, comparative studies should define fracture-specific protocols, including collar type, duration, adherence, imaging follow-up, functional outcomes, adverse events, and thresholds for surgery.19

Geriatric and frailty-focused trials remain a priority. Older adults with odontoid fractures are especially important because treatment success cannot be judged by union alone. Implementation science should also evaluate how collar-removal protocols affect emergency department flow, intensive care burden, nursing care, pressure injury prevention, and missed unstable injury.8,15 AI tools require external validation, prospective workflow testing, and transparent reporting before they can be integrated into collar pathways.25,122

The 4D Trauma Collar Framework proposed in this review should be interpreted within these limits. It is a practical conceptual model, not a formal guideline, validated algorithm, or clinical decision rule. Its role is to organize decision-making around four recurring questions: whether to apply, clear, continue, or de-escalate the collar. It should support clinical reasoning, not replace specialist judgment, local protocols, or individualized patient care. The framework itself requires prospective evaluation of its feasibility, interclinician consistency, safety, effects on collar duration and adverse events, and generalizability across different trauma systems.

High-level evidence syntheses indicate that several exercise approaches can improve pain and disability in chronic neck pain, although comparative certainty and optimal dosage remain limited.126–129 Individual studies further suggest that relevant recovery domains may include proprioception, balance, kinesiophobia, mood, and sleep.130,131 Although these findings cannot be directly extrapolated to acute trauma or collar management, future post-clearance studies should evaluate whether structured rehabilitation improves patient-centered recovery after collar removal.

Strengths and Limitations

This review has several strengths, including a clinically structured synthesis spanning initial collar application, diagnostic clearance, treatment after confirmed fracture, and harm-related de-escalation. It integrates contemporary guidelines, evidence syntheses, and clinically relevant studies, while the supplementary evidence map improves transparency regarding the principal sources informing each domain. However, the narrative design has inherent limitations. The review did not include protocol registration, duplicate study selection, formal risk-of-bias assessment, certainty-of-evidence grading, or meta-analysis, and the restriction to English-language publications may have introduced selection bias. Heterogeneity in patient populations, collar protocols, imaging pathways, and outcome definitions also limits direct comparison across studies. Finally, the proposed 4D framework remains conceptual and requires prospective validation before it can inform formal clinical recommendations.

Conclusion

Cervical collars remain important but should be used selectively in adult trauma care. Routine application is unsupported when the risk of unstable injury is low or immobilization may interfere with time-critical management. In awake and evaluable adults, validated decision rules and high-quality CT-based pathways can facilitate early clearance. In adults with impaired consciousness, a negative high-quality CT may support (but does not automatically determine) collar removal; CT adequacy, neurological findings, clinical context, examination reliability, institutional protocols, and any radio-clinical discrepancy should guide the decision, with selective MRI or specialist assessment when uncertainty persists. After fracture confirmation, collar use becomes a therapeutic decision that should be individualized according to stability, neurological status, frailty, treatment goals, and tolerance. Collar-related harms require repeated reassessment. The proposed Four-Domain Trauma Collar Framework organizes application, clearance, continuation, and de-escalation but remains conceptual and unvalidated.
