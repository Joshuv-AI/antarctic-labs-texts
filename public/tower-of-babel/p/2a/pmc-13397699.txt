Cross-layer multiomic and digital pathology analysis identifies a malignant keratinization state linked to immune exclusion in cervical squamous carcinoma
Authors: Xiao Chen, Dongmei Zhou, Yiting Lin, Wanzhen Lin, Zuolian Xie, Lijun Chen, Linying Liu, Yang Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune exclusion contributes to heterogeneous benefit from immunotherapy in cervical squamous carcinoma, but the malignant epithelial state most closely associated with this phenotype and its tissue- and morphology-level correlates remain unclear. We investigated whether a lesion-grade-associated malignant epithelial state was linked to immune-excluded tissue architecture and could be translated across transcriptomic and pathology modalities.MethodsWe integrated single-cell RNA-seq discovery (GSE208653), spatial transcriptomic evaluation (GSE208654), bulk RNA-seq translation in primary squamous TCGA-CESC tumors, external whole-tumor evaluation in CGCI-HTMCP-CC, and whole-slide H&E analysis of 259 slides from 250 TCGA patients. External immune-focused datasets, a local neoadjuvant immunotherapy-treated cervical squamous carcinoma cohort, and a representative pilot whole-section multiplex immunofluorescence were used as supportive layers.ResultsA basal-squamous stress keratinization (BSK) program was the malignant epithelial state most consistently associated with the cross-sectional normal–HSIL–squamous carcinoma spectrum. Across four spatial sections, BSK showed a section-consistent core-boundary-shell organization comprising a BSK-rich tumor core, a stromal-myeloid boundary, and a more peripheral lymphoid shell. In primary squamous TCGA-CESC tumors, this biology was translated most clearly into an epithelial-exclusion bulk state associated with fibro-myeloid niche enrichment and weaker engagement of inflamed/dysfunctional CD8 T-cell programs. Patient-level out-of-fold morphology scores from matched TCGA H&E slides correlated positively with epithelial exclusion, supporting a detectable histologic correlate within the matched pathology arm. In a local 18-patient neoadjuvant immunotherapy-treated cohort, H&E-derived morphology scores were associated with postoperative pathological response grade, providing exploratory clinical-pathology support rather than predictive validation. The exclusion-centered ordering was directionally preserved in CGCI-HTMCP-CC and aligned with stromal/EMT/TGFβ, angiogenesis, and more moderate gMDSC-related programs.ConclusionsBSK marks an exclusion-associated cervical squamous carcinoma state that is spatially organized, measurable in bulk transcriptomes, and partially reflected in routine histology. These findings provide a human-data-derived translational framework for future immune-access stratification and prospective biomarker testing but do not establish BSK as a causal driver or validated predictor of immunotherapy response.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08567-3.Keywords: Cervical squamous carcinoma, Immune exclusion, Tumor microenvironment, Spatial transcriptomics, Single-cell RNA sequencing, Digital pathology, Biomarker

Background

Cervical cancer remains a major global health challenge and continues to impose a disproportionate disease burden in low- and middle-resource settings [1]. Persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) is the central etiologic driver of cervical carcinogenesis and underlies the vast majority of cervical cancers worldwide [2]. Although concurrent chemoradiotherapy remains the standard of care for locally advanced disease, the incorporation of immune checkpoint blockade has only partially improved outcomes across newly diagnosed high-risk, recurrent, and metastatic settings, underscoring the need for biologically informed stratification beyond conventional clinicopathologic variables [3–5]. Recent institution-based studies have also highlighted the potential value of scalable stratification tools in cervical cancer, including magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-based response prediction and radiographic prognostic modeling [6, 7].

Recent single-cell, single-nucleus, spatial, and multiomic studies have established that cervical cancer is characterized by marked epithelial, stromal, and immune heterogeneity [8–12]. These studies have identified malignant epithelial diversity, fibroblast subtypes, macrophage remodeling, CD8+ T-cell state variation, and histology-specific microenvironmental features in both squamous carcinoma and adenocarcinoma [8–13]. In other tumor types, transforming growth factor-beta (TGFβ) activity, cancer-associated fibroblast programs, stromal barriers, and tumor–immune interface organization have been shown to restrict CD8+ T-cell access to malignant nests and contribute to immune-checkpoint resistance [14–16]. However, an important translational question remains unresolved: whether immune exclusion in cervical squamous carcinoma is preferentially associated with a cross-sectionally lesion-grade-associated malignant epithelial state rather than being distributed equivalently across multiple tumor programs. Likewise, although prior work has demonstrated substantial ecosystem heterogeneity, it is still unclear whether immune-poor regions in cervical cancer represent a reproducible boundary-structured architecture that can be connected to a clinically scalable bulk transcriptomic state and subsequently recovered in routine histology [9–12].

Work in other tumor systems suggests that spatial structure is not merely descriptive but biologically informative and clinically relevant. Spatial transcriptomic studies have shown that tumor–microenvironment interfaces and core-edge architectures can define conserved invasive programs, cell-state transitions, and clinical associations [17, 18]. In parallel, mechanistic and translational studies have demonstrated that immune exclusion can be shaped by fibroblast-rich stromal barriers, TGFβ signaling, extracellular matrix remodeling, and direct cancer-associated fibroblast (CAF)-T-cell crosstalk that restricts CD8+ T-cell penetration into the tumor core [16, 19–22]. Integrated single-cell and spatial analyses in other cancers have further emphasized that structured immune niches, rather than immune abundance alone, can govern progression and treatment response [23]. Together, these observations support the possibility that cervical squamous carcinoma may also contain a malignant-state-associated, boundary-structured exclusion program, but such a framework has not yet been clearly defined.

To address this gap, we used a staged human-data framework to test whether a dominant malignant epithelial state was associated with spatial immune exclusion in cervical squamous carcinoma and whether this biology could be translated into scalable transcriptomic and pathology-linked readouts. Here, immune exclusion refers to a tissue-level pattern in which lymphoid cells are retained predominantly in stromal or peripheral compartments rather than infiltrating malignant epithelial nests. We identified a basal-squamous stress keratinization (BSK) program as the malignant epithelial state most consistently linked to this exclusion-prone architecture. Operationally, BSK denotes a continuous malignant epithelial transcriptional program enriched for basal/squamous stress-keratinization genes, rather than a distinct histologic subtype or a binary class defined by any single marker. Across predefined single-cell, spatial, bulk, and pathology-linked analysis layers, BSK mapped to a BSK-rich tumor core, a stromal-myeloid boundary, a peripheral lymphoid shell, an epithelial-exclusion bulk phenotype, and a partially reflected H&E correlate. In this framework, epithelial exclusion refers to the bulk transcriptomic correlate of this tissue-level organization, whereas fibro-myeloid niche enrichment describes the accompanying stromal and myeloid programs at the tumor boundary. Rather than defining a therapy-response biomarker outright, this study provides a translational framework for testing whether malignant epithelial state can stratify immune-access phenotypes in cervical squamous carcinoma.

Methods

Study design and cohort assignment

Public datasets and local pathology resources were assigned predefined roles within a staged cross-layer framework. GSE208653 served as the primary single-cell discovery cohort for malignant epithelial program identification, GSE208654 as the primary spatial evaluation cohort, and The Cancer Genome Atlas Cervical Squamous Cell Carcinoma and Endocervical Adenocarcinoma (TCGA-CESC) together with the matched TCGA whole-slide H&E set as the principal bulk/pathology translation layer. Independent whole-tumor bulk support was obtained from Cancer Genome Characterization Initiative HIV+ Tumor Molecular Characterization Project-Cervical Cancer (CGCI-HTMCP-CC). Additional resources were incorporated only as supportive layers. The processed natgenet_2023 atlas was used for external epithelial projection, GSE171894 and GSE168652 for immune-support analyses, PXD055203 for orthogonal proteogenomic support, and the local manual H&E review for pathology interpretability support. These resources were used to strengthen, rather than redefine, the principal cross-layer interpretation [9, 24, 25].

Single-cell RNA-seq preprocessing and quality control

Raw 10x count matrices from the nine retained GSE208653 samples were assembled into a unified AnnData object [26]. Per-cell quality control was performed using Scanpy (≥1.10). Cells were retained if they expressed at least 100 genes, had at least 200 total counts, and contained no more than 25% mitochondrial transcripts. No dedicated doublet-removal step was applied. After quality filtering, 77,099 cells were retained across all nine samples.

Expression matrices were normalized to 10,000 counts per cell (target_sum = 1e4) and log-transformed (log1p). The top 3,000 highly variable genes were selected using the seurat_v3 flavor in Scanpy. Cross-sample integration was performed with scVI (≥v0.20) [27] using sample identity as the batch covariate.

Cell-type identification and malignant epithelial cell annotation

Cell-type annotation used a hierarchical marker-scoring strategy. Immune lineages were assigned by scoring canonical marker panels for T/NK cells (CD3D, CD3E, TRBC1, NKG7, KLRD1), regulatory T cells (FOXP3, IL2RA, TIGIT, CTLA4), myeloid cells (LYZ, FCER1G, CTSB, SAT1, TYMP), dendritic cells (FCER1A, CST3, HLA-DRA, CD74), and B/plasma cells (MS4A1, CD79A, CD74, MZB1, JCHAIN) using scanpy.tl.score_genes with use_raw = False. Each cell was assigned to the lineage with the highest score (argmax). Epithelial cells were identified by scoring a canonical epithelial panel (EPCAM, KRT5, KRT14, KRT17, DSG3, TP63, SFN, KRT15) relative to fibroblast (DCN, LUM, C7, COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1, TAGLN) and endothelial (PECAM1, VWF, KDR, EMCN, RAMP2, ESAM) contamination modules, yielding 20,328 epithelial cells.

Within the epithelial compartment, malignant cells were distinguished from non-malignant epithelial cells using a conservative composite transcriptomic annotation strategy. Because stress-keratinization genes can also be induced in inflamed or injured non-malignant squamous epithelium, BSK-associated markers alone were not treated as sufficient evidence of malignancy. Instead, epithelial cells were scored jointly for BSK-associated stress-keratinization markers (S100A8, S100A9, KRT6A, KRT6B, SPRR3, KRT16, KRT17), mucosal-defense markers (KRT13, KRT4, PI3, SLPI, WFDC2), and normal-differentiation markers (KRT15, DSG3, TP63). Cells were retained as putative malignant epithelial cells only when they originated from cancer samples and showed elevated malignancy-associated/stress-keratinization scores together with reduced normal-differentiation or mucosal-defense signatures. This procedure retained 12,562 putative malignant epithelial cells from the 20,328 epithelial cells, which were carried forward for transcriptional program discovery.

To audit this marker-based annotation, we performed an inferCNV-derived CNV-like analysis using inferCNV v1.28.0. Raw count matrices were analyzed with genes ordered by hg38 genomic coordinates, and non-epithelial immune, fibroblast, and endothelial cells were used as reference populations. After inferCNV denoising, a per-cell CNV-like burden score was calculated as the mean absolute deviation of smoothed inferCNV expression values from the neutral baseline across ordered genes. CNV-like burden was compared between putative malignant and non-malignant epithelial cells and between BSK-high and BSK-low putative malignant epithelial cells. BSK-high cells were defined as the upper quartile of BSK score among putative malignant epithelial cells. This analysis was interpreted as a transcriptome-derived CNV-like audit rather than as matched genomic copy-number profiling.

Malignant epithelial program discovery and external projection

Counts from putative malignant epithelial cells were aggregated to sample-level pseudobulk profiles by original_sample_id and transformed to counts per million followed by log1p transformation. Non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) [28] was then applied to the nine-sample malignant epithelial matrix using scikit-learn (init = ‘nndsvda’, random_state = 0, max_iter = 1000) after removal of genes with zero variance across samples. The locked analysis used k = 3 as a parsimonious working decomposition that separated a BSK-like basal/squamous stress-keratinization program from two mucosal-defense-associated counter-states. This rank was used to define a practical downstream discovery framework rather than to claim a uniquely optimal or definitive taxonomy of cervical epithelial heterogeneity. The top 50 genes per component were used to define downstream program signatures, designated BSK, SG-MD, and SI-MD on the basis of their dominant loading structure. BSK was therefore defined operationally as the locked NMF-derived malignant epithelial program represented by the program_3 loading vector and its top-ranked genes. Across downstream analyses, BSK was treated as a continuous program score rather than as a binary cell-state label. No individual gene was considered necessary or sufficient for assigning BSK identity. Instead, BSK identity was based on the coordinated enrichment of basal/squamous stress-keratinization genes, including S100A8, S100A9, KRT6A, KRT6B, KRT16, KRT17, SPRR3, FABP5, and LY6D, together with preservation of the locked loading structure in rank-sensitivity and leave-one-sample-out analyses. The locked BSK definition was used for single-cell scoring, spatial projection, and external epithelial projection; bulk and H&E analyses were interpreted as translation layers or morphology-level correlates of the BSK/exclusion framework rather than as independent redefinitions of BSK. To assess the robustness of BSK identity, NMF was repeated across k = 2–6 and in nine leave-one-sample-out refits at k = 3 using identical preprocessing and fitting parameters. In each refit, the BSK-like component was selected primarily by cosine similarity of the full gene-loading vector to the locked program_3 BSK component; top-50 gene overlap, canonical BSK-marker enrichment, and sample-score Spearman concordance were evaluated as complementary robustness metrics. For the principal external projection, the top 30 genes per program were scored per cell in the processed natgenet_2023 epithelial compartment using scanpy.tl.score_genes. For the BSK robustness projection, locked and sensitivity-derived top-30 BSK-like scores were averaged within patient and tumor-status group. Patients contributing both tumor and non-tumor epithelial cells were analyzed using a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and paired Cohen’s dz was calculated as the mean within-patient difference divided by the standard deviation of those differences. Sensitivity-derived signatures were treated as correlated robustness readouts rather than independent validation tests.

To make the NMF parameter choice explicit, the primary malignant epithelial decomposition used rank k = 3 as a parsimonious working rank because it provided an interpretable three-program decomposition and separated the BSK-like program from the other major malignant epithelial programs. This rank was not selected by optimizing any downstream spatial, bulk, pathology, survival, or clinical-response endpoint. To evaluate the impact of rank choice, we repeated NMF across ranks k = 2–6 and performed nine leave-one-sample-out refits using identical preprocessing and fitting parameters. In each sensitivity model, the BSK-like component was identified by maximum cosine similarity to the locked BSK loading vector, and robustness was summarized using top-50 gene overlap, full-loading cosine similarity, canonical BSK-marker enrichment, and sample-score Spearman concordance. These sensitivity analyses were used to assess whether the BSK identity and downstream interpretation depended on a single NMF rank or one discovery sample.

Ecosystem association, state refinement, and targeted communication analysis

Sample-level ecosystem association was based on the locked malignant-program outputs and all-cell ecosystem summaries from the GSE208653 discovery cohort. For each sample, BSK summary scores were compared with malignant-intrinsic fibro-like metrics, broad cell-type fractions, and all-cell state fractions using Spearman correlation, with sample as the unit of analysis.

For focus-state refinement, Leiden-defined [29] all-cell states were interpreted using a combined evidence framework that included broad cell-type composition, pre-existing program-linked working annotations, and marker and differential-expression support within the relevant compartment. States 13, 8, 24, 6, and 11 were retained as the focus states because they showed the strongest positive or negative associations with BSK.

By-sample ligand-receptor analysis used the locked cell-level object prepared for LIANA [30] together with the pre-computed BSK sample ranking to define BSK-high and BSK-low groups (4 and 5 samples, respectively). LIANA was run at the broad-compartment level using epithelial, fibro, immune, and endothelial labels, and results were summarized at the pair level across samples. Interpretation prioritized axes supported in at least two samples per group and focused on the NOTCH, extracellular matrix (ECM)-integrin, matrix-sensing, and immune-contrast modules that remained on the manuscript mainline.

Targeted CellPhoneDB [31] analysis was used only as an orthogonal confirmation layer. Candidate fibro-to-epithelial and immune-contrast axes were re-audited against literal CellPhoneDB pairs and against database-supported proxy representations. Direct support was accepted when the literal pair was recovered, whereas module-level support was recorded when the corresponding biology was represented through curated integrin-complex or related receptor interactions.

External immune-support cohorts and immune-focused refinement

Two external public datasets were incorporated as immune-support resources without redefining the discovery-layer malignant epithelial axis. GSE171894 served as the principal immune refinement cohort. Processed matrices were parsed at the GSM level, merged across the four tumors, and subjected to immune-enrichment followed by removal of epithelial-like contaminating clusters. In the final working version, clusters 0, 3, 4, and 10 were excluded before re-running immune-focused refinement, yielding retained T/NK, Treg, DC/APC, B/plasma, and activated myeloid-like states. GSE168652 was used as a small metadata-guided tumor–adjacent-normal support cohort. Raw 10x matrices were combined, immune cells were retained using the accompanying malignancy and lineage annotations, and residual epithelial-like clusters 1 and 3 were removed before re-analysis. Because GSE168652 comprised a single donor pair with modest post-filtering cell counts, it was interpreted as directional support rather than equal-weight validation. These external cohorts were used to test whether the immune branch of the BSK-associated ecosystem remained recoverable outside the discovery dataset [8, 32]. Cohort-level retained cell counts, removed contaminating clusters, and dominant refined immune states are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Spatial cohort definition, program projection, and deconvolution alignment

The spatial evaluation arm used only the four GSE208654 sections with preserved spatial coordinates and complete deconvolution metadata. Adjacent serial sections or additional geographically distinct sections from the same tumor blocks were not available in the public dataset in a form that could be paired with the analyzed sections. Therefore, we could not perform adjacent-section or within-block geographic-position replication of the spatial boundary scripts. To prevent pseudo-replication, raw duplicates and processed objects lacking spatial information were excluded before constructing the final four-section spatial analysis set. Internal structural consistency was instead evaluated within the available sections using section-wise shell/interface summaries, within-section permutation controls, tumor-rich subset analyses, and leave-one-section-out recalculation of the overall boundary effect.

Epithelial programs discovered in GSE208653 were projected into the final four-section spatial analysis set using the locked summary table of top marker genes per program. Mean expression across matched markers was used to score SI-MD, SG-MD, and BSK for each spot. Section-level deconvolution tables were merged back to the spatial object using barcode diagnostics comparing raw and suffix-stripped versions of original barcodes, section-prefixed barcodes, and combined spot identifiers. Original-barcode matching produced the highest recovery in all four sections and yielded non-missing Cancer cells, Fibroblast, Macrophage, and Lymphocyte scores in more than 99% of spots.

Spatial shell, boundary, and interface-extension analyses

Spatial geometry was quantified using a k-nearest-neighbor graph (k = 6). BSK hotspots were defined within each section as spots in the top 20% of the section-specific BSK distribution. Hotspot shells were enumerated from shell 0 (hotspot core) through shell 3 (outer boundary), and mean Cancer cells, Fibroblast, Macrophage, and Lymphocyte signals were summarized within each shell. Boundary contact fractions were calculated as the proportion of BSK-boundary spots adjacent to state-high neighborhoods defined at the 75th percentile for each microenvironmental score.

Covariate-controlled spot-level associations were evaluated using ordinary least squares models with microenvironmental score as the dependent variable and BSK score, Cancer-cell content, and section as covariates. Models were run across all main-text spots and repeated in tumor-rich subsets defined within section by above-median Cancer-cell content. These models were used only as descriptive local-association checks at matched cancer-cell content. Because spots within a section are spatially autocorrelated and are not statistically independent, spot-level OLS p values were not treated as the primary inferential support for the boundary model. We did not use spatial autoregressive or mixed-effects models as the main analysis because the spatial arm contained only four analyzable sections spanning different tissue contexts, making section-level random-effect estimation and spatial-autocorrelation parameterization underpowered and sensitive to model specification. Additional signature-based proxy scores for CD8-like effector, Treg-like, and exhausted-T-like states were calculated from spatial expression and summarized relative to BSK hotspot shells. Because spots within a section are not statistically independent, these spot-level covariate-adjusted models were interpreted as descriptive local-association analyses rather than as the primary basis for inference. The primary support for the spatial boundary interpretation instead came from section-wise shell/interface contrasts, within-section permutation controls that preserved section structure, tumor-rich subset analyses, and leave-one-section-out recalculation of the overall boundary effect.

For the interface-oriented robustness analyses, spot-level signed distance to interface was defined relative to a tumor-side seed and an outer-shell seed. The tumor-side seed used a section-specific high-BSK indicator, whereas the outer-shell seed used spots in the upper tertile of the combined Fibroblast + Macrophage + Lymphocyte score within section. Signed distance was positive on the tumor side and negative on the outer-shell side. Three-band stratification defined tumor-core, interface, and outer-shell bands by thresholding signed distance at plus or minus one within-section standard deviation. For each section, we computed the tumor-core minus outer-shell BSK difference, tested within-section permutation controls by shuffling BSK values across fixed bands 1,000 times, and assessed section dominance using leave-one-section-out recalculation of the overall effect. Three-band, permutation, and leave-one-section-out interface summaries are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

TCGA-CESC bulk cohort definition, locked score calculation, and supportive genomic extension

Bulk transcriptomic analyses were performed in the matched TCGA-CESC cohort using a predefined primary analysis set restricted to primary squamous tumors. Sample-level integration across transcriptomic, clinical, and pathology-linked data was performed using harmonized sample identifiers, yielding 250 tumors from 250 patients for the main bulk analyses.

Three bulk transcriptomic axes were evaluated: epithelial exclusion, fibro-myeloid niche enrichment, and inflamed dysfunctional CD8 engagement. The gene composition, sign direction, and gene-level weights of these signatures are provided in Supplementary Table S3, and requested-versus-present gene coverage in the primary squamous TCGA-CESC cohort is summarized in Supplementary Table S4. For each gene, expression was z-scored across the 250 samples. Signature scores were calculated as the arithmetic mean of signed weighted gene-wise z-scores across all genes present in the corresponding signature. All three bulk signatures achieved complete gene coverage in the main analysis set.

To translate the discovery-layer BSK signal into bulk RNA, we evaluated a small set of BSK-derived bridge candidates. Candidate ranking used alignment with epithelial exclusion as the primary criterion because the study was designed to test whether the spatially organized BSK-associated state mapped onto an exclusion-oriented bulk phenotype. The retained loading-weighted bridge was therefore used as the principal bulk carrier of the BSK signal for downstream analysis, whereas fibro-myeloid niche enrichment and inflamed dysfunctional CD8 engagement were interpreted as related ecosystem dimensions rather than alternative primary bridge-selection targets (Supplementary Tables S5–S6). Accordingly, downstream bridge-centered analyses were interpreted within a locked exclusion-centered translational framework and not as an unbiased discovery procedure across all potential downstream bulk phenotypes. To make the bridge-score parameterization transparent, the retained bridge genes, sign directions, and loading-derived weights were fixed before downstream cohort scoring and were not re-estimated to improve spatial, histologic, survival, or treatment-response associations. The same locked bridge definition was applied to TCGA-CESC and CGCI-HTMCP-CC. Bridge gene-set composition and parameterization are provided in Supplementary Table S6, and bridge-selection, cohort-projection, and stage-adjusted robustness summaries are provided in Supplementary Table S5. Thus, the bridge score was treated as a pre-specified translation layer rather than as an outcome-optimized predictor. Because whole-tumor bulk RNA-seq may contain variable amounts of non-malignant mucosal epithelium, the bridge score was not interpreted as a purified malignant-cell abundance estimate or as a deconvolution-based tumor-cell fraction. The selected bridge was used as a signed, loading-weighted bulk translation of the discovery-layer BSK program within an exclusion-centered framework. Normal mucosal keratinocyte contamination could therefore attenuate or dilute the translated BSK/exclusion signal, particularly through mucosal-defense or normal-differentiation epithelial programs, but it was not assumed to be fully removed by the bridge score.

Mutation metadata were reconstructed from TCGA-CESC masked somatic mutation files with record-level deduplication. Copy-number variation (CNV) metadata were reconstructed from both thresholded GISTIC-like outputs and exact segment-derived layers. These genomic variables were used as supportive contextual analyses relative to the selected BSK bridge and epithelial exclusion, rather than as primary evidence for the RNA-centered model.

Clinical association, mediation, partial models, and stage robustness

Clinical variables were derived from the frozen default analysis input. The principal stage comparison used early-stage (I-IIA) versus late-stage (IIB-IV) tumors. Two-group comparisons used the Mann-Whitney U test, multi-group comparisons used the Kruskal-Wallis test, and score-to-score relationships used Spearman correlation.

Simple mediation-oriented models were evaluated for selected bridge → epithelial exclusion → fibro-myeloid niche and selected bridge → epithelial exclusion → inflamed dysfunctional CD8 engagement using ordinary least squares models adjusted for numeric stage and 5,000 bootstrap resamples to derive percentile 95% confidence intervals for indirect effects. A serial mediation-oriented model further decomposed selected bridge → epithelial exclusion → fibro-myeloid niche → inflamed dysfunctional CD8 engagement into exclusion-only, fibro-only conditional, and serial components. Because the TCGA-CESC bulk data are cross-sectional and observational, these models were used only as ordering-consistency and variance-partitioning analyses within the frozen exclusion-centered framework. They were not interpreted as formal causal mediation and cannot control for bidirectional feedback between malignant epithelial compartments, stromal remodeling, myeloid programs, and immune dysfunction. Partial Spearman correlations were computed by residualizing each variable on the specified covariates and correlating the residuals. Complementary stage-adjusted multivariable models were fit for epithelial exclusion, fibro-myeloid niche, and inflamed dysfunctional CD8 engagement to determine whether bridge associations persisted after conditioning on related bulk axes. Stage robustness was assessed both by early-versus-late stratified Spearman correlations and by interaction models testing bridge-by-stage terms.

Orthogonal mapping to pembrolizumab benchmark signatures

To position the exclusion-centered bulk framework relative to published immune-checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)-relevant transcriptomic axes, we reconstructed four prespecified consensus signatures from the published pan-cancer pembrolizumab monotherapy benchmark: stroma/ epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)/TGFβ, angiogenesis, granulocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cell (gMDSC), and monocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cell (mMDSC). For each cohort, bulk expression values were log2-transformed and z-scored across samples, after which signature scores were computed as the arithmetic mean of per-gene z-scores across genes present in the signature. Signature-to-signature relationships with epithelial_exclusion_score, fibro_myeloid_niche_score, and BSK_consensus_bridge were evaluated using Spearman correlation separately in TCGA-CESC and CGCI-HTMCP-CC. Requested-versus-present gene coverage was summarized for each reconstructed signature to verify that the observed associations were not driven by sparse gene overlap.

Whole-slide cohort definition, Trident embeddings, and ABMIL training

The pathology arm was designed to test whether the exclusion-associated state defined in the matched TCGA-CESC bulk cohort was partially reflected from routine H&E morphology as a morphology-level correlate, rather than to establish an independent de novo pathology-defined disease axis or to define BSK by histologic features alone. After cohort assembly, 259 whole-slide images from 250 patients were retained for the primary pathology analysis. Because nine patients contributed two slides, all data splitting was performed at the patient level. The relaxed epithelial-exclusion label served as the primary whole-slide endpoint, whereas stricter labels were reserved for secondary extension analyses because of their smaller positive class counts.

Patch-level features were obtained from Trident preprocessing output using uni_v1 foundation embeddings [34]. Whole-slide images were tiled at 20× magnification into 256 × 256-pixel patches, and each retained patch was represented by a 1024-dimensional uni_v1 embedding. Slide-level training used an attention-based multiple-instance learning (ABMIL) model in which each slide was represented as one bag of patch embeddings. The ABMIL architecture first projected 1024-dimensional patch embedding into a 256-dimensional hidden representation, then applied a 128-dimensional gated-attention module to learn patch-level attention weights, followed by dropout of 0.25 and a single-slide binary classification head for the relaxed epithelial-exclusion endpoint. Training used AdamW with a learning rate of 1 × 10−4 and weight decay of 1 × 10−4 for 25 epochs with class-weighted binary cross-entropy. During inference-compatible sampling, a maximum of 4096 patches per slide was used to generate slide-level probabilities, which were then averaged within patient to derive patient-level morphology scores when multiple slides were available.

Evaluation was performed using patient-level five-fold stratified group cross-validation so that all slides from the same patient remained within the same fold. Primary reported metrics were fold-wise validation area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) together with aggregated out-of-fold AUROC, AUPRC, balanced accuracy, and F1 score.

Patient-level morphology aggregation, bulk coupling, error audit, and duplicate sensitivity

To quantify coupling between the whole-slide model and the matched bulk phenotype, slide-level out-of-fold probabilities from the primary relaxed epithelial-exclusion model were averaged within patient to generate a patient-level morphology score. This score was matched to the corresponding TCGA-CESC bulk epithelial-exclusion score and selected BSK bridge using harmonized patient identifiers and evaluated using Spearman correlation. For descriptive stratification, patients were additionally grouped into epithelial-exclusion tertiles, and morphology scores were compared across tertiles using the Kruskal-Wallis test.

To assess the impact of duplicate slides, patient-level aggregation was recalculated using mean, maximum, median, and top-two-mean slide probabilities. AUROC, AUPRC, balanced accuracy, F1 score, and correlations with epithelial exclusion and the selected BSK bridge were recomputed under each aggregation strategy. Duplicate-only summaries were also generated for the nine patients contributing two slides.

For error audit, out-of-fold slide predictions were classified at a threshold of 0.5 into true-positive, true-negative, false-positive, and false-negative categories. Category-level summaries included predicted probability, bulk epithelial exclusion, selected BSK bridge, strict-label rate, BSK-strict rate, patch count, duplicate-patient status, and slide-class composition. Representative true-positive, true-negative, false-positive, and false-negative slides were ranked using out-of-fold predictions, and the corresponding fold-specific best checkpoint was reloaded to recompute patch-level attention weights for exemplar export. Attention concentration was summarized using maximum attention, top-five attention share, normalized attention entropy, and spatial span of attended patches. These analyses were used as morphology-oriented interpretability and robustness assessments rather than as a separate classifier-development objective.

Local neoadjuvant immunotherapy-treated response-associated pathology support analysis

As an additional pathology interpretability layer, pretreatment hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) biopsy sections from patients treated at Fujian Cancer Hospital were retrospectively reviewed. Consecutive biopsy cases collected between 2023 and 2024 were eligible for screening. For the response-associated pathology analysis reported in the revised manuscript, the analyzable cohort was restricted to patients with cervical squamous cell carcinoma who received neoadjuvant immunotherapy and had available pretreatment diagnostic H&E biopsy slides together with postoperative institutional pathological response annotations. Adenocarcinoma, adenosquamous carcinoma, neuroendocrine carcinoma, and other non-squamous histologies were excluded from the response-associated analysis to maintain consistency with the squamous keratinization-centered BSK framework. Any broader preliminary slide review was treated as exploratory morphology familiarization and was not used as an independent mixed-histology validation cohort. Only pretreatment biopsy specimens were included, and one representative biopsy slide was reviewed per patient when available.

Slides were independently evaluated by two tumor pathologists under blinded conditions, without access to model predictions, bulk-derived exclusion scores, or cross-layer grouping information. Discordant assessments were resolved by consensus review. No additional preselection requirement was imposed for minimum tumor area, visually complete tumor-stroma boundary, or stromal integrity beyond routine diagnostic readability of the biopsy section, reflecting the intended pragmatic support role of this analysis.

Manual scoring was performed using predefined morphology features designed to capture exclusion-associated histologic tendencies, including stromal reaction, lymphocyte distribution / exclusion-like pattern, tumor-stroma boundary-related appearance, and tumor nest border-related appearance. Feature-level scores were oriented such that higher values indicated greater alignment with the exclusion-associated morphology direction. A composite manual H&E score was then calculated from standardized feature-level readouts for downstream correlation analyses.

The local manual H&E analysis was interpreted as a secondary interpretability layer and was not used to redefine the primary pathology endpoint of the matched TCGA-CESC whole-slide arm. Associations between manual H&E scores and model-derived morphology scores were evaluated using Spearman correlation. Feature-level directionality was summarized descriptively to identify the morphology components contributing most strongly to the composite signal.

To provide exploratory clinical-pathology context in immunotherapy-treated patients, we analyzed a local retrospective neoadjuvant immunotherapy-treated cervical squamous carcinoma subset comprising 18 patients with postoperative institutional pathological response annotations. The response cohort and H&E morphology analysis were based on pretreatment diagnostic H&E biopsy slides from the same patients. Adenocarcinoma and other non-squamous histologies were excluded, and real hospitalization numbers were replaced with anonymized local case identifiers. We did not perform an independent non-squamous subset analysis because the excluded non-squamous records were too few and biologically outside the squamous keratinization-centered BSK framework to support a reliable separate test. Postoperative pathological response grade was extracted from institutional surgical pathology reports issued by one pathologist and reviewed by one additional pathologist, with response assessment blinded to manual H&E composite scores and Trident/ABMIL-derived morphology scores. Because this endpoint reflected institutional pathological response grading rather than standardized pCR/MPR, light, moderate, and marked responses were analyzed as an ordinal variable coded as 1, 2, and 3, respectively. Case-level manual H&E composite scores and Trident/ABMIL-derived morphology scores were compared with pathological response grade using Spearman correlation and non-parametric group-wise tests.

Statistical analysis and reproducibility

All statistical tests were two-sided unless otherwise specified. Exact sample sizes are reported in the text and figure legends for each analytic layer. Independent two-group comparisons used the Mann-Whitney U test, matched tumor–non-tumor comparisons used the two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, multi-group comparisons used the Kruskal-Wallis test, and score-to-score associations used Spearman correlation unless otherwise indicated in the corresponding subsection. Ordinary least squares models were used for covariate-adjusted analyses in the spatial and mediation-oriented bulk analyses, as described above. For the mediation-oriented models, 5,000 bootstrap resamples were used to derive percentile 95% confidence intervals for indirect effects.

All analyses were conducted within a fixed dataset-assignment framework in which the single-cell, spatial, bulk, proteogenomic, and pathology layers were interpreted according to their predefined analytic roles. Reproducibility-oriented summaries, including NMF rank and leave-one-sample-out robustness, interface robustness, bridge-selection support, pathology error audit, and duplicate-patient sensitivity analyses, are provided in the Supplementary Data and Supplementary Tables.

Results

Study design established a staged cross-layer framework for discovery, spatial support, and bulk/pathology translation

We organized the study into a staged cross-layer framework comprising single-cell discovery, spatial support, and bulk/pathology translation. GSE208653 served as the primary single-cell discovery cohort for malignant epithelial program identification, GSE208654 as the primary spatial evaluation cohort, and TCGA-CESC together with TCGA whole-slide H&E as the main translational backbone for bulk RNA and morphology-level correlation. Independent whole-tumor bulk support was provided by CGCI-HTMCP-CC.

Additional datasets were incorporated according to predefined supportive roles. The processed natgenet_2023 atlas was used for external epithelial projection, GSE171894 and GSE168652 for immune-support analyses, PXD055203 for orthogonal proteogenomic support, and the local manual H&E review for pathology interpretability support rather than co-primary validation (Fig. 1).

Malignant epithelial program discovery prioritized BSK as a lesion-grade-associated malignant epithelial program for downstream spatial and bulk projection

Having fixed GSE208653 as the single-cell discovery cohort in the frozen study framework, we next asked whether the malignant epithelial compartment contained a parsimonious lesion-grade-associated working decomposition that could anchor the remainder of the manuscript. The resolved discovery set comprised 77,099 post-quality-control cells, including 20,328 epithelial cells and 12,562 putative malignant epithelial cells retained for program discovery. Using sample-level malignant epithelial pseudobulk profiles followed by three-component non-negative matrix factorization, we obtained a three-component epithelial decomposition that formed the basis of the downstream cross-layer analysis (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Table S8). Program 3 was designated the basal-squamous stress keratinization (BSK) program on the basis of prominent loadings for S100A8, S100A9, KRT13, SPRR3, FABP5, KRT6A, and LY6D, whereas Program 2 was designated the secretory-glandular mucosal defense (SG-MD) program and Program 1 the secretory-immunoglobulin mucosal defense (SI-MD) program. Because SI-MD contained prominent immunoglobulin transcripts, it was interpreted as a mixed epithelial-immune mucosal-defense state rather than a purely tumor-intrinsic axis.

(A) Top genes and component loadings defining the three malignant epithelial programs identified in the GSE208653 discovery cohort. (B) Mean program scores across progression states (normal, HSIL, and cancer). (C) Mean program scores stratified by HPV status. (D) External projection of the three programs in the processed natgenet_2023 epithelial compartment, stratified by tumor flag. The three retained components were not biologically equivalent. Among them, BSK showed the clearest cross-sectional lesion-grade association, with higher scores across the normal-high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL)-squamous carcinoma spectrum and higher levels in HPV-positive than in no-HPV samples (Fig. 2B–C). Because the discovery and spatial datasets were cross-sectional rather than longitudinal, this pattern was interpreted as lesion-grade association rather than direct evidence of temporal evolution from a precursor state into a stable malignant differentiation state. By contrast, SI-MD was highest in normal and no-HPV samples and declined steadily with progression, consistent with loss of a mucosal-defense state. SG-MD also behaved as a counter-state, but its relative enrichment in HSIL rather than cancer suggested an intermediate secretory-differentiation program rather than a temporally ordered malignant trajectory. Thus, within the retained three-component solution, the discovery layer supported BSK as the clearest lesion-grade-associated malignant epithelial program, together with two mucosal-defense counter-states (SG-MD and SI-MD) (Fig. 2B–C; Supplementary Figs. S1, S2, S3S4).

We next tested whether BSK identity was robust to NMF rank choice and sample composition. A BSK-like component was recovered at every tested rank from k = 2 to k = 6, and all rank-sensitivity and leave-one-sample-out models converged. Across the alternative ranks k = 2, 4, 5, and 6, the median top-50 overlap with the locked BSK component was 36.5 genes (range, 33–49), and the median full-loading cosine similarity was 0.977 (range, 0.973–0.999). Sample-score concordance was more variable across alternative ranks (median Spearman rho = 0.570; range, 0.475–0.933), indicating that rank sensitivity supported preservation of the BSK loading structure more strongly than invariant sample ordering. Across nine leave-one-sample-out refits, the BSK-like component retained a median top-50 overlap of 44 genes (interquartile range, 41–47; range, 33–49) and a median full-loading cosine similarity of 0.995. Concordance with the locked BSK scores remained high among the eight retained samples (median Spearman rho = 0.970) and after projection of all nine samples into each refitted model (median rho = 0.979). These parameter-sensitivity analyses indicate that the central BSK interpretation was not driven solely by one NMF rank choice or by one withheld discovery sample: the BSK-like loading structure was preserved across k = 2–6, remained stable under leave-one-sample-out refitting, and retained external tumor-enrichment behavior in matched-patient epithelial projection. We therefore interpret the BSK component as a recurrent working malignant epithelial program within the available discovery framework. However, we do not interpret the three-component NMF solution as an enduring biological taxonomy or definitive architecture of cervical carcinogenesis. The k = 3 solution is instead used as a parsimonious discovery scaffold for downstream spatial, bulk, and pathology translation (Supplementary Fig. S5A-D; Supplementary Table S9). Thus, BSK was formalized as a stable, loading-defined transcriptional program with continuous scores, not as a marker-threshold-defined cell class.

We then asked whether this retained program structure remained detectable outside the discovery cohort. Projection of the GSE208653-derived loadings into epithelial-like cells from the processed natgenet_2023 atlas showed that related program signals were detectable externally, with BSK showing the clearest enrichment in tumor-associated epithelial compartments. In the matched-patient analysis, the locked BSK score was higher in tumor epithelial cells than in the corresponding non-tumor epithelial cells in 11 of 12 patients, with a mean within-patient difference of 0.292, a paired Cohen’s dz of 0.947, and a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank p value of 0.0015. All 13 sensitivity-derived BSK-like signatures also showed positive paired effects (dz range, 0.851–0.937), although these correlated signatures were interpreted as robustness readouts rather than independent validation tests. BSK additionally reached its highest levels in selected tumor cell states, including MP8_SCANPY and MP6_SCANPY (Fig. 2D–E; Supplementary Figs. S5E and S6–S7). We further performed an inferCNV-derived CNV-like audit to assess whether the marker-defined putative malignant epithelial compartment showed tumor-enriched large-scale CNV-like signal. In the inferCNV audit set, putative malignant epithelial cells showed higher CNV-like burden than non-malignant epithelial cells (median, 0.0141 versus 0.0101; Wilcoxon rank-sum p = 8.43 × 10−218). Within the putative malignant epithelial compartment, BSK-high cells showed greater CNV-like burden than BSK-low cells (median, 0.0253 versus 0.0120; p < 2.2 × 10-308), and BSK score was positively correlated with CNV-like burden (Spearman rho = 0.492; p < 2.2 × 10−308). These results support CNV-like abnormality enrichment in the BSK-associated putative malignant epithelial compartment, while recognizing that transcriptome-derived inferCNV analysis is not equivalent to matched genomic copy-number profiling (Supplementary Fig. S5F-I; Supplementary Table S9). Taken together, these results support BSK as the principal cross-sectional lesion-grade-associated putative malignant epithelial program within the retained three-component working decomposition, while leaving open the possibility that larger cohorts, genomic CNV profiling, or alternative factorization strategies could resolve additional epithelial structure. This framing established the transition to the next section: rather than treating epithelial heterogeneity as an endpoint in itself, we carried BSK forward as the discovery-defined malignant input for testing whether the same biology was subsequently organized into a boundary-structured spatial architecture and, later, represented in bulk as an exclusion-centered transcriptomic state.

BSK-high malignant samples are associated with ecosystem rewiring and candidate fibro-to-epithelial boundary communication

Having identified BSK as the principal lesion-grade-associated malignant epithelial program in the cross-sectional discovery framework, we next asked whether BSK-high tumors simply reflected generalized stromalization or instead marked a more specific ecosystem organization. At the sample level, BSK was inversely associated with malignant-intrinsic fibro-like features, including both the malignant-intrinsic fibro-like score and the malignant fibro-marker fraction (Fig. 3A). These relationships argue against a direct epithelial-to-fibro mimicry model and instead support an intrinsic-extrinsic interpretation in which a BSK-rich malignant epithelial compartment is associated with an external fibro-rich niche rather than adopting fibro-like identity itself.

This ecosystem shift became clearer when we examined all-cell states rather than broad cell-type fractions alone. The strongest BSK-positive state was Leiden 13, which was refined as an inflammatory APC/myeloid-like antigen-presentation state, whereas Leiden 8, 24, 6, and 11 were all negatively associated with BSK and corresponded to epithelial counter-states spanning mucosal-defense, ciliated, secretory, and differentiated glandular-like programs (Fig. 3B–C; Supplementary Fig. S8; Supplementary Table S10). Broad lineage fractions were directionally consistent but weaker, indicating that the dominant BSK-associated ecosystem shift is better captured at the state level than by gross abundance changes alone.

A supportive spatial consistency check showed that this ecosystem organization was compatible with the independently analyzed spatial layer. In GSE208654, BSK remained positively associated with fibroblast and lymphocyte signals and negatively associated with macrophage after adjustment for cancer-cell content, and shell-level summaries showed that immune-related signals were preferentially shifted toward more peripheral spatial compartments in HSIL and SCC rather than accumulating uniformly within BSK-high regions (Fig. 3D). These observations are consistent with a boundary-structured organization and are developed in detail in the primary spatial analyses below.

We then asked whether the BSK-associated ecosystem was accompanied by a selective interaction structure. By-sample LIANA analysis showed that the dominant BSK-high communication pattern was not a uniform increase in intercellular signaling, but a selective reinforcement of fibro-to-epithelial modules (Fig. 3E; Supplementary Fig. S9; Supplementary Table S11). The strongest reproducible BSK-associated axes clustered in three modules: NOTCH signaling, ECM-integrin adhesion, and matrix-sensing/anchoring interactions. Representative examples included MAML2/JAG1 to NOTCH1/3, LAMB1/COL12A1 to ITGA2_ITGB1, and MDK/TNC/FN1 to SDC1/CD44. By contrast, several epithelial-immune or immune-epithelial control axes, including TGFB1 to ITGB8 and MIF to CD44_CD74, were relatively stronger in BSK-low samples. Thus, BSK-high tumors were not characterized by a broadly immune-engaged state, but by a selective fibro-to-epithelial interaction pattern consistent with boundary reinforcement.

Targeted CellPhoneDB re-analysis served as an orthogonal confirmation layer and showed that support clustered at the module level rather than at every pre-specified literal interaction pair (Fig. 3F; Supplementary Fig. S10; Supplementary Table S11). JAG1–NOTCH1 was directly recovered and stronger in BSK-high than in BSK-low samples, whereas COL12A1-, FN1-, and MDK-related support was recovered through corresponding integrin-complex or related proxy interactions in the CellPhoneDB output. Together, these results support the interpretation that BSK-high tumors are best viewed not as generically stromalized tumors, but as tumors associated with a candidate fibro-to-epithelial boundary-communication pattern. Because LIANA and CellPhoneDB infer potential communication from transcriptomic ligand-receptor co-expression rather than from perturbation of ligand-receptor interactions, these axes should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating support for boundary-associated communication rather than experimental proof that BSK epithelium mechanistically drives immune exclusion.

External immune-focused support cohorts reinforced this interpretation without redefining the discovery layer. In GSE171894, removal of epithelial-like contaminating clusters yielded an immune-enriched compartment of 8,479 retained cells that resolved coherent T/NK, Treg, DC/APC, B/plasma, and activated myeloid-like states, indicating that the immune branch of the BSK-associated ecosystem remained detectable outside the discovery cohort (Fig. 3G; Supplementary Table S1). In the metadata-guided tumor-adjacent-normal support cohort GSE168652, removal of residual epithelial-like clusters preserved 404 immune-lineage cells and yielded the same directional pattern, with relatively greater myeloid-like abundance in tumor and relatively greater T/NK- and DC-associated composition in adjacent normal tissue (Fig. 3H; Supplementary Table S1). Because GSE168652 comprised a single donor pair with modest post-filtering cell counts, it was interpreted as directional support only. Together, these external analyses support a selective immune configuration consistent with an exclusion-prone boundary state.

Spatial analysis resolves a BSK-rich tumor core bordered by stromal-myeloid structure and a peripheral lymphoid shell across the squamous lesion spectrum

We next asked how the BSK-associated ecosystem was organized in tissue space. To address this, we projected the discovery-defined epithelial programs into the independently analyzed spatial cohort GSE208654. After restricting analysis to the four sections with preserved spatial coordinates and complete deconvolution metadata, the spatial arm comprised 4,983 analyzed spots spanning normal epithelium, HSIL, SCC, and an adenocarcinoma (ADC) comparison section that was retained mainly as supplementary context. Projection of the epithelial programs recovered complete marker coverage for SI-MD, SG-MD, and BSK, and BSK was higher across the cross-sectional normal–HSIL–SCC spectrum, whereas ADC remained outside the main squamous lesion-associated axis (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Fig. S11). These findings established that the malignant epithelial state identified in the single-cell discovery cohort remained spatially reflected and could be used to organize section-level architecture.

We then quantified the radial structure surrounding BSK-enriched regions. In both HSIL and SCC, BSK hotspots defined tumor-enriched cores in which Cancer-cell signal declined outward, whereas Fibroblast and Macrophage signals increased toward the boundary and outer shells (Fig. 4B–C; Supplementary Fig. S12). Lymphocyte-associated signal was comparatively more peripheral and contributed to an outer-shell immune compartment rather than to uniform mixing within the BSK-high core. HSIL and SCC showed the same directional organization, indicating that the spatial layer does not depict BSK as a generically stromalized or immune-mixed program, but as a structured architecture composed of a BSK-rich epithelial core, a stromal-myeloid boundary, and a more peripheral lymphoid shell.

We next asked whether this interpretation remained directionally consistent after accounting for variable tumor content. Barcode-level diagnostics showed that original-barcode matching yielded the highest deconvolution recovery across all sections and produced non-missing Cancer-cell, Fibroblast, Macrophage, and Lymphocyte scores in more than 99% of analyzed spots (Supplementary Fig. S11). In covariate-adjusted spot-level OLS models, BSK remained positively associated with Fibroblast and Lymphocyte signals and negatively associated with Macrophage after adjustment for Cancer-cell content, both across all spots and within tumor-rich subsets (Fig. 4D; Supplementary Fig. S13). Because neighboring spots are spatially autocorrelated, these OLS models were interpreted as descriptive local-association checks rather than as independent-spot inferential tests. The spatial interpretation therefore did not rest on spot-level p values alone. Instead, the main support came from section-wise shell/interface summaries, tumor-rich subset consistency, within-section permutation controls, and leave-one-section-out recalculation. Under this interpretation, the outward enrichment of macrophage at the boundary and outer shells is compatible with a negative within-spot association between macrophage and BSK in covariate-adjusted models. When the coarse lymphocyte compartment was further decomposed using CD8 effector-like, Treg-like, and exhausted-T-like proxy signatures, normal tissue retained a more core-biased pattern, whereas HSIL and SCC showed increasing outer-shell enrichment of these immune states, with SCC showing the strongest Treg-like peripheral reinforcement (Supplementary Fig. S14).

Finally, we asked whether the observed structure could be formalized as an interface program rather than a descriptive hotspot pattern alone. Distance-to-interface analysis showed progressive elevation of BSK toward the tumor side of the interface, and three-band stratification showed positive tumor-core minus outer-shell BSK gradients in HSIL and SCC, whereas normal tissue lacked a comparable positive gradient (Fig. 4E; Supplementary Fig. S15; Supplementary Table S2). These gradients remained significant in within-section permutation controls and were preserved under leave-one-section-out recalculation, indicating that the effect was not driven by a single analyzed section. Adjacent serial sections or additional geographic positions from the same tumor blocks were not available for direct internal replication; therefore, these analyses do not establish adjacent-section reproducibility. Together, the available spatial results support a section-consistent boundary-structured pattern within the four analyzable sections, in which a BSK-rich tumor core is bordered by stromal-myeloid structure and separated from a more peripheral lymphoid shell. Because the spatial arm comprised four analyzable sections, this result was interpreted as internal spatial consistency and cross-layer spatial support rather than as adjacent-section validation, within-block geographic replication, or large-scale spatial replication.

Epithelial exclusion represents the bulk transcriptomic correlate of the BSK-associated boundary architecture

We next asked whether the BSK-associated spatial phenotype could be represented coherently in bulk transcriptomes from primary squamous TCGA-CESC tumors. To translate the discovery-layer signal into bulk RNA, we evaluated a limited set of prespecified BSK-derived bridge candidates and retained the loading-weighted bridge because it best preserved the study’s exclusion-oriented translation hypothesis while remaining biologically interpretable across downstream analyses (Fig. 5A; Supplementary Figs. S16–S17; Supplementary Tables S5–S6). Within this locked translation framework, epithelial exclusion emerged as the primary bulk correlate to be tested, whereas fibro-myeloid niche enrichment and inflamed dysfunctional CD8 engagement were interpreted as linked ecosystem dimensions of the same translated state. This framing is important because the bulk arm was designed to evaluate a prespecified translation hypothesis, not to claim an exhaustive screen of all possible downstream phenotypes. The retained bridge genes, sign directions, and loading-derived weights were locked before downstream scoring and were not re-estimated against spatial, histologic, survival, or local treatment-response endpoints. Bridge composition and parameterization are disclosed in Supplementary Table S6, and bridge-selection and projection-impact summaries are provided in Supplementary Table S5. This bulk translation should be distinguished from cell-type deconvolution. The selected bridge was designed to carry the BSK-centered exclusion signal into whole-tumor RNA-seq, not to estimate the absolute fraction of BSK malignant cells or to remove all normal mucosal keratinocyte signal. Accordingly, tumors with substantial normal mucosal epithelial admixture could show attenuated bridge scores, and the bulk analyses were interpreted as cohort-level translational associations rather than as single-sample malignant-cell classification.

(B) Association between the selected BSK bridge and epithelial exclusion, showing epithelial exclusion as the strongest bulk correlate of the translated BSK signal. (C) Stage robustness of the bridge-to-exclusion relationship across the full cohort, early-stage tumors, and late-stage tumors, together with the stage-adjusted model summary. Using this selected bridge, epithelial exclusion emerged as the strongest bulk correlate of the translated BSK signal. The selected BSK bridge showed its clearest association with the epithelial exclusion score, whereas relationships with fibro-myeloid niche enrichment and inflamed dysfunctional CD8 engagement were more consistent with secondary ecosystem extensions of the same bulk state than with alternative primary representations (Fig. 5A–B; Supplementary Fig. S17; Supplementary Table S5). These findings suggest that the principal bulk transcriptomic representation of the BSK-associated spatial architecture is an exclusion-centered state rather than generalized stromalization alone.

We then examined whether this relationship was explained mainly by stage composition. In stage-adjusted models, the selected BSK bridge remained significantly associated with epithelial exclusion, and the direction of association was preserved in stage-stratified analyses (Fig. 5C; Supplementary Figs. S18–S19; Supplementary Table S5). By contrast, epithelial exclusion showed the clearest downstream connectivity to the surrounding ecosystem, being positively associated with fibro-myeloid niche scores (Fig. 5D; Supplementary Table S5) and negatively associated with inflamed dysfunctional CD8 engagement (Fig. 5E; Supplementary Table S5). Together, these results support epithelial exclusion as the primary bulk representation within this framework.

To test whether the exclusion-centered translational framework remained detectable beyond TCGA-CESC, we analyzed an independent whole-tumor RNA-seq cohort from CGCI-HTMCP-CC and restricted the primary validation set to discovery-phase primary tumors annotated as squamous cell neoplasms (n = 107). All locked signatures achieved complete gene coverage in this cohort. Within this external set, the BSK-centered bridge remained positively associated with epithelial exclusion (BSK_consensus_bridge versus epithelial_exclusion_score: Spearman rho = 0.377, p = 6.19 × 10−5), and epithelial exclusion remained positively associated with fibro-myeloid niche enrichment (rho = 0.360, p = 1.37 × 10−4) while showing an inverse association with terminal CD8 dysfunction-related signal (rho = −0.560, p = 3.73 × 10−10). These results reproduced the direction of the main translational relationships observed in the TCGA discovery framework and support the portability of the exclusion-centered bulk interpretation in an independent whole-tumor cohort (Supplementary Fig. S20).

To place this exclusion-centered bulk state within a pembrolizumab-relevant transcriptomic framework, we reconstructed four prespecified consensus signatures from the published pan-cancer pembrolizumab monotherapy benchmark (stroma/EMT/TGFβ, angiogenesis, gMDSC, and mMDSC) and mapped them against our locked bulk scores in both TCGA-CESC and CGCI-HTMCP-CC. Across the two cohorts, fibro_myeloid_niche_score showed the strongest and most reproducible orthogonal alignment with stroma/EMT/TGFβ (TCGA: rho = 0.660, p = 1.07 × 10−32; CGCI: rho = 0.668, p = 3.75 × 10−15) and angiogenesis (TCGA: rho = 0.449, p = 8.47 × 10−14; CGCI: rho = 0.432, p = 3.36 × 10−6), whereas BSK_consensus_bridge showed a more moderate but directionally consistent association with gMDSC (TCGA: rho = 0.303, p = 1.05 × 10−6; CGCI: rho = 0.327, p = 5.75 × 10−4). By contrast, mMDSC showed less stable cross-cohort coupling and was therefore not retained as a principal orthogonal anchor. Gene coverage remained high for all reconstructed signatures in both cohorts (range, 0.881–0.982), arguing against sparse overlap as the explanation for these associations. Together, these analyses place the exclusion-centered bulk framework within a stromal/angiogenic/myeloid resistance-associated transcriptomic axis previously linked to pembrolizumab response, while still stopping short of direct cervical cancer ICI response validation (Supplementary Fig. S21).

To further examine whether the bridge-to-ecosystem relationships were more consistent with an exclusion-centered ordering than with independent parallel associations, we evaluated mediation-oriented models across the same cohort. These analyses were statistically consistent with a model in which bridge-to-fibro and bridge-to-CD8 relationships were captured largely through epithelial exclusion (Fig. 5F; Supplementary Fig. S22; Supplementary Table S12). However, because the data are cross-sectional and observational, these models cannot determine causal direction or exclude bidirectional epithelial-stromal-myeloid feedback. Accordingly, fibro-myeloid enrichment and weaker inflamed dysfunctional CD8 engagement were interpreted as ecosystem correlates statistically coupled to exclusion-centered state, rather than as proven downstream consequences of epithelial exclusion. The absence of a dominant stage shift sufficient to account for the full bridge-to-exclusion relationship further supports the view that the bulk phenotype reflects biologically meaningful translation of the spatial architecture rather than a trivial clinicopathologic confounder (Supplementary Fig. S23; Supplementary Table S5).

An orthogonal proteogenomic analysis in PXD055203 supported this interpretation. After restricting analysis to squamous tumors and applying tumor-only normalization, subgroup-level separation was stronger than stage-level variation across both TMT and DIA protein quantification. In particular, ECM/stromal support was highest in Cluster1, whereas BSK-directional support was highest in Cluster2, indicating that the stromal and epithelial axes identified in the transcriptomic analyses map onto distinct proteomic strata in an independent cervical cancer cohort. By contrast, the immune-engagement module showed weaker subgroup separation, suggesting that the dominant orthogonal signal in this dataset lies in partitioning between matrix-rich and BSK-like epithelial states rather than in full recapitulation of the immune axis (Supplementary Fig. S24; Supplementary Table S7).

Whole-slide histology identifies a histologic correlate of the frozen epithelial-exclusion phenotype

We next asked whether the exclusion-associated state identified in the matched TCGA-CESC bulk cohort had a detectable morphology-level correlate in routine H&E histology. Using the matched TCGA-CESC whole-slide set, we analyzed 259 H&E slides from 250 patients and designated the relaxed epithelial-exclusion label as the primary pathology endpoint. All splitting was performed at the patient level so that replicate slides from the same patient remained within the same fold. Using 20 × 256 × 256-pixel Trident/uni_v1 patch embeddings and slide-level attention-based multiple-instance learning, the primary recognizer showed stable cross-validated performance, with a mean validation AUROC of 0.762 and a mean validation AUPRC of 0.799 across folds; aggregated out-of-fold performance remained above random with an AUROC of 0.734 and an AUPRC of 0.742, together with a balanced accuracy of 0.707 and an F1 score of 0.716 at the default threshold of 0.5 (Fig. 6A; Supplementary Fig. S25; Supplementary Table S13).

Because the pathology labels were derived from the matched TCGA-CESC bulk cohort, the aim of the whole-slide arm was not to define an independent pathology taxonomy but to test whether the exclusion-associated molecular state had a detectable morphologic correlate. Under strictly patient-level out-of-fold evaluation, patient-level morphology scores were positively correlated with bulk epithelial exclusion (rho = 0.398, p = 6.28 × 10−11, n = 250), and morphology scores increased progressively across epithelial-exclusion tertiles (Kruskal-Wallis p = 6.76 × 10−9) (Fig. 6B–C; Supplementary Fig. S26; Supplementary Table S13). These findings indicate that routine H&E captures a non-random histologic correlate of the same cross-layer state. Because the morphology endpoint was derived from the matched TCGA-CESC bulk framework, this analysis was interpreted as patient-level out-of-fold morphology correlation within the TCGA pathology arm rather than as independent external whole-slide validation.

To complement the matched TCGA-derived whole-slide arm, we performed a local blinded manual H&E review of pretreatment biopsy sections from Fujian Cancer Hospital. This analysis was designed as an interpretability-oriented support layer rather than an external validation cohort and was therefore analyzed separately from the primary TCGA pathology endpoint. The standardized composite manual H&E score showed positive alignment with the out-of-fold morphology score (Fig. 6D; Supplementary Table S13), indicating that part of the model-derived signal was also recognizable by human review. The strongest concordance arose from stromal reaction and lymphocyte-distribution / exclusion-like features, whereas sharply defined boundary features were less consistent, supporting the interpretation that the histology signal reflects exclusion-associated tissue organization rather than a single pathognomonic border pattern. Because the BSK framework was developed around squamous stress-keratinization biology, this local manual review was not interpreted as evidence that the morphology signal generalizes to non-squamous cervical cancer histologies.

As exploratory treated-patient support, we further evaluated a local 18-case neoadjuvant immunotherapy-treated cervical squamous carcinoma subset with postoperative institutional pathological response annotations after excluding adenocarcinoma and other non-squamous histologies. The response grades were light in five cases, moderate in eight cases, and marked in five cases (Supplementary Fig. S27A; Supplementary Table S14). Case-level manual H&E composite scores were positively associated with ordinal pathological response grade (Spearman rho = 0.805, p = 5.62 × 10−5; Kruskal-Wallis p = 0.0038; Supplementary Fig. S27B; Supplementary Table S14). Case-level Trident/ABMIL-derived morphology scores showed a similar association with response grade (rho = 0.718, p = 0.0008; Kruskal-Wallis p = 0.0077; Supplementary Fig. S27C; Supplementary Table S14). Manual H&E composite scores were also positively aligned with Trident/ABMIL-derived morphology scores at the case level (rho = 0.613, p = 0.0069) and across paired pretreatment slide-level observations (n = 23; rho = 0.469, p = 0.0241; Supplementary Fig. S27D; Supplementary Table S14). A representative whole-section pilot mIF assessment provided descriptive visual support for the spatial concurrence of KRT17-high PanCK-positive tumor nests, α-SMA-associated stromal boundary formation, CD68-positive myeloid participation, and exclusion-like CD8 localization within the same tissue context (Supplementary Fig. S28; Supplementary Table S15). These local data were interpreted as exploratory response-associated clinical-pathology support rather than as powered predictive validation. Because this retrospective 18-case cohort received neoadjuvant combination treatment and used institutional postoperative pathological response grading as an ordinal endpoint, the positive association should be viewed as evidence that the H&E-derived morphology score has clinical-pathological correlates in treated cervical squamous carcinoma. It should not be interpreted as showing that the BSK/exclusion axis, or exclusion-like morphology alone, predicts immunotherapy benefit or resistance direction.

Error-audit analyses supported this interpretation. Out-of-fold predictions yielded 96 true-positive, 87 true-negative, 43 false-positive, and 33 false-negative slides. True-positive and false-negative slides were both enriched for positive bulk epithelial exclusion relative to true-negative and false-positive slides, whereas false-positive slides were centered predominantly in the bulk-negative direction. Accordingly, the exemplar panels and attention overlays are best interpreted as morphology-oriented evidence for a detectable histologic correlate of the same exclusion-associated biology rather than as stand-alone classifier demonstrations (Fig. 6E; Supplementary Figs. S26 and S29).

Secondary whole-slide analyses were retained as supportive extensions rather than co-primary objectives. Transfer of the relaxed morphology representation to the stricter exclusion endpoint remained feasible but less stable than the primary task, yielding a mean validation AUROC of 0.819 and a mean validation AUPRC of 0.477, with aggregated out-of-fold AUROC and AUPRC of 0.709 and 0.250, respectively. When the strict-derived score was further evaluated against the BSK-linked strict subset as a ranking endpoint, enrichment remained detectable at the high-score tail (AUROC = 0.732; AUPRC = 0.183; top-20% enrichment OR = 7.01; p = 5.3 × 10−4). Together, these extension analyses suggest that the relaxed recognizer retains biologic specificity, while supporting the interpretation that strict transfer and BSK-linked ranking should remain secondary readouts rather than replacement primary endpoints.

Cross-layer synthesis links the BSK-associated state to spatial organization, epithelial exclusion, and morphology-level correlation

Across the analytic layers, a consistent pattern emerged. The single-cell discovery cohort identified BSK as the principal cross-sectional lesion-grade-associated malignant epithelial program; the spatial cohort localized this program to a BSK-rich tumor core bordered by stromal-myeloid structure and a peripheral lymphoid shell; the bulk cohort represented the same biology through an epithelial-exclusion phenotype; and the pathology arm supported an associated histomorphologic correlate in routine H&E sections (Fig. 7). Together, these results support a cross-layer interpretation in which a BSK-like malignant epithelial state is associated with boundary-structured immune exclusion and remains detectable across single-cell, spatial, bulk transcriptomic, and histologic scales.

Epithelial exclusion is positioned as the primary translational node within the locked framework linking BSK-associated spatial organization to morphology-level correlation

Discussion

Cervical squamous carcinoma remains clinically challenging because benefit from immunotherapy is heterogeneous and conventional clinicopathologic variables do not adequately capture immune-access phenotypes [3–5]. Recent single-cell, single-nucleus, spatial, and multiomic studies have established marked epithelial, stromal, and immune heterogeneity in cervical cancer, but the malignant epithelial programs most tightly linked to immune-vulnerable states remain insufficiently resolved [8–12]. In this study, we used a staged human-data design to connect malignant cell state, tissue architecture, bulk transcriptomes, and routine histology. Across these layers, a basal-squamous stress keratinization (BSK) program emerged as the malignant epithelial transcriptional program most consistently linked to immune exclusion. We interpret BSK as a continuous, operationally defined program rather than as a discrete histologic subtype or a binary cell-state class. BSK marked a BSK-rich tumor core separated from a peripheral lymphoid compartment by stromal-myeloid structure, was translated in bulk most clearly as epithelial exclusion, and showed a measurable correlate in whole-slide H&E. Directional preservation in an independent whole-tumor cohort and orthogonal alignment with stromal/EMT/TGFβ, angiogenesis, and gMDSC-related programs place this framework within a clinically relevant resistance-associated context. However, these analyses do not establish predictive value for immunotherapy response, which will require prospective or adequately powered retrospective testing in treated cervical cancer cohorts with standardized response and survival endpoints. If such cohorts show no association between BSK/exclusion scores and progression-free survival after anti-PD-1 therapy, the clinical-translation component of the model would need to be narrowed: BSK would remain an immune-access and tissue-architecture-associated state, but would not be considered a standalone predictor of anti-PD-1 benefit or patient outcome.

Across analytic layers, the main interpretation was supported by concordant but non-equivalent forms of evidence. The discovery layer identified BSK within a retained three-component working decomposition; the spatial layer supported a boundary-structured core-boundary-shell architecture through shell summaries, interface gradients, permutation controls, and leave-one-section-out recalculation; the bulk layer supported an exclusion-centered translation framework that remained directionally preserved after stage adjustment and in an independent whole-tumor cohort; and the pathology layer supported a detectable morphology-level correlate of the same phenotype under strictly out-of-fold patient-level evaluation. Taken together, these analyses support a coherent cross-layer interpretation while also clarifying that the evidentiary strength and intended role of each layer are not identical.

Importantly, this interpretation was strengthened not only by independent whole-tumor validation in CGCI-HTMCP-CC but also by orthogonal alignment with transcriptomic programs previously associated with pembrolizumab monotherapy response. In response to the lack of direct immunotherapy-treated clinical-pathology support in the original submission, we added a local neoadjuvant immunotherapy-treated cervical squamous carcinoma subset with postoperative institutional pathological response annotations. Because the response cohort and H&E morphology analysis were derived from pretreatment slides from the same patients, the local analysis provides exploratory response-associated clinical-pathology context for H&E-derived exclusion-associated morphology in treated patients. However, this cohort was retrospective, modest in size, treated in a neoadjuvant combination setting, and based on institutional pathological response grading rather than standardized pCR/MPR, survival, durable clinical benefit, or progression-free survival endpoints. Therefore, the observed positive association between morphology scores and postoperative pathological response grade should be interpreted only as a clinical-pathological association of the H&E-derived morphology score in this small treated cohort. It should not be taken to mean that BSK/exclusion biology or exclusion-like morphology independently predicts immunotherapy sensitivity, resistance, or the direction of clinical benefit. Across both TCGA-CESC and CGCI-HTMCP-CC, the fibro-myeloid niche axis showed its most reproducible positive coupling to stroma/EMT/TGFβ and angiogenesis, whereas the translated BSK bridge showed a moderate but directionally consistent association with gMDSC. This pattern supports the view that the exclusion-centered state identified here lies within a broader stromal/angiogenic/myeloid resistance-like continuum relevant to checkpoint therapy. This interpretation is consistent with broader oncology literature linking angiogenic biology, including VEGF-related prognostic programs, cytokine-mediated tumor sensitization, and TNF-α/VEGFC-associated lymphangiogenic remodeling in cervical cancer to treatment vulnerability and tumor-microenvironmental progression, while still falling short of direct cervical cancer-specific ICI response validation [35–37].

This interpretation is conceptually important because the BSK-associated state is better viewed as a boundary-structured exclusion program than as a generic stromalized or immune-cold phenotype. However, the spatial core-boundary-shell pattern should not be interpreted as a temporal sequence of BSK emergence or stabilization. The current data show that BSK is associated with lesion grade and spatial organization in cross-sectional tissue snapshots, but they do not determine whether BSK represents a progressive transition state, a stable malignant differentiation state, or a state maintained by reciprocal epithelial–stromal–immune interactions. This distinction is important because prior cervical cancer and broader tumor-interface studies have shown that spatial architecture, stromal barriers, and fibroblast-immune crosstalk can shape immune access and therapeutic vulnerability rather than merely reflect differences in total immune abundance [11, 12, 16–18, 20–23]. In the discovery cohort, BSK was inversely related to malignant-intrinsic fibro-like features, arguing against a trivial epithelial-to-fibro mimicry explanation. In tissue space, BSK resolved into a tumor-core-to-outer-shell organization in which a BSK-rich epithelial compartment was bordered by stromal-myeloid structure, whereas lymphoid components were retained predominantly in a peripheral shell. Within the pre-specified exclusion-centered bulk translation framework, the selected BSK bridge aligned most strongly with epithelial exclusion, and epithelial exclusion showed the clearest statistical coupling to fibro-myeloid enrichment and reduced inflamed dysfunctional CD8 engagement. Because these relationships were inferred from cross-sectional bulk data, they should be interpreted as an exclusion-centered ordering consistent with the data rather than as proof that epithelial exclusion causally precedes stromal or myeloid remodeling. Together, these results support an associative model in which the BSK program marks a spatially organized uncoupling between malignant epithelium and effective immune access. The candidate NOTCH, ECM-integrin, and matrix-sensing ligand-receptor axes provide plausible communication routes by which BSK-high epithelial compartments and adjacent stromal-myeloid niches may interact, but these axes remain transcriptomically inferred and hypothesis-generating. The current study does not experimentally dissect whether these epithelial-stromal communication programs are required for CD8+ exclusion or dysfunction, nor does it establish that BSK is necessary or sufficient to drive immune exclusion.

The pathology layer adds translational accessibility to this framework, but its role should be stated carefully. The whole-slide arm did not define a new independent disease axis; rather, it tested whether a phenotype frozen in the matched TCGA-CESC bulk cohort had a detectable correlate in routine H&E morphology under strictly out-of-fold patient-level separation. The whole-slide pathology analysis should therefore be interpreted as morphology-level correlation of a matched molecular phenotype under patient-level out-of-fold evaluation, not as independent external validation of a deployable pathology classifier. The association between patient-level morphology scores and bulk epithelial exclusion, together with duplicate-patient sensitivity analyses and the error-audit structure, supports the interpretation that the recognizer captured a non-trivial histomorphologic correlate of the cross-layer state rather than a generic prognostic image signal. The stricter transfer and BSK-linked ranking analyses further suggest biologic specificity, but these should remain secondary extension readouts rather than replacement primary pathology endpoints.

This interpretation was further supported by a local manual H&E review using pretreatment biopsy slides from Fujian Cancer Hospital. This local analysis was designed as a pragmatic human-read interpretability layer rather than as an independent validation cohort. Because the BSK framework is centered on squamous stress-keratinization biology, the response-associated local analysis was restricted to cervical squamous cell carcinoma, and adenocarcinoma and other non-squamous histologies were excluded. We did not test the non-squamous subset independently because the excluded non-squamous cases were too few and biologically outside the squamous keratinization-centered framework to support reliable subset inference. Within this restricted interpretability role, the manual composite showed directionally coherent alignment with the model-derived morphology signal, with the strongest contribution arising from stromal reaction and lymphocyte-distribution features rather than from all predefined morphology items equally. This pattern supports the view that the whole-slide recognizer captured at least partly recognizable exclusion-associated morphology rather than an entirely black-box image signal, while leaving generalizability to non-squamous cervical cancer histologies unresolved.

Several limitations define the interpretation of this work. First, this is an observational human-data study and therefore supports association and spatial co-organization rather than causal proof that BSK epithelial states actively drive immune exclusion. The current analyses cannot distinguish whether BSK promotes immune exclusion, emerges in response to stromal or immune microenvironmental pressures, or co-develops with a boundary-structured niche. Similarly, the mediation-oriented bulk models cannot resolve bidirectional feedback between malignant epithelial programs, stromal remodeling, myeloid enrichment, and immune dysfunction. These models were retained as ordering-consistency and variance-partitioning analyses within a frozen observational framework, not as causal mediation analyses. Functional perturbation studies, epithelial–stromal–immune co-culture systems, organoid-based models, or spatially resolved longitudinal sampling will be required to determine whether BSK is a causal driver, a consequence, or a stable co-marker of immune-excluded tissue architecture. Similarly, the inferred NOTCH, ECM-integrin, matrix-sensing, fibro-myeloid remodeling, and CD8-related dysfunction signals should be interpreted as candidate mechanistic links rather than experimentally validated pathways. Direct perturbation of BSK-associated epithelial programs and candidate ligand-receptor axes will be required to determine whether they actively regulate stromal boundary formation, CD8+ T-cell localization, or immune dysfunction. Second, malignant program discovery was derived from nine samples, and the initial malignant epithelial annotation was based on a conservative composite transcriptomic strategy. To address the possibility that highly stressed or inflamed non-malignant squamous epithelial cells could have been retained as putative malignant epithelial cells, we added an inferCNV-derived CNV-like audit. This analysis showed higher CNV-like burden in putative malignant epithelial cells than in non-malignant epithelial cells and further enrichment of CNV-like burden in BSK-high putative malignant epithelial cells (Supplementary Fig. S5F-I; Supplementary Table S9). These results support malignant enrichment of the BSK-associated epithelial compartment. However, inferCNV is based on transcriptome-derived large-scale CNV-like signal and is not equivalent to matched genomic copy-number profiling. Therefore, although the CNV-like audit reduces the likelihood that the BSK signal simply reflects stressed normal squamous epithelium, it does not fully eliminate the possibility of residual stressed non-malignant epithelial contamination. Rank-sensitivity and leave-one-sample-out analyses consistently recovered a BSK-like loading structure, but sample ordering was less invariant across alternative ranks and the analysis does not establish k = 3 as uniquely optimal. Accordingly, the three-component NMF solution should be viewed as a parsimonious working decomposition for downstream translation rather than an exhaustive map of epithelial heterogeneity. The main conclusion therefore does not depend on treating the full three-component NMF solution as a definitive disease taxonomy; rather, it depends on the recurrent detectability and cross-layer portability of the BSK-like loading-defined program. In addition, no single BSK-associated gene, gene module, or histologic feature should be interpreted as necessary or sufficient for BSK classification across modalities. The cross-layer analyses instead use a locked transcriptional program, a pre-specified bulk bridge, and morphology-level correlates to represent related but non-identical readouts of the same exclusion-associated framework. Third, the primary spatial arm included four analyzable sections, so the boundary model rests on directional concordance, shell/interface consistency, tumor-rich subset analyses, within-section permutation controls, and leave-one-section-out robustness rather than large-scale spatial replication. Adjacent serial sections or additional geographic regions from the same tumor blocks were not available in the public spatial dataset, and we therefore could not run the spatial boundary scripts on paired adjacent sections or same-block geographically distinct sections. In addition, individual spots within a section are spatially autocorrelated and should not be treated as statistically independent biological replicates. For this reason, spot-level OLS models were retained only as descriptive covariate-adjusted local-association checks, and their p values were not used as the primary evidence for the boundary model. We did not use spatial autoregressive or mixed-effects models as the main inferential framework because the four-section design provides limited section-level replication for stable random-effect estimation and makes spatial-autocorrelation modeling sensitive to neighborhood and residual-structure assumptions. Accordingly, the spatial findings should be interpreted as section-consistent support for a boundary-structured pattern within the available sections rather than as independent-spot statistical proof, adjacent-section validation, within-block structural replication, or a definitive spatial atlas of cervical squamous carcinoma. The same limitation applies to temporal interpretation. Because the single-cell and spatial datasets are cross-sectional, the observed normal–HSIL–squamous carcinoma ordering and core-boundary-shell architecture cannot resolve whether BSK reflects temporal progression, stable malignant differentiation, or microenvironmentally maintained state coexistence. Longitudinal sampling, lineage tracing, perturbation models, or temporally staged organoid and co-culture systems will be required to define the temporal evolution and stability of the BSK state. Fourth, the bulk bridge was evaluated within a prespecified exclusion-oriented translation framework, so epithelial exclusion should be interpreted as the primary tested representation rather than the only possible bulk correlate of BSK biology. In addition, the bridge score was derived from whole-tumor bulk RNA-seq and does not explicitly deconvolve malignant epithelial cells from contaminating normal mucosal keratinocytes. Therefore, samples with a high fraction of non-malignant mucosal epithelium could show dilution or attenuation of the translated BSK/exclusion signal. The bulk bridge should be viewed as a cohort-level translational approximation of the BSK/exclusion framework rather than as a purified malignant-cell score or a single-sample diagnostic classifier. Spatially guided sampling, tumor-enrichment, microdissection, or matched single-cell/spatial validation will be required to determine how robust the bridge score is to normal mucosal epithelial admixture in future clinical specimens. Fifth, the whole-slide pathology arm demonstrates morphology-level correlation of a matched molecular phenotype, but not independent external validation of a de novo classifier or deployable pathology biomarker. Independent external whole-slide validation was not available in the current study. The local manual H&E review provided exploratory human-read interpretability support, but it did not constitute independent external validation of the TCGA-trained whole-slide model. The response-associated local analysis was restricted to cervical squamous carcinoma after exclusion of adenocarcinoma and other non-squamous histologies. Therefore, the current study does not test whether the BSK-associated H&E morphology signal applies to adenocarcinoma or other non-squamous cervical cancer subsets, and the excluded non-squamous records were too few to support independent subset analysis. Although we added a local neoadjuvant immunotherapy-treated cervical squamous carcinoma subset with postoperative institutional pathological response annotations, this cohort was retrospective and modest in size. The response-associated analyses were based on institutional pathological response grading rather than standardized pCR/MPR, survival, or durable clinical benefit endpoints. The pilot mIF analysis was limited to one representative whole-section descriptive case and was not designed as quantitative spatial validation. Therefore, the local treated cohort provides exploratory clinical-pathology support rather than powered predictive validation. A future anti-PD-1-treated validation cohort showing no association between BSK/exclusion scores and progression-free survival would argue against standalone predictive value of the BSK/exclusion axis for anti-PD-1 benefit. Such a null result would not necessarily negate the cross-layer tissue-architecture observation that BSK is associated with immune-access uncoupling, but it would indicate that this architecture alone is insufficient to determine clinical outcome. Under that scenario, the model would need to be revised toward a context-dependent immune-access stratification framework in which anti-PD-1 outcome also depends on additional determinants such as antigen presentation, PD-L1 biology, HPV-related antigenicity, baseline effector T-cell competence, stromal/myeloid suppression, treatment regimen, and clinical stage. Prospective immunotherapy-treated cervical cancer cohorts with standardized response endpoints, survival follow-up, harmonized PD-L1/HPV and treatment-regimen covariates, and independent validation will be required to determine whether the BSK/exclusion axis has standalone or context-dependent predictive value. These limitations notwithstanding, the convergence of single-cell, spatial, bulk, and histology layers supports the robustness of the main cross-layer interpretation.

This framework aligns directly with ongoing clinical trials such as KEYNOTE-826 and ENGOT-cx11/KEYNOTE-A18, where immune checkpoint blockade outcomes remain heterogeneous [3, 4]. Patient-level H&E morphology scores derived from the relaxed epithelial-exclusion recognizer may provide a scalable morphology-linked stratification readout for future study. However, any integration into routine pathology reporting or treatment selection would require independent external whole-slide validation and direct testing in cervical cancer-specific immunotherapy-treated cohorts.

Conclusions

Overall, our data support an association between a BSK-like malignant epithelial state and an exclusion-prone tissue architecture in cervical squamous carcinoma. This state was spatially organized, represented in bulk transcriptomes, partially reflected in routine H&E morphology, and supported by exploratory local pathology together with a representative pilot multiplex immunofluorescence observation. These findings provide a human-data-derived framework for future investigation of immune-access stratification. However, the current study does not establish BSK as a causal driver of immune exclusion or as a validated predictor of immunotherapy response. Independent external pathology validation and prospective evaluation in adequately powered immunotherapy-treated cohorts remain necessary.
