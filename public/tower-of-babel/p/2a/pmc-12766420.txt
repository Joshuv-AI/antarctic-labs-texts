Increased risk of early-onset gastric cancer among young adults age 20–49 years with diabetes: a nationwide cohort study
Authors: Tae-Se Kim, Kyungdo Han, Yang Won Min, Hyuk Lee, Jun Haeng Lee, Poong-Lyul Rhee, Jae J Kim, Byung-Hoon Min
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundAlthough overall gastric cancer (GC) incidence has declined, the incidence of early-onset GC (EOGC) is rising. Given the increasing prevalence of diabetes in younger populations and its link to malignancies, we investigated the association between glycemic status and EOGC risk.MethodsThis nationwide cohort study followed 2,439,112 adults (aged 20–49 years) without a prior cancer diagnosis who underwent national health screening in 2012, through 2022. EOGC risk was assessed using multivariable Cox models, with restricted cubic spline and sensitivity analyses (inverse probability weighting [IPW] and exclusion of early diabetes).FindingsThe incidence rate (IR) per 1000 person-years rose with worsening glycemic status: 0.23 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.22–0.23) in individuals with normoglycemia, 0.30 (95% CI, 0.28–0.32) in individuals with impaired fasting glucose, 0.41 (95% CI, 0.34–0.48) in newly diagnosed individuals with diabetes, 0.51 (95% CI, 0.40–0.64) in individuals with diabetes <5 years, and 0.62 (95% CI, 0.48–0.80) in individuals with diabetes ≥5 years. In adjusted models, EOGC risk was higher in newly diagnosed diabetes (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.27; 95% CI, 1.07–1.52), diabetes <5 years (aHR, 1.36; 95% CI, 1.07–1.74), and diabetes ≥5 years (aHR, 1.53; 95% CI, 1.17–2.01). Results were consistent in spline, IPW, and exclusion analyses. Current smoking and both light-to-moderate and heavy alcohol consumption were associated with increased risk across all glycemic groups, with the highest risk in diabetes.InterpretationWorsening glycemic status is associated with increased risk of EOGC in a dose-dependent manner. Optimal glycemic control, smoking cessation and reduced alcohol consumption may mitigate EOGC risk. However, residual confounding, missing data on Helicobacter pylori infection, family history, dietary factors, and histological subtypes, together with potential detection bias and limited information on glucose-lowering therapy or longitudinal glycemic status, should be acknowledged as limitations.FundingNone.Keywords: Age factors, Diabetes mellitus, Incidence, Stomach neoplasms

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed for articles published in English from database inception to December 31, 2024, using the terms "diabetes", "gastric cancer", "early-onset gastric cancer", "young adults", "smoking", and "alcohol consumption". Although several studies have explored the association between diabetes and gastric cancer, most have focused on the general population without distinguishing between early- and late-onset disease. Evidence specifically addressing early-onset gastric cancer (EOGC), and its interplay with modifiable lifestyle factors, such as smoking and alcohol consumption, is still scarce.

Added value of this study

This nationwide cohort study of over 2.4 million young adults is the first to quantify EOGC risk across the full spectrum of glycemic status. We found a dose-dependent increase in EOGC risk with worsening glycemic status, including impaired fasting glucose and diabetes. Current smoking and both light-to-moderate and heavy alcohol consumption were associated with an increased risk of EOGC across all glycemic status groups. These findings provide epidemiological evidence of the potential combined impact of metabolic and lifestyle factors on EOGC development in young adults.

Implications of all the available evidence

The rising prevalence of diabetes and unhealthy lifestyle behaviors in young adults, along with increasing EOGC incidence, underscores an emerging public health concern. Current evidence, including our findings, indicates that worsening glycemic status, smoking, and alcohol use are jointly associated with increased EOGC risk. However, the association between diabetes and EOGC is subject to several confounding factors, like socioeconomic status, environmental exposures, access to medical care, medication use, and lifestyle factors and detection bias may also play a role. These limitations suggest that the observed associations should be interpreted with caution. Nonetheless, our findings support the potential value of early glycemic control and behavioral interventions in younger adults.

Introduction

Although gastric cancer (GC) remains a global health concern, its incidence has gradually declined in recent years.1,2 Conversely, the incidence of early-onset GC (EOGC), defined as GC occurring in individuals under 50 years of age, has shown an upward trend.2,3 EOGC is associated with a higher risk of long-term health complications, such as infertility and cardiovascular disease, which contribute to reduced productivity and increased healthcare costs that constitute significant social and economic burdens for society.4 Therefore, investigating the risk factors for EOGC is essential not only for individual patient care but also from a broader public health perspective.

Hereditary GC is rare, with germline mutations causing only 3–5% of all GC cases; the majority arise from somatic mutations.5 Given that somatic mutations responsible for cancer development accumulate over an extended period, the increase in EOGC among individuals under 50 years of age is likely driven by environmental factors, as well as genetic predisposition. Established and potential environmental factors include Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection, smoking, alcohol consumption, and obesity. Diabetes has also been linked to the development of various malignancies6; however, its association with EOGC remains unclear.2,7, 8, 9 In South Korea, the prevalences of H. pylori infection and smoking have declined.10, 11, 12 However, the prevalence of diabetes among younger individuals increased more than fourfold from 2002 to 2016.13 Investigation of the relationship between diabetes and EOGC may provide valuable insights into underlying causes of the rising incidence of EOGC and inform strategies for its prevention.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the association between glycemic status and EOGC risk using a nationwide cohort. Additionally, we sought to examine relationships between modifiable risk factors and EOGC risk according to glycemic status.

Methods

Data sources

The Korean National Health Insurance Service (KNHIS), a single-payer system covering 97% of the South Korean population, offers biennial health screenings for individuals aged 40 years and older, as well as for all employees regardless of age. Approximately 72% of eligible individuals participate in this health screening program.14 Our analysis utilized KNHIS data, which include health examination records (e.g., laboratory results, anthropometric measurements, and self-reported behaviors) and claims information (e.g., medical services, prescriptions, and ICD-10-CM diagnoses).

EOGC definition and study population

The primary endpoint was newly diagnosed EOGC, defined as GC diagnosed before the age of 50 years. GC cases were identified using the ICD-10-CM code C16 and the cancer-specific reimbursement code V193. Since 2006, the KNHIS has offered reduced copayments (up to 5%) for cancer-related care through the V193 code, which is assigned after physician certification of a confirmed cancer diagnosis.15,16

Fig. 1 illustrates the flowchart of the study population. Initially, the study included 4,910,068 individuals aged 20 years or older who underwent health examinations through the KNHIS between January 1 and December 31, 2012. Of these, 2,261,691 individuals aged over 50 years were excluded, leaving 2,648,377 individuals aged 20–49 years. Further exclusions were applied for participants diagnosed with type 1 diabetes mellitus (n = 463), those with a cancer diagnosis prior to cohort entry (n = 26,690), and individuals with missing data regarding key study variables (n = 178,866). Participants with missing information on covariates were excluded from the analyses. The extent of missingness for each covariate is presented in Supplementary Table 4. No other covariates had missing values. To address potential preexisting conditions and early detection bias,17 individuals who developed cancer or died within the first year of follow-up (n = 3246) were also excluded. Ultimately, 2,439,112 participants were included in the final analysis. The cohort was followed until the occurrence of EOGC, death, the end of the calendar year in which participants turned 49 years old, or December 31, 2022—whichever occurred first.

Flowchart of the study population.

Definition of glycemic status

Fasting plasma glucose (FPG) levels were measured for all participants as part of the national health examination program. Blood samples were collected by certified healthcare professionals at KNHIS-approved hospitals after overnight fasting. Participants were categorized into three glycemic groups: normoglycemia (FPG <100 mg/dL), impaired fasting glucose (IFG, FPG 100–125 mg/dL), and diabetes (FPG ≥126 mg/dL or at least one annual prescription claim for antidiabetic medication coded under ICD-10-CM E11.x–E14.x). These classifications are consistent with widely accepted criteria from previous studies.15,18 Diabetes duration was determined based on the date of the first recorded diagnosis of diabetes and the initiation of glucose-lowering medication prior to the health examination (index date). Patients were classified as having diabetes duration ≥5 years if the interval between the first diagnosis and the index date was 5 years or longer.

Definition of clinical variables

In accordance with the Sex and Gender Equity in Research (SAGER) guidelines, sex was defined as a biological variable, and information on participants' sex (male/female) was obtained from the KNHIS database.

Information on health-related behaviors was gathered through a standardized, self-administered questionnaire during the national health examination.

Smoking status was categorized as never-smoker, former smoker, and current smoker. Never-smokers were defined as individuals who had smoked fewer than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime.16 Alcohol consumption was classified based on average daily intake: none, light-to-moderate (<30 g/day), and heavy drinking (≥30 g/day).19 Regular physical activity was defined as engaging in vigorous-intensity exercise for at least 20 min on ≥3 days per week, or moderate-intensity exercise for at least 30 min on ≥5 days per week.

Anthropometric data, including height, weight, and waist circumference, were measured directly during the national health examinations. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing body weight (kg) by the square of height (m2). Blood pressure, both systolic and diastolic, was measured after participants had been seated and at rest for at least 5 min. Blood tests were performed to assess levels of total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and creatinine. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using a creatinine-based formula derived from the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease study.20 Chronic kidney disease (CKD) was defined as an eGFR of less than 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 or a history of dialysis prior to the health screening, identified by reimbursement codes (V001, V003, or V005).21

Dyslipidemia was identified by either a serum total cholesterol level ≥240 mg/dL or the presence of an ICD-10-CM code (E78) along with prescriptions for lipid-lowering medications.22 Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg, or ICD-10-CM codes (I10–I13 and I15) associated with claims for antihypertensive therapy. Low-income status was defined as individuals in the lowest quartile of the mandatory insurance fee or those receiving free medical services.

Statistics

Baseline characteristics were analyzed using analysis of variance for continuous variables and the chi-square (χ2) test for categorical variables. The distribution of continuous variables was visually inspected using histograms. Triglyceride showed a skewed distribution and was therefore log-transformed for analysis, with results presented as geometric means. Given the very large sample size, we did not perform formal tests of normality, as the central limit theorem ensures approximate normality of the mean. The incidence rate (IR) of EOGC was calculated by dividing the number of new cases by 1000 person-years of follow-up. Kaplan–Meier curves were constructed to illustrate the cumulative incidence of EOGC according to glycemic status, and log-rank tests were used to compare differences across groups.

To examine the dose-response relationship between glycemic status and EOGC, we used multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We specified a directed acyclic graph (DAG) to guide covariate selection (Fig. 3). Based on clinical reasoning and prior literature, age, sex, income, smoking, alcohol intake, regular exercise, and BMI were treated as confounders; hypertension, dyslipidemia, and CKD were considered possible mediators. Cox models were fitted hierarchically: Model 1 presented unadjusted results. Model 2 adjusted for age and sex. Model 3 further adjusted for potential confounders including income, smoking status, alcohol consumption, regular exercise, and BMI. Model 4 additionally included potential mediators (hypertension, dyslipidemia, and CKD. In a sensitivity analysis, we additionally applied inverse probability weighting (IPW) based on age and all covariates included in the final model to balance the distribution of baseline characteristics across groups. Subgroup analyses were conducted by adding the subgroup-defining variable and its multiplicative interaction term with glycemic status to the final Cox model. The significance of interactions was assessed using Wald chi-square tests, and P values for interaction were reported.

Directed acyclic graph illustrating the hypothesized relationships among diabetes, early-onset gastric cancer (EOGC), and covariates. Age, sex, socioeconomic status (income), lifestyle factors (smoking, alcohol consumption, and regular exercise), and body mass index were conceptualized as potential confounders. Hypertension, dyslipidemia, and chronic kidney disease were considered possible mediators on the pathway from diabetes to EOGC.

To evaluate the dose–response association between FPG levels and the risk of EOGC, we performed restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression within a Cox proportional hazards framework. Five knots were placed at FPG values of 70, 100, 126, 140, and 200 mg/dL, with 100 mg/dL set as the reference. The Cox model was adjusted for age, sex, income, body mass index, smoking, alcohol consumption, regular exercise, hypertension, dyslipidemia, CKD, and antidiabetic medication use. Because diabetes status was defined based on both fasting glucose and antidiabetic medication use in the main analyses, medication use was not included as a covariate in those models. However, in the RCS analysis where fasting glucose itself was the exposure of interest, antidiabetic medication use was additionally adjusted to mitigate potential confounding. Likelihood ratio tests were used to assess non-linearity, and both overall and non-linearity P values were reported.

In supplementary analyses, we additionally examined the incidence of diabetes during follow-up, defined as a new diagnosis of diabetes (ICD-10 codes E11–E14) accompanied by a prescription of antidiabetic agents after cohort entry. Because serial health examination data were not available, time-varying analyses could not be performed. Instead, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by excluding participants with pre-existing diabetes at baseline and those who developed diabetes within 2 years after cohort entry, to minimize potential misclassification of glycemic status during follow-up.

The statistical significance threshold was regarded as P < 0.05 for all two-sided tests. All analyses were performed using Statistical Analysis System (SAS) software (version 9.3; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Ethics

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Samsung Medical Center, Seoul, Korea (approval no. 2025-05-063). The requirement for written informed consent was waived because the analysis utilized deidentified and anonymized data provided by the KNHIS. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

This research received no specific grant from any funding agency. As no funding was provided, there was no role of funders in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report.

Results

Baseline characteristics and incidence of diabetes during follow-up

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the study population (n = 2,439,112) according to glycemic status. Normoglycemia, IFG, and diabetes were observed in 1,871,053 (76.7%), 470,901 (19.3%), and 97,158 (4.0%) individuals, respectively. A trend of worsening clinical and metabolic profiles was observed as glycemic status progressed from normoglycemia to IFG to diabetes (P < 0.0001 for all variables). BMI and abdominal obesity increased steadily across the groups, with the highest prevalence observed in the diabetes group. Comorbidities such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, and CKD also became more prevalent with worsening glycemic status. Metabolic parameters deteriorated progressively, with increases in fasting glucose and triglyceride levels, and a decrease in HDL-C levels as glycemic status worsened. Lifestyle factors, including smoking and heavy alcohol consumption, were more commonly reported in the IFG and diabetes groups than in the normoglycemia group.

During follow-up, 46,775 participants in the normoglycemia group (2.5%) and 44,646 in the IFG group (9.5%) developed incident diabetes (Supplementary Table 2).

Dose-dependent associations between glycemic status and EOGC

The association between glycemic status and the EOGC risk is shown in Table 2. Over approximately 17.9 million (17,878,333.1) person-years of follow-up, 4400 cases of EOGC were diagnosed, yielding an IR of 0.2461 per 1000 person-years (95% CI, 0.24–0.25). The IR of EOGC progressively increased with worsening glycemic status. In the normoglycemia group, the IR was 0.23 (95% CI, 0.22–0.23) per 1000 person-years; it rose to 0.30 (95% CI, 0.28–0.32) in the IFG group. The IR further increased to 0.41 (95% CI, 0.34–0.48), 0.51 (95% CI, 0.40–0.64), and 0.62 (95% CI, 0.48–0.80) per 1000 person-years in the newly diagnosed diabetes, diabetes duration <5 years, and diabetes duration ≥5 years groups, respectively. The Kaplan–Meier curve demonstrated a dose-dependent relationship between glycemic status and EOGC risk (Fig. 2; P for trend <0.0001).

Guided by the directed acyclic graph (DAG) shown in Fig. 3, we estimated hazard ratios hierarchically. In Models 1–3, worsening glycemic status was consistently associated with an increased risk of EOGC. In the fully adjusted model (Model 4), IFG was not significantly associated with risk (HR 1.06, 95% CI 0.98–1.14), whereas new-onset diabetes showed a significant association (HR 1.27, 95% CI 1.07–1.52). When diabetes was further categorized by duration, both diabetes <5 years (HR 1.36, 95% CI 1.07–1.74) and diabetes ≥5 years (HR 1.53, 95% CI 1.17–2.01) were associated with elevated risks compared with normoglycemia. A dose–response trend was observed across categories of glycemic status (P for trend <0.0001).

In the fully adjusted model, compared with normoglycemia, overall diabetes was significantly associated with an increased risk of EOGC (HR 1.34, 95% CI 1.18–1.54), whereas IFG was not significantly associated. In a sensitivity analysis using IPW to balance age and other covariates across the three groups, the results remained consistent: diabetes was significantly associated with increased risk (HR 1.37, 95% CI 1.20–1.56), whereas IFG was not significant (HR 1.04, 95% CI 0.97–1.13).

In a sensitivity analysis excluding individuals with pre-existing diabetes and those who developed diabetes within 2 years after cohort entry (n = 2,306,071), new-onset diabetes remained significantly associated with an increased risk of EOGC, whereas IFG was not significant in the fully adjusted model (Supplementary Table 3). Nonetheless, a graded trend of worsening glycemic status associated with higher risk of EOGC persisted (P for trend = 0.0163).

RCS analysis demonstrated a positive and approximately linear association between FPG levels and the risk of EOGC (Fig. 4). The risk began to rise progressively above the reference value of 100 mg/dL. The test for overall association was statistically significant (P = 0.0441), while there was no evidence of non-linearity (P = 0.72).

Associations of smoking and alcohol consumption with EOGC risk by glycemic status

Table 3 presents the elevated EOGC risk associated with smoking and alcohol consumption according to glycemic status. The reference groups were never smokers and non-drinkers within the normoglycemic population. Across all glycemic categories, current smoking and both light-to-moderate and heavy alcohol consumption were linked to a higher EOGC risk. This association was most pronounced among individuals with diabetes. In particular, within the diabetic group, current smokers exhibited 60.2% higher EOGC risk (adjusted HR [aHR], 1.602; 95% CI, 1.316–1.949), whereas former smokers demonstrated 45.4% increased risk (aHR, 1.454; 95% CI, 1.078–1.961). Similarly, heavy drinkers displayed 64.5% elevated risk (aHR, 1.645; 95% CI, 1.211–2.235), and light-to-moderate drinkers showed 36.3% increase (aHR, 1.363; 95% CI, 1.124–1.653). Comparable but attenuated patterns were observed among individuals with IFG and normoglycemia.

Discussion

Despite the declining prevalence of H. pylori infection and a general reduction in GC incidence, the global burden of EOGC occurring in individuals under 50 years of age continues to rise.3 The Republic of Korea has the highest EOGC rate worldwide, with approximately 15% of GC cases diagnosed in individuals under 45 years of age.23 Because hereditary factors explain only a small proportion of total GC cases,5 environmental influences are believed to play an important role in EOGC development. Given the rising prevalence of diabetes and its established links to various cancers,6,13 investigations of its associations with EOGC are both timely and necessary. In this large-scale, nationwide cohort study, we found that worsening glycemic status was associated with increased EOGC risk in a dose-dependent manner. Specifically, newly diagnosed diabetes and diabetes of <5 and ≥5 years' duration were associated with 27%, 36%, and 53% higher EOGC risks, respectively, after adjustment for clinically relevant variables. To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate a dose-dependent relationship between glycemic status and EOGC incidence using comprehensive nationwide health screening and insurance claims data.

Previous studies have revealed inconsistent findings regarding the association between diabetes and overall GC across all age groups. A meta-analysis of 18 cohort studies found that diabetes was associated with increased GC risk (relative risk: 1.11; 95% CI: 1.00–1.24).24 Similarly, a large retrospective cohort study of nearly 200,000 patients who underwent baseline and follow-up upper endoscopy reported increased GC risk (HR: 1.76; 95% CI: 1.04–2.97).25 Conversely, a more recent meta-analysis of 22 cohort studies26 and an international consortium analysis involving over 30 case-control studies27 found no significant association between diabetes and GC development. Although most studies have focused on the link between diabetes and overall GC across all age groups, few have specifically examined the association between diabetes and EOGC.

Various mechanisms have been proposed to explain the potential link between diabetes and EOGC. Hyperglycemia may directly induce DNA damage or indirectly promote it by increasing the production of reactive oxygen species.28,29 Additionally, hyperinsulinemia and elevated levels of insulin-like growth factors, commonly observed in diabetes, may contribute to carcinogenesis by enhancing cellular proliferation and inhibiting apoptosis.30 Based on these mechanisms, prolonged exposure to abnormal glycemic status may lead to increased GC risk. In our study, although IFG did not exhibit statistical significance in the fully adjusted model (Table 2), the IR of EOGC progressively increased from IFG to diabetes, and further with longer diabetes duration. Notably, elevated EOGC risk was observed even among individuals with newly diagnosed diabetes. These findings underscore the importance of proactive glycemic control during the prediabetic stage because EOGC risk significantly increases upon diabetes development. In support of this point, a previous multicenter prospective study conducted in Korea showed that insulin resistance led to greater GC risk, even in the absence of overt diabetes.31 Thus, early intervention to manage glycemic abnormalities in the IFG stage may be crucial for reducing EOGC risk.

The current Korean national health screening program recommends biennial upper endoscopy starting at age 40. However, in clinical practice, the screening interval or starting age may be adjusted based on individual patient risk factors.32 In the present study, the impact of diabetes on EOGC risk differed across age groups (Supplementary Table 1). Among individuals aged 40–44 years, diabetes was significantly associated with increased EOGC risk, with aHRs of approximately 1.5. Conversely, no statistically significant association was observed in those aged 20–39 years. Several factors may explain this discrepancy. In the 20–39 age group, the low absolute incidence of EOGC may have limited the statistical power to detect significant associations. Moreover, in very young individuals, other risk factors—such as genetic predisposition or infectious etiologies—may play a more prominent role in EOGC development, potentially attenuating the observed effect of glycemic status. Based on these findings, there is currently insufficient evidence to support lowering the starting age for Korea's screening program below 40 years even in individuals with diabetes. However, given the increased EOGC risk observed in individuals aged 40 and older with diabetes, this condition may merit classification as a high-risk factor requiring annual surveillance rather than biennial endoscopy. This recommendation is further supported by previous findings indicating that the mean sojourn time for GC in the 40–49 age group is approximately 1.25 years.33 Further large-scale studies are warranted to assess the cost-effectiveness of implementing such a strategy.

The potential concern that the observed association might be attributable to age-related detection bias was carefully addressed. All multivariable models were adjusted for age, and consistent associations were observed in age-stratified subgroup analyses. Furthermore, in a sensitivity analysis using IPW to balance age and other covariates, the results remained robust, with diabetes consistently associated with an increased risk of EOGC while IFG was not (Table 2). These findings suggest that the association between worsening glycemic status and EOGC is unlikely to be merely an artefact of age-related detection bias, but rather reflects a genuine relationship.

Although smoking and alcohol consumption are suggested as modifiable risk factors for EOGC,7 limited data exist regarding their associations with EOGC risk among individuals with IFG and diabetes. In particular, little is known about the impact of smoking cessation and reduced alcohol intake on EOGC risk in these populations. In our study, current smoking was associated with increased EOGC risk across all glycemic statuses with the highest risk in individuals with diabetes (Table 3). Smoking cessation consistently conferred a lower EOGC risk compared with current smoking in all glycemic groups. Furthermore, even light-to-moderate alcohol consumption was associated with an increased risk of EOGC across all glycemic categories, with the association being more pronounced among individuals with IFG or diabetes. The risk associated with light-to-moderate drinking remained lower than that with heavy drinking (Table 3). These findings provide new evidence that modification of lifestyle factors such as smoking and alcohol consumption may play a critical role in EOGC prevention particularly among individuals with IFG and diabetes, in the context of declining H. pylori infection rates.

This study had some limitations. First, H. pylori test results were unavailable because they are not included in the KNHIS biennial health screening check-up. The prevalence of H. pylori infection in South Korea has steadily declined, with rates in 2016 reported at 14.7%, 30.4%, and 40.6% among individuals aged 20–29, 30–39, and 40–49 years, respectively—compared with 29.3%, 49.1%, and 57.8% in 2005.34,35 Moreover, a meta-analysis demonstrated that the association between diabetes and increased GC risk persisted despite adjustment for H. pylori infection.36 Additionally, a previous study revealed that the association between diabetes and incident GC did not differ by the presence of intestinal metaplasia or mucosal atrophy.25 Therefore, our findings are likely not substantially affected by H. pylori infection status. Nonetheless, future large-scale studies that include detailed data regarding H. pylori infection would offer a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between diabetes and EOGC. Second, we were unable to assess histological subtypes or anatomical locations of EOGC because such information was not available in the dataset. Third, GC cases were identified using ICD-10 codes, which may have inadvertently included gastrointestinal stromal tumors or neuroendocrine carcinomas. However, given that more than 90% of all GC cases are gastric adenocarcinomas, this inadvertent inclusion is unlikely to have significantly influenced our results. Fourth, we lacked data concerning hemoglobin A1c, post-load plasma glucose, and random plasma glucose, which may have led to misclassification of glycemic status and underestimation of diabetes prevalence. Fifth, our findings are based on a population-based study conducted in a single country, which limits the generalizability of the results and the ability to assess potential ethnic differences. Sixth, our dataset contained limited information on the type, dose, and duration of glucose-lowering medications, which may have resulted in residual confounding. As our primary objective was to evaluate glycemic status rather than drug-specific effects, detailed analyses by medication class were beyond the scope of this study. Seventh, information on the frequency of endoscopic screening was not available in our dataset, and therefore we could not adjust for potential differences in surveillance intensity. In Korea, however, a substantial proportion of endoscopic examinations are performed as self-paid opportunistic screening outside the national program, which are not captured in claims data. Accordingly, even if screening frequency data within the reimbursement system were available, adjustment based on such information would have provided only limited validity. Thus, while residual detection bias cannot be completely excluded, its impact on our findings is expected to be modest. Eighth, although we adjusted for multiple potential confounders, some important factors such as family history of gastric cancer and dietary habits (e.g., high intake of salt-preserved foods) were not available in the dataset. Therefore, residual confounding by these unmeasured variables cannot be excluded. Ninth, as lifestyle information was self-reported, some missing values occurred. In addition, income information was missing in about 5% of participants, and laboratory variables such as lipid profiles also had modest proportions of missingness. These cases were excluded from the analyses. Although we cannot completely rule out systematic differences, the missingness was considered most likely to be at random and is unlikely to have biased the observed associations. Furthermore, lifestyle factors such as smoking, alcohol consumption, and physical activity were self-reported during the health screening program and may therefore be subject to reporting bias. Tenth, because the follow-up extended until 2022, part of the observation period overlapped with the Coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic. While the pandemic temporarily disrupted cancer screening and healthcare utilization in Korea, these effects are unlikely to have introduced substantial bias into our study. Most events were accrued prior to 2020, and any potential diagnostic delays would likely have been nondifferential across glycemic status groups, biasing the results toward the null rather than generating artifactual associations. Eleventh, race/ethnicity was not collected in the database, as the vast majority of participants were of East Asian (predominantly Korean) descent.

A major strength of our study is the use of the KNHIS database, a nationwide single-payer system that covers nearly the entire Korean population (approximately 97%). Moreover, about 72% of eligible individuals participate in the biennial health screening program, providing a large and broadly representative sample. These features enhance the generalizability of our findings, although some degree of selection bias cannot be entirely excluded given the voluntary nature of participation.

In conclusion, this nationwide cohort study demonstrated a dose-dependent association between worsening glycemic status and an increased risk of EOGC among young adults aged 20–49 years. In addition, current smoking and both light-to-moderate and heavy alcohol consumption were associated with elevated EOGC risk across all glycemic categories, with the highest risk observed in individuals with diabetes. These findings suggest that personalized cancer prevention strategies—emphasizing effective glycemic control, smoking cessation, and reduced alcohol intake early in life—may help mitigate the risk of EOGC.
