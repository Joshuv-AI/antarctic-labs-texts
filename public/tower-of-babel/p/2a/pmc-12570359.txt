Measles trend in Gabon: Insight from 18 years of surveillance data
Authors: Guy Francis Nzengui Nzengui, Hervé M’boyis Kamdem, Ayawa Claudine Kombila Koumavor, Gaël Mourembou, Pélagie Mougola Bissiengou, Daniella Delpo, Rotimi Myrabelle Avome Houechenou, Fabrice Lotola Mougeni, Fortune Barondi Diambou, Annie Badila Missogni, Frédéric Ngoulou, Marcel Mouketou Mabika, Thierry Mba, Alain Mombo Mombo, Paulin Mbagou, Urlucha Marina Mbani Okoumba, Alyssa Sbarra, Saki Takahashi, Anicet Christel Maloupazoa Siawaya, Amandine Mveang Nzoghe, Ofilia Mvoundza Ndjindji, Samira Zoa Assoumou, Ann Moormaan, Joel Fleury Djoba Siawaya, Angélique Ndjoyi Mbiguino
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryMeasles is a vaccine-preventable infection that can lead to serious illness and death. The delayed implementation of the World Health Organization (WHO)-recommended two doses of measles-containing vaccine (MCV1 and MCV2) and immunity gaps compromise the effort to elimination. Eighteen years (2006–2023) of surveillance data from Gabon measles surveillance program were collected and analyzed. Variables included age, sex, vaccination status and date, disease date, and anti-measles immunoglobulin M (IgM) status (determined by immunoassay). The rate of infection and association studies was done. Results showed that 60.6% (1,640/2,708) of suspected cases were unvaccinated against measles. Of suspected cases, 26.1% were laboratory-confirmed cases. Unvaccinated individuals were 5.9–7.7 times more likely to be anti-measles IgM positive (p < 0.0001). Children aged ≤5 years represented 70.8% of laboratory-confirmed cases; 15.5% (30/193) of childbearing age females were confirmed cases. In Gabon, nonimmunized and underimmunized individuals are driving up measles disease and constitute a challenge to measles elimination.Subject areas: Medical surveillance, Public health, Virology

Introduction

Measles is one of the most contagious human diseases transmitted from person to person through large respiratory droplets. The disease is caused by an airborne virus of the Paramyxoviridae family.1,2,3,4 Humans are its only known reservoir and source of infection.2,4 It manifests as a feverish rash, and the incubation period for measles is usually 10 to 14 days (range 7–23 days) from exposure to the onset of symptoms.3 The first symptoms (prodrome) are usually fever, discomfort, cough, conjunctivitis, and rhinitis.3 The characteristic maculopapular rash appears two to four days after the onset of the prodrome. Patients are usually contagious about four days before the appearance of the rash and up to four days after it. The transmission source is frequently unknown because someone often infects the patient in the pre-eruption stage. It is a potentially fatal disease in people at risk, such as pregnant women, people living with HIV, or people who are unvaccinated.5

In 2023, the global number of measles cases was estimated at 10.3 millions.6 Children are the most vulnerable to measles infection and disease,3 and children in low-income countries are at the highest risk of death from measles.7 It was estimated that in 2022 and 2023, 136,000 and 107,500 people (mostly children) died of measles.6,7 The control of measles requires three key elements: a good diagnosis of measles, a strong and sustainable routine immunization strategy, including a catch-up immunization schedule, and community awareness and engagement on the importance and need for vaccination. Indeed, the generalization of vaccination through the Expanded Program on Immunization (EPI), launched in 1974 to ensure that children from all countries have access to life-saving, has reduced the overall incidence of the disease and thus significantly reduced mortality and morbidity.8 However, the disease has not yet been eradicated, in part due to decreases in vaccination coverage. On the contrary, the disease is on the rise worldwide,1,9 particularly in Gabon, which has never been eligible for the Global Alliance for Vaccines and Immunization (GAVI) support and which has been confronted since 2013 with recurrent epidemics not only in children but also in unvaccinated or insufficiently vaccinated adults.10,11,12

Gabon’s vaccination coverage rates are under the 90% target for vaccination in children. In 2021, the coverage of one-dose measles-containing vaccine (MCV1) was 64% and dropped to 52% in 2022, before reaching 66% in 2023.13,14 The MCV2 has not yet been introduced or implemented in Gabon.

This report describes data from 18 years of measles surveillance (2006–2023), including numbers of measles cases, age- and sex-based distribution of suspected and confirmed cases, and case distribution and the impact of vaccination. Being able to collect and analyze these data will allow the understanding of determinants of measles incidence and risk factors of ongoing transmission, which is critical for informing effective public health strategies against measles.

Results

From 2006 to 2023, 2,708 samples of suspected measles cases were referred to the reference laboratory. Country mapping of suspected cases across second-administrative units is illustrated in Figure 1. Gender repartition among cases was almost equal, with 49.7% of males and 50.3% of females; 60.6% (1,640/2,708) of suspected cases were unvaccinated, and 29.8% (807/2,708) were vaccinated against measles. Among vaccinated cases, 1% (8/807) were within the vaccine serological window (i.e., the lapse of time between the vaccination date and disease onset was ≤1 week). The vaccination status of 9.6% (261/2,708) of suspected measles cases was unknown. Also, 7.4% (200/2,708) of suspected measles cases were below the age of measles vaccination (<9 months), representing 12.3% (200/1,622) of unvaccinated cases. The yearly distribution of vaccinated and unvaccinated individuals among measles-suspected cases is illustrated in Figure 2.

Seven hundred and eight (26.1% of suspected measles cases) cases were confirmed measles immunoglobulin M (IgM) positives over the entire 18-year period. Measles IgM test results for 47 (1.7%) cases were borderline/inconclusive, meaning the positivity threshold was not reached. One thousand nine hundred and fifty-four (72.2%) suspected cases were negative for measles IgM.

Between 2006 and 2012, very few laboratory-confirmed measles cases were observed (9 cases; 1.6% of suspected measles cases). In 2013, 121 laboratory-confirmed measles cases were recorded (representing 49.6% of suspected measles cases and 17.1% of all laboratory-confirmed cases). In 2014, 33 confirmed cases were recorded (9.7% of suspected measles cases and 4.7% of all laboratory-confirmed cases). In 2015, we recorded 29 confirmed cases (11.9% of suspected measles cases and 4.7% of all laboratory-confirmed cases). The year 2016 saw an exponential increase in confirmed cases, reaching 295 cases (55.6% of suspected measles cases). In 2017, we recorded 40 measles laboratory-confirmed cases (31.7% of suspected measles cases). The number of laboratory-confirmed measles cases dropped drastically between 2018 and 2020 (11 cases for the 3 years, representing 4% of suspected measles cases). Between 2021 and 2023, the number of confirmed measles cases rose again, with 21 cases in 2021 (20.8% of suspected measles cases), 84 cases in 2022 (64.6% of suspected measles cases), and 67 cases in 2023 (44.7% of suspected measles cases). We observed that the highest numbers of confirmed cases were recorded in 2013, 2016, 2022, and 2023 (Figures 3 and 4).

Anti-measles IgM and vaccination status

The prevalence of anti-measles IgM in unvaccinated suspected cases was 33.3% (546/1,640). The rate of borderline or inconclusive anti-measles IgM tests was 1.8% in the same population. Also, 36.4% of cases with an unknown measles vaccination status were positive for anti-measles IgM. Only 9.7% (67/708) of cases with a history of measles vaccination were positive for anti-measles IgM. Moreover, 3 of the 67 vaccinated individuals with laboratory-confirmed measles disease were within the vaccine serological window. After adjustment, 9% (64/708) of cases with a history of measles vaccination were positive for anti-measles IgM. Unvaccinated individuals accounted for 77% (546/708) of laboratory-confirmed measles cases recorded between 2006 and 2023. Vaccination status affected significantly measles infection (Figure 5A). Cases with an unknown measles vaccination status accounted for 13%(95/708) of laboratory-confirmed measles cases recorded between 2006 and 2023. Univariate analysis showed that unvaccinated individuals had a 5.9 increased odds of being positive for measles IgM than individuals with a history of measles vaccination (p < 0.0001) (Figure 5B). Vaccination therefore reduced the odds of measles disease (odds ratio [OR]: 0.17; p < 0.0001). The multivariate analysis confirmed that vaccination reduced the odds of measles disease (OR: 0.13; p < 0.0001) (Table 1). Based on multivariate analysis, unvaccinated individuals had a 7.7 increased odds of being positive for measles IgM than individuals with a history of measles vaccination (p < 0.0001).

Age-based distribution of measles cases

The age of the suspected measles cases was recorded for 2,707 cases—7.6% (205/2,707) of suspected measles cases were below the age of measles vaccination (<9 months), and 57.8% (1565/2,707) of suspected measles cases were aged ≤5 years; 31.6% (855/2,707) were between the ages of 6 and 14 years, and 10.7% (289/2,707) were 15 years and older (Figure 6).

Age-based distribution of suspected and confirmed measles cases

Children aged ≤5 years

Thirty-two percent (501/1,565) of children with suspected measles aged ≤5 years were IgM positive, representing 70.8% (501/708) of all laboratory-confirmed cases (Figure 6). Data showed that 14% (70/501) of all laboratory-confirmed cases aged ≤5 years were below the age of measles vaccination (Figure 6). Of the 1,470 children aged ≤5 years with an informed measles vaccination status, 62.4% (917/1,470) had not received a measles vaccine. Only 37.6% (553/1,470) were vaccinated against measles. After correcting for eight children within the vaccine serological window (considering the effect of the vaccine to be nil at this point), the rates of laboratory-confirmed measles infection in unvaccinated and vaccinated cases were 38.3% (354/925) and 8.6% (47/545). The rate of laboratory-confirmed measles disease in children aged ≤5 years with uninformed measles vaccination status was 49.5% (47/95).

Children aged 6 to 14 years

In children aged 6 to 14 years, 18.6% (159/855) were positive for anti-measles IgM, representing 22.4% (159/708) of all laboratory-confirmed cases (Figure 6). Of the 759 children aged 6 to 14 years with an informed measles vaccination status, 67.9% (515/759) were unvaccinated, and only 32.1% (244/759) of them were vaccinated against measles. In these cases, the rates of laboratory-confirmed measles cases were 23.3% (115/515) and 5.7% (14/244) in unvaccinated and vaccinated cases, respectively. The rate of laboratory-confirmed measles disease in children with uninformed measles vaccination status was 31.2% (30/96).

Patients aged ≥15 years

Regarding patients aged 15 years and older, 16.8% (48/286) were positive for anti-measles IgM, which represented 6.7% (48/708) of all laboratory-confirmed cases (Figure 6). Two hundred eighteen patients aged ≥15 years had an informed measles vaccination status; 78.4% (171/218) of those with an informed vaccination status were not vaccinated against measles, whereas only 5% (11/218) were vaccinated. The rates of laboratory-confirmed measles disease were 13.3% (29/218) in unvaccinated and 9.1% (1/11) in vaccinated individuals. Also, the rate of laboratory-confirmed measles disease in individuals with uninformed measles vaccination status was 26.5% (18/68).

Measles in females of childbearing age (15–49 years)

Females of childbearing age represented 7.1% (193/2,707) of suspected measles cases; 15.5% (30/193) were anti-measles-IgM-confirmed measles cases. Only 3.1% (6/193) of females of childbearing age had an informed measles vaccination status. The rates of laboratory-confirmed measles disease were 11.5% (15/130) in unvaccinated and 16.7 (1/6) in vaccinated females. Also, the rate of laboratory-confirmed measles disease in individuals with uninformed measles vaccination status was 24.6% (14/57).

Measles in urban and rural areas

A proportion of 28.3% (589/2,079) of suspected cases from rural/semi-rural areas were vaccinated, whereas 34.6% (208/601) of suspected cases from urban areas were vaccinated. Rural/semi-rural areas accounted for 77% (2,079/2,707) of suspected measles cases and 71% (503/708) of anti-measles-IgM-confirmed cases. Case distribution is illustrated in Figures 7A and 7B.

Discussion

Despite the existence of an effective measles vaccine, epidemics continue to occur in Gabon. As shown by our surveillance data, about 30% of suspected measles cases were vaccinated. The prevalence of laboratory-confirmed measles cases among suspected cases from 2006 to 2023 was 26.1%, with peaks of positivity in 2013 (49.6%), 2016 (55.6%), 2022 (64.6%), and 2022 (44.7%). Measles epidemics in Gabon between 2006 and 2020 followed the global trend of measles epidemics with a couple of differences: first, in Gabon, the increase in cases occurred earlier in 2013 when globally it was recorded in 2016; second, the confirmed measles cases also occurred earlier (in 2018) than globally (in 2020).15 Gabon, Angola, Kenya, and Guinea have been recently cited as countries facing an increased risk of measles outbreaks, a disease that can be best prevented by vaccination.11 The importance of vaccine uptake is emphasized in this study by the fact that 77% of laboratory-confirmed measles cases recorded between 2006 and 2023 were unvaccinated. In Nigeria, 70.8% of the confirmed cases were unvaccinated.16 In South Sudan, up to 80.6% of reported cases were unvaccinated.17 Our data revealed that unvaccinated individuals had a 5.9 increased odds of developing measles than individuals with a history of measles vaccination. In South Sudan, the odds were even higher (8.5).17 As highlighted by numerous studies on measles surveillance in Africa, unvaccinated people drive the epidemic.16,17,18,19 Rural/semi-rural areas concentrated the majority of both suspected and confirmed measles cases (77% and 71%), as well as the majority of unvaccinated patients (71.7%). This may be the reflection of low vaccine access in rural and semi-rural areas, which may constitute pockets that drive measles circulation. Studies conducted during the resurgence of measles in southern African countries showed that the principal cause of resurgence was the low vaccination rate of eligible populations, causing measles-susceptibility pockets that sustain measles.20 In Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), the increase in the population of unvaccinated measles-susceptible individuals was pointed out as the cause of resurgence.21

There are differences when comparing Gabon, which is a GAVI non-eligible country, to GAVI-eligible countries such as Gambia. Both countries have more or less the same population size (2.4 vs. 2.6 million people). However, the rate of laboratory-confirmed cases among vaccinated individuals was lower in Gabon (9%) than in Gambia (24%). Also, in contrary to Gambia, urban areas had the majority of cases (72.8%); in Gabon, the majority of cases were concentrated in semirural/rural areas (71%). Beyond GAVI’s support, other factors including socioeconomical factors constitute a key determinant of measles control. It has been reported that education and economic strength drive higher vaccination rates.22,23,24 Indeed, Gabon’s gross domestic product (GDP) per capita (7,802 USD [World Bank]) and literacy rate (86%) are higher than Gambia’s (GDP per capita: 888.2 USD and a literacy rate: 59%).25

The American experience showed that the elimination of measles is possible. Indeed, in the American World Health Organization (WHO) region, the high coverage of two doses of measles vaccine associated with immunization has led to the elimination of measles in 2000.26 However, imported disease cases and transmission among unvaccinated individuals in the community have led to measles resurgence in America.26,27 Gabon has not yet adopted the two-dose measles vaccine strategy. This may explain, in part, why nearly 8% of previously vaccinated individuals developed measles disease. In Canada, children who received a single dose were five times more likely to contract measles than were children who received two doses of vaccine.28 Another explanation would include vaccine failure to elicit a protective antibody response.15 Indeed, the likelihood of IgM positivity among previously successfully vaccinated people is quite low.29 Vaccinated people who tested IgM positive for measles likely never seroconverted or had a low IgG seroconversion.30

Gabon should update its anti-measles immunization strategy according to the epidemiological facts. The country should adopt the two-dose measles vaccine strategy to guarantee immunization among children who did not develop immunity after the first dose,30,31 and also, women of childbearing age should be targeted for catch-up vaccination. Supplementary immunization activities or campaigns should be implemented in rural/semi-rural areas to increase opportunities for immunization and awareness. Our surveillance data also revealed the difficulties in the differential diagnosis of measles. Indeed, with only 26.1% of laboratory-confirmed measles cases among suspected measles cases, clinicians must familiarize themselves with measles clinical features. It seems that clinically (based on signs and symptoms) measles is overdiagnosed.

Limitations of the study

The absence of contact tracing follow-up to identify new cases and cases follow-up to estimate morbidity and mortality rates, as well as the lack of sex or gender influence constituted the main limits of this report.

Conclusion

Gabon is prone to sporadic measles epidemics driven by unvaccinated individuals. Due to the existence of an effective vaccine that has been around since the 1960s, eliminating measles should not be that difficult for upper- and middle-income countries like Gabon. As part of routine immunization, the countries that have not done so must adopt and implement the WHO-recommended two- dose measles-containing vaccine (MCV1 and MCV2).

Experimental model and study participant details

Case definition

Measles vaccine seroconversion appears 10 to 14 days after vaccine administration, with a peak seen between the 21st and 28th days.32,33 Only individuals vaccinated for more than 2 weeks were considered to be adequately immunized. A suspected measles case definition was defined as an illness with maculopapular rash, fever, and one or more of the following symptoms: conjunctivitis, coryza, and cough, or any patient in whom the clinician suspects measles. Laboratory-confirmed measles case was defined as a case that met the clinical case definition and had a measles-specific immunoglobulin M (IgM) antibody-positive test result.

Data collection

Retrospective laboratory data on measles and rubella surveillance from 2006 to 2023 (18 years) was collected and analyzed from the country's measles surveillance reference laboratory records. Cases were from sentinel sites covering all 51 health departments including 63 country’s districts and cities. The data collected included the patient's place of residence, age, sex, measles vaccination status, date of vaccination (via vaccine cards), disease starting date, and measles laboratory testing results (ELISA IgM).

The National Laboratory of Public Health approved the present study. The collection was done in accordance with Chapter 3, section 2, article 70, of Law No. 025/2023 of 09/07/2023 amending Law No. 001/2011 of 25 September 2011 on the protection of personal data.

Method details

Reporting and case notification

Measles is a compulsory notifiable disease. Surveillance sentinels collect demographic and epidemiological information, vaccine status and blood specimens of all individuals meeting the case definition. Blood specimens are sent to the reference laboratory for testing and confirmation. Both suspected and confirmed cases are reported to health authorities.

Laboratory confirmation of measles disease

Laboratory testing was done using the EUROIMMUN® Anti-Measles Virus ELISA (IgM) kit (Germany) provided by the World Health Organization (WHO) local bureau. Assays were carried out following manufacturer instructions.

Succinctly, to detect measles IgM antibodies 100 μl of diluted (1/100) samples and controls were added into the well coated with measles antigen and incubated for 30 minutes at room temperature. Unbound material was washed out (3 washes). 100 μl of enzyme-conjugated anti-human IgM was added and the test plate was incubated for 30 minutes. After washing (3 times) to remove unbound conjugate, 100μl chromagen/substrate was added into the wells and incubated 15 minutes away from light. The reaction was stopped by adding 100 μl of the stop solution to each well. The optical density was read at 450 nm, with the reference wavelength between 620 nm and 650 nm. The ratio value for the positive and negative controls and each specimen is determined by dividing the specimen OD value by the cutoff calibrator OD. Based on the ratio value determined, the result could be positive (ratio ≥1.1), borderline/inconclusive (ratio ≥0.8 and <1.1), or negative (ratio <0.8). The same laboratory procedure was used over the 18 years.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Initially, we controlled for the validity of vaccination by analyzing the lapse between the date of vaccination and the onset of disease.

The statistical analysis was done using GraphPad Prism software version 6. Rates were expressed in percentage by dividing the number of the selected events by the total number of patients and then multiplying the result by 100. Association analysis was done using the contingency table and results were expressed in odds ratio. A p-value inferior to 0.05 marked the significance of the association.

The pairwise approach to the missing data was used in this study.34 We conducted two main analyses, one ignoring missing data and the other one imputing missing data using multiple imputations with chained equations. An overview of missing data is shown in the supplemental figure.
