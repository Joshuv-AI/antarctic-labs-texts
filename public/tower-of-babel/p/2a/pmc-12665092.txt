Light‐Signalling Regulators GmSTF3/4 Enhance Soybean Susceptibility to Cyst Nematodes via Activation of Sugar Transporters
Authors: Bohong Wu, Shuyi Wang, Yuan Zhou, Shuangling Zhao, Wei Zhu, Yin Gao, Guohui Liu, Lijie Chen, Haiyan Fan, Yuanyuan Wang, Xiaoyu Liu, Ning Yang, Yuxi Duan, Shoudong Wang, Yuanhu Xuan, Xiaofeng Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSoybean cyst nematode (SCN; Heterodera glycines Ichinohe) is a plant‐parasitic nematode that causes substantial yield losses in soybean production. Light signalling is a critical environmental factor that influences photomorphogenesis and carbohydrate metabolism. However, its transcriptional regulation under pathogen‐induced stress remains unclear. In this study, the biological function and regulatory mechanism of TGACG‐motif binding factor 3/4 (GmSTF3/4), a shoot‐to‐root mobile protein in soybean ( Glycine max ), were investigated during H. glycines infection. Evidence was provided that light signalling modulated soybean susceptibility to cyst nematode, marked by light‐enhanced nematode infection and upregulation of photoreceptor gene expression post‐infection. GmSTF3/4 interacted with CONSTITUTIVE PHOTOMORPHOGENIC1a (GmCOP1a) and mediated its degradation. The nematode was identified to accelerate the shoot‐to‐root translocation of GmSTF3/4. Phenotypic analysis revealed that GmSTF3/4 promoted nematode development, whereas GmCOP1a exerted an antagonistic effect. Furthermore, the integrated analyses of Cleavage Under Targets and Tagmentation (CUT&Tag) and RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq) indicated that GmSTF3/4 bound to the promoters of multiple sugar transporter genes, including GmSWEET8, GmSWEET10b, GmSWEET13d, GmSUC8, and GmERD6‐like. The subcellular localization confirmed their plasma membrane targeting, and functional validation in yeast demonstrated the sucrose transport activity of four of these genes. Transient expression assays of five candidate genes during nematode infection supported the positive regulatory role of GmSWEET10b in facilitating nematode infection and development, a result further supported by the Gmsweet10b mutant. Collectively, this study revealed that GmSTF3/4 enhanced soybean susceptibility to cyst nematodes by transcriptionally activating sugar transporter genes, offering a new avenue for SCN‐resistance research.

Introduction

Soybean ( Glycine max [L.] Merr.) is a major oilseed crop and an essential source of plant protein in global demand. Soybean production is affected by various pathogens, with the soybean cyst nematode (SCN; Heterodera glycines Ichinohe) being one of the most damaging. In the United States alone, the yield losses have exceeded $1.5 billion (Bandara et al. 2020; Peng et al. 2021), with the estimates of $0.3–1.3 billion in China according to our survey. SCN infection results in stunted growth, wilting, and an increased vulnerability to other pathogens. Second‐stage juveniles penetrate soybean roots during early infection, remodel the root cells into a syncytium, and absorb nutrients to develop into adults (Endo 1964). The female forms a cyst to contain eggs, which hatch over three to 4 weeks and may persist in the soil for many years, making SCN difficult to eradicate (Wyss and Grundler 1992). As an obligate parasite, SCN management relies heavily on crop rotation and host resistance (Niblack et al. 2006). Functional analysis of two quantitative trait loci, rhg1 and Rhg4, has shown promising results (Cook et al. 2012). Several resistance genes, including GmSNAP18 (Bayless et al. 2016), GmAAT (Guo et al. 2019), GmSHMT08 (Liu et al. 2012), and GmWI12 (Dong and Hudson 2022), have been successively verified. Although these genes inhibit SCN development, additional resistance candidate genes are increasingly important for long‐term SCN management.

Light plays a critical role in plant growth and development by converting light energy into chemical energy stored as carbohydrates through photosynthesis. It also functions as an environmental cue that regulates processes such as germination, seedling development, vegetative growth, and flowering (Kami et al. 2010). Various classes of photoreceptors absorb specific wavelengths of light to mediate these processes. Higher plants possess multiple photoreceptors that enable them to perceive a broad range of light conditions (Chen et al. 2004; Quail 2002; Somers and Fujiwara 2009). Unlike animal photoreceptors localised to sensory organs, plant photoreceptors are broadly distributed throughout plant tissues (Galvao and Fankhauser 2015). In many cases, light‐induced responses are initiated at distant sites through long‐distance signalling pathways (Chen et al. 2024, 2016; Wang et al. 2021).

Light signals are transduced from photoreceptors into transcription factors that regulate photomorphogenesis by modulating gene expression. ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL 5 (HY5) is a key transcription factor involved in regulating seedling hypocotyl elongation in response to light (Chattopadhyay et al. 1998; Oyama et al. 1997). HY5 belongs to the basic leucine zipper (bZIP) family of transcription factors (Jakoby et al. 2002), featuring a dimerization domain and a DNA‐binding basic region in its C‐terminal. HY5 binds to cis‐acting elements, such as the G‐box and Z‐box, in promoter regions to regulate transcription (Lee et al. 2007). The E3 ubiquitin ligase CONSTITUTIVE PHOTOMORPHOGENIC1 (COP1) negatively regulates HY5 by targeting it for degradation. In darkness, nuclear‐localised COP1 represses photomorphogenesis via HY5 degradation (McNellis et al. 1994; Osterlund et al. 2000). Bioinformatic analysis of HY5 orthologs in soybean identified TGACG‐motif binding factors GmSTF3 and GmSTF4 (GmSTF3/4) (Wang et al. 2021). GmSTF3/4 serves as a mobile protein involved in shoot‐to‐root communication and can modulate light‐responsive processes. In soybean, GmSTF3/4 translocates from the shoots to the roots and initiates nodule formation in response to light (Wang et al. 2021). It also regulates nodulation through isoflavone synthesis in roots and UV‐B perception in shoots (Chen et al. 2024). In Arabidopsis thaliana , HY5 regulates plant defence responses (Chai et al. 2015; Chen et al. 2013; Wu et al. 2024). However, the role of soybean GmSTF3/4 in biotic and abiotic stress responses remains unclear.

During pathogen infection, pathogens alter host metabolism and transcriptomic responses. Infection can significantly influence sugar metabolism and mobilisation and induce the expression of plant sugar transporters to acquire sugars from host tissues (Breia et al. 2021; Chen et al. 2010). In plants, sugar transporters primarily include sucrose transporters (SUCs) (Ayre 2011; Kühn and Grof 2010) and sugar‐exporting transporters (SWEETs) (Chen et al. 2010). By modulating the transport of different sugar types, plants can effectively reshape the nutritional landscape, thereby inhibiting pathogen invasion and maintaining plant vitality.

SUCs participate in various physiological processes, such as seed filling, apoplasmic phloem loading of sucrose, and abiotic stress resistance (Gong et al. 2015; Lu et al. 2020; Milne et al. 2017; Radchuk et al. 2017). SUCs are essential in plant‐pathogen interactions, particularly with nematodes and parasitic fungi, through their role in sucrose exchange (Doidy, Grace, et al. 2012; Doidy, van Tuinen, et al. 2012; Hennion et al. 2019). SWEET gene families are broadly expressed in plants, with four distinct clades identified in soybean (Patil et al. 2015). Among these, clade III members play a key role in intracellular sugar transport. GmSWEET8 orthologs such as AtSWEET9 localise to the plasma membrane and facilitate sucrose export from parenchyma cells to the apoplast (Chen et al. 2012; Lin et al. 2014). GmSWEET10b orthologs, such as AtSWEET15, are the major regulators of sucrose accumulation in seeds (Chen et al. 2015). AtSWEET10 expression modulates sucrose distribution in the phloem (Chen et al. 2015) and demonstrates high homology to GmSWEET13d. Thus, clade III is a critical component of the SWEET family, responsible for sugar translocation. In general, sugar transporters contribute significantly to plant growth and stress adaptation through their involvement in the nutrient flow between hosts and pathogens. However, their exact mechanistic roles require further elucidation.

To facilitate cyst nematode development, feeding cells form near the vascular bundle to supply sufficient nutrition. Our findings indicated that GmSTF3/4 expression was induced by nematode infection in soybean. These proteins, previously identified as light‐responsive transcription factors, could activate the expression of sugar transporter genes during infection. GmSWEET8, GmSWEET10b, GmSWEET13d, GmSUC8, and GmERD6‐like were confirmed to be direct targets of GmSTF3/4, which could function as negative regulators of nematode resistance. Coordinated expression of sugar transporter genes and GmSTF3/4 promoted nematode susceptibility. Therefore, the GmSTF3/4‐sugar transporter regulatory module represents a potential target for improving soybean resistance to cyst nematode infection.

Results

Light Promotes Soybean Susceptibility to Cyst Nematode

Light is a critical environmental cue that influences plant growth throughout the life cycle. In this study, we investigated the effects of different light wavelengths on nematode infection by pretreating germinating Williams 82 (W82) seedlings under various light conditions prior to SCN inoculation (Figure 1a). Seedlings emerging on soil surfaces were exposed to specific wavelengths to stimulate plant responses. In our previous study, Arabidopsis thaliana exhibited elongated hypocotyls under red light and etiolation under dark conditions (Wu et al. 2024). After 15 days of exposure to light of varying wavelengths, W82 seedlings displayed diverse developmental stages, consistent with those observed in Arabidopsis thaliana (Figure 1b). To determine whether the soybean sensitivity to nematodes under different light environments mirrored that of Arabidopsis thaliana , SCN penetration assays were performed using 15‐day‐old W82 seedlings under the different light conditions. Nematode numbers were significantly higher under all light treatments than in the dark control (Figure 1c). Second‐stage juvenile (J2) nematodes were markedly less in proportion under all light conditions than in the dark group (Figure 1d). These results demonstrate that soybean sensitivity to cyst nematodes is regulated by light signalling.

Light signals perceived by plant photoreceptors regulate numerous physiological and developmental processes. Primary photoreceptors include phytochromes (PHYs), which absorb red light; cryptochromes (CRYs); and phototropins (PHOTs), which absorb blue light (Su et al. 2017). RNA‐seq analysis revealed the induction of photoreceptor genes in both roots and stems of nematode‐infected soybean plants, with stronger expression observed in stems (Figure 1e). Further analysis confirmed that the expression of GmPHYA, GmCRY1, and GmPHOT1 was upregulated at 1, 5, 10, and 15 days after nematode infection (Figure 1f). We hypothesize that nematode infection may activate a long‐distance signalling mechanism associated with light signalling pathways. Recent studies have indicated that GmSTF3/4 are mobile proteins acting downstream of photoreceptors and involved in nodulation via shoot‐to‐root communication (Chen et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2021). These findings prompted us to examine light‐dependent regulation of GmSTF3/4 proteins. Under different light conditions, GmSTF3 and GmSTF4 protein levels were consistently higher than those in the dark‐treated seedlings (Figure 1g).

The Soybean Mobile Proteins GmSTF3/4 Exhibit a Positive Correlation With Nematode Parasitism

To evaluate whether GmSTF3/4 expression can be induced by nematodes, transcript levels were measured at multiple time points during nematode infection. Significant upregulation of GmSTF3/4 transcripts was observed throughout nematode development (Figure 2a). Previous studies have indicated that GmSTF3/4 can move from the shoot to the root, thereby participating in rhizobia‐mediated root nodulation in soybean (Chen et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2021). To determine whether nematode infection could induce GmSTF3/4 protein accumulation in roots via shoot‐to‐root transport, Agrobacterium expressing GmSTF3/4‐GFP was infiltrated into soybean leaves. GmSTF3/4‐GFP was subsequently detected in the roots, and nematode infection further enhanced the accumulation of GFP signals (Figure 2b,c). Fluorescence localization in soybean root sections revealed that GFP signals were concentrated in the syncytia and nematode anterior regions (Figure 2d). These findings suggest that the nematodes accelerated long‐distance GmSTF3/4 protein transport and that GmSTF3/4‐mediated signalling was accumulating in nematode feeding sites.

To determine the role of GmSTF3/4 in nematode parasitism, GmSTF3/4 overexpression lines were generated using a GFP‐tagged hairy root system (Figure 2e). No obvious mass differences of hairy roots were found between the GmSTF3/4 overexpressing transgenic lines and empty control (Figure S5a). The expression and protein levels of GmSTF3/4 were significantly higher in the overexpression lines than in the empty vector controls (Figure 2f, Figure S1a). The susceptibility of GmSTF3‐OE and GmSTF4‐OE lines to nematode infection was assessed. No significant differences in nematode numbers were observed between the overexpression lines and controls (Figure 2i). However, the GmSTF3‐OE and GmSTF4‐OE lines showed accelerated nematode development (Figure 2h,j).

To further explore the regulatory roles of GmSTF3/4 in response to H. glycines , both genes were silenced using VIGS. Because of the high sequence similarity between GmSTF3 and GmSTF4, constructs co‐silencing lines of GmSTF3/4‐RNAi were designed. Quantitative and semi‐quantitative PCR confirmed the efficient silencing of the GmSTF3/4 line compared to the empty carrier (Figure 2g, Figure S1b). Silencing of GmSTF3/4 retarded nematode development compared to the empty control (Figure 2k,m). In contrast, the infection number of nematodes in all silencing lines remained unaffected (Figure 2l). Collectively, these results suggest that GmSTF3/4 positively modulates soybean cyst nematode development.

CUT&Tag Analysis in Soybean Hairy Roots Utilising a Transient Expression System

The CUT&Tag assay was employed to collect GmSTF3 targets and identify the downstream factors directly regulated by GmSTF3/4. This method enabled the capture of GmSTF3 target sites through cleavage by pA/G‐Tnp Pro transposase. Given the time required to generate stable GmSTF3 transgenic soybean, a rapid and efficient approach was developed. Hairy roots expressing strong GmSTF3‐GFP signals were ground in liquid nitrogen, nuclei were isolated, and a transient expression system was used to analyse the global binding profiles of GmSTF3 (Figure 3a). The intergenic regions accounted for 65.67% of the 2002 identified binding peaks, while the exonic and intronic regions represented 1.40% and 3.35%, respectively. Promoter regions constituted 29.59% of the detected peaks (Figure 3b). This efficient CUT&Tag protocol allowed profiling of GmSTF3 binding sites in soybean hairy roots. KEGG enrichment analysis was performed to investigate the functional significance of the targets. The GmSTF3 targets were found to be enriched in metabolic pathways, photosynthesis, starch and sucrose metabolism, and plant–pathogen interactions (Figure 3c; Table S1).

Integration of CUT&Tag and RNA‐Seq Reveals 5 GmSTF3/4 Targets in the Sugar Transport Pathway

RNA‐seq analysis was conducted on hairy roots of GmSTF3‐OE and empty vector‐transformed plants. A total of 13,329 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified by transcriptome analysis between GmSTF3‐EV and GmSTF3‐OE, among which 5908 genes were upregulated and 7421 were downregulated (Figure 3d). KEGG analysis revealed that compared to GmSTF3‐EV, the DEGs in GmSTF3‐OE were mainly enriched in the starch and sucrose metabolism, MAPK signalling, and plant‐pathogen interaction pathways (Figure 3e; Table S2).

To explore the functional relevance of GmSTF3, CUT&Tag and RNA‐seq data were integrated. A total of 127 GmSTF3 target genes were found to be differentially expressed between GmSTF3‐EV and GmSTF3‐OE (Figure 3f). KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that 12 of these genes were involved in the sugar transport pathway, and their encoded proteins were implicated in bidirectional sugar transport (Figure 3g, Figure S2; Table S3). Compared with GmSTF3‐EV, the transcript levels of sugar transporter genes were significantly upregulated in GmSTF3‐OE (Figure 3i). These findings suggest that GmSTF3/4 may bind directly to the promoters of sugar transporter genes, thereby enhancing nematode development.

To confirm the direct binding of GmSTF3/4 to sugar transporter genes, five representative genes from the 12 identified targets were selected for in vitro and in vivo validation. These included GmSWEET8, GmSUC8, GmSWEET10b, GmSWEET13d, and GmERD6‐like (Figure 3h,i). Binding between GmSTF3/4 and the promoter regions of the five genes was validated using Y1H and DLR assays. Promoter fragments (~1.5 kb) of the five target genes were cloned into the pAbAi vector for Y1H. Self‐activation was observed for the GmSWEET8 and GmSWEET13d promoters, and three truncated segments (each ~500 bp) were cloned into pAbAi to eliminate self‐activation (Figure S3). Yeast strains containing pGADT7‐empty and pAbAi‐ProTargets failed to grow on SD/Leu/+AbA medium, whereas strains with pGADT7‐GmSTF3/4 and pAbAi‐ProTargets exhibited growth, confirming the direct binding of GmSTF3/4 to the five promoters (Figure 4a).

The DLR assay was subsequently conducted by co‐expressing the five proTarget:LUC constructs in N. benthamiana leaves with either empty vector or 62SK‐GmSTF3/4 (Figure 4b). Compared to co‐expression with the empty vector, the luminescence signals of proTarget:LUC co‐expressed with 62SK‐GmSTF3/4 were markedly enhanced (Figure 4c). These results indicated that the transcriptional activation capacity of the target promoters was enhanced by GmSTF3/4. Quantitative PCR analysis showed that the expression of the five target genes increased significantly in GmSTF3/4‐OE compared with the EV control (Figure S4a,b). Overall, these results demonstrated that GmSTF3/4 directly activated the transcription of sugar transporter genes by binding to their promoter regions.

Sugar Transporter Localises to the Plasma Membrane and Exhibits Transport Activity

In Arabidopsis thaliana , distinct sugar transporters are localised to various cellular compartments. AtSWEET9, AtSWEET15, and AtSWEET10 are homologous to GmSWEET8, GmSWEET10b, and GmSWEET13d, respectively, and encode proteins localised to the plasma membrane (Chen et al. 2015; Lin et al. 2014). Among the nine AtSUCs, except for AtSUC7 (a confirmed pseudogene) and AtSUC4 (localised to the tonoplast), the remaining seven can also be targeted to the plasma membrane (Seitz et al. 2023). To confirm whether the target genes in this study could be localised to the plasma membrane, GFP fusion vectors were constructed for the five target genes and transiently expressed in N. benthamiana epidermal cells. The co‐localisation of the GFP fusion proteins (green fluorescence) with the plasma membrane marker OsSWEET11‐RFP (red fluorescence) demonstrated that all five proteins were localised to the plasma membrane (Figure 5a).

(c) Functional complementation of hexose transport‐deficient EBY.VW4000 yeast with GmSWEET8, GmSWEET10b, GmSWEET13d, GmSUC8, GmERD6‐like, and AtSWEET1. Yeast cells were cultured in a medium supplemented with 2% maltose, 2% glucose, 2% fructose, and 2% galactose.

To investigate whether the target genes encode functional sugar transporters, these genes were expressed in SUSY7/ura3 mutant yeast lacking the ability to transfer sucrose. SUSY7/ura3 cells expressing AtSWEET12, GmSWEET8, GmSWEET10b, GmSWEET13d, GmSUC8, or GmERD6‐like exhibited significantly enhanced growth on sucrose‐containing medium compared to the empty vector. In contrast, GmERD6‐like expression resulted in growth similar to that of the vector control. These results suggest that GmSWEET8, GmSWEET10b, GmSWEET13d, and GmSUC8 encode functional sucrose transporters, whereas GmERD6‐like lacks detectable sucrose transport activity in yeast (Figure 5b). To further determine whether the five target genes could also transport hexose sugars (glucose, fructose, and galactose), they were expressed in the hexose transport‐deficient yeast mutant EBY.VW4000. On the medium containing only a single hexose as the carbon source, EBY.VW4000 yeast expressing GmSWEET8, GmSWEET13d, GmSUC8, or GmERD6‐like demonstrated no growth, whereas GmSWEET10b expression enabled yeast growth on glucose‐ and fructose‐containing media (Figure 5c). These observations are consistent with previous reports that GmSWEET10b facilitates the transport of both sucrose and hexose (Wang et al. 2020). Collectively, these results suggest that GmSWEET10b functions as a dual sucrose and hexose transporter, whereas GmSWEET8, GmSWEET13d, and GmSUC8 are specific to sucrose transport.

Sucrose Transporters Play Diverse Regulatory Roles in Nematode Parasitism

To evaluate whether the five target genes were induced by light, the expression levels were assessed under different light conditions. All five genes exhibited increased expression compared to the dark treatment (Figure 6a). To clarify the direct role of sugar transporters in nematode resistance, overexpression assays were conducted in transgenic hairy roots, and gene silencing was performed using VIGS. No obvious root mass differences were observed between the overexpression lines and the EV control (Figure S5b). The expression and protein abundance of sugar transporter genes in the overexpression lines were significantly higher than those in the empty vector control, while the silencing efficiencies were higher than those in the control plants (Figure 6b–d; Figure S6a,b). The results indicated that nematode development was accelerated in the roots overexpressing GmSUC8, GmSWEET10b, and GmSWEET13d, whereas development was delayed in GmSWEET10b and GmSWEET13d silenced lines (Figure 6f,j). Notably, the GmSWEET10b‐silenced line displayed a phenotype of suppressed nematode infection (Figure 6i), suggesting that GmSWEET10b facilitated both the infection and developmental progression of nematodes. Based on these observations, Gmsweet10b mutants were inoculated with nematodes to further characterise their gene function. Gmsweet10b mutant plants exhibited no significant difference in the root length and root mass compared to WT plants (Figure S7). These results were consistent with those from the silencing lines, as the Gmsweet10b mutants significantly suppressed nematode infection and development (Figure 6g,h). Interestingly, both overexpression and silencing of GmSWEET8 enhanced resistance to nematode infection without affecting developmental progression (Figure 6e,i). These results imply that GmSWEET8 may contribute to a resistance pathway that is distinct from nutrient‐related mechanisms.

GmCOP1a Binds to and Degrades GmSTF3/4 Proteins via Ubiquitination

AtCOP1 is known to regulate the abundance of AtHY5 during the light‐mediated development of Arabidopsis thaliana (Gangappa and Botto 2016). Bioinformatic analysis has identified two copies of GmCOP1 in the soybean genome: GmCOP1a and GmCOP1b (Ji et al. 2022). A recent study showed that GmCOP1a/b can interact with GmSTF1 and GmSTF2 under both light and dark conditions to regulate isoflavonoid biosynthesis in soybean (Song et al. 2024). To investigate the interaction between GmCOP1a/b and GmSTF3/4, Y2H assay was performed. The results revealed specific interactions between GmSTF3 and GmSTF4 with GmCOP1a (Figure 7a), but not with GmCOP1b (Figure S8), in yeast cells. To confirm this interaction in vivo, bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) was conducted. GmSTF3‐nYFP or GmSTF4‐nYFP was co‐expressed with GmCOP1a‐cCFP in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. The reconstituted YFP signals colocalized with the nuclear marker H2B‐mRFP1, confirming nuclear interaction (Figure 7b). Additionally, the co‐immunoprecipitation (co‐IP) assays were performed by transiently co‐expressing GmSTF3‐GFP or GmSTF4‐GFP with GmCOP1a‐3 × FLAG in N. benthamiana leaves. Successful co‐IP was observed, whereas the GFP empty vector control showed no interaction (Figure 7c). Collectively, these findings confirmed the interaction between GmSTF3/4 and GmCOP1a.

Given that GmCOP1a functions as an E3 ubiquitin ligase, it might degrade GmSTF3 and GmSTF4. To test this hypothesis, Agrobacterium‐mediated co‐infiltration of N. benthamiana leaves with GmCOP1a‐3 × FLAG and GmSTF3‐GFP or GmSTF4‐GFP was performed. After 3 days of incubation, immunoblotting was performed to assess protein abundance. The levels of GmSTF3 and GmSTF4 were reduced in the presence of GmCOP1a, indicating that GmCOP1a negatively regulated their abundance. Moreover, co‐injection with the 26S proteasome inhibitor MG‐132 largely restored GmSTF3 and GmSTF4 to levels comparable to those in samples without GmCOP1a expression. These results indicated that GmSTF3 and GmSTF4 were targeted for degradation by GmCOP1a via the 26S proteasome pathway (Figure 7d,e).

GmCOP1a Counteracts GmSTF3/4‐Mediated Nematode Susceptibility

To determine the direct role of GmCOP1a in nematode parasitism, GmCOP1a overexpression lines were generated using a GFP‐tagged hairy root system (Figure 7f). GmCOP1a overexpression lines exhibited no mass changes in hairy roots compared to EV control (Figure S5a). The transcripts and protein accumulations of GmCOP1a in the tested lines were validated by qPCR and western blot assays (Figure 7g, Figure S1a). After inoculation with H. glycines , compared with the EV control, the GmCOP1a overexpressing lines exhibited no significant differences in nematode numbers (Figure 7j). However, the GmCOP1a overexpressing lines showed retarded nematode development, whereas the opposite trend was observed in GmSTF3/4 overexpressing lines (Figure 7i,k).

To further confirm the role of GmCOP1a in inhibiting cyst nematode development, we generated GmCOP1a silence lines using TRV‐mediated virus‐induced gene silencing technology, and three silencing lines of GmCOP1a‐RNAi were obtained. Quantitative and semi‐quantitative PCR assays revealed that the expression level of GmCOP1a in the roots of TRV: GmCOP1a‐infiltrated plants was significantly decreased compared to the EV control (Figure 7h, Figure S1b). Two lines (GmCOP1a‐RNAi1 and GmCOP1a‐RNAi3) with high silencing efficiency were selected for the nematode infection assay. The results showed that the proportion of juveniles was significantly decreased in the TRV:GmCOP1a‐infiltrated plants compared with the EV control (Figure 7l,n), while no significant difference in nematode numbers was found between the TRV: GmCOP1a‐infiltrated plants and EV control (Figure 7m). Taken together, these data suggest that soybean employs GmCOP1a to counteract GmSTF3/4‐mediated susceptibility through its degradation mechanism.

Discussion

Light signalling is a crucial environmental factor that regulates plant growth and development. In the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana , the regulatory mechanism involving HY5 and the COP1/SPA module within the light signalling pathway has been extensively characterised (Gangappa and Botto 2016; Mankotia et al. 2024). This study demonstrated that light signalling increased soybean susceptibility to cyst nematodes and clarified the functional role of the GmSTF3/4‐mediated signalling pathway in SCN pathogenicity. Our results indicated that sugar transporter genes directly activated by mobile GmSTF3/4 proteins promoted nematode development (Figure 8). These findings underscore the significance of the GmSTF3/4‐sugar transporter signalling module in facilitating nematode developmental progression, offering important theoretical insights for managing plant‐parasitic nematodes.

Proposed working model of GmSTF3/4 in promoting nematode development. Nematode infection triggers a light signalling pathway response, accelerating the long‐distance transport of the mobile protein GmSTF3/4 from the shoot to the root. In the root, GmSTF3/4 bind to the promoters of sugar transporter genes such as GmSWEET10b and GmSWEET13d, promoting sucrose transport across the plasma membrane and thereby enhancing nematode development. Conversely, GmCOP1a interacts with GmSTF3/4 and mediates their ubiquitination, leading to loss of their regulatory function.

Plant light receptor genes are known to respond to biotic stress (Ballaré 2014; Iqbal et al. 2021). In both Arabidopsis thaliana and tomato, blue and red light receptors have been implicated in regulating immune responses and enhancing resistance to Pseudomonas syringae , thereby improving plant survival under biotic stress (Hao et al. 2025; Yang et al. 2015). Unlike light‐enhanced plant resistance to pathogens in leaf tissues, we found that light promotes soybean susceptibility to the SCN, including facilitating infection and development. Previous studies have shown that light activates the blue light receptor GmCRY1 to induce the formation of rhizobial infection threads (Wang et al. 2021). Another study demonstrated that the UV‐B receptor GmUVR8 promotes the accumulation of isoflavones and daidzein to fine‐tune soybean nodulation (Chen et al. 2024). These data suggest that light activates photoreceptors involvement in plant responses to biotic stress. Our previous studies reported that light promotes root‐knot nematode infection in Arabidopsis thaliana , and subsequent validation confirmed that photoreceptor genes mutation inhibits nematode infection (Wu et al. 2024). This indicates that a mechanism exists through which light influences nematode infection. Further experiments are indeed required to investigate the molecular mechanism by which light‐promoted cyst nematode infection in soybean. Our study showed that nematode development induced the expression of genes associated with light perception, with a particularly strong induction observed in the root and stem tissues. This may be attributed to nematode‐triggered host resource redistribution mechanisms that activate shoot‐to‐root mobile proteins in the light signalling pathway.

An increasing body of evidence suggests that light signalling can modulate root development either directly or indirectly through shoot‐to‐root communication involving various phytohormones and mobile proteins (Burko et al. 2020; Chen et al. 2016; Lee et al. 2017; Sassi et al. 2012; van Gelderen et al. 2018). As essential light signalling regulators, Arabidopsis thaliana HY5 and its soybean homologues GmSTF3/4 respond to biotic stress at specific light wavelengths (Chen et al. 2024; Gangappa and Botto 2016; Wang et al. 2021). Studies have shown that dodder (Cuscuta spp.) acquires phloem‐mobile proteins from host plants (Jiang et al. 2013). XSP10, a mobile protein abundant in tomato xylem sap, is required for susceptibility to Fusarium wilt (Krasikov et al. 2010). Our results indicated that nematodes facilitated the shoot‐to‐root translocation of GmSTF3/4 proteins, thereby enhancing cyst nematode development. During infection in Arabidopsis thaliana , cyst nematodes can markedly increase the number of sieve elements and companion cells surrounding syncytial complexes, thereby improving macromolecule transport efficiency to the feeding site (Hoth et al. 2005, 2008). The remodelling of phloem formation by cyst nematodes may account for the accelerated movement of GmSTF3/4. Previous research has shown that blue light can promote nodule formation by activating GmSTF3/4 and inducing nodulation factor genes GmNIN and GmNF‐YA/B (Wang et al. 2021), suggesting that GmSTF3/4 may similarly participate in both nodulation and nematode development.

Although GmSTF3/4 are involved in nematode stress responses, relatively few direct downstream targets have been identified. GmSTF3/4 has been shown to bind to the promoters of GmMYB12L, GmMYB12B2, and GmCHS9, thereby activating flavonoid biosynthesis in soybean (Chen et al. 2024). Integrated CUT&Tag and RNA‐seq analyses, along with subsequent Y1H and DLR assays, further confirmed that GmSTF3/4 can bind directly to several sugar transporter gene promoters, reinforcing its critical role in the soybean response to SCN stress.

Sugar transporters are key players in plant‐pathogen interactions, particularly in parasitic nematode infections. Following root invasion, nematodes can induce the formation of specialised feeding structures (e.g., giant cells or syncytia), which require a continuous sugar supply to support nematode development and reproduction. Evidence indicates that nematodes can reprogram host sugar transport systems. For instance, Arabidopsis thaliana AtSWEET1 expression can be induced during root‐knot nematode infection to redirect carbohydrates to the giant cells (Zhou et al. 2023). Similarly, SWEET transporter family members are selectively upregulated in tomato roots to meet nematode nutritional demands (Zhao et al. 2018). Root‐knot nematodes also exploit transcription factors to activate sugar transporter genes, thereby ensuring nutrient acquisition (Wu et al. 2024). These findings reflect the evolutionary strategies of nematodes to hijack plant carbohydrate metabolism. In this study, we identified the soybean GmSTF3/4‐sugar transporter regulatory module as a key contributor to nematode development. Notably, GmSWEET10b exhibited significant susceptibility to cyst nematodes, suggesting that it may serve as a promising target for improving SCN resistance in soybean. GmSWEET13d moderately promoted nematode development while also showing potential in resistance regulation. Although GmSUC8 silencing did not affect nematode development, its overexpression did, implying possible functional redundancy among GmSUC8 homologues. Further research is warranted to dissect the contribution of specific GmSUC8 homologues to nematode susceptibility.

Surprisingly, GmSWEET8 exhibited nematode infection‐suppressing activity without affecting subsequent developmental progression. This observation indicated functional divergence among SWEET family members, whose roles extended beyond nutritional provisioning to include responses to pathogen infection. In addition to their role as energy sources, sugars may function as signalling molecules to activate defence‐related gene expression, as suggested in recent studies (Lemonnier et al. 2014; Trouvelot et al. 2014). Reports have shown that sucrose can induce the expression of pathogenesis‐related (PR) genes (Gomez‐Ariza et al. 2007; Thibaud et al. 2004) and that sugar metabolism positively influences salicylic acid signalling (Gebauer et al. 2017). Measuring root sugar content and analysing resistance gene expression may offer valuable insights into the diverse phenotypic responses observed during nematode infection.

Comparative genomic analysis indicated that soybean contained four HY5 homologues, whereas Arabidopsis thaliana harboured only a single copy, suggesting a potential functional divergence in light signal transduction. Immobile GmSTF1/2 proteins displayed highly conserved canonical structures. In contrast, GmSTF3/4 proteins were shorter than GmSTF1/2 and lacked key motifs, which may confer unique functional properties. Recent studies have shown that GmSTF1/2 interacts with both GmCOP1a and GmCOP1b (Song et al. 2024). Our data demonstrated that GmSTF3/4 specifically interacted with GmCOP1a, indicating distinct interaction patterns between GmSTF3/4 and the two COP1 paralogs. Previous studies in Arabidopsis thaliana , the AtCOP1 acts as a repressor of AtHY5, positively regulating plant resistance to root‐knot nematode stress (Wu et al. 2024). In this study, we reveal that soybean employs GmCOP1a to counteract GmSTF3/4‐mediated susceptibility through its degradation mechanism, demonstrating a functionally conserved role of GmCOP1a in nematode stress.

Experimental Procedures

Plant Material and Growth Conditions

All experiments were conducted using the soybean cultivar Williams 82 (W‐82), which is susceptible to soybean cyst nematodes. Gmsweet10b mutant was obtained from the Northeast Institute of Geography and Agroecology, Chinese Academy of Sciences. After surface sterilisation for 8 min with 1% NaClO, W‐82 seeds were rinsed at least three times with sterile water. The seeds were germinated in plastic pots (10 cm in diameter and 12 cm in height) containing equal proportions of potting substrate and sterilised vermiculite. The growth conditions were maintained at 24°C–27°C with 45% relative humidity under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod.

For light treatment after germination, seedlings were grown under white light in a 16 h light/8 h dark cycle for 5 days. They were then transferred to continuous darkness for 2 days before being subjected to different light conditions. Light treatments included white (400–720 nm, 200 μmol m−2 s−1), red (620–720 nm, 200 μmol m−2 s−1), blue (400–500 nm, 200 μmol m−2 s−1), and continuous darkness. After light exposure, the seedlings were inoculated with J2s. Each biological replicate contained 15 plants.

Second‐Stage Juveniles (J2s) Collection and Inoculation

A nematode‐susceptible soybean cultivar (Liaodou 15) was used to maintain the population of soybean cyst nematode race 3 in the greenhouse of the nematology laboratory, Shenyang Agricultural University. After isolation from nematode‐infested soil, the eggs were purified by centrifugation for 5 min in 30% sucrose. J2s were collected using 3 mM ZnSO4 at 26°C.

For SCN infection and development assays, five holes were made approximately 5 cm from the root surface of each plant. The nematode suspension (1000 J2s per treatment) was injected evenly into the holes using pipette tips to distribute nematodes around the root system. The holes were then covered with soil. To assess the light‐induced responses in nematodes, different light wavelengths were applied during infection. On the 15th day after inoculation, the roots were washed to remove soil and decolorized using 5.5% NaClO for 5 min. The root material was subsequently boiled for 30 s in an acid fuchsin solution (3.5% acid fuchsin in 25% acetic acid), rinsed with water, soaked in 20 mL glycerin containing 200 μL 5 N HCl, and heated to boiling (Bybd Jr et al. 1983). Nematodes of various developmental stages were observed under a stereomicroscope. The developmental stage was quantified using the following formula: J2s number/total number of nematodes.

RNA Extraction and Quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted using the RNAiso Plus reagent (Takara Bio, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions. cDNA synthesis was performed using a PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara Bio, Japan). Quantitative PCR was performed using One‐Step SYBR (Takara Bio, Japan). Gmactin and Gmubi‐3 were used as the reference genes, and the gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. All primers are listed in Table S4. Each qPCR assay included three technical replicates and was repeated with four independent biological replicates.

Yeast Two‐Hybrid Assay

For Y2H assays, the CDS sequences of GmSTF3, GmSTF4, GmCOP1a, and GmCOP1b were ligated into the pGADT7 and pGBKT7 vectors. The yeast cells were cultured on SD/−Trp‐Leu medium following transformation with various vector combinations at 28°C for 3 days. The transformed yeast cells were serially diluted in ddH2O at ratios of 100, 101, 102, and 103 and plated on SD/−Trp‐Leu or SD/−Trp‐Leu‐His selective media. Yeast co‐transformed with AD‐SV40T and BD‐P53 served as positive controls.

BiFC and CoIP Assay

For BiFC assays, the coding sequences of GmSTF3, GmSTF4, and GmCOP1a were amplified from soybean cDNA and inserted into the pXNGW and pXCGW vectors. Constructs of GmSTF3‐nYFP, GmSTF4‐nYFP, and GmCOP1a‐cYFP were generated. Each construct combination was co‐infiltrated with the H2B‐mRFP1 nuclear marker and P19 at a 1:1:1:1 ratio in N. benthamiana leaves. Confocal microscopy was performed 2 days after infiltration to capture the fluorescence signals.

For CoIP assays, GmSTF3, GmSTF4, and GmCOP1a were cloned into the pGDGm and pGD‐3 × Flag vectors (Goodin et al. 2002) to generate the GmSTF3‐GFP, GmSTF4‐GFP, and GmCOP1a‐3 × FLAG constructs. The constructs were transformed into GV3101. For each assay, the infiltration mix included P19 at a ratio of 5:5:3 and was injected into N. benthamiana leaves. Two days post‐infiltration, proteins were extracted, and total protein lysates were immunoprecipitated using anti‐GFP magnetic beads (Beyotime Biotechnology). Anti‐GFP (1:5000, Invitrogen) and anti‐FLAG (1:5000, Invitrogen) antibodies were used for immunoblotting.

Protein Degradation Assay

The CDS of GmSTF3, GmSTF4, and GmCOP1a were individually cloned into pGDGm and pGD‐3 × Flag vectors and transformed into Agrobacterium GV3101. Positive colonies were cultured in LB overnight and resuspended in infiltration buffer (0.1 mmol·L−1 MES, 0.1 mmol·L−1 MgCl2 and 0.4 μmol·L−1 acetosyringone) to an OD600 = 1. The cultures were mixed at a 1:1 ratio and co‐infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. Two days after infiltration, 100 μmol/L of MG132 (dissolved in DMSO and buffer) was injected into the infiltrated regions 4 h before harvest. An equal volume of DMSO in buffer was used as a control. Leaves of comparable size were harvested, and protein extraction was conducted using a lysis buffer. Protein levels of GmSTF3, GmSTF4, and GmCOP1a were assessed by immunoblotting with an anti‐GFP antibody (1:5000, Invitrogen). An anti‐α‐actin antibody was used as a loading control.

Soybean Leaf Injection and Fluorescence Localization

The CDS sequences of GmSTF3 and GmSTF4 were cloned into the pNI9001 vector with a GFP tag. The constructs were transformed into Agrobacterium GV3101 for transient expression. The bacterial cultures were adjusted to OD600 = 0.8 in infiltration buffer (0.1 mmol L−1 MgCl2, 0.1 mmol L−1 MES, and 0.4 μmol L−1 acetosyringone) and kept in darkness for 3 h before infiltration.

Soybean plants were exposed to light to induce stomatal opening, and the infiltration solution was introduced into the lower epidermis of the leaf under pressure using a vacuum pump. This process continued until the leaf surface was fully saturated. The transformed soybean seedlings were then maintained in continuous darkness for 1 day prior to nematode inoculation.

The protein levels of GmSTF3/4‐GFP were detected using anti‐GFP antibodies in the leaves and roots at 7 dpi. α‐Actin served as the loading control. For cryosectioning, soybean roots were harvested at 7 dpi following H. glycines infection and embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound. The samples were flash‐frozen in liquid nitrogen and sectioned into slices of approximately 50 μm thickness using a Leica CM1860 cryostat. Fluorescence signals were visualised using an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Construction Overexpressing Hairy Root of Soybean

The coding sequences of GmSTF3, GmSTF4, and GmCOP1a were amplified from the soybean cDNA and inserted into the overexpression vector pNI9001 (Yang et al. 2021) to generate the fusion constructs GmSTF3‐OE, GmSTF4‐OE, and GmCOP1a‐OE. Using the freeze–thaw transformation method, overexpression plasmids were introduced into Agrobacterium rhizogenes strain K599. Williams 82 seeds were germinated in a greenhouse for 6 days. Primary roots were excised near the hypocotyl, and the cut seedlings were infected with K599 in the hypocotyl region. Infected seedlings were then transferred to a humid environment (Kereszt et al. 2007). After 20 days of growth, the soybean hairy roots were rinsed with running water and examined under a fluorescence lamp (Luyor, China) for GFP signal detection. Hairy roots exhibiting strong GFP fluorescence were selected for nematode inoculation experiments.

VIGS

Three conserved fragments of approximately 300 bp from the CDS regions of GmSTF3/4 and GmCOP1a were amplified and cloned into the tobacco rattle virus RNA2 vector (TRV2) using EcoRI and KpnI restriction sites (Liu et al. 2002). The fusion constructs, TRV2‐GmSTF3/4, TRV2‐GmCOP1a‐1, TRV2‐GmCOP1a‐2, and TRV2‐GmCOP1a‐3, were transformed into Agrobacterium GV3101. Agrobacterium cultures harbouring TRV2 or the RNA1 vector (TRV1) were shaken and cultured to OD600 = 0.5–0.6. The cultures were mixed at a 1:1 ratio and resuspended in infiltration buffer (0.1 mmol·L−1 MgCl2, 0.1 mmol·L−1 MES and 0.4 μmol L−1 acetosyringone) for 2 h in the dark. Approximately 15 mL of the suspension was applied to irrigate two‐week‐old soybean seedlings (Li et al. 2023). Inoculation was performed every 5 days for a total of three treatments. Leaves displaying obvious chlorosis were selected for subsequent nematode inoculation experiments.

CUT&Tag

CUT&Tag was performed using the Hyperactive Universal CUT&Tag Assay Kit for Illumina Pro (Vazyme, China), in strict accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, hairy roots displaying strong GmSTF3‐OE signals were ground in liquid nitrogen, and nuclei were isolated using a nuclear separation kit for plant samples (SHBIO, China). The isolated nuclei were resuspended in Wash Buffer (Vazyme, China) and incubated with ConA Beads (Vazyme, China) for 5 min. Following the removal of the Wash Buffer, the sample was incubated with 2 μL of primary anti‐GFP antibody (1:5000, Invitrogen) overnight at 4°C. Subsequently, 50 μL of Dig‐Wash Buffer containing secondary antibodies was added, and the nuclei were incubated for 1 h. Nuclei were then treated with pA/G‐Tnp Pro transposase for 1 h at room temperature. The reaction mixture was combined with 10% SDS and incubated for 10 min at 55°C. The resulting supernatant was transferred to DNA Extract Beads Pro and incubated for 20 min, followed by washing with 1× B&W Buffer. DNA was eluted and amplified using 2× CAM (Vazyme, China). The amplified product was purified using VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme, China) and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform.

RNA‐Seq

The hairy roots of soybean transformed with GmSTF3 overexpression or empty vector were harvested for RNA‐seq. Three independent roots were pooled per sample with three biological replicates. The reference genome of Williams 82 (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gwh/Assembly/37536/show) was used for read assembly.

Yeast One‐Hybrid Assay

The 1500‐bp promoter regions of sugar transporter genes were cloned into the pAbAi vector (Takara Bio, Japan), while the CDS sequences of GmSTF3 and GmSTF4 were inserted into the pGADT7 vector (Takara Bio, Japan). To assess the self‐activation potential of GmSWEET8 and GmSWEET13d promoters, three 500‐bp fragments (designated P1, P2, and P3) from the promoters of GmSWEET8 and GmSWEET13d were cloned separately into the pAbAi vector. These constructs were transformed into Y1H Gold yeast strain for stable genome integration. The binding activity of GmSTF3/4 to the promoters was assessed on the SD/−Leu medium supplemented with various concentrations of Aureobasidin A.

Dual Luciferase Reporter (DLR) Assays

A DLR assay was conducted in N. benthamiana leaves. The promoters of the target genes were inserted into the pGreenII0800‐LUC vector to generate reporter constructs, whereas the CDS of GmSTF3 and GmSTF4 was cloned into pGreenII62‐SK to serve as effectors. These constructs were independently introduced into N. benthamiana leaves. After 2 days, the infiltrated leaves were sprayed with 2 mM luciferin, and the LUC signal was detected using an IVScope 7200 multicolor chemiluminescence imaging system (ClinX, China).

Subcellular Localization Assay

The coding sequences of GmSUC8, GmERD6‐like, GmSWEET8, GmSWEET10b, and GmSWEET13d were cloned into the pNI9001 vector to generate fusion constructs, which were transformed into the GV3101 strain of Agrobacterium tumefaciens . These constructs were co‐infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves along with the plasma membrane‐localised marker OsSWEET11‐RFP. Fluorescence signals were visualised using an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) 48 h after infiltration.

Sucrose and Hexose Transport Assays in Yeast

The full‐length CDS of GmSUC8, GmERD6‐like, GmSWEET8, GmSWEET10b, and GmSWEET13d was cloned into the pDR196 vector (Rentsch et al. 1995). The AtSWEET1 and AtSWEET12 genes were also cloned into pDR196 and used as positive controls. For complementation analysis, recombinant plasmids and the empty vector were introduced into the Saccharomyces cerevisiae mutant strain SUSY7/ura3, which lacked sucrose transport activity (Riesmeier et al. 1992). Transformants were cultured in a medium containing 2% sucrose to assess sucrose transport. To assess hexose transport capacity, the constructs were introduced into the hexose transport‐deficient S. cerevisiae strain EBY.VW4000 (Wieczorke et al. 1999). The cells were cultured overnight in liquid medium with 2% maltose as the sole carbon source, then plated onto solid medium containing 2% glucose, 2% fructose, or 2% galactose, and incubated for 2 days at 30°C.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8. Student's t‐test or one‐way ANOVA was conducted to determine statistically significant differences between treatments.
