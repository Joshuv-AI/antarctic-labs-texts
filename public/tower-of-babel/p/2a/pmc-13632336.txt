A bivalent multi-epitope vaccine targeting CSP and TRAP of Plasmodium vivax designed through immunoinformatics and structural bioinformatics
Authors: Muharib Alruwaili, Intisar Alruwaili, Bayan Fallatah, Muhammad Umer Khan, Hasan Ejaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalaria associated with Plasmodium vivax is still one of the main public health concerns due to relapse-associated infections. Moreover, the absence of a broadly effective licensed vaccine makes the situation more alarming. In this scenario, epitope-based vaccine design helps to improve safety, immunogenicity, and population coverage. The integrated workflow of immune informatics and structural bioinformatics was used to establish a bivalent multi-epitope vaccine that targets pre-erythrocytic antigens, circumsporozoite protein (CSP), and thrombospondin-related adhesive protein (TRAP). Antigenicity, allergenicity, toxicity, and transmembrane topology were evaluated before epitope prediction. B-cell, MHC class I, and MHC class II epitopes were identified, screened, and their antigenic origins were suggested. The vaccine construct was assembled by using linkers and an adjuvant. Population coverage analysis, physicochemical characterization, three-dimensional structure prediction, refinement, and validation help to strengthen the goal. Molecular docking with Toll-like receptors (TLR2 and TLR4), immune response simulation, codon optimization, and in silico cloning were performed. The final construct was predicted to be antigenic, non-allergenic, nontoxic, and structurally stable, with broad global population coverage (98.8%). Docking analyses predicted potentially stable interactions with both TLR2 and TLR4. Immune simulation suggested coordinated innate and adaptive immune activation. The feasibility of expressing a protein was predicted using codon optimization and cloning analysis studies. Introduction

Malaria is among the serious public health concerns. Yearly, it infects millions of people globally. Environmental and socioeconomic factors of tropical and subtropical countries contribute to spreading the disease. Malaria can easily spread under such conditions [1]. Mortality from the disease has decreased over recent decades [2]. Nevertheless, it is still not possible to completely eradicate the disease. The above situation shows that current methods of controlling malaria are not very efficient [3].

The causative agents of malaria are Plasmodium parasites. Malaria continues to be one of the global public health concerns. Among all species of malaria, Plasmodium vivax causes significant health effects but receives the least amount of scientific and research attention compared to others [4]. It mostly occurs in Asia, Latin America, and the Western Pacific [5]. In the past, Plasmodium vivax malaria has been known as a minor health concern, causing fewer deaths than other types of Plasmodium parasites. Hence, there has been little scientific interest in researching it [6]. However, it appears that according to the latest data, P. vivax malaria is far from being a non-life-threatening condition that requires minimal attention [7]. Actually, this disease leads to the recurrence of infections, which is a heavy burden for the sufferers and the health care system [8].

Controlling P. vivax malaria has never been an easy task because of the peculiarities of this parasite. One of the distinctive properties of P. vivax is the ability to form hypnozoites (dormant liver stage) [9]. These hypnozoites may remain alive for weeks or months without causing visible effects until they become active and cause reactivation of the disease [10]. The patients experience repeated episodes of malaria. This episodic pattern results in recurrent infections, increased morbidity, prolonged illness, and greater treatment demands. Moreover, recurrent infections contribute actively to the spread of infection. It creates additional difficulties in eliminating this type of malaria, especially in low-endemic countries [8].

Several biological and operational restrictions exist within the strategy for coping with P. vivax. Currently available anti-malarial drugs mostly affect parasites in the blood, while hypnozoites in the liver remain largely unaffected due to their dormancy [11, 12]. Though the radical cure therapies target the liver stage parasite, their usage is hindered by the toxic effects of the therapy, low adherence among patients, and certain genetic predispositions such as deficiency of G6PD enzyme [7]. Vector control measures are able to reduce the mosquito-borne transmission rate; however, they cannot prevent repeated infections [13, 14]. Collectively, these examples suggest that the existing control measures are ineffective in dealing with Plasmodium vivax infection and new solutions are required [15].

Against this background, vaccine-preventive strategies targeting the early phases of infection may be viewed as the potential solution [16, 17]. The circumsporozoite protein (CSP) constitutes one of the main surface antigens in malaria sporozoites and is pivotal in liver cell invasion, being exposed at the earliest stages of the infection process. Consequently, CSP presents itself as an appropriate and logical candidate for the malaria vaccine. Yet CSP-based vaccines aimed at fighting P. vivax have yet to prove efficient and represent a difficult task [18]. Solving this problem by combining pre-erythrocytic antigens related to the movement and traversal of sporozoites through host cells (e.g., thrombospondin-related adhesive protein) will allow for a wider scope of immunogenic activity [19]. Therefore, developing a vaccine targeting both CSP and TRAP provides for the dual use of biological functions vital to the early stage of the parasite establishment. Moreover, efficient malaria vaccine production requires triggering of the early innate immunity responses through the action of receptors, such as Toll-like receptors (TLR2 and TLR4), responsible for recognizing parasite antigens [20].

With the advancement of computational and immunoinformatic-based approaches play a significant and fruitful role in medicine. Traditional wet laboratory vaccine development is time-intensive, costly, and resource-demanding [21, 22]. Now, computational methods provide an efficient and rational framework for designing the vaccine with the use of the systematic identification and evaluation of antigenic, immunogenic, and safe epitopes before experimental validation [23]. Immunoinformatic tools facilitate in silico screening and optimization that reduce both time and cost. These approaches enhance reproducibility and precision by making them particularly valuable for early-stage vaccine discovery [24].

Collectively, there is a gap, and existing CSP-based vaccine approaches have predicted limited and inconsistent protective efficacy, which may be attributed to antigenic variability and suboptimal epitope selection [25]. Therefore, the development of a rationally designed multi-epitope vaccine shows a promising strategy to enhance immunogenicity and protective potential. In this study, immunoinformatic and computational approaches were employed to design and evaluate an in silico bivalent multi-epitope vaccine construct derived from both CSP and TRAP of Plasmodium vivax, providing a systematic and cost-effective framework for early-stage pre-erythrocytic vaccine discovery.

Methodology

Retrieval and selection of proteins

A total of two reference antigen protein sequences, Circumsporozoite Protein (CSP) and Thrombospondin-Related Adhesive Protein (TRAP) of Plasmodium vivax, were retrieved from the UniProt database [26] in FASTA format. Protein selection was performed based on their relevance to host invasion and vaccine design. Antigenicity of selected proteins was evaluated by using the VaxiJen server [27]. Allergenicity was evaluated using AllerTOP v2.0 [28]. And toxicity was predicted using the ToxinPred server [29]. Transmembrane topology and extracellular accessibility were analyzed using the DeepTMHMM server [30].

Epitope prediction and filtration

Cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL; Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) class I) and helper T-lymphocyte (HTL; MHC class II) epitopes were predicted using Immune Epitope Database (IEDB) Next Generation Analysis Resource [31]. Linear B-cell epitopes were predicted by using the ABCPred server [32]. Predicted epitopes were further screened based on antigenicity (VaxiJen), allergenicity (AllerTOP v2.0: [20]) and toxicity (ToxinPred: [33]) to ensure immunogenicity and safety.

Epitope selection and origin confirmation

Shortlisting of epitope candidates was further finalized for the construction of a multiepitope vaccine structure by taking into consideration immunogenic relevance as well as the safety profile of selected epitopes from the pool of shortlisted epitopes. In order to ensure traceability of antigens used for the construction of epitopes of a multiepitope vaccine, the origin of the epitopes was ascertained before proceeding towards the construction of the epitope-based structure of the vaccine. Each of the selected epitopes was individually subjected to BLASTp analysis using the Plasmodium vivax non-redundant proteome database [34, 35]. The top-scoring hit query coverage and percent identity were recorded to confirm derivation from either Circumsporozoite Protein (CSP) or Thrombospondin-Related Adhesive Protein (TRAP).

Vaccine construct assembly

Selected epitopes were arranged into a single multi-epitope vaccine construct using appropriate linkers to enhance structural flexibility, processing efficiency, and optimal epitope presentation. AAY (Ala-Ala-Tyr) linkers were employed between MHC class I epitopes to facilitate efficient proteasomal cleavage and improve antigen processing through the MHC-I presentation pathway. GPGPG (Glycine-Proline-Glycine-Proline-Glycine) linkers were incorporated between MHC class II epitopes due to their flexible spacer properties, which minimize junctional epitope formation and promote independent folding and presentation of helper T-cell epitopes. Although flexible linkers may theoretically influence conformational continuity, in linear multi-epitope constructs, GPGPG has been widely applied to preserve epitope separation and maintain immunogenic independence. KK (Lys-Lys) linkers were used to connect B-cell epitopes in order to introduce lysine-mediated cleavage sites that support antigen processing efficiency. The selection of these linkers was guided by previously reported experimental and computational multi-epitope vaccine studies demonstrating improved epitope presentation, reduced steric interference, and enhanced structural organization [36, 37].

Human β-defensin-2 (hBD-2/DEFB4A) was incorporated as the N-terminal adjuvant to enhance immunogenicity and innate immune activation. The corresponding adjuvant amino acid sequence is provided in Supplementary Table S4. The construct obtained after fusion was tagged with 6×His at the carboxyl terminus to enhance expression and purification. The vaccine construct was tested further for antigenicity (VaxiJen), allergenicity (AllerTOP v2.0), and toxicity (ToxinPred).

The final assembled multi-epitope vaccine construct was evaluated for transmembrane helices and overall membrane topology using the DeepTMHMM server [38]. Signal peptide prediction was performed using SignalP version 6.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/SignalP-6.0/) to evaluate the presence of N-terminal signal peptide regions within the final vaccine construct. DeepTMHMM analysis revealed no predicted transmembrane helices across the full-length construct. SignalP 6.0 predicted the presence of an N-terminal signal peptide (Sec/SPI) with a prediction likelihood of 0.9998 and a predicted cleavage site between amino acid positions 22 and 23 (cleavage probability = 0.977827). All remaining residues were classified as non-membrane-associated, indicating that the vaccine construct is likely to remain soluble and suitable for recombinant expression without structural complications related to membrane insertion.

Population coverage analysis

Population coverage of selected T cell epitopes was assessed by using the IEDB Population Coverage tool [39]. This analysis estimates that the proportion of individuals across different ethnic and geographic populations is likely to mount an immune response based on HLA allele distribution.

Solubility prediction and physicochemical properties

The Protein Sol server was used to predict the vaccine construct’s solubility [40]. Physicochemical properties, including molecular weight, theoretical isoelectric point (pI), instability index, aliphatic index, GRAVY score, and half-life, were calculated using the ExPASy ProtParam tool [41].

Secondary structure prediction

The secondary (2D) structure of the final multi-epitope vaccine construct was predicted using the PSIPRED server to evaluate the distribution of α-helices, β-strands, and random coil regions before tertiary structure modeling [42].

Refinement of structure and prediction of tertiary structure

The 3D structure of the vaccine construct was predicted using AlphaFold2 implemented through ColabFold [43, 44]. To enhance the stereochemical quality of the predicted structure, the GalaxyRefine server was used [45]. This improves backbone geometry and side chain conformations, while preserving the global fold. Among refined models, the best-scoring model was selected based on MolProbity score, steric clash reduction, and Ramachandran plot statistics.

Structural validation

Structural validation of the refined three-dimensional vaccine model was performed to assess stereochemical integrity and overall structural reliability. The PROCHECK program [46] was used to check the backbone dihedral angles (phi and psi) of amino acid residues through Ramachandran plot analysis. This analysis identifies whether the residues are in energetically favored conformational areas. In this regard, the more residues fall into the most favored or additionally allowed areas, the better the stereochemical quality of the constructed model and its proper backbone folding.

Both Ramachandran plot statistics and ERRAT scores have been calculated with the help of the Structural Analysis and Verification Server (SAVES). The ERRAT analysis [47] assesses the quality of non-bonded atomic interactions by evaluating the statistical frequency of atom-atom contacts in the model against that of the high-resolution crystallographic structures. The output quality factor gives an indication of structural reliability, with higher values representing better results.

Codon optimization and genetic preparation

The sequence of amino acids of the vaccine construct was back-translated to the nucleotide sequence, with the help of the EMBOSS Backtranseq tool [50]. This is necessary for ensuring that the reading frame is maintained. Codon optimization was carried out for the E. coli K-12 strain utilizing different servers, such as JCat [51], IDT Codon Optimization Tool (https://www.idtdna.com/CodonOpt), and GenSmart (https://www.genscript.com/tools/gensmart-codon-optimization). Criteria include GC balance, elimination of rare codons, and no premature stop codons. The optimized sequence was named Vaccine_Optimized_EcoliK12.

In silico cloning and restriction digestion validation

The vaccine sequence was optimized in silico and then cloned into the pBR322 plasmid vector using Benchling software [52]. The Gibson assembly simulation allows for a seamless cloning process devoid of any restriction site problems. Plasmid integrity, insert orientation, and plasmid length were confirmed.

Restriction digests (in silico) were done using Nei (digestion alone) and NdeI/XhoI (double digestions) to ensure that the insert is excisable from the plasmid as well as its correct orientation. The fragment sizes were determined automatically by Benchling using NEB-compatible conditions.

In silico based immune simulation

To simulate the immune system response, the C-ImmSim web server was used [53]. The vaccine sequence was used as an antigen for the simulation. Some of the outputs generated in relation to antigen recognition and internal antigenic peptides presentation (MHC I/II), immune cells population, and cytokine profile include.

Molecular docking and interaction analysis

Docking studies were carried out for the assessment of the vaccine construct with the innate immune receptors of humans, TLR2 and TLR4. Protein docking was done using the ClusPro server [48].

Detailed residue-residue interaction analysis for the docked complexes was performed using LigPlot+ [49]. These were used to detect hydrogen bonds, polar interactions, and important residues contributing to receptor binding.

Molecular dynamics simulation

After molecular docking, the structural stability and dynamic behavior of the vaccine-TLR2 complex were assessed using molecular dynamics (MD) modelling. The top-ranked vaccine-TLR2 docked complex obtained from ClusPro was selected for simulation based on its favorable weighted docking score and stable residue-level interaction profile. The vaccine-TLR4 complex was not included in the MD simulation because the vaccine-TLR2 complex demonstrated comparatively stronger docking affinity and more consistent intermolecular interactions during docking analysis.

The protein parameterization was conducted using the ff14SB force field. In the process of system preparation, the protonation states for ionizable amino acids were defined under physiological conditions of pH. The explicit solvent TIP3P model was employed to simulate the structure in an orthorhombic periodic box with a buffer distance of 10 Å from the solute molecules. The LEaP module was applied to neutralize the system through the addition. To remove unwanted steric contacts and optimize the system geometry, initial energy minimization was conducted. The system was then equilibrated under constant pressure conditions at 1 atm while being gradually heated up from 0 K to 300 K under constant volume conditions. Subsequently, a production molecular dynamics simulation for 100 ns at 300 K under NPT ensemble was conducted. With the aid of the CPPTRAJ module of AMBER20, trajectory analysis, including root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), dynamic cross-correlation matrix (DCCM), and radius of gyration (Rg), was carried out. Binding free energy (BFE) calculation was performed by employing AMBER’s MMGBSA/MMPBSA modules [54–56].

Results

Selection of antigenic proteins for multi-epitope vaccine construction

Two important antigens of the Plasmodium vivax, namely Circumsporozoite Protein (CSP) and Thrombospondin-Related Adhesive Protein (TRAP), were selected as the epitope source to rationally develop a multi-epitope chimeric. The Circumsporozoite Protein (CSP) (UniProt ID: P08677; Gene: CSP; Taxonomy ID: 31273) is associated with sequence version SV = 2 and has been experimentally supported at the protein level (PE = 1). The second protein is identified as a fragment from Plasmodium vivax (Taxonomy ID: 5855) and is called Thrombospondin-Related Adhesive Protein (TRAP) (UniProt ID: Q968R4; PE = 4; SV = 1). Figure 1 shows the predicted three-dimensional structures of both proteins.

Predicted three-dimensional structures of selected Plasmodium vivax antigens used as epitope sources for vaccine design: (A) CSP and (B) TRAP

Antigenicity assessment using VaxiJen classified both selected proteins as probable antigens. Both proteins were evaluated as antigenic sources rather than independent vaccine targets. Combining epitopes from CSP and TRAP was intended to broaden immune coverage by targeting distinct sporozoite surface antigens involved in host invasion. The Circumsporozoite Protein (CSP) showed a 0.9905 score, which is high antigenicity, indicating promising immunogenic potential, while Thrombospondin Related Adhesive Protein (TRAP) achieved a VaxiJen score of 0.5169, also meeting the threshold for probable antigenicity. The predicted toxicity and allergenicity results indicate that both proteins are non-toxic and non-allergenic, meeting the basic safety requirements for selecting vaccine targets.

Transmembrane topology prediction (DEEPTMHMM) provided information regarding the possible membrane-binding sites for two proteins. CSP does not have any predicted transmembrane helices. Rather, the presence of a signal peptide in residues 1–22 was detected. This indicates an extracellular domain between residues 23–378, implying that the protein is largely exposed to the surface. In contrast, TRAP was predicted to possess only one transmembrane helix in residues 443–452. Most of the TRAP sequence was extracellular (residues 1-442), along with a minor cytoplasmic domain in residues 453–490. The extensive extracellular localization of both proteins supports their suitability for epitope mapping and rational vaccine design (Fig. 2).

Vaccine construct assembly

The developed multi-epitope construct was engineered with suitable linkers to maintain structural mobility, thus ensuring effective antigen processing and presentation. The KK linkers were used for linking the epitopes of B-cell so that there is a site for lysine cleavage, while MHC class I epitopes were linked with AAY linkers, allowing for easy proteasomal cleavage. GPGPG linkers were used between MHC class II epitopes to prevent the formation of junctional epitopes and the presentation of independent helper T-cell epitopes.

Human β-defensin peptide was utilized at the N-terminal of the designed vaccine as an effective molecular adjuvant through the EAAAK linker to ensure proper structure and dissociation from other epitopes. β-Defensin peptides are innate host defense peptides, which can serve as adjuvants due to their ability to improve antigen processing, recruit dendritic cells and T lymphocytes through chemotaxis, and stimulate cytokines. Being proteins and having no side effects, defensins may be considered effective adjuvants for multiepitope vaccines.

To facilitate recombinant expression and purification of the construct, a 6×His tag was added at the C-terminus using the same KK linker. The strategy involving the removal of a signal sequence was used when required (Fig. 3).

Analysis of potential coverage

Using the IEDB Population Coverage Tool, the predicted epitopes were evaluated for their ability to bind to the various alleles of different population groups. It can be seen that the predicted epitopes show very good worldwide coverage with a total population coverage of 98.8%. This clearly indicates that a major proportion of individuals in all regions is expected to have an immune response. On average, the number of epitope hits identified by individuals was 4.55, while 90% of the population was expected to identify at least 2.26 epitopes (Table S3; Fig. 4).

Using the IEDB Population Coverage tool, a global population coverage study of the anticipated epitopes revealed 98.8% combined coverage with an average of 4.55 epitope hits per individual and a PC90 value of 2.26

Population coverage was carried out with the help of both the set of predicted MHC Class I epitopes and MHC Class II epitopes, along with their corresponding HLA alleles (Supplementary Tables S1 & S2).

Immunological and biochemical profiling of vaccine construct

The finalized multi-epitope vaccine construct was predicted to be non-allergenic by AllerTOP and free of toxic regions by ToxinPred, indicating a favorable safety profile. Topology prediction of the fully assembled construct confirmed the absence of transmembrane helices. SignalP 6.0 analysis predicted the presence of an N-terminal signal peptide (Sec/SPI) with a prediction likelihood of 0.9998 and a predicted cleavage site between amino acid positions 22 and 23 (cleavage probability = 0.977827). All remaining residues (23–242) were predicted to be located within the non-membrane-associated region, supporting structural accessibility of incorporated epitopes and suitability for recombinant expression. All residues (1-242) were predicted to be in the non-membrane-associated region, supporting its suitability for cytoplasmic recombinant expression and ensuring structural accessibility of incorporated epitopes. This confirms that the construct is fully soluble and not membrane-anchored, supporting efficient recombinant cytoplasmic expression in E. coli.

Potential after an antigenicity assessment using VaxiJen produced a score of 0.7746. A projected scaled solubility value of 0.688 was found by Protein-Sol analysis, indicating favorable solubility under physiological circumstances (Fig. 5). The construct’s theoretical isoelectric point (pI) was determined to be 9.70, which is consistent with its fundamental nature and supports its compatibility with downstream vaccine formulation and recombinant production.

Predicted solubility of the vaccine construct (0.688) compared with the average solubility of E. coli proteins (0.45), indicating favorable solubility for recombinant expression

Physicochemical properties

The final vaccine construct comprised 242 amino acids with a calculated molecular weight of 25,702.12 Da and a theoretical isoelectric point(pI) of 9.70, indicating its basic character. The sequence contains 14 negatively charged residues (Asp + Glu) and 31 positively charged residues (Arg + Lys), demonstrating predominance of basic amino acids. The atomic composition was determined as C1118H1767N347O330S11 corresponding to a total of 3,573 atoms.

The extinction coefficient at 280 nm was estimated to be 19,410 M⁻¹cm⁻¹ (absorbance 0.755 at 0.1% concentration), assuming all cysteine residues form disulfide bonds and 18,910 M⁻¹cm⁻¹ (absorbance 0.736) under reduced cysteine conditions. The predicted half-life of the construct was 30 h in mammalian reticulocytes (in vitro), greater than 20 h in yeast (in vivo), and greater than 10 h in Escherichia coli (in vivo), suggesting favorable stability across multiple expression systems.

The instability index is 20.35, which classifies the protein as a stable one. The aliphatic index is 65.79, suggesting moderate thermostability and the negative GRAVY score (− 0.558) indicates a hydrophilic nature, which helps in ensuring good solubility in physiological conditions.

Secondary structure prediction

The prediction of the secondary structures using the PSIPRED web server demonstrated that there were heterogeneous yet well-balanced distributions of α-helices, β-strands, and coils throughout the entire length of the vaccine sequence. This suggested that there was an abundance of coils punctuated by occasional stretches of α-helices and β-strands. Extended stretches of coil are considered flexible regions and thus beneficial for the recognition of epitopes during antigen processing and presentation. Good spacing of α-helix stretches helps with overall structural stability, while the presence of β-strands indicates folding tendencies. It should be noted that most of the identified B-cell and T-cell epitopes were situated in the coil regions and thus structurally favorable for immunogenic recognition. There is no presence of abnormal clustering of structural rigidity regions, meaning proper folding potential was obtained.

3D structure prediction

The designed multi-epitope vaccine structure was modelled using AlphaFold 2, where a highly compact and well-organized structure was observed. After that, the structure was optimized. In the generated structure, various secondary structures such as alpha helix and beta sheet could be recognized along with the surface-exposed sites that matched the chosen epitopes. Such a structuring enables epitope accessibility, thus providing the basis for the docking analysis as seen in Fig. 6.

Structural validation

The stereochemical quality and reliability of the refined multi-epitope vaccine structure were evaluated using PROCHECK and ERRAT2 analyses. Ramachandran plot assessment predicted that 91.7% of residues were in most favored regions. On the other hand, 7.3% and 1.0% of residues were found in additionally and generously allowed regions, respectively. No residues were observed in disallowed regions, indicating acceptable backbone geometry and absence of severe conformational strain (Fig. 7).

Ramachandran plot of the predicted vaccine structure

Moreover, the ERRAT2 method was used to evaluate the global non-bonded interactions in the model. The optimized model has obtained an overall quality score of 85.71, which is within the acceptable range for theoretical protein models and indicates the acceptability of the structure of the vaccine. Most of the residues have shown errors lower than the rejection limit with just a few localized errors (Figure S4). Together with Ramachandran and ERRAT2 analysis, the results show that the optimized structure of the vaccine has an acceptable stereochemistry and is suitable for molecular docking studies.

Docking and interaction analysis

Residue-level interaction analysis of the best-ranked vaccine TLR2 complex

To gain insights into the molecular interactions stabilizing the docked complex, residue-level interaction analysis was performed for the best-ranked vaccine-TLR2 complex, selected based on the most favorable ClusPro weighted score. The analysis revealed that the vaccine construct forms multiple hydrogen bonds and polar interactions with residues located across the TLR2 ectodomain, indicating a potentially stable and well-distributed binding interface.

At the primary interaction interface, TLR2 residues Arg321(B), Tyr323(B), Tyr376(B), Asn379(B), Glu375(B) and Lys347(B) were observed to interact with vaccine residues Glu375(A), Glu407(A), Asn379(A), Ile319(A), Tyr323(A) and Arg321(A) through hydrogen bonds and polar contacts. These interactions suggest potential surface complementarity and contribute significantly to the stabilization of the vaccine-TLR2 complex (Fig. 10A).

Further interaction analysis involving additional TLR2 chains suggested consistent binding behavior. Residues Asn550(C), Gln554(C), and Asn61(C) of TLR2 formed hydrogen-bond interactions with Asn61(A), Ser40(A), and Asn550(A) of the vaccine construct, supporting robustness of the interaction across multiple receptor regions (Fig. 10B).

Codon optimization of vaccine construct for prokaryotic expression

Following reverse translation of the designed multi-epitope vaccine amino acid sequence, the corresponding nucleotide sequence was generated to enable downstream genetic manipulation. Reverse translation preserves the original reading frame and the amino acid integrity of the vaccine construct. The resulting nucleotide sequence was subsequently subjected to codon optimization. This enhances expression efficiency in the prokaryotic host system.

Codon optimization was performed specifically for Escherichia coli K-12, a widely used bacterial host for recombinant antigen production. Optimization focused on adapting codon usage to host-specific bias while maintaining complete amino acid fidelity with original vaccine design. Among the evaluated codon optimization platforms. The final optimized sequence was selected based on balanced GC content, absence of rare codons, elimination of premature stop codons, and preservation of translational efficiency parameters suitable for E. coli expression.

The finalized codon-optimized construct designated Vaccine_Optimized_EcoliK12 consisted of approximately 726 bp, excluding additional cloning-associated sequences, and exhibited GC content compatible with stable transcription and translation in Escherichia coli. No frame shifts or internal stop codons were detected following optimization. This indicates sequence integrity.

In silico based cloning of the optimized vaccine construct

To evaluate cloning feasibility and construct stability before experimental implementation, the codon-optimized vaccine sequence was subjected to in silico cloning. A pBR322-bHLAased expression backbone was selected due to a potentially stable replication origin, ampicillin resistance marker, and established use in recombinant expression studies.

In silico assembly was performed using the Gibson assembly strategy. Enabling homology-based, seamless integration of 726 bp vaccine insert into vector backbone without introduction of extraneous restriction sites or cloning scars. The insert and backbone regions were correctly defined, and assembly simulation suggested proper junction formation and correct insert orientation.

Successful assembly resulted in a recombinant circular plasmid of approximately 5.0 kb following insertion of the codon-optimized vaccine sequence into the pBR322 backbone. The backbone structural integrity was preserved, and no vector disruptions were observed. In silico immune simulation

The immune response elicited by the vaccine construct was evaluated using the C-ImmSim agent-based immune simulator. The simulation reproduced coordinated innate and adaptive immune activation over 35 days following antigen exposure.

Immune cell population dynamics

The simulation indicates activation and expansion of both humoral and cellular immune compartments following antigen administration.

B cell kinetics showed a rapid increase in total B cell counts early after antigen exposure, stabilization over the simulation period (Figure S6). State-based analysis revealed predominance of active B cells, with limited internalized, antigen-presenting (MHC-II), duplicating, and anergic populations (Figure S6). This indicates potential antigen recognition without excessive tolerance induction.

T helper (TH) cells exhibited pronounced expansion with total TH counts increasing sharply during the early phase and continuing to rise throughout the simulation (Figure S6). Memory TH subsets (TH Mem) accumulated progressively, suggesting the establishment of long-term immunological memory. State resolved TH analysis showed transition from resting to active and duplicating states consistent with antigen-driven clonal expansion (Figure S6).

Analysis of TH functional subsets shows a dominant TH1-type response, while TH2, TH17, and regulatory T-cell (TR) populations remained comparatively low (Figure S6). This polarization supports cell-mediated immune bias, which is desirable for vaccine-induced protection.

Cytotoxic T cells and innate immune activation

TC cells have a relatively higher number after exposure to antigens. While memory TC cells remained stable, the state-based approach showed that active and dividing TC cells were transiently increasing before gradually reverting to the resting stage.

In the innate immune system, there was a concerted response of NK cells, which showed dynamic variations within a physiologically stable range for the entire period of the simulation (Figure S7). MA cells showed an increasing number of internalized and antigen-presenting stages, implying effective antigen processing and presentation (Figure S7). The same trend was seen for DC cells, indicating their function as a link between innate and adaptive immunity (Figure S7). EP cells maintained their stability, showing no significant immune disturbance at the tissue level (Figure S7).

Antigen clearance and immunoglobulin response

The antigen kinetics plot revealed a characteristic vaccine-like profile with an initial increase in antigen load followed by progressive clearance over time (Figure S8, 1st panel). Immune complex formation coincided with antigen decline, indicating potential immune engagement.

While antibody titers (IgM, IgG subclasses) remained at low relative levels in this single dose. Simulation overall pattern supports initiation of humoral immune mechanisms and immune complex formation during antigen clearance.

Cytokine and interleukin signaling profile

The analysis of the cytokines revealed that there was enhanced immune signaling after the exposure to the antigen (Figure S8, 2nd panel). High concentrations of IFN-γ, IL-2 and TNF-α were detected. This reveals that there is activation of cell mediated immune responses. The presence of cytokines such as IL 12, IL18 further confirms the TH1 polarization, while other cytokines like IL 10 and TGF β are normally regulated indicating normal immune status.

As evident from the inset diagram, there was enhanced danger signal along with IL-2, implying immune activation.

MD simulation analysis

Vaccine TLR2 complex structural stability

A 100 ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulation was conducted to assess the docked vaccination TLR2 complex’s physiological stability. Initially, the root mean square deviation (RMSD) estimates of the protein backbone, protein-ligand binding pocket, and vaccine construct were used to quantitatively analyse structural changes.

The RMSD plot for the TLR2 receptor showed quick stabilization within the first 20–30 ns and thereafter maintained its deviation within the range of about 3.0-3.5 Å for the rest of the simulation, showing that the structure has preserved its protein fold (Fig. 12A). The protein-ligand binding pocket demonstrated low RMSD values below 1.2 Å during the simulation, indicating high local stability (Fig. 12A).

Vaccine complex structure stability during MD simulation. A RMSD plots of the TLR2 backbone, pocket residues, and vaccine complex during 100 ns of MD simulation show that the receptor and pocket structures are stable while the vaccine is flexible. B The vaccine-TLR2 complex’s structure, highlighting its dynamic behavior and stability

On the other hand, the vaccine complex exhibited higher RMSD values that increased gradually and stabilized around 7–8 Å. This is an indication of structural variation and not dissociation for a large and flexible protein ligand undergoing surface protein-protein interactions.

It can be observed from these RMSD profiles that the vaccine-TLR2 complex has structural potential stability during the simulation period.

The superimposition of the MD trajectories indicated that the receptor maintained its stability and the vaccine construct was adaptive (Fig. 12B).

Residue level flexibility analysis

The complete protein sequence was subjected to root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) analysis to examine local flexibility. The vaccine protein’s N-terminal and linker portions exhibit greater variations in the RMSF plot, but the majority of residues show oscillations less than 3 Å (see Fig. 13A). Significantly, residues present on the interaction site of the vaccine with TLR2 have lower RMSF values. This implies limited movement and sustained contact between the molecules.

Compactness and global structural integrity

The vaccination TLR2 complex compactness was evaluated during simulation, the radius of gyration (Rg) was computed. There is no consistent drift or expansion, and the Rg value varies only slightly between roughly 27.7 and 28.5 Å (Fig. 13B), indicating the absence of unfolding or aggregation events. These results support that complex retains compact and shows a potentially stable architecture throughout the simulation.

Solvent accessibility and interface stabilization

Solvent accessible surface area (SASA) analysis was conducted to examine surface exposure changes during complex formation. The SASA profile showed a gradual reduction from approximately 2350 Å² to 1900 Å² over simulation time (Fig. 13C).

This progressive decrease suggests partial burial of surface residues upon potentially stable complex formation and indicates tightening of vaccine TLR2 interface rather than global structural collapse. Minor fluctuation observed toward the end of the simulation reflects normal breathing motions of protein-protein complexes.

Dynamic cross-correlation analysis

To explore correlated motions, a complex dynamic cross-correlation matrix (DCCM) analysis was performed. The correlation map revealed distinct regions of positively correlated motions, particularly across interacting domains of receptor and vaccine construct, along with limited anti-correlated movements between distal regions (Fig. 14).

During molecular dynamics modelling, the large-scale collective movements of the vaccination TLR2 complex were characterized using principal component analysis (PCA). About 66% of the variance was explained by the first two principal components, with PC1 accounting for about 50% and PC2 for about 16% of the total motion (Fig. 15B).

Estimating binding free energy using MM-PBSA

The molecular mechanics Poisson-Boltzmann surface area (MM-PBSA) method was used to determine the binding energetics of the vaccination TLR2 complex. The analysis revealed favorable van der Waals (ΔE_vdW ≈ − 177 kcal/mol) and electrostatic gas phase contributions (ΔE_ele ≈ − 2229 kcal/mol) partially counterbalanced by polar solvation energy (ΔG_pol, sol ≈ + 2290 kcal/mol) (Fig. 16).

MM-PBSA energy components that contribute to free energy binding

The net binding free energy remained favorable with calculated total binding energies of approximately − 116 kcal/mol (PB model) and − 85 kcal/mol (GB model), indicating energetically potentially stable association. These results suggest that electrostatic interactions are the dominant contributors to complex stabilization.

Decomposition of per-residue energy

A per-residue free energy decomposition study was carried out in order to pinpoint important factors that contribute to binding stability. Multiple residues across the vaccine-TLR2 interaction interface contributed moderately to binding free energy, with individual contributions ranging approximately from − 1 to − 5 kcal/mol arising from both van der Waals and electrostatic interactions (Figure S9A, B). No single dominant hotspot residue was observed, indicating the absence of localized overstabilization (Figure S9B). This distributed interaction profile suggests a multivalent binding mode that is characteristic of biologically relevant protein-protein interactions and supports robust and potentially stable vaccine-receptor engagement.

Discussion

This study employed the use of an integrated immunoinformatic and structural bioinformatics approach in designing and analyzing a rationally engineered bivalent multi-epitope vaccine aimed at Plasmodium vivax. In this case, the vaccine was formulated based on the epitopes of two major pre-erythrocytic antigenic targets, the thrombospondin-related adhesive protein (TRAP) and the circumsporozoite protein (CSP). Both antigens were used in an effort to improve immune recognition by enhancing vaccine efficiency and minimizing dependency on one antigenic target since both function differently at the same developmental stages [57, 58].

CSP and TRAP were chosen since there is already sufficient biological knowledge about their function, availability, and utility in conducting epitope mapping analysis [59]. This is because CSP performs an important role in the binding of parasites to hepatocytes, while TRAP helps in the motility of the sporozoites through host tissues [60, 61], while TRAP facilitates the motility of sporozoites through host tissues [62, 63]. Since epitopes from both these antigens were used to build this construct, it ensures the induction of well-coordinated humoral and cellular immune responses against several functional antigens in the pre-erythrocytic stage of P. vivax life cycle, which is especially important because of the diversity of the antigens, relapse, and infections [64].

The final output of epitope screening was a balanced mix of epitopes with excellent antigenicity, low percentile rank, non-toxicity, and non-allergenicity for B-cell, MHC I, and MHC II epitopes, respectively [65]. Use of the three linkers -AAY, GPGPG, and KK- was aimed at improving epitope antigen processing, eliminating immunogenicity of junctions, and preserving epitope independence in the recombinant antigen design strategy. Finally, by using the principle of broad coverage of HLA-binding sites, an evident drawback in epitope-based malaria vaccine design [66], has been overcome, since previous designs of such malaria vaccines have shown restriction of their applicability for different populations. However, the use of epitopes with high population coverage (98.8% of the worldwide population), as in this study, is crucial considering the heterogeneous prevalence of P. vivax infection worldwide [67, 68].

Physicochemical characterization and prediction of solubility are key components in assessing the biochemical feasibility of vaccine constructs engineered by man. In the present study, in silico tools like ProtParam, Protein-Sol, SOLpro and similar web servers show desirable features like stability indices within acceptable limits, hydrophilic GRAVY score, and solubility values above acceptable limits, supporting that the engineered construct has a tendency towards stability, solubility and can thus be easily expressed and purified from commonly used hosts like E. coli. Such in silico predictions are indicative of the feasibility of the designed vaccine construct for recombinant expression [69]. Hydrophilicity, stability and solubility predicted for the construct indicate its suitability for expression and formulation [70]. Three-dimensional modelling of the intended vaccine construct using AlphaFold2 indicates a compact, effective structure with important epitope sequences. Likely to be found on the protein’s surface, increasing their availability to immune cells. Further refining of the model through various techniques like Galaxy Refine or optimization algorithms, optimized the backbone and side-chain structures. The quality of the model is evidenced by stereochemical validation tests (Ramachandran plot analysis indicates > 90% residues in favorable regions; MolProbity/Z score). These features make the designed vaccine biologically plausible, consistent with recent immunoinformatics vaccine designs [71, 72]. Even if computational structure prediction cannot be considered a substitute for experimental identification, these validation techniques offer critical computational assistance towards validating experiments.

Activation of the natural immune system has become one of the most prominent factors that influence the performance of vaccines, particularly in the case of epitope-based vaccines, when increased immunostimulatory features are absent [73]. In this respect, the present study included the application of molecular docking simulation to analyze the possibility of interaction between the vaccine design and the innate immune receptors, such as TLR-2 and TLR-4. Molecular docking results can be regarded as potentially stable interactions of the vaccine construct with the receptors in question [74]. These results reveal little difference in docking scores. Based on the literature available, TLR-4 receptor was traditionally known to interact with malaria parasites; however, the present finding of interactions between TLR2 and TLR4 supports the same and highlights the possible involvement of various pattern recognition receptors [75]. The better docking score obtained for TLR-2 is more likely to indicate structural complementarities within the docking [76]. This interpretation conforms with the existing concept among immunoinformatics researchers, according to which the docking approach should be viewed only as a prediction tool for the receptor interaction potential and should be followed by an experimental verification of the resulting downstream immune responses [77].

In the present work, β-defensins were chosen as molecular adjuvants due to their dual regulatory activity and excellent safety profile. In contrast to artificial TLR agonists that act as activators of one specific receptor pathway and lead to a pronounced inflammation response, β-defensins play the role of naturally occurring molecules and can recruit antigen-presenting cells, enhance cytokine production and increase humoral and cell-mediated immunity response. Several experimental works showed that recombinant β-defensins are highly potent in enhancing immune responses induced by vaccination against different infections, including viruses and mucosal infections. Therefore, they would be appropriate for incorporation into computationally designed multi-epitope vaccines [78, 79].

In vitro immune simulations also indicated that the designed construct is indeed immunogenic. Expansion of T helper cells, increased memory cell subpopulations, and presence of a dominant TH1 cytokine profile are indicative of immune reactions involved in the resistance to the liver stage of malaria parasites [80]. Cytokines like interferon-gamma and IL-2 are especially important in the inhibition of intracellular parasites [81]. While the humoral response is relatively weak in this single-dose experiment, this is not unusual in epitope-based vaccines and might require boosting to increase antibodies and class switching [82].

Codon optimization and in silico cloning investigations indicated the technical viability of recombinant expression in Escherichia coli from a translational perspective. An optimized sequence is characterized by a balanced GC content, lack of rarely used codons, and a stable plasmid structure [83, 84]. This guarantees effective transcription and translation in the prokaryotic host cell system. Although bacterially-based expression does not provide for post-translational modifications specific to eukaryotic expression, it is still frequently used for initial stages of antigen development and vaccine studies [85].

To analyze the stability of the vaccine-TLR2 complex under the conditions of molecular dynamics simulation, we studied its dynamics over 100 ns. The backbone of TLR2 and its binding pocket demonstrated potential structural stability, whereas the vaccine construct was more flexible but had converging dynamics, which is typical for interactions between receptors and proteins carrying multiple epitopes [86]. Potential stability of the complex based on the radius of gyration data and the solvent-accessible surface area gradually decreased, which also confirmed it [87].

The analysis of collective motion pointed to the approach to the stable conformational state associated with a single minimum of the free energy landscape. The binding free energy was estimated to be favorable, with contributions made by several interface residues and not a single hotspot [88, 89]. While such findings are purely theoretical and need further experimental verification, the MD simulations provide an insight into the feasibility of the proposed vaccine-TLR2 complex structure [90–92].

Various P. vivax vaccine candidates using CSP, TRAP, and DBP as antigens were already evaluated in preclinical and clinical phases, such as VMP001/AS01B, ChAd63-MVA PvTRAP, and vaccines formulated based on DBP. However, the vaccine candidates mainly considered single-antigen or full-antigen approaches and showed different levels of immunogenicity and partial protection. Contrary to previous studies, the current research used a multi-epitope immunoinformatics approach by incorporating selected B-cell, cytotoxic T-lymphocyte, and helper T-lymphocyte epitopes from CSP and TRAP. The rationale behind this methodology is to increase the coverage of targeted antigen epitopes while simultaneously limiting the use of unnecessary epitopes or those that might not elicit high immunogenic responses. Although promising in silico results were obtained in terms of antigenic properties, binding affinity with receptors, and immune simulation parameters, the vaccine candidate needs to undergo further experimental studies, particularly in vitro and in vivo experiments [93, 94].

In addition to the positive outcomes obtained by the study, there are also certain limitations. All the outcomes have been computationally predicted and need experimental confirmation. They give suggestions on which experiments to conduct, but to prove the immunogenicity, receptor binding, and effectiveness of the vaccines in protecting against infection, experiments need to be conducted [95]. Docking and immune simulation serve only as indirect proof of biological activity [77]. Further studies must include both in vitro and in vivo experiments.

While the prediction of stimulation of both cellular and humoral arms by the immune simulator supported the finding that both IgM and IgG levels were fairly low, part of this could be attributed to the simulation carried out for one dose of the vaccine in C-ImmSim. Considering that most vaccines developed against malaria, especially those targeting the pre-erythrocytic stage, require both humoral and cellular arms to mount an effective defense, the predicted biased TH1 response warrants a cautious interpretation. Multi-dose simulations are yet to be considered in this study; this could possibly improve the Ig levels in future work.

Conclusion

The study integrates an immune-informatics and structural bioinformatics framework for designing a bivalent multi-epitope vaccine candidate against Plasmodium vivax by integrating epitopes derived from “circumsporozoite protein” and thrombospondin-related adhesive protein. Construct shows favorable antigenicity, safety, physicochemical stability, and broad predicted population coverage. Findings are also supported by structural validation, receptor docking, and immune simulation analyses. Further evidence of the technological viability of recombinant expression comes from codon optimization and in silico cloning. When taken as a whole, these results provide a computational basis for further experimental testing of the suggested pre-erythrocytic vaccination candidate.
