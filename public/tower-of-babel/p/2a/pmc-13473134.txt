Assessment of microplastics on the coastal urban waterways in a developing country: a spectrometric study
Authors: Israt Zahan Oyshi, Md. Shamsuzzoha, Md. Rasheduzzaman, Md. Tariqul Islam, Md. Sazedul Hoque, Suprakash Chakma, Sanjida Hossain Setu, Md Kamal Hossain, Zulhilmi Ismail, Md. Saiful Islam, Abubakr M. Idris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microplastic pollution has become a significant environmental issue, especially in growing urban waterways of coastal regions. To investigate the sources, spatial distribution, ecological, and health effects of microplastics, water samples were collected from the coastal waterways that flow to the Bay of Bengal in Bangladesh. The concentration of microplastics was 181.11 ± 26.63 items/L. The size distribution was dominated by particles < 0.5 mm (70.34%), followed by 0.5–1 mm (21.85%). Transparent particles were most abundant (47.73%), followed by black (27.61%), while fibre-shaped particles were the dominant morphotypes (48.83%). Through Fourier Transform Infrared Spectrometer (FTIR) analysis, five polymer types were discovered in the water sample, including polyethylene, polyamide 6, polyamide 6,6, polyether-urethane, polyether-ester urethane, and alkyd resin. Principal component analysis (PCA) using six sites (S1-S6) showed a strong correlation between microplastic characteristics and sample sites. Water quality pollution index values indicated good to slightly polluted conditions at several locations. Redundancy analysis (RDA) based on 18 water samples (each sample with replicates) revealed a significant relationship between water quality parameters and microplastic characteristics (P < 0.05), except for abundance and size. This suggests that the microplastic distribution is strongly influenced by human activities in the study area. The Nemerow’s pollution index (NPI), polymer hazard index (PHI), and potential ecological risk (PERI)- all ecological risk indicators show minor, moderate, and high categories of pollution due to microplastic exposure, where Sagordi Khal falls into the high-risk category. According to survey data, the main causes of microplastic pollution were improper trash disposal and high use of plastic bags, while 41.15% of respondents were unaware of the problem. Overall, the observed environmental pollution is strongly driven by anthropogenic behaviors, highlighting the human-driven nature of microplastic pollution in the study area.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56280-4.

Introduction

Microplastics are plastic particles smaller than 5 millimeters. These particles have been detected in various locations globally, including on freshwater bodies like lakes, rivers, and the ocean as well1–3. The degradation and persistence rates of plastics are contingent upon the type of polymer, shape, density, environment, and intended purpose4–6. The classification of microplastics typically encompasses a range of shapes, including fragments, fibers, granules, pellets, and other irregular shapes7. Additionally, microplastics are synthetic or semi-synthetic substances that are produced from petrochemicals8. Moreover, because of their unique strength, quality, longevity, and flexibility, plastics are highly used in healthcare, manufacturing, beauty products, and the electrical industries9. Due to its pervasive distribution in the aquatic and terrestrial environment, it gives rise to grave threats to the environment and human health7,10,11.

Worldwide plastic use was approximately 460 Mt in 2019, which might increase to 1321 Mt in 206012. Recently, the presence of microplastics has been determined in the Yellow River (China), U-Taphao River (Thailand), Surabaya River (Indonesia), Manzur and Pulumur Rivers (Turkiye), Neuse River (USA), and many other rivers all around the world13–15. Microplastics have been reported to affect aquatic creatures through toxicological effects, tissue inflammation, and physical blockages16. In addition, microplastics in the food web can pose a significant hazard to human health17. It is even more alarming that microplastics are already found in human tissues, through the direct inhalation of contaminated air and ingestion of microplastic-contaminated foods18.

Uptake of microplastics exerts lethal and sub-lethal effects on aquatic life by causing false satiation, behavioral effects (abnormal swimming and lethargy), pathological stress, decreased predatory performance, reproductive complications, developmental inhibition, histological changes in the intestines, genetic damage, and liver metastasis19,20. Additionally, microplastics pose a potential human exposure route through dietary uptake21–25. They may accumulate in the gastrointestinal tract of humans, potentially causing inflammation, tissue damage, cancer, and altered gut microbiota18,26. As the extent of microplastics is ubiquitous from the air, we breathe to the food we eat, it is essential to know exactly the ecological and human health risks of microplastics. Moreover, an increase in mosquitoes carrying malaria has been observed in areas where plastic bags have been utilized as a means of blocking drains, as evidenced in Bangladesh27. However, it is a matter of concern that the assessment is still not done properly28.

These days, microplastics have become a severe environmental issue for coastal-marine ecosystems in Bangladesh due to their indiscriminate use and improper disposal practices in upstream rivers, which eventually find their way into the Bay of Bengal29–31. The usage and production of plastic-related debris have increased dramatically in just nine years (2005–2014)32–34. Additionally, the annual influx of microbeads into the water resources of Bangladesh is approximately 7928 billion8. Moreover, a high amount of microplastics was identified in the Cox’s Bazar Sea beach, St. Martin Island, Moheshkhali, the northern Bay of Bengal, and the Sundarbans mangrove estuarine zone35–39. This highlights the role of rivers in transporting microplastics downstream, underscoring the need to identify the sources and distribution of microplastic pollution in upstream rivers, particularly urban ones.

The Barishal City Corporation area is a place where microplastic pollution is a growing environmental problem. The complex network of rivers and canals along the Bay of Bengal makes it susceptible to microplastic pollution40,41. Additionally, the city is growing quickly, and has a lot of people living beside the waterways. Moreover, the daily generation of household solid waste in the city is approximately 140 tons, with a population of 400,000 or more42. Furthermore, the household waste and other sectors continue to degrade and are transported through runoff, sewage systems, and various human activities into the waterways. In recent times, the residents drink water and eat fish from the waterways, which worsens the situation. Only a few studies have addressed microplastic pollution in different rivers of Bangladesh. Specifically, no research has yet examined microplastic pollution in the waterways of the Barishal City Corporation area, Bangladesh. Therefore, the objectives of this study are to find out the spatial distribution of microplastics contaminated source locations in the urban waterways of the Barishal City Corporation area, Bangladesh; to assess their potential impacts in the study area; and to identify the sources of microplastics in the urban waterways of the Barishal City Corporation area. The novel aspect of this study lies in presenting the first integrated assessment of microplastic pollution in the coastal urban waterways of Bangladesh, combining spatial distribution analysis, FTIR-based polymer identification, and household surveys to link microplastic occurrence with local plastic usage patterns, sources, and impacts.

Methodology

Study area

The Barishal City Corporation area is the sixth most populous city in Bangladesh and lies between 22°38’ and 22°45’ north latitude and 90°18’ and 90°23’ east longitude (Fig. 1a)43,44. It lies along the banks of the Kirtonkhola River, which connects to the Bay of Bengal in south-central Bangladesh. The region has a tropical monsoon climate, with substantial seasonal rainfall that affects surface runoff, dilution, and the movement of pollutants. The river-canal system is also influenced by tides, which cause variable flow conditions and water exchange, especially during the dry season, when flow rates may be reduced. There are numerous canals in the study area, but only a handful remain hydrologically active year-round. Among these 5 canals were purposefully selected based on their hydrological activity, proximity to densely populated urban areas, and varying degrees of anthropogenic influence, such as drinking, fishing, and garbage disposal (Fig. S1). The sample locations include Sagardi Khal (S1), Chadmari Khal (S2), Jail Khal (S3), Nabogram Khal (S4), and Bhatar Khal (S5) (Fig. 1b). Untreated domestic sewage, solid waste disposal, and small-scale industrial effluents are the primary sources of pollution, and their relative contributions likely vary regionally across different land-use scenarios. In addition, one sampling site on the populated area of the Kirtonkhola River (S6) was included to capture the river’s condition as it receives flow from the urban canal network.

Sampling

Three samples were collected from each sample site, including Sagardi Khal (SK1, SK2, SK3), Jail Khal (JK1, JK2, JK3), Bhatar Khal (BK1, BK2, BK3), Chadmari Khal (CK1, CK2, and CK3), and Nabogram Khal (NK1, NK2, NK3), and Kirtankhola River (KR1, KR2, and KR3) (Fig. 1 (b)). Water samples were collected at a depth of about 10 cm below the surface using pre-washed glass containers, rinsed three times with sample water before filling. Samples are collected from October 5 to October 10, 2025. The sample jars were transported to the Food Processing Laboratory at the Department of Marine Fisheries, Faculty of Fisheries, Patuakhali Science and Technology University. All experiments were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations of the concerned authority of Patuakhali Science and Technology University, Bangladesh.

Laboratory analysis

After transportation to the lab, the samples were filtered using a 5 mm sieve (Fig. S1B). Three replicates were measured for each sample location. Working areas and utensils were cleaned before and after use. Laboratory coats and hand gloves were worn during the experiment. All experimental equipment, glassware, and metal tools were rinsed with filtered distilled water before use for each sample. The sample was measured and put into the beaker (Fig. S2). Then the sample water is treated with 25 mL of 30% H2O2 (Merck, Germany) and covered with foil paper for 24 h at room temperature45,46. After digestion, the stored water sample was filtered through a 0.45 μm filter paper under an air-free vacuum. Then the filter paper was carefully transferred to a Petri dish and used for further analysis. The whole laboratory process was executed in a non-ventilated, low-traffic room.

Quality assurance and contamination control

Working areas and utensils were cleaned before and after use. Laboratory coats and hand gloves were worn during the experiment. Beakers were kept covered with aluminum foil to minimize airborne contamination. All experimental equipment, glassware, and metal tools were rinsed three times with distilled water before use for each sample. The entire laboratory process was conducted in a non-ventilated, low-traffic room to minimize external contamination. Procedural blank controls were conducted using distilled water processed through all analytical steps to assess potential contamination.

Identification of microplastics

According to various studies, microplastics were identified visually by examining each filter paper under a SOLARIS-T-LED microscope at magnifications ranging from 4X to 40X (see Fig. S2)47,48. Although this methodology is quite time-consuming. To avoid contamination, the samples were handled using clean tweezers. The enumerated MPs were categorized and identified based on their shape, color, and size, and the images of each particle were captured. Based on size, microplastics were classified into four categories: 0.5 mm, 0.5–1 mm, 1–5 mm, and > 5 mm. In the present study, the colors of microplastics were classified visually into nine groups, such as transparent, black, red, yellow, green, blue, purple, orange, and pink. Furthermore, microplastics were divided into four shapes: filaments, irregular, angular, and spherical. Physical morphology was used to further categorize the subjects, resulting in their classification as fibers, filaments, and particles in accordance with current classification methodologies.

FTIR analysis

The Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Nicolet™ iS50 FTIR Spectrometer, Thermo Scientific™ USA), which uses infrared radiation to identify the polymer types of microplastic found in each water sample49. Every sample was previously examined visually using a magnifying microscope. In order to accurately position the minuscule plastic particles in the sample insertion area, any suspicious particles were carefully removed using stainless steel forceps and examined using an FTIR-iD7 ATR (attenuated total reflectance) spectrometer fitted with a high-resolution camera. With a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹ and 16 scans each measurement, the spectral range used to detect each microplastic (MP) was 4000–400 cm⁻¹. For each measurement, a total of sixteen co-scans were considered. To determine the polymeric makeup of each particle, its spectra were examined and contrasted with the polymer spectra in the OMNIC (Thermo Fisher) software library. If a particle’s spectra were more than 70% matched a polymer spectrum from the OMNIC collection, it was considered plastic. Samples that matched 60% to 70% were examined visually and contrasted with polymers that had already been found50. For each water sample, the total number of plastic particles and their polymeric composition were recorded.

Map-based analysis

Using the latitude and longitude of the sample collection sites, a study area map was created. Additionally, a buffer and an IDW map were created to show the most to least vulnerable areas of the study area according to the microplastic pollution.

Questionnaire survey

A semi-structured questionnaire survey was conducted with 175 respondents in the study area during September-October 2025. The questionnaire was focused on the sources of microplastics, the waste dumping system, and the impact of microplastics on the research area, which was gathered through in-depth interviews utilizing the Kobo Toolbox. Respondents were selected randomly from the study area.

Statistical analysis

The data were meticulously documented in a spreadsheet and were analyzed using Microsoft Excel, R 4.5.2, and IBM SPSS Statistics 26. The analysis employed a range of statistical techniques and visualization tools available in these programs. Descriptive analysis, including the mean and standard deviation was calculated. Additionally, analyses such as regression, PCA, and RDA were performed. Finally, various types of visual representations, including bar charts and pie charts, are prepared on the sources, sizes, polymer types, colors, and abundance of microplastics in the study area. Furthermore, a variety of cartographic representations are generated, encompassing the study area, the sample site, the IDW, and the buffer map, employing ArcGIS 10.8.2. Finally, FTIR data plotting was analyzed using Origin 2026 10.3. To ascertain whether there were statistically significant variations in the mean abundance of microplastics across the sampling stations, a one-way ANOVA (P < 0.05) was conducted.

Results

Qualitative properties of water samples

Table 2 shows the mean and standard deviation of physicochemical characteristics of water samples across six locations, including Sagordi Khal, Chadmari Khal, Jel Khal, Nobogram Khal, Bhatar Khal, and Kirtankhola River. The overall results show that the water quality in Jel Khal and Chadmari Khal was worse than that of other Khal’s. The temperatures were similar across locations and ranged from 27◦C to 31◦C, indicating uniform thermal conditions. The pH values ranged from 7.69 to 8.29, indicating the water samples were slightly alkaline. However, considerable variation was observed in total dissolved solids (TDS) and salinity, with mean values of 307.72 ± 122.42 ppm and 385.39 ± 151.61 ppm, respectively (Table 2). Among all the sample sites, Chadmari Khal showed the highest TDS up to 536 ppm and salinity up to 615 ppm, along with higher electrical conductivity values up to 1.02 ms. The results indicate higher mineral content and possible pollution, which may have resulted from reduced water flow in the Khal. In contrast, the Kirtankhola River shows the lowest TDS, salinity, and EC values, reflecting relatively clean and less saline water.

Abundance of microplastics

Microplastics were seen at all 6 sample sites (Fig. 2). The mean concentration of microplastics was 181.11 ± 26.63 items/L. Among all the locations, the Sagordi Khal has the highest concentration of microplastics with an average concentration of 75.11 ± 3.36 Items/L, while Nabogram Khal has the lowest with an average concentration of 49.78 ± 17.72 Items/L (Table S1).

Spatial distribution of microplastic concentration (Fig. 3a) also indicates that the microplastics are significantly distributed throughout the study area. The maximum concentration of microplastics was found at BK3 (Bhatar Khal) and SK1 (Sagordi Khal), where NK3 (Nabogram Khal) had the lowest amount of microplastics. Additionally, the geostatistical model “Inverse Distance Weighted (IDW)”, predicts microplastics concentration in the un-sampled areas by assigning higher values to nearby measured points, under the assumption that spatial influence diminishes with increasing distance (Fig. 3b). The interpolated surface demonstrates a clear spatial pattern, where elevated concentrations of microplastics are primarily concentrated in the southern and southeastern sectors, especially around sampling points Sagordi Khal, Chadmari Khal, BK3, JK1, KR1, and KR2, represented by yellow, orange, and red zones (Values between 38.90 and 64.25). Moderate levels occur within the central parts of the study area, while lower concentrations are observed toward the northern and northwestern zones of the study area, indicated by green to blue shades (values ranging from 22 to 38.90).

Characteristics of microplastics

Sizes of microplastics

In the present study, microplastics were classified into four groups, including < 0.5, 0.5–1 mm, 1–5 mm, and >5 mm (Fig. 4). Microscopic images revealed that particles smaller than 0.5 mm overwhelmingly dominated all sites, which is approximately 70.34% and particles greater than 5 mm were present in the lowest proportion, approximately 2.71% (Table S2). Additionally, the highest proportion of > 0.5 mm microplastics was observed at Bhatar Khal (79.86%), while the Kirtankhola River had the lowest (60%). By contrast, microplastic size in the 0.5–1 mm range represented a substantial fraction at Sagordi Khal (33.14%) and contributed 16–21% at other locations (Fig. 4a). Moreover, 1–5 mm-sized particles were generally less frequent, ranging from 1.44% at Bhatar Khal to 11.67% at Kirtonkhola River. However, particles larger than 5 mm were rare in all sites except in the Kirtonkhola River (7.50%). The photographs of the microplastic are presented in Fig. S3.

Color of microplastics

There is a considerable variation in color composition across six sample sites (Fig. 4b). Most of the microplastics found in the study area were transparent (47.73%), with purple (0.24%) and pink (0.98%) colors being the least (Table S3). In addition, the highest number of transparent was observed at Sagordi Khal (60.9%) and the lowest at Jel Khal (32.6%). Black microplastics were the second most prevalent at most sites, ranging from 14.2% at Sagordi Khal to 47.5% at Bhatar Khal. Other colors, including red, yellow, green, and blue, were present in smaller fractions, generally below 10% at most sites. Notably, Chadmari Khal exhibited a high portion of green microplastic (20.3%), and Jel Khal contained a relatively higher fraction of red particles (11.4%) (Fig. 4b).

Shapes of microplastics

Various shapes of microplastics were identified during analysis (Fig. 4c). Filaments (48.83%) and Irregular (43.93%) fragments were the most dominant kinds of microplastics (Table S4). At the Nabogram Khal, filament-shaped microplastics were approximately 59.8%. Additionally, irregularly shaped objects were also abundant at Bhatar Khal, exhibiting the highest proportion at 56.1%. Round and angular particles were relatively rare across all sites, collectively making up less than 15% of the total (Fig. 4c).

Morphotypes of microplastics

According to the results, Fibres (48.83%) (Table S5) were the most abundant type at most of the locations, with Nabogram Khal showing the highest proportion at 59.8%, while Bhatar Khal had the lowest at 37.4%. Besides, fragments were also prominent, particularly at Bhatar and Jel Khal, contributing 58.3% and 52.3% respectively of the total microplastics of each site (Fig. 4d). Conversely, particles (7.24%) were the smallest amount at all sites, ranging from 3.6% at Sagordi Khal to 15% at Kirtonkhola River.

Identified polymers of microplastics

By using FTIR, the chemical composition of the microplastic particles was measured. A total of 50 representative samples were analyzed by FTIR, where 45 samples showed clear polymeric spectral matches and were confirmed as microplastics (45,53 c). Five major polymer types were detected among confirmed microplastics, including Polyethylene, Polyamide 6, Polyamide 6,6, Polyether-urethane, Polyether-ester urethane, and Alkyd Resin, based on characteristic FTIR absorption peaks (Table S6). The transmittance vs. wavenumber graph of the particle’s chemical bonds is presented in Fig. 5. Among all the sample sites, Sagordi Khal exhibited mixed polymer composition, including Polyamide 6, Polyamide 6,6 (33.33%), polyether urethane (20%), alkyd resin (26.67%), and polyether–ester urethane (20%) (Fig. S4). Bhatar Khal (S5) contained both alkyd resin (40%) and polyethylene (60%), while the remaining sites were dominated by only alkyd resin. These percentages were calculated based on the number of FTIR-confirmed microplastic particles from each sampling site.

Principal component analysis

This PCA is organized into several groups that describe the relationship between microplastic characteristics (shape, color, and size) and sampling sites (Fig. 6). The PCA result explained 60.11% of the total variance (PCA1: 40.48% and PCA2: 19.63%) (Fig. 6). Along PC1, variables such as fragment, irregular shape, black, orange, pink, purple and angular show strong positive correlation in Sample 3 (Jel Khal) and Sample 5 (Bhatar Khal). Conversely, PC1 shows negative loadings for fiber, filament, transparent, and round particles, which are primarily associated with Sites 1 (Sagordi Khal) and 4 (Nabogram Khal). Along PC2, the vector for green color shows a strong positive loading, influencing Site 2 (Chadmari Khal), whereas yellow, red, and blue colors and 0.5-1 sizes have negative loadings, relating more closely to Site (Sagordi Khal). The length of the arrows represents the strength of each variable’s contribution to the principal components.

Relationship between microplastics and water quality parameters

The Redundancy analysis explains the relationship of microplastics related to water quality in the study area. This analysis used five variables as physicochemical parameters (Temperature, pH, DO, EC, TD, and saline), abundance, size, color, shape, and morphotype of microplastics. Findings show that water quality is correlated with MP shape, morphotype, and color (P-value < 0.05). In contrast, the water quality did not affect the size of microplastics at the experimental site (P-value > 0.05). Higher MP abundance is associated with increased salinity, conductivity, and pH levels, such as the BK3, SK2, and CK2 cluster, influenced by these parameters, reflecting higher microplastic abundance (Fig. 7a). In addition, parameters like salinity, TDS, and EC are associated with darker or more pigmented microplastics, such as orange, purple, and black, where pH influenced colors like red and pink, suggesting that alkaline conditions may enhance the occurrence of these color types (Fig. 7b). Spatially, sites such as JK4 and KR2 are more influenced by pH and salinity, while CK2, BK2, and NK3 show weaker associations with water quality parameters. Moreover, round and angular microplastics cluster near the plot center, indicating weaker associations with the measured water quality variables (Fig. 7c). Moreover, sites like CK3, BK3, and JK2 cluster near pH and salinity vectors and mainly feature fragments, while sites such as CK1, NK3, and KR1, positioned near the temperature vector, show a higher presence of fibers and particles, implying that these are more common in warmer, less saline conditions (Fig. 7d). In contrast, temperature is negatively correlated with other parameters and is associated with colors like transparent, green, blue, yellow, as well as fibers and particle-shaped or filament-morphotyped microplastics.

Ordinal logistic regression analysis of respondents’ perception of the impact of microplastics

To understand which factors, influence how people perceive the impacts of microplastics, an ordinal logistic regression was performed (Table 3). The analysis considered three variables based on the study area, such as knowledge of microplastics, understanding of laundry wastewater, and their perception of the plastics blocking canals. Respondents who reported limited understanding (Estimate = -1.349, p = 0.009) or moderate experiences (Estimate = -1.548, p = 0.008) tended to underestimate the severity of microplastic impacts (Table 3). Likewise, perception regarding laundry wastewater showed a significant influence. Respondents who only partly believed that laundry wastewater contributes to microplastic pollution (Estimate = -0.926, p = 0.010) were less likely to see microplastic impacts as severe compared to those who fully acknowledged it as a contributing factor (Table 3). On the other hand, the perception that plastics block drainage canals did not show a significant relation (p = 0.897). Overall, the model showed a significant improvement over the intercept-only model (χ² = 19.654, df = 6, p = 0.003), with the predictors explaining approximately 6–13% of the variance in perception levels based on pseudo-R-square measures, indicating that awareness-related factors contributed meaningfully to explaining differences in perceived microplastic impacts (Tables S8, S9, and S10).

Discussion

This study aimed to comprehend microplastic contamination in the coastal urban waterways by investigating their characteristics, spatial distribution, sources, and impact. Microplastic concentrations varied among sample sites, with higher levels observed in canals compared to the Kirtankhola River. This widespread occurrence highlights a strong influence of urbanization and human activities and suggests that urban canals act as major accumulation zones, where limited water flow and continuous waste input promote retention of microplastics, whereas the river system facilitates transport and dilution54–59. Similar trends have been reported in other urban environments, such as Birmingham and Jakarta, where drainage systems function as significant pathways transferring microplastics to larger river systems and ultimately to marine ecosystems60,61.

Microplastics of varying sizes, colors, shapes, and morphotypes were identified across all the sampling sites, reflecting diverse sources and environmental processes. Smaller particles (< 0.5 mm) dominated all sites, particularly at Bhatar Khal (79.86%), indicating long-term fragmentation and prolonged environmental exposure due to continuous plastics input and limited water movement62. In contrast, the presence of relatively larger particles in the Kirtonkhola river suggests active transport and ongoing degradation under dynamic flow conditions45,63–65. In terms of color, transparent and black microplastics together accounted for more than 70% of the total, suggesting major contributions from packaging materials, disposal plastics, tire wear, and open burning of plastics3,66,67. Localized variations, such as higher green particles in Chadmari Khal and red particles in Jel Khal, indicate site-specific activities, such as fishing and domestic waste disposal68. Other colors were present in comparatively lower percentages, consistent with findings from similar studies69. Morphologically, irregular fragments and filaments/fibers were dominant, indicating that most particles are secondary microplastics derived from the breakdown of larger debris and synthetic textiles56,70. The lower portion of angular, around, and particle-shaped microplastics further suggests minimal contribution from primary industrial sources, such as microbeads. The relationship between microplastics characteristics and water quality parameters suggests that environmental conditions influence particle distribution and behaviour. Significant correlations between parameters such as TDS, pH, EC, temperature, and salinity with microplastics characteristics (shape, morphotype, and color) indicate that physicochemical conditions can affect transport, degradation, coloring, and aggregation processes71. However, the absence of a significant relationship with particle size suggests that fragmentation is influenced more by long-term physical and chemical processes than by immediate water quality conditions, consistent with findings from similar river systems in the Tambakoso River in Surabaya, Indonesia72.

Polymer identification using FTIR spectroscopy revealed the presence of polyethylene, polyamide 6, 6, polyether urethane, polyether–ester urethane, and alkyd rasin. These polymers are commonly associated with plastic bags, beverage bottles, ropes, fishing ropes, clothing, paints, coatings, and packaging materials73–79. The variation in polymer composition across sampling sites reflects localized anthropogenic inputs and land-use patterns, reinforcing the link between human activities and microplastic pollution sources. Moreover, survey results indicate widespread use of plastic materials, particularly plastic bags, alongside improper disposal practices, including dumping waste into open areas and canals. Such activities facilitate the direct entry of plastic waste into aquatic systems, where it undergoes fragmentation and contributes to long-term microplastic accumulation. During the rainy season, this plastic debris is transported from canals to the Kirtonkhola River, indicating that the urban drainage network functions as a continuous pathway for microplastic transfer to downstream environments. Similar findings have been reported in other urban regions where untreated wastewater and runoff are the major contributors to freshwater microplastic contamination80.

Pollution assessment indices, including Nemerow’s Pollution Index (NPI), Potential Ecological Risk Index (PERI), and Pollution Hazard Index (PHI), indicate moderate to high pollution levels across the study sites, with Sagordi Khal classified as a high-risk hotspot. This clearly indicates continuous waste input, high population density, and limited water exchange. Such conditions enhance the potential for bioaccumulation and trophic transfer of microplastics, particularly in fish populations, which are widely consumed by the local communities81–85. Consequently, this rises concern regarding food safety and ecosystem health in the study area, where a large portion of the population depends on locally sourced fish86–88. Furthermore, spatial analysis using IDW interpolation and buffer analysis reinforces these findings by visually identifying and representing commercial and densely populated areas, particularly Sagordi Khal and Chadmari Khal, as microplastic hotspots. These results represent zones of increased human exposure risk, where residents are more likely to encounter microplastics through daily activities and consumption of contaminated aquatic resources. Such insights are valuable for identifying priority areas for targeted mitigation and management strategies89–91.

Overall, these findings demonstrate that microplastic pollution in the study area is driven by a combination of urbanization, inadequate waste management, and hydrodynamic conditions. Each site reflects distinct local pressure, including Sagordi Khal, which represents the stress of urban trade and household waste; Chadmari Khal depicts industrial waste, fishing activities; Bhatar Khal collects household waste in its non-movable water flow; Jel Khal collects household waste and hampers the fishing facilities; Nabogram Khal also collects household waste in a water body, which is filled with water hyacinth. Addressing these issues requires integrated strategies, including improved waste management infrastructure, increased public awareness, enforcement of environmental regulations, and promotion of sustainable alternatives such as jute bags92–95. Without such interventions, these urban waterways will continue to act as conduits transporting microplastics to larger aquatic systems and ultimately to the Bay of Bengal.

Conclusions

This study examined the abundance and spatial distribution of microplastics across six sampling sites, as well as their associated health and ecological risks. Water quality analysis indicated that all sites were in good condition. The most common microplastic types were fibers, mostly < 0.5 mm in size, predominantly transparent and black, and mainly composed of alkyd resin and polyethylene. Principal component analysis revealed a correlation between microplastic characteristics and sampling sites, while redundancy analysis showed that water quality parameters influenced most microplastic features, except for concentration and size. Moreover, the pollution indices (NPI, PERI, and PHI) indicated moderate to high levels of microplastic pollution with site-specific ecological and health risks, particularly concentrated at Sagordi Khal (site 1) compared to other sampling sites. To better understand the potential drivers behind these observed patterns, questionnaire survey data were integrated, and several household and industrial activities were identified, including plastic bag use, improper waste disposal in canals and open areas, and limited awareness.

Overall, the results indicate that residents, along with commercial and industrial activities near waterways, significantly contribute to increased microplastic pollution. To mitigate this, municipal authorities should strengthen regular canal waste collection and drainage maintenance. Enforcement of single-use plastic regulations should be reinforced through market monitoring and regulatory compliance measures. Furthermore, community-level awareness programs through local institutions and media should promote waste disposal practices and reduce household plastic consumption. These measures are essential to reduce the transport of microplastics from canals and rivers into the Bay of Bengal, thereby improving environmental sustainability. Therefore, by combining spatial, analytical, and socioeconomic approaches, this work bridges a significant research gap in coastal urban waterways pollution in Bangladesh and improves understanding of the sources and pathways of microplastics in urban aquatic systems.

Limitations

Due to limitations in laboratory equipment, resource availability, and analytical procedures, this study solely uses established methodologies to measure microplastics in surface water. More ground needs to be covered, which will surely give a more accurate view of the source and extent. Furthermore, only one season was used for sampling, making it impossible to account for seasonal variations in the concentration of microplastics. Due to their small sizes, the scientists also had trouble using FTIR to identify every polymer type of microplastic. Additionally, this study’s limited sample size is a significant drawback for a more comprehensive approach. This study recommends future research on human and eco-toxicological effect studies, improved source identification, and more cooperation between scholars, decision-makers, and the general public, which would promote sustainable waste management, according to this study.
