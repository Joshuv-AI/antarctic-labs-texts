Nutritional Status and Fecal Microbiota in School Children from the Galapagos and the Andean Region
Authors: Paul Cárdenas, Valeria Carpio-Arias, Melanie Chávez, Andrea Denisse Benítez, Alexis D Baldeón, Andrés Suárez-Jaramillo, Marco Fornasini, Julieta Robles, Gabriela Loza, Manuel E Baldeón
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSchoolchildren from the Galapagos and the Andean region present the worst indices of malnutrition in Ecuador and are exposed to distinctive food and water insecurity. We compared the nutritional status, the fecal microbiota composition of schoolchildren from the Galapagos (n = 51; 8.88 ± 2.15 years) and the Andean region (n = 114; 8.69 ± 1.83 years). Children had a nutritional evaluation and provided fecal samples for microbiota analysis by 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Excess weight was more prevalent in Galapagos (41.18%) than in the Andes (24.5%). Additionally, intestinal parasitosis was more prevalent in children from the Andes (76.4%) than in Galapagos (13.0%). Species richness was lower in fecal samples of children from the Galapagos than those from the Andes (Chao1 index p = 0.001). Beta-diversity metrics also showed significant differences between these samples. Bacteroidota and Proteobacteria were enriched in the microbiota of Galapagos children, whereas Firmicutes A and Cyanobacteria were enriched in the Andean children. At the genus level, the top 3 genera present in schoolchildren from the Galapagos were Bacteroides, Phocaeicola, and Escherichia, while in children from the Andes were Cryptobacteroides, Prevotella, and Clostridium. Cyanobacteria were inversely associated with BMI z-score in the Galapagos region (q = 0.009), while, Firmicutes D had a direct relationship with BMI z-score in children from the Andes (q = 0.05). At the genus level, only Butyrivibrio was inversely associated with BMI z-score in children of the Galapagos (q = 0.04). We conclude that schoolchildren with different degrees of malnutrition from two distinct geographical areas have dissimilar fecal microbiota characteristics. Introduction

Malnutrition in the pediatric population is a public health problem in Ecuador. It is characterized by undernutrition in children under 5 years of age and overnutrition in school-age children [1]. In Ecuador, 23% of children under 5 and 27.2% of children under 2 years of age had undernourishment [1]. In comparison, approximately 35% of school children had excess weight (overweight and obesity) [1]. The provinces of Chimborazo, in the Andean region, and Galapagos have the highest rates of malnutrition in the country, with overweight and obesity problems being the most serious ones. In the Galapagos, 46.3% of school children have overweight and obesity, which is greater than the prevalence in Chimborazo 28.2% and higher than the 35.3% prevalence at the national level [1]. It is important to study the social and biological determinants of malnutrition, including the composition and function of the intestinal microbiota, in different geographic regions around the world including Ecuador.

The Galapagos are the insular province of Ecuador, home to approximately 30,000 inhabitants. The population of Galapagos has tripled in the last 30 years, primarily due to the migration of Ecuadorians from the mainland [2]. Due to its status as a World Heritage Site, Galapagos is also a popular tourist destination with approximately 300,000 visitors annually [3]. The growing number of residents and tourists in the Galapagos Islands puts increasing pressure on the availability of clean water and food aggravating the food insecurity of its inhabitants [4]. Local food production in the islands is determined by limited agricultural surface for farming, the frequent presence of droughts, limited irrigation, and the restricted use of land and agrochemicals [5]. Additionally, transportation of food from the mainland to the islands is variable and costly, further affecting the availability of food and favoring the consumption of processed foods [5]. Also, the quality of water for human consumption in Galapagos is poor [4]. These characteristics in Galapagos could explain the high prevalence of malnutrition in the islands [6, 7]. Food insecurity is also common in the Andean region, and it has been associated with greater consumption of processed food and with higher levels of non-communicable chronic diseases (NCCD) [8]. A study in this region showed that 25% of underage children do not have an adequate diet [9]. In that study, the economic stress, rather than food availability, was the main concern for the families. Also, in parts of the Ecuadorian Andes, the access to clean water and sewage is limited [10]. The food insecurity that societies may suffer influences their biology and consequently their health and nutritional status.

The gut microbiota has essential physiological functions including metabolic homeostasis, immune regulation, and psychological well-being [11]. Diet (including water intake) is a significant determinant of gut microbiota composition and function [12]. Common pathological conditions such as obesity, metabolic syndrome, hypertension, and cancer have been associated with the consumption of unhealthy diets, rich in high in calories, with low fiber, low saturated fatty acids, and high in added refined sugars [13]. There is limited information on the nutritional status and structural and functional characteristics of the fecal microbiota in schoolchildren in Ecuador. Considering the high prevalence of malnutrition in schoolchildren from the Galapagos and the Andean region, this study compared the nutritional status and the composition of their fecal microbiota in students from the Santa Cruz Island, in Galapagos, and in Penipe, Chimborazo, in the Andean region of Ecuador.

Materials and Methods

Participants and Study Design

This cross-sectional study included school children from middle-low or low socioeconomic strata from Galápagos (n = 51) and from the Andean region (n = 114). We included children between 6 and 12 years of age of any sex who were attending the school regularly for at least 3 months before the beginning of the study and whose guardians signed an informed consent. We excluded children if they had signs of infection and were receiving micronutrient supplementations, pre- or probiotics, or had been on antibiotics within 30 days prior to the beginning of the study. Children with congenital diseases, mal-absorption syndromes, cancer, or HIV infections were also omitted.

Anthropometric Measurements

Trained personnel measured weight and height using standardized techniques. Weight was measured with an electronic scale SECA 803 (Hammer Steindamm 3–25 22,089, Hamburg, Germany); and for height a SECA 213 stadiometer was mounted vertically on a wall above a hard, flat surface following vendor instruction (Hammer Steindamm 3–25 22,089, Hamburg, Germany); height was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm. Anthropometric measurements were carried out with the children wearing minimal clothing and without shoes. The body mass index (BMI) z-score was used as an indicator of nutritional status. The BMI z-score is determined dividing the weight in kilograms by the square of height in meters; the result is then plotted on a sex- and age-specific chart to determine a BMI-for-age percentile for each child. The weight, height, and BMI-for-age and sex growth charts from Anthro Plus WHO were used for data analysis [14, 15]. The following indicators were considered to determine the nutritional status considering the BMI/age z-scores: underweight < − 2 SD); normal weight ≥ −2/≤ 1 SD; overweight > 1/≤ 2 SD; and obesity > 2 SD [14].

Blood Sample Collection and Glucose and Lipid Profile Measurements

Blood samples were collected under basal conditions in the morning after approximately 10 h of fasting. Samples were centrifuged within 2 h and processed immediately [15]. Serum total cholesterol, triglycerides, and fasting glucose were measured using an Automatic Biochemistry Analyzer PKL PPC 125 using standard reagents (Pontecagnano Faiano, (SA) Italia). All assays were performed using internal and external controls.

Fecal Sample Collection

The legal guardians of the participating children were instructed to collect stool samples (approximately 3 g) at home in sterile plastic containers and store them at 4 °C until delivery to the laboratory. In general, samples were stored for less than 4 h before reaching the laboratory. Upon arrival, stool samples were stored at −80 °C until DNA extraction [16].

Copro-parasitic Analysis

Stool sample from each participant was filtered and concentrated for 5 min at 2500 rpm using Parasep® Midi filter devices (Apacor Ltd., Wokingham, UK). Drops of 10% formalin were added to the sediment in a 1:3 ratio for microscopic analysis. All stool samples were observed microscopically at 10 × and 40 × magnifications to detect structures such as eggs, cysts, oocysts, trophozoites, and larvae.

Microbiota Analysis

The fecal microbiota analysis was done on a subsample of participants children, 25 from Galapagos and 55 from the Andean region. The selection of the subsamples was obtained by simple random sampling.

DNA was extracted from 180 to 220 mg fecal material using the QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Elution was performed twice with a final volume of 70 ml, and DNA was stored at 4 °C until further use. Prior to library preparation, an additional purification step using magnetic beads was performed prior to library preparation to remove impurities such as bile salts. DNA concentration and purity were evaluated using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

The full-length 16S rRNA gene was amplified and prepared for sequencing using the 16S Barcoding Kit 24 V14 (SQK-16S114.24) from Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT, UK) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The PCR amplification conditions were those specified by the ONT protocol. Reactions were run on a MiniAmp Plus Thermal Cycler (Applied Biosystems, USA). Sequencing libraries were loaded onto a FLO-MIN114 flow cell and run on a GridION device. Sequencing was performed using MinKNOW software version v24.02.16, with super-accuracy base calling, automatic demultiplexing (barcoding), and adapter removal enabled.

The analysis of 16S rRNA gene sequences was conducted using the MetONTIIME meta-barcoding pipeline [17]. Reads were processed using VSEARCH, applying the novo clustering to generate Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs). Taxonomic assignment was performed against the Greengenes2 database version 2022.10. Singleton OTUs was removed prior to downstream analysis. Subsequent abundance calculations and microbial diversity analyses were carried out using MicrobiomeAnalyst [18]. Alpha-diversity calculations (the number of bacterial species per sample) were estimated by the Shannon diversity index (community diversity based on both richness and evenness), the Chao1 index (a quantitative estimator of community richness) and observed OTUs (the number of distinct taxa per sample). The normality of alpha-diversity data was assessed using a Python script (Shapiro–Wilk test), confirming that none of the metrics followed a normal distribution. Consequently, non-parametric statistical tests were applied: the Mann–Whitney U test for two-group comparisons and the Kruskal–Wallis test for multi-group comparisons. In addition to assessing beta-diversity among groups of samples (differences in the complete microbiota patterns), the following metrics were calculated: Jaccard metric distance (qualitative community dissimilarity), Bray–Curtis distance (quantitative community dissimilarity), and Jensen-Shannon divergence (similarity between probability distributions of communities). Comparisons between samples from the Galapagos and the Andean region were done using non-multidimensional scaling (NMDS) based on Bray–Curtis distances as the ordination method.

Differential abundance analyses at the taxonomic level were performed in R v. 4.4.1. Taxa present in > 25% of the cohort with a mean relative abundance of at least 0.1% were retained for analyses. As suggested by previous comparative studies, more than one method was employed for these analyses to enhance the robustness of the findings [19]. Specifically, linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) [20] and analysis of composition of microbiomes with bias correction 2 (ANCOM-BC2) [21] were employed using the microbiomeMarker [22] v 1.10.0 and ANCOMBC 2.6.0 packages, respectively. LEfSe evaluated the effect of a single categorical variable, while ANCOM-BC2 models were adjusted by age, sex, and BMI z-score. Additionally, p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons in ANCOM-BC2 using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Both tools were run on their default settings. Significance was set at a linear discriminant analysis score of 2 (LEfSe) and an alpha of 0.05 (ANCOM-BC2).

Prediction of Functional Pathways in Fecal Data

Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States (PICRUSt2) v 2.6.0 was used to infer the functional profile based on 16S rRNA sequences [23]. Orthologs of the Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KO), enzymes (EC), and MetaCyc pathway abundances were predicted. Due to computational limitations, the analyses were performed separately for different data batches and later merged into a table for KO, EC, and pathway. Differential expression was analyzed with LEfSe [20]. The threshold on the linear discriminant score (LDA) for discriminative pathways was set to ≥ 2.0 [24].

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics with their respective measures of dispersion were calculated for continuous variables; frequencies and percentages were calculated for categorical variables. Differences between groups were assessed using chi-square tests. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Microbiota Analysis

After the microbiota analysis of the 16S rRNA gene and removal of singleton, 1630 taxonomic groups were identified. An average of 64,230 sequences per sample were analyzed. The sequencing experiment produced a range of approximately 30 to 148,890 reads per sample. Due to computational memory limitations, the analyses were performed separately for different data batches, and the results were later collapsed at the taxonomic level. Therefore, the total number of OTUs could not be directly calculated across all samples; instead, the number of taxonomic groups after collapsing at the species level was used for reporting.

Comparison of the community composition of the microbiota showed that children from Galapagos presented lower species richness than children from the Andean region (Chao1 index p = 0.001), Fig. 1A, while the differences in the Shannon index were not statistically significant p = 0.1158. Considering the nutritional status within each province, we observed that species richness was greater in children with normal BMI values than in children with excess weight, although those differences were not statistically significant, Fig. 1B. However, species richness was statistically greater in fecal samples from children with normal BMI values from the Andean region than from Galapagos (Chao1 index p < 0.003); similarly, species richness was statistically greater in fecal samples from children with excess weight from the Andean Region than in children from Galapagos (Chao1 index p < 0.007), Fig. 1B.

Predicted Fecal Microbiota Functional Profiles in School Children from GaláPagos and the Andean Region

When the samples from the Galapagos and the Andean region were compared, considering a linear discriminant score (LDA) ≥ 2.0, we found a greater presence of 43 orthologs, supplementary Table 2S, 7 enzymes Tables 3S, and one pathway in samples from the Andean Region. These identified orthologs, categorized according to the Brite categories of Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes, included enzymes, exosome, peptidases and peptidase inhibitors, membrane trafficking peptidoglycan biosynthesis and degradation proteins, and lipid biosynthesis proteins. The enzymes were primarily involved in nucleotide metabolism, peptidoglycan metabolism and signals transduction pathways. The metabolic pathway was associated with peptidoglycan maturation (meso-diaminopimelate containing).

Discussion

The results demonstrated that overweight and obesity were more prevalent in schoolchildren in the Galapagos than in the Andes. Fecal microbiota analysis showed that species richness was lower in fecal samples of children from the Galapagos than those from the Andes. Beta-diversity was significantly different between children with normal weight and between children with excess weight from both locations. At the phylum level, Bacteroidota and Proteobacteria were enriched in the microbiota of Galapagos children, whereas Firmicutes A and Cyanobacteria were enriched in the Andean children. At the genus level, the top 3 genera present in schoolchildren from Galapagos were Bacteroides, Phocaeicola, and Escherichia. In contrast, in children from the Andes, the genera were Cryptobacteroides, Prevotella, and Clostridium. Considering the nutritional status, only Butyrivibrio (A-180067) was inversely associated with BMI z-score in children of the Galapagos. Functional predictions on fecal microbiota in the children from the Andes microbiota determined a higher presence of peptidoglycan synthesis pathways and nucleotide metabolism.

Food and water insecurity influence the structure and function of gut microbiota contributing to malnutrition in children [25, 26]. We compared two populations with different degrees of malnutrition and with varying conditions of food and water insecurity. The prevalence of excess weight in the Galapagos and the Andean region was similar to that reported in a national study [1]. Excess weight in schoolchildren from Galapagos was approximately 17% greater than in children from the Andean region and 6% greater than the reported for schoolchildren in Ecuador. Previous work indicates that the consumption of processed and ultra-processed foods by the residents of the Galapagos is high while consumption of unprocessed or minimally processed food is limited [7]. Ultra-processed foods usually contain colorants, emulsifiers, added sugars, and trans-fatty acids, which have been linked to obesity and non-communicable chronic diseases [27]. Another contributing factor for malnutrition in Galapagos is the poor water quality available to its population. A study that included children and adult populations showed that the joint exposure to inadequate water and food insecurity is associated with malnutrition and infections in the Galapagos [4]. Also, it has been reported that the fish consumed in the islands contain toxic levels of cadmium [28] which can lead to compositional differences of the intestinal microbiota [29].

Food insecurity in the Andean region is also common and it has been associated with greater consumption of processed food [8]. According to the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, 36.9% of Ecuadorians experienced moderate or severe food insecurity in 2024 [30]. A study involving 173 families in the Ecuadorian Andes, which assessed food security and access to basic services, found that 25% of underage children did not have an adequate diet [9]. In that study, the economic stress, rather than food availability, was the primary concern for the families. Additionally, the communities in the Andes, where the present study was conducted, lack access to clean drinking water and sewage, and the majority of people have access to piped water and septic tanks [10]. This situation could explain the high prevalence of intestinal parasitic infections observed in the schoolchildren from the Andes. The most common parasite found in schoolchildren was E. histolytica, a colonic protozoan that could cause diarrhea and hepatic liver abscess. E. histolytica pathogenicity has been associated with its interaction with colonic bacteria, which either resists or enhances the host’s immune response [31, 32]. How the dysbiosis caused by the complex interactions between intestinal microbiota and E. histolytica infection modulate the nutritional status in the host requires further research. The high prevalence of excess weight observed in schoolchildren from the Galapagos and the Andean region in the present study is consistent with the reported water and food insecurity in these provinces.

A consistent feature described of the gut microbiota in individuals with malnutrition is a lower bacterial diversity which has been associated with low intestinal epithelium permeability and inflammation [33, 34]. In a recent study in China that included 30 schoolchildren with obesity and their normal weight controls matched by sex and age, the Chao 1 diversity index of gut microbiota was significantly lower in children with obesity [35]. A similar study from Egypt, which included 40 children with obesity and 9 children without the disease, analyzed the gut microbiota, the nutritional status, the residence, and their demographic conditions and showed that alpha-diversity was lower in children with obesity than in normal weight individuals; and that beta-diversity was different between these two groups of children [36]. Here, we studied schoolchildren from two regions with different severity of overweight and obesity and found lower species richness in the fecal microbiota of children from Galapagos than of children from the Andean region and a greater prevalence of excess weight in the former group. The functional predictions on the fecal microbiota of the participating children suggested a more active microbiome turnover and replication in fecal samples from the Andes which could be related with the greater microbiota diversity observed in this region. When we considered the nutritional status of the children in each geographical region species richness was higher in children with normal weight than in children with excess weight, although these differences were not statistically significant; these results were similar to those reported previously [37, 38]. Remarkably, fecal microbiota species richness was significantly lower in children with excess weight from Galapagos than in children with excess weight from the Andean region. These data shows that the bacterial dysbiosis in children with overweight and obesity from Galapagos could be greater than in the Andean region.

We observed substantial differences in the taxonomic patterns of children from the Andean region and from Galapagos. The percentages of the phylum Firmicutes and Cyanobacteria were higher in the Andean region than in Galapagos. In comparison, the percentages of Bacteroidota and Proteobacteria were significantly higher in Galapagos than in the Andes. Previous work in China and Egypt has shown that in children with obesity, the abundance of Firmicutes increases while Bacteroidetes decreases, leading to an increased Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio [35, 36]. Contrary to these previous reports, we did not observe increases in the F/B ratio in the microbiota composition of children with obesity. In children with excess weight from the Galapagos, the abundance of Firmicutes decreased while Bacteroidetes increased, leading to a decrease in the F/B ratio compared to children with normal weight. Squillario M, et al. also show a reduction in the F/B ratio in children with obesity compared with normal-weight children [38]. The authors of that study indicate that the F/B ratio could not be a biomarker for obesity and present results support that contention. Notably, the abundance of the phylum Bacteroidota was much higher in Galapagos than in the Andean Region. The abundance of Bacteroidota genera, including Bacteroides, has been associated with adiposity, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and with an inflammatory phenotype [39]. These results would indicate that there is no generalized pattern of intestinal dysbiosis associated with obesity, but rather specific alterations in the structure and function of local microbiotas would be responsible for the increase in intestinal permeability and inflammation characteristic of this disease [38, 40]. More specific studies that include diverse groups of people in different geographic areas are needed to identify local microbial patterns associated with normal weight or obesity and its associated diseases.

The importance of microbiota studies in different locations, particularly in groups with different degrees of malnutrition, is illustrated in the present study, where we found significant differences in the gut microbiota composition of the participating children. For instance, we found a high percentage of Proteobacteria in schoolchildren from the Galapagos, which was greater than in schoolchildren from the Andes, particularly in children with excess weight. The human intestinal microbiota usually contains a minor proportion of Proteoacteria and an increase in this phylum leads to dysbiosis and a risk for the development of metabolic diseases [41]. The usually low proportions of Proteobacteria maintain a state of symbiosis with the host and when their abundance increase; they can trigger deleterious states of inflammation as those observed in metabolic disorders [41]. The greater abundance of potential proinflammatory Proteobacteria has been reported in schoolchildren with obesity in Mexico where they found a positive correlation between Proteobacteria abundance and fat intake [42]. Indeed, preclinical and clinical studies have shown that consumption of high-fat diets, processed foods, artificial sweeteners, and emulsifiers can lead to metabolic alterations and intestinal inflammation, which have been associated with increased percentages of intestinal Proteobacteria [43, 44]. The environmental conditions of food and water insecurities that the schoolchildren from Galapagos experience could trigger a Proteobacteria “bloom” that is compatible with the fecal microbiota dysbiosis, and the excess weight observed in the present study.

Contrary to Proteobacteria dysbiosis in schoolchildren from Galapagos, children from the Andean region had a lower Proteobacteria load, regardless of their nutritional status.We also observed an inverse association of BMI z-score and Cyanobacteria abundance in children from Galapagos. However, others have shown an association between Cyanobacteria abundance and obesity, as well as with intestinal infections (reviewed in [45]). There is limited information on the health relevance of Cyanobacteria in humans and more research is necessary to identify the role of the Cyanobacteria phylum in human health [45]. At the genus level, only Butyrivibrio was inversely associated with BMI z-score in children from Galapagos, although the same trend was observed in children from the Andean region, it was not statistically significant. One of the main functions attributed to Butyrivibrio is the fermentation of complex carbohydrates into butyrate which contributes to preserving the intestinal barrier function [46]. The greater abundance of Butyrivibrio in normal weight children agrees with its described positive intestinal functions.

Within the Bacteroidota phylum, the loads of Prevotella and Bacteroides were significantly different in our study groups. Previous work has used the distribution of these genera to distinguish fecal communities in enterotypes associated with specific diets [47]. Thus, greater abundance of Bacteroides has been associated with long-term consumption of animal fat and proteins, while the Prevotella enterotype has been related with complex carbohydrates intake [47]. The increasing consumption of processed food and refined sugars, combined with lower consumption of fruit and vegetables has been associated with the dominance of Bacteroides over Prevotella in a Chilean community [48]. Here, the greater abundance of Bacteroides in Galapagos could be related with the limited availability of fresh foods and the increased consumption of processed foods [4, 7]. On the other hand, the predominance of Prevotella over Bacteroides in the Andean region could indicate greater intake of fruits and vegetables by the schoolchildren. The distribution of Prevotella and Bacteroides in the present study allows the distinction of enterotypes characteristic of the Andes and in Galapagos. Finally, the predicted greater abundance of peptidoglycans by the fecal microbiota from the Andes samples could indicate these molecules can modulate the immune response and metabolic functions of the host in a different manner than the ones produced in samples from the Galapagos [49]. In this sense, peptidoglycans from different bacterial species present variations in their biochemical structure and antigenicity. Invitro studies show that depending of their bacteria source, peptidoglycans modulate the function of intestinal epithelial cells, dendritic cells, which stimulation determine the type of adaptive immune response, to produce proinflammatory of anti-inflammatory mediators [50–52]. In addition, host receptors for peptidoglycans, the Nucleotide-Binding Oligomerization Domain-like (NOD), are now recognized as a bridge between gut microbiota function, metabolism, and immunity in the host [53]. Preclinical studies show that stimulation or inhibition of NOD receptors modulate the pathophysiology of metabolic disease [54]. It will be important to study how the peptidoglycans derived from the gut microbiota stimulate the NOD receptors in the contexts of local immunity and regarding the inflammatory component of metabolic diseases.

Strengths and Limitations

Limitations include the descriptive nature of the research, which prevents us from specifically determining the causality of the nutritional status related to microbiota dysbiosis. However, the characteristics of the fecal microbiota are compatible with the food and water insecurities faced by the studied populations. Also, we included a limited number of children for the microbiota analysis, and we did not directly record data related to diet or physical activity. On the other hand, the sequencing methodology used allowed us to obtain the complete 16SrRNA gene sequences, providing greater accuracy for identifying bacteria at the genus level in the studied population.

Conclusions

This study showed that malnutrition is a common problem in schoolchildren from Galapagos and the Andean region. The greater prevalence of malnutrition in the Galapagos could be related to a more severe intestinal dysbiosis, characterized by the greater abundance of the phylum Proteobacteria and the genus Bacteroides, which is compatible with the consumption of contaminated water and processed food, as well as with limited access to fresh produce. In the Andean region, on the other hand, the use of piped water and restricted access to a high-quality diet, due to the economic stress also contribute to the high prevalence of excess weight. It is essential to conduct studies to address the dysbiosis observed in the studied population, including improved sanitation, education, and targeted nutritional interventions.
