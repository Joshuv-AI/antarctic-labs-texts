Safety and Efficacy of Sodium Glucose Cotransporter‐2 Inhibitors in Patients With Cancer and Diabetes
Authors: Moran Gvili Perelman, Rafael Y. Brzezinski, Shmuel Banai, Ido Wolf, Michal Laufer‐Perl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetes and cancer exhibit a high likelihood of co‐occurrence. Diabetes serves as a risk factor for various forms of cancer and is associated with a poorer prognosis. SGLT2 (sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2) inhibitors (SGLT2i) are effective antidiabetic therapies associated with reduced all‐cause mortality in the general population; however, data among the population with cancer are scarce. We aimed to assess the safety and efficacy of SGLT2i therapy among patients with diabetes and cancer.MethodsA large retrospective, single‐center study including 849 patients diagnosed with diabetes and active cancer. Patients were divided into 2 groups: 169 patients treated with SGLT2i before cancer diagnosis and 680 patients SGLT2i naive. The primary end point was all‐cause mortality. The secondary end point was the composite of cardiovascular outcomes, including heart failure, acute coronary syndrome, and arrhythmias.ResultsAfter a median follow‐up of 48 months (interquartile range, 27–72), all‐cause mortality was significantly lower in the SGLT2i group (67% versus 53%, P=0.001). Multivariable Cox regression identified SGLT2i as an independent predictor of reduced all‐cause mortality (hazard ratio, 0.676 [95% CI, 0.532–0.860], P=0.001). Cardiovascular outcomes were higher in the SGLT2i group (28% versus 16%, P=0.001), driven by increased heart failure events (14% versus 7%, P=0.008). After propensity score matching, SGLT2i remained a significant predictor of reduced mortality (P=0.016), with no significant differences in cardiovascular outcome (P=0.067).ConclusionsSGLT2i was associated with lower all‐cause mortality in patients diagnosed with diabetes and cancer. Randomized clinical trials are needed to confirm these findings and explore the underlying mechanism.

Diabetes and cancer frequently co‐occur, with up to 18% of patients with cancer also diagnosed with diabetes. 1 , 2 This association is primarily attributed to shared risk factors, particularly aging 3 and obesity. 4 The interplay between these diseases is complex and multifactorial. Diabetes is a well‐established risk factor for several malignancies 5 and is linked to poorer response to cancer therapies 6 and reduced survival. 7 Potential mechanisms underlying the relationship between diabetes and cancer include hyperinsulinemia, hyperglycemia, and inflammation. 3 In vitro studies have demonstrated that insulin promotes tumor cell proliferation, 8 , 9 , 10 and elevated plasma insulin levels are independently correlated with an increased risk and accelerated progression of various cancer types. 11 , 12 Moreover, hyperglycemia plays a role in activating inflammatory pathways, fostering angiogenesis, accumulating oncometabolites, disrupting the balance of proto‐oncogenes and tumor suppressors, and driving posttranslational modifications, all of which contribute to cancer initiation and progression. 13 , 14

Diabetes medications have also been linked to cancer outcomes with controversial results. Although insulin use has been associated with reduced survival, 15 metformin has been correlated with improved prognosis in patients with breast cancer. 3 In recent years, the landscape of diabetes treatment has been transformed by the introduction of novel therapies, including GLP‐1 (glucagon‐like peptide 1) agonists and SGLT2 (sodium glucose cotransporter 2) inhibitors (SGLT2i). The benefits of these medications extend beyond glucose lowering, encompassing reduced body mass, improved myocardial energetics, and indirect anti‐inflammatory and antifibrosis activities, leading to favorable cardiovascular and kidney outcomes and ultimately reducing all‐cause mortality. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 However, the interaction of these medications among patients with cancer has not yet been fully elucidated. As glucose levels, obesity, and inflammation are significant players in cancer outcomes, it has been suggested that SGLT2i may improve outcomes in patients with cancer who are also diagnosed with diabetes. On the other hand, these medications carry the potential risk of ketoacidosis, 16 which might be higher among this fragile population. A recent observational cohort studies 20 and a meta‐analysis, 21 including 4 observational studies, suggested an association between SGLT2i treatment and a lower rate of cardiac events and overall mortality among patients with diabetes and cancer. 20 , 21 However, these studies focused mainly on anthracycline therapy, left ventricle dysfunction, 22 and heart failure (HF) hospitalizations. 20

We aimed to evaluate the impact of SGLT2i therapy on all‐cause mortality and cardiovascular outcomes in a large cohort of patients with cancer and preexisting diabetes.

METHODS

Data Availability

The data underlying this article cannot be shared for the privacy of individuals who participated in the study. The data will be shared on a reasonable request to the corresponding author.

Study Population and Protocol

We conducted a retrospective, single‐center, observational study at Tel‐Aviv Sourasky Medical Center, a tertiary cancer center in Israel. We included adult (≥18 years old) patients diagnosed with diabetes and initiating systemic therapy for all types of cancer between November 2015 and November 2022. There were no exclusion criteria, therefore representing a real‐world, general, and diverse population. The treating oncologist selected the specific therapy protocol in accordance with current best practices and guidelines.

The cohort was divided into 2 groups: those treated with SGLT2i before initiating cancer therapy (the SGLT2i group) and those who were SGLT2i naive (the non‐SGLT2i group).

The study was approved by the local Helsinki regulatory ethics committee (Identifier: TLV‐0228‐16). Due to the trial's retrospective nature, informed consent was waived by the Helsinki Committee.

Data Collection

Baseline clinical characteristics were obtained from electronic health records and included baseline medical history: sex, body mass index, ischemic heart disease (IHD), HF, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, chronic kidney disease (CKD), chronic medical therapy (mainly antidiabetic and cardioprotective medications), cancer type and therapy, and blood tests (complete blood count, renal function, hemoglobin A1C and high‐sensitivity troponin I).

Study End Point

The primary end point was all‐cause mortality, extracted from computerized patient charts and the population registry bureau.

The secondary end point was the composite of cardiovascular outcomes, including HF events, acute coronary syndrome, and arrhythmias (including atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, supraventricular tachycardia, and ventricular tachycardia), extracted from the patient charts.

Statistical Analysis

All continuous variables are displayed as mean [±SD] for normally distributed variables or median (interquartile range (IQR)) for variables with nonnormal distributions. Categorical variables are displayed as the number (%) of individuals within each group.

Continuous variables were compared using a 2‐tailed unpaired t test for normally distributed variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for nonnormally distributed ones. To evaluate the associations among categorical variables, we used a chi‐square test. Bonferroni‐adjusted P values were calculated for multiple comparisons. The median follow‐up time for all‐cause mortality was calculated using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method.

All‐cause mortality and the occurrence of the cardiovascular composite outcome were evaluated using univariate and multivariable Cox proportional hazard regression. Cumulative survival curves divided by SGLT2i treatment status are presented. We adjusted our model for age, sex, prior IHD, prior HF, dyslipidemia, and use of medications (statins, beta blockers, furosemide, GLP‐1 agonists, and renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system inhibitors, including angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers and sacubitril/valsartan). We also adjusted for additional cancer therapies, including chemotherapy (fluorouracil and anthracyclines) and monoclonal antibodies (trastuzumab and bevacizumab) (Tables S1 and S2). Given the potential cardiovascular and survival effects of GLP‐1 receptor agonists, we prespecified 4 sensitivity analyses. Proportional hazards assumptions were evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals and were not violated. Functional forms of continuous covariates were assessed using Martingale residuals; linear specifications were retained as restricted cubic spline modeling did not improve model fit by Akaike information criterion. Unadjusted Kaplan–Meier curves with 95% confidence bands are presented for descriptive purposes; adjusted associations are reported from multivariable Cox models.

The primary analysis was conducted using multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression. To assess robustness, we additionally performed a 1:1 nearest‐neighbor propensity score‐matched sensitivity analysis using the “MatchIt” package in R. 23 Propensity scores were estimated using a logistic regression, including all potential confounders prespecified a priori (hypertension, HF, hyperlipidemia, IHD, obesity, CKD, age, and sex). We performed 1:1 nearest‐neighbor matching without replacement. Covariate balance was assessed with standardized mean differences before and after matching (Table S3), and a Love plot is provided (Figure S1); all postmatching standardized mean differences were <0.1. In the matched cohort, Cox models were fitted with cluster‐robust SEs clustered by matched pair; a shared frailty specification was also evaluated in sensitivity analyses. A 2‐tailed P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed with the SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics, version 28, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, 2016), The R statistical package (version 3.3.1) (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and GraphPad Prism version 9.00 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA).

RESULTS

Baseline Patient Characteristics

Our cohort included 849 patients with diabetes and active cancer, of whom 169 (20%) were in the SGLT2i group and 680 (80%) in the non‐SGLT2i group (Figure S2).

The mean age was 70±8 years, with 43% female representation. The most common types of cancer were gastrointestinal (29%), lung (14%), and breast (8%), as presented in Table 1. The majority of the cohort was treated with chemotherapy (81%), and 16% of the patients were treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors. As expected among these older populations with diabetes, cardiovascular risk factors and IHD (24%) were frequent (Table 1).

Baseline characteristics, including age, hypertension, and CKD, were similar between the 2 groups. However, baseline hyperlipidemia (56% versus 46%, P=0.035), IHD (39% versus 21%, P<0.001), and HF (11% versus 6%, P=0.014) were more prevalent in the SGLT2i group. Female representation was lower among the SGLT2i group (30% versus 46%, P<0.001). No significant differences in the types of cancer were observed between the groups. Although the use of chemotherapy or immune checkpoint inhibitors was similar, the SGLT2i group had higher use of anthracyclines (6% versus 3%, P=0.043) and fluorouracil (39% versus 29%, P=0.029), both known to be potentially cardiotoxic. 24

The majority of patients (82%) received empagliflozin, and the remaining cohort was treated with dapagliflozin. The use of metformin (76% versus 59%, P<0.001) and GLP‐1 agonists (9% versus 3%, P<0.001) was higher in the SGLT2i group. Additionally, the use of cardioprotective medications, such as angiotensin receptor blockers (34% versus 26%, P=0.045), beta blockers (53% versus 40%, P=0.003), and statins (76% versus 63%, P=0.001), was more prevalent in the SGLT2i group (Table 1). However, there were no significant differences in the use of angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors or mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists.

The groups had no significant differences in hemoglobin A1C, creatinine, and baseline high‐sensitivity troponin I levels. As expected, baseline hematocrit was significantly higher in the SGLT2i group (36.7±6.4% versus 34.8±5.6%, P<0.001).

Primary End Point

Over a median follow‐up period of 48 months [IQR, 27–72], 547 (64%) patients died, with a significantly lower all‐cause mortality among the SGLT2i group (53% versus 67%, P=0.001) (Table 2) (Figure 1).

Using a multivariable Cox regression analysis that included all baseline characteristics with possible clinical significance (Table S1), SGLT2i therapy emerged as an independent significant predictor of reduced all‐cause mortality (hazard ratio [HR], 0.676 [95% CI, 0.532–0.860], P=0.001). Older age and use of beta blockers emerged as significant independent predictors for increased all‐cause mortality (HR, 1.018 and 1.237, respectively).

In a subgroup analysis of various cancer types, we observed that patients treated with SGLT2i consistently had lower mortality rates, except for breast cancer (Figure 2). Following a multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression within each cancer type subgroup, adjusting for baseline cardiovascular comorbidities (hypertension, HF, hyperlipidemia, IHD, obesity, CKD), age, and sex, no significant differences were observed for breast cancer (Table S4).

Secondary End Point

Cardiovascular outcomes were significantly higher in the SGLT2i group (28% versus 16%, P=0.001), primarily driven by increased HF events (14% versus 7%, P=0.008) (Table 2). In a multivariable Cox regression analysis that accounted for all baseline characteristics with potential clinical significance (Table S2), SGLT2i therapy remained an independent and significant predictor of increased cardiovascular outcomes (HR, 1.801 [95% CI, 1.170–2.771], P=0.001). Additionally, age, baseline IHD, HF, and furosemide therapy were identified as independent predictors of increased cardiovascular outcomes (HR, 1.040, P=0.001; HR, 1.761, P=0.014; and HR, 3.277, P<0.001, respectively).

Given the potential cardiovascular and survival effect of GLP‐1 agonists, we conducted 4 distinct analytical approaches to assess the robustness of our findings to GLP‐1 agonists confounding (Table S5). All sensitivity analyses maintained statistical significance (P≤0.002), indicating that the survival benefit is not dependent on GLP‐1 agonists confounding. Even after excluding 34 GLP‐1 agonists users (4% of the cohort), the analysis retained 815 patients with preserved effect size and significance. Formal interaction testing between SGLT2i and GLP‐1 agonist use revealed no significant interaction for either mortality (P=0.892) or cardiovascular outcomes (P=0.803).

Given the higher prevalence of IHD and HF in the SGLT2i group, we conducted a propensity score matching analysis to minimize differences in baseline covariates. After matching, covariate balance was excellent (all standardized mean differences <0.10; see Table S3 for the standardized mean differences table and Figure S1 for Love plot). Patient characteristics of the matched groups are presented in Table S3 and Figure S1. Following this analysis, SGLT2i remained an independent predictor of reduced all‐cause mortality (P=0.016), whereas no significant differences in cardiovascular outcomes were observed (P=0.067) (Figures 3 and 4).

DISCUSSION

In this large cohort of patients with diabetes and active cancer, treatment with SGLT2is was independently associated with a 32% reduction in all‐cause mortality. Our findings suggest that SGLT2i therapy is safe and may offer clinical benefit beyond glycemic control in patients with cancer.

Developed initially as antidiabetic therapy, SGLT2i have demonstrated robust cardiovascular and renal protective effects in multiple landmarks randomized controlled trials, showing reduction in major cardiac adverse effects, HF hospitalizations, CKD progression, cardiovascular mortality, and all‐cause mortality. 25 , 26 As a result, SGLT2i therapy is now recommended as a class I indication for treating diabetes, HF, and CKD. 27 , 28 Despite the remarkable clinical outcomes associated with SGLT2i, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear, thought to stem from a combination of improved myocardial energetics, weight reduction, natriuresis, anti‐inflammatory and antifibrotic effects, and metabolic reprogramming. 18 , 19 However, it is important to note that the randomized controlled trials excluded patients with active cancer, limiting the generalizability of their findings to the population with cancer. Patients with cancer and diabetes represent a particularly vulnerable group, with a potentially higher risk of ketoacidosis and hypoglycemia. A recent retrospective study 29 showed the safety and efficacy of SGLT2i among patients with diabetes and cancer who developed cardiomyopathy or HF. Our findings provide reassuring real‐world evidence supporting the safety and potential benefit of SGLT2i in patients with cancer and diabetes, regardless of HF.

Several retrospective studies have explored the impact of SGLT2i among patients with diabetes and specific cancers. 30 , 31 , 32 Hendryx et al. 30 and Luo et al., 32 using Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results‐Medicare data, found that SGLT2i therapy was associated with improved survival in patients with nonsmall cell lung cancer or hepatocellular carcinoma. A meta‐analysis 21 of 4 observational studies, including >4000 patients, similarly demonstrated reduced all‐cause mortality, HF hospitalization, and arrhythmias among patients with cancer treated with SGLT2is. Chaing et al. 33 and Bhatti et al. 22 have reported lower rates of HF hospitalizations and overall mortality 33 and the development of left ventricle dysfunction. 22 Our study extends these findings by evaluating a diverse population with a longer median follow‐up, with a higher prevalence of chemotherapy and immune checkpoint inhibitors, and a higher use of empagliflozin. 20 , 22 , 33 SGLT2i therapy remained an independent predictor of improved survival following a multivariable Cox regression analysis, incorporating variables such as age, sex, and the presence of IHD and HF.

GLP‐1 receptor agonists have also demonstrated a favorable impact on cardiovascular outcomes in patients with diabetes. 34 Although the use of GLP‐1 receptor agonists was more prevalent in the SGLT2i group, the actual number of patients treated with GLP‐1 receptor agonists in our cohort remained relatively low. The remarkable consistency of effect sizes across multiple analytical approaches strongly suggests that these benefits represent true therapeutic effects rather than confounding artifacts. SGLT2i and GLP‐1 agonists provide survival benefits through distinct, noncompeting pathways. SGLT2i primarily have hemodynamic benefits, including preload reduction, improved cardiac energetics, and reduced inflammation, 16 , 18 whereas GLP‐1 agonists predominantly have antiatherosclerotic effects, endothelial protection, and direct cardioprotection. 4 These findings strengthen the evidence base for SGLT2i use in patients with cancer and support their consideration regardless of concurrent GLP‐1 agonists therapy status. Future studies focusing on GLP‐1 agonists therapy in patients with cancer are necessary.

The interest in SGLT2i therapy in the context of anticancer activity is primarily due to the well‐established fact that cancer cells typically switch their metabolism from fatty acid oxidation to glucose use. 35 This is because cancer cells often contain dysfunctional mitochondria, which do not provide enough energy from the metabolism of fatty acids. The presence of SGLTs have been confirmed in various types of cancer cells. 36 , 37 , 38 The metabolic reprogramming of cancer cells involves adaptive changes in cellular metabolism that enable malignant cells to support rapid proliferation, survive under stress, and resist therapy. The most recognized feature is the “Warburg effect,” where cancer cells preferentially use aerobic glycolysis over oxidative phosphorylation, even in the presence of sufficient oxygen, to generate adenosine triphosphate and metabolic intermediates for biosynthesis. This shift supports increased glucose uptake and lactate production. 39 , 40 This effect is shown to be a hallmark of many cancer types and has led to the development of new diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for cancer. Therefore, one possible theory for the observed reduction in all‐cause mortality is the antineoplastic effect of SGLT2i, which block SGLT2 transporters in cancer cells, leading to decreased glycolytic flux, lower lactate production, and reduced intracellular adenosine triphosphate and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate levels, all central features of the Warburg phenotype. This metabolic stress can induce cell cycle arrest, promote apoptosis, and suppress proliferation in various cancer models. 36 Supporting this theory are several clinical studies demonstrating reduced tumor growth in patients treated with SGLT2i with lung adenocarcinoma, gastrointestinal, liver, pancreas, and prostate cancers. 41 , 42 , 43 Other theories involve the anti‐inflammatory effect 44 , 45 and impaired mitochondrial respiration and glutamine metabolism, further disrupting the metabolic flexibility of cancer cells. 46

The cardioprotective effects of SGTL2i are likely multifactorial, 47 including anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant mechanisms. Inflammation 44 , 45 and the generation of reactive oxygen species and increased cellular oxidative stress 48 , 49 are known to play active roles in patients with cancer. SGLT2i have been shown to mitigate these harmful processes. 50 , 51 Although previous trials demonstrated reduced cardiovascular outcomes, 22 particularly in HF hospitalization among patients with cancer, 21 , 33 we observed a higher rate of cardiovascular outcomes in the SGLT2i group, driven mainly by HF events. However, this likely reflects a higher baseline burden of cardiovascular disease, including IHD and HF, which did not exist in the previous trial, 30 , 32 , 33 as well as more frequent exposure to cardiotoxic agents 24 such as anthracyclines and fluorouracil among this group. After adjusting for these variables using propensity score matching, the association between SGLT2i and cardiovascular outcomes was no longer statistically significant. This suggests that the initial statistical signal should be interpreted with caution and may not be clinically meaningful.

From a clinical standpoint, our findings highlight the potential role of SGLT2i as a safe and possibly beneficial therapy in patients with diabetes and cancer and should not be withheld due to concerns regarding patient fragility or potential adverse events. However, although our results are suggestive of an anticancer benefit, prospective randomized controlled trials are needed to validate these findings and determine whether SGLT2i should be integrated into oncologic treatment paradigms.

CONCLUSIONS

The use of SGLT2i therapy was associated with significantly reduced all‐cause mortality among patients diagnosed with diabetes and cancer. Given the high prevalence of diabetes in patients with cancer, highlighting the positive prognostic value of SGLT2is paramount. However, further randomized controlled multicenter studies are warranted to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and validate the overall impact of SGLT2i on cancer‐related outcomes and cardiovascular outcomes.
