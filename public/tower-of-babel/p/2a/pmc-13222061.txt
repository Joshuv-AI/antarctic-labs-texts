Disparities in lung function trajectories among tobacco-exposed individuals
Authors: Benjamin Grobman, Amy L Non, Elizabeth Baker, Gabriela R Oates, Elizabeth A Regan, James L Crooks, Meredith C McCormack, Nadia N Hansel, Alejandro A Diaz, James C Ross
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe relationship of social determinants of health (SDOH), environmental exposures and medical history to lung function trajectories is underexplored. A better understanding of these relationships could inform preventive strategies for lung health.MethodsWe analysed data from COPDGene, a US longitudinal, observational study. Participants were tobacco-exposed (≥10 pack-years of smoking) non-Hispanic Black and non-Hispanic White adults aged 45–80 years. We analysed 2990 males and 2945 females, using Bayesian trajectory modelling on post-bronchodilator forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) and forced vital capacity (FVC). We applied multinomial logistic regression to assess the association of SDOH, environmental exposures and medical history with lung function trajectories.Measurements and main resultsSix trajectories were identified within each sex. Non-Hispanic Black race was more prevalent in trajectories characterised by lower FEV1 and FVC values (ie, lower lung function trajectories) compared with non-Hispanic White adults. In adjusted models, non-Hispanic Black race, residence in the Southeastern USA, lifetime asthma and a father with COPD were associated with significantly higher odds of the lowest trajectory (ie, trajectory six vs the reference trajectory) for both sexes. Higher income and private insurance showed inverse associations with lower lung function trajectories. The Social Vulnerability Index socioeconomic theme (based on census-level poverty, unemployment, income and educational attainment) was associated with the lowest trajectory in males.ConclusionsSignificant disparities in lung function trajectories exist between non-Hispanic Black adults and non-Hispanic White adults. Individual- and community-level factors are associated with lower lung function trajectory in people exposed to tobacco.Keywords: Pulmonary Disease, Chronic Obstructive

Introduction

Lung function trajectories include a growth phase during childhood and adolescence followed by a decline phase throughout adulthood. In adulthood, distinct lung function trajectories can be characterised by varying levels of peak lung function obtained and different rates of decline, and these patterns can be influenced by individual and environmental factors.1 2 Both low peak lung function and accelerated decline in adulthood can lead to chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).3 A study from 2015, which included data from three major cohorts across multiple countries, found that people with lower Forced Expiratory Volume in one second (FEV1) before 40 years of age had a significantly elevated risk of COPD in later life.3 A 2016 study among 599 participants from the Tucson Children’s Respiratory Study found that multiple childhood exposures, including history of maternal asthma, prior illness due to respiratory syncytial virus and adult asthma, were associated with a persistently low lung function trajectory in adulthood.4 Additional childhood risk factors for subnormal peak lung function include low birth weight, family history of asthma and secondhand smoke exposure. Exposure to tobacco smoking—including maternal exposure during pregnancy and personal cigarette smoking—is an established exposure linked to these patterns. Previous studies have shown that lower peak lung function and faster rates of decline are associated with higher risk of COPD, independent of tobacco exposure.2 5 Additionally, social determinants of health (SDOH) are significantly linked to lung health and mortality risk.6 People with lower income and less education have higher self-reported respiratory symptoms, higher rates of COPD and lower measures of pulmonary function compared with those with higher income.6 7 However, there remain significant limitations in our understanding of how SDOH are related to lung function trajectories in adulthood.

Historically, physicians have used race-specific pulmonary function equations based on the inaccurate assumption that the social construct of race is an accurate indicator of biological reality.8 Recent work shows that race-adjusted measures tend to obscure disparities among marginalised groups,9 and the American Thoracic Society now recommends against race-adjusted pulmonary function test equations.8 Race-neutral equations may reveal racial disparities in lung function trajectories, as recent research has shown that these equations more accurately reclassify Black adults as having higher rates and severity of lung impairment.9 Additionally, these updated equations more accurately predict clinical outcomes and trajectories of lung function decline than prior race-adjusted equations, as recent work shows that race-adjusted measures tend to underestimate COPD severity, particularly in early disease among non-Hispanic Black individuals.10

While prior research has examined the relationship between SDOH and COPD, there is minimal prior research examining the relationship between SDOH and lung function trajectories, though social and environmental exposures may relate to lung function trajectories across the life course. Additionally, given the new implementation of race-neutral lung function equations, it is important to examine the relationship between SDOH, smoking exposure and lung function trajectories to help contextualise potential disparities among tobacco-exposed individuals. While the current study focuses on adults with prior tobacco exposure, examining trends in lung function within this high-risk group may still reflect broader health disparities. As such, the current study examines the association between SDOH, environmental exposures, medical and family history and lung function trajectories in a cohort of current and former tobacco-exposed individuals with and without COPD. We hypothesised that adverse SDOH would be associated with lower lung function trajectories.

Methods

Study cohort

The COPDGene Study is an ongoing, multicentre, longitudinal study designed to investigate the genetic and epidemiologic characteristics of COPD in the USA.11 COPDGene enrolled 10 198 non-Hispanic White and non-Hispanic Black ever-smokers with and without COPD and 454 never-smokers. Participants were between the ages of 45 and 80 years, and ever-smokers had a minimum of 10 pack-years cigarette smoke exposure at baseline. Full inclusion and exclusion criteria are described in the initial study manuscript.11 The participants were enrolled between November 2007 and April 2011. Additional examinations were conducted at year 5 (phase 2) and year 10 (phase 3). Assessments included questionnaires, spirometry and chest CT imaging. As part of the phase 2 visit for COPDGene, participants provided information about their current address. These addresses were geocoded with ArcGIS and linked to Federal Information Processing Standard codes that were used to determine census block groups. The census block groups were used to define the area deprivation index (ADI) for each participant based on the 2018 national rankings.12 The institutional review board at each participating clinical centre approved the COPDGene study, and all participants gave written informed consent.

Trajectory Analysis

We applied Bayesian trajectory modelling to absolute post-bronchodilator FEV1 and forced vital capacity (FVC) measures from participants enrolled in the COPDGene Study. We excluded data entries with missing spirometry or smoking status information and those corresponding to more than 150 pack-years’ smoke exposure. We retained only those individuals for whom there were at least two longitudinal time points (see supplement for additional details regarding data selection). Trajectory modelling was performed on pooled data from self-identified non-Hispanic Black and non-Hispanic White individuals. Trajectories were modelled separately in males and females. We used the bayes_traj software package (https://github.com/acil-bwh/bayes_traj), which includes tools for prior specification, model fitting, visualisation and model summarisation, all of which were used in this study.13 Although each participant contributes 5–10 years of follow-up data, the observation windows are staggered across individuals, resulting in population-level coverage over several decades. Trajectory modelling leverages this overlap to infer group-level trajectories that extend beyond any single individual’s observation period.

We simultaneously modelled both FEV1 and FVC as functions of age, height, pack-years and smoking status. We centred the age variable at 20 for men and 18 for women—the ages of presumed peak lung function, respectively.14 Additionally, we centred the height variable at 177 cm for men and 164 cm for women. Trajectory model predictors included an intercept term, years since presumed lung function, (years since presumed peak lung function)2, height centred, pack-years smoke exposure, current smoking status and an interaction between smoking status and years since presumed peak lung function. We included the quadratic term for age (age centred)2, as lung function decline in adulthood is nonlinear. We modelled lung function trajectories in males and females separately, as sex is a significant predictor of absolute FEV1 and FVC measurements and because the relationship between sex and lung function trajectories is not well established.

We adopted a Bayesian framework to incorporate knowledge of population averages in early adulthood for peak lung function, using data from Global Lung Function Initiative (GLI) Global (race-neutral) reference equations.15 This allowed us to mitigate the absence of early adulthood data in the current cohort. By centring the age variable at 18 years (females) or 20 years (males) and centring height at mean adult values (164 cm for females, 177 cm for males), the model intercept became interpretable as the expected lung function of a never-smoker of average height at peak lung function age.

We executed the trajectory routine using several hundred random initialisations, and we retained the models corresponding to the lowest Watanabe-Akaike information criterion (WAIC2) scores. We chose to model six trajectories for both men and women in order to achieve a parsimonious yet descriptive representation of the data that aligns with prior studies.2 For each participant, trajectory modelling produced a probability of assignment to each of the identified trajectories. We assigned each participant to their most probable trajectory and treated this assignment as our main outcome for further analysis. Trajectory assignments were treated as categorical variables throughout. Additional details regarding the trajectory analysis can be found in online supplemental file 1.

Exposures

Individual-level SDOH

We examined multiple individual-level SDOH. Race was self-reported by the participant and categorised as non-Hispanic Black or non-Hispanic White. For education, the highest education degree awarded or level of school completed was recorded at baseline for each participant (1=8th grade or less; 2=high school, no diploma; 3=high school graduate or GED; 4=some college or technical school, no degree; 5=college or technical school graduate; 6=master’s or doctoral degree). At phase two, questionnaires recorded multiple measures of socioeconomic status (SES): housing was categorised as lives with family, assisted living, nursing, no permanent place, with caregiver, rent and own home; insurance was categorised as military, Medicare, Medicare and private, private or Medicaid; healthcare access was categorised as a binary variable based on access to a primary care physician or other provider; current employment status was categorised based on current employment (work now); income level was based on categories (1 = < US$15K, 2 = US$15K–US$35K, 3 = US$35K–US$50K, 4 = US$50K–US$75K, 5 > US$75K, 6=decline to answer).

Community Level SDOH

At phase two, we recorded percentile rankings for the social vulnerability index (SVI) based on four distinct themes at the county level. The socioeconomic theme (theme 1) is determined based on county-level poverty, unemployment, income and educational attainment. Household composition and disability (theme 2) is based on numbers of people aged 65 and older, number of people aged 17 or younger, numbers older than age 5 with a disability and single-parent households. Minority status and language (theme 3) is based on minority population and population that speaks English ‘Less than Well’. Housing and transportation (theme 4) includes mobile homes, crowding, population without vehicles, crowding, ‘multi-unit structures’ and ‘group quarters’. Also recorded at phase 2 were ADI values measured at the state and national levels. The ADI is a composite metric of neighbourhood socioeconomic disadvantage ranging from 1 to 100 (national) and 1 to 10 (state), with higher scores indicating more ‘disadvantage’.12 ADI scores for participants in the COPDGene study were obtained by geocoding address data collected during phase 2 of COPDGene. Geo-location codes were linked to 2018 national ADI scores obtained from the Neighborhood Atlas (https://www.neighborhoodatlas.medicine.wisc.edu/). Region was determined based on study site and categorised as Northeast, Northwest, Southwest and Southeast.

Environmental exposures

We examined childhood exposure to smoking (parental smoking status, years lived with a smoker and mother smoking during pregnancy). We also included occupational dust and fume exposure.

Medical history

Information about participants’ medical history as well as their parents’ medical history was recorded at baseline. Lifetime asthma was included as an individual history variable. Parental variables included history of parental COPD, asthma, chronic bronchitis and emphysema. Baseline data collection procedures were repeated at approximately 5 years (phase 2) and 10 years (phase 3). Study data consisting of smoking history (pack-years exposure and current smoking status), body mass index (BMI) and post-bronchodilator spirometric measures of lung function were recorded at each phase. Spirometry was performed per ATS recommendations.16

Statistical analysis

We built multivariate models in three stages. In each stage, we performed multinomial logistic regression (using the MNLogit routine in python’s statsmodels library, V.0.13.5) using trajectory assignment as the outcome. Trajectory 2 was selected as the reference group for analyses comparing trajectories because, for both males and females, the mean GLI Global Z-scores for FEV1 and FVC were consistently closest to zero across all visits. This pattern indicates lung function closest to expected norms, providing a clinically meaningful basis for interpreting differences across other trajectories. We a priori selected all variables described above based on prior literature. We treated categories of housing and insurance responses as separate binary variables; we treated education and income as ordinal variables. First, we performed univariate analysis and removed from further consideration those variables that showed no statistically significant association with trajectory assignment. Second, we independently considered variables in defined groups: SDOH community-level factors, SDOH individual-level factors (education, employment, insurance, housing), medical history and early-life smoke exposure (see supplement for a complete list of variables considered in each category). Within each group, we considered all possible predictor combinations and selected the model with lowest Akaike information criteria value. For each group, this stage identified the subset of variables to retain for further consideration. Third, using an intercept term, pack-years, lifetime asthma and BMI as initial covariates, we iteratively built up the final multivariate model by considering in turn each variable from the second stage, using the likelihood ratio test to evaluate if including the variable resulted in a statistically significant improvement. On adding a new variable, we then tested whether any previously added variable could be removed. Race was considered a final covariate. To investigate the relationship between individual and social factors and lung function trajectories, we used a forward selection procedure, adding race last. This ordering allowed us to first evaluate associations between SDOH and trajectory assignment without prematurely attributing variance to race, which is often a surrogate for unmeasured structural and social exposures. By including race at the end of the selection process, we were able to assess whether it remained significantly associated with trajectory assignment after accounting for known SDOH. Additional details are contained in the supplement.

Association of SDOH, environmental exposures and medical history with lung function trajectories

ORs from multinomial logistic regression analysis comparing lung function trajectories against reference trajectory 2 are shown in figure 3 and online supplemental eTable 8. Among males, race was associated with trajectory membership, with non-Hispanic Black males less likely to belong to trajectory 1 and more likely to belong to lower trajectories 3, 4 and 6 compared with non-Hispanic White males. Higher income and having private insurance were associated with a lower likelihood of being in trajectories 4 and 6. Having a father with COPD significantly increased the odds of being in trajectory 6, and having a mother with COPD significantly increased the odds of being in trajectory 5. Increasing pack years of smoking were associated with higher odds of being in trajectories 5 and 6. Higher BMI increased the odds of being in trajectories 3 and 4 relative to the reference trajectory. Finally, a history of asthma increased the odds of being in trajectories 5 and 6.

Several socioeconomic and health-related factors influenced trajectory membership among females. Non-Hispanic Black females were more likely to belong to trajectories associated with poorer lung function compared with their non-Hispanic White counterparts, with the strongest associations observed for trajectory 4 (OR=5.01). Higher educational attainment and income were consistently associated with reduced odds of being in these worse trajectories, suggesting a protective effect. Specifically, a higher education level decreased the odds of being in trajectory 4 (OR=0.85), 5 (OR=0.76) or 6 (OR=0.63), while higher income showed a similar trend across trajectories 3 to 6. Additionally, having a father with COPD significantly increased the odds of being in trajectories 3, 5 and 6, and a personal history of asthma markedly elevated the odds for membership in trajectories 4, 5 and 6, indicating a substantial impact of familial and personal respiratory health history on trajectory membership.

Discussion

We examined the association between SDOH, environmental exposures and medical history with lung function trajectories among a large cohort of non-Hispanic Black and non-Hispanic White Americans exposed to tobacco smoke. We found a strong association of non-Hispanic Black race with worse lung function trajectories even when adjusting for multiple measures of SDOH and medical/family history. Participants with higher pack-years, participants living in the Southeastern USA and participants with lower SES also had a higher likelihood of worse lung function trajectories.

Compared with non-Hispanic White adults, non-Hispanic Black adults in COPDGene were significantly more prevalent in low lung function trajectories. A prior study on this topic using data from the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities Study, a population of both those with and without prior tobacco exposure, found that White adults had a faster decline in FEV1 than their Black counterparts from ages 45 to 75, while declines were faster among Black adults older than age 75.17 However, in this study, Black adults had lower lung function at baseline than White adults.17 Similarly, another study using data from the Cardiovascular Health Study (a study of elderly adults), which adjusted for participant smoking status, found that Black adults had lower FEV1 and FVC at baseline but had slower declines in their lung function trajectories than White adults.18 These findings align with the current study’s finding of Black adults being overrepresented in low lung function trajectories characterised by low initial baseline measurements but stable declines in lung function. However, further work is required to better understand the discrepant results between these studies regarding rates of decline among Black and White adults.

In the current study, the association between Black race and low lung function trajectories was found to be significant even when controlling for individual and area-level SES. While reasons for this finding cannot be directly elucidated, it is likely that these variables do not fully incorporate the ways that the socio-cultural experience of race impacts life experiences and health. For example, interpersonal discrimination has previously been shown to be associated with a higher risk of asthma and poorer asthma control among Black Americans.19 Similarly, prior research has shown that the association between air pollution and mortality is stronger among Black Americans, regardless of income, as compared with high-income White Americans.20 This is supported by prior research showing that a large portion of disparities in pulmonary function tests between Black and White people is explained by disproportionate exposure to adverse SDOH among Black people.21 These findings mirror those in multiple other areas of the medical literature, in which Black people have worse health outcomes even at equivalent levels of SES. For example, prior studies in heart disease and life expectancy overall have shown that Black adults do not experience the same benefits of higher SES as their White peers.22

Lifetime asthma was associated with worse lung function trajectories in both male and female participants. Prior studies, which have included both tobacco-exposed and non-tobacco-exposed populations, have shown that individuals with active asthma have accelerated lung function decline and worse lung function trajectories.4 5 23 24 While the current model included lifetime asthma, a significant proportion of adult asthma has a childhood onset.25 A prior study using the COPDGene cohort showed that among adults exposed to tobacco, those with childhood asthma had worse lung function and were at a higher risk of COPD in adulthood.26

Among both males and females, residence in the Southeast was associated with worse lung function trajectories. There are multiple potential reasons for this finding, including high smoking rates in the Southern USA,27 lower access to smoking cessation therapy28 and high air pollution exposure in the Southeastern USA.29 Previous research has shown that places within the Southeast such as Appalachia and the Mississippi Delta Region have the worst outcomes for multiple diseases including cancer, heart disease and COPD.30 Similarly, the Southeastern USA has previously been described as the ‘Stroke Belt’ due to a high stroke incidence in the setting of high rates of diabetes, hypertension, obesity and smoking.31 Notably, prior research has shown that SES has a minimal impact on this disparity.32 Prior research has also shown higher rates of death from COPD in the Midwest and South.33 The fact that residence in the Southeastern USA is associated with worse trajectories even when accounting for multiple measures of SES is further evidence of the impact of unmeasured social and environmental factors on the risk of impaired lung function, similar to the excess risk seen due to Black race. Additionally, Black Americans are disproportionately concentrated in the Southern USA,34 and Black Americans born in the Southern USA have higher mortality than those born in other regions:35 as such, future work should aim to further disentangle the impact of race and region in understanding disparities in pulmonary health.

People who had parents with COPD were also more likely to have lower trajectories in adulthood. Prior research using the present cohort has shown that parental history of COPD is a risk factor for the development of COPD even when controlling for SES, family and personal history of smoking, and childhood exposures.36

Multiple measures of lower individual SES were associated with a higher probability of being in a worse lung function trajectory. Among females, lower income and education were the most predictive, while among males, lower income and lack of private insurance were the most predictive. Prior research in the USA has shown that people with lower income have higher self-reported respiratory symptoms, higher rates of COPD and lower measures of pulmonary function (FVC and FEV1), associations which were not fully attenuated by smoking status.6 Similarly, a study of multiple lower and middle income countries found that lower SES was associated with a higher rate of COPD.7

Our finding that neighbourhood SES as measured by the socioeconomic theme of the SVI is associated with worse lung function trajectory aligns with prior research that has found that lower area SES is associated with more severe symptoms among people with COPD.37 Prior studies have also shown that people with lower individual SES are exposed to negative environmental factors across the life course and have on average lower lung function and higher respiratory disease mortality.6 Overall, our findings demonstrate associations between social and structural determinants of health and mid- to late-adulthood lung function trajectories. These associations should not be interpreted as evidence of causal effects across the full life course, but rather as indicators of accumulated disadvantage that manifest in lung function patterns observed in adulthood.

In recent years, trajectory analysis has become a common approach to improve understanding of patterns of lung growth and decline.2 In recent work, we used race- and sex-adjusted (GLI) reference equations to compute FEV1 and FVC Z-scores; we pooled data from non-Hispanic White and non-Hispanic Black males and females from the COPDGene study and identified seven trajectories overall.38 Notably, we observed significantly different prevalences of non-Hispanic Black adults across trajectories in that study: 28% (trajectory 1), 28% (trajectory 2), 32% (trajectory 3), 38% (trajectory 4), 34% (trajectory 5), 19% (trajectory 6) and 16% (trajectory 7). This comparison, together with recent analyses showing that race-specific equations underestimate disease severity among non-Hispanic Black adults and misclassify non-Hispanic Black adults into less severe GOLD categories,9 39 argues against using race-adjusted prediction equations.

A strength of this study is that it directly analyses unadjusted FEV1 and FVC values, potentially avoiding biases associated with race-based adjustments and reflecting raw physiological measurements. This may offer more accurate insights into the impact of socioeconomic and environmental factors on lung function trajectories. This study also has multiple limitations. As this study was conducted among a population of individuals exposed to tobacco smoke and was limited to only two races (Black and White), the lung function trajectory distribution shown is unlikely to be representative of the general population. Furthermore, our analysis of region was limited to specific centres within each geographic region and, as such, is likely not comprehensive in characterising the impact of geography on lung function trajectories. Additionally, many of the questions were based on current respiratory exposures, although prior research has shown that trajectories have early life and even prenatal roots. While the current analysis did examine some early-life predictors, such as childhood smoke exposure, these were not found to be significant in iterative model testing. Additionally, some SDOH are static (eg, education) while others, such as income and insurance, change over time; we did not explicitly account for this. Finally, because our data do not include childhood or young adulthood spirometry, we cannot directly observe peak lung function or attribute trajectory patterns to specific early-life exposures. Nonetheless, group-level patterns—such as persistently low lung function without subsequent rapid decline—may be indicative of early functional impairment. Additionally, as COPDGene is a large multicentre study with participants representing multiple socioeconomic and ethnic backgrounds, we believe our study adds new and valuable insight into risk factors associated with abnormal lung function trajectories.

Conclusions

Among a population of individuals exposed to tobacco smoke, non-Hispanic Black race, lower SES (both individual and community), history of asthma, residence in the Southeastern USA and parental COPD were attributes more likely to be associated with worse lung function trajectories in adulthood. These findings reveal an interplay of SDOH, demographic and familial factors impacting lung function over time, which require further investigation and which serve as potential targets for intervention.
