Protolytic properties and biological activity of spinorphin and its butyric acid derivatives in aqueous solution
Authors: Marek Pająk, Elżbieta Kamysz, Marcin Banach, Wojciech Michał Jankowski, Aleksandra Tarasiuk-Zawadzka, Jakub Fichna, Magdalena Woźniczka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present work describes the protolytic properties and in vitro biological activity of spinorphin (Leu-Val-Val-Tyr-Pro-Trp-Thr) and three spinorphin derivatives containing butyric acid residue: Butyryl-Lys-Lys-Leu-Leu-Val-Tyr-Pro-Trp-Thr, Butyryl-Lys-Lys-Leu-Val-Val-Tyr-Pro-Trp-Thr (butyric acid bound to the α-amino group of lysine), Lys(Butyryl)-Lys-Leu-Val-Val-Tyr-Pro-Trp-Thr (butyric acid bound to the ε-amino group of lysine) in an aqueous solution. The overall protonation constants and the stepwise dissociation constants of the ligands studied were calculated by the potentiometric method. The percentage of each species formed was estimated from the species distribution curves as a function of pH. The biological activity of all tested compounds was characterized in vitro, in the neutral red uptake and Griess assay tests in RAW264.7 macrophage cell line. The three protonation constants for spinorphin and four for its derivatives suggest that metal ions may bind to these peptides and form complexes by coordination with the functional groups of the respective amino acid residues. In vitro biological activity tests suggest that two peptides deserve attention for their potential anti-inflammatory role.Keywords: Spinorphin, Potentiometry, Protonation constant, Neutral red uptake, Griess assay

Introduction

Anatomical and physiological research has revealed the existence of endogenous pain modulation mechanisms that play a crucial role in the development of chronic pain conditions. It has been proposed that before pain is fully perceived, nociceptive signals are modulated by both excitatory and inhibitory mechanisms at different levels within the central nervous system (CNS) (Vincenot et al. 2024; Marchand 2008). In addition to serotonin and norepinephrine, the primary neurotransmitters involved in the intrinsic inhibition of pain signaling are endogenous opioid-like compounds such as dynorphins, beta-endorphins and enkephalins (Voscopoulos and Lema 2010; Mason 2005; Argoff 2011).

Spinorphin (Leu-Val-Val-Tyr-Pro-Trp-Thr), an endogenous inhibitor of enkephalin-degrading enzymes, has been isolated from bovine spinal cord (Hazato 1994; Nishimura and Hazato 1993a). It inhibits several enzymes including aminopeptidase, dipeptidyl aminopeptidase III and angiotensin converting enzyme. Spinorphin has been reported to have antinociceptive properties as well as inhibitory effects on electrically induced contractions in mouse vas deferens and guinea pig ileum (Yamazaki et al. 2001; Nishimura and Hazato 1993b). It has also been shown to have anti-inflammatory activity (Yamamoto et al. 1997, 1998). Spinorphin is related to the beta chain of hemoglobin, similar to LVV-hemorphin-6 and valorphin (Glämsta et al. 1991; Erchegyi et al. 1992). Human polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs) mediate several inflammatory responses, including chemotaxis, O−2 generation and exocytosis, all of which are triggered by N-formylmethionyl-leucyl-phenylalanine (FMLP). Spinorphin inhibits PMN function by blocking the binding of FMLP to its receptor on PMNs (Christiansen 1990). In addition, intracerebroventricular administration of spinorphin has shown analgesic effects in the tail-pinch procedure, possibly due to its inhibition of enkephalin-degrading enzymes in the brain (Nishimura et al. 1993).

Butyric acid (butanoic acid, C4) is a short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) and a vital source of energy for colon cells, promoting their growth and differentiation (Pituch et al. 2013). Despite the fact that its role in the gastrointestinal tract has been the focus of research for many years, recent studies suggest that it still has significant potential (Gerunova et al. 2024). Butyric acid occurs naturally in foods such as butter, hard cheese, milk, yoghurt, cream and various fermented products such as sauerkraut, pickles and fermented soy. Along with other SCFAs, butyric acid is produced in the human colon, with acetic, propionic and butyric acids making up approximately 83% of the total SCFAs in the mammalian colon (Rechkemmer et al. 1988; Topping and Clifton 2001). Butanoic acid is mainly produced by the breakdown of pentoses found in whole grains (e.g. wheat bran, whole meal bread, pasta and brown rice), legumes, vegetables and fruit (Roy et al. 2006). The following bacteria are known to produce butyric acid Clostridium spp, Eubacterium spp, Fusobacterium spp, Butyrivibrio spp, Megasphaera elsdenii, Mitsuokella multiacida, Roseburia intestinalis, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii and Eubacterium hallii (Hold et al. 2003). In healthy individuals, the colon produces between 300 and 400 mmol of SCFAs daily, with the physiological concentration of butyric acid in the intestinal contents ranging from 1 to 10 mmol/L (Roediger 1980). SCFAs are rapidly absorbed in the colon, with butyric acid being absorbed primarily in the ascending colon. It is completely metabolized within the colonic epithelial cells, with only a small fraction entering the bloodstream (Vanhoutvin et al. 2009).

As mentioned above, spinorphin exhibits a wide range of biological activities but remains poorly understood, with relatively few synthetic analogues. Therefore, the aim of this study was to characterize the protolytic properties of three novel spinorphin analogues: (P1) Butyryl-Lys-Lys-Leu-Leu-Val-Tyr-Pro-Trp-Thr, (P2) Butyryl-Lys-Lys-Leu-Val-Val-Tyr-Pro-Trp-Thr (butyric acid attached to the α-amino group of lysine), (P3) Lys(Butyryl)-Lys-Leu-Val-Val-Tyr-Pro-Trp-Thr (butyric acid attached to the ε-amino group of lysine) and (P4) spinorphin (Fig. 1) in aqueous solution using potentiometric techniques. Moreover, the biological effect of (P1)–(P4) was characterized in vitro, in the neutral red uptake and Griess assay tests in RAW264.7 macrophage cell line. A peptide that is stimulated by pH changes can act as a carrier that is sensitive to the pH of the environment. For instance, poly (L-lysine) has been shown to form pH-sensitive peptide nanocarriers with a variety of structures. It is evident that this property also promotes enhanced interactions with cell membranes and receptors. The presence of lysine further increases the solubility of the peptide due to its positive charge under physiological conditions. Furthermore, the lysine amino group provides a convenient site for bioconjugation, for example with drug carriers, which may result in greater stability and bioavailability of the peptide (Guo et al. 2024).

Materials and methods

Peptide synthesis and purification

Spinorphin and its analogues (Table 1) were obtained manually by the solid-phase method: on a 2-chlorotrityl chloride resin (loading 0.3–0.9 mmol/g, 1% DVB, 100–200 mesh, Orpegen Peptide Chemicals GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany) using 9-fluorenylmethoxycarbonyl (Fmoc) chemistry. N-α-Fmoc-protected amino acids and Boc-Lys(Fmoc)-OH used for the solid-phase synthesis were obtained from Iris Biotech GmbH (Marktredwitz, Germany). The following amino acid derivatives were used: Fmoc-Lys(Boc)-OH, Fmoc-Thr(tBu), Fmoc-Trp(Boc)-OH, Fmoc-Leu-OH, Fmoc-Tyr(tBu)-OH, Fmoc-Val-OH, Fmoc-Pro-OH. In addition, in the synthesis of analogue (P3) Boc-Lys(Fmoc)-OH was also used.

Peptide chains were elongated in consecutive cycles of deprotection and coupling. Deprotection was performed with 20% (v/v) piperidine (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) in N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF; Avantor Performance Materials Poland SA, Gliwice, Poland), whereas the chain elongation was achieved with equimolar mixtures of a protected amino acid derivative, N,Nʹ-diisopropylcarbodiimide (DIC; Carbolution Chemicals GmbH, St. Ingbert, Germany) and Oxyma Pure (Carbolution Chemicals GmbH, St. Ingbert, Germany). Three equivalents (in respect to resin reactive groups) of these reagents were used. Coupling reactions of butyric acid (Aldrich, St. Louise, MO, USA) with N-terminal part of molecules were performed using the same method as that used for protected amino acids.

The peptides were cleaved from the resin using a mixture of trifluoroacetic acid (TFA; Apollo Scientific, Denton, UK), triisopropylsilane (TIS; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louise, MO, USA), phenol (Pol-Aura, Gdansk, Poland) and water (94:2.5:1:2.5; v/v/v/v). Other details of the synthesis were the same as for spinorphin described earlier (Kamysz et al. 2016).

Finally, the peptides were purified by solid-phase extraction (SPE) on Isolute ™ SPE columns (flash, C18, 25 mL) (Kamysz et al. 2004). The eluates were fractionated and the purity of the peptides was determined on reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC) on a Knauer system controlled by an LP-chrom data system with a Phenomenex Gemini-NX C18 column (4.6 × 150 mm, 110 Å pore size, 5 µm particle size) and UV detection was carried out at 214 nm. The solvent systems used were: 0.1% aqueous TFA (A) and 0.1% TFA in acetonitrile (Iris Biotech GmbH, Marktredwitz, Germany) (B).

The potentiometric method

The carbonate-free 0.1 M NaOH solution was purchased from J.T. Baker (Radnor, PA, USA). The perchloric acid solution from Laborchemie Apolda (Apolda, Germany) was standardized by titrations with a NaOH. The standard solution of sodium perchlorate monohydrate (Laborchemie Apolda, Germany) was used to adjust the ionic medium. High purity argon (Linde, Dublin, Ireland) was used. All solutions were prepared in double-distilled water.

Potentiometric titrations have been determined by using an automatic titrator system Titrando 905 (Metrohm, Herisau, Appenzell Ausserrhoden, Switzerland). A combined polymer microelectrode InLab Semi-Micro (Mettler Toledo) was calibrated with NaOH on the hydrogen ion concentration (Irving et al. 1967). The solution of the acid was calibrated alkalimetrically and determined by the Gran method (Gans and O’Sullivan 2000; Gamov et al. 2022). The electrode was free of KCl, which allowed the investigators to work in perchlorate medium (avoiding the formation of poorly soluble KClO4). The measurements were carried out in a thermostatted vessel at a constant temperature of 25.0 ± 0.1 °C and an ionic strength of 0.1 M (NaClO4). All titrations were carried out in aqueous solutions in 4 mL samples. A stream of pure argon was passed over the surface of the sample to remove carbon dioxide.

The system was tested at total ligands concentrations of 1.0 × 10–3 M and 2.0 × 10–3 M over the entire pH range (2.00–12.00). All experiments were performed in triplicate with very similar reproducibility. The fitting procedure using Hyperquad 2013 software allowed the calculation of the concentration formation constants according to the formula: βlh = [LlHh]/[[L]l[H]h. The goodness of fit was checked by the objective function U = åi=1,m Wi ri2, where W is the weight, and r is a residual (equal to the difference between observed and calculated EMF values), m—number of experimental points, n—number of refined parameters. The weighting factor Wi is defined as the reciprocal of the estimated variance of the measurements, dependent on the estimated variances of the EMF and volume readings. The value of the normalized sum of squared residuals, d = U/(m – n) was compared with the c2 (chi-squared) test of randomness at a number of degrees of freedom equal to m – n (Gans et al. 1996).

Graphical simulations of speciation diagrams based on the calculated constants were generated using HySS 2009 (Alderighi et al. 1999).

In vitro experiments

Reagents

Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM) was purchased from Gibco (Life Technologies, Warsaw, Poland) and used as the base medium for cell culture. Reagents for enriching the culture medium, including HEPES, sodium pyruvate, alanyl-glutamine, bovine calf serum (BCS), and penicillin/streptomycin, were obtained from Merck Life Science (Poznań, Poland). The reagents required for the Neutral Red Uptake (NRU) assay, including Neutral Red (NR) solution, and the Griess assay, such as Griess reagent and dimethylsulphoxide (DMSO), were also sourced from Merck Life Science.

Cell culture

The study employed the RAW264.7 macrophage cell line (ATCC; ref: TIB-71) derived from a male mouse tumor. Macrophages were maintained in sterile 250 mL culture flasks with a growth area of 75 cm2. The cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with L-glutamine, glucose, sodium pyruvate, HEPES, alanyl-glutamine, penicillin/streptomycin, and 10% BCS. The cultures were incubated under controlled conditions at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ and 95% humidity. The culture medium was replaced every 48–72 h. Cell passaging was performed when cell confluence reached approximately 80%.

NRU assay

The NRU assay was used to evaluate the cytotoxicity of the tested compounds on RAW264.7 cells. Peptides (P1)–(P4) were tested at concentrations of 1 × 10–6, 1 × 10–7, 1 × 10–8, and 1 × 10–9 g/mL, with DMSO included at 1 × 10–3 g/mL. RAW264.7 cells were seeded into 96-well plates (20,000 cells/well, 200 µL/well) with controls containing only medium without test compounds or DMSO. Each condition was tested in six technical and two biological replicates (Fig. 2). After 48 h of incubation, the medium was replaced with 100 µL of NR solution (5 × 10–5 g/mL) and incubated for 1 h. Following NR solution removal and washing with PBS, the dye was eluted using 100 µL of a solution containing 10% acetic acid, 40% ethanol, and 50% water. Absorbance was measured at 540 nm using an iMark™ spectrophotometer (BioRad, Warsaw, Poland).

Schematic representation of a sample plate layout for the NRU assay

Griess assay

The Griess assay was used to determine nitrite (NO₂⁻) levels in cell culture supernatants. RAW264.7 cells were seeded into 96-well plates (20,000 cells/well, 200 µL/well) and treated with four peptides (P1), (P2), (P3), (P4) at concentrations of 1 × 10⁻⁶, 1 × 10⁻⁷, 1 × 10⁻⁸, and 1 × 10⁻⁹ g/mL dissolved in DMSO (1 × 10⁻3 g/mL). Cells were stimulated with LPS (1 × 10⁻⁶ g/mL), with reference groups including unstimulated cells and those treated with LPS and budesonide. Six technical and two biological replicates were performed for each condition (Fig. 3). After 24 h, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing the appropriate treatments. Following another 24-h incubation, supernatants were collected, and 100 µL of Griess reagent (4 × 10⁻2 g/mL) was added to each well. After a 15-min incubation at room temperature in the dark, absorbance was measured at 540 nm using an iMark™ spectrophotometer.

Schematic representation of a sample plate layout for the Griess assay

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted utilizing GraphPad Prism 10.4.1. Initially, the Grubbs test was performed for a significance level of α = 0.05 to identify outliers, followed by the Shapiro–Wilk test to assess the normality of the distribution.

The outcomes of the Griess assay exhibited a distribution that deviated from normality, necessitating the application of the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by the Dunn's multiple comparisons test as a post-hoc analysis. Conversely, the results of the NRU assay manifested a normal distribution, though they did not demonstrate homogeneity of variance. Consequently, the Welch ANOVA and Dunnett's t3 test were employed.

The results are presented below and the deprotonation process of each functional group is described for the following ligands:

(P4) Leu-Val-Val-Tyr-Pro-Trp-Thr

The first dissociation constant, equal to 2.21, is assigned to the deprotonation of the carboxyl group of threonine ([LH3]+ → [LH2], Fig. 1). Literature value for L-(α)-threonine alone is 2.20 (Günther et al. 2006). The metal ions coordinate with threonine via the carboxylate group and the amino group, forming a chelate ring. Pettit and Swash (1976) also found a monoprotonated copper-threonine complex M(HL)2+ by titration at low pH. Threonine also contains a hydroxy group whose dissociation in aqueous solution occurs well beyond the basic limit of the pH range commonly investigated. No dissociation can be detected even at high ligand concentrations. The contribution of the hydroxyl group to metal complexation is controversial. Deprotonation of the hydroxyl group and/or its interaction with the central metal atom to form a more membered chelate ring seems to play a decreasing role above pH 8. No significant stereoselective effect was found in the formation of ML2 Cu(II)-threonine complexes on the basis of enthalpy determinations. However, a slight effect of this kind was observed in the formation of ML2H-2 after deprotonation of the hydroxyl group (Berthon 1995; Sharma 1967). The [LH3]+ species is 63% present in solution at pH ≤ 2.0 and disappears at pH ~ 5.0 (Fig. 4).

The second dissociation constant, equal to 9.97, is assigned to the deprotonation of the amino group of leucine ([LH2] → [LH]−, Fig. 1). Literature value for L-(α)-leucine alone is 9.57 (Burger 1990). Research has shown that leucine forms stronger complexes with Cu(II) and Ni(II) ions under alkaline conditions, where the amine group is deprotonated. In acidic environments, where the amine group remains protonated, leucine forms weaker complexes or may not coordinate effectively at all. Leucine complexes are involved in a wide range of biological processes, including protein–protein interactions, enzyme activity and signaling pathways that are critical for metabolism, muscle health and neurological function. One notable example is leucine's activation of the mechanistic target of rapamycin complex (mTORC1), a critical regulator of cell growth, protein synthesis and autophagy. Leucine directly interacts with sestrin, a leucine-sensing protein, releasing its inhibition of mTORC1. This interaction plays an important role in controlling cellular responses to nutrient availability (Saxton and Sabatini 2017). In addition, leucine forms complexes with α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) that support muscle metabolism and tissue repair. These complexes are key components of branched-chain amino acid metabolism and contribute to energy production, particularly during periods of intense exercise (Garlick 2005). Leucine also forms complexes with zinc in pancreatic β-cells, influencing insulin secretion. Zinc is essential for the stabilization and storage of insulin, and leucine promotes insulin release by modulating ion channels and the cellular secretory machinery (Kolahian et al. 2015). The [LH2] species is present in solution from pH < 2.0 to pH 11.5 (maximum is practically 100% at pH 5.0–7.00).

The third dissociation constant, equal to 10.18, is assigned to the deprotonation of the hydroxyl group of tyrosine ([LH]− → [L]2−, Fig. 1). Literature value for L-(α)-tyrosine alone is 10.11 (Burger 1990). The phenolic hydroxyl of tyrosine is significantly more acidic than the aliphatic hydroxyls of serine or threonine and has a pKa of about 9.8 in polypeptides (as with all ionizable groups, the exact pKa is highly dependent on the environment within the protein). The hydroxyl group of the tyrosine residues in the peptides has a very similar pKa value to the hydroxyl group of free tyrosine and is also expected to undergo carbamylation. Carbamylation of amino and tyrosine hydroxyl groups can therefore be used to study the 'active sites' of enzymes and other proteins. In addition, carbamylation is a useful reaction for reversibly blocking tyrosine hydroxyl groups, as the O-carbamyl substituent is readily removed by hydrolysis at neutral and alkaline pH values (Smyth 1967; Canel et al. 2006). The [[LH]− species is present in solution in the pH range 7.0–12.0 and its maximum ~ 38% at pH 10.00 (Fig. 4). The fully deprotonated [L]2− species begins to dominate in a highly alkaline solution (Fig. 4).

(P1) Butyryl-Lys-Lys-Leu-Leu-Val-Tyr-Pro-Trp-Thr

The first dissociation constant, equal to 2.43, is assigned to the deprotonation of the carboxyl group of threonine ([LH4]2+ → [LH3]+, Fig. 1). The [LH4]2+ species is 75% present in solution at pH ≤ 2.0 and disappears at pH ~ 5.5 (Fig. 4). The second dissociation constant, equal to 9.75, is assigned to the deprotonation of the hydroxyl group of tyrosine ([LH3]+ → [LH2], Fig. 1). The [LH3]+ species is present in solution in the pH range 2.0–11.0 and its maximum is practically 100% at pH 5.0–7.0 (Fig. 4). The third and fourth dissociation constants, equal to 10.00 and 10.37, respectively, are attributed to the deprotonation of two ε-amino groups from two lysines ([LH2] → [LH]− and [LH]− → [L]2−, Fig. 1). Literature value for L-(α)-lysine alone is 10.68 (Burger 1990) The protonation steps for Lys at the ε- and α-amino groups are not completely separated. In terms of first and second protonation, Lys with deprotonated α-amino and protonated ε-amino groups predominate over Lys with protonated α-amino and deprotonated ε-amino groups. Lysine binds metal ions via the α-amino and α-carboxylate groups in the glycine mode to form complexes of monoprotonated Lys, M(HL)n, in acid-neutral solution. The ε-amino group is about 10 times more easily deprotonated than the guanidinium group of Arg, but does not participate in metal binding in solution because chelation through the two amino groups would result in an unstable eight-membered ring. The log K1 value (∼11) of the proton complexes is slightly lower than that of Arg, but high enough to maintain the positive charge in proteins. The ε-amino group of metal-coordinated Lys, as in M(HL)2, dissociates to give ML(HL) and ML2 at pH > 9. Fully deprotonated Lys, coordinates to metal ions essentially as a substituted Gly (Yamauchi and Odani 1996; Pająk et al. 2023a, b). The [LH2] species is present in solution in the pH range 7.0–11.5 (maximum ∼ 35% at pH 9.8), while the [LH]− species is present from pH 8.5 to pH > 12.0 with a maximum of ∼ 40% at pH 10.4. The fully deprotonated [L]2− species begins to dominate in a highly alkaline solution (Fig. 4).

(P2) Butyryl-Lys-Lys-Leu-Val-Val-Tyr-Pro-Trp-Thr

The first dissociation constant, equal to 2.65, is assigned to the deprotonation of the carboxyl group of threonine ([LH4]2+ → [LH3]+, Fig. 1). The [LH4]2+ species is 80% present in solution at pH ≤ 2.0 and disappears at pH ~ 5.5 (Fig. 4). The second dissociation constant, equal to 9.60, is assigned to the deprotonation of the hydroxyl group of tyrosine ([LH3]+ → [LH2], Fig. 1). The [LH3]+ species is present in solution in the pH range 2.0–11.0 and its maximum is practically 100% at pH 5.0–7.0 (Fig. 4). The third and fourth dissociation constants, equal to 10.34 and 10.42, respectively, are attributed to the deprotonation of two ε-amino groups from two lysines ([LH2] → [LH]− and [LH]− → [L]2−, Fig. 1). The [LH2] species is present in solution in the pH range 6.5–11.5 (maximum ∼ 50% at pH 9.8), while the [LH]− species is present from pH 8.5 to pH > 12.0 with a maximum of ∼ 35% at pH 10.5. The fully deprotonated [L]2− species begins to dominate in a highly alkaline solution (Fig. 4).

(P3) Lys(Butyryl)-Lys-Leu-Val-Val-Tyr-Pro-Trp-Thr

The first dissociation constant, equal to 2.26, is assigned to the deprotonation of the carboxyl group of threonine ([LH4]2+ → [LH3]+, Fig. 1). The [LH4]2+ species is 65% present in solution at pH ≤ 2.0 and disappears at pH ~ 5.0 (Fig. 4). The second dissociation constant, equal to 9.50, is assigned to the deprotonation of the α-amino group of lysine ([LH3]+ → [LH2], Fig. 1). Literature value for L-(α)-lysine alone is 9.12 (Burger 1990). The [LH3]+ species is present in solution in the pH range 2.0–11.0 and its maximum is practically 100% at pH 5.0–7.0 (Fig. 4). The third dissociation constant, equal to 9.89, is assigned to the deprotonation of the hydroxyl group of tyrosine ([LH2] → [LH]−, Fig. 1). The [LH]2 species is present in solution in the pH range 6.5–11.5 (maximum ∼ 42% at pH 9.5) (Fig. 4). The fourth dissociation constant, equal to 10.26, is assigned to the deprotonation of the ε-amino group of lysine ([LH]− → [L]2−, Fig. 1). The [LH]− species is present in solution from pH 8.2 to pH > 12.0 with a maximum of ∼ 40% at pH 10.4. (Fig. 4). The fully deprotonated [L]2− species begins to dominate in a highly alkaline solution (Fig. 4).

NRU assay

The NRU assay showed that peptides (P1)-(P4) were generally non-toxic towards RAW264.7 macrophages. Consequently, a statistically significant decrease in macrophage viability was observed only for (P4) at the concentration of 1 × 10–9 g/mL and was approximately 5.55% relative to the controls. This indicates a very low cytotoxicity of this solution. The results are shown in Fig. 5.

Griess assay

In order to evaluate the anti-inflammatory properties of the peptides under investigation, the Griess test was employed. For (P2) and (P3) we observed a tendency to alleviate inflammatory response in LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages; noteworthy, the effect of concentration-dependent for (P3) and reverse concentration-dependent for (P2). However, none of the results obtained achieved statistical significance. The coupling of butyric acid to spinorphin increases its activity. In the case of the (P2) and (P3) analogues, regardless of which lysine amino group (α or ε) the butyric acid is attached to, they both have the tendency to exhibit anti-inflammatory properties. In contrast, the attachment of butyric acid to the N-terminus of spinorphin via a Lys-Lys linker, with a concomitant conversion of valine to leucine, leads to a loss of anti-inflammatory activity of the ligand (P1). The results are illustrated in Fig. 6.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the tested peptides show potential as effective and specific ligands for various metal ions due to their abundance of potential donor atoms. The three protonation constants for spinorphin and four for its derivatives suggest that metal ions can bind to these peptides and form complexes by coordination with the functional groups of the respective amino acid residues. Interactions between these amino acid residues can promote specific peptide conformations, which can significantly influence the coordination equilibria between the metal ions and the peptides, both from a thermodynamic and structural point of view (Barnea 1989; Meng et al. 2005; Gerega et al. 1988; Kozlowski et al. 1990; Kochman et al. 1992). The in vitro testing of biological activity of spinorphin and its novel derivatives suggested that peptides (P2) and (P3) deserve attention for their potential anti-inflammatory role and indicated the need for further comprehensive investigation.
