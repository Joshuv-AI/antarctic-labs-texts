‘Poverty is the biggest barrier’: stakeholder perspectives on social determinants of malaria in Wakiso District, Uganda
Authors: Edwinah Atusingwize, David Musoke, Kevin Deane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite significant resources invested in malaria control interventions for several years, the disease remains a persistent health challenge impacting millions of people especially children and pregnant women in the lower income regions of the world. Progress towards eliminating malaria has stagnated, in part due to the limited consideration of the social determinants of the disease such as poverty. We conducted a qualitative study exploring stakeholder perspectives on social determinants of malaria including lived experiences with the disease in Wakiso, one of the districts with high malaria cases in Uganda.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional study using fourteen key informant interviews at community, health facility, district and national levels, 10 focus group discussions among community members and community health workers, and 11 in-depth interviews with household heads. Data collection was conducted in English and Luganda (local language), transcribed, coded using Atlas.ti. (version 6) and Nvivo (version 14) and analysed using a thematic approach. This paper focuses on the perspectives on poverty as a social determinant of malaria.ResultsThe participants linked poverty to malaria through five themes: poor housing conditions; limited access to malaria control services; limited water and sanitation-related exposures; occupational and psychological exposures; and heavy economic burden (consequences) of malaria. The themes are presented under various sub-themes which separately highlight in detail the different pathways of the malaria-poverty relationship. These include: substandard housing structures enabling mosquito exposure; building close to mosquito breeding grounds; homelessness and street life; inadequate malaria prevention and treatment services; limited access to water supply; poor sanitation and waste management practices; occupational exposures; social and psychological constraints; high treatment costs; undernutrition causing low immunity; and loss of income. These perspectives emphasize a cyclic poverty-malaria relationship through the high treatment costs and loss of income due to illness or livelihood disruption.ConclusionMalaria is rooted in economic inequalities suggesting that improvement in malaria control interventions through policies and frameworks that address poverty as a consequence, and driver of the disease should be prioritised. The study underscores the need for integrated malaria control strategies that address vulnerabilities related to poverty in all its dimensions—economic, environmental and psychosocial.

Background

Malaria continues to be a major health challenge particularly in low-income settings around the world. Progress has stagnated over recent years, especially with respect to elimination of malaria [1] pointing to a likely failure to meet the 2030 target of reducing mortality and incidence cases by at least 90% [2], unless significant changes are made over the next 4 years. Globally, 282 million cases were reported in 2024 [1], with Africa accounting for 95% (translating to 266–268 million cases) despite advances in malaria control interventions in the region [1, 3]. For instance, of the 3.1 billion insecticide treated nets supplied between 2004 and 2023 globally, 87% (2.7 billion) were delivered to Sub-Saharan Africa, one of the poorest regions that remains significantly affected [4]. Uganda, one of the five African countries that share half of the global malaria burden (the others being Nigeria, Democratic Republic of Congo, Ethiopia, and Mozambique) [1, 5], accounted for 4.8% of the malaria cases globally in 2024 [1, 4]. The country experiences high transmission of malaria all year round, but seasonal outbreaks also occur in some parts of the country. In 2022, the country experienced a malaria epidemic which affected 64 (out of 146) districts, with over 3.3 million excess cases and nearly 150,000 excess health facility admissions [6]. Additionally, districts in Uganda with intensive interventions including Indoor Residual Spraying (IRS) and Integrated Community Case Management experienced higher malaria epidemic levels than their counterparts [6].

Besides highlighting stagnation in malaria cases and death rates since 2015, the world malaria reports confirm that the disease continues to disproportionately affect the poorest populations, with economic vulnerability being the key driver of the burden [1, 4]. This aligns with evidence that malaria is a disease of poverty—both driven by poverty and with a heavy impact on the poorest communities [7–11]. Some of the ways that poverty influences malaria are through poor housing and nutritional status [9, 10], demonstrating that investments for economic developments could be effective malaria control interventions [12–14]. For instance, improved housing facilitated malaria control and cessation of indigenous transmission by reducing the reproduction number below sustainable levels in Sri Lanka [15] and offered 47% lower odds of malaria infection compared to traditional houses in Sub-Saharan Africa [13]. Also of importance is the evidence from developed regions such as Europe, China, and USA demonstrating that socio-economic improvements contributed to the elimination of malaria [16–18]. Similarly, a study in Uganda indicated that wealthier households were more likely to own and use malaria preventive tools [26]. However, one of the gaps in malaria control efforts is lack of focus on the social determinants approach including addressing poverty [19–21], affecting majority of African countries [22].

In Uganda, despite a general increase in national wealth levels in recent years, poverty remains a major concern across its regions. Over the past decade, Uganda’s poverty levels have fluctuated between 18 and 22%, reflecting limited progress since 2012/13. Indeed, poverty was estimated at 21.4% in 2016/17, with a decline to 18.7% in 2019 and a rise during the COVID‑19 period to 21.9% [23]. By 2024, an estimated 16.1% of Ugandans lived below the national poverty line according to the Uganda National Household Survey Report [24]. Nonetheless, while malaria control is one of the top priorities as reflected by Uganda’s Parliamentary Forum for Malaria formed in 2018 and the Malaria Reduction and Elimination Strategic Plans [25], poverty remains a neglected issue in malaria interventions in the country. In particular, although the national malaria control strategy briefly mentions poverty as one of the main drivers, [25], there is a lack of evidence on efforts to address poverty issues as part of the programme. Uganda’s 2021–25 malaria strategy covers interventions such as the Integrated Community Case Management and chemoprevention, IRS and distribution of ITNs [6, 25, 26], while efforts to improve net use face challenges at regional and district levels [6]. An evaluation of the 2020–2021 national mass distribution campaign showed that most households (93.4%) owned at least 1 ITN, although only 56.8% were adequately covered by the nets calling for a better understanding of the drivers of ITN use within households [26]. Addressing such challenges requires a focus on social determinants including the persistent poverty. However, poverty targeted malaria interventions are lacking in Uganda and Africa although the need to address social determinants of health is mentioned within the Africa Health Strategy 2016–2030 to reduce malaria burden by 2030 [27].

Despite calls for increased investment in interventions to support socioeconomic development which is both a consequence and driver of the disease, malaria control largely remains behavioural and biomedical [7, 18, 28–30]. Furthermore, the existing malaria—poverty literature has primarily focused on poor quality housing and the distribution and use of bed nets, with majority of the evidence pointing to lack of research on the malaria – poverty pathways [31, 32]. There is hardly any literature that comprehensively explores poverty and its influence on the malaria burden in Uganda. The explicit application of a poverty-focused approach to malaria control remains unexplored, underscoring the need for comprehensive strategies that contextualises evidence with deeper, nuanced perspectives [7, 28, 29].

The overall aim of the study was to explore stakeholder perspectives on the social determinants of malaria in Uganda. This aim was motivated by the limited engagement with the social determinants in malaria control efforts and the resulting stagnation of malaria outcomes in many low- and middle-income settings [7, 28, 29]. Alongside the role of engaging in livelihoods that increase exposure to mosquitoes and contribute to the creation of new breeding sites that has been reported elsewhere [33], another key social determinant that our participants consistently highlighted was the role of poverty. Therefore, this paper focuses on the relationship between malaria and poverty which is currently under-explored. This focus deepens and extends our analysis of the social determinants of malaria.

Methods

Study area and setting

The study was conducted in Busiro south health sub-district of Wakiso district, located in central Uganda. The district neighbours Kampala, Uganda’s capital city. One of the major health concerns in Wakiso district is malaria with high a overdiagnosis rates [34, 35]. The Uganda Ministry of Health reported 2996, 3387, and 2518 confirmed malaria cases in Wakiso in the weeks of 12–18 February, 26 February–2 March, and 18–20 December, respectively in 2024 which highlights the district’ consistent appearance among Uganda’s ten highest burden districts (out of 146 districts) [36]. Wakiso district is characterized by warm temperatures (mean annual temperature of approximately 20.8 °C), and annual rainfall of approximately 1834 mm (72.2 inches) with bi-annual seasonal peaks typically occurring between February–May and September–November [37]. These conditions create ideal breeding environments for Anopheles mosquitoes, the primary malaria vector in Uganda [38].

The population of Busiro south health sub-district is estimated at 390,754 people living across Kajjansi, Kasanje, and Katabi town councils, and Bussi sub county [39]. The health sub-district has one health centre IV, three health centre IIIs and four health centre IIs, and households engaged in several commercial businesses, agriculture including poultry and livestock farming activities. Overall, the sub-district was selected as one of the areas with a mix of rural, urban and peri-urban settings and livelihoods ranging from typical urban activities to rural farming and fishing offering a representative population in Uganda.

Design and participants

This was a cross-sectional study using In-depth Interviews (IDI); Focus Group Discussions (FGDs); and Key Informant Interviews (KIIs), with participants purposively selected across the rural and urban settings of the study area. A total of 10 FGDs were conducted among community members and community health workers (CHWs), mostly known as village health teams (VHTs), to assess their perception of social determinants of malaria including its prevention. Putting age and gender into consideration to support equitable representation, the FGDs (each with 6–8 members) were organized in categories of youth (males mixed with females aged 18–35 years) and adults (36–80 years) in groups of males only, females only, mixed adults (males mixed with females), as well as VHTs only (males mixed with females). The categories were further divided into rural or urban setting (Table 1). Community members were included in FGDs regardless of whether they had suffered from malaria or not to solicit for their general perceptions. Furthermore, eleven IDIs were conducted with household heads to explore individual experiences with malaria and its prevention efforts in the community. The IDIs only involved households (both male and female headed) that had an experience of suffering or losing a member to the disease in recent years. In addition to FGDs and IDIs, 14 KIIs including among local leaders (such as the local council chairpersons and religious leaders), district health officials, Ministry of Health officials, health practitioners, non-governmental organisation staff, and other stakeholders in malaria prevention were conducted. The selection of key informants was purposive, primarily based on individual responsibilities and tasks related to malaria prevention programmes. Altogether, the selection of study participants was conducted by the first author (EA) in consultation with the community health workers and local council leaders. Using multiple methods (IDIs, FGDs and KIIs) provided an opportunity to collect various perspectives necessary for comprehensive understanding of poverty as a social determinant of malaria.

Data collection

This paper uses data that was collected in a study on social determinants of malaria which included poverty. Research assistants at graduate level with a background in public health, as well as knowledge and experience in conducting qualitative research collected the data using KII, FGDs and IDI guides. The guides were developed by the research team based on existing literature [7, 28, 29, 40]. The research assistants were trained on the study objectives and research ethics for 2 days during which they were re-oriented on the social determinants of health.

The social determinants of health framework by Dahlgren-Whitehead [66] (Fig. 1) was printed and used during interviews and discussion to support explanation of the topic to the participants. All the FGDs and IDIs were conducted in Luganda (the local language) which was also used for a few KIIs that did not prefer speaking English. The interviews were 30–60 min long while the FGDs lasted 1.5–2 h. FGDs were conducted at schools, churches, and other community spaces such as parish halls. IDIs were conducted at each of the participant’s households while KIIs were interviewed in their offices or homes depending on individual preferences. The study based on the principle of inclusion and consultation with a wide range of stakeholders and community members to ensure that all voices were included. A total of 100 individuals (52% female, 48% male) participated in the study (Table 1). More details on the demographics including groups of participants have been described in a separate paper [33] focused specifically on understanding how livelihood systems contribute to malaria. In contrast, the current study offers an in‑depth exploration of the mechanisms through which poverty influences malaria vulnerability.

Data analysis

All the KIIs, IDI and FGDs were audio recorded, transcribed verbatim and translated to English (for those in Luganda). All transcripts were coded independently by two authors (EA and KD) after initially reading the transcripts several times to familiarize themselves with the data. Each author used their own preferred software (Atlas ti (version 6) and NVivo (version 14) respectively. Analysis was done by thematic analysis [41]. Codes were discussed by all authors (EA, KD and DM), and coding continued in an iterative process until the final codes were reached. The connected codes were then grouped to form sub-themes that were later categorised to form themes. The initial findings were presented at a stakeholder workshop held in Kampala in August 2024 where 30 experts together with representatives of the study participants validated results. Their feedback confirmed that our analysis was well conducted and resonated with their understanding of the poverty and malaria link in the area.While the social determinants of health framework by Dahlgren-Whitehead [66] guided the data collection process, it was not applied in analysis and presentation of findings which took an inductive approach allowing themes to emerge from the data beyond any framework.

Reflexivity statement

This study was conducted by a multidisciplinary team with expertise ranging from environmental health, public health, social sciences, and community based participatory research. This diversity shaped how we approached the research from study design through analysis and interpretation of findings (Table. 2). The Ugandan researchers (DM, EA) have extensive work experiences in malaria research in addition to their lived reality in communities impacted by the disease, which enriched their understanding of local contexts. Their understanding of culture including language in Wakiso district supported nuanced engagement with participants and aided interpretation of expressions emerging from all discussions and interviews. We note that although DM has long-term work experience in the study area, he was not physically involved in field-based activities. EA together with the trained research assistants with extensive qualitative experience led all community interactions including interviews and focus group discussions. Recognizing potential gender influences, we organized gender and age specific groups that encouraged more open dialogue. For similar gender considerations, research assistants included both males and females familiar with cultural dynamics in Wakiso which also supported respectful engagement.

The long-standing research presence of Makerere University School of Public Health in the study area may have shaped how participants perceived the study, potentially signaling expectations for action, but also in ensuring mutual and trusted engagement. On the other hand, the UK based researcher (KD) has a long-standing collaborative relationship with DM, which is also critical for trust and credible engagement with the Ugandan team. Like DM, KD did not participate in data collection which excluded any perceived foreign influence on participants’ engagement.

Throughout the project, we engaged in continuous reflection on our positionalities as individuals and as a team. EA and DM, while as Ugandan citizens and malaria researchers bring locally contextualized insights, there are also potential biases they bring based on their desired health improvement in these communities. Regular in person and virtual team meetings through the process allowed us to interrogate the emerging themes during analysis and reach consensus in every step of this study. This iterative process together with a validation workshop including some of the study participants and other stakeholders strengthened the credibility and trustworthiness of the findings. To support transferability, we present findings with examples of actual narratives from participants that highlight the mechanisms linking poverty and malaria, providing opportunities for readers to assess results’ relevance to other contexts.

Results

The study results are categorized into five themes: poor housing conditions; limited access to malaria control services; limited water and sanitation-related exposures; occupational and psychological exposures; and heavy economic burden (consequences) of malaria. The themes are composed of different sub-themes including: substandard housing structures enabling mosquito exposure; building close to mosquito breeding grounds; homelessness and street life; inadequate malaria prevention measures; inadequate malaria treatment services; limited access to water supply; poor sanitation and waste management practices; and occupational exposures, social and psychological constraints. Other sub-themes are high treatment costs; undernutrition causing low immunity; and loss of income (livelihood disruption) due to malaria. Poor housing conditions

Under this theme, participants described poor housing standards, living in proximity to breeding grounds and poverty induced environmental sanitation as key factors in malaria transmission.

Substandard housing structures enable mosquito exposure

Almost all participants indicated that poor households lived in incomplete or poorly constructed houses which had gaps in the walls and lacked other essential features such as ceilings, adequate windows or doors and screens, thus facilitating mosquito exposure. Participants described how some households used old clothes to cover broken or incomplete windows, doors and ventilation holes which still allowed spaces for mosquito entry.‘Where I reside, there is a certain woman in a house full of holes in its walls, its roof even leaks. Now, that is a poor person, that even if they had a mosquito net, it might not help them…her house’s door is not that good to be properly shut…and with no ventilators, but she sleeps there’. [Male, VHTs FGD, Rural]

‘Where I reside, there is a certain woman in a house full of holes in its walls, its roof even leaks. Now, that is a poor person, that even if they had a mosquito net, it might not help them…her house’s door is not that good to be properly shut…and with no ventilators, but she sleeps there’. [Male, VHTs FGD, Rural]

In addition, overcrowding in homes was raised in many discussions and interviews indicating that most poor households lived in congested houses which increased the vulnerability of the members to transmission of malaria. In poor households, many members were reported to share small rooms and beds which increased the chances of mosquito bites and transmission in case one of the members was infected. They were also concerned that overcrowded households made it difficult for members to effectively use mosquito nets, for example because they lacked adequate rooms to hang them. Furthermore, participants noted that the poor usually lacked proper lighting and ventilation in their houses mainly due to affordability issues and obstruction from overcrowded houses (limited space). These challenges together created dark interiors that provided friendly environments for mosquitoes to hide in the houses thus increasing their presence in such homes.‘The living condition is not good, a house which is supposed to accommodate maybe 3 people, you find that they are many, maybe 10 people and you know when you are many, if you are overcrowded in the house which doesn't have good ventilations…when one person is infected, the mosquito can bite you, and bites another one, they get the disease’. [Male, KII, In-charge, Public Health Facility]

[Male, KII, In-charge, Public Health Facility]

Overall, participants emphasized that inadequate housing structures and overcrowding created environments that favour mosquito entry (for example through holes in the walls) and breeding, making poor housing a key driver of malaria.

Building close to mosquito breeding grounds

Most poor people were reported to live in proximity to swampy (waterlogged areas) and livestock or agricultural-based breeding grounds for mosquitoes.

Swampy areas

Participants reported that due to limited financial resources, many poor people could only afford to rent or buy land and construct their houses in locations that increased exposure to mosquitoes. Indeed, it emerged that most people were forced to live in areas that were waterlogged/swampy which are ideal mosquito breeding grounds, places which wealthy households would never inhabit.‘People do not have money to buy land in posh areas like [place]. They instead buy in swamps and that is where there are more mosquitoes, they are exposed to them, or they have no access to mosquito nets and for such a person, it is very difficult to avoid getting malaria. We also have a swamp nearby, one day I went there, and I felt sorry for the people who stay nearby, it has many mosquitoes’. [Female, VHTs FGD, Urban]

‘People do not have money to buy land in posh areas like [place]. They instead buy in swamps and that is where there are more mosquitoes, they are exposed to them, or they have no access to mosquito nets and for such a person, it is very difficult to avoid getting malaria. We also have a swamp nearby, one day I went there, and I felt sorry for the people who stay nearby, it has many mosquitoes’. [Female, VHTs FGD, Urban]

Urban agriculture

It was perceived that most households in urban areas owned and lived on small pieces of land due to poverty, on which everything for their survival was conducted. For instance, most households reportedly practiced agriculture especially rearing animals such as pigs and chicken, and growing crops such as maize in household compounds. Some of the animal environments were described as mosquito grounds especially in situations of limited hygiene which was reportedly common among neighbours. Improper waste disposal on the farming spaces such as pigsty among those that practiced agriculture in the vicinity of houses was perceived as a common situation in poor neighbourhoods which created breeding grounds for mosquitoes.‘I have a 30 by 30 meters plot of land, on which my house sits, and I also do piggery on the same piece of land. I also have neighbours who do piggery and keep other animals. I may regularly clean my pig-sty, but my neighbours are not cleaning theirs…that has also contributed to the persistent spread of malaria’. [Male, FGD, Rural]

‘I have a 30 by 30 meters plot of land, on which my house sits, and I also do piggery on the same piece of land. I also have neighbours who do piggery and keep other animals. I may regularly clean my pig-sty, but my neighbours are not cleaning theirs…that has also contributed to the persistent spread of malaria’. [Male, FGD, Rural]

Homelessness and street life

Beyond incomplete or poor houses, key informants were concerned about a challenge of people in the study area especially urban settings who did not have housing at all, due to poverty. They noted that many people including children lacked living shelters, forcing them to spend nights on the streets and engaging in night activities that exposed them to mosquito bites.‘Most poor people are homeless because they can’t afford the rent prices. If you go on the streets, you see the street children who are there, they are so, so many. When you go to new the upcoming cities, many people are just spending nights outside, drinking till morning’. [Male, KII, MOH]

‘Most poor people are homeless because they can’t afford the rent prices. If you go on the streets, you see the street children who are there, they are so, so many. When you go to new the upcoming cities, many people are just spending nights outside, drinking till morning’. [Male, KII, MOH]

Limited access to malaria control services

This theme describes how access to both prevention and treatment of malaria was limited by poverty by highlighting poverty-driven decisions on how resources such as mosquito nets are used or health facilities are accessed due to in adequacy.

Inadequate malaria prevention measures

Inability to afford prevention tools

Participants indicated that low-income households often struggled to afford prevention methods such as repellents, insecticide sprays, and quality mosquito nets or could not easily replace them when they are torn leaving families vulnerable to mosquito bites.‘Poor people can’t afford repellents which chase away mosquitoes… but those who can afford, can buy a repellent. And you know our houses, some of them don’t have nets (some costly wire mesh used to prevent mosquito entry) in the ventilators’. [Mixed FGD, Urban]

‘Poor people can’t afford repellents which chase away mosquitoes… but those who can afford, can buy a repellent. And you know our houses, some of them don’t have nets (some costly wire mesh used to prevent mosquito entry) in the ventilators’. [Mixed FGD, Urban]

There were concerns among most participants that the government distributed mosquito nets were often of poor quality and insufficient quantity for large families, leaving some members of poor households unprotected.‘The problems come when we tell people to share mosquito nets; I mean, there are 8 members in the household, and you are receiving only 2 or 3 mosquito nets. Only 2 people have received, and the rest have not’. [Male, VHTs FGD, Rural]

‘The problems come when we tell people to share mosquito nets; I mean, there are 8 members in the household, and you are receiving only 2 or 3 mosquito nets. Only 2 people have received, and the rest have not’. [Male, VHTs FGD, Rural]

Nets-misuse due to poverty

Due to financial desperation, it was perceived that the government distributed nets were mostly sold by poor households in order to get food or other necessities thus prioritizing immediate survival over health which they saw as a long-term goal. In further discussion, participants said that other poor people who did not sell the received nets from the government did not prioritize sleeping under them but rather repurposed them to serve their priorities. For example, some were using them to create poultry houses, as fences around vegetable gardens to protect them from chicken, or as fishing nets.‘Even if you give me a mosquito net when I am poor, I will automatically sell it because one cannot sleep hungry when they have no money. Poverty is the biggest barrier, so all government programmes should target the poor. There is no rich person who can sell a net because they already have money’. [Male, Youth FGD, Urban]

‘Even if you give me a mosquito net when I am poor, I will automatically sell it because one cannot sleep hungry when they have no money. Poverty is the biggest barrier, so all government programmes should target the poor. There is no rich person who can sell a net because they already have money’. [Male, Youth FGD, Urban]

Inadequate malaria treatment services

Delayed malaria treatment

There were worries about delays in seeking treatment among the poor. Financial constraints often led to delays in seeking for care as the cost of malaria treatment was noted to be very high. Even when treatment was available at public (government funded) health facilities, transport difficulties were believed to be a barrier for the poor. Many residents were believed to be unable to seek treatment when ill because public health facilities were located far from their homes requiring high transportation costs.‘They don't have money which means that much as we tell them; please you come to the facility that it is free…instead of someone coming to the facility early and we treat, you find that it's something hard’. [Female, KII, In charge, Public Health Facility]

‘They don't have money which means that much as we tell them; please you come to the facility that it is free…instead of someone coming to the facility early and we treat, you find that it's something hard’. [Female, KII, In charge, Public Health Facility]

Due to the financial limitations, most people delayed seeking professional care until symptoms worsened which increased the risk of complications and transmission of malaria to other people.

Inadequate doses and recurrent malaria

In almost all discussions and interviews, concerns regarding how common stock-outs in public health facilities negatively drove the malaria burden among the poor Ugandans were raised. It was emphasized that low-income households suffered from the inability to buy full doses of malaria medication, during the stock outs periods, leading to underdosing and related severe or recurrent malaria. Due to cost constraints, other people were perceived to heavily rely on or resort to the herbal remedies in usually improperly regulated doses. The limited financial capacity to seek proper malaria treatment resulting into underdose is highlighted in the quote below:‘When you go to the government facilities, it means you have no money. When they give us Panadol and prescribe the malaria drugs(medicine) for us to buy in private facility, you end up buying under dose like for 2000 shillings because you can’t afford to buy the full dose to take and you don’t get better’. [Male, FGD, Rural]

‘When you go to the government facilities, it means you have no money. When they give us Panadol and prescribe the malaria drugs(medicine) for us to buy in private facility, you end up buying under dose like for 2000 shillings because you can’t afford to buy the full dose to take and you don’t get better’. [Male, FGD, Rural]

Limited financial capacity was seen as a major obstacle to accessing prevention tools and timely and quality treatment, reinforcing malaria vulnerability among low-income households.

Limited water and sanitation-related exposures to mosquitoes

Participants noted that poor households and communities experienced water and sanitation challenges that exposed them to mosquitoes as described below:

Limited access to water supply

It was noted that although most urban areas have piped water systems, poor communities had limited access to water supply, forcing households to store water in drums or open containers. This practice created stagnant water around homes, providing breeding grounds for mosquitoes. Additionally, poor people walked long distances for up to one hour especially in the evenings to collect water from distant water sources exposing them to mosquitoes in the process (at the water sources and on the way—to and from there). These included children who as part of helping parents with domestic duties walked to collect water, sometimes in the night after their school time.‘Many times, even when our children come from school, we send them to fetch water yet it is late, and mosquitoes bite them while they are at the well’. [Female, FGD, Rural]

Talking about the practice of water collection drums which become mosquito breeding grounds, participants mentioned that this practice and the long distances to water sources were especially common in the rural settings most of which had limited water supply.‘Some of us in the village have drums when it rains, water is harvested and then mosquitoes start laying eggs in the water and hatch the eggs. This causes a multiplication for more mosquitoes which bring malaria’. [Female, Youth FGD, Rural]

Poor sanitation and waste management practices

It was reported that most poor households did not afford proper garbage collection. Inadequate water drainage and solid waste collection services also resulted in unhygienic surroundings including stagnant wastewater around homes which were noted to be conducive environments for mosquito breeding. Participants revealed inadequate waste collection practices in some communities where poor households resorted to illegal and informal waste collection practices. For instance, they described practices such as households hiring some of the poorest young people to work as informal collectors paid to dispose of waste improperly including in drainage channels. Such practices were said to worsen drainage and sanitation creating mosquito breeding grounds.‘We are in small, rented places…you get a cheap guy and pay them small money, remember those cars for garbage are expensive, now this cheap guy is the one going around throwing garbage anyhow’. [Male, FGD, Urban]

‘We are in small, rented places…you get a cheap guy and pay them small money, remember those cars for garbage are expensive, now this cheap guy is the one going around throwing garbage anyhow’. [Male, FGD, Urban]

Many participants in particular noted that there was stagnant water in slums and strongly expressed need for proper urban planning and housing regulations with improved and equitable garbage collection services in all communities. Besides the practices by the slum dwellers, some participants reported that the inadequate sanitation that the poor experienced was created by wastewater from the rich households with poor drainage systems.‘The challenge is from the people who stay inside fenced gates ‘people perceived to be rich’. For me as a regular person, I will stay in my rental where that person pours all that. I usually see these things’. [Female, FGD, Urban]

Occupational and psychological exposures

Poverty related stress and some occupations were perceived to increase exposure to mosquitoes, illustrating how livelihoods and psychosocial factors intersect with malaria risk.

Occupational exposures

Participants revealed that most poor households were workers in places and times that exposed them to mosquitoes. Examples of such occupations included night security keepers, fisher men who work around lakes, and brick makers who slept near forests and open brick making ditches with stagnant water. Others were the vendors who worked along drainage channels and markets including selling food at night as well as truck drivers.‘I used to drive trucks in (place name), sometimes I would sleep in the vehicle, I got sick, in vehicle, and nothing can stop mosquitoes from entering. Mosquitoes bit me that I came back with malaria, I almost died...most people who work in forests are liable to catching malaria’. [Male, Youth FGD, Rural]

Mosquitoes bit me that I came back with malaria, I almost died...most people who work in forests are liable to catching malaria’. [Male, Youth FGD, Rural]

Social and psychological constraints

Poverty driven priority and social shifts

Poverty was perceived as a limitation to participation in health interventions such as sensitization meetings due to priority shifts at personal versus community levels. These shifts were linked by participants to economic hardships among communities. Participants revealed that people were no longer willing to gather for the community health activities including general cleaning exercises as before.‘Poverty has caused so many problems in that even if they announce that there is a meeting about malaria, people do not turn up, they imagine going for this meeting to spend 2 hours and yet they do not have what to eat, so such a person decides to go and look for food’. [Male, KII, Religious Leader2]

‘Poverty has caused so many problems in that even if they announce that there is a meeting about malaria, people do not turn up, they imagine going for this meeting to spend 2 hours and yet they do not have what to eat, so such a person decides to go and look for food’. [Male, KII, Religious Leader2]

Focusing on earning a daily wage to afford basic needs such as food and housing rent was believed to drive the money-oriented attitude among people ignoring activities that do not yield immediate financial benefits. The overlooked health activities included communal clearing of drainage channels and bushes that harbour mosquitoes.

Poverty-related stress and mosquito exposures

Participants also revealed some poverty-related psychological impact that exposed households to mosquitoes. For instance, it was reported that poverty caused some hopelessness that led to neglect of health and hygiene activities by some individuals. Participants believed that people who were not employed had been victims of malaria because of spending their time in places where mosquitoes can easily bite them. Due to lack of employment and limited income, some people were believed not to have timely and adequate sleep due to psychological distress that led them to spending the nights outside houses thinking about what to do. Such experiences exposed them to mosquitoes.‘When they have low income, of course, they cannot sleep on time, one may be forced to stay outside their houses while thinking on what to do, …where does such a person get sleep from? so that is why they stay outside and mosquitoes get you’. [Female, Youth FGD, Urban]

Heavy economic burden (consequences) of malaria

All interviews and discussions demonstrated how malaria led individuals into poverty in different ways including loss of income due to illness and high malaria treatment costs faced by households, increasing their vulnerability to the disease.

High treatment costs

Participants noted that while both rich and poor people suffered from malaria, the poor had treatment challenges and a heavy economic impact. It was noted especially by the key informants that most people spent a considerable portion of their income on malaria treatment including consultation, medication and transport. The costs were believably overwhelming for the poor households diverting funds from other essential needs like food and education, leading some to resort to traditional healers as the first option. Such delays in treatment resulted in severe disease and related expenses driving them into more poverty.‘The rich and the poor are all affected by malaria, but what makes this other one better is that he treats himself faster than me. By the time I get malaria I may not have money (P: herbal medicine). I may use the herbal medicine, and it fails to cure me, but the other one, the way he suffers is not the same way I suffer’. [Female, FGD, Urban]

I may use the herbal medicine, and it fails to cure me, but the other one, the way he suffers is not the same way I suffer’. [Female, FGD, Urban]

Other concerns that were raised by participants were related to low immunity due to food insecurity and insufficient feeding which they identified as common among the poor households due to low incomes. Such food insecurity limited nutrition making household members including children to be vulnerable to malaria, through making them susceptible to the disease or limiting proper treatment in absence of adequate food.‘They may be told to eat at certain intervals, they cannot afford, so that is the impact I can talk about. So, youth unemployment can affect, even the accessibility of anti- malarial drugs and the nutrition on how one can feed themselves’. [Male, KII, In charge, Private, Urban]

Loss of income (livelihood disruption) due to malaria

The participants described how frequent malaria illness disrupted work and income generation for families especially those in informal employment or those involved in daily wage- based activities which was believed to be the case for most communities in the study area. In these situations, findings indicate that when the household head suffered from malaria, they at times lost such income from the jobs or business they operated and the whole family suffered. It also emerged that severe malaria causing disability which further disrupted or reduced an individual’s earning capacity was a major concern.‘Someone can start a business, starts trading and it succeeds…that person falls sick and goes to the hospital without getting better, moving from one hospital to another and there is no one to attend to the business, so malaria can lead to the failure of business. We are being disorganized, we would be better, but malaria is disturbing us’. [Male, FGD, Urban]

This theme highlights how malaria was described as both a health and economic shock for the poor individuals draining their resources and disrupting income generation.

Discussion

Findings from our study emphasized that poverty and malaria are closely related, with poverty reported to increase mosquitoes’ breeding grounds, limit access to prevention and treatment, while the related costs caused and intensified poverty by draining household income as well as reducing productivity due to absence from work from illness. Poor housing structures including living in areas with inadequate water and sanitation increased the exposure to mosquitoes as such conditions are ideal for their breeding, reducing the effectiveness of preventive interventions such as mosquito nets. Our findings further expand previous research by also highlighting psychosocial dimensions of poverty, in particular stress and shifting priorities, that are rarely addressed in malaria control literature. These findings together call for malaria control interventions that address the structural and psychological vulnerabilities to malaria due to poverty.

Incomplete housing structures and overcrowded households due to low-income or unemployment were commonly reported as drivers of exposure to mosquitoes. These findings align with previous studies that have linked substandard housing to increased mosquito entry and transmission risk [13, 42, 43] National surveys indicate that housing quality was an important risk factor for malaria including among 0–5-year-olds in Sub-Saharan Africa [13, 44]. A recent systematic review shows that good quality housing protects against malaria regardless of the disease transmission settings [42]. A study in Yaoundé showed that several house characteristics such as the presence of holes on walls, opened eaves, unscreened window and living close to breeding sites, favour mosquito presence in houses [45]. In Tanzania [43] housing improvements including window screening, repairs in the holes in walls, door covers and closing of eave spaces was among the best understood and preferred intervention by community members with their efforts often slow and delayed due to high costs and limited household incomes. It was seen as an intervention that would make other strategies effective in eliminating malaria [43]. In a systematic review of randomised trials of improved housing interventions in Africa and south America, a pooled analysis showed reduced indoor densities of Anopheles and a lower risk of malaria across all settings [46]. Modern homes reduced human biting rate and the odds of malaria infection [47], and living in houses built using straw and thatch was a risk factor for severe malaria readmissions in children under 5 years of age in Uganda [48]. Therefore, programmes aimed at improvement of housing structures should be an integral component of all malaria reduction efforts.

Beyond housing structures, results from our study show that crowded living conditions were believed to be drivers of mosquito breeding, hiding and exposure. There were also concerns that the mosquito nets distributed by the government were limited in quantity leaving most poor households with many household members unprotected. Studies including a post net distribution campaign evaluation in Uganda highlighted such inequities in protection from mosquitoes [6, 26] Besides housing structures and spacing, our findings indicate that location of houses in proximity to swampy or waterlogged areas with stagnant water, and other breeding grounds exacerbates mosquito exposures. In addition, the inadequate water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) infrastructure and related behaviours common in poor communities create mosquito breeding grounds. Improvised water collection and storage practices at household level such as drums created stagnant water that offered conducive environments for mosquito breeding while others especially children were exposed while fetching water from distant water sources. Exposure to mosquitoes during the process of fetching water related to limited water supply in communities has been previously reported elsewhere [49, 50]. It is therefore important that malaria control efforts include interventions to ensure access to safe water that reduces mosquito breeding and human exposure. Poor solid waste management processes that litter the environment, and block drainage channels while creating mosquito breeding grounds were also reported as a consequence of poverty in our study. Poverty-sanitation insufficient link has also been described in other poor communities in Uganda, particularly in slums where living on the margins daily (hustling for money) overrode neighbourhood community development initiatives. This emphasizes the need for integrating environmental interventions in malaria control as has also been advocated in other studies [43, 51, 52]. In 2016, 80% of the malaria burden in low- and middle-income countries was attributed to inadequate WASH [51] underscoring how related interventions could be highly effective in reducing malaria risk.

Occupational exposures to mosquitoes were another poverty related social determinant that was raised especially among informal and night workers, as well as those practicing agriculture such as maize growing which has also been reported elsewhere including Tanzania [53]. Other studies have indicated occupational exposures among night workers including those involved in fishing [33, 49], highlighting the need for occupational considerations in malaria control. Indeed, livelihood activities which vary across regions and communities contribute to the exposure to mosquitoes [50, 54]. Additionally, psychological and behavioural dimensions of livelihoods and poverty’s influence on malaria were also raised in our study. Poverty induced stress, unemployment and shifting community priorities due to survival struggles especially among the informal workers were believed to reduce participation in health-promoting activities such as community cleanups and sensitization meetings. These psychological dimensions of poverty contribute to a sense of hopelessness and reprioritization of preventive health behaviours that reduces access to prevention information. Similarly, a study in Tanzania indicated that households in informal work had limited access to information regarding malaria control [53]. This finding calls for malaria approaches that address psychosocial challenges and other occupational vulnerabilities particularly those faced by informal workers and high-risk livelihood groups. Besides the specific occupational exposures, urban agriculture, which was generally practiced on small plots in front compounds or backyards (farming close to houses) as a supplementary livelihood, was highlighted as a key source of mosquitos. The increase in this agricultural practice was believed to be driven by poverty due to the need to help families reduce food expenses or earn extra income through quick‑maturing vegetables and small livestock. This is different from rural agriculture—the main source of food and income involving a wide range of animals (such as cattle, goats, poultry) and staple crops like maize, cassava, and bananas on big pieces of land. These contrasts in farming practices shaped by not only differences in land tenure systems [55], but also labour patterns and efforts to escape poverty create malaria vulnerabilities that should be considered in malaria programmes. In particular, unlike rural agriculture where exposure is mostly outdoor and during farming hours, this urban practice may bring risks closer to home and expose to the vector household members when they are in and around the houses.

Barriers to malaria prevention and treatment were raised where limited financial capacity among poor households restricted access to mosquito nets, and repellents. Even when ITNs were distributed by government programmes, issues of misuse due to economic desperation were raised in all discussions and interviews. These findings echo concerns in other low- and middle-income settings where ITN coverage was undermined by socioeconomic constraints [14, 56]. Similarly, malaria treatment-seeking behaviour was delayed by both direct (high cost of medication in private facilities) and indirect (related costs such as transportation, medicine stockouts and long queues at government funded facilities) limitations. The inability to afford full doses and reliance on herbal remedies due to cost constraints exacerbate the problem by contributing to recurrent or severe malaria, consistent with findings from other resource-limited settings [57, 58]. Relatedly, other findings from our study indicate concerns on how poor feeding in poor households limited malaria treatment and increased transmission. There is evidence that malnutrition is risk factor for malaria especially among the under-fives [9] and that food insecurity and undernutrition including stunting co-exist with poverty [58–60]. However, our findings further suggest that poverty limits people’s choices and shapes malaria-related behaviours, rather than simply indicating that poverty and malaria are linked because of poor decision making or limited knowledge about the disease. Moreso, economic related factors such as the need to engage in livelihood activities were understandably prioritised over gaining knowledge about malaria control.

Overall, the findings from our study underscore the economic toll of malaria and its role in perpetuating the poverty trap and vice versa. Participants emphasized how the disease places a significant burden on low-income households through treatment expenses and lost workdays due to illness. A recent malaria cost and burden analysis in Uganda confirm that economic vulnerability remains a key driver of disease burden, with households bearing over 70% of the illness related costs which is costly for majority of Ugandans who are the low-income households [61]. Similarly, the findings suggest a strong need for economic empowerment which has also been strongly advocated for instead of concentrating malaria control as a medical issue within only the health sector with minimal integration in development programmes [11, 62, 63]. Furthermore, current national poverty reduction interventions such as the Parish Development Model (PDM) should be improved to ensure that they reach the poor people who deserve them most. The PDM launched in 2022 is Uganda’s poverty eradication strategy that targets households at the parish level by providing them with financial support, promoting agricultural productivity, and facilitates access to credit and skills development aiming to address poverty drivers. The need for sustainable job creation and other poverty eradication programmes to help young people escape poverty and related malaria cannot be understated. Benefits of economic empowerment have been reported elsewhere including improvement in health seeking and treatment of HIV among children in Uganda [64]. Economic empowerment alongside social protection initiatives such as those that protect child rights and human dignity can address vulnerabilities that drive homelessness and street life [65] and related risks to malaria.

Taken together, our findings emphasize the need for malaria control strategies to go beyond biomedical interventions. Whilst issues such as the use of ITNs for purposes other than malaria prevention or poor disposal of household waste highlights the need to continue with behavioural communication campaigns, other factors point to key structural issues that drive high rates of malaria amongst the poor. For example, financial inequalities are a key mechanism which put at risk those who cannot afford to rent or buy a house away from mosquitoes breeding sites. Environmental management and improved water and sanitation systems require addressing at a systemic level, and health systems need to be developed to better serve poorer communities.

Study limitations and strengths

This study has several limitations starting with a cross-sectional design that limits any conclusions on causation. Further, the qualitative approach does not allow quantification of the poverty and malaria relationship in this context. The study’s focus on participants’ perceptions and experiences relies on self-reports, thus introducing potential bias such as desirability issues that cannot completely be ruled out. A key strength is that we used the Dahlgren and Whitehead conceptual framework [66] during the data collection. This allowed for collection of comprehensive in-depth perspectives on malaria and poverty within a social determinants lens that is largely limited in the malaria-control literature. Using the framework stimulated discussions on the social determinants based on its coloured and structured levels that offered a simple breakdown/categories that were easily explained by research assistants and understood by the participants. The other strength is that we selected participants across different groups of stakeholders considering different levels of key informants, and characteristics of participants including setting of IDIs and FGDs. In particular, the organisation of FGDs by gender, age, and rural–urban setting ensured equitable representation and created discussion spaces where participants felt comfortable expressing their experiences without social pressure. This arrangement strengthened the study by reducing power imbalances such as men dominating most discussions or adults overshadowing youth, thus offering appropriate collection of perceptions across different social groups. Our study therefore provides in depth insights of how poverty and malaria are cyclic within a Ugandan context across both rural and urban communities making this relevant to policy makers and other stakeholders in malaria control. We present the common themes across rural–urban participants that suggest rural–urban variations in exposures to the vector as highlighted by concerns on urban agriculture and limited water supply. It is however important to note that a focused settings-segregated analysis on how different malaria-poverty pathways intersect and reinforce each other was beyond this study and should be considered in future research.

Conclusion

This study highlights the cyclical relationship between poverty and malaria, whereby poverty not only increases vulnerability to the disease but also perpetuates socioeconomic deprivation through its health and economic consequences. Poverty shapes malaria vulnerability through multiple interconnected pathways, including inadequate housing, limited access to prevention and treatment services, poor water and sanitation conditions, and occupational as well as psychosocial exposures. These structural and individual constraints heighten the risk of mosquito bites and the economic burden of malaria, reinforcing a cycle of illness and poverty. Future national malaria control efforts should embed poverty reduction measures which emphasize both community level impacts and other structural factors such as housing quality which are critical in the relationship between malaria and poverty.
