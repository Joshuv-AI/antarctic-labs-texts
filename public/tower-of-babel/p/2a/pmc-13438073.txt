Myocardial fat fraction in Becker muscular dystrophy and women carrying pathogenic DMD gene variants assessed by Dixon cardiac MRI
Authors: Zhe Lyu, Bjørk Teitsdóttir, Heini Joensen, Maria Buhl Hjortrup, Anna Slipsager, Adam Espe Hansen, Niels Vejlstrup, John Vissing, Nanna Scharff Poulsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/ObjectivePathogenic variants in the dystrophin gene (DMD) cause dystrophinopathies. These variants can cause skeletal and cardiac involvement. Disease progression in skeletal muscle is characterized by fatty replacement, but whether similar processes occur in the myocardium remain unclear. Dixon cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) can quantify myocardial fat fraction (FF). This study used Dixon CMR to quantify myocardial FF in patients with BMD and women carrying DMD gene variants, and to explore associations with cardiac function, assessed by left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), age, and skeletal muscle FF.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 20 patients with BMD, 27 women carrying DMD gene variants, and 40 healthy controls who underwent three-point Dixon CMR to quantify myocardial FF. Muscle MRI was performed to assess lower-limb muscle FF.ResultsMyocardial FF did not differ significantly among the three groups (p = 0.11); mean values were 10.0% in patients with BMD, 11.2% in women carrying DMD gene variants, and 10.4% in healthy controls. Subgroup analyses showed no significant differences between patients with BMD and healthy men or between women carrying DMD gene variants and healthy women. Myocardial FF was not associated with LVEF, age, or skeletal muscle FF.ConclusionsDixon CMR revealed no increase in myocardial FF in patients with BMD or women carrying DMD gene variants compared with healthy controls. Unlike skeletal muscle, myocardial remodeling in dystrophinopathies does not appear to be adipogenic and is likely predominantly fibrotic. Dixon CMR is useful for skeletal muscle imaging but has limited clinical use for myocardial assessment.Keywords: Becker muscular dystrophy, women carrying pathogenic DMD gene variants, Dixon sequence, myocardial FF, cardiac magnetic resonance

1. Introduction

Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) and Becker muscular dystrophy (BMD) are X-linked recessive disorders caused by pathogenic variants in the dystrophin (DMD) gene, which encodes dystrophin, an essential structural protein for muscle cell integrity and function. 1 Variants in DMD produce a spectrum of phenotypes collectively referred to as dystrophinopathies, including DMD, BMD, and X-linked dilated cardiomyopathy. 2 The severity of these conditions largely depends on the extent of dystrophin deficiency, determined by the specific mutation’s effect on dystrophin structure and function. 3

Although X-linked and typically manifesting in men, women carrying DMD gene variants can be symptomatic.4,5 Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) is well established as an important tool to assess myocardial fibrosis and ventricular function in dystrophinopathies, and can detect abnormalities even when echocardiography appears normal.6,7

However, routine CMR protocols used for cardiac surveillance in dystrophinopathies primarily assess ventricular function and fibrotic changes, but do not directly quantify myocardial fat fraction (FF), although fat may be qualitatively visible on certain sequences in specific contexts.

Distinguishing myocardial fat from fibrosis may nevertheless be important, as these processes may reflect different aspects of myocardial remodeling.

Dixon magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a chemical-shift-based method that separates water and fat signals and is widely used to quantify skeletal muscle FF.

It has been widely used to quantify skeletal muscle FF. 8 In dystrophinopathies, Dixon MRI of skeletal muscle has demonstrated sensitivity to disease progression and correlation with functional impairment. 9

Given the parallels between skeletal and cardiac striated muscle pathology, Dixon CMR may provide novel insights into myocardial disease processes.

In a pediatric DMD cohort, there was no increase in myocardial FF compared with healthy controls, suggesting minimal fat replacement in early disease. 10

By contrast, age-related fat replacement is well documented in skeletal muscle, raising the possibility that myocardial fat replacement could emerge later in the disease course. 11 Whether myocardial FF increases with aging or disease progression has not been clarified, underscoring the need for studies in adult cohorts.

This study aimed to apply Dixon CMR to quantify myocardial FF in patients with BMD and women carrying DMD gene variants, to compare these groups with healthy controls, and to examine associations with cardiac function, age, and skeletal muscle FF.

Through this approach, we sought to determine whether myocardial fat replacement contributes to cardiac involvement in dystrophinopathies and whether it parallels skeletal muscle pathology.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and participants

This was a cross-sectional study. Participants were recruited from the Copenhagen Neuromuscular Center at Rigshospitalet.

Patients with BMD and women carrying DMD gene variants underwent Dixon CMR, CMR with late gadolinium enhancement (LGE), whole-body MRI, and medical history review, whereas healthy controls underwent Dixon CMR only.

Inclusion criteria were age ≥18 years and eligibility for MRI.

Participants additionally had a genetically confirmed pathogenic DMD gene variant, either in men with BMD or women with a carrier status. Healthy controls had no muscle disease.

Exclusion criteria were contraindications to MRI (e.g., MR-incompatible metallic implants, pregnancy, claustrophobia, atrial fibrillation), or inability to provide informed consent, or history of heart transplantation.

2.2. Cardiac and whole-body magnetic resonance imaging

All MRI scans were performed at follow-up using a 3.0 Tesla scanner (MAGNETOM Verio, Siemens, Erlangen, Germany).

All participants were scanned in the supine, head-first position using a peripheral angiographic coil, a body matrix coil, and a cardiac coil.

The same CMR and whole-body MRI protocols were used for patients with BMD and women carrying DMD gene variants.

Whole-body MRI images were acquired using three-dimensional localizers, T1-weighted, and Dixon sequences, following a previously described protocol. 12

CMR was performed during end-expiratory breath-holds with retrospective electrocardiographic gating. Standard two-, three-, and four-chamber views were obtained, followed by contiguous short-axis and transverse stacks covering the entire heart.

The CMR protocol included cine steady-state free precession imaging, three-point Dixon imaging, native T1 mapping using a modified Look-Locker inversion recovery (MOLLI) sequence, and LGE. 13

Myocardial fat content was quantified using three-point Dixon imaging.

Images were acquired at three echo times to separate fat and water signals and to correct for magnetic field inhomogeneities, generating in-phase, opposed-phase, fat-only, and water-only images. Dixon images were acquired in coronal and axial planes.

Sequence parameters were as follows: repetition time (TR), 230.38 ms; flip angle, 15°; echo times (TE1/TE2), 1.30/2.77 ms; ECG-triggered acquisition; a single 3D coronal slab covering the heart with 120 slices; slice thickness, 1.0 mm; field of view, 320 × 320 mm; matrix size, 320 × 320; acquired in-plane spatial resolution, 1.0 × 1.0 mm; and T2 preparation duration, 50 ms.

Native and post-contrast T1 mapping were performed in a single mid-ventricular short-axis slice.

Gadobutrol (Gadovist®, Bayer Schering, Germany) was administered intravenously at 0.15 mmol/kg (maximum 15 mL), and LGE imaging was started 10 minutes after contrast injection. Contrast was only administered if the estimated glomerular filtration rate was ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2 and serum cystatin C was within the normal range.

LGE images were acquired using a T1-weighted gradient-echo sequence with the inversion time manually adjusted to null normal myocardium.

Areas of LGE were confirmed with additional cross-sectional images and identified as regions with signal intensity >6 standard deviations (SD) above the signal of remote, visually normal myocardium. In the present study, LGE was used primarily to assess the presence or absence of myocardial fibrosis.

Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) <56% was considered abnormal. 14

2.3. Imaging analysis

2.3.1. CMR analysis

CMR datasets were processed in Cvi42 (v6.2.2; Circle Cardiovascular Imaging, Calgary, Canada).

Images were initially analyzed by one investigator and subsequently reviewed by a second investigator. All assessments were performed blinded to clinical information and time of visit.

2.3.1.1. Slice selection

Three slices were selected for myocardial FF analysis. The first slice was the mid-ventricular slice, defined as a slice with complete circumferential myocardium and without outflow-tract involvement. The left ventricular long-axis length was measured on the vertical long-axis view to determine the mid-ventricular level.

Two additional slices were then selected at 30% of the half-length toward the base and 30% toward the apex, resulting in three representative slices (basal-mid, mid, apical-mid).

These three slices were used for myocardial FF analysis, and their mean value was reported as the patient-level result. Basal slices were avoided because of blood pool contamination and motion artifacts, whereas very apical slices were excluded due to thin myocardium and partial volume effects.

2.3.1.2. ROI definition

In each selected slice, endocardial and outer myocardial contours were manually traced to define the compact left ventricular myocardium. The same contours were applied to paired.

Dixon reconstructions displayed side by side (fat-only on the left viewport and water-only on the right). Blood pool, visible artifacts, and border regions affected by partial-volume effects were excluded. Because the outer myocardial border may be indistinct on Dixon images, contours were drawn conservatively, slightly within the apparent outer myocardial border when needed, to minimize contamination from adjacent epicardial and pericardial fat. Epicardial and pericardial fat were therefore not included in the ROI.

We performed an additional exploratory analysis of subcutaneous fat in 9 representative participants: 3 patients with BMD, 3 women carrying DMD gene variants, and 3 healthy controls. In each group, these participants represented the highest, lowest, and median myocardial FF values. Subcutaneous adipose tissue was assessed on the same Dixon CMR images as the myocardial fat fraction by manual region-of-interest delineation. Subcutaneous FF was calculated using the same approach as for myocardial FF.

For Dixon CMR, image quality was visually assessed using a four-point Likert scale 15 (Figure 1).

The main factors reducing image quality were motion-related artifacts, including blurring at the myocardial borders, likely related to respiration or imperfect breath-holding, as well as occasional fat–water swap artifact. In addition, partial-volume effects from the adjacent blood pool or epicardial fat were considered potential sources of error and were minimized by conservative contouring of the myocardium.

2.3.3. Muscle MRI analysis

We used Horos MRI software (version 3.3.6; The Horos Project, Annapolis, MD, USA) to analyze muscle FF in the thigh and calf.

Muscle segmentation was performed on MRI slices acquired at 50% of the femoral length and 33% of the tibial length, corresponding to standard anatomical landmarks. 16

The thigh muscles were categorized into three groups: 1) anterior group: rectus femoris, vastus lateralis, vastus medialis, vastus intermedius, and sartorius; 2) medial group: adductor longus, adductor magnus, and gracilis; 3) posterior group: biceps femoris (long and short heads), semitendinosus, and semimembranosus.

The calf muscles were divided into two groups: 1) Anterior group: tibialis anterior, extensor digitorum longus, and peroneus longus and brevis; 2) Posterior group: tibialis posterior, soleus, and gastrocnemius.

We excluded bone, extramuscular fat, and peripheral intramuscular vessels and nerves. To minimize partial volume with subcutaneous fat, we avoided the outermost muscle rim where signal contamination was suspected.

Muscle FF (%) was calculated in the same way as the myocardial FF of CMR.

2.4.2. Statistical methods

Analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data distributions were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± SD, and non-normally distributed variables as median with interquartile range (IQR).

Because age in the healthy control group was not normally distributed, age is reported as median (IQR) for all groups to ensure comparability.

Results are reported as mean or median difference along with the (bootstrapped) 95% confidence interval (CI), where applicable.

Group comparisons of myocardial FF among patients with BMD, women carrying DMD gene variants, and healthy controls were conducted using one-way ANOVA.

Homogeneity of variances was tested with the Brown–Forsythe test.

When assumptions were met, we used the omnibus ANOVA with Tukey’s HSD as a post-hoc test; when variances were unequal, we used Welch’s ANOVA with Games–Howell post hoc.

Independent t-tests were used for subgroup comparisons (men with BMD vs. healthy men; women carrying DMD gene variants vs. healthy women).

To assess the overall association between myocardial FF and LVEF, we first performed a correlation analysis (Pearson’s r when both variables were normally distributed; otherwise, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient [ρ]) and reported correlation coefficients (r/ρ) with corresponding p-values. Subsequently, multivariable linear regression adjusted for age and sex was performed to evaluate the independent association between myocardial FF and LVEF.

All statistical tests were two-tailed, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

2.4.3. Missing data

Participants with missing or unusable cardiac imaging data were excluded listwise from relevant analysis.

2.5. Ethical approval

The study was approved by the Danish National Committee on Health Research Ethics (H-23007727) and was registered on Clinicaltrials.gov (Identifier: NCT05715957). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and all participants provided written informed consent.

3.1.3. Healthy controls

The overall median age of healthy controls was 39 years (IQR 28–53).

By sex, men had a median age of 39 years (IQR 29–59), and women had a median age of 36 years (IQR 26–52).

3.2. Image quality assessment of the Dixon sequence

Dixon sequence image quality varied across scans.

Scans with a score of 0 (n=3) were excluded a priori because the myocardium could not be segmented reliably; of these, 1 was from a woman carrying a pathogenic DMD gene variant, and 2 were from healthy controls.

Scans with a score of 1 (n=12) included 3 patients with BMD, 5 women carrying pathogenic DMD gene variants, and 4 healthy controls, and were subsequently excluded from the main analysis because of reduced image quality.

Image-quality score was negatively correlated with myocardial FF (Spearman ρ = –0.65, p = 0.004), indicating that lower image quality was associated with higher measured myocardial FF. Although the direction of this relationship cannot be determined from the present data, this finding still supports the exclusion of images with low quality scores to ensure reliable myocardial fat quantification.

When excluding scans with a score of 1, myocardial FF in patients with BMD decreased from 10.8% ± 2.6% to 10.0% ± 1.9%.

All downstream analyses used scans with scores 2 (good, n=45) and 3 (excellent, n=25) unless otherwise stated.

3.3. Myocardial FF in healthy controls

The mean myocardial FF in healthy controls was 10.4% (SD 2.0%; 95% CI 9.8%–11.1%).

By sex, the mean myocardial FF was 10.7% (SD 1.9%) in men and 10.2% (SD 2.0%) in women; there was no statistical difference between men and women.

There was no statistically significant association between age and myocardial FF in men (p = 0.05; R2 = 0.25) or in women (p = 0.79; R2 = 0.004) (Figure 3).

Myocardial FF of the left ventricle was 10.0 ± 1.9 (95% CI 9.0–11.0) in patients with BMD, 11.2 ± 1.4 (95% CI 10.6 –11.8) in women carrying DMD gene variants, and 10.4 ± 2.0 (95% CI 9.8 –11.1) in healthy controls.

In pairwise comparisons, no significant differences were observed between patients with BMD and healthy controls (mean difference 0.4%, 95% CI –0.8 to 1.7, p = 0.46) or with women carrying DMD gene variants (mean difference 1.2%, 95% CI 0.1 to 2.3, p = 0.05), or women carrying DMD gene variants and healthy controls (mean difference 0.8%, 95% CI –0.2 to 1.8, p = 0.12).

In sex-stratified subgroup analyses, myocardial FF did not differ significantly between patients with BMD and healthy men (mean difference 0.7%, 95% CI –0.7 to 2.1, p = 0.33) or between women carrying DMD gene variants and healthy women (mean difference 1.0%, 95% CI –0.1 to 2.1, p = 0.08).

In an additional exploratory analysis, the mean ±SD subcutaneous FF was 98.3% ±0.5% in 9 participants.

3.5. Correlation between myocardial FF and cardiac function

Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was used as a measure of cardiac function.

A combined regression analysis of patients with BMD and women carrying DMD gene variants revealed no statistically significant correlation between myocardial FF and LVEF, neither before nor after adjustment for age and gender (r = 0.22, p = 0.20, adjusted p = 0.65) (Figure 5(a)).

3.6. Correlation between myocardial FF and lower limb muscle FF

Correlation between myocardial FF and lower limb muscle FF (thigh and calf muscles) was not significant in patients with BMD (ρ = 0.18, p = 0.55), or in women carrying DMD gene variants (ρ = 0.21, p = 0.37) (Figure 5(b)).

4. Discussion

This study is the first to quantify myocardial FF of the left ventricle in adult patients with BMD and women carrying pathogenic DMD gene variants using three-point Dixon CMR.

We found no significant differences in myocardial FF among patients with BMD, women carrying DMD gene variants, and matched controls. Therefore, it is not surprising that myocardial FF showed no association with cardiac function, age, or skeletal muscle FF.

Notably, cardiac Dixon imaging did not reveal fat replacement even in individuals with high skeletal muscle fat replacement.

Taken together, these findings suggest that myocardial fat replacement is not a prominent or early imaging feature of cardiac involvement in dystrophinopathies, supporting a remodeling phenotype dominated by fibrosis rather than adipose tissue accumulation.

4.1. Relation to prior studies

Two prior studies reported different myocardial FF values. One study examined myocardial FF in boys with DMD using the four-point Dixon and found that, even in the presence of fibrosis, myocardial FF was similar to healthy controls (median ∼5–6%, range ∼3–9%). 18

However, another study reported substantially lower myocardial FF in healthy adults (1.0 ± 0.4%, range 0.3–2.4%) using two-point Dixon with bias correction and validation against localized 1H MR spectroscopy. 19

These differences most likely reflect variation in both population and methodology (e.g., Dixon implementation, corrections, reference standard, and sequence-related characteristics), which can shift absolute myocardial FF estimates.

In our cohort, absolute myocardial FF values were higher than those reported in some prior studies.

As an additional contextual check, subcutaneous fat measured on the same Dixon CMR acquisitions showed very high FF values in a representative subset, supporting the plausibility of the Dixon-derived FF scale. Similarly, very high subcutaneous adipose tissue FF values have been reported in prior MRI-based studies (94.2% ± 1.2%), 20 although these are not directly comparable because they were obtained in a different anatomical region and population.

We therefore consider the relatively high absolute myocardial FF values observed in our cohort to be most likely attributable to methodological rather than biological factors. Accordingly, we interpret these measurements primarily as protocol-specific Dixon-derived FF values rather than absolute myocardial lipid content.

Because all groups, including age- and sex-matched healthy controls, were scanned and analyzed using the identical three-point Dixon protocol, within-study comparisons between groups are likely more robust than direct comparison of absolute myocardial FF values across studies.

Importantly, the relatively high absolute myocardial FF values were observed not only in patients but also in healthy controls, and no between-group increase in myocardial FF was detected.

In this context, our overall conclusions remain consistent with previous studies.

We also applied strict image-quality criteria and excluded scans of low quality, thereby reducing bias. Nonetheless, residual artifacts and scanner variability may still attenuate between-group differences and limit cross-study comparability of absolute values.

4.2. Biological interpretation

Our findings extend prior pediatric observations into adulthood, showing that myocardial FF remains comparable to controls in adult patients with BMD and women carrying DMD gene variants, even in the presence of reduced LVEF or myocardial fibrosis. 21

In contrast, skeletal muscle shows marked fat replacement.

In dystrophin-deficient muscle, repeated cycles of necrosis and ineffective regeneration activate and expand fibro-adipogenic progenitors, which progressively differentiate into myofibroblasts and adipocytes, resulting in fibro-fatty replacement that is readily quantified by Dixon MRI. 22

This process may be further amplified by ongoing degeneration, poor vascularization, metabolic dysregulation, and reduced mechanical loading—particularly after loss of ambulation. 23

The myocardium, however, appears to follow a distinct remodeling trajectory.

Compared with skeletal muscle, the adult heart has limited regenerative capacity and relatively low adipogenic stromal progenitor activity. 22

When cardiomyocytes are lost in dystrophin deficiency, repair is therefore predominantly collagen-rich replacement fibrosis driven by activated cardiac fibroblasts.24,25

Given the heart’s continuous mechanical workload and the need for synchronized electrical conduction, fibrosis may help maintain structural integrity and electrical continuity, whereas adipocyte infiltration could be both mechanically and electrically destabilizing. 26

Together, these factors may explain why myocardial remodeling in our study was dominated by fibrosis rather than fat—even in advanced dysfunction—and why the cardiac environment may favor fibrogenesis over adipogenesis. 27

Although focal myocardial fat has been described in other cardiomyopathies (e.g., arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy), these arise through distinct mechanisms unrelated to dystrophinopathy. Focal myocardial fat deposition was not systematically assessed in the present study. 28

Relevant animal models provide additional context for our findings. In dystrophin-deficient rodent models, cardiac involvement is described predominantly by myocardial injury, inflammation, and fibrosis.29,30 By contrast, canine models such as golden retriever muscular dystrophy show progressive cardiomyopathy, and myocardial fatty or fibro-fatty infiltration can occur, particularly in more advanced disease.31,32

Taken together, these animal data suggest that myocardial remodeling in dystrophinopathy may vary by model and disease stage.

In the present adult human cohort, increased global myocardial FF was not detected with the present three-point Dixon CMR protocol, making it unlikely that we missed a broader myocardial fat-replacement phenotype, although small focal or late-stage fibro-fatty remodeling cannot be excluded.

In dystrophinopathy, cardiac involvement typically begins in the subepicardial inferolateral wall, where fibrosis predominates. 6

While small fat deposits may exist in these regions, they are often minimal and patchy. 33 Even regional Dixon CMR may be limited by partial volume effects, spatial resolution, and the dominance of fibrosis over adipose tissue—factors that collectively reduce the sensitivity for detecting myocardial fat. Taken together, these findings support a biological and imaging-level divergence between skeletal and cardiac muscle responses.

Fat replacement is a hallmark of skeletal muscle involvement in dystrophinopathies.

In the present study, increased global myocardial FF was not detected with the three-point Dixon CMR protocol, whereas fibrosis remained the predominant myocardial abnormality. Because myocardial FF was assessed globally and Dixon and LGE were acquired as separate sequences, small focal fatty changes within fibrotic myocardium may have been underestimated and cannot be excluded.

4.3. Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design and the relatively young BMD cohort may underestimate myocardial fat that develops later; fat replacement may appear after fibrosis or with older age.

Second, recruitment challenges may have introduced selection bias: several patients with BMD who had severe heart problems declined participation, likely leading to underrepresentation of those with the most advanced cardiac involvement. However, this is unlikely to have substantially affected our results, as our cohort still included some patients with severe cardiac problems, and we did not observe an association between myocardial FF and cardiac function.

Third, a combined Dixon-LGE approach could have been more informative for assessing whether fat and fibrosis were present in the same myocardial regions; however, because Dixon and LGE were acquired as separate sequences, direct spatial co-localization of fat and fibrosis was not possible, and small focal fatty changes within fibrotic myocardium may therefore have been underestimated.

Fourth, the study may have been underpowered to detect small between-group differences in myocardial FF, particularly in the BMD group. Although the original sample-size calculation yielded a planning estimate of approximately 32 participants, only 20 patients with BMD were enrolled, and 15 remained in the main analysis after exclusion of low-quality Dixon scans. These image-quality exclusions were necessary to improve measurement reliability, but they further reduced the analyzable sample size and may also limit the robustness and generalizability of the method in routine clinical practice. In addition, despite conservative contouring and quality control, residual artifacts may still have influenced myocardial FF estimates. Therefore, small true differences in myocardial FF cannot be excluded, although we found no evidence of a clear increase in myocardial FF in the present cohort.

4.4. Future directions

Although case reports and small series describe focal intramyocardial fat co-localizing with fibrosis in advanced cardiomyopathies, our data indicate that in patients with BMD and women carrying DMD gene variants, overt myocardial fat replacement is uncommon with our current CMR protocol.

The absence of excess myocardial FF in these groups indicates that Dixon-derived myocardial FF is unlikely to provide added value as a routine biomarker for cardiac surveillance.

A potential exception may be old patients with long-standing cardiac affection in whom fat replacement could occur, which merits a targeted study.

Cine imaging for ventricular function, LGE, native T1, and extracellular volume (ECV) remain the most informative CMR tools for detection, risk stratification, and follow-up.

5. Conclusion

Under a quality-controlled three-point Dixon CMR protocol at 3.0 T, left ventricular myocardial FF in patients with BMD and in women carrying pathogenic DMD gene variants was comparable to matched healthy controls and showed no association with ejection fraction, age, or skeletal muscle FF. These findings support a myocardial phenotype characterized primarily by fibrosis rather than fat replacement. Cardiac surveillance in dystrophinopathies should therefore continue to prioritize cine function, late gadolinium enhancement, and T1/ECV mapping.
