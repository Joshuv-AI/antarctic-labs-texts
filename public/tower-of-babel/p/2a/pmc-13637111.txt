Barriers to uptake of vaccination among communities in the city of Kigali, 2025
Authors: Amr Magdi Hassan, Asawir Mohammed, Hiba Makki, Hana Makki, Mounkaila Noma, Mamoun Homeida, Fatu Yumkella
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionImmunization is a fundamental health component preventing an estimated 2.5 million deaths annually. Despite the documented successes of immunization, disparities of vaccine uptake exist in low- and middle-income countries and across their respective communities. This research assessed the barriers related to recall-based vaccination uptake among communities in Kigali city, the capital of Rwanda.MethodsA mixed-methods study was implemented on a sample of 210 households and communities across the three districts of the City of Kigali. The quantitative study used a multistage sampling technique according to the WHO Immunization Coverage Survey to select the households from the districts of Kigali city. While the 3 focus group discussions and 6 interviews were implemented in communities not included in the quantitative survey. A thematic analysis was performed on the qualitative data. The quantitative data were analyzed using SPSS 23 and summarized numerically and graphically. Statistical tests performed for association were Chi-square tests and a binary logistic regression was used to establish the relationship between recall-based vaccine uptake and its associated factors. All statistical tests were considered significant when p < 0.05.ResultsQuantitative findings of this study reported that all 155 children ≤ 6 years were vaccinated. It also divided the barriers into four main categories: decision barriers, information barriers, accessibility barriers, and challenges in health facilities. A binary logistic regression predicted recall-based vaccine uptake based on 11 explanatory variables. The variables statistically significant were age and household size, with respective p < 0.001, and knowledge of vaccine-preventable diseases with a p-value of 0.038. Qualitatively, FGDs and interviews revealed fear of side effects, denial of vaccination in absence of husbands, long waiting time and poor communication from providers in facilities as factors that discourage attendance for immunization.ConclusionBarriers to recall-based categorization of vaccination frequency were mainly health system-related challenges, including insufficient supplies, costs for pregnant women for first-time vaccinations and denial vaccination in the absence of husbands. These findings call upon Rwandan health authorities and partners to address predictors of vaccination behavior associated with systemic challenges, intentional refusal and enhance immunization service delivery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28521-8.

Introduction

Vaccination has been recognized as one of the most effective, equitable, and cost-efficient public health interventions globally. Optimal vaccination uptake is the complete and timely vaccination among eligible populations, in line with national and global targets [1]. Despite the global success, disparities in vaccination uptake persist between low and middle-income countries and among their communities. In 2018, middle-income countries recorded the highest number of unvaccinated children, equating to 70% [2, 3]. Global evidence reported in systematic reviews demonstrates that barriers to uptake are multifaceted. Service delivery factors, not exhaustive, include supplies, health workforce factors, urban dynamics and health care seeking factors as well as caregiver knowledge gaps, safety concerns, perceived risks, cultural beliefs, misinformation, financial and geographical accessibility [4, 5, 6, 7].

The 2023 World Health Organization Immunization Agenda reports that 20 million infants have not completed the full course of basic vaccines. More than 80% of them are unvaccinated – the “zero dose” children [2]. According to the WHO/UNICEF Estimates of National Immunization Coverage, 60% of these children are located in ten countries. These countries include Nigeria, India, Pakistan, Indonesia, Ethiopia, Philippines, Democratic Republic of Congo (DR Congo), Brazil, Angola and Vietnam [8]. Despite its benefits, various barriers were identified, Bangura et al. [9] divided them into three main categories. These include Parental/caretaker barriers, health system barriers and providers’ barriers. Parental/caretaker barriers were further divided into parental perception, maternal education and occupation, socioeconomic status and influence of male partner. Health system barriers include irregular supplies, transportation and limited human resources. Providers’ attitude toward mothers was reported to be a major immunization barrier [2, 8, 9].

Elsewhere in the literature, African authors address the influence of knowledge and awareness, but few explain the barriers to vaccine uptake. Sibanda et al. [10] identified misperceptions as associated factors for vaccine uptake and demonstrated high impact of prior vaccination behavior and having a university education. Miyahara et al. [11] addressed the birth-dose timeliness and identified predictors of vaccine uptake, which were maternal education and distance to facility. Sibanda et al. [10] as well as Miyahara et al. [11] identified additional predictors of vaccination uptake, including age, gender, education level, transportation difficulties, trust in local healthcare workers, vaccine misinformation exposure and distance to health care facilities. Namuyonga et al. [12] reported additional predictors, among which older age groups, females, and individuals with tertiary education were more likely to be vaccinated. Transportation emerged as one of the dominant predictors of vaccination.

In East Africa, Rwanda is widely known for having strong political leadership, innovative delivery models, and high immunization coverage [13, 14]. Studies identified several cross-cutting themes. Despite high awareness of vaccines, knowledge gaps persist regarding vaccine schedules, side effects, and the purpose of newer vaccines. Trust in community health workers and national systems remains strong; however, inconsistency in messaging can undermine understanding and timely uptake. Economic and logistical constraints in households, including transport costs, waiting time, competing priorities, and household workload, continue to play a crucial role in delayed/missed vaccination. At the points of service, occasional stockouts and poor communication with healthcare providers reduced service reliability, and caregivers who encounter such obstacles may delay or abandon subsequent visits. Misinformation remains a significant barrier, particularly in urban and peri-urban settings, where digital information spreads quickly. Hence, community engagement strategies require continuous reinforcement to address evolving concerns and new vaccine introductions [15, 16, 17]. In this framework, despite the reported statistics of high child immunization in Rwanda of 95.5% [18], this research on barriers towards recall-based vaccination frequency among communities in Kigali city will also contribute to identifying the barriers, which apparently are unknown, to boost and improve the immunization coverage.

Methods

Study design

A mixed quantitative-qualitative community-based cross-sectional study was implemented in Kigali, the capital city of Rwanda.

Study area and population

Rwanda is a landlocked country with an area of 26,338 km²; it has five international borders (Burundi, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Tanzania, and Uganda). Administratively, the country is divided into 5 provinces, the study area is the province of Kigali, which harbor 13.2% of the country’s population (1,745,555/13,246,394). The province of Kigali, with an estimated population of 1,745,555 (86.9% urban and 13.1% rural) comprises three districts (administrative level 2) and is subdivided into sectors (35), cells (161), and villages (1,061). To avoid selection bias, all three districts of Kigali Province, namely Gasabo, Nyarugenge and Kicukiro, were included in the study.

Sampling size and technique

Quantitative section

The sampling technique used was a modified WHO/EPI Immunization Cluster Surveys Sampling technique [1]. According to this modification and with respect to the procedure of WHO/EPI, Kigali City province is administratively divided in 3 districts. A district was considered as a cluster, and in each cluster, a spatial sampling technique was used to select 10 geographically representative sectors. Then in each of the selected sectors, as shown in Fig. 1 below, 7 households were randomly selected (3 clusters x 10 sectors x 7 households).

Qualitative section

Three Focus group discussions (FGDs) were conducted in each of the three clusters (districts), targeting mothers of children < 5, ranging from 8 to 11 participants per group. Six interviews were also implemented in the three clusters, targeting religious/church leaders and traditional healers. Two interviews were conducted per cluster. Both FGDs and interviews were purposively biased to enable the inclusion of only participants residing in rural settings.

Data collection

The data were collected through a standardized, pre-tested research tool developed by the researcher in English language and administered in Kinyarwanda for easy understanding of the participants. The interviewed person was either the head him/herself, the spouse of the head of the household, or an adult member of the household (≥ 18 years). The quantitative data collected were the characteristics of the study participants (age, gender, education level, profession, residence, position in the household, number of children), the knowledge about vaccination (previous health education, source of information, recommended vaccines in Rwanda), and the perception, challenges and barriers towards vaccination uptake (recall-based). The focus group discussion and interview data were recorded in communities not covered by the quantitative research in order to obtain rural community perspectives on the knowledge of vaccine-preventable diseases, awareness of recommended vaccines, missed vaccinations, perceptions and challenges towards vaccine uptake, challenges in facilities and motivating factors for taking vaccines. When, for each of the 11 themes, the answers of 2–3 participants do not add any new information, the research team considered that pursuing the discussion would result in collecting unnecessary data. A threshold for getting new information was set when two or more focus group participants provided new information. Otherwise, the answers were considered redundant and were labelled as data saturation in this qualitative research.

The quantitative data were computerized through a template elaborated through Google Form and analyzed through the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS version 23). Qualitative data were collected as audio through a recorder operated by a moderator and a notetaker who spoke the language of participants (Kinyarwanda). Profiles of participants were obtained through an adopted guide from Dalan Development Consultants Firm, Sierra Leone, Qualitative Research in 2016. The moderator and notetaker transcribed the recorded data in English language. This data was coded by four researchers and comparisons were done to assess consistency, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus. Data were then digitalized in Microsoft Excel for further analysis.

Data analysis

Qualitative data

The data collected consisted of six main probing questions on identifying the demographic profile of participants, their views about vaccination willingness, hesitancy, barriers, challenges and enablers for vaccine uptake. This data was collected in Kinyarwanda through a standardized tool and tape recordings which were transcribed in English and entered into Microsoft Excel. On entering the standardized tool information with the transcribed recordings, the data were triangulated to get a comprehensive view of each participant in the focus group. The researchers adopted a hybrid, deductive and inductive methodology to code the entered information. A theoretical framework and concepts were developed from the original probing questions by a data coding team. Upon review of the entered data, responses were compared against the framework to identify the new themes that emerged from the respondents. Therefore, the final themes were a combination of the initial framework and new themes identified by the respondents. They were namely Knowledge of recommended vaccines, Vaccine hesitancy, Challenges to accessing vaccines, Challenges faced by special groups, Missed vaccinations and Motivating factors to taking vaccines. Final thematic summaries were triangulated with quantitative findings to enrich interpretation.

Quantitative data

Descriptive statistics summarized quantitative data numerically in terms of mean, standard deviation and median, and graphically in form of frequency tables and graphs. Associations between variables were assessed through Chi-Square tests, correlation, and ANOVA where appropriate. A binary logistic regression analysis was performed to establish the relationship between recall-based vaccine uptake and its associated factors. The outcome variable was recall-based vaccine uptake, which was created after cross-tabulation of the household vaccination status per year with the household age profile. The household vaccination status was recorded into 13 categories according to the year vaccination was reported to have been received. Whereas the household age profile was categorized into 3 groups, which were namely ≤ 6 years, 7–18 years and > 18 years. The result of the cross-tabulation was further regrouped into three categories and labelled as recall-based vaccine uptake (theoretical variable which conveys a theoretical concept of recalled vaccine frequency), as demonstrated in Fig. 2. The three categories were distributed as follows: those reported to be vaccinated in 1 year were coded as 1; they represent 38% of the study population (80/210). The second category was those reported to be vaccinated in 2 consecutive years and coded 2 (57%, 119/210). The third category coded 3 were those reported to be vaccinated over > 2 consecutive years (5%, 11/210). Furthermore, the theoretical concept variables of recalled vaccine frequency were transformed into an epidemiological variable to enable quantifying statistically the recall-based vaccine uptake. Therefore, the second and third categories were combined into one variable named reported vaccination in ≥ 2 consecutive years and coded 1. The final dichotomous variable of recall-based vaccine uptake was therefore coded as 0 for those who reported vaccination in 1 year, and 1 for those reported vaccination in ≥ 2 consecutive years. This definition of recall-based vaccine uptake is not an operational definition, as it was self-reported data from adult household members. Therefore, its operational use cannot allow the estimation of immunization completeness or timeliness. It provides the overall status/magnitude of vaccine uptake in the study population. Therefore, it is subject to vaccine uptake recall bias, and the transition across age groups cannot be precisely estimated. Knowledge, attitudes, and practices of vaccination uptake

Vaccination history and knowledge patterns

Vaccination history and practices of participants varied significantly by household structure. Households with children ≤ 6 years represent 73.8% (155/210), children 7–11 were 6.7% (14/210), and with children ≥ 12 years were 19.5% (41/210). Among these categories, all 155 children aged ≤ 6 years were reported to benefit from vaccines. Vaccination rates were high during the COVID-19 pandemic years, with 31% coverage in 2020–2022. Vaccine knowledge among respondents identified 73.8% (155/210) moderate knowledge, 22.9% (48/210) good knowledge and 3.3% (7/210) poor. Attitudes of participants show that 99.5% believe in the effectiveness of vaccines, with 96.7% rating their knowledge as good/moderate (Table 2).

Most of the participants were able to identify, HPV (100%, 210/210), Polio (99.5%, 209/210), Hepatitis B (99.5%, 209/210), Pneumococcus infection (98.6%, 201/210), Tetanus (96.7%, 203/210), and Measles (94.8%, 199/210) as vaccine-prevented diseases (Supplement 1). These findings were supported by FGDs and interviews that revealed general awareness on vaccinations recommended for children, where a very high number of respondents were able to indicate the various vaccination schedules.

Attitudes and practices toward vaccination uptake

Pain was the predominant concern for 84.3% (177/210) of the participants. Approximately, 58% (58.6%, 123/210) were concerned about allergic reactions, 54.3% (114/210) about fever and 18.1% (38/210) had no concerns or fears. Participants reported that information on vaccination was predominantly obtained through mass media (96.7%, 203/210), healthcare workers (95.2%, 200/210) and friends, co-workers or neighbors (94.8%, 199/210). Secondary sources of information were reported to be schools and social media. 10% (10%, 21/210) of the participant received the information from religious and traditional leaders. Information provided by health care workers included side effects (100%, 210/210), when to get vaccinated (97.1%, 204/210), where to get vaccinated (96.7%, 203/210), recommended vaccines (95.7%, 201/210), and instructions on vaccination card use (95.2%, 200/210) (Supplement 2). FGDs and interviews revealed variation in the perception and willingness toward vaccination.

While majority recognized vaccines as preventive measures, some showed concerns and were inclined towards the use of herbal remedies.

“I’m less confident about children’s vaccines because they are not produced locally, and I sometimes worry that foreign vaccines could contain unknown substances. Vaccines are important, and I understand their value. However, in our church, many people still hesitate because they believe vaccines may contain hidden symbols or technologies related to the devil.” (Church leader, KII, Nyarugenge).

Barriers to vaccination uptake as reported by participants

The barriers towards vaccination were categorized into decision-related barriers, information barriers, accessibility barriers, and challenges in health facilities, as described in Table 3. Fear of side effects dominated decision-related barriers, representing 91.4% (192/210) of the participants, while fear of injection represented 90% (189/210). Efficacy doubts such as misconceptions and disbeliefs were minimal (13.3%, 28/210). Religious and traditional beliefs were barriers to 3.3% (7/210), while family influence was not a reported as a barrier. Accessibility challenges were divided into transport cost/time (10.9%, 23/210) and cost of vaccines (0.5%, 1/210). Majority (93.8%, 197/210) of the participants obtained their vaccination in health care centers. 86% (86.2%, 181/210) of participants have vaccination facilities within 1–5 km, and more than half (71.4%, 150/210) use motorcycle to reach their destination. Long waiting time (82.4%, 173/210) was the main health facility challenge reported. FGDs and interviews revealed that religious beliefs, misinformation, and service-related challenges were the base of vaccine hesitancy. Herbal medicine is believed by many, as reported by traditional healers and religious leaders, to treat and prevent diseases. Discussions have revealed that participants’ beliefs are majorly influenced by the church and fear of side effects/ poor medical service discourage vaccination.

“Most people I receive with hesitation about vaccines are those with some side effects like skin rashes asking if I have a drug that can replace vaccine but I didn’t encourage them to not get vaccinated, instead I help them with these manifestations.” (Traditional healer, KII, Gasabo).

The outcome variable recall-based vaccine uptake, coded as 1 when reported vaccination in one year, 2 when reported vaccination in 2 consecutive years, and 3 when reported vaccination in > 2 consecutive years, was regrouped as a dichotomous variable, with values of 0 for reported vaccination in one year and 1 for reported vaccination in ≥ 2 consecutive years.

The normal value of r2 ranges between 0 and 100%, with a higher r2 indicating a better fit. This shows that our model fits the equation moderately up to 34.0%. The likelihood test revealed a Chi-Square value of 60.357, with a p < 0.001, indicating that despite the model moderately fitting the equation, the 11 predictors selected are significantly representative of the data. Discussion

This study revealed the patterns and barriers affecting recall-based vaccination uptake in Kigali city. The barriers were grouped into four major barriers affecting communities. They were decision barriers, information barriers, accessibility, and health facility challenges. Decision-related barriers were the most frequently reported and largely modifiable; they include fear of side effects, fear of injection, efficacy doubts, religious and traditional beliefs. Information barriers encompassed patient- healthcare workers communication on side effects, when and where to get vaccinated, recommended vaccine, utilization of vaccination card and children’s growth pattern. Accessibility barriers were linked to transportation cost and time, location of vaccination centre, distance to the nearest facility and means of transport. Health facility challenges were long waiting time, absences from work for vaccination, expensive vaccines, and shortage of vaccines.

The identified decision-related barriers such as sociocultural beliefs and efficacy doubts align with the existing evidence on vaccine hesitancy. The refusal of vaccination by traditional healers and religious leaders observed in this study mirrors the findings of Iwu et al. [19], who reported the prioritization of herbal treatment and demonstrated the communities’ trust in traditional remedies. In the literature, studies established the significant influence of religious leaders and traditional healers, thus advocating for critical integration to enhance vaccine legitimacy and acceptance [19, 20]. Facility challenges identified in this study were vaccine stockouts, lack of information on service days and long waiting hours, as published by Bangura et al. [9], who described them as health system barriers that hinder immunization service delivery. This study findings pointed out distance of facilities as a challenge. It also stressed the importance of the attitude of health workers and their communication with healthcare beneficiaries as critical factors for vaccine uptake. Knowledge of vaccine-preventable diseases was generally high for the core childhood vaccines under the Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI) in Rwanda, such as Measles, Polio, Tetanus, and Hepatitis B, reflecting strong national investment in vaccination education. Reports from Rwanda Biomedical Center and UNICEF documented the gender differences in vaccination knowledge, with females demonstrating higher levels of awareness [13, 14]. In the findings of this study, this was reflected and could be explained by the women-led caregiving roles within households. Secondary education or higher was reported by majority of the participants and was associated with increased vaccine awareness. These findings align with Trepanowski and Drazkowski [21] who pointed out the association between education and vaccination uptake.

Regarding the trend of reported recall-based vaccine uptake, from 2020 to 2025, maybe related to the past experience of COVID-19 outbreak and, later on, the recent outbreaks of Monkeypox and Marburg disease (no recommended vaccines yet available), which the country experienced subsequently in 2024 and 2025. This may be supported by the qualitative research across the replies of the religious leaders, who mostly recognized that vaccination in case of epidemics is a governmental decision, and ethically, all citizens should comply.

This study identified three statistically significant predictors of recall-based vaccine uptake, which were age (OR = 0.855, 95% [CI: 0.797–0.917], p = < 0.001), household size (OR = 3.277, 95% [CI: 2.151–4.991], p = < 0.001), and knowledge of vaccine-preventable diseases (OR = 2.955, 95% [CI: 1.062–8.22], p = 0.038). The regression analysis findings revealed that seven other predictors explained recall-based vaccine uptake by < 2 times. The contribution of transportation challenges of > 2 times (OR = 2.823, 95% [CI: 0.825–9.655], p = 0.098) was not considered due to its wide confidence interval. Namuyonga et al. [12] reported predictors of vaccine uptake, which were older age groups (OR = 2.94, p < 0.001), females (OR = 1.63, p = 0.001), individuals with tertiary education (OR = 4.26, p < 0.001) which more likely to be vaccinated and absence of transportation difficulties (OR = 5.97, p < 0.001).

The predominance of modifiable decision-related barriers emphasize the importance of strengthening immunization communication strategies within the national policies of Rwanda. Health promotion packages should include culturally tailored communication tools, unified information, in addition to appropriately trained healthcare workers in delivery of messages, counselling and interpersonal communication. This will efficiently address fears of injections, side effects and reinforce trust in vaccines. Engagement and integration with traditional religious systems within communities would further boost uptake by aligning beliefs with health outcomes and goals. The role of schools as the primary source of information is paramount, hence necessitating the evaluation of the quality of information delivered to ensure informed confidence in vaccines rather than hesitancy and uncertainty. Furthermore, as recommended by many respondents in themes of motivating factors toward vaccination, addressing facility barriers such as waiting time, vaccine availability, and flexible waiting hours could improve uptake among special groups such as pregnant women and older populations.

Limitations

A limitation of this study was related to the mixed-methods design, which included a quantitative cross-sectional community-based survey of 210 adult participants reporting immunization uptake in their households. In a country like Rwanda, where population data are available at all levels (administrative level 1/Province to administrative level 5/Village), a multistage sampling technique could be more appropriate for implementation, as it will enable the combination, according to the administrative levels, of various probability sampling techniques. This may end up providing a more representative sample size, with its associated degree of accuracy and the power of the sample size calculation. However, the method used was a modified WHO/EPI survey method, which was more appropriate for a rapid survey, and in the best case, would provide a sample size ranging from 300 to 400 participants. Therefore, most often, it should be restricted to descriptive statistics and inferential statistics limited to associations rather than demonstrating a relation-based. We acknowledge the limitations for generalization in future studies. A second crucial limitation of the mixed methods quantitative-qualitative design, in which the qualitative study was limited only to the rural areas and not executed at the same time as the quantitative study, but performed back-to-back. However, as the qualitative study provides information from the rural community perspective, this design missed the information from the urban communities survey, with a selection bias. In such mixed-methods, both quantitative and qualitative studies should be implemented in both rural and urban populations to strengthen the triangulation, which is one of the robust methods to assess the validity and reliability (worthiness) of qualitative findings.

Moreover, the qualitative study was limited to a few focus group discussions and interviews. Hence, the quantitative study findings were not linked with the qualitative study. The quantitative survey, which was the driving method in this mixed study, has a limitation as the answers provided by the household members were not tested through the Cronbach’s alpha test for reliability and validity. Furthermore, the thematic analysis has not been performed through a software for thematic analysis (e.g. NVivo). It was an expert opinion which led to the grouping of the qualitative findings in themes.

Another limitation was related to the research survey template, which was a Google form which aggregated the variables with multiple answers. Therefore, the data curation and compilation had been hectic and may introduce bias in defining some key predictors.

Additional crucial limitation of this study was the outcome variable called recall-based vaccine uptake, while it is not the standard definition of vaccine uptake as full immunization status, age-appropriate vaccination and perception proportion could not be derived. Therefore, future research should use the standard definition of vaccine uptake. Nevertheless, this research, being a mixed-methods study, can be considered as an explorative study to better understand the barriers related to vaccination uptake. Future research can employ a mixed-methods quantitative-qualitative design to be implemented in the same communities to ensure triangulation and to facilitate the link between quantitative and qualitative data for estimating vaccination coverage.

Conclusion

This study addressed the barriers to vaccination, beyond early childhood, related to decision-making, communication, and the health systems. These findings call upon Rwandan health authorities and its partners to sustain and enhance vaccination uptake through targeted communication strategies, improvements in service delivery and community engagement.
