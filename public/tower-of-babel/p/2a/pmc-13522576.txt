Total phenolics, flavonoids and antioxidant activity in thirty litchi genotypes of Eastern India
Authors: Narayan Lal, Abhay Kumar, Alemwati Pongener, S. D. Pandey, Bikash Das, Vishal Nath
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A good amount of phenolics have been found in fruit of litchi which shows diverse biological activities. The purpose of this present study was to evaluate phenolics and antioxidant activity in 30 different genotypes of litchi growing at National Active Germplasm Site, ICAR-NRC on Litchi, Muzaffarpur. The total phenolics (0.20 to 124.99 mg GAE/ g FW) and total flavonoids (0.50 to 96.37 mg Catechin/g FW) varied significantly among genotypes in different parts of plant. Total phenolics in thirty genotypes varied from 18.20 to 48.7 mg GAE/g, 46.10 to 107.30 mg GAE/g, 62.70 to 115.50 mg GAE/g, 19.82 to 124.99 mg GAE/g, 0.20 to 8.41 mg GAE/g, 8.57 to 61.24 mg GAE/g, 23.68 to 86.56 mg GAE/g, in inflorescence, flower bud, new and mature leaf, pulp, pericarp and seed, respectively. The total flavonoids contents in thirty genotypes ranged from 8.61 to 21.11 mg CAE/g, 17.26 to 46.79 mg CAE /g, 2.29 to 23.02 mg CAE /g, 8.15 to 26.55 mg CAE/g, 0.50 to 23.88 mg CAE/g, 0.75 to 96.49 mg CAE/g, 2.41 to 27.50 mg CAE/g in inflorescence, flower bud, new and mature leaf, pulp, pericarp and seed, respectively. A 32.3-fold difference in phenolics in pulp was found between highest and lowest genotypes, IC-0615586 and IC-0615600 (p < 0.05). A 7.14-fold difference in phenolic in pericarp was found between highest and lowest genotypes, IC-0615613 and IC-0615606 (p < 0.05). A 47.7-fold more difference in flavonoids in pulp was found between highest and lowest genotypes, IC-0615613 and Coll. 36 (p < 0.05). A 128.6-fold more difference in flavonoids in pericarp was found between highest and lowest genotypes, IC-0615610 and Coll. 37 (p < 0.05). Total antioxidant capacity in pulp varied from 3.39 to 7.8 mol TE/ g FW with highest in genotype IC-0615596 and lowest in genotype IC-0615600 and DPPH radical-scavenging activities varied from 25.63 to 73.15% with highest in genotype IC-0615593 and lowest in genotype IC-0615594. Similarly, total antioxidant capacity of pericarp varied from 6.35 to 10.28 mol TE/ g FW with highest in genotype IC-0615591 and lowest in IC-0615597 and DPPH radical-scavenging activity varied from 56.75 to 90.35% with highest in IC-0615585. A significant (p<0.01) positive relationship was found between antioxidant and total phenolics in pulp (r = 0.708) and total phenolics in pericarp (r = 0.571). Similarly, radicle scavenging activity of pulp was positively correlated with phenolics in pulp (r = 0.552) and antioxidant activity (r = 0.838) in pulp. This information on diverse accessions can help breeders select superior litchi varieties as well as in parental selection in crop improvement programmes.

Introduction

Fruits, vegetables, and other plants are important sources of polyphenols which impart colour and flavour, and defend a plant from abiotic (ultraviolet rays) and biotic (pathogens) stresses1. The consumption of vegetables and fruits can reduce risk of chronic cardiovascular diseases like cancer, diabetes, and neurodegenerative diseases which are associated with oxidative stress2, 3. The fruits and vegetables are rich in phenolics which possess potent antioxidant activity. Bioactive compound of plant can reduce the oxidative damage of biomolecules and control the gene expression by regulating reactive oxygen species4. Litchi (Litchi chinensis Sonn.; Family- Sapindaceae) is a subtropical fruit tree native to China and now widely cultivated throughout the World. It is a source of livelihood for millions in Bihar, India. Litchi is mainly grown in northern parts of India where it is available in the market during summer season. Flowering in litchi commences during February-March in North India and August-September in South India. Its attractive red or pink coloured fruit, and delicious blend of sweet and acidic taste, and health benefits make litchi a preferred and popular fruit among consumers. The pericarp and seed accounts for approximately 20% and 15%, respectively, by weight. The pericarp contains good amounts of total phenolics (7.5 to 62.2 mg GAE /g )5. The phenolics content in leaf boosts flowering in litchi. The phenolics of litchi pericarp are confirmed to have antioxidant and anticancer properties6, and immunomodulatory7 activities. Litchi pericarp is, thus, considered an important source of pharmaceuticals. A negligible attempt has been made to assess the phenolics content and antioxidant capacity of litchi grown in India. Both quantitative and qualitative information on phenolics and their antioxidant activity in various parts of litchi are scanty. Pulp phenolic content and their antioxidant activity has been investigated in northern Mauritius8. Little is known about the complete profiles of phenolic compounds and antioxidant activity of Indian litchi. Furthermore, the time of fruit maturation among litchi cultivars is different, which also differs from one region to the other, exhibiting both temporal and spatial differences. Thus, composition of phenolic and their activities are supposed to differ because of differences in cultivation environment and genotypes. Therefore, the present study was attempted to characterize litchi genotypes based on their bioactive (phenolics, flavonoids and antioxidant activity) compounds and assess the level of variations in the content of phenolics and flavonoids in different parts of fruits and plants of target genotypes. Litchi, being an introduced fruit in India, has narrow genetic base and presents considerable hurdles in crop improvement. This study will be helpful to generate baseline data on bioactive compounds and antioxidant activities of litchi genotypes and accessions, and will be valuable information in the development of new varieties, as well as in food and pharmaceutical applications.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

The Inflorescence at full grown stage, flower bud (M1 Male flower) before a day of anthesis, new young leaf (20–25 days old) and old mature leaf were collected during 10th March to 20th March from thirty different genotypes of litchi planted at ICAR-National Research Centre on Litchi, Muzaffarpur, Bihar. Twenty fresh fruit from each genotype were collected at optimum harvest maturity stage during 25th May to 10th June. The fruits were transported to the laboratory, peeled, and separated into different components of peel, pulp, and seed. An electronic weighing balance (AND GH-252, Made in Japan) was used to record the weights of different components.

Chemical and reagents

Gallic acid, catechin, folin-ciocalteu reagent (1 N), sodium carbonate (20%), ethanol (80%), sodium nitrite (5%), aluminum chloride (10%), sodium hydroxide (1 M), 1,1-Diphenyl-2- picryldydrazyl (DPPH), copper (II) chloride solution, neocuproine solution, ammonium acetate buffer solution were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Co. (New Delhi, India). All other chemicals used in this study were of analytical grade and obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Co. (New Delhi, India).

Morphological characterization

Morphological characters like fruit weight, percentage of pericarp, colour of the fruits and maturity (each variety represented by random collection of 20 fruits from 3 different trees) were recorded. Fruit weight was recorded with the help of electronic weighing balance (AND GH-252, Made in Japan) and percentage of pericarp was computed against weight. Maturity of fruit was calculated from fruit set to harvest and colour of fruit at harvest was recorded as per litchi descriptor, IPGRI.

Extraction

The experiments were performed in the crop improvement lab of ICAR-NRC on Litchi, Muzaffarpur, Bihar. Briefly, 5 g of each sample inflorescence, flower bud, new leaf, old mature leaf, peel, seed and 10 g pulp were weighed in electronic weighing balance and ground with 5 ml of 80% ethanol. The samples were homogenized for 3 min using a Polytron homogenizer, and extracted for 2 h at room temperature. The aliquot was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min and supernatant was collected and stored. The supernatant was concentrated under vacuum by a rotary evaporator at 45 °C. The dried extract was dissolved in 50 ml of water used for further analysis.

Determination of total phenolic content

The total phenolics contents in extracts were estimated using a modified colorimetric Folin-Ciocalteu method9. Briefly, extract (100 µl) was taken in test tube and added deionized water (3.0 ml) and 0.5 ml of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent (1 N). After 3 min, 20% sodium carbonate solution (2 ml) was added, and the mixture was kept for 90 min at room temperature before measurement at 750 nm using a Shimadzu UV-1800 UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Tokyo, Japan). The measurement values were compared with standard curve of gallic acid solutions and expressed as milligrams of gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per gram fresh weight (FW).

Determination of total flavonoids

The total flavonoids content was estimated as described by Sethi et al.,9. Briefly, extract (1 ml) was taken in test tube and distilled water (4 ml) and 5% sodium nitrite solution (0.3 ml) was added to each tube. After standing for 5 min, 10% aluminium chloride (0.3 ml) was added to the solution and allowed to react for 6 min. Then, 1.0 M sodium hydroxide (2 ml) was added, and the mixture was diluted with another 2.4 ml of distilled water. The absorbance of the mixture was measured at 510 nm and compared with standard curve of catechin solution and flavonoid content was expressed in milligrams of catechin equivalents (CAE) per gram fresh weight (FW).

Determination of radical scavenging activity

Radical scavenging activity [DPPH (2, 2-diphenyl-1- picrylhydrazyl)] was determined with following method of Brand-Williams et al.,10. DPPH solution (0.0634 mM) in 95% methanol was prepared and 3.9 ml DPPH was added to 0.1 ml of sample extract. The change in absorbance of the sample was recorded at 515 nm for 30 min against 95% methanol as blank, till the absorbance reached a steady state. Radical scavenging activity (%) was calculated as (A0 -A1)/A0 where, A0 was the initial absorbance at 515 nm and A1 was the final absorbance of the same volume of sample extract.

Determination of antioxidant activity

Total antioxidant capacity was determined following CUPRAC (Cupric reducing antioxidant capacity) assay of Apak et al.,11. About 0.1 ml extract of sample (in 80% ethanol) was added in a test tube in which 1 ml each of copper (II) chloride solution, ammonium acetate buffer solution, neocuproine solution and distilled water were already mixed. Then, mixture was kept for 30 min and the absorbance was read at 450 nm in a spectrophotometer. The result was expressed as mol Trolox equivalent g− 1 FW.

Statistical analysis

Means ± standard deviations were expressed and then analysed by SPSS version 16 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Duncan’s New Multiple-range test were employed to analyse the differences among the means. To investigate the relationship between fruit weight and phytochemicals attributes, Correlation coefficient for quantitative traits was calculated using method of Pearson correlation coefficient. Relationships among morphological characters in litchi accessions were determined by principal component analysis (PCA). Cluster analysis was performed based on Euclidean distance using unweighted pair-group method of arithmetic aver-ages (UPGMA) by cluster function in R software v 3.1.3. Scatter plot was prepared according to the PC1 and PC2 using R software.

Results and discussion

Morphological characterization

Variation in morphological characters of the fruits of different litchi genotypes was recorded (Table 1). Fruit weight varied from 15.55 to 25.76 g with the highest in IC-0615585 and lowest in IC-0615595. The proportion of pericarp varied among genotypes with maximum in IC-0615595 (36.96%) and lowest in IC-0615587 (12.89%). Fruit colour varied from reddish yellow (46.66% ) to dark red depending on the genotype. Furthermore, genotypes were grouped into two categories, i.e. Early and Late, based on maturity. The variation in fruit weight of litchi was reported by earlier workers12, 13. Fruit size is a genetic character of litchi cultivars and it is used for identification and discrimination among cultivars. The variation in fruit size was also reported by earlier workers14. Litchi pericarp accounts for about 15% to its total fresh weight15. The fruit colour is also a good indicator for the maturity period but colour of fruit varies with the cultivars and is significantly influenced by the growing conditions. Singh and Nath,16 reported green to dark red fruit colour in litchi. Variation in fruit colour has been reported by Wei et al.,17. Madhou et al.,18 also reported early and late type genotypes of litchi in Mauritius.

Contents of total phenolics and flavonoids of litchi genotypes

Total phenolics varied significantly (p < 0.05) among genotypes (Table 2). Total phenolic contents of the thirty genotypes varied from 18.20 to 48.7 mg GAE/g, 46.10 to 107.30 mg GAE/g, 62.70 to 115.50 mg GAE/g, 19.82 to 124.99 mg GAE/g, 0.20 to 8.41 mg GAE/g,8.57 to 61.24 mg GAE/g, 23.68 to 86.56 mg GAE/g, with an average of 32.27 mg GAE/g, 69.67 mg GAE/g, 90.95 mg GAE/g, 56.63 mg GAE/g, 1.41 mg GAE/g, 31.18 mg GAE/g and 46.33 mg GAE/g in inflorescence, flower bud, new and mature leaf, pulp, pericarp and seed, respectively.

Mature leaf exhibited higher level of total phenolics as compared to new leaf, inflorescence, flower bud, seed, pericarp and pulp (Table 1). For instance, mature leaf of genotype IC-0615611 showed highest phenolics (124.99 mg GAE/g FW) but least was found in pulp (0.20 mg GAE/g FW) of genotype IC-0615600. The coefficient of variation (CV) of phenolic content in litchi genotypes was 41.32%, 33.72%, 20.56%, 106.75%, 689.11%, 176.73% and 66.88% for inflorescence, flower bud, new and mature leaf, pulp, pericarp and seed, respectively, indicating that there were significant genotype differences in phenolic contents in mature leaf, pulp, pericarp and seed among the litchi genotypes. A 2.6-fold difference in phenolic in inflorescence was found between highest and lowest genotypes, IC-0615604 and IC-0615590 (p < 0.05) and 2.32 fold more phenolics in flower bud was found between highest and lowest genotype IC-0615591 and IC-0615590.

A 1.84 fold more difference in phenolic in new leaf was found between highest and lowest genotypes, IC-0615587 and IC-0615586 (p < 0.05) and 6.30 fold more phenolic in old leaf was recorded in genotype IC-0615611 as compare to genotype IC-0615589 with lowest phenolic content (p < 0.05). Similarly, 32.3-fold difference in phenolics in pulp was found between highest and lowest genotypes, IC-0615586 and IC-0615600 (p < 0.05). A 7.14 fold difference in phenolic in pericarp was found between highest and lowest genotypes, IC-0615613 and IC-0615606 (p < 0.05) and 3.65 fold difference in phenolics in seed was found between the highest and lowest genotypes, IC-0615597 and Coll. 35. Li et al., 19 reported that total phenolic contents of the nine varieties of Chinese litchi varied from 9.39 to 30.16 mg GAE/g FW in litchi pericarp. Feng et al.,20 concluded that the total phenol ranged from 5.1 to 7.4 mg/g in Chinese litchi. Duan et al.,21 also found phenolics in litchi pericarp tissues after purification was about 11.8 mg/g fresh weight. Zhang,22 found total phenolic contents varied from 10.15 to 25.91 mg of gallic acid equivalents/ g in thirteen varieties. The phenolic content of litchi grown in northern Mauritius was reported at 28.8 ± 1.7 mg GAE/100 g of FW8 found that the total soluble phenolic content of litchi pulp in southern Florida was 77.01 mg GAE/100 g of FW. In our study, genotype IC-0615613 exhibited highest content of phenolics (61.24 mg/g) which was much more than Chinese litchi. This indicated that the bioactive compounds in litchi pericarp varied greatly different cultivars. The different parts of litchi exhibited high phenolics contents which create problem of browning in the micropropagation of litchi when these parts used as ex plants23–26.

The flavonoids profiles of the thirty litchi genotypes are shown in Table 2. The total flavonoids contents of the thirty genotypes ranged from 8.61 to 21.11 mg CAE/g, 17.26 to 46.79 mg CAE /g, 2.29 to 23.02 mg CAE /g, 8.15 to 26.55 mg CAE/g, 0.50 to 23.88 mg CAE/g,0.75 to 96.49 mg CAE/g, 2.41 to 27.50 mg CAE/g, with an average of 14.55 mg CAE /g, 28.42 mg CAE/g, 9.49 mg CAE/g, 17.49 mg CAE /g, 4.83 mg CAE/g, 24.66 mg CAE /g and 9.91 mg CAE/g in inflorescence, flower bud, new and mature leaf, pulp, pericarp and seed, respectively (Table 2). Like total phenolic contents, total flavonoid content also varied among the genotypes.

The genotype IC-0615587 had the highest flavonoid content (21.11 mg CAE/g FW) in inflorescence, with a 2.45-fold difference compared to the genotype with the lowest flavonoid content (IC-0615585, p < 0.05) and genotype IC-0615591 had the highest flavonoid content (46.79 mg CAE/g FW) in flower bud, with a 2.71-fold difference compared to the genotype with the lowest flavonoid content (Coll. 38, p < 0.05). A 10-fold more difference in phenolic in new leaf was found between highest and lowest genotypes, IC-0615611 and IC-0615592 (p < 0.05).A 3.25 fold more difference in phenolic in old leaf was found between highest and lowest genotypes, Coll. 36 and IC-0615606 (p < 0.05). A 47.7 fold more difference in phenolic in pulp was found between the highest and lowest genotypes, IC-0615613 and Coll. 36 (p < 0.05). A 128.6 fold more difference in phenolics in pericarp was recorded between highest and lowest genotypes, IC-0615610 and Coll. 37 (p < 0.05). A 11.4 fold more difference in phenolic in inflorescence was found between highest and lowest genotypes, IC-0615586 and IC-0615597 (p < 0.05). The present results clearly showed that there was a great diversity in total phenolics and flavonoid contents.

Different parts of litchi exhibited flavonoids contents. The flavonoid compounds have been extracted from litchi27, 28. The mature leaf exhibited higher level of total phenolics as compared to other parts, however, the phenolics and flavonoids varied among the genotypes (Tables 2 and 3). For instance, old leaf portion of the IC-0615611 showed highest phenolics (124.99 mg GAE/g fw) but lowest was noted in IC-0615589 (19.82 mg GAE/g fw). Similarly, the maximum total flavonoids content was recorded in the flower bud portion of the IC-0615591 (46.79 mg CAE/g FW) and lowest in IC-0615587 (17.26 mg CAE/g FW) (Table 3). The variation in pericarp phenolic content was also reported by Prasad et al.,29 with 16 mg/g in Baila variety and by Ruenroengklin et al.,30 with 100 mg/g in Feizixiao variety on dry weight basis. Li et al., 19 reported total phenolics content in pericarp varied from 9.39 to 30.16 mg GAE/g and average phenolics 13.90 mg/g reported by Duan et al.,21 which are lower than Indian litchi genotypes (8.57 to 61.24 mg GAE/g). These differences might be due to different cultivars or the methodology used for analyses of phytochemical. The total phenolic contents of tested litchi genotypes were comparable with grape seed with 1.4–22.3 mg GAE/g31 and skin with 4.9–13.8 mg GAE/g32. Therefore, pericarp of litchi fruit is excellent source for phenolic compounds. For human diet, flavonoids are common group of polyphenolic compounds and are also found ubiquitously in plants. They are commonly known for their antioxidant activities and flavonoids might play a protective role in preventing breast cancer33, 34. Li et al., 19 reported that total flavonoid of nine varieties of Chinese litchi ranged from 7.12 to 23.46 mg CAE/g FW in litchi pericarp. Feng et al.,20 concluded total flavonoid ranged from 5.8 to 7.8 mg/g in Chinese litchi. The cultivars of ‘Caomi’ and ‘Ziniangxi’ possessed the highest and lowest content of total flavonoid. In our study, genotype IC-0615613 exhibited highest flavonoids (96.37 mg/g) which is much more than Chinese litchi. The concentration of phenolics and flavonoids in litchi varied greatly among different parts of genotypes. Pericarp exhibited more content of phenolics than pulp in present study. The variations in phenolics content in pericarp and pulp have been reported by Wang et al.,35 in litchi and Boyer and Liu and36 in apple.

Radical scavenging activity (%)

The DPPH radical-scavenging activities of litchi pulp and pericarp of selected genotypes are shown in Figs. 1 and 2. The radical-scavenging activities in pulp varied from 25.63 to 73.15 (%). The genotype IC-0615593 had the most effective DPPH radical scavenging activity, with a 2.85-fold difference observed when compared to the genotype with the lowest DPPH radical-scavenging activity (IC-0615594, p < 0.05). Similarly, DPPH radical scavenging activity in pericarp was highest in genotype IC-0615585 (90.35%) and lowest in genotype IC-0615600 (56.75%). Pericarp exhibited more DPPH radical scavenging activity than pulp. Thus, pericarp can be used as valuable source of antioxidant. Several fruit crops are used as important sources of antioxidants. These valuable properties make litchi pulp and pericarp a potential source for nutraceutical development.

Total antioxidant capacity

As shown in Fig. 3, the CUPRAC values of pulp in thirty litchi genotypes ranged from 3.39 to 7.80 mol TE/1 g FW, averaging 5.42 mol TE/g FW. The highest CUPRAC value was observed in genotype IC-0615596 (7.80 mol TE/ g) as expected from its high contents of total phenolics, with a 2.3-fold difference compared to the genotype with the lowest CUPRAC value (IC-0615600, p < 0.05). Similarly, the CUPRAC values of pericarp in thirty litchi genotypes ranged from 6.35 to 10.28 mol TE/1 g FW with highest in genotype IC-0615595 and lowest in genotype IC-0615597 (Fig. 4).

Litchi pericarp showed highest antioxidant activity than pulp. Soong and Barlow,38 also exhibited highest value of antioxidant activity of litchi waste than pulp. The variation in phenolic content and antioxidant activity has been reported in litchi fruit pericarp39 and seeds40. The genetic and environmental factors play important roles in content of phenolic compounds. Significant varietal differences in phenolic contents of Chinese litchi were recorded by many workers and present investigation also showed variation in phenolics content in litchi. These genotypic discrepancies are due to genetic make-up of genotypes (Table 4).

Correlation studies among different traits in litchi and antioxidant

While developing relationship between fruit weight and phytochemical attributes, no significant relationship has been found. However, fruit weight was negatively correlated with flavonoids in inflorescence (-0.386). Inflorescence phenolics were positively correlated with flavonoids (0.613) which indicated that flavonoids contributed 60% to total phenolic content. The results revealed that phenolics and flavonoids had positive correlation. A genotype having more phenolics content in pericarp was also good source of flavonoids. Ramis et al.,37 reported positive correlation (0.805) between TPC and TFC. The flavonoids content in pericarp was inversely correlated with flavonoids content in seed (-0.436).

It means the genotype having more flavonoids in pericarp possessed low flavonoids in seed because flavonoids accumulation diverted to seed from pericarp. Higher pericarp content in fruit also reflected as higher flavonoids. Phenolics in new leaf was positively correlated with phenol in pericarp (0.462), flavonoids in old leaf (0.734) and negatively correlated with flavonoids in seed (-0.381). Phenolics in mature leaf was positively correlated with flavonoids in new leaf (0.818) indicating 81% contribution of flavonoids in total phenolics and negatively correlated with flavonoids in flower bud (-0.431). Phenolics in pulp were positively correlated with pulp antioxidant (0.708). This correlation coefficient suggested that phenolic compounds of litchi extracts contributed by 70% to their antioxidant activities. Thus, antioxidant activity of plant extracts is not fully depending on phenolics content. Activity may also come from other antioxidant secondary metabolites other than phenols, such as volatile oils, carotenoids, and vitamins, among others41. Similar positive correlation between phenolic contents and antioxidant potential was also reported by Zhang et al.,22. Pericarp phenolics were positively correlated with old leaf flavonoids (0.449), pericarp flavonoids (0.413), pulp antioxidant activity (0.571), pericarp antioxidant (0.581), pulp radicle scavenging (0.423) and pericarp radical scavenging activity (0.390). Radical scavenging activity was correlated with pulp antioxidant activity (0.838).

Pericarp radical scavenging activity has positive correlation with pericarp antioxidants (0.674). The total phenolics are important indicators of antioxidant properties42. Phenolics were positively correlated (0.900) with antioxidant activity37. The DPPH scavenging activities of the pericarp of litchi were also different among the cultivars20. A significant positive correlation established in antioxidant activity of apple by DPPH (r = 0.544) and FRAP assay (r = 0.447) and with total tannins43.

In our study, genotypes exhibited significant variations in total phenolics, flavonoids and antioxidant activity which reveal that environmental conditions as well as different parts of the plants and fruit affect the bioactive active content in litchi. Vrhovsek et al.,44 reported that total phenols ranged from 66.2 to 211.9 mg/100 g of fw among 41 apple. Correlation study showed that both CUPRAC and DPPH scavenging ability were positively correlated with total phenolics, and this indicated that phenolics were the mainly bioactive compounds in the pulp and pericarp of litchi. The research showed that litchi pulp and pericarps were rich in antioxidant compounds and their abilities were also different among the genotypes and phenolics represented major portions of antioxidant capacity. Therefore, litchi pericarp and pulp extracts can be proposed as potential sources of natural additives for food quality and pharmaceutical applications. In the present study, total phenolics and antioxidant activity were higher in pericarp as compared to pulp is in agreement with other studies on litchi43 and apple45. Thus, it can be concluded that the presence of phenolics in pericarp are potent contributor for enhancing total antioxidant and antiproliferative activities of the whole fruit than those in apple pulp46.

Assessment of variability through cluster analysis

Cluster analysis based on flavonoids, phenolics and antioxidant activity divided 30 genotypes of litchi into two main lineages that was further categorized into different clusters. Basic statistics i.e. range, mean, standard deviation (SD) and coefficient of variation (CV %) of the variables is given in Table 5. Hierarchical clustering method is relevant for analysing the phenotypic and genetic diversity of germplasm. Clustering method grouped genotypes of litchi based on the characters they possessed. The results of cluster analysis on 30 litchi genotypes suggested that there is enough variation among the studied genotypes for different traits (Figs. 5, 6 and 7). Accessions with greater similarity for morphological traits were placed in the same cluster. Cluster analysis has great practical importance for plant breeders as it distributes the genotypes into different clusters. Representative genotypes from each cluster can be selected for use in crop improvement programmes. Maximum variation in the populations can be achieved by selecting and using genotypes from diverse clusters. The information of relationships achieved from these studies may be useful in exploitation of the available germplasm resources.

Principal components analysis

Analyses of principal component along with biplot reduce the dimensionality of interrelated variables and reveal the trait-genotypes relationships. The first (PC1: 20%), second (PC2: 15%), third (PC3: 13%) and fourth principal components (PC4: 9%) with eigen values above unity explained the total variability. Together, they explained 57.74% of the total variation (Table 6). The traits that contributed most weight to the first principal component axis were phenolics in pericarp (0.412), radical scavenging activity in pulp (0.383), antioxidant in pulp (0.336), radical scavenging activity in pericarp (0.330) and flavonoids in old leaf (0.312). These characters are relevant criteria for selection of genotypes with big fruit size. The second principal component axis was associated mainly with phenolics in pulp (0.480), flavonoids in pulp (0.359), flavonoids in seed (0.351) and antioxidant in pulp (0.344). This component reflects that there is a strong genetic correlation between traits responsible for higher yield. The traits that contributed most weight to the third principal axis was flavonoids in flower bud (0.413) and inflorescence (0.367) whereas percentage of pericarp (0.431) and flavonoids in pericarp (0.306) contributed the most weight to the fourth principal component axis. Figure 8 shows the relationship among the various traits. The angle size between any two or more character is directly proportional to correlation between these characters. Higher correlation is indicated by the closer traits. Consequently, a high correlation was noticed between antioxidant activity and radical scavenging activity in pulp and maximum genotypes were found clustering in PC1 under the influence of these traits. A high correlation was also observed between total antioxidant in pericarp and radical scavenging activity in pericarp. The traits contributing in the maximum divergence should be more focused for selection in breeding. Genotype exhibited variation for bioactive compounds such as total phenolics, flavonoids, and antioxidant activity in pericarp. Total phenolics and flavonoids were related with radical-scavenging activities. This indicated that litchi pericarps rich in phenolics have the potential to be used as functional ingredients for food and pharmaceutical applications.

Conclusions

Significantly higher phenolics and antioxidant activity were recorded in the litchi fruit pericarp as compared to whole fruit and edible pulp/aril. Based on the results, it is concluded that genotype IC-0615593 exhibited maximum pulp DPPH scavenging and total antioxidant activity, and holds potential for further evaluation in breeding and crop improvement. Genotypes exhibited significant differences in bioactive profiles and antioxidant activities. Litchi being an introduced fruit in India, the genetic base of the crop is quite narrow with rather only a few commercially cultivated varieties. The results of this study unravel significantly to the diversity that exists in the different litchi accessions, and add to the wealth of knowledge that can assist litchi breeders in crop improvement programmes. The picture of phenolic profiles and antioxidant activity of pulp in different genotypes will help to explore their potential applications in human health, including in the food and pharmaceutical industries.
