First-in-human application of robotic vascularized intercostal nerve grafts for thoracic sympathetic trunk reconstruction: a technical innovation
Authors: Lisa Wen-Yu Chen, Yin-Kai Chao, Chin-Pang Lee, Chuan Cheng, Kuo-Hsuan Chang, Amina Ilias, Tommy Nai-Jen Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPost-sympathectomy complications affect thousands globally, yet reconstruction remains limited by donor site morbidity and inability to address extensive nerve defects. Extended sympathectomy procedures of the past created defects exceeding 10 cm—beyond the reach of conventional free nerve grafts. This study presents the first robotic technique for harvesting vascularized intercostal nerve grafts within the same operative field, potentially revolutionizing sympathetic trunk reconstruction.MethodsThis retrospective technical innovation study enrolled patients undergoing robot-assisted sympathetic trunk reconstruction from December 2023 to June 2025. Eight patients received either vascularized intercostal nerve grafts (n = 4) or propensity score-matched free intercostal nerve grafts (n = 4), with one additional patient receiving dual vascularized grafts for bilateral T1-T10 defects. Primary outcomes included technical feasibility, Visual Analog Scale symptom scores, and safety parameters.ResultsAll vascularized procedures achieved 100% technical success with zero vascular complications. The vascularized group demonstrated remarkable improvements within 6 months: temperature regulation (9.0 vs. 3.75, P = 0.01), chest compensatory sweating (9.25 vs. 4.25, P = 0.04), back compensatory sweating (9.5 vs. 6.25, P < 0.01), and psychological symptom with loss of interest (4.25 vs. 1, P = 0.01) - outcomes typically requiring more than 6 months with conventional grafts. The dual-graft patient achieved complete reconstruction of previously “unreconstructable” bilateral defects spanning 9 intercostal levels, with 3-points improvement across all symptom domains.ConclusionsRobotic vascularized intercostal nerve grafting represents a paradigm shift in sympathetic reconstruction, eliminating donor site morbidity while accelerating recovery and enabling treatment of previously impossible extensive defects. This innovation may establish new standards for complex nerve reconstruction globally.

Background

Thoracic sympathetic trunk reconstruction (STR) has emerged as the definitive treatment for debilitating post-sympathectomy complications [1], with robotic approaches using free sural or intercostal nerve (ICN) grafts demonstrating successful reversal of autonomic symptoms including compensatory sweating (CS) and thermoregulation dysfunction through precise microsurgical techniques [2–6]. However, before modern robotic STR matured, extended sympathectomy emerged in the late 20th and early 21 st centuries as an attempted solution for patients with intractable CS. This procedure involved additional sympathectomy at lower thoracic levels (T5-T8), but rather than treating CS, it paradoxically caused more severe lower extremity symptoms and created extensive nerve defects [1, 7, 8].

These extended thoracic sympathetic trunk defects present unique reconstructive challenges. Conventional free nerve grafts are inadequate for such extensive defects, necessitating vascularized nerve reconstruction. However, current literature demonstrates outcomes exclusively with free nerve graft reconstruction, leaving significant limitations for patients with extensive defects where conventional grafts are insufficient. Vascularized nerve grafts represent a solution to overcome ischemic limitations, with experimental studies and clinical applications in peripheral nerve reconstruction demonstrating enhanced regeneration through preserved blood supply [9–12]. However, clinical adoption has been constrained by technical complexity, including challenging donor site access, intricate vascular anastomoses, and substantial surgical morbidity from large incisions required for traditional vascularized nerve harvest.

The anatomical architecture of thoracic STR presents a unique opportunity that has not been previously exploited: the ICNs’ intimate anatomical relationship with their segmental vascular supply and immediate proximity to sympathetic trunk defects. This configuration enables same-field vascularized graft harvest, and the integration of robotic microsurgical platforms provides the technical precision necessary to execute this complex harvest safely, offering superior visualization, enhanced dexterity, and real-time perfusion assessment capabilities that overcome historical limitations of vascularized nerve grafting.

Despite the logical advantages of this approach, no previous study has demonstrated the clinical application of robotic vascularized intercostal nerve (vICN) harvesting specifically for thoracic STR. This represents a significant gap in surgical innovation, as the combination of anatomical opportunity, technological capability, and clinical need has not been systematically explored.

Therefore, this study presents the first-in-human application of robotic vICN grafts for thoracic STR, establishing technical feasibility, describing surgical methodology for varying clinical scenarios, and reporting early clinical outcomes. This technical innovation potentially addresses fundamental limitations in current reconstructive approaches while establishing a foundation for broader applications in complex nerve reconstruction involving extensive defects.

Methods

Study patient

This retrospective study evaluated patients who underwent robot-assisted thoracic STR at Linkou Chang Gung Memorial Hospital, Taiwan, between December 2023 and June 2025, utilizing either free or vascularized ICN grafts. Patients who failed to complete outcome questionnaires were excluded. The study received ethical approval from the local institutional review board (approval No. 202500858B0), and all patients provided informed written consent for data publication.

Preoperative and postoperative survey

A comprehensive preoperative evaluation protocol was systematically implemented for all potential STR candidates. Detailed medical histories were obtained with particular emphasis on symptomatology following thoracic sympathectomy interventions. Autonomic symptom severity was quantified using patient-reported Visual Analog Scale (VAS) assessments, with scores ranging from 0 to 10 (where 0 represents complete absence of symptoms and 10 indicates maximum symptom severity) across all relevant clinical parameters [5]. Patients reporting exercise intolerance underwent exercise-limited cardiac stress testing to objectively document cardiovascular autonomic dysfunction.

The multidisciplinary preoperative assessment included comprehensive psychological evaluation by an experienced psychiatrist, with patients who failed to demonstrate psychological suitability or who harbored unrealistic outcome expectations being excluded from surgical consideration. Additionally, thorough consultations with anesthesiology, cardiology, and neurology specialists were conducted to facilitate comprehensive risk-benefit analysis regarding reconstructive intervention. Following detailed discussion of potential adverse outcomes, including temporary exacerbation of sympathetic symptoms and potential risk of injury to adjacent anatomical structures, written informed consent was obtained from all surgical candidates.

Patients were followed on an outpatient basis or through telehealth interviews every 6 months. Patient-reported outcomes were assessed using identical instruments to those employed preoperatively to quantify clinical symptoms and potential sequelae, including donor site morbidity.

Surgical technique

Following induction of general anesthesia and intubation with a double-lumen endotracheal tube, the patient was positioned semi-prone. After establishing pneumothorax and creating appropriate thoracic access ports, trocar placement proceeded as follows: (1) a 30-degree angled thoracoscope was inserted through the seventh intercostal space (ICS) at the posterior axillary line; (2) the right robotic arm was positioned in the fifth ICS at the posterior axillary line, medial to the scapula; (3) the left robotic arm was placed in the ninth ICS at the posterior axillary line; and (4) the assistant port was established in either the sixth or seventh ICS at the mid-axillary line. The da Vinci® Xi robotic system (Intuitive Surgical Inc., Sunnyvale, CA) was docked at 45 degrees counterclockwise to the patient’s craniocaudal axis.

Upon thoracic entry, adhesions overlying the previous sympathectomy site were carefully lysed. Using monopolar scissors and curved bipolar forceps, we identified the scarred proximal and distal stumps of the sympathetic trunk and affected ICNs. After localizing healthy nerve stumps, we proceeded with ICN dissection along with its vascular pedicle - a step that can be performed by either the thoracic or plastic surgeon. Given that defects typically span T2-T4, the T5 ICN was selected as the vascularized donor in most cases (Fig. 1), though for very proximal, short defects, the T3 ICN was chosen for its proximity to the defect and to eliminate possible tethering from the pedicle. Usually, one vascularized nerve graft suffices for defects of less than 10 cm; however, for defects exceeding 4 to 5 intercostal spaces, dual vascularized nerve grafts can be employed. Two adjacent vICNs are dissected in parallel, identified, transected distally, and rotated—one counterclockwise, one clockwise—then coapted to their respective healthy stumps and connected centrally (Fig. 2). Harvesting the T4 ICN is avoided to prevent nipple numbness. After rotating the vascularized graft and tailoring it to the defect, the graft is secured with several sutures to the parietal pleura. The proximal and distal ends of the ICNs are then transected at appropriate sites for tension-free coaptation, while the vascular pedicle remains intact proximally. In this modified technique, we avoid passing the vascularized graft beneath the affected ICN to prevent vascular compression. While distal and proximal nerve segments are transected for coaptation to sympathetic stumps, excessive liberation of the nerve from its pedicle should be avoided to preserve maximal perfusion. Following nerve grafting, indocyanine green (ICG) is used to confirm graft perfusion (Figs. 1 and 2), and fibrin sealant (TISSEEL) is applied to secure the reconstruction sites and immobilize the nerve graft to the chest wall (Fig. 1).

Upon completion, careful hemostasis is achieved, and a pigtail catheter is inserted through the assistant port. Negative pressure suction is avoided to prevent coaptation site rupture. The identical approach is performed contralaterally when indicated. Postoperatively, patients are monitored overnight in the intensive care unit before transfer to the ward the following morning. Chest drainage can usually be removed on postoperative day 1, and patients are typically discharged on postoperative day 3 or 4.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). During the study period, 55 free ICN and 8 vICN procedures were performed. Given the novel nature of this first-in-human technique, the statistical approach prioritized descriptive analysis and clinical significance assessment over inferential testing due to inherent sample size constraints.

Primary analysis focused on within-group changes in the vICN cohort (n = 4) to establish preliminary technique efficacy. Given the small sample size inherent to first-in-human studies, individual patient Visual Analog Scale trajectories were presented graphically to demonstrate clinical response patterns rather than performing formal statistical testing. Continuous variables are presented as means with standard deviations for normally distributed data. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages.

To provide comparative context, propensity score matching was utilized to minimize selection bias and balance baseline characteristics between the vICN and free ICN graft groups, with matching performed on age, sex, and medical history. A total of 4 patients in each group were matched and included in the analysis. Within-group changes from baseline to 6-month postoperative follow-up were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Between-group comparisons of postoperative outcomes at 6 months were performed using Mann-Whitney U tests, with results reported as mean ranks. Baseline equivalence between matched groups was confirmed using Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 for all analyses. All analyses were conducted on an intention-to-treat basis, and no adjustments were made for multiple comparisons given the exploratory nature of this pilot study.

When comparing vICN to free ICN graft outcomes at 6 months, propensity score matching successfully balanced baseline characteristics between groups, and no significant differences in pre-operative symptom severity (all p > 0.05, Table 2); however, the vICN group consistently demonstrated lower mean ranks (indicating better outcomes) across CS measures: chest (mean rank 3.38 vs. 5.63), back (mean rank 3.75 vs. 5.65), and gustatory sweating (mean rank 3.75 vs. 5.25), foggy brain (mean rank 4.25 vs. 4.75), and loss of interest (mean rank 3.75 vs. 5.25), though these differences did not reach statistical significance.

In our series, there was only 1 case requiring dual vascularized nerve grafts (excluded from the comparative analysis) - a 26-year-old man who underwent thoracic sympathectomy in 2019 for palmar hyperhidrosis and subsequently received repeated extensive bilateral thoracic sympathectomy, resulting in defects spanning T1 to T10 bilaterally. We harvested vascularized fifth and sixth ICNs for the left side (both 12 cm in length) and sixth and seventh ICNs for the right side (14 cm and 11 cm, respectively). The operative time was 14 h, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 4 without complications. Following STR, he demonstrated improvement of at least 3 points across all symptom domains (Fig. 3). Scores for cognitive symptoms and loss of interest were completely eliminated at 6 months postoperatively. Discussion

This study represents the first clinical application of robotic vascularized intercostal nerve harvesting for thoracic STR, addressing critical unmet needs in reconstructive surgery. Our technique eliminates traditional limitations while demonstrating accelerated functional recovery and clinically meaningful advances.

For peripheral nerve reconstruction, vascularized nerve grafts are especially indicated for extensive defects (typically >10–20 cm) or when the recipient bed is scarred, irradiated, or poorly vascularized, as these conditions further compromise free graft survival and regeneration. The superior performance stems from preserved vascular architecture that prevents the 2- to 3-week ischemic period characteristic of free grafts. During this critical window, free grafts undergo central necrosis and fibrosis formation while requiring weeks for revascularization from surrounding tissues—a process that limits effectiveness in long gaps [13–15]. In contrast, vascularized grafts maintain immediate perfusion, enabling continuous Schwann cell proliferation, accelerated myelin formation, and optimal neurotrophic factor delivery through preserved blood-nerve barrier function while preventing inflammatory infiltration that impedes regeneration [16–18]. Notably, the vICN group demonstrated continuous improvement without obvious early sympathectomy effects, whereas progression of some symptoms was observed in the free ICN group at 6 months postoperatively. This early sympathectomy effect - causing temporary worsening of sympathetic symptoms during the initial weeks following reconstruction - requires preoperative patient counseling to prevent dissatisfaction [1]. The absence of this effect with vascularized grafts supports the theory that direct vascularization prevents the initial ischemic period that free grafts experience, reducing central necrosis and fibrosis risk.

Previous studies have established foundations for vascularized intercostal nerve applications, with serratus anterior intercostal nerve grafts successfully used in large ulnar nerve reconstructions and animal models demonstrating superior reinnervation compared to conventional grafts [19–21]. However, systematic reviews suggest that while vascularized grafts may offer advantages in animal models, clinical significance in humans remains uncertain [21, 22]. Our study addresses the unique scenario of thoracic STR, where compromised postsurgical environments and scar tissue formation may particularly benefit from enhanced vascularity [23]. Although evidence exists for motor nerve reconstruction, robust data on sensory and autonomic nerve reconstruction using vascularized grafts remains limited, representing a significant knowledge gap this study begins to address.

Clinical significance

The practical implications extend beyond statistical measures. Patients achieved improvement in their most bothersome symptom—chest compensatory sweating—from severe (VAS 9.25) to mild symptoms (VAS 4.25) within 6 months, a timeline that typically requires more than 6 months with conventional grafts [1, 4, 5]. This accelerated recovery translates to earlier return to work, reduced healthcare utilization, and improved quality of life. Most notably, the absence of early sympathectomy effects eliminates the temporary symptom worsening that characterizes conventional reconstruction [1].

More importantly, this technique solves previously unsolvable clinical challenges: patients with extensive sympathectomy defects exceeding 10 cm who were considered unreconstructable due to insufficient donor nerve availability. Free nerve grafts are limited to defects under 10 cm, and defects spanning more than 5 intercostal levels typically exceed this threshold, rendering conventional reconstruction impossible. Our dual vascularized graft case successfully reconstructed defects spanning T1-T10 bilaterally - impossible with conventional free grafts. The ability to harvest multiple adjacent vascularized segments within the same operative field eliminates traditional donor nerve length limitations.

Despite technical complexity, operative times remained comparable (8 h mean for both groups) with no additional complications observed clinically. This demonstrates that technical innovation need not compromise surgical efficiency or safety, making the technique clinically practical for widespread adoption.

Technical innovation and clinical advantages

The robotic platform uniquely enables this technique through three key innovations: (1) enhanced visualization allowing identification of 0.5-mm intercostal vessels, (2) articulated instruments enabling precise dissection within confined thoracic spaces, and (3) integrated indocyanine green angiography providing real-time perfusion assessment [1, 3, 24]. These capabilities overcome historical limitations that previously made vascularized harvesting technically prohibitive, enabling safer harvesting of longer nerve segments while maintaining vascular integrity, as demonstrated in our complex case requiring dual vascularized grafts spanning T1 to T10.

No vascular complications occurred in our series, with 100% graft patency confirmed by indocyanine green angiography, while the theoretical risk of intercostal vessel injury during harvest was mitigated by robotic precision. Lower chest numbness in the dual-graft patient represents expected sensory sacrifice for extensive reconstruction, with minimal functional impact. While initial robotic costs are higher, potential economic benefits compared with traditional thoracotomy include shorter hospital stays, decreased surgical pain, and faster return to productivity. The elimination of donor site morbidity reduces overall healthcare utilization, while accelerated recovery may offset initial investment through improved patient outcomes and reduced long-term care requirements [1].

However, this technique has important contraindications and limitations. Patients with severe chest trauma, prior major thoracic surgery, or extensive pleural adhesions may have compromised intercostal vessels, making vascularized harvest impossible. When the primary intercostal nerve target proves un-harvestable, shifting to lower intercostal levels requires robot redocking, increases pedicle length requirements, and may result in inadequate graft length due to tethering constraints. Therefore, we recommend detailed review of surgical history and preoperative counseling regarding the possibility of unsuccessful reconstruction in complex cases. The combination of enhanced surgical precision, improved patient outcomes, and reduced morbidity establishes this robotic technique as a promising advancement in reconstructive nerve surgery that may redefine the standard of care for STR.

Study limitations

This pilot study’s primary limitation is sample size (n = 4 per group), inherent to first-in-human innovations but limiting generalizability. Single-surgeon experience raises reproducibility questions, necessitating multicenter validation. The absence of objective measures (thermography, quantitative sudomotor testing) represents a significant gap requiring future investigation.

Future directions

These preliminary findings support the need for larger, prospective studies with standardized follow-up intervals and objective outcome measures. We recommend standardizing patient-reported outcome measures using validated VAS instruments for symptom assessment. While subjective feedback is extremely important for this patient group, future investigations should include quantitative assessments such as thermography and sudomotor testing, cost-effectiveness analyses, and multicenter validation to establish broader applicability.

Conclusion

This technical innovation demonstrates the feasibility of robotic vascularized intercostal nerve grafting for sympathetic trunk reconstruction with promising early outcomes that address fundamental limitations in current practice. While larger studies are needed, the consistent clinical improvements and theoretical advantages suggest this technique may establish a new standard of care, particularly for complex sympathetic trunk reconstruction involving extensive defects.
