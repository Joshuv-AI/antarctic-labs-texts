The combined inhibitory effect of butaselen and decitabine against lung cancer cells
Authors: Yifan Chen, Chutong Lin, Yingyuan Lu, Huihui Zeng, Lixiang Xue, Guangliang Qiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The DNA methyltransferase inhibitor decitabine (DAC) is clinically approved for hematologic malignancies, but its efficacy in solid tumors, including lung cancer, remains limited. This study aimed to investigate the combined effects of the thioredoxin reductase (TrxR) inhibitor butaselen (BS/BS1801) and DAC on lung cancer cells in vitro. Human lung cancer cell lines (A549 and H1299) were used as an in vitro model. Cytotoxicity was assessed using the CCK‑8 assay. Combination index (CI) and dose‑reduction index (DRI) analyses were performed to evaluate the combined effects of BS and DAC. Cell proliferation was detected by colony formation assay, apoptosis by Annexin V/PI staining with flow cytometry, and migration by wound healing assay. Protein expression levels of DNMT1, Bcl‑2, Bax, HOXA9, p21, and E‑cadherin were determined by western blotting. The combination of BS and DAC resulted in enhanced inhibition of proliferation and migration, and increased induction of apoptosis compared with either agent alone in A549 and H1299 cells, under specific ratio and time conditions. The combined treatment was associated with downregulation of DNMT1, a reduced Bcl‑2/Bax ratio, and upregulation of HOXA9, p21, and E‑cadherin. These preclinical findings suggest that the combination of BS and DAC represents a mechanistically rationalized and promising therapeutic strategy for lung cancer that warrants further evaluation in in vivo models and early‑phase clinical trials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45054-7.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with a five-year survival rate below 20%1,2. Despite advances in targeted therapy and immunotherapy, effective treatments for the majority of patients remain an unmet need. Epigenetic dysregulation, particularly aberrant DNA methylation, is a hallmark of lung cancer and contributes to the silencing of tumor suppressor genes3,4. This has prompted interest in epigenetic therapies, among which decitabine (DAC), a DNA methyltransferase inhibitor, has been clinically approved for myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) and acute myeloid leukemia (AML)5–8. In solid tumors, including lung cancer, DAC as a single agent exhibits only modest antitumor activity9–11. Low doses may induce DNA damage without sufficient cytotoxicity, while dose escalation to achieve therapeutic efficacy is often accompanied by myelosuppression and neurotoxicity12,13. Accordingly, combination strategies that enhance DAC’s efficacy while minimizing its toxicity have garnered considerable interest14.

Butaselen (BS/BS1801, 2-bis[2-(1,2-benzisothiazol-2(2H)-ketone)] butane) is an organic selenium compound that specifically targets the Trx system. It was independently designed and synthesized by our group15. Our previous studies have demonstrated its antitumor activity in liver cancer, lung cancer, and glioma16–18 , as well as synergistic effects with cisplatin and anti‑PD‑1 immunotherapy in preclinical liver cancer models19,20. The absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion of BS have been characterized in prior studies involving humans and rats21,22. BS is currently under Phase I clinical evaluation for safety in healthy volunteers (ChiCTR2100045813) and for anti-tumor activity in high-grade gliomas (CTR20234234). In addition, BS has received investigational approval under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FDCA) for the treatment of primary liver carcinoma/cholangiocarcinoma (IND166278). However, its suitability for combination with epigenetic modulators such as DAC remains to be fully explored.

In this study, we investigated the combined effects of DAC and BS on human NSCLC cell lines A549 and H1299, and examined the molecular correlates associated with these effects. Our findings demonstrate that the combination of BS and DAC resulted in enhanced inhibition of proliferation and migration, and increased induction of apoptosis, under specific experimental conditions. These phenotypic changes were associated with downregulation of DNMT1, a reduced Bcl‑2/Bax ratio, and upregulation of HOXA9, p21, and E‑cadherin. Together, these preclinical observations suggest that the BS + DAC combination represents a mechanistically rationalized and promising strategy that warrants further evaluation in in vivo models and early‑phase clinical trials.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Butaselen (BS) was synthesized by Pharmaron and was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a 10 mM stock solution in vitro. Decitabine (DAC) (Selleck) was freshly prepared, protected from light, and dissolved in DMSO to prepare a 10 mM stock solution in vitro. The final DMSO concentration was identical across all treatment groups to exclude solvent confounding.

Cell culture, cell viability assay and Chou-Talalay median-effect analyses

A549 and H1299 cells (Solarbio) were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin solution (Macgene) at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Cell viability was measured using Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) (Dojindo Laboratories) as previously described23. Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2000 cell/well. After culturing for 24 or 48 h, 10 μl of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, followed by further incubation at 37 °C for 1 ~ 4 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was read using the microplate reader (Tecan). The CCK-8 assay was performed in three biological replicates. Cell viability was calculated as: Viability (%) = (OD_sample-OD_blank)/(OD_control-OD_blank) × 100, where OD_control represents the mean absorbance of untreated cells and OD_blank represents the background absorbance of cell‑free medium. Inhibition (%) was derived as: Inhibition = 100-Viability. The IC50 (half maximal inhibitory concentration) of BS and DAC were calculated with Origin 2018. Dose‑response curves were fitted using a four‑parameter logistic (4PL) model: Y = Bottom + (Top- Bottom)/(1 + 10^((LogIC50-X) × HillSlope))24. “MI” or (Imax) stands for maximal inhibition rate of the drug/inhibitor. The maximal inhibition rate of a drug/inhibitor on cell growth is a pharmacodynamic parameter that defines the upper limit of its capability to inhibit cellular proliferation. It is associated with the upper plateau value of the dose–response curve and, together with the IC50 (a potency parameter), comprehensively characterizes the in vitro activity profile of a drug/inhibitor. The median-effect analysis based on the median-effect principle was performed as described previously to measure the combination treatment effect of BS and DAC25. BS:DAC ratios of 1:1, 2:1, and 3:1 was chosen based on preliminary dose–response data. Results from at least three independent experiments were pooled and mean values were used to calculate the combination index (CI) and dose-reduction index (DRI). CI values reflect the ways of interaction between two drugs. CI > 1.1, 0.9 < CI < 1.1, and CI < 0.9 indicate antagonism, additive effect, and synergism respectively. DRI values reflect how many folds the dose of each drug can be reduced at a given effect level in a combination treatment, compared with the doses of each drug alone. DRI > 1 implies a favorable dose reduction and a greater DRI value indicates a greater dose reduction effect. The software CompuSyn was used to analyze the Chou-Talalay median-effect.

Colony formation

Colony formation assay was performed as previously described26. A549 and H1299 cells were seeded in six-well plates and treated with BS, DAC, or DAC + BS for 48 h, after which the medium was replaced with fresh drug-free complete medium, and they were cultured for an additional 7–14 days to allow colony formation. Then the surviving cells were stained with crystal violet, and colonies containing more than 50 cells were counted and percent area coverage were calculated by ImageJ. The colony formation assay was performed in three biological replicates.

Wound healing assay

The experiment was performed as described previously27. Cells (6 × 105/well) were planted in 6-well plate and grown to about 95% confluence after 24 h. Monolayer cells were scratched with 10 μl sterile pipette tips and washed with PBS twice. Cells were cultured for indicated period with BS, DAC, or BS + DAC. Wound width was observed and three images of each sample were taken with DMIL LED microscope (Leica) at 0 h and 48 h after treatment. Scratch area was obtained with ImageJ. Migration area was calculated by the following formula: Migration area = Scratch area at 0 h-Scratch area at 48 h. Percentage of migration area after compounds treatment compared to control groups was presented in the figures. The experiments were run in triplicate.

Apoptosis assay

The cells were collected, and the apoptosis assay was conducted using the Annexin V-FITC/ propidium iodide (PI) Dual Staining Kit (KeyGEN) as instructed by the manufacturer and described previously28. Briefly, after treatment, the cells were trypsinized and resuspended in 500 µl of 1 × Binding Buffer and incubated with Annexin V-FITC for 15 min, followed by incubation of PI for another 5 min in the dark. After staining, the cells were analyzed using the flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter). The apoptosis assay was measured at least three times with independent samples. The data were analyzed with CytExpert software.

Western blot

The experiment was performed as described previously29. The cells were collected and lysed in RIPA buffer (Thermo Scientific) containing Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (AMRESCO), and protein concentrations were measured by the BCA Protein Assay kit (Thermo Scientific). A total of 20 to 60 μg of protein was separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore). The primary antibodies used for immunoblotting analysis are as follows: DNMT1 (Proteintech, 24,206–1-AP), Bcl-2 (Proteintech, 12,789–1-AP), Bax (Proteintech, 50,599–2-Ig), HOXA9 (Proteintech, 18,501–1-AP), β-actin (Bioss, bs-0061R), p21 (MBL, K0081-3), and E-cadherin (PROTEINTECH, 20,874–1-AP). Horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (ZSGB-Bio, 1: 2000) were applied for incubation with membranes at room temperature for 1 to 1.5 h before visualized by ECL kit (Advansta) and analyzed by Tanon. The quantification of proteins was determined using ImageJ. The Bcl-2/Bax ratio was calculated for each replicate as (Normalized Bcl-2 intensity)/(Normalized Bax intensity) and subjected to statistical analysis alongside the individual protein levels.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed by GraphPad Prism 9.3.0 statistical software. The data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). The statistical analysis was done by using One-way ANOVA among multiple groups followed by Tukey’s post hoc test analysis. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and the statistical details are shown in the figures and figure legends.

Effect of treatment with BS and DAC alone and in combination on gene expression

We examined the expression levels of DNMT1, HOXA9, and p21, as both BS and DAC have been reported to inhibit DNMT117,31, and HOXA9 and p21 are known to be regulated by DNA methylation32,33. p21 is a well-known inhibitor of tumorigenesis34. Although HOXA9 has context-dependent roles across cancer types32, it has been reported as a suppressor of lung carcinoma metastasis35. As shown in Fig. 5, DNMT1 expression was reduced by BS or DAC alone, and the combined treatment was associated with a greater reduction in DNMT1 levels compared with either single agent. DAC alone did not appreciably alter HOXA9 or p21 expression, whereas BS alone slightly increased their levels. The combination of BS and DAC was associated with higher expression of HOXA9 and p21 than either treatment alone. We further assessed the expression of Bcl-2, Bax, and E-cadherin. The combination treatment was associated with downregulation of Bcl-2, upregulation of Bax, and consequently a decreased Bcl-2/Bax ratio, as well as increased E-cadherin expression, relative to individual treatments. E-cadherin is a marker associated with epithelial phenotype and cell migration. Discussion

This study investigated the combined effects of DAC and BS on A549 and H1299 cells and explored the underlying molecular associations. The combination of DAC and BS was associated with enhanced cytotoxicity, inhibition of proliferation and migration, and induction of apoptosis. Furthermore, the combined treatment was associated with increased expression of HOXA9, p21, and E‑cadherin, alongside decreased DNMT1 levels and a reduced Bcl‑2/Bax ratio. Lung cancer remains a challenging disease to cure due to high recurrence rates and drug resistance, necessitating the development of novel therapeutic strategies. Epigenetic dysregulation, particularly DNA methylation, plays a critical role in lung cancer pathogenesis, prompting interest in combining epigenetic agents with conventional therapies. DAC is clinically approved for hematologic malignancies and its safety profile is well established. However, its efficacy in solid tumors as a single agent is limited. BS is an investigational organoselenium compound currently in Phase I clinical development. Its safety in healthy volunteers is under evaluation (ChiCTR2100045813), and its anti‑tumor activity has not yet been established in patients. The present preclinical study provides the first evidence that BS enhances the anti‑proliferative and pro‑apoptotic effects of DAC in NSCLC cell lines. These findings offer a rationale for further evaluation of this combination in preclinical in vivo models and, if supported by such studies, in early‑phase clinical trials to assess its safety, tolerability, and preliminary efficacy in patients with lung cancer.

Our quantitative assessment of drug interaction using the Chou-Talalay method revealed that the synergy between BS and DAC is condition-dependent (Tables 1, 2). The combination index (CI) varied with the drug ratio, treatment duration, and effect level (Fa). Notably, more consistent and robust synergy (CI < 1) was observed at the 48-h time point, and at specific fixed ratios (e.g., 3:1 or 2:1 ratios). This observed condition dependence may be mechanistically rationalized as follows. First, the time-dependent nature of synergy likely reflects the distinct pharmacological dynamics of each agent. DAC, as a nucleoside analog, requires incorporation into DNA during replication to exert its epigenetic and cytotoxic effects. The enhanced synergy at 48 h may correspond to the time needed for DAC incorporation, subsequent DNMT1 depletion, and the downstream transcriptional reactivation of target genes like HOXA9 and p21(as observed in Fig. 5). Second, the ratio dependence underscores the importance of achieving a critical threshold concentration for both agents to engage their complementary mechanisms effectively. The optimal ratios identified (3:1 and 2:1) may represent a balance where BS-induced oxidative/ER stress and TrxR inhibition optimally sensitize cells to the DNA damage and hypomethylating effects of DAC. From a methodological perspective, our data highlight a crucial principle in evaluating drug combinations that synergy is not an absolute property but a quantitative continuum influenced by the experimental landscape. While our study employed fixed-ratio designs to identify promising combinations, the conditional synergy underscores the necessity for future studies to employ two-dimensional dose–response matrices. Such an approach would precisely map the synergistic landscape, identify the most potent combination indices, and potentially reveal schedules (e.g., sequential dosing) that maximize therapeutic efficacy. Ultimately, this condition dependence should be viewed not as a limitation, but as a guide for the clinical translation of the combination, informing dosing regimens and schedules to be tested in subsequent in vivo models.

DNA methylation is an epigenetic modification that takes place at cytosine bases within CpG dinucleotides and is facilitated by enzymes called DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs). This process commonly occurs at CpG islands, which often contain gene promoters or exons in normal mammalian cells. These CpG islands are typically unmethylated, allowing gene transcription to proceed, which is essential for regulating cell differentiation and phenotype. Abnormal methylation of CpG sites is frequently observed in various cancers, providing tumor cells with a growth advantage by promoting chromatin condensation, gene silencing, and regulation of microRNAs. In lung cancer, DNA methylation significantly influences the disease’s development, progression, and prognosis. Research has identified that numerous genes, such as CDKN2A (p16INK4a), CDKN1A (P21Cip1/Waf1), and RASSF1A, are often hypermethylated in lung cancer36,37. Therefore, targeting DNA methylation may allow for optimized treatment of lung cancer. As an inhibitor of DNA methyltransferase and DNA methylation, decitabine has been approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for treating MDS and AML due to its successful therapeutic outcomes in hematologic malignancies38. High doses of DAC demonstrated significant cytotoxicity but can also result in side effects such as myelosuppression. Therefore, in clinical practice, a low-dose and short-term regimen of decitabine is typically used to treat MDS and AML. For example, the FDA has approved the 3-day regimen (15 mg/m2 every 8 h for 3 consecutive days/cycle/6 weeks) and the 5-day regimen (20 mg/m2 once daily for 5 consecutive days/ cycle/4 weeks) for the treatment of MDS and AML39. In solid tumors, while low doses of decitabine may offer some benefits, the limited clinical effectiveness has prompted a shift in focus towards combining it with alternative therapeutic approaches38. Moreover, it has been observed that short-term decitabine monotherapy often fails to achieve a clinical response. This might be due to the fact that the incorporation of decitabine into genomic DNA requires cell division and proliferation, highlighting the need for longer treatment durations of decitabine to address solid tumors40. Recent studies have shown that combining immunotherapy with decitabine can further boost the anti-tumor effects of immunotherapy in solid tumors41,42. In our study, we found that the simultaneous use of BS and DAC showed enhanced inhibitory effects on lung cancer cells. When combined with BS, the dose reduction effect of decitabine was evident. The combination of BS and DAC showed enhanced effects in decreasing the levels of DNMT1 and Bcl-2/Bax, and increasing the levels of p21, HOXA9, and E-cadherin. These changes ultimately lead to the inhibition of growth, survival, and migration of lung cancer cells. Although BS alone was effective in inhibiting DNMT1 and DNA methylation, the drug combination strategy can benefit from synergism and reduced toxicity. It is important to emphasize that BS is still in the early phase of clinical translation. Although its preclinical profile is encouraging and IND approval has been granted for liver cancer indications, no clinical efficacy or safety data are currently available for NSCLC patients, nor for combination regimens with DAC. The combination strategy proposed herein therefore represents a hypothesis‑driven, mechanistically rationalized approach that awaits rigorous testing in appropriate in vivo models and subsequent clinical investigation.

It is also important to note that this proof-of-concept study was designed to interrogate the intrinsic efficacy and mechanistic associations of the novel BS and DAC combination, rather than to conduct a direct head-to-head comparison with established regimens. While such a comparison is undoubtedly critical for clinical positioning, our primary goal was to establish whether this biologically rational combination yields a synergistic anti-tumor effect in vitro. The observed potentiation of apoptosis and inhibition of proliferation/migration provides a strong rationale for its further evaluation. Future studies will be essential to benchmark the efficacy of the BS + DAC combination against current standard-of-care agents (e.g., platinum-based chemotherapy, pemetrexed, or targeted therapies like osimertinib for EGFR-mutant NSCLC43–45) in both in vitro and in vivo models to definitively assess its potential comparative advantage.

Limitations

First, the findings of this study are derived exclusively from in vitro cell culture models. While the use of two distinct NSCLC cell lines (A549 and H1299) provides internal validation of the observed effects, the absence of in vivo data limits direct extrapolation to therapeutic efficacy in a whole organism. Future studies employing patient-derived xenograft (PDX) or subcutaneous tumor models in immunocompromised mice are essential to validate the anti-tumor activity and potential dose-limiting toxicities of the BS and DAC combination in a complex physiological environment, which is a critical prerequisite for clinical translation.

Second, although DAC is known to deplete DNMT1 and is clinically validated as a hypomethylating agent, we did not directly measure DNA methylation changes (e.g., global 5‑mC or bisulfite sequencing) in this study. Therefore, while the observed DNMT1 downregulation is consistent with its known mechanism, we cannot definitively conclude that demethylation was the direct cause of gene reactivation in this specific combination context. Future studies employing DNMT1 overexpression or knockdown are warranted to establish whether DNMT1 downregulation is necessary for the observed synergistic effects.

Third, the translational potential of our findings regarding therapeutic window is currently limited by the absence of data from normal lung epithelial cells. While this study was primarily designed as a proof-of-concept to investigate the anti-tumor efficacy and underlying molecular associations of the BS and DAC combination in NSCLC models, we acknowledge that assessing its cytotoxic effects on non-malignant cells is a critical next step for evaluating tumor selectivity and predicting a potential safety window. Future studies employing immortalized normal bronchial epithelial cells (e.g., BEAS-2B) or primary human lung epithelial cells will be essential to determine whether the observed synergistic anti-proliferative and pro-apoptotic effects are preferentially targeted to cancer cells, thereby strengthening the clinical relevance of this combination strategy.

Fourth, our study does not include a direct comparison with current first- or second-line therapies for NSCLC. This limits our ability to comment on the potential clinical superiority or niche of the BS + DAC combination relative to existing standards. Addressing this gap is a pivotal next step. Future preclinical work will involve parallel testing of the combination alongside chemotherapeutic (e.g., cisplatin, paclitaxel) and targeted agents in a panel of NSCLC cell lines, including those with relevant driver mutations, to define its relative potency and potential synergy within standard treatment contexts.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates that the combination of DAC and BS is associated with enhanced inhibition of proliferation and migration, and increased apoptosis in NSCLC cell lines. These effects coincide with reduced DNMT1 expression, a decreased Bcl‑2/Bax ratio, and upregulation of HOXA9, p21, and E‑cadherin. Collectively, these preclinical findings suggest that BS combined with DAC represents a promising therapeutic strategy for lung cancer that warrants further investigation in in vivo models and, ultimately, in early‑phase clinical trials.
