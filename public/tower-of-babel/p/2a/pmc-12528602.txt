Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs)-score: a novel prognostic marker for colorectal cancer patients
Authors: Xiao-Li Wu, Di Hu, Guo-Fa Xu, Mei-Yu Zhou, Bin Li, Xiao-Qiao Hu, Gen-Dou Zhou, Ya Fu, Rui Huang, Shou-Yan Xiang, Miao-Miao Tao, Hong-Bo Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the third most prevalent cancer globally, displaying significant tendencies towards recurrence and metastasis. Advanced CRC typically necessitates systemic treatments such as chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy, yet the development of primary and acquired drug resistance remains a significant challenge. Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) represent a complex network structure comprising DNA, histones, and antimicrobial peptides generated by activated neutrophils, which have been implicated in promoting cancer progression. However, the precise mechanisms underlying the interaction between NETs-related gene signatures and cancer cells remain poorly understood.MethodsThe gene expression profile of TCGA-CRC was obtained from the Xena database. Using the expression levels of 69 initial NETs biomarkers, a LASSO Cox regression model was used to develop a 10-gene NETs score. This score was then comprehensively analyzed, focusing on various aspects such as the tumor microenvironment (TME), drug resistance, tumor mutations, and immunotherapy response.ResultsA prognostic model for NETs was developed using multivariate Cox regression and LASSO regression analysis. A customized nomogram was developed to improve the forecast precision of the NETs-score. The NETs-score is involved in the TME and clinicopathological characteristics, where a lower score is associated with a more favorable prognosis. Further examination indicated that the NETs-score can predict how well a patient will respond to chemotherapy and identify those who could potentially gain advantages from anti-CTLA4 and PD-L1 treatments.ConclusionThe NETs-score introduces a new, effective, and precise prognostic model for CRC, enhancing the prediction of responses to chemotherapy and immunotherapy. This advancement also propels personalized cancer treatment strategies in the realms of chemotherapy and immunotherapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03708-4.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Neutrophil extracellular traps, Immune cells, Immunotherapy

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is recognized as the third most common malignant tumor and is the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1]. It has a lower incidence rate compared to lung cancer, but it is remarkable for its high likelihood of recurring and spreading to other parts of the body [2]. Unfortunately, many patients are diagnosed and treated during the advanced stages of the disease, resulting in a significant decrease in their 5-year survival rate. Therefore, it is crucial to take action in a timely manner for individuals at high risk of CRC to improve clinical outcomes. The American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) classification is a widely used tool in clinical practice to assess patient risk and treatment needs based on their clinical stage. The current staging system may have limitations that hinder its ability to effectively provide clinical management. This is because the decision to administer adjuvant chemotherapy (ACT) is primarily based on the clinicopathological stage, without considering the impact of molecular biology characteristics. This methodological approach has the potential to result in both excessive treatment and insufficient treatment. immunological checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are a new and promising form of cancer immunotherapy that directly focuses on immunological checkpoints. Although this therapeutic method has the potential to be effective, only a small group of CRC patients now have positive outcomes from it [3]. Therefore, it is important to identify reliable biomarkers to tailor treatment plans and maximize the effectiveness of pharmacological therapy for CRC.

Neutrophils, the most abundant immune cells in peripheral blood and infiltrating immune cells in CRC tissues [4], have been demonstrated to influence patient survival based on their neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) [5]. Neutrophils have the unique ability to release neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), which have recently been discovered to have a role in tumor growth by trapping circulating tumor cells and modifying tumor cell metabolism [6–8]. Neutrophil Elastase (NE) and matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9) within NETs have been shown to induce the transition of dormant tumor micrometastases in murine models to a proliferative state [9]. Additionally, the DNA constituents present in NETs have the capacity to interact with CCDC25 on tumor cells, facilitating tumor metastasis [10]. Furthermore, tumor cells have the ability to stimulate the production of NETs by releasing granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF), thereby enhancing their own metastatic potential [11]. Recent studies have demonstrated that MMP9 has the capability to catalyze the conversion of TGF-β sequestered within NETs into its active form, consequently facilitating epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and fostering the development of resistance to chemotherapy [12]. These studies suggest that various components and mechanisms within the NET play a crucial role in the development of tumor metastases, making them a promising therapeutic target.

The study aims to present and validate a novel NETs-score model based on 10 NETs-associated genes, which effectively predicts the risk of NETs in patients with CRC. The study seeks to illustrate the model’s accuracy in reflecting the immune microenvironment, prognosis, and response to immunotherapy and chemotherapy, as well as to identify NETs-associated genes as potential carcinogenic factors in CRC patients.

Materials and methods

Data collection and preprocessing

Data on gene expression profiles, survival rates, and clinical information for COADREAD was collected from the Xena database. The GSE39582 and GSE106584 datasets from the GEO database were used for validation purposes following the processing of the GEO dataset. Samples from TCGA and GEO data that lacked clinical information were eliminated. The GSE123813 dataset, containing single-cell transcriptome data from 11 basal cell carcinoma (BCC) patients, was retrieved from GEO. Analyzed were the single-cell data of three patients using the cell annotation results obtained by Kathryn Yost et al.

A recent study presented a compilation of documented gene sets and descriptions related to NETs [13]. The gene set associated with NETs consisted of 69 genes that possessed NET primordial biomarkers (tables, S1, S2). The appendix contains clinical information statistics for the TCGA CRC cohort table S3 and the GSE39582 dataset table S4.

NETs genes associated with CRC prognosis

To examine the predictive significance of NETs genes in CRC, patients were categorized into two groups based on their levels of NETs gene expression. Kaplan-Meier (K-M) curves were generated with log-rank tests to compare survival distributions between groups. The study examined the differences in survival rates among these groups using the “survminer” package in R. A significance level of p < 0.05 was used to determine if there was a potential link between the expression of NETs genes and the prognosis of CRC.

Prognostic model construction

The study employed Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) multiplicative Cox regression analysis to construct a prognostic model to reduce the risk of overfitting. The LASSO approach, implemented using the “glmnet” package, efficiently detects and reduces variables, resulting in certain regression coefficients being limited to zero and enabling the development of an interpretable model. Patients were grouped into high and low-score cohorts according to the median NETs-score, followed by survival analysis utilizing the ‘survminer’ package to compare survival outcomes between the groups. The prediction capacity of the NETs-score was evaluated using the ‘survminer’ and ‘timeROC’ packages using time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Univariate and multivariate Cox analyses were also conducted to investigate the independent prognostic significance of the NETs-score. The computation of the NETs-score was uniform across the validation cohorts GSE39582 and GSE106584.

Construction of nomogram

To combine the findings of the NETs-score with clinical data, we used the “rms” R packages to establish a nomogram in the TCGA CRC cohort using significant independent prognostic variables identified using multivariate Cox regression analysis. Calibration curves were then created to assess the agreement between the observed and anticipated survival results. The nomogram’s credibility was next evaluated by examining time-dependent ROC curves for each independent prognostic component and the NETs-score. Finally, decision curve analysis (DCA) was used to determine the net benefit at various threshold probabilities, providing insight into the nomogram’s clinical value.

Genomic alterations

Single-nucleotide variant (SNV) and somatic copy number variation (CNV) data for CRC were obtained from the TCGA database. GISTIC 2.0 was used to identify genomic modifications by comparing high and low peak NETs-score group CNVs and alterations. Somatic mutation data acquired with mutect2 were retrieved using the R package “TCGAbiolinks”. Fisher’s exact test was used to compare SNV mutations in the high and low NETs-score groups.

Estimation of immune cell infiltration in the TME

The relative abundance of each cell infiltrate in CRC patients’ TME was assessed using the ssGSEA, Mcpcounter, and Cibersort algorithms. The “IOBR” package was used to implement the Mcpcounter and Cibersort algorithms, whereas Charoentong’s research provided the ssGSEA immune cell type gene set, which included a variety of human immune cell subtypes such as activated CD8T cells, activated dendritic cells, macrophages, natural killer T cells, and regulatory T cells.

Prediction of response to immunotherapy

The ICI directed at CTLA-4 and PD-1/PD-L1 have shown promising effectiveness in treating cancer. The Tumor immune dysfunction and exclusion (TIDE) and SubMap algorithms were employed to predict the clinical outcomes of ICIs. TIDE, a computational methodology, utilizes gene expression profiling to predict responses to immune checkpoint blockade. The study evaluates two distinct immune evasion methods, namely the inhibition of tumor-infiltrating Cytotoxic T Lymphocytes (CTLS) and the resilience of CTLS against immunosuppressive agents. Analyzed utilizing SubMap, a method that indicates possible therapeutic efficacy, the concordance of gene expression patterns was examined. The SubMap algorithm was utilized to predict the likelihood of response to anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-1 immunotherapies.

Prediction of chemosensitivity

The oncoPredict algorithm was employed to evaluate the disparity in drug sensitivity between high and low-risk groups targeted by chemotherapy drugs. The analysis was based on genomics research on cancer drug sensitivity, using the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) dataset and the R package “oncoPredict” to build a regression model. The Wilcox statistical test was used to compare the variance in IC50 values between high and low NETs-score groups.

Single-cell transcriptomics

The Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data were preprocessed with the R tool Seurat. The “NormalizeData” function was used to normalize the scRNA-seq dataset, while the “FindVariableFeatures” function was used to find 2000 hypervariable genes. The data was next scaled and principal component analysis was used to minimize the dimension, selecting the top 20 principal components for further study. Because these data were collected from multiple samples, batch correction was conducted using the R package “Harmony” to prevent batch effects from interfering with downstream analysis. The UMAP technique was used for data visualization and analysis, followed by cell population annotation in accordance with the findings of Yost et al. [14]. To evaluate changes in individual patients before and after therapy, the NETs-score was computed especially for tumor cells, taking into consideration the prevalence of malignant cells in tumor samples.

Statistical analysis

The statistical studies were performed using R version 4.1.2. The Wilcoxon rank sum test was used to compare the disparities between two groups of samples across multiple parameters, including expression level, infiltration ratio, and other characteristic values. The Kruskal-Wallis test was employed to assess differences among various sets of samples. Survival curves were generated using the K-M method to analyze prognostic factors. The log-rank test was used to establish the significance of differences. In the graphical representation, the abbreviation “ns” indicates that the p > 0.05. The symbol “*” indicates that the p < 0.05. The symbol “**” indicates that the p < 0.01. The symbol “***” indicates that the p < 0.001. The symbol “****” indicates that the p < 0.0001.

Results

Construction and validation of NETs-score

The association between NETs gene expression levels (high vs. low based on cutoff 50%) and patient survival outcomes was assessed using K-M analysis. The findings indicated that 21 out of 69 genes displayed a significant association with overall survival (OS) (Table S5). The K-M curve displays the top 6 genes that have the most statistically significant p-values. Figure 1 shows these genes, while the appendix has the data for the remaining genes. To address the impracticality of analyzing a large number of genes in a clinical situation, the LASSO regression analysis was used to further refine the gene selection. The paths of each factor were established (Fig. 2A). The confidence interval, as shown in Fig. 2B, indicates that the model is most efficient when there are 10 genes. Through our comprehensive analysis, we identified 10 candidate genes. Afterwards, the coefficients of these 10 genes, along with their corresponding expression levels, were used to calculate the NETs-score. The effect of this NETs-score, which was calculated based on the expression of 10 genes, on the OS was evaluated. Initially, the successful samples within the TCGA colorectal cohort were divided into high-score and low-score groups based on the median NETs-score (Fig. 3A, B). Later on, it was observed that the occurrence of deceased samples was higher in the group with higher scores. A survival study was conducted using K-M analysis to compare the high and low-score groups. The results showed a significantly lower OS rate among patients in the high-score group compared to those in the low-score group (Fig. 3D). The NETs-score had a robust predictive capacity for OS in the training set, as evidenced by the area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.686, 0.706, and 0.654 at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively (Fig. 3E). To determine the trustworthiness of the NETs-score obtained from the TCGA training set, its ability to predict outcomes was assessed in all validation sets using the identical technique. The results of the study showed consistent findings between the training and validation sets. The group with low scores had a considerable survival advantage, as shown in Figs. 4 and 5.

Independent prognostic value of NETs-score

The study conducted both univariate and multivariate Cox analyses to evaluate the independent prognostic significance of the NETs-score in patients with CRC. The univariate Cox analysis showed a strong correlation between the TCGA cohort and OS (TCGA cohort: HR = 3.05, 95% CI 1.96–4.74, p < 0.001; GSE39582 cohort: HR = 2.17, 95%CI = 1.60–2.94, p < 0.001). The multivariate Cox analysis also showed that the NETs-score continued to be a significant predictor of OS even after accounting for other potential confounding factors. In the TCGA cohort, the HR was 2.77 with a 95% CI of 1.68–4.58 and a p-value of less than 0.001. In the GSE39582 cohort, the HR was 1.91 with a 95% CI of 1.40–2.61 and a p-value of less than 0.001. The comprehensive findings of these analyses are outlined in the following section (Fig. 6).

The nomogram for predicting OS in CRC patients was developed using stage, age, and NETs-score, as shown in Fig. 7A. The predicted accuracy of the nomogram for OS in colon cancer patients was confirmed by using standard curves at 1, 3, and 5 years, as shown in Fig. 7B. NETs-score and genomic alterations

To explore the possible relationship between somatic mutations and the NETs-score, we performed an analysis of single nucleotide variations (SNVs). The findings indicated that the group with lower scores showed a wider range of tumor mutations, as illustrated in Fig. 8A–B. Patients with lower NETs scores were more likely to have SYNE1 mutations than those with higher NETs scores, with proportions of 25% and 35%, respectively. Furthermore, the analysis of copy number alterations indicated that individuals with either high or low NETs scores exhibited significant variations in copy numbers at different locations on the chromosomes (Fig. 9A–B), particularly on chromosome 8. In particular, the low NETs-score group showed a greater amplification of copy numbers compared to the high NETs-score group. These findings reveal a novel association between NETs-related gene signatures and specific tumor mutational profiles.

Immune status and TME analysis

To examine the relationship between the NETs-score and immune status in CRC patients in the TCGA cohort, several algorithms, including MCPcounter, ssGSEA, and Cibersort were used. These algorithms were used to measure the levels of immune-infiltrating cell populations based on factors such as the NETs-score, tumor stage, tumor grade, sex, and age (Fig. 10A). The results of the Cibersort study indicated that individuals with a low NETs-score showed a greater abundance of immune-infiltrating cells, such as Macrophages M1, Mast cell activated, and T cell follicular helper. In addition, the MCP counter-analysis revealed an increase in the population of Myeloid dendritic cells and Neutrophils in these individuals. The results of the ssGSEA analysis indicated an increased number of Activated CD4T cells, Activated CD8T cells, Activated dendritic cells, and Neutrophils in patients with low NETs-scores. Furthermore, the correlation analysis demonstrated a negative relationship between the NETs-score and the quantity of neutrophils (Fig. 10B–D). In addition, the GSEA results revealed that patients with a low NETs-score had a higher degree of participation in crucial immune-related pathways (Fig. 10E), such as antigen processing and presentation, chemokines, T cell receptors, and TNF signaling pathways. Individuals with a high NETs-score showed increased involvement in pathways relevant to tumor progression, such as MAPK, PI3K-Akt, PPAR, and Wnt signaling pathways (Fig. 10F). These findings suggest a significant correlation between the NETs-score and the tumor immune microenvironment.

Potential immunotherapy and chemotherapy response associated with NETs-score

Recent advancements in immunotherapy, particularly PD-1 inhibitors, have greatly enhanced the effectiveness of conventional chemotherapy in managing recurrent and metastatic CRC by employing chemo-immunotherapy. This strategy entails the synergy between chemotherapy and immune cell mechanisms to augment current cancer treatment techniques. To assess the potential efficacy of NETs-score based therapy, an analysis was performed to establish the correlation between NETs-score and immune checkpoint levels in CRC patients. The results of the study showed a connection between lower NETs-scores and increased levels of immunological checkpoints, specifically CD274 and CTLA4 (Fig. 11A), as demonstrated by the heatmap analysis. Further examination indicated that patients with low NETs-scores had a greater probability of responding positively to ICI, whereas those with high NETs-scores were linked to a reduced incidence of not responding to immunotherapy (Fig. 11B–C). Moreover, the NETs-score displayed a significant association with several chemotherapy agents, including Irinotecan, Trametinib, 5-Fluorouracil, Oxaliplatin, Topotecan, KRAS (G12C) Inhibitor, and others (Fig. 12). This suggests that patients with lower NETs-scores were more likely to respond positively to chemotherapy. This information is illustrated in Fig. 12 of the supplementary material.

Discussion

CRC is a multifactorial disease influenced by both genetic predisposition and environmental factors, with diverse clinical manifestations. Neutrophils are the most common form of white blood cell in the bloodstream, and they can stimulate tumor growth in the TME. The TME can recruit neutrophils to the tumor and impact their activity, resulting in the development of NETs. Excessive NET production has been associated with autoimmune illnesses, cardiovascular problems, inflammation, and cancer [15]. The NETs promote hypoxic response, surgical stress, and activation of dormant metastatic cells via extracellular matrix remodeling, all of which contribute to tumor growth and metastasis [9, 16]. Tumor cells can affect neutrophil activity and ability to create NETs by producing cytokines such as IL-6, IL-1β, IFN-γ, and TNF-α. Tumor cells can exploit NETs in the microenvironment to avoid immune cells during metastasis [10, 17]. Recent studies have demonstrated that NETs can promote tumor recurrence, metastasis, and therapy resistance. Moreover, targeting NETs exhibits synergistic antitumor effects when combined with immunotherapy [18]. Using multigene scores enhances the accuracy of predicting tumor development caused by dysregulated gene expression. This study developed a NETs-score to forecast outcomes and assess the effectiveness of immunotherapy in patients with CRC. Similar to a pharmacologist, the score was utilized to assess the tumor immune microenvironment, immune checkpoints, and drug sensitivity, indicating its potential in predicting prognosis and guiding immunotherapy for CRC patients.

Through an extensive analysis of 69 initial biomarkers, we have successfully identified 10 potential candidate genes that are closely associated with NETs in CRC. These genes, namely AKT2, ATG7, CLEC6A, CRISPLD2, ENTPD4, IL-17A, ITGAM, MAPK1, SELPLG and SIGLEC14 were discovered using the TCGA dataset. Through the calculation of the risk score, samples were categorized into high-risk and low-risk groups using the median as a basis. The heatmap revealed distinct variations in gene expression among the groups, while the K-M curve demonstrated a significant reduction in OS for the high-risk group in comparison to the low-risk group. By combining the risk score with clinical factors, a nomogram was developed to gain deeper insights into the predictive value of genes associated with NETs. It was found that the risk score, age, and stage had a significant impact on the prognosis of OS. By incorporating these features, the nomogram improved predictive accuracy by synergizing the risk score with other clinical indicators. The AUC value of OS demonstrated that the nomogram had excellent discrimination in both the training and validation groups. The calibration plot and DCA curve further confirmed the clinical utility of the nomogram.

Accumulating evidence has established that bidirectional communication between tumor cells and immune components within the TME critically facilitates metastatic progression [19]. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms whereby NETs modulate cellular crosstalk and immune regulation remain incompletely characterized. Our comprehensive analysis delineates distinct immune landscape profiles between CRC patients stratified by NETs-score, offering novel mechanistic insights into NETs-mediated immunosuppression and tumorigenic processes. Previous studies have elucidated specific NETs-dependent immunomodulatory pathways: 1. Macrophage polarization: Zhang et al. [20] demonstrated that ovarian cancer cells induce ERK pathway-dependent M0-to-M2 macrophage differentiation, creating a pro-metastatic niche. This aligns with our observation of M1 macrophage depletion in high NETs-score patients. 2. T-cell dysfunction: Kaltenmeier et al. reported that NETs-rich microenvironments promote exhaustive phenotypes in both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, characterized by sustained overexpression of inhibitory receptors [17]. 3.Treg induction: Wang et al. [21] further revealed that NETs drive metabolic reprogramming to foster CD4+ T cell differentiation into immunosuppressive Tregs.

Patients with high microsatellite instability (MSI-H) show positive responses to immune therapy, which can be particularly beneficial in CRC where immune response-related genes are involved [3]. Blocking autophagy through ATG7 has been found to significantly improve the efficacy of anti-PD1 therapy in CRC [22]. The IL-17 A has the potential to induce mitochondrial dysfunction and elevated levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [23]. It also enhances the release of inflammatory factors and aids in the infiltration of CD8+T cells into tumors. Inhibition of IL-17 A can enhance the effectiveness of anti-PD1 immunotherapy in CRC [24]. CLEC6A, or dectin-2, is a type II membrane receptor with an extracellular C-type lectin-like domain fold, is expressed at a higher level in tumors compared to normal tissues. It has a significant function in triggering immune responses against malignancies [25, 26]. VNN3, a member of the vanin family associated with oxidative stress, together with vanins and myeloperoxidase (MPO), participate in the regulation of neutrophil migration and contribute to processes such as inflammation, metabolism, and cellular stress [27]. MAPK1 (ERK2), a key component of the MAP kinase signaling cascade, critically regulates diverse cellular processes including proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and migration [28]. ENTPD4 regulates extracellular ATP levels, a key NETosis inducer. ATP depletion impairs immune cell function in the TME by limiting pyrimidine metabolism-a crucial energy source for anti-tumor immunity-while paradoxically being co-opted by tumors for survival [29]. SELPLG (PSGL-1) represents a novel phagocytic checkpoint molecule that is frequently overexpressed on tumor cells. Mechanistically, tumor-associated PSGL-1 impairs macrophage-mediated phagocytosis by disrupting the critical ICAM-1/LFA-1 interaction, thereby facilitating tumor immune evasion [30]. Studies confirm ITGAM’s involvement in intercellular adhesion processes across monocyte-macrophage-granulocyte populations, establishing its significant contribution to metastatic progression [31]. Siglec-14, an activating member of the Siglec receptor family, has been demonstrated to attenuate neutrophil activation through suppression of ROS production and cytotoxicity. This regulatory mechanism consequently dampens T-cell responses and contributes to an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [32, 33].

The present study found that individuals with a low-risk NETs-score showed heightened responsiveness to specific chemotherapeutic medications, such as Irinotecan, Trametinib, 5-Fluorouracil, Oxaliplatin, Topotecan, and KRAS (G12C) Inhibitor. In contrast, patients with a high-risk profile showed a reduced incidence of response to treatment and encountered faster disease progression. Previous studies have shown that NETs might cause immunosuppression and reduce the effectiveness of immunotherapy and chemotherapy in certain situations [12, 34]. Therefore, this study may have the ability to improve the efficiency of ICIs and chemotherapy results in patients with CRC.

Although the essential role of NETs in promoting tumor growth is well recognized, there is a lack of comprehensive knowledge regarding their interaction with other immune cells in the TME. The IFN induces the multiplication and stimulation of macrophages, resulting in the development of proinflammatory and tumoricidal properties. The presence of M1 macrophages in persons with solid tumors hinders the advancement of the tumor and is associated with a more positive prognosis [35]. On the other hand, the presence of a large number of neutrophils in solid tumors is associated with a poor prognosis for the patient. Neutrophils display diversity and are capable of performing several roles in the context of cancer treatment. Immunotherapy that is efficient can greatly increase the number of neutrophils that infiltrate tumors in the population. Neutrophils’ reactions depend on important components of antitumor immunity, including BATF3-dependent dendritic cells, IL-12, and IFNγ [36]. The present research supports the previous studies, as increased infiltration of macrophage M1, myeloid dendritic cells, neutrophils, and T cells, along with enhanced responses to immunotherapy, have been noted in patients with low NETs-scores. Teijeira’s research [18] showed that NETs act as a protective barrier for tumor cells against the cytotoxic effects of CD8+ T cells and NK lymphocytes. This is achieved by producing CD8+T cell and NK lymphocytopathy, which hinders the interaction between immune cells and effector cells. Furthermore, NETs can hinder T-cell responses through metabolic and functional deficiencies, hence promoting the advancement of tumors [17]. Essentially, within the TME, NETs impede the ability of T cells and NK cells to destroy tumors by creating physical obstacles. Moreover, NETs contribute to T-cell depletion, which further weakens their capacity to efficiently eradicate cancer cells.

Nevertheless, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of our current study, such as the lack of detailed experimental investigations. Future research will involve utilizing cell lines, animal models, and human samples to elucidate the potential mechanisms underlying the promotion of colorectal cancer metastasis by NETs. Furthermore, the validity of the NETs-related gene signatures and NETs-score examined in our study will need to be confirmed in clinical settings through extensive large cohort and multi-center studies.

In conclusion, this study developed the NETs-score using 10 prognostic NETs genes to assess NETs levels in CRC patients. The NETs-score predicts clinicopathological characteristics, prognosis, chemotherapy sensitivity, and suitability for anti-CTLA4 and PD-L1 therapy. By integrating the NETs-score with clinical data, this model can guide clinical practice and personalize follow-up strategies, enhancing individualized cancer treatment.
