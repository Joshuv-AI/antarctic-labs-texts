Optimal fibrinogen threshold in non-traumatic massive bleeding: a multicenter retrospective observational study
Authors: Siying Chen, Shiyu Liu, Bingkui Ren, Yuping Zhang, Jinglong Dai, Ziyan Ye, Lu Xu, Yun Fan, Chuanbao Li, Zhengying Jiang, Qian Liu, Kang Zou, Zhiyuan Zhang, Wenqing Lian, Shuangling Li, Peng Wang, Lu Ke, Weibang Pan, Wenjian Mao, Feng Guo, Yi-miao Xu, Ting Lin, Wenjun Tan, Weihai Wei, Qiuhua Yang, Jun Duan, Dezhi Ren, Zheng Duan, Luchuan Xing, Hua Xu, Lin Dou, Wensong Su, Linghui Zhan, Fengxue Zhu, Zhigang Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCurrent guidelines make no recommendation regarding appropriate fibrinogen replacement thresholds for non-traumatic massive bleeding. We aimed to determine the optimal post-treatment fibrinogen level associated with survival in critically ill patients with non-traumatic massive bleeding.MethodsThis multicenter retrospective cohort study enrolled adult ICU patients with non-traumatic massive bleeding and hypofibrinogenemia (fibrinogen <2.0 g/L) from 12 centers in China (2018–2025). Maximum fibrinogen level (FIBmax) achieved during treatment within 3 Days of hemorrhage onset was the primary predictor. We used the Boruta algorithm for variable selection, multivariable logistic regression to assess the independent association between FIBmax and hospital mortality, restricted cubic splines to examine dose-response relationships, and pairwise group comparisons with Bonferroni correction to identify the optimal threshold.ResultsAmong 358 patients (median age 59 years; 62.6% male), 74 (20.7%) died during hospitalization. FIBmax was independently associated with hospital mortality after adjustment for confounders (OR 0.37 per g/L increase; 95% CI 0.25–0.57; P < 0.001). Adding FIBmax to the base model significantly improved discrimination (AUC 0.899 vs. 0.868; P = 0.017), calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow P = 0.096 vs. 0.031), and reclassification (NRI 0.814; IDI 0.076). Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a non-linear dose-response relationship (P for non-linearity = 0.026), with an inflection point at 2.03 g/L. Pairwise comparisons identified a significant hospital mortality transition at 2.0 g/L: patients with FIBmax of 1.5–2.0 g/L had 3.23-fold higher hospital mortality compared to those achieving 2.0–2.5 g/L (P = 0.004), representing the only significant transition point across all adjacent group comparisons. Hospital mortality plateaued at higher levels, with no additional benefit observed above 2.5 g/L. Patients achieving FIBmax ≥ 2.0 g/L had hospital mortality of 10.1% versus 48.0% for those below this threshold (P < 0.001).ConclusionsIn critically ill patients with non-traumatic massive bleeding, a post-treatment fibrinogen level of 2.0–2.5 g/L appears optimal, and 2.0 g/L may represent a potential therapeutic threshold.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-026-05991-1.Keywords: Non-traumatic massive hemorrhage, Fibrinogen, Coagulopathy, Critical care

Introduction

Massive hemorrhage, defined as receipt of ≥ 10 units of packed red blood cells (RBC) in 24 h or ≥ 6 units of RBC in 6 h [1–4], remains a significant contributor to preventable mortality in critically ill patients [5–7]. Due to the rare and devastating clinical emergency, it is difficult to arrange RCT study [8]. Studies specifically addressing non-traumatic massive bleeding remain exceedingly scarce, and clinical management of these patients largely relies on extrapolation from trauma-related evidence, underscoring an urgent need for dedicated research in non-traumatic massive hemorrhage [9]. Timely bleeding control, hemodynamic resuscitation, and correction of coagulopathy are recommended as key components of management [7]. Among coagulation factors, fibrinogen is the first to reach critically low levels during major bleeding due to consumption and dilution [10, 11]. As the precursor to fibrin and a key mediator of platelet aggregation via glycoprotein IIb/IIIa receptors, fibrinogen plays a central role in achieving hemostatic competence [12].

Current guidelines recommend initiating fibrinogen replacement therapy at ≤ 1.5 g/L for perioperative and trauma bleeding [7, 13]. These thresholds represent a substantial upward revision from the historical benchmark of 1.0 g/L, which was originally established for congenital fibrinogen deficiency based solely on abnormal clotting times [11]. The mechanistic rationale for higher targets has since been clarified by viscoelastic studies demonstrating that clot strength increases in a concentration-dependent manner, with levels above 2.0 g/L associated with improved clot formation rates [14]. Consistent with this mechanistic insight, accumulating evidence from obstetric hemorrhage and cardiac surgery suggests that higher targets (≥ 2.0 g/L) may be associated with improved hemostatic outcomes [15, 16].

However, these findings cannot be directly extrapolated to critically ill patients in the ICU setting, as these patients present with complex pathophysiology and dynamic, multifaceted clinical courses that evolve rapidly [17, 18]. Importantly, non-traumatic massive bleeding—encompassing surgical, gastrointestinal, and other medical etiologies-differs pathophysiologically from trauma-induced coagulopathy [19–21]. The latter is characterized by hyperfibrinolysis, endotheliopathy, and the lethal triad of acidosis, hypothermia, and coagulopathy, whereas non-traumatic bleeding often occurs in patients with distinct hemostatic profiles [9, 21]. Furthermore, current guidelines on massive bleeding in critically ill patients provide little to no specific recommendations regarding fibrinogen targets [9], whether fibrinogen thresholds derived from trauma populations are applicable to non-traumatic settings remains an important knowledge gap.

In a recent expert survey [22], 29.6% of responders would set a Fib replacement threshold at 1.5 g/L, 49.5% at 2.0 g/L, while 12.9% at 2.5 g/L in bleeding critically ill patients. A recent large sample retrospective analysis found a replacement target at 2.0–2.5 g/L could significantly improve survival in critically ill patients with major bleeding with coagulopathy [17]. However, there is no study regarding critically ill patients with non-traumatic massive hemorrhage. Therefore, this multicenter study aimed to determine the optimal post-treatment fibrinogen level associated with survival in critically ill patients with non-traumatic massive hemorrhage, and to identify a potential therapeutic threshold that may inform clinical practice.

Methods

Study design and Participants

This multicenter retrospective observational study was conducted across 12 ICUs in China from January 2018 to January 2025. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Hospital (No. 2022BJYYEC-322-04), and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. This study adhered to the Strengthening Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (Fig. 1).

Study flow diagram for patient selection and enrollment

We included adult patients (≥ 18 years) admitted to the ICU with massive bleeding and hypofibrinogenemia (plasma fibrinogen ≤ 2.0 g/L) requiring ICU supportive care. Massive bleeding was defined as transfusion of ≥ 10 units of red blood cells (RBCs) within 24 h or ≥ 6 units within 6 h. Patients were excluded if they: (1) received palliative care or died within 24 h of hemorrhage onset; (2) sustained traumatic injuries; or (3) had any known congenital coagulation disorders.

Data collection

Data were collected using a standardized CRF across all 12 centers. D0 was defined as the period from hemorrhage onset to 7:00 AM the following day, corresponding to ICU admission. Baseline characteristics included demographics, comorbidities, vital signs, fluid intake, urine output, and estimated blood loss. Laboratory parameters included complete blood count, coagulation profile, liver and renal function tests, electrolytes, arterial blood gas, and inflammatory markers. Disease severity was assessed using the SOFA and APACHE II scores. Treatment data included use of vasopressors, hemostatic agents, surgical hemostasis, renal replacement therapy, mechanical ventilation duration, and transfusion of blood products. Serial laboratory measurements were recorded at D1–D3 and D7, and clinical outcomes were additionally assessed at D7 and D28. The maximum fibrinogen level (FIBmax) achieved during treatment within the first 3Days following hemorrhage onset was recorded as the primary exposure variable, and Hospital mortality was the primary endpoint.

Statistical analysis

Patients with > 30% missing data were excluded. For remaining participants, missing values were imputed using multiple imputations (mice package in R). The proportion of missing data for each variable is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Continuous variables were reported as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test; categorical variables were compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Variable selection was performed using the Boruta algorithm (a random forest-based feature selection method that identifies variables significantly more important than random noise). Confirmed variables were entered into univariate logistic regression (P < 0.1 threshold for inclusion), with multicollinearity assessed by variance inflation factors (VIF ≤ 5). A base model was constructed excluding FIBmax; FIBmax was subsequently added to form the new model. Discrimination was assessed by AUC, calibration by the Hosmer-Lemeshow test and Brier score, and incremental predictive value by continuous NRI and IDI (95% CIs via bootstrap, 1000 iterations). Decision curve analysis evaluated clinical utility.

The dose-response relationship between FIBmax and hospital mortality was examined using restricted cubic splines (3 knots). Non-linearity was tested via likelihood ratio test. Patients were categorized into seven fibrinogen groups (< 1.5, 1.5–2.0, 2.0–2.5, 2.5–3.0, 3.0–3.5, 3.5–4.0, ≥ 4.0); pairwise comparisons between adjacent groups used logistic regression with Bonferroni correction. Thromboembolism rates across groups were compared using Fisher’s exact test. Patients were then dichotomized by FIBmax ≥ 2.0 vs. < 2.0 g/L for clinical threshold validation.

Subgroup analysis by postoperative status was performed by including an interaction term (FIBmax × postoperative status) in the logistic regression model (likelihood ratio test).

All analyses were performed using R version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 358 critically ill patients with non-traumatic massive bleeding were enrolled (median age 59 years; 62.6% male), with an overall hospital mortality of 20.7% (74/358). Postoperative patients accounted for 70.1% of the cohort (Table 1).

Non-survivors exhibited significantly higher disease severity than survivors, with greater SOFA scores (11.0 [6.0–14.0] vs. 6.0 [4.0–10.0], P < 0.001) and APACHE II scores (21.0 [14.0–27.0] vs. 15.5 [10.0–22.0], P < 0.001). The distribution of bleeding sites differed markedly between groups (P < 0.001): gastrointestinal hemorrhage was the predominant etiology among non-survivors (50.0% vs. 28.9%), whereas surgical site bleeding was more common in survivors (39.8% vs. 27.0%) (Table 1).

Despite receiving more aggressive hemostatic interventions—including higher rates of hemostatic agent use (70.3% vs. 49.3%, P = 0.002) and tranexamic acid administration (37.8% vs. 21.1%, P = 0.005)—non-survivors required substantially greater cumulative transfusion support over 28 days, including higher volumes of red blood cells (20.25 vs. 14.00 U, P = 0.004), fresh frozen plasma (2,140 vs. 1,600 mL, P = 0.004), and fibrinogen products (High-level use: 39.2% vs. 15.8%, P < 0.001) (Table 2).

Non-survivors demonstrated more severe coagulopathy at baseline, with prolonged clotting times across multiple parameters and markedly elevated fibrinolytic markers (D-dimer 7.1 vs. 2.8 mg/L, P < 0.001). Baseline fibrinogen levels at admission were similar between groups (1.35 [1.03–1.67] vs. 1.49 [1.13–1.73] g/L, P = 0.144); however, the maximum fibrinogen level achieved during treatment was markedly lower in non-survivors (1.84 [1.55–2.37] vs. 2.90 [2.25–3.87] g/L, P < 0.001) (Tables 1 and 2).

Prognostic model development and incremental value of FIBmax

A total of 15 variables were identified as important predictors by the Boruta algorithm (Fig. 2). These variables were subsequently entered into univariate logistic regression analysis. Twelve variables demonstrated significant associations with hospital mortality (P < 0.1).All VIF values were below 5 (range: 1.08–1.57), indicating no severe multicollinearity. Multivariable logistic regression was then performed using the 11 candidate variables (excluding FIBmax) to construct the base model. Five variables remained independently associated with hospital mortality (P < 0.05) and constituted the final base model: post-operative patient status, platelet count, urine output, CRP, and surgical hemostasis.

The base model achieved an AUC of 0.868 (95% CI 0.820–0.917) for predicting hospital mortality. After adding FIBmax, the new model demonstrated significantly improved discrimination with an AUC of 0.899 (95% CI 0.858–0.941). The increase in AUC (ΔAUC = 0.031) was statistically significant. FIBmax alone achieved an AUC of 0.790 (95% CI 0.732–0.848) (Fig. 3A). Model calibration was assessed using multiple metrics. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test P-value improved from 0.031 in the base model to 0.096 in the new model (indicating acceptable calibration). Additionally, the Brier score decreased from 0.1062 to 0.0926 (Fig. 3B).

Decision curve analysis demonstrated that the new model provided greater net clinical benefit compared with the base model across all clinically relevant threshold probabilities (1%–50%), suggesting superior clinical utility for guiding treatment decisions in clinical practice (Fig. 3C).

Dose-response relationship and optimal threshold identification

Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a significant non-linear dose-response relationship between maximum fibrinogen level and hospital mortality. The analysis suggested an inflection point at 2.03 g/L. The predicted hospital mortality at this inflection point was 28.4% (Fig. 4).

(A) Odds ratio (95% CI) for hospital mortality across maximum fibrinogen levels. (B) Predicted mortality (%) across maximum fibrinogen levels

Patients with fibrinogen levels below this threshold demonstrated significantly elevated hospital mortality risk. At a fibrinogen level of 1.0 g/L, the odds of hospital mortality was 5.50-fold higher compared to the reference (OR = 5.50, 95% CI 2.92–10.35); at 0.5 g/L, the risk increased to 12.11-fold (OR = 12.11, 95% CI 4.79–30.61). Conversely, patients with fibrinogen levels above 2.03 g/L showed progressively reduced hospital mortality risk. At 3.0 g/L, the OR was 0.29 (95% CI 0.20–0.41), representing a 71% risk reduction; at 5.0 g/L, the OR was 0.10 (95% CI 0.03–0.33), corresponding to a 90% risk reduction.

Using the Youden index method, the optimal cutoff value of FIBmax for predicting hospital mortality was 2.04 g/L, with a sensitivity of 68.9%, specificity of 81.0%, positive predictive value of 48.6%, and negative predictive value of 90.9%.

Pairwise comparisons between adjacent fibrinogen groups revealed a single significant transition point. Hospital mortality risk decreased significantly from Group 2 to Group 3 (OR = 3.23, 95% CI 1.44–7.23, P = 0.004), remaining significant after Bonferroni correction (adjusted P = 0.026). No other adjacent comparisons reached statistical significance. These findings suggest that Group 3 (2.0–2.5 g/L) may represent the optimal post-treatment fibrinogen level (Table 5).

Using Group 3 as reference, after Bonferroni correction, patients with fibrinogen levels below this threshold demonstrated substantially elevated hospital mortality risk: Group 1 showed a 6.60-fold increase (95% CI 2.42–18.01, P < 0.05) and Group 2 showed a 3.23-fold increase (95% CI 1.44–7.23, P < 0.05); Fibrinogen levels above the Group 3 threshold were not associated with significant hospital mortality reduction.

Safety outcomes and clinical validation of the 2.0 g/L threshold

Using Group 3 (1/58, 1.72%) as the reference, Fisher’s exact test showed no statistically significant differences in thromboembolism risk across all groups. Rates ranged from 0% in Group 5 to 7.14% in Group 1 (2/28; OR 4.30, 95% CI 0.22–262.64, P = 0.246), with Group 7 showing a rate of 5.88% (4/68; OR 3.53, 95% CI 0.34–178.31, P = 0.373). However, the overall low event rate (12/358, 3.35%) resulted in wide confidence intervals.

Patients were dichotomized based on whether they achieved FIBmax ≥ 2.0 g/L: of 358 patients, 72.1% reached this target while 27.9% did not. Compared with patients achieving FIBmax ≥ 2.0 g/L, those failing to reach this threshold presented with higher disease severity (SOFA 9 [6, 14 ] vs. 6 [4, 10 ], P < 0.001; APACHE II 19 [13, 24 ] vs. 16 [10, 23 ], P = 0.008), more severe coagulopathy (baseline fibrinogen 1.30 vs. 1.50 g/L, P = 0.003; APTT 49.2 vs. 38.4 s, P < 0.001), and a higher prevalence of liver disease (38.0% vs. 12.4%, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 2).

Hospital mortality was markedly higher in patients who did not reach the 2.0 g/L threshold (48.0% vs. 10.1%, P < 0.001). The below-threshold group also demonstrated a significantly higher rate of persistent bleeding at day 28 (31.0% vs. 6.6%, P < 0.001), required more intensive organ support including renal replacement therapy (30.0% vs. 12.0%, P < 0.001) and vasopressors (89.0% vs. 74.8%, P = 0.004) at day 28, and had a longer ICU length of stay (8.0 vs. 5.0 days, P = 0.031) (Supplementary Table 3).

Furthermore, patients failing to reach the target required greater cumulative blood product support over 28 days, including higher volumes of red blood cells (18.25 [10.75, 24.00] vs. 14.00 [10.00, 22.00] U, P = 0.04) and fresh frozen plasma (2,000 [1,200, 3,550] vs. 1,500 [800, 2,400] mL, P < 0.001), and more frequent high-level fibrinogen product use (34.0% vs. 15.5%, P = 0.002) (Supplementary Table 2).

Subgroup analysis by postoperative status

Baseline characteristics differed substantially between subgroups. Non-postoperative patients were predominantly those with gastrointestinal hemorrhage (73.8% vs. 15.9%), and presented with higher disease severity (APACHE II 21.0 [14.0, 27.0] vs. 15.0 [10.0, 22.0], P < 0.001), more pronounced systemic inflammation (CRP 37.50 [11.20, 81.90] vs. 10.44 [3.40, 44.97] mg/L, P < 0.001), impaired renal function (creatinine 100.60 [67.00, 158.00] vs. 73.00 [52.10, 103.00] µmol/L, P < 0.001; BUN 11.20 [7.08, 19.50] vs. 5.74 [3.98, 10.60] mmol/L, P < 0.001), and more severe coagulopathy (PT 18.00 [16.20, 21.50] vs. 16.30 [14.10, 19.00] s, P < 0.001; APTT 45.10 [36.30, 59.10] vs. 40.10 [31.80, 53.90] s, P = 0.011). In contrast, postoperative patients had substantially greater estimated blood loss (2,000 [930, 3,380] vs. 850 [500, 1,400] mL, P < 0.001) and received more intensive early transfusion support (RBC 10.00 [6.00, 12.00] vs. 7.00 [6.00, 10.00] U, P < 0.001; FFP 600.00 [0.00, 1,200.00] vs. 0.00 [0.00, 630.00] mL, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Tables 4, 5).

The association between FIBmax and hospital mortality was not significantly modified by postoperative status. Binary threshold validation confirmed the discriminative value of the 2.0 g/L cutoff in both subgroups: among postoperative patients, hospital mortality was 5.5% in those achieving FIBmax ≥ 2.0 g/L versus 38.5% in those who did not (P < 0.001); among non-postoperative patients, the corresponding rates were 25.4% versus 58.3% (P <0.001).

Discussion

In this multicenter retrospective study of 358 critically ill patients with non-traumatic massive bleeding, we observed that the maximum fibrinogen level achieved during treatment was independently associated with hospital mortality, with each 1 g/L increase corresponding to a 63% reduction in death odds. We identified 2.0 g/L as a potential therapeutic threshold, below which hospital mortality increased substantially (48.0% vs. 10.1%). The optimal fibrinogen range appeared to be 2.0–2.5 g/L, with no significant additional survival benefit observed at higher levels.

The clinical management of non-traumatic massive bleeding has long relied on evidence derived from trauma populations, yet the underlying pathophysiology differs in ways that may warrant a distinct approach. Trauma-induced coagulopathy is driven primarily by tissue injury and hemorrhagic shock, which activate the protein C pathway on damaged endothelium, leading to systemic anticoagulation and hyperfibrinolysis [19, 20]. Coagulopathy in non-traumatic massive bleeding, by contrast, is predominantly consumptive and dilutional—resulting from ongoing hemorrhage, large-volume fluid resuscitation, and, in many patients, impaired hepatic synthetic capacity [21, 23]. In our cohort, gastrointestinal hemorrhage accounted for 50.0% of deaths, and 38.0% of patients who failed to achieve the 2.0 g/L threshold had underlying liver disease. These patients exist in a state of “rebalanced hemostasis” [21]—a fragile equilibrium that may be readily overwhelmed by acute hemorrhagic stress [23]. These considerations underscore the need for population-specific fibrinogen targets rather than direct extrapolation from trauma guidelines.

A notable finding of this study is that the optimal post-treatment fibrinogen threshold may be higher than currently recommended by the European trauma guideline, which suggests replacement therapy when fibrinogen level ≤ 1.5 g/L [7]. In the non-traumatic ICU setting, where ongoing consumption, impaired synthesis, and multi-organ dysfunction coexist, a higher fibrinogen reserve may be necessary to maintain hemostatic competence under sustained challenge. In vitro viscoelastic studies demonstrate that clot firmness increases in a concentration-dependent manner, with a minimum fibrinogen concentration of approximately 2.0 g/L required to maintain adequate clot formation [14]. Our data provided clinical evidence consistent with this rationale. The restricted cubic spline analysis identified an inflection point at 2.03 g/L, and the Youden index independently converged on 2.04 g/L—two distinct statistical approaches yielding a concordant estimate. Moreover, pairwise comparisons between adjacent fibrinogen groups revealed a telling gradient: compared with Group 3 (2.0–2.5 g/L), Group 1 (< 1.5 g/L) carried a 6.60-fold mortality increase (95% CI 2.42–18.01; P < 0.05), and Group 2 (1.5–2.0 g/L) still carried a 3.23-fold increase (95% CI 1.44–7.23; Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.026). Our study demonstrates that achieving a post-treatment fibrinogen level of ≥ 2.0 g/L is associated with better survival among critically ill patients with non-traumatic massive hemorrhage.

In our study, the dose-response curve plateaued at fibrinogen levels above 2.5 g/L, with no further reduction in mortality observed at higher concentrations. No adjacent group comparison above this range reached statistical significance. As the overall thromboembolism rate was low (12/358, 3.35%), group differences were not statistically significant, however, the numerically higher rate at FIBmax ≥ 4.0 g/L (5.88% vs. 1.72% in the 2.0–2.5 group) raises the possibility that excessive fibrinogen levels may not be without risk. This signal, while underpowered in our study, is biologically plausible, as elevated fibrinogen concentrations have been shown to produce denser fibrin networks with increased resistance to fibrinolysis [24], and large-scale epidemiological data have identified elevated fibrinogen as an independent risk factor for cardiovascular events [25]. Notably, current guidelines define only lower thresholds for fibrinogen replacement but do not establish an upper boundary [7, 9, 13], which may inadvertently encourage continued supplementation beyond the point of benefit—incurring unnecessary costs and exposing patients to potential thrombotic harm without measurable survival advantage. Our findings provide a therapeutic window of 2.0–2.5 g/L, supporting a goal-directed approach that avoids both under- and over-supplementation.

A clinically important observation is that failure to achieve FIBmax ≥ 2.0 g/L may serve as more than a prognostic marker—it may function as an integrative signal of treatment refractoriness. The 27.9% of patients with FIBmax < 2.0 g/L exhibited not only markedly higher mortality (48.0% vs. 10.1%), but also a constellation of adverse outcomes suggesting broader resuscitation failure: persistent bleeding at day 28 (31.0% vs. 6.6%; P < 0.001), higher vasopressor dependence (89.0% vs. 74.8%; P = 0.004), more frequent renal replacement therapy (30.0% vs. 12.0%; P < 0.001), longer ICU stay (8.0 vs. 5.0 days; P = 0.031), and greater cumulative transfusion requirements. Baseline fibrinogen at admission was similar between survivors and non-survivors (1.49 vs. 1.35 g/L; P = 0.144), yet their trajectories diverged sharply during treatment—suggesting that the difference may reflect treatment response rather than initial severity alone. Patients below the threshold had a significantly higher prevalence of liver disease (38.0% vs. 12.4%; P < 0.001), raising the possibility that impaired hepatic synthetic capacity may underlie the failure to achieve adequate fibrinogen restoration. In conclusion, for critically ill massive bleeding patients, persistent hypofibrinogenemia despite replacement therapy may warrant reassessment of source control and investigation of underlying consumptive or synthetic causes rather than simply escalating fibrinogen supplementation [18, 26].

Finally, our findings may have potential clinical implications. First, current critical care guidelines make no recommendation regarding fibrinogen targets in critically ill patients with massive hemorrhage [9], while existing treatment targets derived from trauma and perioperative settings (trigger at ≤ 1.5 g/L) may be insufficient for this population. Our data suggest that a post-treatment fibrinogen level of ≥ 2.0 g/L is associated with a significant and clinically meaningful reduction in hospital mortality, providing empirical evidence in this setting. Second, we provide a therapeutic window: supplementation beyond 2.5 g/L conferred no additional survival benefit while carrying theoretical thrombotic risk — an upper limit not specified in prior guidelines.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design precludes causal inference; patients achieving higher FIBmax may also reflect more effective overall resuscitation or less severe coagulopathy rather than a direct benefit of fibrinogen supplementation. Second, fibrinogen was measured at D0 without systematic capture of pre-ICU trajectories or hemorrhage-to-treatment intervals, and data on anticoagulant/antiplatelet use were not collected — both representing important gaps for future prospective work. Third, the modest sample size limited statistical power for subgroups and thrombotic safety analyses. Fourth, assay heterogeneity across centers and unmeasured confounding cannot be excluded. Fifth, we did not differentiate between fibrinogen replacement strategies (concentrate versus cryoprecipitate, or FFP) or account for concurrent blood product administration, limiting our ability to isolate independent treatment effects.

Conclusions

In critically ill patients with non-traumatic massive bleeding, a post-treatment fibrinogen level of 2.0–2.5 g/L may be associated with improved survival, with 2.0 g/L representing a potential therapeutic threshold.
