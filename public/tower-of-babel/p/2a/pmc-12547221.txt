A liberalized approach to directly observed therapy for HCV retains excellent cure rates among PWID in Vienna
Authors: Caroline Schwarz, Angelika Schütz, Maximilian Anibas, David JM. Bauer, Lukas Burghart, Michael Schwarz, Enisa Gutic, Thomas Reiberger, Michael Gschwantler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDirectly observed therapy (DOT) is an effective strategy to optimize hepatitis C virus (HCV) cure rates in people who inject drugs (PWID) on stable opioid agonist therapy (OAT). While adherence to daily DOT is excellent, it remains unclear if extended DOT distribution intervals result in similar sustained virologic response (SVR) rates.MethodsPWID undergoing DOT with direct-acting antiviral agents (DAA) alongside OAT for HCV infection at a low-threshold institution were included. Social distancing requirements during the COVID19 pandemic led to an extension in DOT dispensation intervals; therefore, the study population was classified according to “tight period” (DAA start 2014–2020) and “liberal period” (DAA start 2020–2023) cohorts. Socioeconomic characteristics, DOT distribution schedules, and rates of SVR at week 12 after end of therapy (SVR12) were compared between groups.ResultsWe included 719 consecutive PWID (male: 76.5 %; median age: 39 years), 441 (61.3 %) were treated in the ”tight period” and 278 (38.7 %) in the “liberal period”. Baseline characteristics were comparable between cohorts, however, socioeconomic features of the “liberal period” group showed more problematic features (unemployment: 83.1 % vs. 67.3 %; lack of housing: 38.5 % vs. 35.1 %; ongoing injection drug use: 64.0 % vs. 57.8 %; each p < 0.001).While the “tight period” group had their DAA most commonly dispensed on a daily basis (78.9 %), the “liberal period” group received their DAA/OAT mostly once weekly (45.0 %) or 2-3x/week (24.1 %; p < 0.001). The number of missed DAA ingestions (0.3 % vs. 0.4 %; p = 0.239) and SVR12 rates by modified intention to treat analysis (exclusion of PWID who were lost to follow-up [FU] or died) were similar (401/404, 99.3 % vs. 194/195, 99.5 %; p = 1.000) between tight and liberal period, respectively.Loss of FU after end of DAA treatment was more common during the "liberal period" (28.8 % vs. 7.9 %; p < 0.001), yet no treatment interruptions or early discontinuations occurred.ConclusionDOT originally aimed to address non-adherence among PWID by strict control through daily supervised OAT and DAA ingestion. While this approach inherently manifests a position of advanced distrust towards PWID, our findings suggest that a strategy of advanced trust and liberalization of DOT alongside effective harm reduction measures yields excellent adherence and results in similarly high HCV cure rates.

Introduction

Since direct-acting antiviral agents (DAA) were introduced in 2014, the World Health Organization (WHO) has declared hepatitis C virus (HCV) elimination a global public health goal for 2030.1, 2, 3 However, despite the availability of highly effective treatment with sustained virologic response rates (SVR) > 95 % even in formerly declared difficult-to-treat populations,4 HCV still accounts for considerable liver-related morbidity and mortality with 56.8 million people suffering from viremic hepatitis C worldwide.5,6

Over the past years, numerous HCV elimination programs have been implemented, leading to substantial treatment success.5,7,8 In accordance with current guidelines and due to the overall low HCV prevalence of approximately 1.0 % in Austria, Austrian HCV elimination strategies mostly focused on high-prevalence and high-incidence populations such as people who inject drugs (PWID), men who have sex with men (MSM), people who are incarcerated, and people who are experiencing homelessness.9,10 Additionally, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) record-based active recall strategies aimed to reach persons suffering from chronic hepatitis C who may not be associated with any of these high-incidence populations, and a physician-based hotline were established to facilitate easy access to HCV care.11, 12, 13 All of these HCV elimination efforts aim to increase screening, linkage to care, and treatment initiation to achieve the goal set by the WHO.1 The dynamic approaches to patient identification and the shifts in target populations in these HCV elimination efforts are a reflection of the diffusion of innovation theory, according to which the first phase of HCV elimination after DAA became available encompassed the patients that were easiest to reach and most receptive to the offer of HCV care, while in the following ongoing phase of the Austrian elimination effort, more difficult-to-reach patient cohorts remain to be identified and informed about the relevance and benefits of HCV-active treatment.14

Facilitating comprehensive HCV care among PWID, a population in whom the prevalence of HCV viremia has been reported to be as high as 38 %,10 is generally challenging as the high prevalence of psychiatric comorbidities, low socioeconomic status, and reluctance to attend health care institutions due to previously experienced stigma represent substantial obstacles in HCV screening and adherence to treatment in most areas of the world.15, 16, 17 These factors as well as a common distrust in the healthcare system often result in insufficient adherence to daily pharmacological therapy including DAA among PWID, thereby potentially impairing HCV treatment outcomes in this vulnerable cohort.18, 19, 20 In addition to these general barriers to treatment PWID face on an international level, Austrian national administrative barriers including the need to attend a tertiary care center for pretreatment evaluation and scheduled routine follow-up (FU) visits as well as the bureaucratic process of prescription approval to facilitate insurance coverage interfere with the aim to provide simplified screening and treatment for HCV in Austria.21

To overcome these manifold barriers to HCV care PWID are facing, the concept of directly observed therapy (DOT) with DAA alongside previously established opioid agonist treatment (OAT) has shown great success in Vienna, Austria22, 23, 24: According to this concept, PWID undergo HCV screening and pretreatment evaluation at an outsourced hepatitis clinic at a low threshold institution (LTI) and consequently receive DAA treatment alongside their OAT, which is dispensed at a pharmacy or the LTI under direct supervision of a health care professional. While DOT was originally established to deliver tuberculosis therapy, it has since become a highly effective approach to ensure not only daily ingestion of a prespecified type and dose of opioid agonist in OAT for PWID, but at the same time reduce the risk of opioid agonists being re-sold on the streets in this population.25 Integration of DAA therapy in this concept has been shown to achieve a significant increase in HCV cure rates among PWID worldwide,26,27 including hundreds of PWID in Vienna who have achieved SVR so far.22, 23, 24 Until recently, the success of the DOT approach was attributed not only to the paramount role of harm reduction measures provided by the LTI including a syringe exchange program and support by social workers, but also to the daily supervised application of DAA and the vigorous control inherent to the program.

However, it is inherent to daily DOT that PWID must attend their OAT dispensing LTI or their pharmacy during opening hours every day.28 From an economic and, importantly, also from PWID's personal aspect, it would be relevant to know if it is essential for DOT to be distributed daily or if intervals for DOT dispensation may be extended without deteriorating adherence to therapy.29 An extension of DOT intervals to thrice, twice or even once weekly with distribution of OAT and DAA for multiple days at once and having PWID ingest only the respective distribution day's doses of therapy on-site while leaving the remaining doses for them to take at home independently would not only disburden pharmacy and LTI staff but most importantly support PWID in what is the general idea behind OAT: To be an active part of society by attending work regularly and participating freely in professional as well as private life activities.19,29,30

The COVID19 pandemic with its social distancing requirements and establishment of access limitations, e.g. through requirements for routine short-term COVID19-PCR testing ahead of personal appointments, was an uncalled-for opportunity to investigate the effects of a liberation in DOT schedules on PWID'sadherence to OAT and DAA.31 This project is one of few HCV elimination strategies that were adapted to current requirements and successfully continued throughout the COVID19 pandemic, and aimed to assess adherence to DAA by HCV cure rates in the setting of extended DOT distribution intervals.12

While daily DOT is highly effective, it implies an advanced distrust in PWID. Through this project, we aimed to assess if a paradigm shift towards advancing trust instead of distrust in PWID may lead to equal results regarding adherence to OAT and DAA while empowering PWID and supporting their independence and responsibility.19

Methods

The objective of this study was the assessment of potential differences in outcomes of DAA therapy for HCV infection according to DOT distribution intervals among PWID to address the question if tight DAA DOT schedules are associated with increased SVR rates as compared to extended DOT dispensation intervals. We performed a retrospective analysis of data collected during routine workup and (post-) DAA treatment surveillance among HCV-viremic PWID in Vienna.

Setting

The physical platform used for this project was a low-threshold institution (LTI; “Ambulatorium Suchthilfe Wien”), centrally located in the city of Vienna and easily accessible via public transportation, that offers multidisciplinary outpatient care provided by physicians, nurses, and social workers, alongside harm reduction measures such as a syringe exchange service, and temporary housing specifically for PWID.32 The LTI is open 365 days a year and offers its services free of charge on a walk-in basis to all PWID.

Through a collaboration between the LTI and an authorized HCV treatment center, a low-barrier outpatient hepatitis clinic was established at the LTI. This outpatient clinic served as an authorized institution for pretreatment HCV evaluation, DAA prescription, and post-treatment FU for PWID, enabling HCV treatment initiation including pretreatment assessment and DAA prescription through a single visit. Subsequent DAA prescriptions were automatically renewed without PWID needing to attend the LTI again. During a second visit twelve weeks after end of DAA therapy treatment outcome was recorded.

All PWID included in this study received DAA alongside their previously established OAT (see Additional File 1) under the supervision of a health care worker at a pharmacy or LTI (directly observed therapy, DOT) to transmit PWID's established and typically excellent adherence to OAT onto DAA therapy. Therefore, it is imperative that OAT distribution schedules and distributing institutions remain unchanged. Hence, DOT intervals were determined according to PWID's preexisting OAT routine and DOT was distributed at the preexisting OAT distributing facility. PWID on three times, twice, or once weekly OAT distribution schedules correspondingly received the respective remaining doses of DAA for at home administration.

Between March 2020 and May 2023 capacities for elective procedures and FU appointments including HCV care were drastically reduced at Viennese hospitals to ensure the availability of health care coverage for individuals with urgent medical conditions despite the high degree of capacity utilization due to the COVID19 pandemic. Additionally, all individuals entering Viennese hospitals as outpatients or as staff had to provide up-to-date negative SARS-CoV-2-PCR testing. Outpatient visits except for emergencies were only accepted provided a specific appointment had been made in advance.

Contrary to these regulations exercised in Viennese hospitals, the LTI, which is per se not only a healthcare institution but a multidisciplinary social care facility, including its outpatient hepatitis clinic, remained open throughout the COVID19 pandemic with very limited restrictions, except for the requirement to wear a face mask and to use hand sanitizer upon entering. Importantly, no requirements to make an appointment ahead of time or to provide a negative SARS-CoV-2-PCR test were implemented for PWID. Instead, the LTI and its outpatient hepatitis clinic continued their “open house” policy, enabling PWID to show up according to their personal need and disposability, throughout the pandemic as was common practice in the emergency units of Viennese hospitals.

Because of COVID19-related social distancing requirements and the aim to disburden healthcare facilities, OAT distribution intervals - and, consequently, also DAA distribution intervals - were extended from daily to thrice, twice, or weekly in many PWID on OAT according to their past and current adherence as defined by missed visits for OAT dispensation at their pharmacy or LTI. Due to the successful implementation of these extended OAT dispensation intervals with PWID showing stable adherence to their liberalized schedules, the extended intervals were retained even after COVID19-related restrictions officially ended.

Patients

PWID on OAT aged 18 years or older who consented to undergoing DAA therapy according to DOT via the outpatient hepatitis clinic at the LTI, “Ambulatorium Suchthilfe Wien”, between September 2014 and May 2023 were included in the study. There were no additional inclusion or exclusion criteria.

Definition of treatment periods

Participants who started DAA therapy on or after March-15-2020 were retrospectively assigned to the “liberal period” cohort and results were compared to those who started DAA on or before March 14–2020 (“tight period”). Through this non-randomized approach, the two groups were naturally assembled according to the timing of DAA treatment initiation in the respective patients. By definition, the groups were not homogenously composed; instead, each group contained different DOT schedules (daily, thrice/twice/once weekly) and DAA treatment regimens (see “Antiviral regimen”) in variable proportions.

Pretreatment workup

Pretreatment assessment comprised of a comprehensive medical history and physical examination, abdominal sonography, liver fibrosis evaluation by transient elastography, and standard laboratory testing, as described in detail in previous publications of our group.21, 22, 23, 24,33 HCV infection was diagnosed according to serum HCV-RNA quantification using the COBAS TaqMan HCV Quantitative Test, version 2.0 (Roche), with a lower limit of quantification of 15 IU/mL. HCV GT and subtype were determined through the VERSANT HCV Genotype 2.0 Assay (Siemens).

In PWID with invalid or unsuccessful transient elastography, the APRI score was used for liver fibrosis evaluation (ULN for AST: 35 IU/L (females) and 50 IU/L (males); F0/F1: APRI ≤ 0.5, F4: APRI > 1.5).21, 22, 23, 24,34,35 Socioeconomic characteristics including employment status, housing status, relationship status, and criminal record, as well as alcohol consumption habits and ongoing IDU, defined as one or more injections within three months prior to assessment, were assessed according to PWID's reporting. OAT distribution schedules (daily vs. once/twice/thrice weekly) during DAA DOT were documented.

Antiviral regimen

All PWID received interferon-free DAA treatment. The choice of antiviral regimen was based on PWID's individual HCV pretreatment history and HCV GT as well as comorbidities, comedications, and current health insurance reimbursement principles. The following combinations were used according to label: ombitasvir/paritaprevir/ritonavir ± ribavirin (2D); ombitasvir/paritaprevir/dasabuvir/ritonavir ± ribavirin (3D); sofosbuvir (SOF) + ribavirin; sofosbuvir/ledipasvir (SOF/LED); sofosbuvir/daclatasvir (SOF/DAC); sofosbuvir/velpatasvir (SOF/VEL); glecaprevir/pibrentasvir (G/P); elbasvir/grazoprevir (EBR/GZR).

Study endpoints

Sustained virologic response 12 weeks after end of treatment (SVR12), defined as HCV-RNA < 15IU/mL, was assessed as primary endpoint. Treatment failure was defined as persisting HCV-RNA viremia ≥ 12 weeks after end of treatment without prior achievement of SVR12. HCV reinfection was defined as HCV viremia following documented SVR12. Patients were considered lost-to-follow-up if they did not show up for their post-treatment surveillance visit, scheduled at 12 weeks after end of therapy to assess achievement of SVR12, within six months after the scheduled date.

SVR12 rates were calculated for all PWID who had received one or more doses of DAA, excluding those in whom SVR12 was not achieved for reasons other than virological failure (i.e. death or loss to FU before documentation of SVR12), respectively (modified intention-to-treat analysis, mITT).

Adherence to DAA was assessed according to missed visits at the DOT dispensing pharmacy in all PWID. DAA adherence, frequency of premature treatment discontinuation (defined as DAA discontinuation before completion of the recommended treatment duration according to the label of the respective DAA regimen without treatment reuptake including compensation for missed doses), frequency of serious adverse events, and distribution of DOT intervals among the study population were investigated as secondary endpoints.

All results were calculated separately for the “tight period” and for the “liberal period” and were compared between the two groups.

Statistics

Continuous variables were described by median and interquartile range (IQR); categorical variables were depicted using numbers/frequencies and percentages of a specific characteristic. Depending on the type of variable and the presence of normal distribution, p-values were calculated using Student's t-test, Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney-test, or chi-squared test. According to sample size, chi-squared test or Fisher's exact test were used for comparison of proportions between groups, respectively. The Clopper-Pearson method was applied for the calculation of two-sided 95 % exact confidence intervals (CI).

Socioeconomic characteristics were summarized using the total number of patients per treatment group as the denominator, rather than limiting calculations to those with available data. This decision was made to reflect real-world differences between treatment periods and to avoid bias introduced by differential missingness, which was substantially more common in the earlier (“tight”) period. The number of patients with missing data per variable and group is reported in full in the table legend. We acknowledge that this approach departs from complete-case analysis but believe it offers a more accurate representation of the study population and context.

We conducted multivariable logistic regression to evaluate the association between patient group (tight vs. liberal period, representative of the pre-COVID vs. COVID era, respectively) and achievement of SVR, adjusting for relevant baseline characteristics and socioeconomic factors according to the above mentioned primary statistical analyses and/or univariate logistic regression analysis.

The statistical analyses were conducted using Microsoft Excel for Mac 2023, Version 16.75.2; Prism 9 for macOS 2023, Version 9.5.1; R by R Foundation for Statistical Computing (Version 4.3.2, 2023); and IBM SPSS Statistics 2022, Version 29. Figures were generated using Microsoft Powerpoint for Mac 2023, Version 16.77.1; Prism 9 for macOS 2023, Version 9.5.1; and the Mind the Graph platform (www.mindthegraph.com).

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The project was performed according to the Declaration of Helsinki, Good Clinical Practice guidelines, and local requirements. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board (Ethikkommission der Stadt Wien, EK 16-098-VK). Due to the retrospective design of the study, the ethics committee waived the need for written informed consent.

Results

Study population (Fig. 1, Table 1)

A total of 719 consecutive PWID who underwent DAA treatment according to DOT during the observation period and who finished post-treatment surveillance before October-23-2023 were included in the study (Fig. 1, Table 1). The study population was predominantly male (n = 550, 76.5 %) and the median age was 39.0 (IQR 11.9) years. Most PWID received OAT with morphine (76.9 %), while 12.4 % were treated with levomethadone, 5.1 % with methadone, and 5.6 % with buprenorphine. A total of 32 (4.5 %) PWID showed HIV coinfection while 8 (1.1 %) were coinfected with HBV. The majority presented with early stages of fibrosis, yet 139 (19.3 %) had cirrhosis and 72 (10.0 %) had previously been treated for HCV. Differentiation of treatment periods (Fig. 1, Table 1)

Between March-15-2020 and May-02-2023, 278 (38.7 %) PWID started DAA DOT (“liberal period”). In 441 (61.3 %) PWID, DOT with DAA was initiated prior to the outbreak of the COVID pandemic, from September-26-2014 to March-14-2020 (“tight period").

Comparison of clinical characteristics (Table 1)

Overall, clinical features in the two treatment periods were comparable including the distribution of OAT regimens (p = 0.871). Yet, during the “liberal period”, more PWID were HCV-treatment experienced (p = 0.019), and GT2 and GT3 occurred more often than in the “tight period” (p = 0.031). Of note, there was also a slightly higher percentage of missing data regarding GT (4.3 % vs. 1.4 % in the “tight period”) in the “liberal period” group.

Comparison of OAT distribution schedules (Fig. 2)

In the “tight period”, daily OAT/DAA distribution was the most frequently used approach (348/441, 78.9 %), while 6 (1.4 %) PWID were on thrice weekly schedules, 13 (2.9 %) were on twice weekly schedules, and 74 (16.8 %) underwent once weekly OAT/DAA DOT (Fig. 2). Treatment regimens and outcomes in the “tight period” (Fig. 3, Additional File 2))

Among the 441 PWID undergoing DAA treatment during the “tight period”, the following DAA regimens were applied: SOF/VEL in 167 (37.9 %), G/P in 152 (34.5 %), SOF/LED in 72 (16.3 %), EBR/GZR in 29 (6.6 %), SOF/DAC in 11 (2.5 %), 3D in 7 (1.6 %), SOF + ribavirin in 2 (0.5 %), and 2D in 1 (0.2 %) PWID.

While 401 (90.9 %) of the patients treated in the “tight period” achieved SVR12, 3 (0.7 %) patients with F1 fibrosis remained HCV viremic upon reevaluation and were therefore labeled as “treatment failures” (Fig. 3, Additional File 2)). All three of these patients completed their respective DAA treatments, yet one of them with GT3a infection undergoing treatment with G/P and another with GT4 infection receiving SOF/VEL did not attend their post treatment visits, hence SVR12 could not be documented. Both patients finished their DAA regimens but practiced ongoing IDU, hence SVR12 followed by early reinfection seemed likely. Nevertheless, to analyze a “worst case” scenario, treatment failure was assumed in both cases. The third patient labeled as “treatment failure” underwent treatment with G/P for GT3 infection. This patient admitted later that he had intentionally vomited all of his medication ingested at the pharmacy during DAA DOT to be able to inject his OAT intravenously later on.

Excluding 35 (7.9 %) individuals who did not present for verification of SVR12 and 2 (0.5 %) who died during post-treatment FU, mITT analysis delivered an SVR12 rate of 401/404 (99.3 %; CI: 97.9–99.9 %).

Comparison of treatment outcomes (Fig. 3)

While in the more recent “liberal period”, more patients were lost to FU (80/278 [28.8 %] vs. 35/441 [7.9 %]; p < 0.001), there was no difference concerning SVR12 rates according to mITT between the two treatment periods (“tight period”: 401/404 [99.3 %] vs. "liberal period”: 194/195 [99.5 %]; p = 1.000) (Fig. 3).

Impact of the COVID19 pandemic on treatment outcomes (Additional File 2)

The impact of the COVID19 pandemic as represented by the two treatment periods (“liberal period” representing the COVID19 era, “tight period” representing the pre-COVID19 era) is shown in Additional File 2. After adjustment for age, employment status, housing status, relationship status, history of imprisonment, alcohol consumption, and ongoing IDU as relevant confounding factors, only older age (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 1.12; 95 % CI 0.01–1.26; p = 0.038) was independently associated with the achievement of SVR 12 while there was no significant impact of the COVID19 pandemic (aOR 1.23; 95 % CI 0.13–26.20; p = 0.865) on treatment outcomes according to multivariable logistic regression analysis.

Health insurance status was not included in the regression models as nearly all participants had insurance coverage. Furthermore, HCV treatment experience, presence of advanced fibrosis, and sex were also excluded from the regression models as all individuals with treatment experience, advanced fibrosis, and/or female sex achieved SVR12, respectively. Due to the resulting model instability (e.g. infinite or undefined odds ratios), these factors were excluded from the regression models, in line with standard practice for handling quasi-complete separation.

Adherence to therapy and premature treatment discontinuation

During the “liberal period”, 26 of the 278 patients missed a total of 90 (1.0 %) of the 9.160 planned DAA ingestions while in the “tight period”, 28 of 441 individuals missed a total of 84 (0.4 %) of 23.688 ingestions (p = 0.129). All missed DAA doses were added at the end of the respective patients' individual treatments to ensure a total duration of therapy according to label of the respective DAA regimens. Importantly, all 719 PWID including those who were lost to FU and those who passed away finished their DAA treatment cycles.

Safety and adverse events

While a total of 16 (″tight period”: 7, 1.6 %; “liberal period”: 9, 3.2 %; p = 0.230) PWID were hospitalized during DAA treatment and/or during post treatment FU, none of the hospital admissions were related to HCV therapy or COVID19. Importantly, no severe adverse events were recorded among the study population.

Follow-up and HCV reinfections (Additional File 2)

Mortality was comparable between the two treatment periods (p = 0.381): 4 (1.4 %) of PWID treated in the “liberal period” died during a median FU of 14.0 (IQR 7.2) weeks while in the “tight period”, 12 (IQR 2.7 %) patients died within a longer (p < 0.001) observation period of 30.0 (57.5) weeks (Additional File 2). Causes of death were not related to DAA therapy in either group and included 2/4 (50.0 %) cases of endocarditis in the “liberal period”, and suicide in 2/12 (16.7 %) patients, malignancies in 2 (16.7 %), endocarditis in 1 (8.3 %), and hepatorenal failure in 1 (8.3 %) PWID undergoing DAA treatment during the “tight period”. For a total of 7/16 (43.8 %) deceased patients the causes of death remained unknown.

One (25.0 %) PWID who died after DAA treatment in the “liberal period” achieved SVR3 prior to his death while among the 12 patients who died after undergoing HCV treatment in the “tight period”, SVR12 was documented in 9 (75.0 %). The remaining 6/16 (37.5 %) patients passed after completion of DAA treatment yet before post-treatment response could be confirmed.

During FU, 12/278 (4.3 %) PWID treated in the “liberal period” experienced HCV reinfection, 2 (16.7 %) of whom achieved spontaneous viral clearance while 4 (33.3 %) were successfully retreated with DAA. By comparison, the reinfection rate was significantly higher in the “tight period” with 41/441 (9.3 %; p = 0.019) PWID presenting with HCV reinfection. Among this cohort, 3/41 (7.3 %) patients showed spontaneous clearance and 21 (51.2 %) underwent successful DAA retreatment.

Discussion

Directly observed therapy is an effective approach to successful HCV treatment among PWID at high risk for non-adherence to DAA therapy.27,36,37 Within the last decade, DOT has become an established approach for HCV microelimination in this otherwise underserved population frequently exposed to HCV.21, 22, 23, 24

The underlying assumption behind the DOT concept is that among PWID, adherence to DAA may be impaired due to manifold reasons such as psychiatric comorbidities, unstable socioeconomic conditions, and substance use disorders themselves.22,23 However, previous studies have clearly demonstrated excellent adherence to OAT among PWID enrolled in opioid agonist programs.28,38,39 The fact that linking supervised DAA application to OAT dispensation in the setting of DOT resulted in comprehensive achievement of SVR12 and extremely low rates of missed doses was previously concluded to be an effect of the strictly supervised treatment mode requiring PWID to attend their OAT distributing pharmacy or LTI every day.22, 23, 24 However, our data demonstrate that in the setting of extended DOT distribution intervals, consecutively implying increased patient responsibility and little to no actual “direct” supervision as in once weekly DOT (total n = 149 PWID included in mITT analyses across both the “tight period” and the “liberal period”), SVR12 rates (100 % vs. 99.7 %; p = 1.000) as well as missed DAA doses (p = 0.484) remained equal to daily DOT (total n = 383 PWID included in mITT analyses across both the “tight period” and the “liberal period”). Thereby, our results support the findings of a previous randomized clinical trial by Beer et al. assessing a smaller study cohort of a total of 110 PWID.40 Of note, among the 10 PWID receiving once weekly DOT across both study periods in whom one or more missed doses of DAA were recorded, 1 (10.0 %) was hospitalized during treatment, suggesting that in some cases the patients' missing presentation to OAT/DAA distribution may not be an adherence issue per se, but may rather be caused by other circumstances preventing their attending the pharmacy/LTI. Of note, recent data showed that even at suboptimal adherence rates, SVR can be achieved in most PWID due to the forgiveness of DAA treatment, and given an adherence of > 50.0 %, 99.0 % of PWID achieve SVR12.41, 42, 43

While PWID on OAT tended to generally show precarious socioeconomic characteristics in our previous studies,21, 22, 23, 24 socioeconomic profiles of our study cohort further deteriorated substantially in the “liberal period” (Table 2), leading to even more unfavorable socioeconomic circumstances among PWID who underwent DOT at extended dispensation intervals as compared to those receiving treatment in the “tight period”. This deterioration in socioeconomic characteristics is likely associated with the effects of the COVID19 pandemic which led to increased rates of unemployment, revealed potentially previously disguised homelessness, and drove substance use, not only in vulnerable populations such as PWID but also in the general population.44, 45, 46, 47 Furthermore, the observed dynamics in socioeconomic characteristics are likely associated with the fact that patients in less problematic overall conditions may have been treated at the beginning of this HCV elimination effort, while those who were most difficult to reach - as a potential reflection of their precarious living situations - were not treated until later in the project.14

Importantly, multivariable logistic regression analysis did not reveal a significant association between potential confounding factors, such as the COVID19 pandemic or PWID's socioeconomic status (Additional File 2), and treatment success, except for age (aOR 1.12; 95 % CI 0.01–1.26; p = 0.038). This suggests that any potential negative effects of socioeconomic disadvantage or of the COVID19 pandemic were effectively mitigated in the care pathway. Nevertheless, some variables showed large 95 % confidence intervals according to the logistic regression models, indicating potential analytical imprecision due to limited sample size, thereby warranting larger prospective trials to verify our findings in the future.

As the LTI that was used as a platform for HCV care in this study followed a “walk-in” principle before, during, and after the COVID19 pandemic, there was no waitlist for PWID to access HCV care or to undergo DAA treatment. While prior to the introduction of DAA in 2014 HCV treatment for PWID was likely postponed in many cases due to psychiatric contraindications to interferon-based antiviral treatment, the availability of DAA after 2014 facilitated comprehensive antiviral treatment uptake among PWID on OAT attending the LTI. With regard to the diffusion of innovation theory,14 this study represents an effort to extend HCV care to patients in need for increased support and/or encouragement, and to those unaware of their HCV infection, resembling an advanced stage of HCV elimination.

While DAA treatment in a regular outpatient setting rendered various complications associated with non-adherence, DOT with increased distribution intervals showed excellent results in terms of SVR12 in our cohort.48 Contrary to previous interpretation, according to our data this effect does not seem to be primarily associated with strict supervision, but rather with the streamlined course of action provided by the DOT concept49: After one single clinical visit at the LTI, DAA treatment was prescribed, ordered, and distributed as determined without PWID needing to attend scheduled checkup visits for prescription renewal requiring organization of appointments, attending a tertiary care medical facility, waiting times, repeated blood draws etc., thereby sparing them stress and potentially stigmatizing encounters.15,18,50,51 This hypothesis is supported by the following clinical observation: Upon initiation of a first pilot phase of the DAA DOT project based at the LTI in Vienna, fibrosis assessment was not performed on-site at the LTI but at an off-site tertiary care hospital in Vienna. The first 40 PWID undergoing DAA DOT in Vienna therefore had to personally attend the hospital during business hours once to be able to start DAA treatment. However, PWID frequently did not show up for their scheduled appointments, with an average of 2.5 visits per patient having to be scheduled to achieve one successful attendance (unpublished data by our group). This finding strongly supports the need for DOT in a low-barrier setting as most PWID would not achieve satisfying adherence in a standard outpatient treatment setting.21,23 Importantly, national organizational regulations regarding HCV treatment must be considered upon interpretation of our study design and our findings: While in Austria, only certified HCV treatment centers are authorized to prescribe DAA and only one monthly supply of DAA can be prescribed at a time,52 other countries already provide simplified approaches to HCV treatment, e.g. prescription of DAA by general practitioners as has been common practice in Australia since 2016.53 The impact of such reductions in bureaucratic barriers to HCV treatment has been demonstrated for example in a randomized controlled trial from 2020 that found increased treatment uptake as well as higher HCV cure rates among PWID receiving HCV treatment in a primary care setting as compared to a standard-of-care approach via a local hospital, promoting accessibility and simplification of HCV care to support HCV elimination.54

While DAA treatment success was independent of DOT distribution intervals in our study population, HCV reinfection rates were higher in the “tight period” (9.3 %) as compared to dose treated in the “liberal period” (4.3 %; p = 0.019). This seems to be an effect of extended FU time in the “tight period” cohort (30.0 [57.5] vs. 14.0 [7.2] weeks; p < 0.001), but may also indicate the success of previous HCV elimination efforts limiting overall HCV prevalence and, hence, reducing risk of transmission.13,21, 22, 23, 24 However, in this study, reinfection proportions were calculated among individuals retained in follow-up; since follow-up durations differed significantly between groups, the observed difference in reinfection rates may reflect differential time at risk rather than differences in underlying reinfection incidence. A formal survival analysis was not performed due to lack of time-resolution of reinfection and death dates. Compared to other international HCV elimination projects reporting reinfection rates between 2.7 % and 27.6 % over substantially longer periods of FU, results from our study are within the lower part of the range.55, 56, 57 Importantly, the long-term FU of the HERO study revealed a decrease in reinfections over time with the highest risk of reinfection occurring close to the achievement of SVR12, suggesting that an extension of FU may not necessarily result in a relevantly higher rate of reinfections.55

Despite our encouraging findings that are supported by the large sample size of 719 PWID and the long observation period of almost 9 years, our study also has some limitations. First, it is a retrospective study and consequently does not dispose of a standardized study protocol, therefore some parameters such as a detailed psychiatric history are not available. Second, while we consider our large sample size a strength of our work, future prospective studies are necessary to further increase sample size and thereby verify our findings as generalizability of our results is naturally limited by the retrospective study design. Particularly, the study participants are a convenient sample that may not be a representative sample of PWID in Vienna or Austria at large. Third, a very limited FU period was available for our study population. While this is inherently associated with the population investigated and in fact part of the basis for this project as HCV care for PWID was enabled without the call for vigorous surveillance, it would be interesting to analyze post-treatment FU in more detail. Specifically, HCV reinfection rates as well as mortality could be assessed over a more extensive period in future prospective trials. Ultimately, there was a higher rate of patients lost to FU (115/719, 16.0 %) as compared to previous studies by our group,21, 22, 23 which was mostly attributable to the high percentage of loss of FU during the “liberal period” (80/278, 28.8 % vs. 35/441, 7.9 % in the “tight period”; p < 0.001) - the impact of which warrants consideration. This effect is likely related to the setting during the COVID pandemic, the shorter FU period since DAA treatment completion, and the specific design of the study. However, the overall loss of FU rates observed in this project were still comparable to those reported in the literature.56 In these PWID who were lost to FU, SVR12 could not be documented, therefore in theory, a deterioration of the actual SVR12 rate among our cohort would be possible. However, literature as well as previous analyses from our group show that SVR12 is reached in virtually all PWID who complete DAA treatment, hence we believe it is reasonable to assume all of the patients lost to FU in this study achieved SVR12 as there were no interruptions or early terminations of DAA treatment.24,41,42 Nevertheless, the PWID who were lost to FU after end of therapy are likely the ones at highest risk for HCV reinfection.

While previously, DOT was designed to address advanced distrust in PWID's adherence, our data suggest that in the setting of streamlined HCV therapy, advanced trust manifested in extended DOT intervals may be the more warrantable approach for HCV treatment among most PWID on OAT, especially given the high rates of SVR induced by DAA even in settings with suboptimal adherence.18,41,42 An additional consequence to be drawn from our findings is the tremendous relevance of practical and organizational simplification of HCV treatment as remaining bureaucratic and administrative barriers may interfere with achieving the WHO goal of HCV elimination.50,51 Conversely, the integration of these measures in current HCV elimination programs may support HCV elimination and reduce administrative costs at the same time. Future HCV care projects for PWID on OAT should aim to encourage advanced mutual trust instead of distrust in patients by means of further simplification of HCV treatment in prospective settings.15,18,50,51 Most importantly, our findings provoke the assumption that dispensation intervals of DOT with OAT alone may also be extended without a deterioration in PWID's adherence, provided harm reduction measures are continuously applied and medical management is streamlined through the support of LTIs specialized on PWID's needs.

Conclusion

A policy of advanced trust instead of distrust towards PWID was associated with excellent adherence to DOT. Hence, loosened DOT schedules represent a promising approach in some PWID, supporting their integration into society and empowering them to be independent and responsible while retaining excellent adherence through streamlined modes of action.
