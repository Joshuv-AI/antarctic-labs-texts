Virulence profiling and biofilm-forming capacity of different E. coli pathotypes and phylogroups isolated from calf diarrhoea
Authors: Lívia Karahutová, Klaudia Čobanová, Dobroslava Bujňáková
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The study identified a broad range of Escherichia coli strains (STEC, NTEC, EPEC, ETEC, and EHEC) in calves suffering from diarrhoea.•In terms of phylogroup analysis, group D (32%) was predominant, followed by B1 (25%), B2 (12%), C (10%), E (10%), A (9%), and F (2%).•Up to 87% of isolates demonstrated the ability to form biofilm, with 32% classified as strong biofilm producers.•Both classical ZnO and ZnO nanoparticles effectively inhibited biofilm formation at all tested concentrations.Keywords: E. coli, Calves, Diarrhoea, Virulence factors, Phylogenetic groups, Biofilm, Antibiofilm activity

Introduction

E. coli is a Gram-negative, usually motile, non-spore-forming rod-shaped microorganism present in the normal gut microbiota of humans and warm-blooded animals (Karakaya et al., 2023). While most strains are harmless and rarely cause illness in healthy animals or people, certain pathogenic strains can lead to both intestinal and extraintestinal diseases (Kaper et al., 2004).

E. coli has become one of the most important causes of calf diarrhoea. It is now customary to classify pathogenic E. coli into several groups based on the presence of different virulent factors. Cattle are a major reservoir of E. coli, particularly Shiga toxin-producing E. coli (STEC), with Shiga toxins (two subgroups: Shiga toxin 1: Stx1 and Shiga toxin 2: Stx2) as the main virulence factors. Enterohaemorrhagic E. coli (EHEC) is a subset of the E. coli pathotype STEC, and in humans, EHEC may progress to a serious life-threatening condition called HUS (Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome), which causes acute renal failure and anaemia. Other pathogenic groups causing diarrhoea are enteropathogenic E. coli (EPEC), which causes adhesion and effacing (A/E) lesions in the small intestine via the outer membrane protein adhesion and effacing factor (intimin eaeA); enterotoxigenic E. coli (ETEC), which produces enterotoxins [Heat-labile enterotoxins (LT) and heat-stable enterotoxins (type STa or STb)]; and necrotoxic E. coli (NTEC), which produces a toxin called cytotoxic necrotizing factor (type 1: CNF1 and type 2: CNF2).

Based on phylogenetic cluster characteristic for E. coli, Carlos et al. (2010) and subsequently Clermont et al. (2013) developed and refined a classification system for sorting these bacteria into a certain phylogroup – A, B1, B2 and D (the main phylogroups), and A0, A1, B1, B22, B23, D1 and D2 (as the subgroups) (Carlos et al., 2010). Clermont et al. (2013) added four additional phylogroups: C, E, F and the Escherichia cryptic clade I. Individual phylogroups differ in phenotypic and genotypic characteristics (Gordon et al., 2008). The ability to identify phylogroups has been useful in predicting health risks. Strains also appear to differ in their ecological niches, life-history characteristics and propensity to cause disease (Gordon et al., 2008).

An important mechanism for bacterial survival in various environments is the ability to form biofilms and to persist within them (Fazeli-Nasab et al., 2022). This trait helps them survive environmental stresses, such as exposure to disinfectants and temperature changes, and also makes them more resistant to various antimicrobial agents (Ban-Cucerzan et al., 2025). As a result, new methods and compounds for eradicating bacterial biofilms are being developed. For example, various zinc derivatives are often used in pharmaceuticals, cosmetics and different areas of medicine (dermatology, dentistry) as well as in the food industry to control microbial growth and biofilm formation (Abendrot & Kalinowska, 2018). Additionally, various metal (including zinc) nanoparticles have been used as effective, eco-friendly and low-cost strategies for disease control (El-Shenawy et al., 2023).

In this study, we focused on detecting and identifying diarrhoea-causing E. coli isolates, investigating the diversity of the phylogenetic groups and classifying their virulence profiles, including biofilm-production capacity. Since in our previous study (Karahutová & Bujňáková, 2024), we demonstrated the effectiveness of different forms of inorganic and organic zinc against multidrug-resistant bacteria, we decided in this study to test the ability to inhibit biofilm formation using another zinc substance (ZnO and nanoparticles ZnO–nZnO).

Materials and methods

Samples

This study was conducted at eight dairy farms in eastern Slovakia, with 200–400 animals of the Holstein and Slovak spotted breeds. The calves were housed separately in calf boxes at 1–4 days of age. The sample collection (2024 – 2025) was based on reported diarrhoea incidence on dairy farms and was carried out in collaboration with a veterinarian from the University of Veterinary Medicine.

A total of 155 samples were collected from calves with diarrhoea (under 3 months of age) and examined for the presence of E. coli. The clinical presentation of the disease was predominantly mild. The calves were slightly apathetic; some were lying down, and all showed watery diarrhoea at the time of sampling. Rectal swabs were collected using sterile swabs (Meus s. r. l., STERILE R, 23,007, ITA) and transferred to the laboratory in coolers with ice packs.

Isolation and DNA extraction

The samples were processed immediately upon receipt. Rectal swabs were inserted into 5 ml sterile test tubes with phosphate-buffered saline solution [(PBS); Oxoid, Basingstoke, CM0509, Hampshire, UK] and vortexed for approximately one minute. Subsequently, 100 µL from the test tubes were inoculated on MacConkey Agar (Oxoid, R383.00, Basingstoke, UK), and the plates were incubated at 37 °C overnight (aerobic conditions). Typical colonies (pink, dry, non-mucoid) were evaluated and identified using the ENTEROtest24 (Erba Lachema, MLT00007, Brno, CZ) biochemical test and confirmed as E. coli by a Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionisation – Time of Flight Mass Spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) Biotyper (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, DE), according to Bessède et al. (2011) with a score of >2.0.

Isolation of bacterial DNA was performed using a commercial kit – the DNeasy PowerLyzer Microbial Kit (Qiagen GmbH, 51,704, Hilden, DE) – according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and purity of extracted DNA were analysed using a NanoDrop 2000c Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, ND-2000, Wilmington, USA). The DNA samples that met the conditions were stored frozen at -80 °C for further use.

Phylotyping of the isolates and detection of virulence genes

Isolates were assigned to phylogenetic groups (A, B1, C, B2, D, E and F) using the quadruplex phylogroup assignment method (Clermont et al., 2013) by multiplex (arpA with yjaA; chuA with TspE4.C2) and simplex (arpAgpE, trpAgpC) polymerase chain reactions (PCR). The classification of the phylogenetic group was based on the presence or absence of genes chuA, yjaA, arpA, TspE4.C2 DNA fragment and additional specific genes for the E (arpAgpE) and C (trpAgpC) groups (Lescat et al., 2013).

Simplex PCR assay (stx1, stx2, eaeA, cnf1, cnf2, stI, stII, ltI – Pass et al., 2000, and papC- Le Bouguenec et al., 1992) were used for the detection of nine genes encoding virulence factors (VFs), namely: papC – P fimbrial adhesin; eaeA – intimin; heat-stable (stI, stII) and labile (ltI) toxin genes, shiga toxins – stx1, stx2 and cytotoxic necrotising factor genes (cnf1, cnf2).

The specific primer sequences, the sizes of the amplified products, the annealing temperatures and the relevant references are provided in Supplementary Table S1. The PCR amplifications were carried out in a single sterile tube with a reaction mixture containing 1.5 μL DNA, 0.2 μL each of the 0.1 – 1 μM primers, 0.2 μL 1 U Taq DNA polymerase, 2 μL 1.5 mM MgCl2, 2.5 μL 200 μM dNTP and 2.5 μL 10x reaction buffer in a total volume of 20 μL.

The PCR protocol consisted of an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 33 cycles of DNA denaturation at 95 °C for 50 s, primer annealing at 57–63 °C (according to the primers) for 50 s, primer extension at 72 °C for 1 min and a final extension at 72 °C for 7 min. For the separation of PCR products, a 1.5% agarose gel stained with GoodViewTM (Beijing SBSGenetech Co., HGV-2, Ltd.) was used together with a Mid-Range DNA Ladder (100 bp to 3 kb linear scale; Jena Bioscience, M-203S, Jena, Germany).

Biofilm formation

The ability to form a biofilm was evaluated using a quantitative crystal violet assay with a 96-well microtiter plate (Thermo Scientific™, 5029,140, Roskilde, Denmark), as was previously described by Karahutová et al. (2021). Isolates were resuspended in a Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) broth (Oxoid, BO1230B, Basingstoke, UK) and adjusted to a turbidity of 0.5 McFarland standard. Then, 200 µL of bacterial suspension was pipetted into the plate. Uninoculated BHI medium was used as the negative control. After incubation (48 h at 37 °C), the adherent cells were washed three to five times using a PBS solution and stained with a 0.1% crystal violet water solution (Mikrochem, 10153SZ90–00,025, Pezinok, Slovakia). For classification, we used the average optical density (OD) value and cut-off value (ODc) (defined as three standard deviations (SD) above the mean OD of the negative control). Antibiofilm activity of ZnO and ZnO nanoparticle substances

Testing of ZnO forms against isolates with strong biofilm-forming ability was performed using classical ZnO – ZnO [puriss. p.a., ACS reagent, ≥99.0%; (Mr = 81.39 g/mol)] and commercially produced ZnO nanoparticles – nZnO (Mr = 81.39 g/mol; particle size <100 nm), both from Sigma Aldrich/Merck, according to a previously reported method (Karahutová & Bujňáková, 2024). Stock suspensions of both zinc forms were prepared at 100 mmol/L and diluted in sterile water. The effects of the ZnO forms were tested in eight replicates per strain.

The antibiofilm testing was performed as mentioned above; however, instead of 200 µL of bacteria, only 100 µL of bacterial solution and 100 µL of the tested compounds were added (concentrations: 50 mmol/L, 25 mmol/L, 12.5 mmol/L, 6.25 mmol/L, 3.12 mmol/L). A solution of bacteria without Zn substances was used as a positive control.

Results

Isolation and phylogenetic classification of E. coli

The results of isolation and identification of samples by cultivation, ENTEROtest24 (Erba Lachema Brno, CZ), and MALDI-TOF MS Biotyper (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, DE), indicated the E. coli prevalence in 100 calves (155/100; 64.5%).

The obtained isolates (one calf equals one sample) were subjected to phylogenetic classification. The most prevalent phylogroup was D (32%), followed by B1 (25%), B2 (12%), C (10%), E (10%), A (9%) and F (2%). The Escherichia cryptic clade I was not detected.

Occurrence of virulence determinants

The proportions of detected virulence genes were as follows: papC (44%), stx2 (20%), cnf1 (16%), eaeA (13%), cnf2 (13%), stx1 (4%), and stI (1%). Conversely, 32 isolates lacked any of the tested genes, and 39 isolates had only one gene (papC = 27%; stx1 = 1%; stx2 = 11%). The most commonly identified E. coli pathotype was STEC (stx1+ and/or stx2+ = 17 isolates), followed by NTEC (cnf1+ and/or cnf2+ = 6 isolates), EPEC (eaeA+ = 6 isolates), ETEC (eaeA+, stI+ = 1 isolate) and EHEC (eaeA+, stx2+ = 1 isolate). We also identified combined pathotypes: STEC/NTEC (isolates containing stx1+, stx2+, cnf1+, cnf2+ = 5) and EPEC/NTEC (eaeA+, cnf1+, cnf2+ = 5). The distribution of gene combinations across individual phylogroups is presented in Table 1 and Graph 1. Among the seven detected genes, the distribution was significantly lower in group B2 (p = 0.005).

The greatest diversity of gene combinations was observed in phylogroup B1, where half of the isolates (13/25) carried at least two of the genes. We found a significantly lower representation of genes cnf1 (p = 0.0005), cnf2 (p = 0.0006) and stx2 (p = 0.004) in the D phylogroup compared with the B1 phylogroup.

Unlike phylogroup B1, phylogroup A showed the lowest gene diversity, with up to 6/9 (66.6%) of the isolates lacking the tested genes. The papC gene was found more frequently (either alone or in combination with other genes) in approximately half of the isolates belonging to the phylogroups D (18/32 – 56.2%), B2 (6/12 – 50%) and E (5/10 – 50%). In phylogroup E, the stx2 gene was the most frequently detected (6/10 – 60%), significantly more than in other phylogroups, (p = 0.004), and even in this group, all isolates carried at least one of the tested genes.

Biofilm formation and antibiofilm properties of ZnO substances

Up to 87 isolates had biofilm-forming capacity, while 13 were classified as non-biofilm formers. Based on the optical density of the adhering dye, the isolates were classified as strong (32%), medium (28%) and weak (27%) biofilm producers. Almost all isolates from the STEC pathotype (14/17 – 82.3%) were strong biofilm producers, followed by NTEC (6/6 – 100%), the hybrid pathotypes STEC/NTEC (4/5 – 80%), ETEC (1/1 – 100%) and EHEC (1/1 – 100%). The remaining five isolates belonged to the group containing only the papC gene. Biofilm formation varies across phylogroups, and it is not possible to definitively identify which groups produced stronger or weaker biofilms (Table 1). However, we can conclude that all isolates belonging to phylogroup E formed at least a weak biofilm, and only one isolate from phylogroup B1 did not produce any biofilm.

Isolates classified as strong biofilm formers (32%) were subjected to investigation of the modulation of biofilm formation by the addition of two different zinc forms (ZnO and nZnO), both at five different concentrations (3.12; 6.25; 12.5; 25; 50 mmol/L). Discussion

Calf diarrhoea is attributed to both infectious (various enteric pathogens: viruses, bacteria, protozoa) and non-infectious (such as calf housing, nutrition – poor quality, too hot/cold milk, lying on cold/wet ground) reasons (Izzo et al., 2011). In our study, we isolated potential disease-causing E. coli strains in approximately 65% of the samples. Of these, 32% lacked all tested virulence genes. This finding was expected, given the reasonable percentage of non-pathogenic E. coli among the isolates, as described by the authors Awad et al. (2020). The reasons for the occurrence of these isolates could be the presence of other virulence genes (as we tested), or the calf could have been affected by other infectious agents or by non-infectious factors.

The isolates were classified phylogenetically according to Clermont et al. (2013). The most common phylogroup detected in our isolates was D (32%). These results contrast with findings from Coura et al. (2017) in that this phylogenetic group was not identified. Similar findings were also reported in another study (Coura et al., 2019) in which phylogroup D was identified in 5.2% of isolates obtained from water buffalo calves. Although this group was the most abundant, it exhibited only low diversity in the occurrence of virulence genes. The most frequently detected gene in the mentioned phylogroup was papC, present in 18 isolates (56%). Fourteen strains carried the papC gene alone, while 4 carried it in combination with other genes. In calves with diarrhoea, Ibrahim et al. (2025) observed a relatively high detection rate (71.42%) of the papC gene, whereas Umpiérrez et al. (2021) reported a lower detection level (35.7%). The papC gene is frequently found in certain virulent phylogenetic groups, such as B2 and D, which are known to cause severe extraintestinal infections (Karami et al., 2017). This gene is primarily associated with adhesion (P fimbriae) to uroepithelial cells in urinary tract infections (UTIs) (Whelan et al., 2023), and with the potential for septicaemia; on the other hand, however, it also enables bacteria to colonise the intestinal mucosa (Karami et al., 2017), although it does not primarily cause diarrhoea. While the situation is well understood for UTI E. coli determinants, including phylogroup markers, it remains unclear for E. coli causing diarrhoea. There is no definitive information about the link between phylogenetic group and clinical data on a diarrhoeal episode (Regua‐Mangia et al., 2010; Mosquito et al., 2015).

The second most prevalent phylogroup in our isolates was B1 (25%). Karakaya et al. (2023), Maciel et al. (2019) and Coura et al. (2017) reported that B1 was the most frequently detected phylogroup of E. coli in calves (28%, 70%, 67.8%), while Bessalah et al. (2016) made similar findings in camel calves (52.8%). In our study, half the isolates (13/25, 52%) in phylogroup B1 carried at least 2 virulence-encoding genes, whereas none were found in 4 isolates. Within this group, the most frequently detected gene was intimin (eaeA), present in 8 of 13 isolates (61.5%). Intimin (eaeA) is an essential genetic element for forming attaching/effacing (A/E) lesions (Mainil et al. 2013), and it is a key virulence factor in enteropathogenic (EPEC) and enterohemorrhagic (EHEC) E. coli. EPEC is an important causative agent of diarrhoeal diseases in human infants and children (Croxen et al. 2013), but it is also isolated from various domestic animals, including dogs, cats and chickens (Krause et al. 2005). A study by Eldesoukey et al. (2022) found that 22.7% of EPEC isolates were derived from calves with diarrhoea.

Based on the presence of virulence genes, E. coli isolates were classified as STEC (17%), NTEC (6%), EPEC (6%), ETEC (1%) and EHEC (1%). We also identified hybrid pathotypes STEC/NTEC (5%) and EPEC/NTEC (5%). The most common pathotypes of E. coli causing colibacillosis in calves are the STEC, ETEC and EHEC (Aref et al., 2018). On the other hand, adult ruminants are considered to be the main reservoir of STEC (Algammal et al., 2020), and disease outbreak depends primarily on animal age, immunity and gastrointestinal tract conditions. However, STEC present a serious public problem because it is associated with haemolytic uremic syndrome and haemorrhagic colitis in humans, and their presence in calves due to insufficient hygiene during processing and handling can cause several food-borne outbreaks (Nguyen et al., 2015). Mixed E. coli pathotypes, particularly STEC/ETEC hybrids, are common in calves with diarrhoea, thereby affecting disease severity and treatment resistance. These hybrid strains, which carry virulence factors from both STEC and ETEC, can cause more severe and prolonged diarrhoea. The study by Belete et al. (2022) found that 29% of identified the E. coli from calves with diarrhoea were hybrid strains. Although ETEC and STEC are commonly observed in calves with diarrhoea, the occurrence of hybrid strains highlights the complexity of E. coli infections in this context. NTEC is characterised by the presence and expression of CNF and CDT toxins, which can be detected in infections in humans and animals, as well as in healthy individuals (Mainil et al., 2014). The expression of these toxins can lead to invasion, essential for causing septicaemia and infections of internal organs (Mainil et al., 2014). NTEC1 (cnf1 gene) is often isolated from diarrhoea cases in domestic animals and ruminants, whereas NTEC2 (cnf2 gene) is mainly associated with septicaemia in ruminants (Umpiérrez et al., 2021). On the other hand, a systematic review and meta-analysis showed that CNF (both cnf1 and cnf2) is 1.8 times more common in isolates from diarrhoeic calves than non-diarrhoeic calves (Kolenda et al., 2015). Our study demonstrated the presence of cnf genes roughly equal (16%vs 13%), which aligns with previous studies demonstrating the co-existence of strains with similar virulence factors (Mainil et al., 2014; Umpiérrez et al., 2021).

E. coli strains that cause enterotoxigenic (ETEC) infections isolated from calves are most frequently linked to phylogroups B1 and A, while EPEC is most often identified in phylogroups B2 and B1 (Modgilet et al., 2020). The fact that these pathotypes predominantly belong to phylogroup A, which includes commensal strains, and B1, which encompasses environmental strains, suggests that these phylogroups may be well-adapted to the calf gastrointestinal tract through factors such as the ability to bind to the gut and produce toxins (e.g., heat-stable or heat-labile toxins in ETEC). The majority of our EPEC isolates (and EPEC/NTEC) belonged to phylogroup B1, and one ETEC isolate fell into phylogroup D.

A total of 87 of the 100 isolates formed biofilm across a spectrum weak–medium–strong. A slightly lower percentage with biofilm-forming ability was reported in studies by Dhaouadi et al. (2023), in which 60% of isolates obtained from diarrhoeic calves formed biofilm, and by Hassen et al. (2022), in which only 45% of isolates formed biofilm. A relatively low percentage of strong biofilm formation (8.1%) in young diarrhoeal animals was also reported by Vijay et al. (2015); however, they focused solely on detecting biofilm formation in EAEC isolates, and other isolates formed at least a weak biofilm (65.3%).

Nowadays, studies focus on removing bacterial biofilm with various mainly non-antibiotic, ecological and sustainable substances. In our case, after the last observation of antimicrobial and antibiofilm properties of different forms of inorganic and organic zinc against multidrug-resistant Escherichia, Klebsiella, Staphylococcus and Pseudomonas (Karahutová & Bujňáková, 2024), we focused on the effect of commercial classic inorganic ZnO and nanoparticle nZnO substances in different concentrations to reduce the production of strong biofilm by E. coli. Nanoparticles in particular have been demonstrated to be safe, non-toxic (because they contain minerals essential to the body) and effective therapeutic agents due to their excellent physicochemical characteristics and versatile mode of action (Meena et al., 2018). The results showed biofilm inhibition by both types of ZnO at all tested concentrations. Existing studies have thus far documented that susceptibility to Zn among microorganisms and even within strains of individual species can be highly variable (Karahutová & Bujňáková, 2024; Fontecha-Umaña et al., 2020; Surjawidjaja et al., 2004); moreover, administration of different forms and quantities of Zn can cause significant differences in inhibitory and/or stimulatory effects.

It should be emphasised that we have performed only in vitro tests evaluating biofilm inhibition with two different forms of commercial zinc (ZnO and nZnO) at five various concentrations (3.12; 6.25; 12.5; 25; 50 mmol/L). Both forms of ZnO prevented biofilm formation at all doses tested, but nZnO treatment resulted in significantly lower biofilm formation. Our in vitro studies provide preliminary data on the high efficacy of mentioned zinc forms in combating our E. coli strong biofilm producers, but they do not take account of complexity of living tissue, such as immune factors, structural factors and other factors, which can significantly limit its efficacy in vivo. Nevertheless, our results at least suggest the potential for their use as a anti-biofilm agent to prevent the further spread of virulent isolates.

It should also be noted that, mentioned commercial nZnO has already been used in an experiment on lambs conducted at our institute. Among other things, this research focused on monitoring changes in the intestinal microbiota, and feeding diets enriched with nZnO reduced the intestinal bacterial population (Ruminococcus-Eubacterium-Clostridium cluster and/or the Bacteroides/Prevotella group) and, moreover, the activity of some bacterial enzymes connected with intestinal pathogenesis in comparison with control group (Čobanová et al., 2026).

Conclusion

The results of this study show that we can identify various phylogroups and pathotypes of E. coli in calves with diarrhoea, along with their distinct virulence genes, their combinations and relatively strong biofilm-forming ability. The high numbers of STEC and NTEC, as well as the presence of EPEC ETEC, and EHEC, can pose a threat not only to other farm animals but also to humans. As mentioned above, many of the studied isolates formed biofilms, which can help bacteria survive in the housing environment despite disinfection and cleaning after calves are moved to an alternative housing system. For this reason, we decided to test the effect of two forms of ZnO on biofilm disruption. Our in vitro data show that both forms of ZnO, inhibited biofilm growth across all tested concentrations, and nZnO treatment resulted in significantly lower biofilm formation at four of the five concentrations compared to ZnO. ZnO in classic and nanoparticle forms has a great potential to be developed as anti-biofilm agents. However, a limitation of this study is that it is merely an in vitro experiment, although the commercially available nZnO used in our study has already been applied to lambs and a reduction in the number of certain bacterial groups has been demonstrated.
