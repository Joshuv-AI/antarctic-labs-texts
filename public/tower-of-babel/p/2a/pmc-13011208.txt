Photoperiod as a neuroendocrine regulator: A review on pineal-melatonin signaling and physiological plasticity in poultry
Authors: Xiaoyan Wang, Aftab Shaukat, Mohamed Tharwat, Asfand Yar Khan, Muhammad Asif Arain, Moar Khan Vistro, Mohamed M. Abdel-Daim, Fahad A. Alshanbari, Ting Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Photoperiod, the cyclical exposure to light and darkness, is a master regulator of neuroendocrine function in poultry, primarily via the pineal-melatonin axis. The pineal gland translates photic cues into rhythmic melatonin secretion, which synchronizes circadian and seasonal biological processes, linking environmental signals to systemic homeostasis. This review systematically explores pineal-melatonin signaling network, highlighting its pivotal role in regulating reproduction, growth, metabolic homeostasis, immune competence, and stress resilience. Mechanistically, melatonin modulates core clock genes (BMAL1, PER, CRY), interacts with hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal and adrenal axes, and activates antioxidant, metabolic and epigenetic pathways, thereby integrating environmental cues with physiological adaptation. Photoperiodic manipulation leverages this axis to enhance energy allocation, feed efficiency, and adaptability to environmental fluctuations. Recent advances reveal that melatonin-mediated effects extend to nutrigenomic regulation and epigenetic reprogramming, offering precision strategies for optimizing poultry productivity, welfare, and health. Moreover, controlled light regimes enhance melatonin-dependent benefits, including improved egg production, enhanced meat quality, and overall welfare promotion. By integrating molecular, cellular, and applied perspectives, this review provides a comprehensive framework for understanding how the pineal-melatonin axis translates temporal environmental information into adaptive physiological outcomes, informing sustainable light management strategies in both commercial and experimental poultry systems.

Introduction

In avian species, the photoperiod is defined as duration of daily light and darkness, serves as the dominant environmental zeitgeber or “time giver”, synchronizing internal circadian and seasonal rhythms with external environmental cycles (Huang et al., 2026; Kumar et al., 2017). This temporal alignment orchestrates a wide array of physiological and behavioral processes, from daily activity patterns and feeding behavior to seasonal reproduction and metabolic adjustments (Tripathi et al., 2025). Foundational work in avian chronobiology, has demonstrated that variations in day length entrain circadian oscillators and seasonal endocrine rhythms via neuroendocrine pathways, thereby optimizing survival and reproductive success under fluctuating environmental conditions (Gwinner and Helm, 2003). Such photoperiodic entrainment ensures that critical life history events, including reproduction and molting, occur in synchrony with optimal environmental resources, highlighting the evolutionary significance of light-mediated temporal regulation in birds (Piccolin, 2018).

Photoperiodic regulation in birds is mediated by a sophisticated network of photoreceptive and neuroendocrine structures (Kumar, 2015). Unlike mammals, where the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) serves as the principle circadian pacemaker, birds possess multiple light-sensing systems, that collectively coordinate rhythmic physiological and behavioral processes. These systems include retinal photoreceptors, deep brain photoreceptors, and a directly photosensitive pineal gland, which together detect changes in ambient light and convey this information to endocrine targets (Ashour et al., 2025; Durieux, 2024; Majumdar et al., 2024). The pineal gland in birds is not merely a secondary component but functions as an autonomous circadian oscillator: pinealocytes harbor intrinsic phototransduction machinery, including opsin-based photopigments and clock genes, enabling them to directly sense light and generate robust circadian oscillations of melatonin (Chaurasia et al., 2005; Trivedi et al., 2017). Retinal and deep brain photoreceptors complement this system by providing additional pathways for photic input, which converge with pineal signals to fine-tune circadian outputs across physiological systems (Prusik and Lewczuk, 2020).

In poultry, melatonin functions as a central neuroendocrine mediator linking photoperiodic cues to diverse physiological systems. Through interaction with G protein-coupled melatonin receptors (MT1, MT2, and MT3) expressed across the hypothalamus, pituitary, gonads, and peripheral tissues, melatonin modulates key hormonal pathways, including the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis (Promket et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2017). Photoperiod-driven melatonin rhythms regulate gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) synthesis and downstream secretion of luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle stimulating hormone (FSH), thereby controlling seasonal gonadal growth, ovulation, and reproductive timing (Abulaiti et al., 2023; Bao et al., 2023; Vieira et al., 2025). Beyond reproduction, melatonin exerts pleiotropic effects on oxidative homeostasis, immunomodulation, and gastrointestinal physiology by influencing the expression of antioxidant enzymes, pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines, and gut barrier proteins (Afzal, 2024; Horodincu and Solcan, 2023; Shaukat et al., 2026). These molecular actions underscore melatonin’s integral role in mediating physiological plasticity, enabling birds to adaptively respond to fluctuating environmental conditions while maintaining metabolic and immune competence.

The manipulation of photoperiod has therefore emerged as a critical strategy in poultry management, aimed at optimizing production performance and welfare. Controlled lighting regimes modulate melatonin secretion patterns, with extended light exposure suppressing nocturnal melatonin peaks and shorter dark periods prolonging melatonin elevation, thereby affecting circadian entrainment and downstream neuroendocrine outputs (Geng et al., 2022a; Relić et al., 2022). These photic interventions influence feed intake, growth, egg production, laying rhythms, stress responsiveness, and immune function, reflecting the systemic integration of light cues into physiological networks (Nega et al., 2024; Saad et al., 2024). Despite extensive empirical application, the precise molecular pathways by which pineal melatonin integrates photoperiodic information with hypothalamic and pituitary regulators remain incompletely characterized. A mechanistic understanding of these pathways including phototransduction in pinealocytes, clock gene regulation, and receptor-mediated signaling in target tissues is essential for refining lighting programs that enhance production efficiency while supporting adaptive physiological plasticity and welfare. This review accumulates current knowledge on photoperiodic neuroendocrine regulation in poultry, emphasizing pineal melatonin signaling as a central integrator of environmental and physiological cues, and identifies key molecular and translational research gaps for future exploration.

Photoperiod perception and light signal transduction in poultry

Retinal and extra-retinal photoreceptors

Photoperiodic perception in poultry is governed by an integrated network of photoreceptive systems that detect environmental light cues and convert them into neuroendocrine signals regulating circadian and seasonal physiological processes, including reproduction, metabolism, and behavior (Egbuniwe and Ayo, 2016; Yan et al., 2025). Birds possess multiple photoreceptive organs, notably the retina pineal gland and deep-brain photoreceptors (DBPs), which operate synergistically to sense light intensity, duration and spectrum. The avian retina contains classical image-forming photoreceptors (rods and cones) alongside non-image-forming photosensitive cells that contribute to circadian entrainment and neuroendocrine regulation beyond visual perception (Rios et al., 2019). Light information captured by retinal photoreceptors is transmitted via opsin-mediated pathways, including intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells, to central circadian regulators such as the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), while simultaneously modulating pineal function through the retina-pineal axis. Retinal illumination, particularly during the subjective night, can induce phase shifts in the circadian rhythm of serotonin N-acetyltransferase (AANAT) activity within the pineal gland, thereby adjusting melatonin synthesis and providing temporal cues for biological clocks in poultry (Korf, 2025; Zawilska et al., 2004). Variations in photoperiod and light spectrum influence pineal melanopsin expression and melatonin content, which in turn modulate immune function, reproductive physiology, and metabolic processes (Geng et al., 2022a; Horodincu and Solcan, 2023). This dynamic interplay underscores the retina’s pivotal role in aligning internal biochemical rhythms with external light-dark cycles, ensuring optimal physiological synchronization.

In addition to retinal and pineal inputs, avian DBPs, located in the hypothalamus and other deep-brain regions, independently perceive light penetrating cranial tissues and express opsins such as melanopsin (Opn4), neuropsin (Opn5), and vertebrate ancient opsin, each responsive to specific wavelengths to mediate photoperiodic signaling (Rios et al., 2019). These DBPs relay photoperiodic information directly to the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis, orchestrating seasonal gonadal growth, regression, and associated neuroendocrine adaptations essential for reproductive timing (Kuenzel et al., 2015; Valdez, 2025). Notably, experimental studies reveal that photoperiodic regulation of reproductive physiology can persist even in the absence of retinal input, highlighting the autonomous function of DBPs in seasonal neuroendocrine control. Collectively, the integrated activity of retinal, pineal, and extra-retinal photoreceptors enables poultry to decode environmental photoperiodic signals accurately, synchronize internal rhythms with external light cues, and optimize physiological performance across reproduction, metabolism, and immune competence.

Spectral sensitivity and light characteristics

Birds possess multiple photoreceptive systems, including the retina, pineal gland, and extraretinal deep-brain photoreceptors, which collectively enable the integration of environmental light cues into coordinated physiological and behavioral responses (Soni et al., 2021). Poultry are tetrachromatic, with retinal cone photoreceptors sensitive to wavelengths ranging from ultraviolet to red (approximately 320–700 nm), thereby extending visual perception beyond the human range (de Souza Granja Barros et al., 2024). The presence of a specialized ultraviolet-sensitive cone type, together with retinal oil droplets, broadens spectral discrimination and enhances wavelength-specific sensitivity. Peak spectral sensitivities are generally observed in the blue (≈400–480 nm), green–yellow (≈545–575 nm), and orange–red (≈580–700 nm) regions (Franco et al., 2022). In addition to single cones, double cones play a crucial role in motion detection and may also contribute to non-visual photic responses (Yang et al., 2016).

These spectral characteristics underlie species- and function-specific photic effects in poultry. Short-wavelength light (blue and green) is frequently associated with improved growth performance, reduced stress, and favorable welfare-related behaviors (Abdel-Moneim et al., 2024), whereas long-wavelength light (red) penetrates cranial tissues more effectively, enabling stimulation of hypothalamic photoreceptors and acceleration of reproductive maturation penetrates cranial tissues more effectively, enabling stimulation of hypothalamic photoreceptors and acceleration of reproductive maturation (Galan et al., 2025). Light intensity further modulates photoreceptor activation thresholds and circadian entrainment. In this context, bird-specific measures such as chicken-corrected lux (clux) provide a more biologically relevant assessment of perceived light intensity than conventional human-based lux units (Remonato Franco, 2022).

Photoperiod duration is a primary regulator of circadian and seasonal timing mechanisms, governing melatonin secretion patterns and downstream gonadotropin release. Importantly, light perception is not solely dependent on total daily exposure; brief light pulses delivered at specific circadian phases can elicit sustained endocrine responses, highlighting the significance of temporal light patterning (Olanrewaju et al., 2018). Intermittent lighting schedules have been shown to differentially affect behavior, metabolic processes, and endocrine function in poultry, indicating that not only the total photoperiod but also the temporal distribution of light and dark periods critically modulates photic signal transduction and circadian regulation (Geng et al., 2022b). Moreover, spectral sensitivity and photic responsiveness vary markedly among poultry species and across developmental stages. Juvenile birds typically exhibit heightened sensitivity to short wavelengths that influence growth and somatic development (Abd El-Hack et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2016), whereas sexually mature layers show stronger reproductive responses to long-wavelength light due to enhanced hypothalamic activation (Reddy et al., 2012). Accordingly, tailoring lighting programs to species- and age-specific photic sensitivities is essential for optimizing both welfare and productivity in modern commercial poultry systems.

Pineal gland architecture and melatonin biosynthesis

Avian pineal morphology and functional autonomy

The avian pineal gland is a pivotal neuroendocrine organ that convert photoperiodic information into rhythmic melatonin secretion, thereby orchestrating circadian and seasonal physiological processes. Morphologically, it is notably distinct from the mammalian pineal, exhibiting a larger size, dense vascularization, and an organized arrangement of follicular or tubular structures dominated by pinealocytes interspersed with glial cells (Fejér et al., 2001; Haldar and Bishnupuri, 2001). These structural features confer a high degree of functional autonomy, enabling the gland to act as an independent circadian oscillator. Unlike mammals, where melatonin rhythms depend on retinal input transmitted via the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) and sympathetic pathways, the avian pineal is intrinsically photosensitive, detecting light directly to regulate melatonin synthesis (Trivedi et al., 2017). This photoreceptive capability is mediated by specialized photopigments and a robust molecular clock within pinealocytes, allowing the gland to function simultaneously as a photoreceptor, circadian pacemaker, and endocrine effector. Light wavelength and intensity critically influence melatonin production, which in turn modulates immune gene expression and neuroendocrine-immune interactions, underscoring the pineal’s integrative role in avian physiology (Horodincu and Solcan, 2023; Qi et al., 2022).

Functionally, the avian pineal orchestrates diverse behavioral and physiological processes through its rhythmic melatonin output. Seasonal fluctuations in photoperiod-driven melatonin regulate reproductive timing, synchronizing gonadotropin release and breeding cycles in species such as geese (Bao et al., 2023). In smaller passerines, like zebra finches, pineal-derived melatonin modulates the circadian timing of vocalization, locomotor activity, and other daily behaviors, with disruptions resulting in altered activity patterns (Wang et al., 2012). Notably, the pineal’s circadian oscillator retains environmental light information even in vitro, demonstrating its capacity to encode and preserve photoperiodic cues independently of systemic input (Brandstätter et al., 2000). Moreover, tissue-specific regulation is evident in turkeys, where retina and pineal differ in melatonin secretion and clock gene expression under constant conditions, reflecting the nuanced and compartmentalized control of circadian rhythms (Lorenc-Duda et al., 2008).

In contrast, the mammalian pineal gland lacks direct photosensitivity and relies entirely on neural signaling from the SCN through sympathetic innervation to regulate melatonin synthesis (Avila et al., 2023). Its compact, lobular architecture and lack of intrinsic photosensitivity exemplify a centralized circadian control strategy. Birds, however, employ distributed circadian regulation involving the pineal gland, retina, and SCN (Brandstätter and Abraham, 2003), facilitating rapid, flexible modulation of melatonin rhythms in response to environmental cues. This functional autonomy supports adoptive behaviors, metabolic homeostasis, and seasonal reproduction illustrating how structural specialization underlies evolutionary divergence in vertebrate photoperiodic regulation. Thus, comparative analyses of pineal morphology and function illuminate the relationship between glandular architecture, melatonin biosynthesis, and circadian organization across taxa (Horodincu and Solcan, 2023).

Molecular pathway of melatonin synthesis

In birds, the pineal gland functions as both a neuroendocrine organ and an autonomous circadian oscillator, endowed with intrinsic photosensitivity that allows direct entrainment to environmental light-dark cycles. Unlike mammals, where pineal activity is predominantly regulated by the suprachiasmatic nucleus via sympathetic innervation, avian pinealocytes contain photoreceptive molecules and a complete enzymatic apparatus that enable self-sustained rhythmic melatonin production (Fig. 1), independent of retinal or hypothalamic inputs (Horodincu and Solcan, 2023). This unique organization permits rapid and direct modulation of melatonin secretion in response to ambient light conditions, thereby ensuring tight synchronization between environmental photoperiods and internal circadian timing systems. Melatonin biosynthesis in birds proceeds through the conserved tryptophan-serotonin-melatonin pathway, with tightly regulated enzymatic steps conferring circadian precision (Adamska et al., 2016; Deeb et al., 2024). Tryptophan is sequentially converted to serotonin, which serves as the immediate precursor of melatonin and exhibits pronounced nocturnal accumulation in the avian pineal gland (Paredes et al., 2009). During darkness, serotonin is acetylated by arylalkylamine N-acetyltransferase (AANAT), the primary rate-limiting enzyme, followed by methylation by hydroxyindole-O-methyltransferase (HIOMT/ASMT) to yield melatonin (Klein et al., 2019). AANAT displays strong nocturnal induction through combined transcriptional activation and post-translational stabilization, while light exposure rapidly suppresses its activity via pinealocyte-intrinsic photic signaling (Hickman et al., 1999). In contrast, HIOMT is more constitutively expressed and fine-tunes melatonin output, contributing to interspecies variation in melatonin amplitude (Awad Alkozi, 2018). Collectively, the coordinated regulation of these enzymes ensures precise circadian and seasonal melatonin rhythms essential for avian behavior, metabolism, and reproductive timing.

At the molecular level, circadian rhythmicity in poultry is orchestrated by a conserved transcription–translation feedback loop involving the core clock genes CLOCK, BMAL1, PER (PER1–3), and CRY (CRY1–2). The CLOCK–BMAL1 heterodimer initiates rhythmic gene expression by activating PER and CRY transcription, while the accumulated PER–CRY complexes subsequently repress CLOCK–BMAL1 activity, generating self-sustained ∼24 h oscillations (Wellard et al., 2025). In avian species, rhythmic expression of these clock components has been documented in both central (pineal gland, hypothalamus) and peripheral tissues (liver, gonads), underscoring the integrative role of circadian clocks in regulating energy metabolism, immune responsiveness, and reproductive competence (Trivedi et al., 2016).

Effective synchronization between central and peripheral oscillators is essential for maintaining physiological homeostasis and productive efficiency in poultry. The avian pineal gland acts as both an autonomous circadian pacemaker and a photoreceptive endocrine organ, with melatonin exerting its effects via specific receptors (Mel1a, Mel1b, Mel1c) widely expressed across tissues (Horodincu and Solcan, 2023). Disruption of photoperiodic cues or melatonin signaling can desynchronize internal clocks, leading to compromised growth performance, immune dysregulation, and reproductive inefficiency (Afzal, 2024). Moreover, circannual rhythmicity critical for seasonal reproduction and long-term metabolic adaptation is mediated by photoperiod driven changes in melatonin duration, which modulate hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis activity and seasonal gene expression profiles (Liu et al., 2025a). Collectively, the photoperiod melatonin clock gene axis constitutes a fundamental regulatory framework that integrates environmental light signals with endogenous timing systems, providing a strong physiological basis for optimized management of lighting programs in poultry production systems.

Melatonin receptors and signal transduction

Melatonin mediates its wide-ranging physiological actions in poultry primarily through high affinity G protein coupled receptors (GPCRs), namely MT1 (Mel1a), MT2 (Mel1b), and the avian-specific Mel1c receptor (Rashid et al., 2024). These receptors exhibit distinct yet partially overlapping tissue distribution patterns, providing a mechanistic basis for the pleiotropic effects of melatonin on circadian rhythmicity, reproductive function, metabolism, immune regulation, and stress adaptation in birds. MT1 and MT2 receptors are abundantly expressed in central circadian regulatory regions, including the pineal gland, hypothalamus, and suprachiasmatic nucleus like structures, as well as in peripheral tissues such as the gonads, liver, gastrointestinal tract, and primary immune organs (Horodincu and Solcan, 2023). In contrast, Mel1c largely absent or nonfunctional in mammals is uniquely conserved in avian species and is highly expressed in the retina, pineal gland, and brain regions involved in photoperiodic time measurement (Yadav and Haldar, 2013). This distinctive receptor repertoire underscores the heightened sensitivity of poultry to photoperiodic cues and justifies the species-specific consideration of melatonin signaling in avian physiology (Zhang et al., 2020).

At the cellular level, melatonin receptor activation initiates multiple downstream signaling pathways that convert photoperiodic information into coordinated physiological responses. MT1 and MT2 receptors predominantly couple to Gi/Go proteins, resulting in inhibition of adenylate cyclase, reduced intracellular cyclic AMP (cAMP) concentrations, and modulation of protein kinase A (PKA) activity (Guo et al., 2017). This signaling axis is fundamental to the entrainment of molecular clockwork, including the regulation of core clock genes (CLOCK, BMAL1, PER, and CRY), and to the suppression of nocturnal neuronal excitability (Poletini et al., 2015). Beyond cAMP-dependent mechanisms, melatonin receptors also engage phospholipase C (PLC) signaling, leading to intracellular Ca²⁺ mobilization and protein kinase C (PKC) activation, processes closely linked to immune modulation and antioxidant defense (Pandi-Perumal et al., 2008). Moreover, emerging evidence highlights cross-talk between melatonin signaling and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK/ERK) pathways, supporting roles in cell survival, anti-inflammatory responses, and metabolic homeostasis (Ge et al., 2019). Collectively, these convergent signaling mechanisms provide a robust justification for the central role of the photoperiod melatonin axis in integrating circadian and seasonal physiology in poultry, with direct implications for productivity, health, and environmental adaptation.

Neuroendocrine integration of photoperiodic signals

Hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis

Photoperiod serves as a primary environmental cue regulating reproductive physiology in birds, primarily through neuroendocrine integration within the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis (Fig. 3). Light perceived by retinal and extra-retinal photoreceptors is conveyed to the hypothalamus, where it modulates the activity of neurosecretory neurons controlling gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) synthesis and release (Mobarkey et al., 2010). Pineal melatonin secretion provides a hormonal representation of day length, which fine-tunes GnRH activity and synchronizes reproductive function with favorable seasonal conditions (Bao et al., 2023). Within the hypothalamus, photoperiodic cues regulate the pulsatile secretion of GnRH, which drives anterior pituitary release of luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) (Fig. 3). Long photoperiods stimulate GnRH neuronal activity, enhancing LH and FSH secretion, which promotes gonadal growth, follicular development, and steroid hormone production (Wang et al., 2019). Conversely, short photoperiods suppress GnRH activity, resulting in lower gonadotropin secretion and reproductive quiescence (Liu et al., 2025a). This precise modulation allows birds to temporally coordinate sexual maturation and reproductive output with photoperiodic changes.

In poultry, photoperiodic manipulation is widely applied to optimize sexual maturation and egg production. Early photostimulation accelerates hypothalamic activation, advances puberty, and promotes ovarian follicular recruitment, as reflected by elevated LH, FSH, and estradiol levels (Liu et al., 2025a). However, excessively early stimulation may compromise long-term reproductive performance, including egg-laying persistency and semen quality, whereas stimulation at an optimal physiological age enhances reproductive efficiency without adverse effects (Shi et al., 2021; Shi et al., 2020). In layer ducks, extended photoperiod exposure increased ovarian weight, advanced follicular hierarchy, and upregulated expression of hormone receptors (FSHR, LHR, ESR), indicating enhanced ovarian sensitivity to gonadotropins (Cui et al., 2019). Collectively, these findings establish the HPG axis as the central conduit for photoperiodic regulation of reproductive timing and efficiency in avian species.

Hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis

The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis represents the central neuroendocrine pathway through which environmental cues, notably photoperiod, are translated into adaptive physiological responses in poultry. Light information, perceived by retinal and deep brain photoreceptors, synchronizes the suprachiasmatic nucleus, the master circadian pacemaker, and modulates pineal melatonin synthesis. Melatonin, conveys photoperiodic signals to the hypothalamus, regulating downstream components of the HPA axis such as corticotropin releasing hormone (CRH) and adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), ultimately controlling glucocorticoid secretion through the CRH and ACTH corticosterone pathway (Udi, 2025). In poultry, changes in lighting regimes that alter melatonin secretion patterns are closely linked with modifications in corticosterone levels and behavioral stress indicators, highlighting melatonin’s pivotal role in stress modulation.

Rhythmic melatonin secretion encodes day-night length and shapes HPA axis responsiveness, enhancing physiological resilience to environmental challenges (Galan et al., 2025). Photoperiod manipulations or melatonin supplementation that increase nocturnal hormone levels attenuate stress-induced corticosterone elevations by dampening hypothalamic CRH and pituitary ACTH release, thereby supporting metabolic stability, immune function, and welfare in broilers and layers (Malek et al., 2020). Moreover, photoperiod-driven melatonin rhythms act as regulators of adaptive capacity, with extended day lengths elevating basal corticosterone and HPA reactivity, while cyclic lighting that optimizes nocturnal melatonin enhances stress tolerance and circadian endocrine regulation (Ozioma et al., 2024). Collectively, these findings underscore an integrated neuroendocrine strategy whereby melatonin–HPA axis interactions enable poultry to modulate energy balance, immunity, and behavior in response to environmental fluctuations.

Hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis

The hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid (HPT) axis represents a pivotal neuroendocrine system that translates environmental photoperiodic cues into precise physiological adaptations, thereby maintaining metabolic homeostasis and supporting seasonal adjustments in vertebrates, including poultry. Within this axis, hypothalamic thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH) stimulates the anterior pituitary to release thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), which subsequently drives the thyroid gland to synthesize and secrete thyroxine (T₄) and its active form, triiodothyronine (T₃) (Debonne et al., 2008). Circulating thyroid hormones (THs) regulate basal metabolic rate, thermogenesis, nutrient metabolism, and growth through nuclear and membrane receptor-mediated pathways, while providing negative feedback to the hypothalamus and pituitary to fine-tune axis activity.

In avian species, photoperiod strongly influences HPT axis activity via deep brain photoreceptors that detect changes in day length, initiating neuroendocrine adjustments (McNabb, 2007). Prolonged daylight enhances TSH secretion from the pituitary pars tuberalis and promotes local conversion of T₄ to T₃ in the mediobasal hypothalamus by upregulating type-2 deiodinase (DIO2) and suppressing type-3 deiodinase (DIO3), thereby elevating hypothalamic T₃ concentrations (Fallahshahroudi et al., 2021). This seasonal regulation aligns energy expenditure, thermogenesis, and reproductive readiness with favourable environmental conditions. High T₃ levels accelerate metabolic rate, stimulate carbohydrate and lipid turnover, and support growth and feed intake, whereas lower TH levels conserve energy during less favourable periods. (Aluwong et al., 2013) Such photoperiod-dependent modulation of thyroid function enables birds to synchronise metabolic and reproductive processes with seasonal cycles, optimizing energy allocation for survival and reproductive success.

Physiological plasticity driven by photoperiod and melatonin signaling

Growth performance and feed efficiency

Understanding the mechanistic basis of melatonin-mediated photoperiodic responses provides a strong scientific rationale for precision lighting strategies in sustainable poultry production. Photoperiod is a dominant environmental cue shaping physiological plasticity, enabling birds to align growth, metabolism, immunity and behavior with day and night cycles (Fig. 4). Melatonin functions as a central endocrine integrator linking external photoperiod to internal metabolic regulation by modulating appetite control, energy partitioning, and growth-related hormonal pathways, particularly the growth hormone insulin-like growth factor (IGF) axis (Galan et al., 2025). Through synchronization of hypothalamic clock genes, melatonin optimizes feeding behavior, activity patterns, and sleep wake cycles, thereby enhancing growth performance and feed efficiency (Wang et al., 2021). Recent evidence further strengthens the biological justification for light-based interventions by identifying a microbiota epithelium melatonin axis that integrates environmental, microbial, and neuroendocrine signals. Previously (Liu et al., 2024), demonstrated that gut microbiota also participated to directly regulates host melatonin synthesis through MyD88-dependent signaling in intestinal epithelial cells. Disruption of microbial composition or epithelial MyD88 signaling markedly reduced intestinal and systemic melatonin levels, underscoring the importance of microbial cues in circadian and metabolic homeostasis (Liu et al., 2024). From a production perspective, optimized lighting programs particularly intermittent or strategically reduced photoperiods promote synchronized feeding patterns, improved nutrient utilization, and reduced maintenance energy expenditure, collectively enhancing feed conversion ratio (FCR) (Takeshima, 2018). In contrast, continuous or excessively long photoperiods can desynchronize circadian rhythms, elevate oxidative stress, and impair metabolic efficiency (Ayo et al., 2018). Melatonin counteracts these effects by regulating glucose and lipid metabolism, improving mitochondrial function, and limiting oxidative damage, thereby supporting efficient growth when lighting regimens are biologically aligned.

Experimental studies across poultry species provide compelling justification for photoperiod manipulation as a non-invasive management tool. Extended light exposure suppresses pineal melatonin expression and disrupts circadian rhythmicity in laying ducks, leading to compromised gut morphology, tight junction integrity, and microbial balance (Cui et al., 2022). In broiler chickens, photoperiod-induced changes in circulating melatonin reshape cecal microbiota toward beneficial taxa, improve muscle fiber morphology, and enhance meat quality (Yu et al., 2025). Moreover, specific light spectra differentially modulate melatonin, corticosterone, and thyroid hormones, influencing stress physiology, metabolic activity, and growth efficiency (Galan et al., 2025). Reproductive physiology and egg quality

Physiological plasticity driven by photoperiod and melatonin signaling is a critical determinant of reproductive function and productive traits in avian species, particularly laying hens. Photoperiodic modulation affects gonadotropin release, ovarian follicular development, and ovulation timing. Extended or seasonally optimized light exposure enhances follicular recruitment and growth through increased secretion of LH and FSH, whereas disruptions in light dark cycles can alter ovarian morphology and reproductive hormone profiles, collectively influencing laying onset and persistency (Wang et al., 2019). On the other hand, melatonin, a pineal derived indoleamine secreted predominantly during periods of darkness, functions as an internal photoperiodic signal that directly affects reproductive physiology via receptors in the ovary and HPG axis (Bao et al., 2023). Exogenous melatonin administration in laying hens elevates serum estradiol levels while downregulating gonadotropin-inhibitory hormone receptor (GnIHR) expression in ovarian tissues, thereby promoting follicular maturation and extending egg-laying rates beyond peak production periods (Jia et al., 2016; Zulfiqar et al., 2025). Furthermore, melatonin interacts with intracellular pathways, including mTOR signaling, and exerts antioxidative effects that mitigate oxidative stress within the ovary, preserve follicle viability, and maintain a larger pool of developing follicles (Hao et al., 2020).

Environmental light manipulation and melatonin supplementation have demonstrable effects on egg production and quality. Optimized photoperiods improve feed efficiency and laying performance, while light wavelength modulates circadian gene expression (BMAL1), linking photic cues to reproductive rhythmicity (Geng et al., 2022a; Wang et al., 2015). Melatonin implantation in post-peak hens enhances both egg-laying rates and shell quality, likely through improved calcium metabolism and hormonal balance (Liu et al., 2025b). Combined strategies integrating melatonin and photoperiod management improve behavioral and hematological parameters, enhance egg quality, and reduce incidences of malformed eggs, particularly in commercial layers (Ozioma et al., 2024). These findings underscore the role of photoperiodic and neuroendocrine regulation in conferring reproductive plasticity, providing practical insights for optimizing laying performance and egg phenotypes in poultry production.

Immune competence and inflammatory regulation

Seasonal and circadian fluctuations in melatonin secretion critically influence immune cell proliferation, cytokine production, and broader immune regulatory networks, driving dynamic changes in host defense and inflammatory status over time (Zhang et al., 2026). Photoperiod-induced variations in melatonin signaling modulate the expression of immune-associated genes and melatonin receptors in peripheral immune tissues, directly linking environmental light cues to immune regulation (Tiwari et al., 2023). Beyond its classical role in circadian rhythm control, melatonin exerts potent immunomodulatory effects by interacting with MT1/MT2 receptors on leukocytes, enhancing innate and adaptive immunity (Černyšiov et al., 2015). It stimulates natural killer (NK) cell activity, shifts T helper cell balance toward Th1 responses, and regulates cytokine secretion, including IL-2, IL-6, IL-12, and interferon-γ (Lavado-Fernández et al., 2026). Mechanistically, melatonin functions as an “immunological buffer,” augmenting suppressed immune responses during stress or immunosuppression while mitigating excessive inflammatory activation. Its effects involve receptor-mediated signaling, modulation of intracellular pathways, and crosstalk with neuroendocrine factors, collectively maintaining immune homeostasis.

Experimental evidence highlights that light spectrum and photoperiod significantly influence melatonin synthesis and immune function in poultry. Shorter wavelengths light enhance nocturnal melatonin secretion in the pineal gland, which, in turn, improves immune competence and inflammatory balance (Horodincu and Solcan, 2023). Photoperiodic changes regulate melatonin receptor expression and immune-related gene profiles in migratory redheaded buntings, with longer day lengths promoting receptor upregulation and favorable immune gene expression (Tiwari et al., 2023). In chickens, melatonin signaling through Mel1a and Mel1c receptors synergistically stimulates bursal B-lymphocyte proliferation under specific monochromatic light conditions (Zhang et al., 2020). Blue-green LED lighting at species-specific illuminance enhances both immune performance and skeletal development in layers (Wei et al., 2022). Moreover, manipulating light programs and administering melatonin in broilers challenged with lipopolysaccharide markedly reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine levels while modulating humoral and cellular immunity, underscoring melatonin’s protective role against inflammatory stress (Gehad et al., 2008). These findings collectively emphasize photoperiod and melatonin as key modulators of avian immune plasticity, with implications for optimizing poultry health and productivity as summarize in Table 1.

Oxidative status and antioxidant defense

Oxidative status in poultry reflects the dynamic balance between the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the efficiency of endogenous and exogenous antioxidant defense systems (Arain et al., 2025). Under intensive production conditions, birds are frequently exposed to environmental, nutritional, and metabolic stressors such as heat stress, high stocking density, mycotoxins, and rapid growth rates that exacerbate mitochondrial ROS production and disrupt redox homeostasis (Li et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2025). Photoperiod-driven melatonin signaling is a key regulator of oxidative status and antioxidant defense in poultry, underpinning physiological plasticity in response to fluctuating environmental conditions. Unlike conventional antioxidants, melatonin exhibits pleiotropic redox activity by directly scavenging a wide range of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (ROS/RNS), including hydroxyl radicals, singlet oxygen, and peroxynitrite (Tan et al., 2007). Importantly, its metabolism yields several bioactive derivatives that sustain an antioxidant cascade, thereby extending protection beyond its primary molecular action. This multifaceted mechanism affords prolonged cellular and mitochondrial defense against oxidative damage, which is particularly critical in poultry due to their high metabolic rate and oxygen consumption (Zhou et al., 2024). Clarifying these distinctive antioxidant properties strengthens the mechanistic basis for positioning melatonin as a central mediator of redox homeostasis rather than a passive free-radical quencher.

In addition to its direct antioxidant capacity, melatonin indirectly reinforces endogenous defense systems by upregulating key antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase, catalase, and glutathione peroxidase, while maintaining intracellular glutathione balance (Tan et al., 2015). These effects are highly relevant under commercial production conditions, where birds are frequently exposed to oxidative challenges associated with intensive growth, high stocking density, and environmental stressors (Arain et al., 2018; Nabi et al., 2020). Empirical evidence indicates that melatonin supplementation or optimized photoperiod regimens reduce lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation, and DNA damage in both broilers and layers, particularly during thermal and photic stress (Bai et al., 2023; Kadim and Alhamdani, 2023). Disrupted lighting schedules suppress nocturnal melatonin secretion and exacerbate oxidative burden, whereas heat stress amplifies ROS generation and inflammatory signaling (Arain et al., 2022b; Shaukat et al., 2025). By stabilizing redox balance, modulating stress-responsive pathways, and attenuating inflammation, melatonin confers systemic protection that translates into improved welfare and productivity (Afzal, 2024). Thus, strategic photoperiod management emerges as a scientifically grounded, non-pharmacological approach to enhance oxidative resilience and adaptive capacity in poultry production systems Table 1.

Photoperiod manipulation strategies in poultry production

Photoperiod manipulation represents a fundamental management tool in modern poultry production because light functions not only as a visual stimulus but also as a powerful environmental regulator of circadian organization, endocrine signaling, growth, reproduction, and welfare. Adjustments in light duration, intensity, and spectral composition directly modulate pineal melatonin secretion, thereby influencing feed intake, metabolic efficiency, immune responsiveness, oxidative balance, and reproductive function (Galan et al., 2025). Accordingly, lighting programs have progressively evolved from simple continuous illumination toward precision-based strategies that aim to synchronize biological rhythms with production demands. This transition is justified by growing evidence that biologically aligned lighting improves physiological stability and long-term performance, rather than merely accelerating short-term growth.

Conventional continuous lighting regimes (20–23 h light) have historically been adopted to maximize feed consumption and early growth in broilers; however, extended photoperiods disrupt circadian rhythmicity, suppress melatonin release, and are associated with compromised immunity, skeletal disorders, and elevated stress (Hieke et al., 2019). In contrast, intermittent lighting programs introduce structured dark phases that allow expression of natural rest activity cycles, leading to improved feed efficiency, bone strength, cardiovascular function, and antioxidant capacity without adversely affecting final body weight, particularly beyond the starter phase (Jiang et al., 2023). The justification for intermittent programs is further strengthened by their ability to restore melatonin rhythmicity, which plays a central role in immune modulation and oxidative stress resilience (Geng et al., 2022a). More recently, LED-based spectral modulation has enabled precise control of light wavelength, allowing targeted physiological outcomes (Lee et al., 2025), blue and green spectra support growth and calm behavior in broilers (Franco et al., 2022), whereas red light preferentially stimulates reproductive performance in layers and breeders (Gongruttananun and Guntapa, 2012). Integrating intermittent photoperiods with LED spectral control thus represents a scientifically grounded shift toward precision lighting systems that optimize productivity while enhancing welfare and physiological robustness in poultry production systems.

Emerging concepts and future directions

Chronobiology has emerged as a compelling framework for refining poultry production by harmonizing management practices with endogenous biological rhythms. Rather than relying on rigid, uniform photoperiods, future production systems are increasingly oriented toward biologically informed lighting strategies that align feeding, vaccination, and thermal management with circadian phases. Such synchronization is biologically justified, as circadian alignment supports metabolic efficiency, immune competence, and stress adaptability (Arain et al., 2022a). Empirical evidence indicates that circadian-aware management improves feed conversion, attenuates inflammatory responses, and enhances tolerance to environmental challenges, particularly heat stress a growing concern under climate change (Hassan et al., 2025; Zulfiqar et al., 2026). Integrating chronobiological principles into routine husbandry therefore represents a rational and welfare-conscious approach to sustaining productivity while reducing physiological strain on birds (Olanrewaju et al., 2019).

Technological advances in LED lighting, sensor networks, and artificial intelligence have accelerated the transition toward precision lighting systems capable of real-time modulation of light intensity, duration, and spectral composition. These innovations are further strengthened by emerging insights from genomics and nutrigenomics, which reveal genetic variation in photoreceptor sensitivity, circadian clock genes (e.g., CLOCK, BMAL1, PER), and metabolic pathways underlying differential responses to light programs (Arsalan et al., 2025). Coupling precision lighting with genomic selection and targeted nutritional interventions provides a biologically sound basis for genotype-specific management aimed at optimizing growth, reproduction, and immune function (Arain et al., 2024). However, important knowledge gaps persist, including limited understanding of long-term and multigenerational effects, molecular crosstalk among light spectra, epigenetic regulation, and nutrient signaling. Addressing these gaps through integrative, systems-level research and large-scale field validation is essential to justify the translation of chronobiology-driven strategies into economically viable and welfare-oriented poultry production systems (Moss et al., 2023).

Conclusions

Photoperiod serves as a pivotal environmental regulator in poultry by transmitting light-dark information to the pineal gland and shaping biological timing through rhythmic melatonin secretion. This pineal-derived melatonin signal coordinates multiple endocrine pathways, including the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis, hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, and hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis, thereby regulating growth, reproductive performance, immune function, metabolic homeostasis, and adaptive responses to environmental stressors. Growing evidence indicates that melatonin signaling extends beyond temporal synchronization to promote physiological plasticity, allowing birds to adapt efficiently to dynamic environmental and management conditions. Conversely, inappropriate or irregular photoperiods can disrupt internal circadian organization, leading to endocrine dysregulation, impaired immunity, metabolic disturbances, and reduced welfare. From a production standpoint, deciphering photoperiod-driven neuroendocrine mechanisms provides a biologically sound basis for optimizing poultry management. Strategically designed lighting programs that align with intrinsic circadian biology can enhance feed utilization, reproductive performance, and overall health while attenuating stress and inflammatory challenges. Notably, photoperiod manipulation is a non-invasive, economically viable, and sustainable tool that bridges productivity with animal welfare.
