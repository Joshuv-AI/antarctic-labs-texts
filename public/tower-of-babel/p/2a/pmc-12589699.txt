Role of exosomes in cancer: from diagnosis to therapy
Authors: Mojgan Esparvarinha, Hamid Nickho, Mohsen Dashti, Majid Ebrahimi Warkiani, Mehdi Yousefi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractExosomes are small extracellular vesicles produced by most cell types. They carry specific markers and biomolecular cargo that reflect the characteristics of cancer cells. These vesicles contain oncoproteins, mutated DNA fragments, and cancer-specific RNA profiles, which can serve as biomarkers for tumor detection, disease progression, and treatment resistance analysis. As a non-invasive diagnostic tool, exosome biomarkers can be obtained from liquid biopsies, including blood, urine, and saliva samples. The cargo of exosomes, such as miRNAs and proteins, is often associated with patient prognosis, providing insights into tumor aggressiveness and survival rates, which helps in patient risk assessment. Due to their natural biocompatibility and minimal immune response, exosomes are promising drug delivery vehicles for anticancer treatments, including chemotherapeutic agents and RNA-based therapies. This review highlights the dual role of exosomes in cancer, focusing on their diagnostic and therapeutic potential.Keywords: Exosomes, Cancer, Diagnostic biomarkers, Therapeutic delivery, Tumor-derived exosomes, Dendritic cell-derived exosomes

Introduction

Over the years, major efforts and significant financial investments have focused on developing advanced cancer treatments, such as chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and molecularly targeted therapies [1–3]. Despite major advancements in treatment regimens, cancer continues to be the world’s second leading cause of mortality [3, 4]. While genetic mutations are considered the primary cause of cancer, many other complex factors also contribute to its onset and progression [2]. Understanding these complexities has led to the adoption of the Complex adaptive therapeutic strategy (CATS) model, which describes tumors as dynamic and adaptable rather than static abnormalities [5]. This perspective has paved the way for developing new therapeutic approaches. Additionally, the human immune system, which also follows CATS principles, can activate sophisticated defense mechanisms against malignancies [6]. Recently, exosomes—specialized extracellular vehicles (EVs)—have emerged as a promising tool to address the limitations of conventional cancer therapies due to their unique biological properties [7]. Recently, exosomes—specialized extracellular vehicles (EVs)—have emerged as a promising tool to address the limitations of conventional cancer therapies due to their unique biological properties Compared to cell-based therapies such as CAR-T cells, exosomes offer several key advantages, including higher biocompatibility, easier storage and handling, and the ability to cross biological barriers more effectively. These features make exosomes a safer and more versatile platform for cancer treatment [8]. Exosomes are a type of EV measuring between 30 and 150 nanometers, surrounded by a lipid bilayer membrane, and formed within the endosomal compartments of eukaryotic cells [9, 10]. Initially identified by Eberhard Trams in 1981 in the supernatant of sheep reticulocytes, the term exosome was later introduced by Johnstone in 1987 [10]. These vesicles are ubiquitous across biological fluids, including tears, saliva, sweat, blood, breast milk, urine, ascitic fluid, and cerebrospinal fluid [7, 10–12]. Exosomes have multiple functions, including aiding in coagulation, removing cellular waste, and facilitating communication between cells [10, 13]. Exosomes, derived from parent cells, retain key characteristics that make them valuable for precise diagnostic purposes [14]. These vesicles are advantageous due to their minimal side effects, excellent in vivo compatibility, and ability to integrate into the tumor microenvironment (TME), thereby influencing the surrounding tissue [15]. One of the main causes of cancer-related death is metastasis, which is the spread of cancer cells from the primary tumor to other organs [2]. Exosomes play a crucial role in multiple stages of metastasis, including the formation of pre-metastatic niches and the modulation of cellular properties. Research indicates that exosomes can condition target cells to support tumor progression and prime distant organs for the arrival of circulating cancer cells by facilitating intercellular communication. This process influences tumor cell behavior and enhances their survival. Moreover, exosomes contribute to organotropism in metastasis, guiding cancer cells toward specific target organs [16–19]. Despite the considerable therapeutic potential of exosomes in cancer treatment, several challenges still exist. Current isolation techniques, such as ultracentrifugation, exhibit limitations in fully isolating exosomes from other EVs. Furthermore, the lack of standardized detection techniques undermines the reproducibility and reliability of experimental outcomes. Nevertheless, exosomes continue to offer significant promise for advancing cancer diagnostics and therapies, while also enhancing our understanding of metastasis mechanisms. Although numerous studies have investigated the role of exosomes in cancer biology, much of the existing literature is fragmented—focusing either solely on exosomal biomarkers or isolated therapeutic uses. There is a lack of integrated perspectives that collectively examine the diagnostic, therapeutic, and translational relevance of exosomes across cancer types. This review aims to bridge this gap by providing a comprehensive and organized summary of exosome biology, their role in cancer progression and immune modulation, recent developments in exosome-based diagnostics, and their potential as therapeutic agents. By highlighting both opportunities and existing challenges, this review offers an up-to-date, consolidated resource for researchers and clinicians working in the field of precision oncology. This review examines the role of exosomes in cancer, emphasizing their diagnostic and therapeutic potential. For better understanding the role of exosomes in cancer, we first dive into the biology of exosome and its role in TME, then we will review its therapeutic potentials, and finally their applications in diagnosis of various cancers.

Discovery and biology of exosomes

In a late endosome, exosomes are created via inward budding, which produces a multivesicular body (MVB) [20]. The intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) develop inside the MVB as a result of this inward budding process. The ILVs are released as exosomes into the extracellular space when the MVB merges with the plasma membrane [21]. These vesicles are enriched with various biomolecules derived from their parent cells, including proteins (such as cytoskeletal components, cytokines, ribosomal proteins, adhesion molecules, metabolic enzymes, and growth factors) and lipids (including lipid rafts, cholesterol, and ceramides). Additionally, exosomes carry nucleic acids, including DNA, mRNA, and miRNA [9]. Exosomes often contain various proteins crucial for membrane trafficking processes, such as RAB GTPases, annexins, and flotillins. They are also abundant in components involved in MVB formation, including ALIX and tumor susceptibility gene 101 (TSG101). Moreover, heat shock proteins (HSP60 and HSP90), as well as tetraspanins (CD9, CD63, and CD81) are frequently present [10, 22]. These molecular constituents that are from their parent cells such as proteins and RNA can serve as potential biomarkers [13, 20]. While the protein composition of exosomes varies depending on the originating cell and tissue type, they consistently share a core set of conserved proteins [23]. The specific molecular cargo of exosomes reflects their parent cells and can differ based on the cell type. Characterizing this diversity is valuable for monitoring diseases like cancer [24]. Exosomes facilitate intercellular communication by transferring biomolecules via vesicle-mediated transport, impacting immune cells such as dendritic cells (DCs) and B lymphocytes, and potentially contributing to the adaptive immune response against tumors and pathogens [25, 26]. The unique properties of EVs have positioned them as promising candidates for clinical applications, particularly as biomarkers and therapeutic tools [27].

Role in cancer progression and metastasis

The TME consists of a diverse array of immune cells, including myeloid-derived cells such as myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), DCs, and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), as well as lymphocytes, both T and B cells [28]. Exosomes facilitate tumor cells, immune cells, and stromal cells exchange bioactive chemicals in this dynamic milieu, which helps cancer cells develop immunological tolerance and avoid immune surveillance. In contrast, immune cell-derived exosomes can exert anti-tumor effects by inhibiting tumor proliferation, dissemination, and metastasis. Thus, exosomes play a dual role in cancer immunity, functioning as either immune suppressors or enhancers depending on their cellular origin and context [10]. Tumor-derived exosomes (TEXs) carry cellular components that can modulate immune responses, including danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) such as mRNA, membrane-bound proteins (e.g., CD9, CD63, and CD81), heat shock proteins, MHC class I molecules, and tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) [29]. These properties enable TEXs to initiate antitumor responses and modulate the tumor microenvironment by recruiting and reprogramming immune cells. Seminal research has highlighted the pivotal role of exosomes in intercellular communication [30]. In 1996, Raposo and colleagues demonstrated that immune cells, including B lymphocytes, secrete antigen-presenting vesicles capable of directly activating CD4 + T cells [10, 31]. Over a decade later, in 2007, Valadi and colleagues further established that exosomes mediate the exchange of genetic material through RNA transfer, unveiling a fundamental mechanism of intercellular signaling [15]. These discoveries significantly enhanced scientific understanding of exosome composition and function, redirecting research toward their potential diagnostic and therapeutic applications [32]. Conversely, programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1), which inhibits CD8 + T lymphocytes’ anticancer activity in vivo, has been discovered to be present in exosomes generated from melanoma cells. TEXs also hinder dendritic cell (DC) maturation in a PD-L1-dependent manner, thereby impairing T-cell-mediated immune responses. Moreover, exosomes derived from tumor cells and mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) play a crucial role in immune regulation, influencing both pro- and anti-tumor responses. While these vesicles are known to promote tumor growth and metastasis, they also contribute to mechanisms that enable cancer cells to evade treatment (Fig. 1) [33, 34]. Due to their unique biophysical characteristics and diverse molecular cargo, exosomes are increasingly recognized as valuable biomarkers for disease diagnosis. Furthermore, their ability to deliver proteins or nucleic acids to target cells presents an exciting opportunity for developing precision cancer therapies with reduced off-target toxicity compared to conventional chemotherapy.

Tumor-derived exosomes (TEXs) modulate the immune microenvironment through multiple mechanisms that promote tumor progression. A TEXs suppress antitumor immunity by inhibiting Th1/Th17 polarization, impairing CTL and NK cell cytotoxicity, and limiting activation of dendritic cells (DCs) and macrophages. B They also expand immunosuppressive populations, including Tregs, Bregs, CD8⁺ regulatory T cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and M2-like macrophages. TEX-activated macrophages display a distinct cytokine profile—reduced TIMP1, IFN-γ, and IL-16, with elevated IL-8, CCL2, MIP2, and IL-1Ra—associated with tumor invasion and metastasis [47, 124]. Additionally, both tumor- and pathogen-derived exosomes promote Treg expansion, induce T cell apoptosis via Fas/FasL signaling, and stimulate IL-10–producing B cells, contributing to immune suppression and tumor immune evasion [47]

Therapeutic applications

In 2013, Chen and Mellman conceptualized the cancer-immunity cycle, describing a series of interconnected steps required for an effective antitumor immune response, beginning with the release of tumor antigens [35]. Following cancer cell death, tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) or neoantigens are released into the surrounding environment, where they are recognized by antigen-presenting cells (APCs), including DCs and macrophages. These immune cells internalize, process, and present the antigens to naive T cells, triggering an adaptive immune response. For effective T cell priming, additional co-stimulatory signals are needed to enhance the immunogenicity of tumor antigens within APCs. Mature DCs then migrate to lymph nodes, where they present the processed antigens via MHC-I and/or MHC-II molecules, thereby activating naive T cells into effector T cells capable of targeting tumor cells. The effectiveness of this antigen presentation step significantly impacts the balance between effector T cells and regulatory T cells (Tregs), ultimately influencing the strength and specificity of the antitumor immune response. Effector T cells differentiate into cytotoxic T cells, which migrate to tumor sites and recognize malignant cells through interactions between their T cell receptors (TCRs) and tumor antigens presented on MHC-I molecules. Upon recognition, cytotoxic T cells initiate the destruction of cancer cells, leading to the release of TAAs and further stimulation of immune responses. This process reinforces the cancer immune cycle, sustaining and amplifying antitumor immunity [35]. However, tumors have evolved various mechanisms to evade immune surveillance at different stages of this cycle, enabling them to resist immune-mediated elimination [36]. As a result, immunotherapies targeting single steps of this process often face limitations in efficacy. In response to this challenge, scientists are investigating approaches that target several stages of the immune cycle concurrently, aiming to improve therapeutic efficacy. Emerging evidence suggests that exosomes play a role in activating several phases of the cancer immune cycle (Fig. 2). For example, exosomes can serve as cell-free therapeutic vaccines, triggering strong antitumor immune responses [37]. Additionally, exosomes contribute to a tumor-suppressive microenvironment by facilitating the proper function of bioactive molecules, thereby exerting significant inhibitory effects on tumor progression [38]. Given these characteristics, exosome-based therapies are gaining attention as a promising avenue for cancer immunotherapy.

Engineered exosomes play a crucial role in enhancing the cancer immunity cycle, a multistep process crucial for effective anti-tumor immunity. This cycle involves: (1) the release of cancer antigens from dying tumor cells, (2) their presentation by antigen-presenting cells (APCs), (3) the activation and priming of T cells in lymph nodes, (4) the migration of T cells to the tumor microenvironment, (5) the infiltration of T cells into tumor tissues, (6) the recognition of cancer cells by T cells, and (7) the elimination of cancer cells by T cells. Exosomes, particularly engineered ones, have been shown to influence and enhance various stages of this cycle, highlighting their therapeutic potential. Modified tumor cell-derived exosomes (TEXs)

Tumor cells release a significantly higher amount of exosomes into circulation and bodily fluids than normal cells [39]. These TEXs interact with surrounding cells and distant tissues, transporting tumor-specific antigens that reflect their cell of origin. Consequently, TEXs have emerged as a promising antigenic source for cancer vaccine development (Fig. 2). The immunogenic potential of TEXs was first reported in 2001, highlighting their ability to stimulate tumor-specific TCRs [40]. TEXs serve as carriers of TAAs which DCs can internalize and process, ultimately triggering potent anticancer immune responses. Studies indicate that TEXs generate stronger vaccine-induced immunity compared to tumor cell lysates [41]. Furthermore, Innate immune cells, including macrophages and DCs, can be affected directly by TEXs, by shifting them toward a proinflammatory state. In tumor-draining lymph nodes, TEX exposure promotes the release of proinflammatory cytokines, including interferon-gamma (IFN-γ, interleukin-6 (IL-6), and IL-12, while concurrently suppressing the production of anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10 [42].

Cancer vaccines often rely on tumor neoantigens and adjuvants to enhance antigen processing and presentation. In this regard, TEXs have emerged as a promising platform for cancer vaccine development due to their dual role as a source of tumor antigens and natural immune adjuvants [43]. However, despite their potential to elicit strong immune responses, the clinical application of TEXs is complicated by their tumorigenic properties, as they retain many characteristics of their parent cancer cells.

TEXs play a very diverse role in the progression of cancer, depending on the type of cancer, its genomic profile, and the stage of the disease. For instance, exosomes derived from pancreatic cancer cells have been shown to induce neoplastic transformations in NIH/3T3 cells, suggesting a potential role in tumorigenesis [43]. Similarly, TEXs secreted by hypoxic glioblastoma (GBM) cells promote pro-angiogenic activities in endothelial cells, facilitating vascular development within the brain TME [44]. Additionally, TEXs originating from gastric cancer cells expressing epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) have been found to interact with Kupffer cells and hepatic stellate cells, contributing to the establishment of a pro-metastatic niche in the liver by activating hepatic growth factor signaling pathways [45].

Dendritic cell-derived exosomes (DEXs) as tumor vaccines

Studies conducted by Zitvogel, Raposo, and Amigorena have demonstrated that DCs release exosomes enriched with functional MHC class I and II molecules, along with co-stimulatory proteins. These DEXs effectively prime cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), thereby facilitating an anti-tumor immune response in a T-cell-dependent manner. Given their ability to induce tumor-specific immunity, DEXs have garnered considerable attention as cell-free tumor vaccines, representing a promising avenue for cancer immunotherapy [46, 47]. The mechanisms underlying the anti-tumor effects of DEXs have been extensively explored. These exosomes contain a high concentration of membrane proteins critical for antigen presentation, including MHC class I and II molecules, MHC class I-like molecules (CD1), co-stimulatory proteins (CD80 and CD86), and adhesion molecules (MFG-E8 and ICAM-1) [48–51]. Proteomic analyses of DEXs have further identified novel exosomal proteins, predominantly cytoskeleton-associated molecules (e.g., cofilin and profilin I) and membrane trafficking/signaling factors (e.g., annexins, Rab7, Rab11, Rap1B, and syntenin), suggesting a pivotal role for exosomes in membrane trafficking and immune modulation [48]. Heat shock protein 73 (HSP73) has been identified within the endocytic compartments of DCs, where it may contribute to antitumor immunity through both T-cell-dependent and T-cell-independent mechanisms [52].

Moreover, DEXs play a pivotal role in immune modulation by facilitating antigen presentation and immune cell activation. Upon in vivo administration, DEXs can stimulate T cells directly by presenting peptide–MHC complexes or indirectly via antigen transfer to APCs (cross-dressing) or exosome uptake followed by antigen processing (Fig. 3) [52]. A key aspect of this process is the activation of MHC class I-restricted, peptide-specific CTLs, which is essential for effective antitumor immunity [53]. However, DEXs exhibit limited efficiency in stimulating naïve CD4⁺ T cells compared to activated or memory T cells, necessitating the presence of DCs for optimal T cell activation. Additionally, B cells serve an auxiliary role in CTL priming, as their absence results in diminished immune responses due to impaired complement activation and inefficient antigen transport [54, 55]. In vitro studies indicate that DEXs alone are insufficient to induce antigen-specific T cell activation unless incubated with mature DCs. These mature DCs enhance adaptive immunity by transferring functional peptide–MHC complexes to neighboring DCs, thereby amplifying antigen presentation and immune responses.

Beyond their role in T cell modulation, DEXs also promote NK cell activation and proliferation, leading to NK cell-mediated tumor rejection [56–58]. Studies have identified several membrane-associated stimulatory molecules within DEXs. These exosomes express functional IL-15R, which facilitates NK cell expansion and IFN-γ secretion. Additionally, DEXs carry multiple TNF superfamily ligands (TNFSFLs) on their surface, interacting with TNF receptors on NK cells and further promoting their activation [56]. The engagement of activating receptors on NK cells is essential for their cytotoxic function against tumor cells. Notably, DEXs have been shown to express ligands such as BAT3 (HLA-B-associated transcription 3) and NKG2D, both of which contribute to direct NK cell activation [57, 58]. Given these findings, DEXs have been explored as a promising cell-free vaccine to stimulate antitumor immune responses and inhibit tumor progression. Clinical trials in patients with metastatic melanoma and advanced non-small cell lung cancer have demonstrated that DEX-based vaccination enhances immune activation and contributes to disease stabilization [59, 60]. However, overcoming immunosuppressive barriers and restoring adequate T cell responses remain critical challenges that require further investigation to optimize the therapeutic potential of DEXs as cancer vaccines.

Diagnostic and prognostic applications

Analyzing exosomal contents provides more precise and comprehensive insights into specific cancer biomarkers compared to conventional tumor biomarker assays. Tracking TEXs offers a non-invasive, efficient, and dynamic approach for cancer diagnosis, disease monitoring, and evaluating treatment efficacy [10].

Exosomal genetic cargo in cancer diagnosis

Nucleic acids, including mRNA and miRNA, serve as promising biomarkers for cancer diagnosis and treatment monitoring. Tumor-derived exosomal mRNAs, such as PD-L1 in plasma and cadherin 3 (CDH3) in urine, correlate with disease status and therapy response. Notably, elevated human telomerase reverse transcriptase (hTERT) in pancreatic and lung cancer exosomes, as well as increased dynamin 3 (DNM3) and P65 in glioblastoma, underscore their diagnostic potential [61–64]. MiRNAs, the most abundant RNAs in exosomes, are protected from degradation, making them stable and reliable biomarkers. Their selective packaging and higher extracellular concentrations than intracellular miRNAs further enhance their role in tumor detection and disease monitoring [11, 65]. Beyond miRNAs, long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) and circular RNAs (circRNAs) in exosomes demonstrate high stability and specificity, making them valuable biomarkers. For instance, exosomal lncRNAs such as HOTTP in gastric cancer [66] and lncRNA-p21 in prostate cancer [67] exhibit superior diagnostic accuracy compared to traditional markers. Additionally, lncRNA PCAT-1 and MALAT1 in urine exosomes are linked to bladder cancer recurrence [68], while lncRNA-ATB in hepatocellular carcinoma is associated with poor survival outcomes [69]. Similarly, circRNAs such as hsa-circ-0004771 in colorectal cancer and circRNA PDE8A in pancreatic cancer have demonstrated potential for early detection and disease monitoring [70]. Moreover, exosomal DNA, including genomic and mitochondrial DNA, reflects tumor-specific mutations and offers potential for genetic diagnostics [71]. However, some studies suggest that extracellular DNA secretion might occur independently of exosomes.

Exosomal proteins as cancer biomarkers

Advancements in proteomic technologies have significantly enhanced the identification of exosomal proteins and their clinical relevance in cancer prediction, diagnosis, and progression. Studies indicate that exosomal protein expression profiles differ across various cancer types, providing valuable diagnostic and prognostic insights (Table 1). For instance, glypican-1 (GPC1)-positive exosomes are markedly elevated in pancreatic cancer patients, demonstrating high sensitivity and specificity for early and late-stage diagnosis [72]. Similarly, exosomes isolated from the serum of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients exhibit overexpression of key oncogenic proteins, such as NY-ESO-1, EGFR, and epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM), all of which correlate with poor prognosis [73]. In breast cancer, phosphorylated exosomal proteins, including RALGAPA2 and PKG1, have emerged as potential diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers [74]. Additionally, the extracellular matrix protein developmental endothelial locus-1 (Del-1) distinguishes malignant tumors from benign conditions, offering a valuable tool for differential diagnosis [75]. Furthermore, polymerase I and transcript release factor (PTRF) levels in serum-derived exosomes from glioma patients have been linked to tumor grade, making it a promising candidate for both diagnostic and therapeutic applications [76]. Beyond serum analysis, proteomic profiling of salivary and serum exosomes in lung cancer patients has revealed differentially expressed proteins, highlighting their potential as non-invasive biomarkers for early detection and disease monitoring [77].

Exosomal lipid biomarkers and metabolites

Exosomal lipids play a crucial role in maintaining vesicle integrity while also acting as key signaling molecules in tumorigenesis and metastasis. Emerging evidence suggests that exosomal membranes facilitate extracellular communication by transferring “mobile rafts,” which modulate cellular pathways—an effect largely regulated by ceramide [78]. High-throughput lipidomics has identified distinct lipid profiles in exosomes derived from prostate cancer patients, underscoring their potential as diagnostic biomarkers. Similarly, metabolomic analyses of exosomes from prostate and pancreatic cancer patients have revealed lipid imbalances and metabolic alterations, further supporting their role in cancer detection and disease progression monitoring [79, 80]. With advancements in analytical technologies, the integration of exosomal lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids is emerging as a powerful approach to enhance cancer diagnosis and prognosis. The simultaneous analysis of multiple exosomal biomarkers—including metabolites, RNAs, and proteins—can significantly improve diagnostic accuracy and sensitivity [34]. Notably, Cho et al. developed a multiplexed in situ detection method capable of quantitatively assessing disease-specific miRNAs and surface proteins in exosomes derived from prostate cancer cells. This innovative approach highlights the potential of exosome-based diagnostics for more precise cancer detection and monitoring [81]. Integrating exosomal biomarkers with conventional diagnostic approaches may enhance sensitivity and specificity, reducing reliance on invasive tissue biopsies. based diagnostic platforms that offer rapid, noninvasive, and highly sensitive cancer detection. These biosensors—ranging from electrochemical and optical sensors to aptamer-based and surface plasmon resonance (SPR) devices—are capable of accurately identifying exosomal biomarkers such as miRNAs, mutant EGFR, and PD-L1 with remarkable sensitivity, even at attomolar levels. Such sensor-based exosome profiling enhances diagnostic precision, overcomes limitations of traditional biopsies, and supports early detection and monitoring of tumor heterogeneity. These innovations are key components of the emerging precision oncology landscape [82].

Future directions and challenges

Despite the significant potential of exosome-based applications in cancer diagnosis and therapy, several challenges must be addressed before their widespread clinical implementation [83]. One of the primary obstacles is the inefficiency of current isolation and large-scale production techniques, which limit the reproducibility and feasibility of exosome-based therapies. Additionally, the lack of standardized purification protocols poses a challenge in ensuring consistency across clinical applications, potentially affecting the reliability of exosome-derived biomarkers and therapeutics [84, 85]. One of the key challenges in the clinical application of exosome-based therapies is their inherent heterogeneity. Exosomes are highly variable in size, content, and biological activity, depending on multiple factors such as the type and state of the parental cell, physiological or pathological conditions, and even the method of isolation and purification. This diversity affects the consistency and reproducibility of exosome-based diagnostics and therapeutics. For instance, tumor-derived exosomes (TEXs) carry distinct oncogenic cargoes that can differ not only between cancer types but also within subpopulations of the same tumor. Additionally, variations in isolation techniques (e.g., ultracentrifugation vs. microfluidics) may yield exosome populations with different profiles. Overcoming this heterogeneity—through standardized protocols, improved characterization techniques, and single-exosome analysis—remains essential for advancing the clinical translation of exosome-based interventions [86]. Another critical concern is the potential immunogenicity and rapid clearance of exosomes by the immune system, which may compromise their therapeutic efficacy. Moreover, achieving precise targeted drug delivery using exosomes remains a challenge, as factors such as uptake efficiency, biodistribution, and stability must be optimized to ensure consistent therapeutic outcomes [87, 88]. Addressing these limitations requires advancements in bioengineering, nanotechnology, and scalable manufacturing strategies to enhance exosome stability, targeting efficiency, and clinical applicability [83]. Continued research in these areas will be essential to unlock the full potential of exosome-based precision medicine, paving the way for their integration into routine clinical practice. Despite these challenges, recent advances highlight the translational progress of exosome-based platforms in both diagnostics and therapeutics. Exosome-based technologies have moved beyond theoretical promise and are increasingly being applied in clinical and laboratory settings. The ExoDx Prostate IntelliScore (EPI), a non-invasive urine-based assay, has received FDA approval and demonstrates over 90% specificity in identifying patients who can safely avoid unnecessary prostate biopsies. Similarly, Guardant360 CDx, another FDA-approved platform, integrates exosomal and circulating free DNA biomarkers to detect actionable mutations in non-small cell lung cancer, thereby informing treatment selection [89]. Beyond these approved platforms, multiple exosome-based diagnostic assays—particularly in lung, breast, and prostate cancers—are under evaluation in observational and early-phase clinical studies (e.g., NCT03542253 [90], NCT04529915 [91], NCT01840306 [92], NCT03031418 [93]). On the therapeutic front, dendritic cell-derived exosomes (Dex) have advanced into Phase I and II clinical trials as cell-free cancer vaccines (e.g., NCT01159288) [94]. Mesenchymal stromal cell-derived exosomes (MSC-Exo) are primarily investigated in preclinical models for drug delivery, but both MSC-Exo and Dex are also undergoing early clinical evaluation for drug delivery and immunotherapy (e.g., NCT03608631, NCT04276987) [95, 96]. Looking ahead, progress in exosome engineering, scalable isolation methods, and targeted delivery strategies—together with integration into nanotechnology and AI-assisted multi-omics profiling—will be critical to fully realize their potential in precision oncology and to advance toward more personalized and effective cancer therapies.

Conclusion

Traditional cancer treatments, including surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, often face limitations such as severe side effects, drug resistance, metastasis, and relapse. In recent years, immunotherapy has emerged as a promising alternative, with exosomes playing a pivotal role in tumor progression and immune modulation. These extracellular vesicles carry bioactive molecules—such as proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids—that mediate intercellular communication and influence cancer dynamics in both beneficial and detrimental ways. Tumor-derived exosomes contribute to cancer progression by facilitating immune evasion, promoting angiogenesis, and enhancing metastasis. In contrast, exosomes derived from immune cells or engineered with therapeutic agents can stimulate antitumor immune responses and inhibit tumor growth, making them attractive candidates for drug delivery and cancer immunotherapy. Exosomes also hold potential as non-invasive biomarkers in liquid biopsy methods, offering the possibility of detecting cancer at early stages and allowing for continuous monitoring of disease progression. Ongoing research and clinical trials will be vital in optimizing exosome-based approaches, leading to more personalized and effective cancer therapies. Future advancements in exosome engineering, scalable isolation techniques, and targeted delivery strategies will be critical for unlocking their full potential in precision oncology.
