Implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) reduce mortality in patients at risk of sudden cardiac death.1–3 Transvenous lead-related complications4–6 have prompted the development of systems utilizing leads outside the vasculature such as the subcutaneous ICD.7 However, the subcutaneous defibrillator has significant limitations including large size, lack of anti-tachycardia pacing (ATP) delivery, and shorter battery longevity.8,9 To address these limitations, the extravascular implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (EV ICD) was developed (projected battery longevity: 11.7 years, size: 33 cm3).10,11 We previously reported on the sensing performance of the system12 and the initial experiences of the first group of 21 patients through 3 months of follow-up.10,13 This report extends follow-up for 14 patients that continue to be followed 3 years post-implant.

The EV ICD is a system designed to utilize the substernal space for lead implantation (Graphical Abstract). The EV ICD pilot study was a prospective, non-randomized study conducted at 4 centres in Australia and New Zealand.10 The study was approved by the regulatory authorities and/or the ethics committees of the participating centres and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki.

All patients included had a Class I or IIa indication for an ICD based on current ESC and/or AHA/ACC/HRS clinical practice guidelines, major exclusions were prior sternotomy, sternal deformity, and the inability to undergo defibrillation testing. Patients were evaluated post-implantation at 2 weeks, 4–6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, and every 6 months thereafter. At follow-up, devices were assessed for system performance and any spontaneous arrhythmic events recorded were reviewed.

Patient chest X-rays were evaluated for distal lead position at pre-hospital discharge, 4–6 weeks, 3 months post-implant, and at a long-term time point between 24 and 36 months.

Descriptive statistics were used for demographics/medical history and summary of most endpoints. All analyses were completed using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Among 26 patients enrolled in the pilot study, 21 underwent attempted implantation and testing of the EV ICD system, with 17 patients discharged with the implanted system (Graphical Abstract). To date, these patients account for 523 patient-months of follow-up with the EV ICD system, and 14 patients continue to be followed beyond 3 years. Lead movement issues did not account for the four failed implants and for only a single elective explant. Patient baseline characteristics were previously reported.10 Although it was not a pre-specified objective, chronic chest X-rays were available for 10 patients and thus included in the chronic lead motion analysis. Most patients (n = 7/10) had stable lead positioning from the pre-hospital discharge to 3 months with two patients experiencing minimal lead movement, and one patient experiencing larger lead movement (Table 1). From 3 months to 36 months, seven patients had stable lead positioning. Lead motion in two patients, as defined by arc length change, was minimal (Patient 5 with 8.2 mm and Patient 9 with 8.8 mm); however, the general distal lead electrode position over the cardiac silhouette was stable in these two patients. A single patient experienced notable lead movement (41.9 mm; Patient 7), although the chest X-ray images were of poor quality. In this patient, pacing capture was not achieved after 6 months, while R-wave amplitude began increasing by the 3-month measurement and remained markedly increased thereafter.

Chronic defibrillation threshold testing was not required as part of the study protocol, but seven subjects did undergo repeat testing 3.2–19.5 months post-implant at the discretion of the treating physician. Chronic defibrillation testing was successful for all patients (defibrillation success ≤ 40 J). At implant, in the most commonly tested vector, the average pacing capture threshold was 5.1 ± 2.0 V [n = 17, median = 6.0, interquartile range (IQR) = 4–6]; this average was 3.8 ± 2.3 V at 3 years (n = 6, median = 2.8, IQR = 2.5–5) among subjects achieving capture. R-wave amplitudes were 3.4 ± 2.0 mV at implant (n = 20, median = 3.1, IQR = 2–5), and 4.2 ± 2.7 mV at 3 years (n = 13, median = 3, IQR = 2.2–6.4). Pacing was perceptible during threshold testing, but this did not prevent programming of ATP.

As previously reported, there was one patient with an inappropriate shock at 78 days post-implantation due to P-wave oversensing subsequent to lead tip deflection towards the right atrial appendage.10 Through 3 years, there were two additional inappropriate shock events due to electromagnetic interference from direct physical contact with ungrounded equipment. One patient received an inappropriate shock due to electromagnetic interference from 50 Hz current in a commercial hot tub. In this case, the device algorithm correctly interpreted the noise as electromagnetic interference but delivered a shock due to prolonged interference and a programmed time-out. A second patient received an inappropriate shock due to performing plumbing on ungrounded equipment. With the addition of the inappropriate shock reported previously due to P-wave oversensing,10 the annualized rate of inappropriate shock over 3 years is 6.9%. At 310 days post-implant, there was one elective device removal due to frequent VT and intolerance to ATP related to high pacing outputs.

After 36 months of follow-up, pacing, sensing, and defibrillation performance of the EV ICD system remained stable in all patients where data was available. Through 3 years, three patients underwent system removal with light traction being sufficient to remove the entire lead without the need for additional extraction tools. Of the remaining 14 patients, 7/10 demonstrated stable lead positioning or minimal lead movement from 3 months to 3 years. This report demonstrates stable performance of the system at 3 years post-implant. As previously reported, there was one patient with early lead tip deflection resulting in inappropriate therapy and elective system explant. Beyond 3 months, there were three cases of notable lead movement, however, none of these resulted in clinically significant changes to device performance. There were two inappropriate shocks in long-term follow-up related to environmental noise artefacts, which are known hazards for all ICDs. Additionally, one patient experienced three appropriate shocks to terminate spontaneous monomorphic VT episodes. No other patients experience a ventricular arrhythmia requiring treatment.

Based on R-wave amplitudes and pacing capture thresholds in patients with data available, we conclude that the EV ICD electrical performance was generally stable through 3 years. The larger EV ICD Pivotal Study is currently ongoing, and results from that will further inform long-term performance.
