Gabapentsal ameliorate cisplatin-induced vomiting in pigeons: neurochemical evidences and the involvement of serotonin and dopamine modulation
Authors: Mushtaq Ahmad, Ihsan Ullah, Gowhar Ali, Mushtaq Ahmad Mir, Nasreena Bashir, Saeed Anwar, Syed Uzair Ali Shah, Nisar Ahmad, Muhammad Ayaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cisplatin is used for treatment of various carcinomas in clinics but is associated with the adverse effects of nausea and vomiting. We investigated the antiemetic effect of gabapentsal; a gabapentinoid derivative against cisplatin (10 mg/kg) induced vomiting in pigeons and the neurochemical mechanisms centrally and peripherally and the involvement of dopaminergic receptors. Established video recording setup was used for behavioral experiments while High Performance Liquid Chromatography system coupled with electrochemical detector (HPLC-ECD) was used for the quantification of neurotransmitters 5-hydroxytryptamine (5HT) and its metabolite; 5-hydroxy indole acetic acid (5-HIAA), dopamine (DA) and its metabolites; dihydroxy phenyl acetic acid (DOPAC), homovanillic acid (HVA) and noradrenaline (NA) centrally in brain stem while, peripherally in small intestine. Cisplatin (10 mg/kg i.v.) induced emesis without lethality up to the observation period (3 h.). Gabapentsal attenuated cisplatin induced emesis ~ 77.22%; the standard drug, gabapentin (GB; 100 mg/kg), produced ~ 57.55% reduction. Gabapentsal suppressed (p < 0.05 - p < 0.01) the concentration of 5HT and 5-HIAA in the brain stem and intestine. In continuation, a decline (P < 0.05 - p < 0.01) in the concentration of DA and its metabolite DOPAC in the brain stem while DA concentration was decreased (p < 0.05) in the intestine only. The standard gabapentin (100 mg/kg) decreased (p < 0.05) the concentration of 5HT and NA in the intestine only. Furthermore; gabapentsal attenuated (p < 0.001) quinpirole (3 mg/kg) induced vomiting but no effect on neurotransmitters and their metabolites was noted. In conclusion, gabapentsal has antiemetic activity against cisplatin and quinpirole induced vomiting mediated by serotonergic and dopaminergic components centrally and peripherally.

Introduction

Chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting (CINV) are one of the main adverse events taking place in patients undergoing cancer chemotherapy, especially the high emetogenic class of anti-neoplastics including cisplatin1,2. These adverse events are so distressing that results in refusal of curative treatment and significantly reduces the quality of life of the patients3. Nausea and vomiting are mediated through complex mechanisms involving both peripheral and central pathways. In the gastrointestinal tract (GIT), cisplatin damages the walls of the stomach and intestines initially by triggering the oxidative stress; resulting in the release of serotonin (5HT). The 5HT stimulates the 5HT3 receptors on vagal afferents and the message is communicated to the brain stem vomiting circuitry4.

Centrally; cisplatin is mediating its emetogenic effects via its action on area postrema (AP), which is responsible for detecting toxins in the blood. The AP which is anatomically connected with the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) and dorsal motor nucleus of vagus nerve (DMVN) collectively initiate the vomiting reflex5. Cisplatin-induced vomiting has two phases; acute phase (within 24 h) which is triggered by 5HT release and delayed phase (after 24 h) which is mainly driven by substance P and also by other mediators in the brain6.

Different chemical mediators are involved in cisplatin-induced vomiting in the brain. During the acute phase, in the gut enterochromaffin cells cisplatin cause the release of 5HT which subsequently cause the activation of 5HT3 receptors on vagus nerves and the message is communicated to the AP and NTS, both of which regulate vomiting7. Some other pathways, like dopamine (D2) and histamine (H1), also contribute to nausea and vomiting. While in the delayed phase, the substance P plays the main role by activating neurokinin-1 (NK1) receptors. Gabapentinoids block the voltage-gated calcium channel, which result in the reduction of the release of excitatory neurotransmitters8. They also have an effect on GABA activity, which helps calm the overactive signals of the brain. This makes the gabapentinoids very useful for reducing both acute and late-stage vomiting caused by cisplatin.

The standard antiemetic therapies, including 5HT3 receptor blockers and NK1 receptor antagonists along with dexamethasone are quite effective in the management of vomiting induced by chemotherapy, but still the patients vomit in clinics9. The gabapentinoids; gabapentin and pregabalin have been studied for their neurochemical modulation properties, particularly in reducing neurotransmitter release through the inhibition of calcium channels10. Gabapentsal is a novel derivative of gabapentin. It is chemically modified to enhance its therapeutic actions. The chemical structure of gabapentsal includes a primary cyclohexane ring, providing the core backbone and a salicylamide functional group, contributing to its anti-inflammatory and analgesic potential. The previous Studies on gabapentsal have explored the pharmacological properties of gabapentsal especially in neuropathic pain management where it is found effective by modulating the calcium channels and also reducing the nerve hyperexcitability11.

This study is designed to evaluate the antiemetic effect of gabapentsal against cisplatin induced vomiting in pigeon vomit model. Furthermore, the study is coupled with investigations to probe into its probable mechanism by quantification of relevant neurotransmitters and its metabolites involved in the act of vomiting centrally and peripherally.

Materials and methods

Drugs and chemicals

Cisplatin was purchased from Pharmedic Laboratories, Pakistan, while Quinpirole and Dimethyl Sulfoxide (DMSO) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich USA. Gabapentin was procured from Hilton Pharma, and Tween 80 was purchased from Scharlau Chemicals, Spain. Normal saline was from Otsuka. High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC)-grade reagents, including acetonitrile, methanol, 1-octane sulphonic acid sodium salt (Fisher Scientific, U.K.), sodium dihydrogen orthophosphate, and ethylene diamine tetra acetic acid (EDTA), were purchased from Merck’s local distributor in Peshawar, Pakistan. NA, DOPAC, DA, 5-HIAA, HVA, and 5HT were obtained from Acros Organics, Belgium.

Animals

Mixed breed pigeons (both male and female) in weight range of 250–350 g were bred and kept at the pigeon breeding facility at the Department of Pharmacy, University of Peshawar, Pakistan. The animals were housed in groups of six (n = 6) with a 24-hour acclimatization period in standard laboratory conditions having temperature range of 22–25 °C and light dark cycle as per nature. The animals were having free access to standard food (millet + wheat grain in a ratio of 1:1) and water. The experimental procedures performed on the protected animals were according to the Animal Scientific Procedure Act (1986) and were subsequently approved by the ethical committee, Department of Pharmacy, University of Swabi (No. PH22SEP2022-A053).

Drugs preparation

Cisplatin was dissolved in acidified saline (154 mM NaCl adjusted to pH 4.0 with 0.1 M HCl). To prevent degradation and to ensure stability the solution was prepared fresh before each experiment. Gabapentsal was dissolved in normal saline containing dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 5%) and tween-80 (1%). Gabapentin and quinpirole were dissolved in normal saline.

Grouping, randomization and drug administration

The standard grouping protocol was adopted consisting positive control, negative control, standard and treatment groups. Latin square experimental design was followed for complete randomization of the experimental animals and blind treatment protocol. Cisplatin (10 mg/kg) and quinpirole (3 mg/kg) was administered using the non-pyrogenic syringes with sharp needles having gauge 23 into the brachial wing vein. Warm water was used to make the wing vein dilate and enhance its visibility while spirit was used as disinfectant. Standard gabapentin (100 mg/kg), Haloperidol (1 mg/kg) and test compound gabapentsal (50, 75 and 100 mg/kg) were administered intramuscularly 30 min before the emetic challenge; in to the chest muscle using syringes with painless needles of 21 gage.

Anti-emetic assay

On the day of experiment, the pigeons were shifted to separate cages, particularly made for behavioral recording/observation. The video recording setup was used to record the pigeon’s behavior for up to 3 h. Water and food were accessible throughout the observation time and each pigeon was used once. The response of emesis with oral expulsion or without oral expulsion was counted like one emesis episode; one emetic episode may comprise of 2 to 80 jerks (emetic behavior)12,13. The onset of vomiting was also recorded and noted. At the end of the experiment; the pigeons were euthanized and were properly disposed-off, as per the animal scientific procedure Act, (United Kingdom) 1986.

Tissue sampling for neurotransmitter analysis

At the end of each experiment, animals were decapitated and the brain stem and intestinal samples 5–6 cm from the pylorus were rapidly collected and placed on a cold ice plate (0 °C). The dissection of brain parts was carried out according to the atlas of Karten and Hodos14 and Henri M. Duvernoy15. The collected brain tissue samples were first cleared of vessels and meninges, while fecal matter and mesenteries were carefully removed from intestinal samples, were weighed and stored at − 80 °C until analysis.

Determination of neurotransmitters and their metabolites

Tissue samples were homogenized in cold 0.2% perchloric acid (PCA) at 5000 rpm with the help of Teflon glass homogenizer (Wise stir HS 30 E). After centrifugation (Centurion UK) at 12,000 g/min (4 °C) and filtered through a 0.45-micron filter. Neurotransmitters and their metabolites were analyzed using High Performance Liquid Chromatography system (HPLC, Shimadzu, Japan) coupled with Electrochemical Detection (ECD, ESA Coulochem III model 5300), a pump (model LC-20AT), and an analytical column (Teknokroma 3 × 150, 3 μm). Electrodes 1 and 2 of the analytical cell were set at + 200 and – 200mV respectively, with a sensitivity of 2 mA, while the guard cell (model 5020) potential was set at 500mV. The mobile phase consisted of 94 mM sodium dihydrogen orthophosphate, 40 mM Citric acid, 2.3 mM sodium 1-octane sulphonic acid, 50 μm EDTA, and 10% acetonitrile (pH 3). The flow rate was maintained at 0.6 mL/min. The limit of detection was 11 pg for each of the neurotransmitter and their metabolites except for HVA which was 19 pg. The standards used NA, DOPAC, DA, 5-HIAA, HVA and 5HT16.

Statistical analysis

Differences in emetic episodes’ data were analysed using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by post hoc analysis (Tukey’s multiple comparison test). Differences in the latency to the first emetic episode were analyzed by a Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparison tests (GraphPad Prism version 5.0, Inc. Version, California, USA). When an animal failed to exhibit retching and/or vomiting, a latency value equal to the test period observation time (i.e. 03 h) was used to perform the statistical analysis. Data are expressed as the mean ± SEM, excepting latency values, which are shown as medians. In all cases, difference between treatment groups was considered significant when p < 0.05.

Results

Effect of gabapentin and gabapentsal on cisplatin-induced vomiting and jerking

Cisplatin (10 mg/kg) induced a reliable vomiting response in all the animals tested with a mean vomiting response of 16.1 ± 1.55 and 77.8 ± 8.105 jerking episodes (Fig. 1A and B). Gabapentsal (50, 75 and 100 mg/kg) attenuated the vomiting response and jerking episodes up to the observation period (3 h.) significantly (P < 0.001, Fig. 1A and B, Figs. 2 and 3) in all the animals tested in a dose dependent manner (Fig. 1D). Moreover, complete suppression of vomiting nor jerking episodes was observed in any animal. The standard gabapentin (100 mg/kg) also ameliorated the vomiting and jerking episodes in 3 h. of observation period (P < 0.001, Fig. 1A and B, Figs. 2 and 3). Gabapentsal all the doses ameliorated the vomiting behavior at every time point of the observation period; where at every time point gabapentsal suppressed the vomiting response except the initial time slot of 30 min where no vomiting behavior was noted (Fig. 2A, P < 0.001). In continuation, the same effect on the reduction of jerking behavior was observed too (Fig. 3A, P < 0.001). The standard gabapentin provided ~ 57% protection as compared to cisplatin control, while the highest protection observed by the gabapentsal treatment was 77.22% (100 mg/kg).

Effect of gabapentin and gabapentsal on cisplatin induced latency to first vomit

Cisplatin induced the vomiting response in all the animals with mean latency of 76.33 ± 2.51. The standard gabapentin (100 mg/kg) and gabapentsal (50, 75 and 100 mg/kg) increased the latency to first vomit significantly (P < 0.001, Fig. 1C).

Effect of gabapentin and gabapentsal on quinpirole-induced vomiting and jerking

Quinpirole (3 mg/kg) induced the vomiting and jerking behavior in all the animals tested with a mean response of 6.33 ± 0.71 and 11.67 ± 1.3, respectively (Fig. 4A and B). All the animals showed the vomiting behavior in response to D2 receptor agonist-quinpirole. Gabapentsal attenuated the vomiting response at the dose of 75 mg/kg (P < 0.05, Fig. 4A) and 100 mg/kg (P < 0.001, Fig. 4A) and the antiemetic effect was dose dependent (Fig. 4D). In continuation, gabapentsal (100 mg/kg) reduced the jerking behavior (P < 0.001, Fig. 4B) and resulted in complete suppression of vomiting and jerking in one animal. The standard haloperidol (1 mg/kg) ameliorated the vomiting and jerking (P < 0.001, Fig. 4A and B). Furthermore, haloperidol (1 mg/kg) suppressed the vomiting and jerking behavior in two animals completely.

Effect of gabapentin and gabapentsal on quinpirole induced latency to first vomit

Quinpirole (3 mg/kg) induced the vomiting and jerking behavior with a latency of 4.5 ± 0.76 min. None of the dose of gabapentsal not the standard haloperidol effected the latency to first vomit any significantly (Fig. 4C).

Effect of gabapentin and gabapentsal on neurotransmitters after cisplatin challenge

The Cisplatin administration resulted in the upsurge of DA (P < 0.01), 5HT (P < 0.05) and its metabolite-5-HIAA (P < 0.05) while a non-significant increase in the concentrations of DOPAC, HVA and NA were observed (Table 3). The treatment gabapentsal (75 and 100 mg/kg) significantly reduced DA (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01 respectively), DOPAC (100 mg/kg, P < 0.05) 5HT (100 mg/kg, P < 0.05) and 5-HIAA (100 mg/kg, P < 0.05); while no reduction was observed in HVA and NA by any of the dose (Table 3). Standard gabapentin (100 mg/kg) does not alter any of the neurotransmitters and their metabolites at the level of the brain stem (Table 3).

Cisplatin (10 mg/kg) increased the concentration of DA (P < 0.05), 5HT and its metabolite-5-HIAA with a significance of (P < 0.001) and (P < 0.05) respectively in the intestine (Table 4). Standard gabapentin (100 mg/kg) attenuated the upsurge of 5HT (P < 0.05) and NA (P < 0.05) while a decreasing trend was observed in DA, DOPAC, HVA and 5-HIAA and the difference was statistically non-significant (Table 4). The treatment gabapentsal is found effective at the doses of 75 mg (5HT, P < 0.05) and 100 mg (DA, P < 0.05); 5HT, P < 0.01 and 5-HIAA, P < 0.05) (Table 4).

Discussion

Cisplatin which belongs to highly emetogenic class of anti-neoplastic agents is used for eradication of various types of carcinomas in clinics; but is associated with the painful side effects of nausea and biphasic vomiting in humans17,18. The current study reports the antiemetic activity of gabapentenoid derivative (gabapentsal) and in parallel the investigations of potential mechanisms in the mediation of antiemetic effect. This study is based on our preliminary findings on screening of gabapentenoid derivatives (gabapentsal and pregsal) against various emetogenics and found effective in reducing cisplatin, ipecac and CuSo4 induced emesis in pigeons19. The finding encouraged us to screen it further for the possible mechanisms behind in the mediation of antiemetic effect. Our finding provides evidences that gabapentsal is effective in reducing vomiting, jerking and modulate the levels of neurotransmitters altered by cisplatin and quinpirole in the pigeon vomit model.

Pigeon is a vomit competent specie and is acceptable for preliminary screening of antiemetic drug candidates. The pigeon model has a reliable emetic behavior in response to multiple emetogenics especially cisplatin. Cisplatin was used at dose of 10 mg/kg based on the study form our laboratory, where the 7 mg/kg dose induced a robust and reliable vomiting response in all the animals and increase in the dose of cisplatin resulted in the increase in vomiting response up to 10 mg/kg with no mortality up to the observation period13,20. The dose of 10 mg/kg was selected for the induction of vomiting and jerking in pigeons as our observation duration was 3 h. as compared to previous studies in our lab; where our observation period was 24 h. The D2 receptor agonist-quinpirole was also used to probe the probable involvement of dopamine receptors in the mediation of antiemetic effect of gabapentsal.

Gabapentsal, which is a derivative of gabapentin11, has proved its effectiveness as antiemetic against cisplatin (10 mg/kg) and quinpirole (3 mg/kg). In both the cases the antiemetic effect was dose dependent (Figs. 1D and 2D). The antiemetic effect observed with the gabapentsal (all the doses) was consistent and significant (P < 0.001) throughout the observation period (3 h.) when analyzed for 30 minutes’ patches (Figs. 2A and 3A), except for first 30 minutes’ interval where no emesis and jerking was noted as the cisplatin induced vomiting with a latency of 76.33 ± 2.51. The delayed onset of vomiting by cisplatin is attributed to the release of oxidative species, resulting in injury to enterochromaffin cells in the GIT and the subsequent release of serotonin21. Alpha is a subunit of calcium ion channel and gabapentin interacts with this alpha subunit. It inhibits the calcium ion signaling. Moreover, gabapentin and pregabalin have inhibitory effects on the release of neuropeptide “Substances P” as well10. Within this context, gabapentin derivatives are hypothesized to have antiemetic effects for its interaction with the calcium ion channel which may result in inhibition of neuropeptides and neurotransmitters responsible for the mediation of CINV.

Pathophysiology associated with CINV is understood in two ways. One is the central pathway and other is the peripheral. Central pathway consists of a chemoreceptor trigger zone (CTZ)/AP). The peripheral pathway involved the vagal nerve and its innervations in the gastrointestinal system5. It is for the mentioned reasons we investigated the impact on selected neurotransmitters and their metabolites centrally in the brain stem and peripherally in the intestine. Neurotransmitters causing nausea and vomiting are DA and 5HT. The 5HT is of prime consideration for the genesis of cisplatin induced acute phase of vomiting22 and this study provide evidences for the reduction of 5HT and its metabolite-5-HIAA in the brain stem (Table 3, Turnover Ratio: 0.62) triggered by cisplatin and the same effect was observed at the level of intestine as well (Table 4, Turnover ratio 0.45). In continuation; cisplatin caused the upsurge of DA at the level of brain stem (Turnover ratio 0.65) and intestine (Turnover ratio 0.97); while gabapentsal attenuated that upsurge of DA caused by cisplatin (Tables 3 and 4) with turnover ratios of 0.66 and 0.86 respectively. Furthermore, gabapentsal didn’t effected the basal neurotransmitter levels and their metabolites centrally and peripherally (Tables 1 and 2).

The antiemetics like anticholinergics, antihistaminic and DA receptor antagonists are found beneficial against CINV when used in combination or even alone23. It was a major step in the clinical management of CIV when 5HT3 receptor blockers were introduced, however they are ineffective in reducing delayed phase24, signifying the involvement of various other mechanisms such as 5-hydroxytryptamine-4 receptors (5HT4)25, neuropeptide “Substance P”6, the D2 and the D3 receptors26, in the pathogenesis of vomiting and nausea. Quinpirole (D2 receptor agonist) induced reliable and identifiable vomiting and jerking response in pigeons and our test compound-gabapentsal didn’t showed any significant effect to alter the neurotransmitters and their metabolites (Tables 5 and 6); but the reduction in vomiting and jerking behavior was noted in the treatment groups, advocating the effectiveness as antiemetic with a no effect on latency to first vomit. This probably explains the involvement of D2 receptors in the mediation of antiemetic activity. Furthermore, NA concentration was decreased in comparison to cisplatin control which will have an impact on the stress being mediated by cisplatin via noradrenergic transmission centrally and peripherally.

Conclusion

The current investigations advocate the antiemetic effect of gabapentsal against cisplatin induced vomiting in pigeon vomit model. Furthermore, the key neurotransmitters; 5HT, DA and their metabolites are proved to be involved in the emesis reflex and are modulated by gabapentsal centrally and peripherally. Moreover, gabapentsal attenuated the quinpirole induced vomiting providing evidence for the involvement of D2 receptors in the mediation of antiemetic effect by gabapentsal. These findings warrant further investigations in other vomit competent species regarding its antiemetic effect and its mechanisms in detail.
