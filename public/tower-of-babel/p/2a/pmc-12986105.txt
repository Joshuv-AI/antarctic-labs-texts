SOCS1 Mimetic Peptide Enhances Empagliflozin Improvement on Kidney Damage in the Type 2 Diabetes Mouse Model BTBR ob/ob
Authors: Marcelo Aguilar-Cartes, Lucas Opazo-Ríos, Alejandra Droguett, Sebastian Mas-Fontao, Juan Antonio Moreno, Carmen Gómez-Guerrero, Jesús Egido, Sergio Mezzano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diabetic nephropathy (DN) is the leading cause of end-stage renal disease worldwide. During the last few years, remarkable advances have been made in the treatment of DN. Sodium–glucose cotransporter type 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) consistently prevent or delay albuminuria and renal failure in patients with DN. Prior research from our group highlights the Janus kinase/signal transducers and activators of transcription axis as a critical target in DN. Specifically, the administration of suppression of cytokine signaling 1 (SOCS1) mimetic peptides (MiS1) modulates aberrant signaling, resulting in profound beneficial effects on renal function and structural integrity in experimental DN. The aim of this study was to evaluate the effect of empagliflozin and MiS1 on kidney damage and its associated inflammatory, oxidative stress and lipotoxic mechanisms in an advanced type 2 DN mouse model BTBR ob/ob. Mice were treated for 7 weeks with empagliflozin and MiS1, alone or in combination, and monitored for glycemia, body weight, albuminuria, histopathological damage, podocyte loss, and gene expression related to inflammation, redox balance, and lipid metabolism. Empagliflozin or MiS1 monotherapies significantly reduced albuminuria and structural renal injury, preserved podocyte number, and downregulated genes involved in inflammatory, oxidative, and mitochondrial–lipid metabolic dysregulation, with empagliflozin additionally improving metabolic parameters. Notably, the combined therapy achieved the greatest reduction in albuminuria and histological damage with enhanced suppression of pathogenic inflammatory and metabolic pathways, resulting in superior renoprotection compared with monotherapy. These findings suggested that add-on therapy with SOCS1 peptidomimetics and SGLT2i may help mitigate residual albuminuria and renal damage in type 2 DN.

1. Introduction

Diabetic nephropathy (DN), the most common cause of end-stage renal disease worldwide, affects one-third of individuals with type 1 diabetes mellitus and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2D) [1]. Hyperglycemia activates several intracellular pathways in the kidney, including protein kinase C, polyol and hexosamine flux, advanced glycation end-products, and Janus kinase/signal transducers and activators of transcription (JAK/STAT), leading to metabolic stress, inflammation, oxidative stress, and lipotoxicity (i.e., ectopic lipid accumulation and altered lipid metabolism in renal cells), a phenomenon known as meta-inflammation [2,3].

Sodium–glucose cotransporter type 2 (SGLT2) is a protein selectively expressed in the kidney and is responsible for the reabsorption of filtered glucose. Given that increased proximal tubular SGLT2 activity enhances sodium–glucose reabsorption and promotes glomerular hyperfiltration in the early stages of chronic kidney disease (CKD), with or without diabetes, targeting this transporter represents a valuable therapeutic strategy [4,5].

The sodium–glucose cotransporter type 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) are the standard and first-line therapy in DN patients due to their cardioprotective and nephroprotective effects [4]. Clinical trials and preclinical studies demonstrate pleiotropic effects of the SGLT2i beyond glucose and body weight control, including a reduction in albuminuria and glomerular hyperfiltration, as well as the modulation of oxidative stress and inflammatory mediators [6]. However, residual albuminuria persists in a subset of patients despite SGLT2 inhibition [7], suggesting that additional pathogenic mechanisms, including inflammatory and metabolic stress pathways, may contribute to disease progression [8].

Extensive evidence demonstrates overactivation of intracellular signaling pathways and the release of multiple inflammatory mediators in the progression of DN [8,9,10]. Transcription factors, such as nuclear factor κ-B (NF-κB) and STAT3, as well as cytokines (e.g., interleukin 17, IL-17) and chemokines (e.g., monocyte chemotactic protein 1, MCP-1 or CCL2), have been proposed as novel therapeutic targets, with ongoing randomized clinical trials in DN [11,12].

In this sense, growing evidence demonstrates the vasculoprotective and nephroprotective effects of JAK/STAT inhibition in diabetic complications [13,14,15]. The Suppressors of Cytokine Signaling (SOCS) proteins are a family of intracellular proteins that finely regulate the inflammatory response, mainly as endogenous regulators of the JAK/STAT pathway and ubiquitin/proteasome system [16]. SOCS1 and SOCS3 are of particular interest because they contain a kinase inhibitory region that interferes with JAK activity. Peptide mimetics of the SOCS1 protein (MiS1) have shown significant protective effects, improving structural and functional damage through anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-lipotoxic actions in preclinical models [17,18,19].

Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the kidney impact of empagliflozin, SGLT2i, in combination with MiS1, through histopathological damage assessment, evolution of the albuminuria-creatinuria ratio, and molecular changes in related genes, including inflammation, oxidative stress, and lipid accumulation, as well as de novo lipogenesis in the BTBR ob/ob mice, a preclinical model of type 2 DN.

2. Results

2.1. Empagliflozin, Alone and in Combination with MiS1, Improves Metabolic Parameters and Albuminuria in BTBR ob/ob Mice

BTBR (Black Tan and Brachyuric) ob/ob (leptin-deficient mutation) mice exhibited marked obesity, sustained hyperglycemia, and progressive albuminuria compared with BTBR WT littermates. Body weight gain was consistent with leptin-deficient metabolic phenotypes of this model. No mortality was recorded during the experimental period.

Longitudinal analysis revealed a significant group × time interaction (p < 0.001) for body weight, glycemia, and albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR). From week 8 to week 22, diabetic BTBR ob/ob mice showed a progressive increase in body weight and glucose levels. Mice treated with MiS1 did not show significant changes in body weight compared to the BTBR ob/ob control group. Interestingly, empagliflozin, alone or in combination with MiS1, significantly reduced body weight gain from week 16 onwards and improved glycemic control compared to the BTBR ob/ob control group (Figure 1A,B).

Albuminuria, assessed as ACR, increased from week 4 in BTBR ob/ob mice, indicating progressive renal dysfunction. The three proposed therapeutic strategies reduced ACR; however, the combined therapy achieved the greatest decrease between weeks 16 and 22 compared to the BTBR ob/ob control group (90% reduction vs. 70% with empagliflozin and 25% with MiS1 alone; Figure 1C).

2.2. Empaglifozin Monotherapy and Combined Therapy Improve Renal Damage in BTBR ob/ob Mice

At the glomerular level, BTBR ob/ob mice showed significant mesangial matrix expansion compared with the WT group (Figure 2A,B). Treatment with empagliflozin, MiS1, and their combination reduced mesangial matrix expansion (53%, 25%, and 75%, respectively) compared to the BTBR ob/ob group (Figure 2C–E,K).

At the tubulointerstitial level, BTBR ob/ob mice exhibited loss of brush border, flattening of the proximal tubular epithelium, tubular lumen enlargement, and focal inflammatory infiltrates (Figure 2F,G). These alterations were partially improved by empagliflozin and MiS1 monotherapies, and more markedly reduced by the combined treatment (54%, 25%, and 78% reduction in tubular injury score, respectively) (Figure 2H–J,L). Additionally, the combined therapy reduced the total histopathological score by 78% (Figure 2M).

BTBR ob/ob mice also displayed a reduced number of positive podocytes per glomerulus of Wilms tumor-1 (WT-1) compared with wild type mice (Figure 3A,B). Treatments significantly increased the podocyte numbers, with this most evident change in the combined therapy group (Figure 3C–F).

2.3. Empagliflozin and MiS1 Modulate Inflammatory Signaling Pathways in BTBR ob/ob Mice

Renal gene expression analysis revealed an increase in transcriptional expression of Stat1, Stat3, and p65 in BTBR ob/ob mice compared with WT controls. Treatment with empagliflozin, MiS1, and the combination of both resulted in a marked effect, with a greater outcome observed with the combined treatment, followed by empagliflozin and MiS1 (Figure 4A–C). Similarly, pro-inflammatory downstream genes, including the chemokine Ccl2, the inflammasome component Nrlp-3 (NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3), and the cytokine Il-17, were markedly increased in BTBR ob/ob mice and significantly downregulated by all treatments, with the greatest suppression achieved by the combined therapy (Figure 4D–F).

2.4. Empagliflozin, MiS1, and Their Combination Improve Redox Balance and Lipid Metabolism in BTBR ob/ob Mice

Renal oxidative stress results from an imbalance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) generating enzymes and antioxidant defenses. Compared to the BTBR WT group, the kidneys of BTBR ob/ob mice showed an increased gene expression of the pro-oxidant enzyme NADPH oxidase 4 (Nox-4) and decreased expression of the antioxidant enzymes as superoxide dismutase (Sod-1) and catalase (Cat). Administration of empagliflozin or MiS1 partially restored redox-related gene expression, whereas the combined therapy normalized Nox-4, Sod-1, and Cat levels toward WT values (Figure 5A–C).

Mitochondrial dysfunction and ectopic fatty acid deposition are key contributors to renal injury in DN, leading to dysfunctional cellular changes and lipotoxic damage. BTBR ob/ob mice displayed decreased renal mRNA expression of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ co-activator 1α (Pgc-1α), and upregulation of sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1 (Srebp-1) and adipose differentiation-related protein (Adrp), which were markers of altered energy metabolism, lipid accumulation, and de novo lipogenesis (Figure 5D–F). This pattern was reversed in treatment groups, with a greater effect observed in the combined therapy, followed by the empagliflozin and MiS1 monotherapies. The protein-protein interaction network analysis identifies a functional link between lipid metabolism, oxidative stress, and the JAK-STAT/NLRP3 axis. These results suggest that metabolic-redox imbalances drive the inflammatory response, with STAT3 and NLRP3 acting as key central mediators (Figure 5E).

3. Discussion

This study demonstrated that empagliflozin and MiS1 (an SOCS1 mimetic) were effective monotherapies for experimental type 2 DN, with longitudinal analysis confirming that they modified disease progression rather than providing isolated benefits. Notably, the dual therapy yielded superior results, suggesting a synergistic modulation of inflammatory pathways via SGLT2 inhibition and JAK/STAT regulation [20]. Given the global rise in T2D and obesity and the historical scarcity of effective nephroprotective targets, this combination could represent a significant advancement since the introduction of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers [21,22].

SGLT2i have revolutionized CKD treatment by demonstrating consistent nephroprotective and cardioprotective benefits across major clinical trials, being the first-line therapy in diabetic and non-diabetic CKD [23,24,25]. While their primary efficacy stemmed from hemodynamic regulation and the modulation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system [26,27], their anti-inflammatory reach has remained partially defined [28]. Our findings showed that SGLT2i only partially reduced inflammatory signaling; however, the dual therapy with MiS1 addressed this residual activity. Furthermore, beyond metabolic control (e.g., body weight, glycemia and arterial pressure) via natriuresis and glycosuria [7], SGLT2i may enhance energy metabolism by activating AMPK and restoring mitochondrial dynamics [29,30].

Evidence from different authors has demonstrated the importance of persistent hyperglycemia in the progression of renal damage, mainly through overactivation of intracellular signaling pathways related to inflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolic homeostasis, including NF-κB, JAK/STAT, AMPK, and ChREBP1/SREBP1 pathways [10]. Our research group has consistently demonstrated the contribution of JAK/STAT activation to disease progression, as well as the vasculo- and renoprotective effects of its pharmacological modulation in diabetes [31,32,33].

The BTBR ob/ob (leptin-deficient) mouse is a well-established model of type 2 DN because it develops morphological and histopathological lesions comparable to those observed in renal biopsies from DN patients [34,35]. In this model, SOCS1 mimetic-mediated modulation of JAK/STAT showed protective effects in early kidney damage [33].

SOCS1 is not solely a suppressor of JAK/STAT signaling. It can directly restrain innate immune pathways by promoting degradation of the TLR adaptor Mal/TIRAP and by limiting IRF7-dependent type I interferon production [36]. Moreover, SOCS1 can regulate nuclear NF-κB activity by controlling p65 stability. These mechanisms have positioned SOCS1 as a central checkpoint in inflammatory signaling, acting both upstream and downstream of cytokine receptors [16,36].

Empagliflozin and MiS1 enhanced WT-1-positive podocyte counts, preserving the glomerular filtration barrier and reducing albuminuria-induced injury [37,38]. While SGLT2i-mediated protection has been linked mainly to glycemic control, JAK/STAT modulation provides additional benefits, potentially by podocyte restoration through progenitor cell activation in parietal epithelial cells [33,38,39].

Studies have shown increased IL-17 and NLRP-3 in diabetic mice and podocyte cultures exposed to high glucose [40,41]. Empagliflozin and JAK/STAT pathway inhibitors (as nifuroxazide) can decrease TNF-α, IL-17, IL-18, and IL-23 expression in preclinical models [33,42,43]. These observations were consistent with our findings of reduced STAT1, STAT3, p65 NF-κB, and NLRP3 expression following empagliflozin and MiS1 treatment, particularly under the combined therapy.

Excessive ROS production is a central mechanism linking metabolic stress to renal inflammation and fibrosis, promoting lipid peroxidation, DNA damage, endoplasmic reticulum stress, cellular senescence, and mitochondrial dysfunction [44,45,46].

Increased activity of pro-oxidant enzymes such as NADPH oxidase subunits, together with reduced antioxidant enzymes, contributes to renal structural damage. In agreement with previous reports showing cytoprotective and antioxidant stress responses of SGLT2i [46,47,48], our results demonstrated that empagliflozin modulates redox-related genes (Nox4, Sod-1, and Cat) in the BTBR ob/ob model.

MiS1 exerted potent vasculo- and nephroprotective effects by downregulating key inflammatory mediators (STAT1/3, p65, CCL-2, NLRP-3, IL-17) and enhancing antioxidant defenses [31,33]. These benefits extended beyond the kidney, as MiS1 attenuated systemic inflammation and provided vascular protection, as seen in models of diabetic atherosclerosis and aortic aneurysm [18,32].

Given that metabolic stress and inflammation are deeply interconnected in type 2 DN, the superior outcomes of the dual therapy with empagliflozin could suggest a complementary renoprotective mechanism. By simultaneously improving metabolic control and modulating systemic redox pathways, this combination can provide a more consistent structural and functional improvement, though clinical validation remains necessary to confirm its therapeutic potential.

Limitations

While this study demonstrated the therapeutic potential of targeting inflammatory pathways alongside SGLT2 inhibition, several limitations exist. The BTBR ob/ob model may not fully replicate advanced human tubulointerstitial fibrosis, and the exclusive use of male mice could leave potential sex-specific responses unexplored. Additionally, the study lacked direct GFR measurements such as iohexol clearance or transcutaneous FITC-sinistrin assessment [49], and relied on gene expression without protein-level validation. Nevertheless, the consistent metabolic, structural, and molecular data strongly supported the enhanced nephroprotective effect of the combined therapy in experimental DN.

4. Materials and Methods

4.1. Experimental Model

Diabetic BTBR ob/ob mice (BTBR.Cg-Lepob/WiscJ; RRID: IMSR_JAX:004824) and their non-diabetic wild-type (WT) littermates were generated as described [34]. Breeding pairs of heterozygous BTBR mice (BTBR ob+/−) were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, Maine) and housed at a density of four animals per cage in a temperature-controlled room (20–22 °C) with a 12 h light/dark cycle. All animals had ad libitum access to food and water. The principles of laboratory animal care were followed, and euthanasia of the mice was performed with the administration of anesthesia, following the protocols approved by the Committee on the Ethics of Animal Experiments of Universidad Austral de Chile (Permit N° 245–2016; 25 February 2016) and conducted in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines and the National Institutes of Health standards for the care and use of laboratory animals.

4.2. Experimental Design and Treatment Protocol in BTBR ob/ob Mice

Male BTBR ob/ob mice at 15 weeks of age were only included in the study due to the more severe and reproducible renal phenotype reported in this strain [50]. More details about the strain are on The Jackson Laboratory website (www.jax.org stock no. 004824 accessed on 26 February 2026). Animals were randomized into five groups: (1) non-treated and non-diabetic wild-type BTBR ob/ob mice (WT group); (2) non-treated and diabetic BTBR ob/ob mice (VH ob/ob group); (3) BTBR ob/ob mice treated with empagliflozin (Jardiance MR, Boehringer) used at a concentration of 10 µg/g body weight dissolved in drinking water (Empa group); (4) BTBR ob/ob mice treated with lyophilized MiS1 peptide (Proteogenix, Schiltigheim, France) dissolved in 100% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and resuspended with 0.9% sodium chloride and then intraperitoneally injected three times per week at a dose of 4 μg/g body weight (MiS1 group); and (5) BTBR ob/ob mice combination therapy with empagliflozin 10 µg/g plus MiS1 4 µg/g (Empa + MiS1 group). After 7 weeks of intervention (week 22), all mice groups (n = 8/group) were analyzed and euthanized. Mice in the diabetic control group (VH ob/ob group) were administered DMSO at concentrations of <0.2% dissolved in 0.9% sodium chloride by intraperitoneally pathway.

4.3. Evaluation of Clinical Parameters and Progression of Renal Damage

Blood glucose and body weight measurements were monitored using an Accu-Chek Performa glucometer (Roche) and a digital balance, respectively. Serum and urine creatinine levels were measured using the Jaffé reaction method (LiquiColor, HUMAN Diagnostics, Wiesbaden, Germany). Urine samples were collected once a week, and albuminuria was analyzed by ELISA (Mouse Albumin, ALPCO, Salem, NH, USA).

4.4. Renal Tissue Collection

Mice were anesthetized with 2,2,2-tribromoethanol at 2% (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA) dissolved in 2-methyl-2-butanol (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA). Subsequently, they were euthanized, blood samples were taken, and renal tissue was extracted. A portion of the tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, while the other part was immediately stored in liquid nitrogen and processed for RNA extraction.

4.5. Histological Analysis and Immunohistochemistry Studies

Formaldehyde-fixed paraffin-embedded kidney samples were cut into 4 µm sections for histochemical staining (periodic acid–Schiff and Masson’s trichrome) and immunohistochemistry. Glomerular and tubulointerstitial lesions were classified according to semiquantitative histopathological damage, similar to the work of Opazo-Ríos et al. [33].

For immunodetection, kidney sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval in a citrate buffer pH 6.0. Tissue was then blocked with 3% BSA in PBS and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 30 min. The primary antibody (Wilms Tumor 1 protein (WT-1); Agilent Cat# M3561, RRID: AB_2304486, dilution 1:100) was incubated for 1 h, washed with PBS, and the secondary antibody (Universal LSAB plus ICQ kit, DAKO Corporation, Carpinteria, CA, USA) was incubated for 20 min, followed by PBS washing. The reaction was visualized with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine, and hematoxylin was used for nuclear counterstaining.

The histopathological damage score and podocyte count by WT-1 glomerular positive cells were performed by a blinded pathologist and researcher.

Positive staining was quantified using Image-Pro Plus v7.0 software and expressed as a percentage of the total area and the number of positive cells (per glomerular cross-section or tubular field).

4.6. RNA Extraction, cDNA Synthesis, and RT-qPCR

Total RNA from renal tissue was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Ambion, Austin, TX, USA). cDNA was synthesized using a high-capacity cDNA archive kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) with 2 µg of total RNA. For gene expression analysis, primers designed using Primer-BLAST v2.x software and synthesized by Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA) (Supplemental Table S1) were used. Quantitative analysis of gene expression was performed using real-time PCR 7500 Applied Biosystems, with 7500 System SDS V.1.2b1c3 software. The expression of target genes was analyzed in duplicate and normalized to 18s transcripts.

4.7. Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). For longitudinal variables measured repeatedly over the course of the study (body weight, blood glucose levels, and urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio [ACR]), a repeated-measures two-way ANOVA was performed following standard methodological recommendations for preclinical studies. This analysis evaluated the main effects of group (treatment) and time (weeks), as well as the group × time interaction, to determine if the disease trajectory differed significantly between experimental groups.

Sphericity was assessed using Mauchly’s test, and the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied when necessary. Upon detecting significant interactions, post hoc multiple comparisons were conducted using Bonferroni’s correction to identify specific differences between groups at each time point. Cross-sectional data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analysis was performed using R version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) via the RStudio v.2026.01.1-403 environment.

Graphs and corresponding statistical tests were performed using GraphPad Prism V.6 software (GraphPad Software Inc., La Joya, CA, USA).

5. Conclusions

Therefore, in BTBR ob/ob mice, a model that recapitulates the clinical and morphological lesions of human DN, we demonstrated that empagliflozin, SOCS1 mimetic peptides, and a combination of both improved functional and structural renal damage while modulating inflammatory-, redox-, and metabolic-related pathways. Combined treatment provided the greatest renoprotective benefit overall. These findings should support further exploration of SOCS1 mimetic peptide therapy as an add-on to SGLT2i to reduce albuminuria and renal damage in type 2 DN.
