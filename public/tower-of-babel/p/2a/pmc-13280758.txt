Establishment of artificial intelligence-driven fluorescence morphometry reveals involvement of osteocyte perilacunar remodeling specifically in mandibular bone of ovariectomized rats
Authors: Atsuko Nakanishi-Kimura, Haruhisa Watanabe, Marie Hoshi-Numahata, Masae Goseki-Sone, Tadahiro Iimura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBone dynamically changes its shape and structure in response to extra-tissue environments, so that bone morphometry has been a substantial method to evaluate pathophysiology of bone. Osteocytes embedded in mineralized bone matrix play key roles in systemic bone metabolism and characterize distinct bone sites. The jawbone has been described as a unique bone in the context of vertebrate evolution and function. Bone loss in the mandibular bone is less obvious in osteoporotic conditions than in other bones, such as vertebral and limb long bones, both in animal models and in clinical studies. Since osteocyte lacunae are complex and small (−10 µm in length) in shape and size, respectively, comprehensive and unbiased morphometrical analysis of changes in the size of osteocyte lacunae was still an obstacle. This study established an artificial intelligence-driven morphometry with wide-field microscopy-based imaging of osteocyte lacunae. Successive comparative analyses demonstrated active perilacunar bone remodeling in the mandibular bone than in the parietal bone. This approach enabled us to statistically compare morphometric parameters in a more comprehensive and unbiased manner. We further discuss the possible unique contribution of the mandibular bone to the pathophysiology of osteoporosis.Keywords: AI-driven morphometry, bone morphometry, osteoporosis, mandibular bone, osteocyte lacunae, bone remodeling

Introduction

The shape and function of bone have changed throughout vertebrate evolution. Acquisition of the jawbone and spine has proven critical for improving feeding and locomotion as vertebrates evolved [1, 2]. Concomitantly, the need to store calcium (Ca) in the body during the transition from aquatic to terrestrial life has led to the development of sophisticated hormonal regulation of bone metabolism by parathyroid hormone, calcitonin and active vitamin D. Therefore, bone mineral metabolism (bone metabolism) and related diseases are closely associated with bone shape, which has led to invent analytical methods based on morphology to evaluate bone function [3].

Bone supplies minerals to the body through a continuous remodeling process involving bone resorption and bone formation by osteoclasts and osteoblasts, respectively. Within the bone matrix, osteocytes extend their projections to form an intercellular network (osteocyte network) that senses mechanical stimuli to the bone and exerts regulatory signals to osteoclasts and osteoblasts [4, 5]. During pregnancy and lactation, osteocytes absorb the surrounding bone tissue to supply Ca to the fetus and infant. During this process, the bone lumen surrounding the osteocytes expands; however, when lactation is discontinued, the expanded lumen shrinks. This is referred to as ‘osteocyte perilacunar bone remodeling’ as a mechanism to supply minerals to the body fluid without compromising the skeletal outline or mechanical properties supported by the ­skeleton [6, 7].

Osteoporosis is a common metabolic bone disease in which an imbalance in bone-resorptive osteoclastic activity outweighs bone-forming osteoblastic activity caused by aging, loss of sex hormones, inflammatory diseases and disuse syndrome [8]. This bone disease increases the risk of bone fracture, which is highly associated with a reduction in the quality of life and an acute increase in the osteoporotic population, especially in aging societies, placing significant burdens on healthcare systems. In the pathogenesis of osteoporosis, it is mainly the vertebral or limb long bones that are fracture-prone and require clinical care. However, craniomaxillofacial bone loss is less pronounced and does not precede trunk and limb bone loss [9, 10]. There are no clinical reports showing that osteoporosis drugs increase jawbone mass. In other words, the jawbone seems to have metabolic specificity that differs from that of other bones.

The word ‘morphometry’ is defined as the quantitative analysis of form, with an emphasis on assigning meaningful numerical outputs to diverse 2- (2D) and 3-dimensional (3D) representations. As mentioned above, the size and shape of the bone are highly associated with pathophysiological conditions, which has led to the invention of the analytical term ‘bone morphometry’ for preclinical and clinical studies in bone biology and medicine [11]. Bone morphometry has traditionally encompassed both the size and shape of the bone but has evolved to include the bone quality, cell-level metrics such as number and surface, and dynamic indices of bone formation.

Our research group previously established an applied method of artificial intelligence (AI)-driven bone morphometry with wide-field fluorescence microscopy focusing on the size and shape of Haversian canals, which successfully evaluated changes in cortical bone metabolism in response to an anti-osteoporosis drug, teriparatide (TPTD) [12]. Using 3D fluorescence morphometry based on high-resolution confocal imaging, we also observed unique morphometric changes in mandibular osteocyte lacunae in response to TPTD, suggesting an active bone metabolic regulation through osteocyte perilacunar remodeling [13]. However, since osteocyte lacunae are complex and small (−10 µm in length and −5 µm in width) in size, and change their complex shape dynamically, comprehensive and unbiased morphometrical analysis of changes in the size and shape of osteocyte lacunae to understand bone site-specific regulation of osteocyte perilacunar remodeling was still an obstacle. The present study established an AI-driven morphometry methodology to compare osteocyte lacunae in a comprehensive and unbiased manner using wide-field fluorescence microscopy with the goal of uncovering the metabolic uniqueness of the jawbone using ovariectomized (OVX) rats as a model of postmenopausal osteoporosis.

Materials and methods

Animals and bone specimen preparation

Ten-week-old female Sprague–Dawley rats (Charles River, Kanagawa, Japan) were maintained under a 12-h light/dark cycle with unrestricted access to tap water and a standard diet containing 1.2% Ca, 0.9% phosphorus, 22% protein, and vitamin D3 (6.2 IU/g) (CRF-1; Oriental Yeast, Tokyo, Japan). The rats underwent sham surgery or ovariectomy at 12 weeks old and were euthanized by exsanguination under anesthesia at 20 weeks old. The mandibular and parietal bones were fixed in 70% ethanol, stained with Villanueva bone stain, dehydrated in a graded ethanol series, cleared in acetone and embedded in polymethyl methacrylate (Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Osaka, Japan). Thin sections (thickness: 5 μm) were prepared from the mandibular and parietal bones for a histomorphometric analysis.

All experimental protocols for animal studies were approved by the experimental animal ethics committee at Hokkaido University (Approval No. 2020 No. 2) and were conducted in accordance with the guidelines for the management and handling of experimental animals. Animal care technicians and staff members who were administered treatments were not included in sample measurements. All sample measurements were performed using objective methods. Data analyses of the fixed bone specimens were performed at Hokkaido University.

X-ray computed tomography scanning and peripheral quantitative computed tomography analyses

The bone mineral density (BMD) and bone mineral content (BMC) of the L4 and L5 vertebral bodies were measured using an X-ray computed tomography (CT) system for small experimental animals in rat mode (LaTheta LCT-100; Hitachi Aloka Medical, Ltd, Tokyo, Japan) as described previously [14]. Bone parameters were computed on the tomographic scanning images at 1.5-mm intervals in the measurement area between the fourth and fifth lumbar vertebrae. The minimum cross-sectional moment of inertia and polar moment of inertia, which represent the flexural rigidity and torsional rigidity, respectively, were also calculated automatically using the software provided with the device. According to the manufacturer, the precision error (as the % coefficient of variation) was within the 2% range for all measurements.

Biochemical analyses of serum

Blood was collected and centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 15 min to extract serum. Sera were collected and stored at −80°C until being thawed for an analysis. Ca was measured using the o-cresol-phthalein complexion color development method, and inorganic phosphorus was determined using the p-methylaminophenol reduction method [14]. Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) levels were determined as previously described [14]. The serum levels of 1alpha, 25-dihydroxyvitamin D3 (1alpha, 25[OH]2D3) were assayed using 1alpha, 25(OH)2D3-RIA methods (Mitsubishi Kagaku Bio-Clinical Labs, Inc., Tokyo, Japan). Serum FGF23 levels were measured using an enzyme immunoassay (Kainos Laboratories, Inc., Tokyo, Japan).

Wide-field DIC and fluorescence imaging

Undecalcified bone sections stained with Villanueva bone staining were imaged using a differential interference contrast (DIC) microscope system (NI-E Microscope; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan), and objective lens, Plan Apo λ20× (numerical aperture = 0.75) (Nikon), and the TxRed filter set was used for autofluorescence. The emission wavelength was 560–640 nm. In the parietal bone, the entire section was comprehensively imaged to obtain large, high-contrast images of the whole section. However, in the mandibular bone, images were taken separately for the buccal, lingual and alveolar bones. Image processing was performed using the NIS-Elements AR imaging software program (Nikon).

Setting of regions of interest and histomorphometry

In the mandibular bone, 4 regions of interest (ROIs) of 200 μm × 200 μm were set in the buccal region, while 3 ROIs of 200 μm × 200 μm were set in the lingual and alveolar bone regions; the osteocyte lacunae within the ROIs were then measured. ROIs were randomly set in each region. For morphometric measurements of the parietal bone, based on histological observations of differential interference images, the parietal bone was divided into 3 layers: the outer plate (i), which is the external side of the parietal bone; the middle plate (ii), which contains marrow; and the inner plate (iii), which is the internal side of the parietal bone. Three (100 μm × 100 μm) ROIs of were set in the outer, middle, and inner plates, and the osteocyte lacunae within the ROIs were measured. ROIs were randomly set in each region. Morphometric parameters of osteocyte lacunae area, perimeter, length, width, sphericity and smoothness were set as depicted in Fig. 3.

Analyses of serum marker levels. (a) Bone markers. (b) Serum protein and cholesterol markers. Results are presented as mean ± SD (Sham: n = 4 rats, OVX: n = 6 rats). Significant differences were determined by unpaired t-test, 2-tailed (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001).

AI-driven bone histomorphometry

AI-driven bone histomorphometry of osteocyte lacunae in the mandibular and parietal bones was performed using AI deep learning methods with the imaging analysis tools NIS-Elements AR and NIS, ai (Nikon), which is a commercially available software program. As shown in Fig. 2a, we first prepared teaching data in which osteocyte lacunae were manually recognized. Machine learning was performed 1000 times using the function: ‘segment function. ai,’ which was included in the software program. The AI training data were applied to the raw data and automatically binarized by AI (Fig. 2b). All obtained images were checked by the researchers. Insufficient recognition of osteocytic lacunae was corrected manually. Using this AI-driven morphometric measurement, the following parameters were measured semi-automatically: Object Number, Object area, Length, Width, Perimeter, Smoothness and Circularity, and these results were analyzed statistically.

pQCT analysis of the lumbar vertebrae. (a) Morphometric parameters. (b) Bone strength parameters. Results are presented as mean ± SD (Sham: n = 4 rats, OVX: n = 6 rats). Significant differences were determined by unpaired t-test, 2-tailed (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001).

Statistical analyses

All data are presented as the mean and standard deviation (SD) and were analyzed by unpaired t-test, 2-tailed. The GraphPad Prism software program (version 9.5.0, Boston, MA) was used to create graphs and perform all the statistical analyses. The threshold for statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

X-ray CT confirmed osteoporotic phenotypes of OVX rats

Two experimental groups were established: sham-operated (control) and OVX rats (Fig. 1a). Rats in each group were subjected to either ovariectomy or sham surgery at 12 weeks old. Eight weeks after the surgery, osteoporotic phenotypes of OVX rats were confirmed (Fig. 1a). Concomitantly, non-decalcified bone sections stained with Villanueva bone stain obtained from the parietal and mandibular bones were analyzed (Fig. 1b and Fig. S1).

Experimental design. (a) Two experimental groups were established: sham-operated (control) and OVX rats. Ovariectomy or sham surgery was conducted at 12 weeks old. Eight weeks after the surgery, osteoporotic phenotypes were evaluated using pQCT and serum bone markers. Non-decalcified bone sections stained with Villanueva bone stain obtained from the parietal and mandibular bones were prepared. Osteocyte lacunae in each bone site, such as the outer, middle, and inner plates in the parietal bone, alveolar bone, and lingual and buccal cortical bones in the mandibular bone were analyzed by AI-driven morphometry. (b) Schematic diagrams demonstrate the bone sites measured in the parietal and mandibular bones. The Scale bars indicate 20 μm in parietal bone and 50 μm in mandibular bone, respectively.

To confirm osteoporotic phenotypes in OVX rats, we performed peripheral quantitative CT (pQCT)-based morphometry (Fig. 2). As previously described, the total BMD of the lumbar vertebral bones in the OVX group was significantly reduced compared with that in control rats (Fig. 2a). Cortical and trabecular BMDs were also significantly reduced in the OVX group. Consistently, bone values of BMC, such as total, cortical, trabecular BMCs, and cancellous BMCs showed significantly reduced scores in the OVX group compared to the control group (Fig. 2a and Fig. S2). Bone strength values of the lumbar spine, such as the minimum moment of inertia of the cross-sectional areas and the polar moment of inertia of the cross-sectional areas, were significantly lower in the OVX group than in the control group (Fig. 2b). These pQCT analyses indicated that the OVX rats in this study successfully developed postmenopausal osteoporosis.

A biochemical analysis of serum parameters showed changes in bone metabolism in OVX rats

Serum Ca and phosphorus levels in the OVX group did not show significant difference between the groups (Fig. 3a). Serum levels of 1alpha, 25(OH)2D3, and ALP tended to be upregulated in the OVX group, albeit not significantly, suggesting an upregulated Ca uptake in the OVX groups. Fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23) levels in the OVX group were significantly downregulated compared to those in the control group, suggesting reduced phosphorus excretion in the OVX group. Serum levels of total protein and albumin were significantly reduced in the OVX group, while the levels of triglyceride and total cholesterol did not show significant differences, suggesting an aged phenotype in protein synthesis but not in cholesterol metabolism in OVX rats (Fig. 3b). These analyses also indicated that the OVX rats in this study successfully developed postmenopausal osteoporosis, as previously described.

Establishment of an AI-driven morphometric analysis

To ensure comprehensive and unbiased analyses, we established an AI-driven morphometric analysis (Fig. 4) with images acquired by a wide-field fluorescence microscope equipped with DIC (Fig. S1), which enabled us to simultaneously compare fluorescence and bright-field DIC images of the same ROIs. We first prepared a set of teaching data that were manually scored for osteocyte lacunae and bone vessel lumen with careful observation of bright-field DIC and corresponding fluorescence images (Fig. 4a). Machine training was then repeatedly conducted up to 1000 times, achieving a 0.05% training loss rate (Fig. 4a). To conduct an AI-driven morphometric analysis, AI-driven binarization of fluorescence signals derived from osteocyte lacunae was performed (Fig. 4b). When insufficient recognition of a fluorescence signal was observed, we manually corrected the recognition (Steps 2 and 3 in Fig. 4b). This approach successfully achieved automatic recognition of osteocyte lacunae, which could not be achieved by conventional binarization based on light intensity.

Methodological flow charts of AI-driven bone histomorphometry. (a) Flow chart of AI training. (b) Flow chart of AI-driven object binarization with and without minor manual correction.

Based on this AI-driven recognition and segmentation of fluorescence images, we set the parameters for the vessel lumen, such as area, perimeter, length, width, circularity and roughness (Fig. 5a). The parameters for osteocyte lacunae were also set, such as the area, perimeter, length, width, circularity and roughness of the osteocyte lacunae (Fig. 5b). We then statistically compared these parameters between the OVX and control groups.

Parameter settings for AI-driven bone histomorphometry. (a) Parameter settings for vessel lumen. (b) Parameters setting for osteocyte lacunae. Parameters of ‘Object area’ and ‘Perimeter’ were simply set as area and perimeter of binarized object, respectively. Parameters of ‘Length’ and ‘Width’ were set as the longest and shortest diameters of binarized object, respectively. Hull convex perimeter was measured as the length of a line connecting a set of the smallest convex points. The parameter of ‘Smoothness’ was calculated as the Hull convex perimeter/Perimeter. The parameter of ‘Circularity’ was calculated as the 4π* object area/perimeter2.

An AI-driven morphometric analysis of vessel lumen in mandibular bone did not show significant differences between the control and OVX groups

To assess the effect of OVX on bone vessel lumen morphology, we focused on the site of the masseter tubercle in the mandibular bone, as the vessel lumen was highly accumulated only in the mandibular bone site (Fig. 6a). By applying the AI-driven morphometry described above, we conducted morphometric analyses of the vessel lumen (Fig. 6b). The number of vessel lumens in the OVX group was comparable to that of the control group. Morphometric parameters of the area, perimeter, length, width, circularity and roughness did not show significant differences between the groups, suggesting that OVX did not affect peri-vessel bone remodeling (Fig. 6c).

An AI-driven analysis of vessel lumen in mandibular bone. (a) Schematic diagram demonstrating a bone site measured in mandibular bone. (b) Representative images of vessel lumen. Fluorescence raw images (upper panels) and binarized images (lower panels) are shown. Scale bars indicate 100 μm. (c) Morphometrical parameters of vessel lumen were compared and statistically analyzed. All data are shown as mean ± SD (n = 3–4 ROIs, 5 rats). Significant differences were determined by unpaired t-test, 2-tailed (*P < 0.05).

An AI-driven morphometric analysis demonstrated expanded osteocyte lacunae in mandibular bone induced by OVX

To uncover the effect of OVX on the morphology of osteocyte lacunae in mandibular bone, we analyzed 3 distinct bone sites: alveolar bone, lingual cortical bone and buccal cortical bone, which showed distinct morphology of the osteocyte lacuno-canalicular network (Fig. 7). At the alveolar bone site, although the total number of osteocyte lacunae was comparable between the OVX and control groups, morphometric parameters, such as the area, perimeter, length, width, circularity and roughness of osteocyte lacunae, were significantly greater in the OVX group than in the control group (Figs. 7 and 8a). Similar results were observed in other bone sites such as the lingual and buccal cortical bones. These analyses suggest that OVX stimulated osteocyte osteolysis in the mandibular bone, which was not obvious in the vertebral and limb bones.

All data are shown as mean ± SD (n = 3–4 ROIs, 5 rats). Significant differences were determined by unpaired t-test, 2-tailed (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001).

An AI-driven morphometric analysis demonstrated modest changes in osteocyte lacunae in parietal bone induced by OVX

We further analyzed the effect of OVX on the morphology of osteocyte lacunae in the parietal bone (Fig. 9). Three distinct bone sites of the parietal bone were set: the outer plate, middle plate, and inner plate, as these 3 regions showed obviously distinct morphology of the osteocyte lacuno-canalicular network (Fig. 9). On the outer plate, the number of osteocyte lacunae in the OVX group was comparable to that in the control group. Morphometric parameters, such as the area, perimeter and roughness, did not show significant changes whereas length was significantly greater in the OVX group than in the control group. Additionally parameters of width and circularity in the OVX group were significantly smaller than those in the control group, suggesting that OVX slightly changed the shape of osteocyte lacunae in this bone site, resulting in a more elongated morphology (Figs. 9 and 10a). In the middle plate, only the parameter of circularity in the OVX group was significantly smaller than that in the control group, while the other parameters did not show significant changes (Figs. 9 and 10b). In the inner plate, only the roughness parameter in the OVX group was significantly greater than that in the control group, whereas the other parameters did not show significant changes (Figs. 9 and 10c). These analyses in the parietal bone suggested that OVX modulated osteocyte peri-lacunar bone remodeling in this cranial bone, but to a lesser extent than in the mandibular bone.

Discussion

This study established an analytical method that was implemented with the more commonly used wide-field microscopy and AI-driven morphometry of osteocyte lacunae. By applying this method to OVX rats, which is a gold standard animal model of postmenopausal osteoporosis recommended by FDA (Food and Drug Administration), we statistically compared morphometric parameters in the osteocyte lacunae of the mandible and parietal bone. Morphometrical phenotypes of osteocyte lacunae of the OVX mandibular bone showed significant expansion, suggesting osteocyte osteolysis, while osteocyte lacunae of the OVX parietal bone showed changes depending on the parameters and bone site, suggesting modulated osteocyte perilacunar bone remodeling. Such morphological changes in osteocyte lacunae induced by the OVX have not been previously described in vertebral or limb long bones, at least to our knowledge [15, 16]. The findings of this study suggest elucidation of the metabolic specificity of craniofacial bones, especially the jawbone.

Osteocyte perilacunar bone remodeling is achieved by regulated demineralization and mineralization on the surface of osteocyte lacunae [7]. This biological phenomenon has been well characterized in the bone during lactation, hyperparathyroidism and tumor-associated osteolysis, whereby activated osteocytes exhibit osteoclast activity to remove. Regarding the mineralized bone matrix, our previous observation of rabbit tibiae supported the conclusion that demineralization on the surfaces of osteocyte lacunae is involved in the pharmacological activity of an anti-osteoporosis drug, TPTD (active form of human parathyroid hormone [PTH]) [17]. By establishing high-resolution 3D fluorescence analyses, our group previously reported that osteocytes in the mandibular bone (specifically alveolar bone) have unique functional characteristics of dynamic regulation of osteocyte perilacunar remodeling in response to TPTD [13]. This study supports our previous observations in a more comprehensive and unbiased manner by establishing AI-driven fluorescence morphometry. These studies suggest that PTH is a critical regulator of osteocyte perilacunar bone remodeling. Supportively, it is well characterized that upregulated PTH and PTH-related protein are key regulator of changes in bone metabolism during lactation [18, 19]. It may be interesting to consider that the mandibular bone could be one of the main sources of Ca supply from mother to baby, which was hardly expected. Alterations of the periosteocyte environment in jawbones of some osteoprotic conditions such diabetes and glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis were also reported [20–22], suggesting roles of jawbones on systemic bone metabolism through periosteocytic bone remodeling.

This study was conducted using OVX rats, which is the gold-standard animal model of postmenopausal osteoporosis for preclinical pharmacological studies, approved by the Food and Drug Administration [23]. Ovariectomy induces high bone turnover and rapid bone loss, which is in clear contrast with the tail suspension models of rats and mice as animal models of disuse osteoporosis. In the OVX rats, bone formation is inhibited, whereas bone resorption is enhanced or unchanged. Although there are some discrepancies in the pathophysiology between clinical cases of disuse osteoporosis and animal models, disuse osteoporosis induces a relatively low bone turnover. In a mouse model, the loss of trabecular bone, but not cortical bone, occurs soon after ovariectomy, and replacement of estrogen with 17-β-estradiol in OVX mice prevented bone loss [24], demonstrating that OVX animals are models of postmenopausal osteoporosis. In rats, significant bone loss occurred in the proximal tibial metaphysis, femoral neck and lumbar vertebral body 14, 30 and 60 days after ovariectomy [23]. Some members of our group previously reported a reduction of serum FGF23 levels in OVX rats [14]. The present findings confirmed this observation. FGF23 is a bone-derived hormone produced by osteocytes that regulates urinary phosphate excretion by interacting with FGF receptors [25]. FGF23 suppresses serum levels of 1alpha and 25(OH)2D3. In the present study, we observed a trend toward upregulation of serum 1alpha and 25(OH)2D3 and significant downregulation of serum FGF23 in the OVX group, although Ca and phosphorus levels were regulated to the control level. The downregulation of FGF23 in the OVX model was demonstrated to involve the JAK/STAT pathway [26]. FGF23 and dentin matrix protein (DMP1), a major extracellular matrix in osteocyte lacunae, are hallmarks of osteocytes in the bone. An in vitro study by our group demonstrated that DMP1-mediated mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling downregulates the expression and production of FGF23 [27]. Therefore, changes in extracellular components in osteocyte lacunae due to osteocyte perilacunar bone remodeling likely regulate FGF23 production. Our observations in this study suggest that mandibular osteocytes can contribute to the systemic progression of postmenopausal osteoporosis through FGF23 regulation.

In terms of BMD loss, the mandibular bone appears to be the most tolerant bone site for systemic osteoporosis [9]. Many researchers have attempted to predict systemic osteoporosis based on the mandibular cortex width shown on dental panoramic radiographs to diagnose osteoporosis in humans [10, 28]. These can be beneficial approaches, as panoramic radiography is a commonly used modality, and regular examinations are performed periodically on patients at a low cost. It remains difficult to investigate the effect of systemic osteoporosis on the jaw in humans. Using OVX rats, Lee et al. [9] observed that the mandible showed a substantially delayed and less intense response in terms of BMD loss than the femur. Our previous study also demonstrated that OVX rats alone or treated with TPTD did not have altered morphometric values of the mandibular bone compared to those in the control [13]. These findings suggest that patients clinically displaying osteoporotic changes in the mandible are expected to show severely advanced osteoporosis in other bones such as the femur and vertebral bones.

The present study elucidated the characteristic features of the mandibular bone in response to estrogen deficiency. We analyzed the parietal bones and mandibular bones, which were developed in the same manner as intramembranous ossification, although these 2 bones have distinct origins of mesoderm and neural crest cells, respectively. Distinct responses of parietal and mandibular bones to ovariectomy possibly imply distinct genetic and epigenetic regulation during bone development, affecting their regulation of bone metabolism even in adulthood. In intramembranous bones, neural crest cell-derived cells have been demonstrated to exhibit enhanced osteogenic ability [29–31]. The unique metabolic action of the mandibular bone observed in this study can be, at least partially, attributed to the developmental features of the mandible as well as its postnatal regulation by extrinsic stimuli such as mechanical loading by mastication and occlusion. Craniofacial bones are initially acquired bones that have dramatically changed during vertebrate evolution, which can be achieved by the functional plasticity of cranial neural crest cells that are free from Hox gene-mediated constraints [32–35]. Evolutionary acquisition of active osteocyte perilacunar bone remodeling, specifically in the mandibular bone, provides a mechanism to maintain predation and mastication in aged organisms without reducing the overt mandibular structure and function but maintaining the supply of minerals to the body fluid.

Our analytical method was implemented with the more commonly used wide-field microscopy and AI-driven morphometry, which enabled us to statistically compare morphometric parameters in a more comprehensive and unbiased manner. The results of this study were consistent with those of our previous study, which established high-resolution 3D fluorescence analyses based on confocal microscopy [13]. Therefore, these 2 methods are compatible. It also should be emphasized on that our methods in this study can thoroughly be applied to conventional procedure of bone histomorphometry with un-decalcified bone sections. However, AI algorithms require large sets of appropriate teaching data that still require manual effort, especially for morphometric recognition and segmentation. Establishing more commonly usable teaching data, irrespective of staining conditions and species, would help improve the realm of bone histomorphometry. Our group previously established a combined analytical method of AI-driven morphometric analyses and geographical information system-based spatial mapping and successfully evaluated bone remodeling in Haversian canals in dogs, which does not require confocal microscopy but is possible with more commonly used wide-field microscopes [12]. This study could not achieve this combined method because of the extremely large data capacity of morphometric information of osteocytes in rats compared to that of Haversian canals in dogs. This will surely be overcome by improving the data-processing capacity.

Concluding remarks

Several limitations associated with the present study warrant mention. We could not perform a functional analysis to uncover how mandibular osteocytes specifically exhibit their unique function in comparison with other bone sites. It is also unclear how much mandibular osteocytes contribute to systemic bone metabolism under pathophysiological conditions. The invention and improvement of spatial methods for assessing cellular and molecular insights will enable us to address these scientific questions [3]. Nevertheless, our approach in this study with AI-driven morphometry combined with commonly used wide-field microscopy (without elaborated systems and complicated techniques), which enabled us to statistically compare morphometric parameters in a more comprehensive and unbiased manner, will highly contribute to pharmaceutical evaluation of anti-osteoporotic drugs, bone biomedicine.
