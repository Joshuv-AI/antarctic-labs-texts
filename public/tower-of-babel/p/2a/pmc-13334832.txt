Sulfur-modified V2O5 nanozymes with altered ROS generation characteristics for enhanced glucose colorimetry
Authors: Qianmi Wang, Jilei Xu, Yanmin Shan, Dali Yao, Jianfeng Xu, Bin Miao, Jingjing Li, Huijun Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Defect and surface-chemistry regulation are widely used strategies to improve the catalytic performance of metal oxide nanozymes and to modulate reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation. However, in V2O5-based nanozymes, the relationship among sulfur-related structural modification, surface oxygen environment, and ROS generation behavior remains insufficiently clarified. Herein, sulfur-modified V2O5 (S-V2O5) nanozymes were prepared through a precursor-transformation route involving sulfurization and subsequent air-oxidation reconstruction. The resulting S-V2O5 retained the main V2O5 crystalline framework, while showing local structural perturbation, altered surface oxygen environment, increased defect-associated features, and a higher contribution of reduced V-related species. These changes were accompanied by enhanced peroxidase-like (POD-like) activity, stronger apparent substrate affinity, and good cycling stability. Radical scavenging and electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spin-trapping results indicated altered ROS generation characteristics under the tested TMB-H2O2 reaction conditions, with a more pronounced superoxide-related signal in S-V2O5 compared with pristine V2O5. However, multiple ROS-related pathways may coexist, and these results should not be interpreted as definitive proof of an exclusive ROS pathway switch. As a proof-of-concept application, a glucose oxidase (GOx)/S-V2O5 cascade system enabled sensitive and selective glucose colorimetric detection. This work suggests that sulfurization-oxidation reconstruction can modulate the local structure and surface chemical environment of V-based oxide nanozymes, thereby improving POD-like catalytic output and colorimetric sensing performance.

Introduction

Nanozymes are generally defined as nanomaterials with enzyme-like catalytic activities that can mimic the catalytic functions of natural enzymes, such as peroxidase, oxidase, catalase, and superoxide dismutase. Owing to their catalytic activity, high stability, tunable physicochemical properties, and relatively low cost, nanozymes have attracted considerable attention in bioanalysis, environmental catalysis, disease diagnosis, and point-of-care testing.1–3 Among various nanozymes, metal oxide nanozymes have demonstrated considerable potential in peroxidase-like catalysis because of their diverse compositions, facile preparation, and redox-active surface structures.4–6 Recent research in this field has gradually shifted from simple material screening toward rational structural regulation, including size and morphology engineering, hierarchical pore design, defect modulation, heterostructure construction, and elemental modification.7,8 According to a review by Gao et al., studying the structure–function relationship and mechanistic insights has emerged as a critical direction in the field of nanozymes.9 However, much of the research still focuses on broad topics like “increased active sites”, “oxygen vacancy enrichment”, or “accelerated charge transfer”, with limited emphasis on the complex interactions between defect chemistry, local electronic structures, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation.1,10 In peroxidase-like catalysis, the type and contribution of various ROS, such as ˙OH and ˙O2−, can significantly impact the oxidation behavior of substrates and overall catalytic performance.11,12

A recent study by Wang et al. on doping-regulated H2O2 cleavage in Prussian blue nanozymes showed that structural modification can alter the ROS generation profile and influence H2O2 activation pathways, thereby contributing to catalytic differences among nanozyme systems.7 Furthermore, peroxidase-like catalytic systems based on colorimetric analysis offer several advantages, including visual readouts, simple operation, and minimal dependence on instrumentation. These qualities make them particularly attractive for point-of-care testing (POCT) and clinical monitoring applications.13–15 Glucose is an important metabolic indicator and is widely used as a model analyte to evaluate the analytical feasibility of nanozyme-based cascade colorimetric systems. Rapid and sensitive glucose detection is of considerable significance for diabetes care and clinical monitoring.4,16–18

Among various metal oxides, V2O5 is a promising enzyme-mimetic catalyst because of its variable valence states, redox-active V centers, and layered structure.19–21 The V centers can undergo valence-state interconversion, facilitating interfacial electron transfer and redox cycling, which is beneficial for peroxide activation and substrate oxidation.22,23 Previous studies have shown that regulating V-based oxide crystal structure, constructing composite interfaces, or introducing defect-associated features can improve catalytic performance. Moreover, the valence distribution of V species and the local oxygen environment are closely related to catalytic behavior.24,25 For example, Sun et al. reported a V2O5-based H2O2/glucose colorimetric detection system using the peroxidase-like (POD-like) activity of V2O5 nanozymes.16 Gangadhara et al. also demonstrated that the morphology of V2O5 nanozymes plays an important role in regulating their POD-like behavior and cascade glucose-sensing performance.21 Despite these advances, current studies on V2O5 nanozymes mainly emphasize structural optimization and activity enhancement, while experimental investigation of how local chemical-environment modulation affects ROS generation characteristics remains limited.26,27 In particular, in POD-like catalysis, defect structures in V2O5 may influence reactant adsorption/activation and alter the relative contribution of reaction channels involving H2O2 and dissolved oxygen, but these correlations remain insufficiently resolved.28–30 Anionic modification provides an effective approach for regulating the local structure and surface chemical environment of metal oxides.31 Compared with cation modification, anionic doping can simultaneously affect composition, metal–oxygen bond electron distribution, local coordination environments, and defect formation tendencies.32,33 Sulfur, in particular, differs from oxygen in electronegativity, orbital energy levels, and bonding characteristics, and thus may alter the local coordination environment and surface chemical properties of V2O5-based materials.34,35 Previous studies in non-enzymatic catalysis and energy-storage systems have shown that sulfur introduction can affect the structural features and charge-transfer behavior of V2O5-related materials, indicating its potential for structural regulation.36 However, in V2O5 nanozyme systems, the influence of sulfur-related modification on local structure, valence-state distribution, surface oxygen environment, and the associated changes in ROS generation characteristics remains inadequately explored.19,37

In this work, S-V2O5 was synthesized through air oxidation of a vanadium sulfide precursor (VS precursor), and its POD-like catalytic behavior and ROS-related characteristics were systematically investigated. Sulfurization-oxidation reconstruction is found to induce local structural perturbation, modify surface oxygen features, and enrich reduced V-related species, which are accompanied by enhanced POD-like catalytic activity. Radical scavenging and EPR analyses suggest altered ROS generation characteristics under the tested reaction conditions, with an enhanced superoxide-related signal in S-V2O5 compared with pristine V2O5. As a proof-of-concept, a GOx/S-V2O5 cascade system was constructed for sensitive glucose colorimetric detection. This study provides experimental insight into the relationship among sulfur-related local structural modulation, ROS generation characteristics, and catalytic performance in sulfur-modified V-based oxide nanozymes.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of V hydrogel precursor

In a typical preparation, 1.0 g of sodium alginate and 100 mg of vanadium chloride (VCl3) were dissolved in 100 mL of deionized water. The mixture was vigorously stirred at room temperature for 3 hours until a homogeneous solution was formed. During this process, V3+ ions crosslinked with the sodium alginate chains, resulting in the gradual formation of a sodium alginate-vanadium hydrogel. After 2 hours of crosslinking, the hydrogel was filtered from the solution and washed repeatedly with ultrapure water to remove weakly adsorbed metal ions and soluble residual species. The washed hydrogel was then rapidly frozen using liquid nitrogen and freeze-dried at −80 °C for 24 hours, resulting in the sodium alginate-vanadium aerogel precursor.

Synthesis of V2O5

The sodium alginate-vanadium aerogel precursor was placed in an air atmosphere and heated at a rate of 10 °C min−1 to 350 °C, followed by annealing at 350 °C for 2 hours. After natural cooling to room temperature, the product was collected as V2O5. The obtained sample was further washed with ultrapure water and dried before subsequent characterization and catalytic tests.

Synthesis of VS precursor

50 mg of the sodium alginate-vanadium aerogel precursor and 500 mg of thioacetamide (TAA) as a sulfur source were placed in two separate ceramic boats. The ceramic boat containing TAA was positioned on the upstream side of the tube furnace. Under a continuous flow of argon gas for protection, the furnace temperature was increased at a rate of 10 °C min−1 to 350 °C and maintained at this temperature for 3 hours to complete the sulfurization process, yielding a VS precursor with VS2-related crystalline features. After the reaction, the obtained sample was sequentially washed for 30 minutes each with ethanol and dilute hydrochloric acid under ultrasonic assistance to remove possible impurities. It was then washed with ultrapure water until the filtrate was neutral (pH ≈ 7), and finally dried in an oven at 60 °C for 24 hours to obtain the VS precursor.

Synthesis of S-V2O5

The prepared VS precursor was subjected to oxidation treatment in an air atmosphere. It was heated at a rate of 10 °C min−1 to 350 °C and annealed at 350 °C for 2 hours. After cooling to room temperature, the final product was collected and designated as sulfur-modified V2O5, abbreviated as S-V2O5. Based on its optimal enzyme-like catalytic performance (see Results and Discussion section), S-V2O5 was selected for subsequent characterization and performance testing.

Results and discussion

Material construction and structural characterization

To investigate the influence of sulfurization–oxidation reconstruction on the local structure and catalytic behavior of V2O5, S-V2O5 was synthesized through a precursor-confined assembly process followed by sulfurization and air-oxidation reconstruction (Fig. 1). Specifically, a sodium alginate-vanadium hydrogel was used as the precursor. The coordination interaction between α-l-guluronic acid (G) units in alginate and V3+ ions forms an “egg-box” structure, which helps immobilize and uniformly disperse vanadium species.38–40 The precursor was first sulfurized at 350 °C under Ar to generate a VS precursor, followed by air-oxidation reconstruction at 350 °C to obtain S-V2O5, abbreviated as S-V2O5. Morphological characterization showed that both V2O5 and S-V2O5 obtained after air calcination exhibited nanorod-like morphologies (Fig. S1 and S3), whereas the VS precursor displayed a two-dimensional nanosheet morphology (Fig. S2), indicating substantial morphological reconstruction during the sulfurization-oxidation process. Compared with pristine V2O5, the S-V2O5 nanorods showed more uniform size and shorter length, suggesting that the formation of the VS precursor and subsequent oxidation reconstruction may influence crystal growth kinetics. High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping revealed uniform V and S distributions in the VS precursor (Fig. S4). After oxidation, V, O, and S remained uniformly distributed in the S-V2O5 nanorods (Fig. 2a), suggesting that sulfur species remained dispersed during oxidation reconstruction without obvious phase segregation.

To further probe the local lattice characteristics of S-V2O5, high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) was performed (Fig. 2b). The selected-area fast Fourier transform (FFT) pattern, together with simulated selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) (Fig. 2c and d), is consistent with an orientation along the [111] crystal zone axis. The interplanar spacing of ∼0.31 nm, calculated from the inverse Fourier transform of the (0–11) spot in the FFT (Fig. 2e), matches the expected value. Combined with the atomic structure model (Fig. 2f), these results indicate that the examined region retains an ordered lattice framework. Based on this, geometric phase analysis (GPA) was used to evaluate local strain distribution (Fig. 2g and h). The strain is mainly distributed within approximately −2% to +2%, with a relatively uniform background in the crystal interior. In contrast, alternating tensile and compressive strain domains are observed near the surface, forming a clear local strain gradient and a surface-enriched distortion feature. These GPA results support the presence of local lattice inhomogeneity after sulfurization-oxidation reconstruction while preserving long-range crystallinity. Considering that the oxidation reconstruction of the VS precursor to V2O5 involves changes in vanadium valence state and anionic coordination environments, such local strain retention is reasonable. In addition, differences in S and O bonding characteristics may contribute to local redistribution of bond lengths and angles near the surface. It should be noted, however, that GPA reflects local strain distribution rather than directly identifying dopant sites or specific defect species. Nevertheless, the observed surface-localized strain feature provides structural evidence consistent with local structural perturbation and subsequent changes in surface chemical environment and catalytic behavior.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns (Fig. 3a) revealed that the oxidation product exhibited the characteristic crystal structure of V2O5 (JCPDS No. 97-005-9961). Compared with V2O5, S-V2O5 showed slight shifts in some diffraction peaks. Additionally, the characteristic diffraction peaks of the VS precursor with VS2-related crystalline features (JCPDS No. 89-1640) were also observed. These changes indicate that the final oxidation product retains the main V2O5 crystalline framework while showing possible lattice perturbation, strain, or local structural variation after sulfurization–oxidation reconstruction. However, XRD peak shifts alone cannot determine the exact sulfur occupation site or directly prove substitutional sulfur doping. In addition, considering the limited sensitivity of XRD toward amorphous or poorly crystalline species, minor mixed V–O/V–S/V–O–S components in the VS precursor cannot be completely excluded. Therefore, the sulfurized product is described as a VS precursor rather than phase-pure VS2. Chemical state and defect-associated feature analysis

To further examine the influence of sulfurization–oxidation reconstruction on the local chemical environment and defect-related features in V2O5, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted for both V2O5 and S-V2O5. Both samples showed V and O signals, whereas sulfur was only detected in S-V2O5, confirming the presence of sulfur-related species in sulfur-modified V2O5. The high-resolution V 2p spectrum of V2O5 exhibited mixed-valence V species on the surface (Fig. 3b). Compared with V2O5, the V 2p spectrum of S-V2O5 showed a clear change in component distribution and a slight shift of the main peaks toward lower binding energy (∼0.1 eV), together with an increased relative proportion of reduced V species. These features suggest that the local chemical environment of V sites is modified after sulfurization-oxidation reconstruction.41 However, the small V 2p binding-energy shift should not be overinterpreted as direct evidence of major electronic restructuring. This behavior can be understood as arising from local coordination reconstruction and charge redistribution: differences in electronegativity and orbital coupling between sulfur and oxygen may induce local charge rearrangement around V–X (X = O, S) bonds and modify bond polarization. In addition, defect formation and charge compensation during sulfurization-oxidation reconstruction may promote electron localization at neighboring V sites, thereby stabilizing reduced V species. The O 1s spectrum also showed clear changes in the lattice oxygen component (∼530.1 eV) and the higher-binding-energy component (∼532.1 eV) (Fig. 3c). Following sulfurization-oxidation reconstruction, the relative proportion of lattice V–O species decreased (from 52.8% to 15.8%), while the high-binding-energy component increased (from 17.3% to 41.0%). This change suggests modification of the surface oxygen environment, including increased defect-/adsorbate-associated oxygen species, rather than direct quantitative evidence of oxygen vacancies. It should be emphasized that O 1s fitting alone does not provide an unambiguous quantification of oxygen vacancies, as the high-binding-energy component may also include contributions from surface hydroxyls, adsorbed oxygen/water, low-coordinated oxygen species, and oxygen in sulfate-like or sulfite-like surface species. Therefore, the XPS analysis here is used primarily to indicate a comparative trend, which is further corroborated by EPR measurements (vide infra).The observed increase in defect-associated features may arise from two factors: (i) sulfur-related local coordination may modulate the local bonding environment of V–O/V–S units and influence surface oxygen features; and (ii) rapid valence and lattice rearrangement during oxidation reconstruction of the VS precursor, together with surface strain gradients and coordination imbalance, may facilitate defect generation and retention after cooling. The S 2p spectrum (Fig. 3d) shows a doublet at 163.9 and 165.1 eV, which can be tentatively assigned to reduced sulfur species, possibly associated with V–S-related local coordination or sulfur-containing local chemical environments. Meanwhile, a weak high-binding-energy component at approximately 168.2 eV can be assigned to minor surface oxidized sulfur species, such as sulfate-like, sulfite-like, or vanadyl sulfate-like species. EPR results (Fig. 3e) show a significantly enhanced symmetric signal at g ≈ 2.001 for S-V2O5, which is commonly associated with oxygen-defect-related paramagnetic centers and is consistent with the XPS trend.42 Taken together, the XPS and EPR results suggest that sulfurization–oxidation reconstruction induces coupled changes in valence-state distribution, local chemical environment, sulfur-related surface chemistry, and defect-associated features in V2O5. These changes provide a possible structural basis for the enhanced POD-like catalytic behavior discussed below, but they do not directly identify a specific type of defect-rich or electronically modulated active site.

POD-like catalytic performance and ROS species generation characteristics

To evaluate the peroxidase-like (POD-like) catalytic behavior of the samples in relation to their structural differences, the 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB)-H2O2 colorimetric system was employed. In the presence of a POD-like catalyst, TMB is oxidized to blue oxTMB, which exhibits a characteristic absorption peak at 652 nm. As shown in Fig. 3f, under identical reaction conditions, S-V2O5 displays a markedly stronger absorption response and a more intense blue color than V2O5. The absorbance at 652 nm for S-V2O5 is approximately 2.2 times that of V2O5, indicating improved POD-like activity under the tested conditions. This enhancement is consistent with the combined structural changes induced by sulfur incorporation, including local lattice inhomogeneity, defect-associated surface features, and enrichment of reduced V species. These factors may collectively improve interfacial adsorption/activation and facilitate redox cycling at V centers. The catalytic enhancement likely arises from the combined effects of local structural perturbation, modified surface oxygen environment, reduced V-related species, and morphology reconstruction, and the relative contribution of each factor would require further targeted experiments to separate. Optimization studies showed that S-V2O5 exhibited the highest activity at pH = 6 (Fig. S6) and remained active over 20–50 °C, with an optimum near 35 °C (Fig. S7), consistent with the weakly acidic preference of many TMB-H2O2 POD-like systems.48,49 Cycling tests further showed that S-V2O5 maintained high activity after 7 cycles (Fig. S8), while its morphology remained largely unchanged (Fig. S9), indicating good operational stability and structural durability. In addition, three independently prepared batches of S-V2O5 showed acceptable consistency in product yield, sulfur content, A652 response, and relative activity, with RSD values below 7.78% (Table S1), supporting the batch-to-batch reproducibility of the synthesis.

To compare ROS generation characteristics before and after sulfurization-oxidation reconstruction, radical scavenging and EPR experiments were conducted. In the scavenging tests, isopropanol (IPA) and benzoquinone (BQ) were used as scavengers for ˙OH and ˙O2−, respectively, following commonly used protocols in nanocatalytic and nanozyme systems.43,44 For S-V2O5, IPA caused only a small decrease in absorbance (85.6%), whereas BQ caused a pronounced decrease and obvious color fading (33.8%), suggesting that ˙O2− related oxidation processes contribute more strongly to TMB oxidation under the tested conditions, with ˙OH still playing a possible synergistic role. In contrast, for pristine V2O5, IPA produced stronger inhibition (from 45.3% to 22.4%), while BQ had a weaker effect (from 45.3% to 30.7%) (Fig. 4a), indicating a larger contribution from ˙OH-associated oxidation. EPR results support the same trend: S-V2O5 showed a stronger superoxide-related signal and a relatively weaker ˙OH-related signal (Fig. 4b and c), consistent with the scavenger results. The characteristic DMPO-˙OH (1 : 2 : 2 : 1) and superoxide-related spin-adduct signals have been widely reported in related systems.45,46 Taken together, these results indicate altered ROS generation characteristics after sulfurization-oxidation reconstruction, with a more pronounced superoxide-related signal in S-V2O5 under the tested conditions. However, ˙OH-related oxidation is not completely excluded, and multiple ROS-related pathways may coexist in the TMB-H2O2 catalytic system.

A plausible ROS-related catalytic interpretation can be proposed based on the scavenger and EPR results. Sulfurization-oxidation reconstruction, together with reconstruction-induced local structural perturbation and defect-associated features, may enhance the adsorption and activation of both H2O2 and dissolved oxygen, allowing multiple ROS generation channels to operate. Meanwhile, enrichment of reduced V species and changes in the local chemical environment may favor interfacial single-electron transfer, thereby increasing the relative contribution of superoxide-related processes.47 This interpretation is consistent with the stronger colorimetric response of S-V2O5, but it should be regarded as a plausible explanation rather than a definitive catalytic mechanism. The higher oxTMB output more likely reflects the combined effects of altered ROS generation characteristics, improved interfacial activation, and more efficient catalytic turnover within modified local structural and surface chemical environment. Kinetic analysis (Fig. S10, S11, and Table S2) further showed that the Km values of S-V2O5 for TMB and H2O2 were 0.081 mM and 3.714 mM, respectively, much lower than those of V2O5 (1.06 mM and 6.10 mM), indicating stronger apparent substrate affinity. Together with the Vmax results and the comparison with other nanozymes listed in Table S2, these data demonstrate improved overall POD-like catalytic efficiency compared with pristine V2O5. When combined with the structural characterization and ROS probing results, the kinetic data further support a coherent correlation between sulfurization-oxidation-induced local modulation and the enhanced POD-like catalytic performance, highlighting the superior efficiency of S-V2O5 relative to other nanozymes.

Conceptual validation of glucose colorimetric detection using the GOx/S-V2O5 cascade system

To test whether the catalytic advantage of S-V2O5 can be translated into analytical performance, we constructed a GOx cascade colorimetric glucose detection platform based on the POD-like activity of S-V2O5. The sensing mechanism follows a “recognition active substrate generation-catalytic amplification” sequence: GOx selectively oxidizes glucose to produce gluconic acid and H2O2, and S-V2O5 then catalyzes TMB oxidation by H2O2 to generate blue oxTMB. In this way, glucose concentration is converted into a visible color change and a quantitative absorbance signal at 652 nm. This design follows the general framework of GOx-nanozyme cascade colorimetry.48,49 The absorbance increased with glucose concentration, showing a clear concentration-dependent color change. A strong linear correlation was observed across the 10–400 µM range, with the equation y = 0.0029x + 0.0501 (R2 = 0.999), and a detection limit of 0.96 µM (S/N = 3) was achieved (Fig. 4e). Under the same conditions, pristine V2O5 gave a higher detection limit of 5.96 µM (Fig. S12), indicating that the enhanced POD-like activity of S-V2O5 improves signal amplification and analytical sensitivity in the GOx cascade system. Compared with reported nanozyme-based glucose colorimetric systems, the GOx/S-V2O5 platform shows favorable analytical performance (Table S3).

For selectivity evaluation, common interfering sugars (e.g., maltose and sucrose) at the same concentration produced much weaker responses than glucose, indicating good anti-interference performance (Fig. 4f). The selectivity mainly originates from the substrate specificity of GOx, while efficient catalytic amplification by S-V2O5 helps convert the generated H2O2 into a stronger colorimetric signal. Overall, the sensitivity and selectivity data provide functional evidence that sulfurization-oxidation-induced local modulation in V2O5 nanozymes can improve POD-like catalytic amplification and support glucose colorimetric analysis.

To further evaluate the matrix tolerance of the sensing system, glucose recovery experiments were performed in artificial sweat using the standard addition method. Artificial sweat samples spiked with 50, 150, and 300 µM glucose were analyzed using the established GOx/S-V2O5 colorimetric assay. As summarized in Table 1, the found concentrations were 45.7, 143.9, and 294.6 µM, corresponding to recoveries of 91.4%, 95.9%, and 98.2%, respectively, with RSD values ranging from 2.18% to 3.45%. These results indicate acceptable accuracy and reproducibility in the simulated sweat matrix, supporting the preliminary applicability of the proposed sensing system.

Conclusions

In this work, sulfur-modified V2O5 nanozymes were constructed through a precursor transformation route involving sulfurization and subsequent air-oxidation reconstruction. The resulting S-V2O5 retained the main V2O5 crystal framework while showing surface-localized lattice inhomogeneity, enhanced defect-associated oxygen features, and a higher proportion of reduced V species. Compared with pristine V2O5, S-V2O5 exhibited significantly improved POD-like catalytic performance in the TMB-H2O2 system, including stronger colorimetric response, lower apparent Km values toward TMB and H2O2, and good cycling stability. Radical scavenging and EPR results indicated altered ROS generation characteristics under the tested conditions, with a more pronounced superoxide-related signal in S-V2O5. However, ˙OH-related oxidation was not completely excluded, and multiple ROS-related pathways may coexist in the TMB-H2O2 catalytic system. As a proof-of-concept application, the GOx/S-V2O5 cascade system achieved sensitive and selective glucose colorimetric detection and outperformed the pristine V2O5 counterpart. Artificial sweat recovery experiments further suggested acceptable matrix tolerance in a simulated sample system. These findings show that sulfurization-oxidation-induced local structural and surface chemical modulation in V-based oxides is an effective way to tune POD-like catalytic behavior and improve nanozyme-based colorimetric sensing performance.
