Microenvironmental Cues That Regulate Cancer Cell Dormancy in Estrogen‐Receptor Positive and Triple‐Negative Breast Cancer Cell Lines
Authors: Jinesh Maniar, Pulkit Datt, Prerana P. Dange, Vaishali Kailaje, Mithlesh Lakhera, Mansi Samarth, Tanuja Durve, Anushree Kadam, Khushboo A. Gandhi, Sudeep Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe processes regulating cellular dormancy in tumor cells remain inadequately characterized. Further, there are very few models available that can recapitulate a dormancy‐like phenotype in vitro. In this study, we investigated the role of microenvironmental cues in inducing a dormancy‐like phenotype in estrogen receptor‐positive (MCF‐7) and triple‐negative (MDA‐MB‐231) breast cancer cell lines. We characterized an in vitro model in distinct conditions defined hierarchically by the following: (a) normoxia or true hypoxia, (b) dishes coated with fibronectin or laminin or none, and (c) presence or absence of 10 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (FGF‐2) in the culture medium, added on day 0 of culture. Cells were cultured at clonogenic densities for 9 days (day −1, day 0, up to day +7). Cells were characterized for dormancy‐ like behavior using increased p‐p38 and decreased p‐ERK expression (dormant, p‐p38High and p‐ERKLow expression), reduced Ki67 expression, elevated p21 and p27 levels by immunofluorescence, absence of senescence using β‐galactosidase staining and resistance to doxorubicin. Fibronectin or laminin were sufficient for the induction of a dormancy‐like phenotype in MCF‐7 cells under hypoxic conditions, whereas the addition of FGF‐2 to fibronectin (but not laminin) could induce a dormancy‐like phenotype under normoxic conditions. FGF‐2 was required with laminin to induce dormancy in MDA‐MB‐231 cells under hypoxic conditions (FGF‐2 plus fibronectin were unable to induce dormancy), whereas laminin or fibronectin alone were sufficient under normoxic conditions. Overall, we established culture conditions that recapitulate an in vitro breast cancer dormancy‐like phenotype and reveal subtype‐specific differences in dormancy regulation.

Background

Metastatic disease is the major cause of death among breast cancer patients [1]. Distant site metastases can occur several years after eliminating the primary tumor. Late recurrence is a consequence of induction of a prolonged quiescent stage in disseminated tumor cells (DTCs). The DTCs likely enter a prolonged non‐proliferative state of reversible growth arrest (quiescence) at the site of the metastatic niche, often called cellular dormancy [2, 3, 4]. Another type of dormancy is tumor mass dormancy, where there is a lack of net tumor growth because of an equilibrium between the rates of cell proliferation and cell death [5, 6, 7, 8]. The cellular‐level dormant phenotype is clinically significant, as it enables DTCs to evade therapeutic elimination and persist over extended periods, ultimately contributing to disease relapse. For example, therapy‐induced dormancy in estrogen receptor‐positive breast cancer has been observed, where cells remain viable but growth‐arrested following anti‐estrogen treatment, implicating trefoil factor 3 (TFF3) as a key mediator of this process [9]. TFF3 has been shown to promote metastatic seeding and survival of breast cancer cells at distant sites, and is also associated with reduced sensitivity to chemotherapeutic agents such as doxorubicin, whereas its inhibition enhances chemosensitivity and can overcome acquired resistance [10, 11].

Cellular dormancy has been proposed to be regulated by the crosstalk between DTCs and their microenvironment but the regulatory processes involved in cellular dormancy remain insufficiently investigated. The fate of DTCs is regulated by immunomodulatory and microenvironmental stresses encountered at secondary organ sites, where they can persist in a dormant state, particularly within the bone marrow, and contribute to late disease recurrence [12, 13, 14, 15]. Subsequently, the extracellular matrix (ECM) plays a pivotal role in deciding the fate of DTCs during metastasis. ECM proteins promote integrin‐mediated adhesions influencing the cell's fate [16]. Therefore, it has been previously hypothesized that DTCs should land in a favorable site to grow. Extensive studies in many cancers have reported that dormant DTCs are present in the bone marrow, suggesting that this niche could provide a favorable microenvironment for DTCs to enter dormancy [17]. Prior studies have shown the presence of the perivascular and endosteal niche and the existence of hypoxic zones at short distances away from the blood vessels in the bone marrow [18, 19, 20], potentially providing a permissive microenvironment for induction and maintenance of dormancy in DTCs. However, the role of hypoxia signaling in the induction and maintenance of dormancy in DTCs is not well studied.

Hence, we planned this study to evaluate whether hypoxic stress induces cellular dormancy in DTCs in a microenvironment that is akin to the bone marrow. We characterized an in vitro model using the components abundantly found in bone marrow. The main elements of our model system include basic fibroblast growth factor (FGF‐2), fibronectin (F), and laminin‐111 (L). FGF‐2 plays an essential role in the morphogenic differentiation of mammary ducts and can halt the growth of breast cancer cells [21]. We used the ECM proteins fibronectin and laminin in this study because of their known relevance in integrin‐mediated adhesions [22]. Fibronectin and laminin are two structural proteins constituting a significant portion of the bone marrow cellular matrix [23]. In this study, we investigated how the FGF‐2, fibronectin, or laminin microenvironment affects the growth of estrogen receptor‐positive (MCF‐7) and triple‐negative (MDA‐MB‐231) breast cancer cells under normoxic conditions followed by hypoxic conditions of 1% oxygen concentration. Experimental analysis was performed in cells obtained on day +7 after exposure to various microenvironmental conditions to determine the possible scenarios involved in the regulation of cellular dormancy in these breast cancer cells (Figure 1a).

Methods

Cell Lines

The human breast cancer cell lines MDA‐MB‐231 (RRID:CVCL_0062) and MCF‐7 (RRID:CVCL_0031) were procured from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Cell line authentication was performed by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling using the PowerPlex 18D System (Promega) and profiles were confirmed to match reference databases. All cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination and were confirmed to be mycoplasma‐free prior to use in all experiments.

Coating and Cell Culture

Thermo‐ScientificTM Nunc 35 mm petri‐dishes (C#150318) and 35 mm glass bottom dish (C# D35‐14‐1.5‐N) were used for ECM coating with Corning Fibronectin, Human, 5 mg (C#356008)/Sigma‐Aldrich Laminin (#L2020). The stock coating solution was diluted further in PBS at a concentration of 10 μg/mL for coating the 35 mm petri dishes. One milliliter of coating solution was added to a single 35 mm petri dish. After adding the coating solution, the petri dishes were kept at 4°C overnight. On the next day, the excess solution was removed and petri dishes were allowed to air dry for at least 45 min at room temperature inside the biosafety cabinet. These petri dishes were further used for performing the cell culture. Both the cell lines, MCF‐7 and MDA‐MB‐231, were maintained in GibcoTM Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Media (C#11995065) supplemented with 10% (v/v) Gibco Fetal Bovine Serum (C#16140071) and 1% (v/v) Gibco Antibiotic‐Antimycotic (100×) (C#15240062). In this study, we cultured MCF‐7 and MDA‐MB‐231 cells, respectively, for 9 days (day −1, day 0, up to day +7) at a clonogenic density to ensure that their primary interaction was with the substratum and not with each other. On day −1, 2000 cells were seeded on 35 mm petri dishes coated with fibronectin, laminin, and none, respectively. At day 0, these cells were kept under normoxic (~20% O2, 5% CO2). Subsequently, 10,000 cells were seeded under hypoxic conditions (1% O2, 5% CO2) using a hypoxia workstation (INVIVO2 400, UK). Basic fibroblast growth factor (FGF‐2; 10 ng/mL, C#354060, Corning) was added once at day 0 where indicated and was not replenished during the culture period. Cells were maintained under these conditions until day +7, at which point they were harvested and analyzed for all experiments, except for calcein‐AM staining assays.

Crystal Violet Staining

Sigma‐Aldrich Crystal Violet (C#6158) was used to stain these breast cancer cells to determine their viability and adhesion. After 7 days of culture, cells were stained with a freshly made 0.1% crystal violet in 2% ethanol/10 mM sodium borate (pH 9.0) solution. Media was aspirated, and 1 mL of crystal violet solution was added for 10 min. Plates were rinsed with water and kept for drying overnight. Colonies were counted optimally at 40× magnification in an inverted phase contrast microscope. Colonies of > 30 cells as growing and colonies of 12 or less cells were considered dormant.

Immunofluorescence Assays

A phospho‐p38 (p‐p38) mitogen‐activated kinase (MAPK) (Thr180/Tyr182) mouse antibody (C#9216), Ki67 mouse antibody (C#9449) and a phospho‐p44/42 MAPK (ERK1/2) (Thr202/Tyr204) rabbit antibody (C#4370) and p21 (12D1, C#2947) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology, and p27 (PA5‐27188) was purchased from Invitrogen. After 7 days in culture, cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and blocked with blocking buffer overnight at 4°C. Blocking buffer was made with 5% (w/v) goat serum (C#642921), 0.3% (v/v) Triton X‐100 in PBS. These cells were then incubated overnight with the p‐p38 primary antibody (1:100 in dilution buffer, 1% (w/v) BSA, 0.3% (v/v) Triton X‐00 in PBS) at 4°C, rinsed with PBS three times, and incubated with a secondary antibody (goat anti‐mouse IgG‐Alexa Fluor 568: 1:1000 in dilution buffer, 1% (w/v) BSA, 0.3% (v/v) Triton X‐100 in PBS) for 2 h at room temperature in the dark, rinsed with PBS three times, and then incubated with the p‐ERK antibody (1:400 in dilution buffer, 1% (w/v) BSA, 0.3% (v/v) Triton X‐100 in PBS) overnight at 4°C, rinsed with PBS three times, and incubated with a secondary antibody (goat‐anti rabbit IgG‐Alexa Fluor 488: 1:1000 in dilution buffer, 1% (w/v) BSA, 0.3% (v/v) Triton X‐100 in PBS) for 1 h and then rinsed with PBS three times. Incubated for 1 min with DAPI (1 μg/mL) in dilution buffer. Similarly, for the Ki67 mouse antibody (C#9449S), p21 (C#2947) and p27 (PA5‐27188) (1:400 in dilution buffer, 1% (w/v) BSA, 0.3% (v/v) Triton X‐100 in PBS) was used for staining a new set of experiments, where cells were stained in a similar manner. Labeled cells were stored in PBS. Images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM 780 confocal microscope (RRID:SCR_020922) with ZEN software. All images within a given experiment were captured under identical acquisition settings (laser power, gain, and exposure) across all conditions to ensure comparability. Any auto fluorescence was negated by using secondary antibody control. FIJI: ImageJ version 1.53t (http://imagej.nih.gov/ij) (RRID:SCR_003070) software was used to quantify the mean fluorescence intensity of each z‐stack to quantify p‐p38, p‐ERK, and the p‐p38 to p‐ERK ratio. For each image stack, the mean fluorescence intensity of a maximum intensity z‐projection was measured for p‐p38 and for p‐ERK using FIJI: ImageJ version 1.53t (http://imagej.nih.gov/ij) software. To account for background noise, intensity measurements of five background regions (regions in each corner and near the center of the image) that did not contain cells were quantified and averaged for each image. Background measurements were subtracted, and normalized intensity values from 10 individual z‐stacks were averaged to obtain the p‐ERK and p‐p38 intensity values. A minimum of 10 z‐stacks from every culture scenario were imaged and quantified.

Senescence Staining Assay

Senescence‐associated β‐galactosidase activity was measured for all scenarios on day +7 using the Senescence β‐Galactosidase Staining Kit (Cell Signaling Technology). We used a similar protocol provided by the manufacturer. Briefly, cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 1× fixative solution for 15 min. Then, the β‐galactosidase staining solution with a final pH between 5.9 and 6.1 was prepared and added to fixed cells. Samples were sealed with parafilm to prevent evaporation and placed in a dry 37°C incubator overnight. Imaging was performed using a Nikon Eclipse Ti Microscope. For the positive control of senescence, MCF‐7 and MDA‐MB‐231 cells were treated with 12.5 μM etoposide (Cell Signaling Technology) for 4 days and allowed to recover for 2 days in normal growth media.

Calcein‐AM Staining

Cells were initially cultured under the indicated microenvironmental conditions for 7 days to establish two distinct states: (i) proliferative conditions, where cells continued to expand, and (ii) dormancy‐inducing conditions, where cells remained growth‐arrested (Ki‐67−, p‐p38high/p‐ERKlow) but viable. Following this 7‐day induction phase, doxorubicin hydrochloride (2 μM) was added directly to the existing cultures without re‐plating, and cells were maintained under the same conditions for an additional 7 days. Invitrogen Calcein‐AM (C#C1430) was used to stain cells on day +14. Cells from all the culture scenarios were stained with 2 mM Calcein AM by removing the existing medium and putting in new culture medium without FBS for 30 min containing Calcein AM and then immediately evaluated by confocal microscopy (LSM780, Zeiss). This assay was used to assess persistence of viable cells under sustained cytotoxic stress rather than direct measurement of apoptosis or clonogenic survival. In this system, survival following doxorubicin exposure was evaluated based on the presence of Calcein‐positive cells at the endpoint, reflecting the ability of cells to persist under prolonged drug treatment.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 9 (http://www.graphpad.com) (RRID:SCR_002798) software. For in vitro experiments, a minimum of three replicates repeated at least three times were used to achieve reproducibility. For the dormant breast cancer cells detection experiments, the p‐values were estimated using an Ordinary one‐way ANOVA non‐parametric test with Dunnett's multiple comparison p‐values ≤ 0.05 considered significant (95% confidence). Represented with: p‐value: ns, no significance, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

Results

Tumor Microenvironment Regulates Induction of Cellular Dormancy‐Like Phenotype in Breast Cancer Cells

We investigated 24 culture scenarios to identify microenvironmental cues that could induce a cellular dormancy‐like phenotype in breast cancer cells. These 24 culture scenarios were determined according to the following parameters (Figure 1b). First, there were four culture conditions as follows: (i) MCF‐7 cells cultured in normoxia; (ii) MCF‐7 cells cultured in hypoxia; (iii) MDA‐MB‐231 cells cultured in normoxia; and (iv) MDA‐MB‐231 cells cultured in hypoxia. Each of these four culture conditions was further subjected to the following six culture scenarios for 7 days for a total of 24 culture conditions. The following example depicts six culture conditions for MCF‐7 cells grown under normoxic conditions for 7 days: (i) MCF‐7 cells grown on fibronectin with FGF‐2 (MCF‐7, FGF + F); (ii) MCF‐7 cells grown on fibronectin without FGF‐2 (MCF‐7, F); (iii) MCF‐7 cells grown on laminin with FGF‐2 (MCF‐7, FGF + L); (iv) MCF‐7 cells grown on laminin without FGF‐2 (MCF‐7, L); (v) MCF‐7 cells grown on uncoated dishes with FGF‐2 (MCF‐7, FGF); (vi) MCF‐7 cells grown on non‐coated dishes without FGF‐2 (MCF‐7, control). Likewise, similar culture scenarios were reproduced for MCF‐7 cells in hypoxic conditions, MDA‐MB‐231 in normoxic conditions, and MDA‐MB‐231 in hypoxic conditions, for a total of 24 culture conditions. This framework helped us to identify specific combinations of the microenvironmental cues involved in the induction of a cellular dormancy‐like phenotype in breast cancer cells.

We performed crystal violet staining to study how different microenvironmental factors affect the growth pattern of MCF‐7 and MDA‐MB‐231 cells, as shown in Figures 2 and 3, respectively. Under the normoxic condition on day +7, we observed that fibronectin and laminin prevented formation of big colonies in both cell lines. The cell count per colony for MDA‐MB‐231 was seven on fibronectin and eight on laminin coated dishes while for MCF‐7, it was 66 on fibronectin and only 16 on laminin coated dishes, which is indicative of the absence of big colonies (Figures 2 and 3). When these cells were grown only with FGF‐2 on non‐coated dishes under normoxia, both the cell lines formed big colonies with a high population density. The cell count per colony for MDA‐MB‐231 was 136 whereas for MCF‐7 it was > 500 cells. Further, crystal violet staining on day +7 led us to primarily identify the formation of possible dormant colonies with ≤ 12 cells as reported by Tivari et al. [24]. To confirm whether these cells were dormant or not, we performed immunofluorescence staining on all of the scenarios demonstrated later in this study.

Figure 2 shows that MCF‐7 cells demonstrated growth arrest when grown on fibronectin coated dishes with 14 cells per colony or laminin coated dishes with 13 cells per colony under hypoxic conditions. On the other hand, growth arrest was observed in MDA‐MB‐231 cells when grown with FGF‐2 on laminin coated dishes with one cell per colony (Figure 3). After observing these results, we proceeded with 22 culture‐competent scenarios under which cells survived till day 7 for further investigation via immunostaining to evaluate whether cell lines were growth‐arrested and whether the phenomenon observed was cellular dormancy.

Dormancy‐Like Phenotype Induction Marked by Phosphorylated p38High /ERKLow Expression Ratio and Expression of Cell Cycle Inhibitor Proteins

Immunofluorescence staining of phospho‐p38 (p‐p38) and phospho‐ERK (p‐ERK) was performed to determine the protein expression levels for confirmation of cellular dormancy. In vivo, studies performed by Aguirre‐Ghiso et al. showed that increased p‐p38 and decreased p‐ERK expression are associated with transition of cancer cells from a state of tumorigenicity to dormancy [12, 25]. There are several lines of evidence that demonstrate the function of p‐p38 lies in the induction of a G0‐G1 cell cycle arrest phase in the cancer cells, whereas p‐ERK acts as a promoter of proliferation. Therefore, a high p‐p38 and low p‐ERK expression has been recognized as a potential marker for the dormant state of cancer cells [12, 25, 26, 27]. Further, it has also been confirmed that this mechanism supports the overexpression of several genes, leading to the suppression of p‐ERK protein for promoting dormancy [26, 27, 28, 29, 30]. Furthermore, we also analyzed the expression of Ki67, a proliferation marker, for all culture scenarios. The expression of Ki67 has been widely used for pathological investigations and to determine whether the cancer cells are proliferating [31]. Lastly, we confirmed high expression of the cyclin‐dependent kinase inhibitors p21 (CDKN1A) and p27 (CDKN1B) which operate at the G1 checkpoint, to further confirm growth arrest (if any) of the cells [32].

Our study demonstrates that different microenvironmental conditions significantly influence p‐p38 and p‐ERK expression patterns in MCF‐7 and MDA‐MB‐231 cells. Figure 4 shows the expression patterns of p‐p38 and p‐ERK in the six culture scenarios for MCF‐7 cells cultured in normoxic conditions. It demonstrates that the expression of p‐p38 was significantly higher than p‐ERK (mean intensity: 23.5 vs. 16.3; p < 0.0001) when the MCF‐7 cells were grown on fibronectin‐coated dishes with FGF‐2 for 7 days, which was accompanied by negative expression of Ki67. Simultaneously, the ratio of p‐p38 to p‐ERK was also calculated. If the observed ratio was above one, it was considered a positive induction of dormancy. The observed ratio of p‐p38 to p‐ERK for MCF‐7 cells grown on fibronectin‐coated dishes with FGF‐2 under normoxia was 1.44, which indicated that this microenvironment scenario is associated with the induction of dormancy in MCF‐7 cells. Further, as can be seen in Figure 4, none of the other five culture scenarios for MCF‐7 cells grown in normoxic conditions induced dormancy.

In summary, we observed that six out of a total of 22 studied culture scenarios showed a dormancy‐like pattern for cell growth with high p‐p38 expression, low p‐ERK expression, and no Ki67 expression. In the remaining 16 culture scenarios, the cells were proliferative.

Further we evaluated expression of p21 and p27 in both the cell lines under previously described conditions. Figure 8 shows the expression of p21 and p27 in MCF‐7 cells under normoxic and hypoxic conditions. Discussion

Several past studies have shown that tumor microenvironmental factors, particularly ECM remodeling, tumor hypoxia, as well as therapy‐induced stress, contribute to the induction of dormancy in breast cancer metastatic niche [33]. In the present study, we found that specific combinations of extracellular matrix proteins (like fibronectin and laminin), growth factor signaling (FGF‐2), and oxygen availability can induce a dormancy‐like phenotype in two breast cancer cell lines, MCF‐7 and MDA‐MB‐231, representing estrogen receptor positive and triple negative breast cancer, respectively. To recapitulate key features of the bone marrow microenvironment, we incorporated FGF‐2, a growth factor abundantly present in the bone marrow niche and known to regulate mammary cell behavior. Previous studies have shown that FGF‐2 exposure can induce a non‐proliferative state in breast cancer cells, accompanied by altered integrin expression and enhanced interactions with ECM [21, 34]. Similarly, strong adhesion to ECM proteins has been reported to support survival of growth‐arrested cells through activation of pro‐survival pathways such as PI3K/Akt signaling [35]. In addition to fibronectin, laminin is a major component of the bone marrow microenvironment, particularly enriched in periosteal and vascular niches. Laminin‐mediated signaling has been implicated in promoting growth arrest and cellular differentiation in breast cancer cells, whereas disruption of these interactions can facilitate tumor progression [36, 37, 38, 39, 40]. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying laminin‐driven regulation of tumor cell dormancy remain incompletely understood. Consistent with these observations, our findings suggest that laminin, in combination with other microenvironmental cues such as FGF‐2 and hypoxia, may play a critical role in regulating the induction and maintenance of a dormancy‐like phenotype in DTCs. Furthermore, our experimental conditions were designed to simulate key features of the hypoxic bone marrow niche [19]. While these experimental conditions do not fully capture the complexity of the bone marrow metastatic microenvironment, which includes stromal, endothelial, and immune components, they enable the identification of causal relationships between defined extracellular signals and dormancy induction.

Additionally, our study used true hypoxic conditions, unlike previous studies which used CoCl2‐induced hypoxia in in vitro models [41]. Current literature on cancer dormancy focuses predominantly on cellular and molecular factors like ECM [42, 43], while non‐cellular factors, such as oxidative stresses in the bone microenvironment, are under‐studied [34, 44]. To our knowledge, the combined role of true hypoxia and laminin‐111 in regulating dormancy in estrogen‐receptor positive and triple‐negative breast cancer cells has not been systematically examined in this way. Therefore, this study improves our understanding of how cellular dormancy is regulated in both normoxic and hypoxic conditions. Key molecular players driving hypoxia‐induced dormancy in breast cancer include HIF‐1α‐dependent pathways as well as HIF‐independent regulators such as NRF2, which enhances antioxidant defenses to counteract ROS‐mediated stress [45, 46]. In parallel, hypoxia modulates cell cycle control through downregulation of proliferative markers (e.g., Ki67, CDKs) and upregulation of CDK inhibitors (p16, p27), collectively enforcing a quiescent, survival‐adapted state [47, 48].

In our system, hypoxia‐dependent dormancy required careful optimization of initial cell seeding density. At low density, cells failed to maintain viability under hypoxia, whereas an empirically determined higher density enabled survival with minimal proliferation. Under these optimized conditions, cells remained viable, with no overt cell death, and displayed a Ki‐67–negative, p‐p38high/p‐ERKlow phenotype, consistent with a dormant state as previously defined by the Aguirre‐Ghiso group [12].

MCF‐7 cells entered dormancy in normoxic conditions in the presence of FGF‐2 and fibronectin together, we have used p‐p38 as a marker here, but studies have also highlighted that both FGF‐2 and fibronectin can regulate other dormancy markers such as p21 and p27, which promote growth arrest through Rb dephosphorylation and inhibition of cyclin‐dependent kinases that might be promoting the dormant phenotype [35, 49, 50]. Consistent with this, we observed increased expression of p21 and p27 under normoxic conditions, supporting their role in inducing a dormancy‐like phenotype. In hypoxic conditions, MCF‐7 cells entered into dormancy in the presence of either fibronectin or laminin and activated p‐p38 whilst lowering the levels of p‐ERK to induce dormancy. This was further reinforced by elevated p21 and p27 levels, particularly in fibronectin‐containing conditions under hypoxia. Studies have also shown that hypoxic stress can activate dormancy markers such as p21 via suppression of c‐Myc [51, 52]. Cellular interactions with fibronectin and laminin have also been shown to initiate the pro‐survival PI3K/Akt signaling pathway [35], which may explain how our dormant phenotype was resistant to doxorubicin.

In contrast, MDA‐MB‐231 cells exhibited distinct, subtype‐specific regulation of dormancy. Under normoxic conditions, either fibronectin or laminin alone was sufficient to induce dormancy, consistent with increased p‐p38 signaling. This was accompanied by elevated p21 and p27 expression, suggesting effective induction of cell cycle arrest. In hypoxic conditions, MDA‐MB‐231 cells in the presence of either fibronectin or laminin were highly proliferative in nature and demonstrated a significantly high expression of p‐ERK. However, when FGF‐2 and laminin were present together, MDA‐MB‐231 cells demonstrated dormant behavior. This suggests that hypoxic stress could be involved in regulating dormancy‐associated mechanisms at a molecular level. Furthermore, fibronectin consistently increased p27 levels under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions, but this alone did not lead to dormancy under hypoxia, suggesting that other signals are also required. However, in this study, we have not performed any experiments to define the regulatory mechanisms responsible for dormancy induction for which further investigations are required. This indicates that although p21 and p27 play a role in growth arrest, they may not be sufficient on their own to drive dormancy, particularly in more aggressive cells. The results from our study provide an opportunity for researchers to develop an experimental model targeting the integrin proteins present in these dormant cancer cells, which interact with ECM. This could potentially reverse dormancy and make them susceptible to chemotherapy.

We also demonstrate that cells in a dormancy‐like state exhibit resistance to doxorubicin, a commonly used chemotherapeutic agent. This is consistent with prior reports indicating that dormant or slow‐cycling breast cancer cells are less sensitive to cytotoxic therapies that target proliferating populations [53]. The persistence of viable cells following prolonged drug exposure in our system supports the concept that dormancy contributes to therapy resistance and disease recurrence.

An important observation from our study is that, at the same seeding density, cells grown under normoxic conditions did not enter dormancy but instead remained proliferative. This may be due to increased cell–cell interactions and contact‐mediated signaling, which support continued growth even in the presence of ECM components. Therefore, these conditions likely reflect a distinct proliferative state rather than the absence of dormancy.

Finally, while our study highlights the role of the microenvironment in dormancy induction, it has a few limitations. The lack of in vivo validation and the use of only two cell lines limit generalizability. Additionally, upstream signaling pathways and downstream survival mechanisms were not directly investigated. From a therapeutic perspective, targeting dormant cancer cells remains a significant challenge. Strategies either to maintain dormancy or selectively eliminate dormant cells by disrupting survival signaling pathways (e.g., integrin/FAK/PI3K axis) may hold promise in preventing metastatic relapse.

Conclusion

To conclude, we were able to create culture conditions where specific interactions of ECM components—fibronectin and laminin with FGF‐2 in normoxic and true hypoxic conditions can lead to a dormancy‐like phenotype in breast cancer cell line. The specific combinations leading to a dormancy‐like phenotype were different in the two cell types and differed between normoxia and hypoxia, suggesting that the induction of cellular dormancy is cell and microenvironment context dependent.
