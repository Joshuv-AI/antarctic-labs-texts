A new conceptual framework for PKD1 in ADPKD; integrating DNA structures and inflammation
Authors: Leslyn A Hanakahi, Tara Subrahmanyan, Gregory B Vanden Heuvel, Erik D Larson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn ADPKD renal function is corrupted by the accumulation and growth of fluid-filled cysts. Disruption of the PKD1 gene product, polycystin-1, is the most frequent cause of ADPKD, but the mechanisms that predispose PKD1 to recurrent somatic mutation remain poorly understood. Because current evidence for sequence- and structure-dependent mutational susceptibility is strongest at the human PKD1 locus, we will focus here on mechanisms that may promote somatic PKD1 inactivation. Experimental evidence for polycistin-1 inactivation supports a two-hit pathway, with the first hit being an inherited germline pathogenic mutation in PKD1 and the second hit mutation arising later in a somatic cell to inactivate the gene or lower the gene’s dosage to lift a barrier to cyst initiation. An affected kidney can have thousands of cysts, each of which is a clonal lineage arising from an independent mutational second-hit event. Why human PKD1 is prone to inactivation and why the rodent orthologs escape similar mutagenesis is a mystery that, once solved, promises to provide important insights into the molecular mechanisms governing cyst initiation. A step toward that goal came from the characterization of the guanine-rich sequence architecture of human PKD1 that distinguishes it from rodent Pkd1. These guanine-rich tracts are intrinsically susceptible to oxidative damage and can adopt non-duplex secondary structures such as guanine-quadruplex DNA. Although guanine quadruplexes serve important regulatory functions, both oxidized guanine lesions and guanine quadruplex structures can interfere with faithful DNA replication and repair, thereby increasing mutation risk. Here, we discuss PKD1 mutagenesis in the context of the renal inflammatory microenvironment, integrating established principles of sequence-dependent mutagenesis and guanine-rich DNA structure biology with mechanisms of cyst initiation in ADPKD. This synthesis supports a conceptual model in which intrinsic sequence-dependent mutational susceptibility at the human PKD1 locus may interact with localized inflammatory microenvironments characterized by oxidative stress and epithelial proliferation to contribute to recurrent somatic second-hit formation.Keywords: Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease, Inflammation, G4 DNA, PKD1

Background

Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) is a significant cause of renal morbidity, affecting approximately 1 in 1,000 individuals worldwide [1]. Although cysts develop in utero, many affected individuals are born with structurally normal kidneys and cysts gradually appear and accumulate throughout life, eventually leading to end-stage renal failure [2–4]. Each cyst arises from renal tubular cells and acquires a proliferative, fluid-secretory phenotype, distorting nephron architecture as it enlarges. Cysts and fibrotic tissue replace healthy renal tissue [5], leading to overall decreased kidney function. The expanding lesions cause ischemia and necrosis of surrounding tissues, provoking local inflammatory responses characterized by increased cytokine production and oxidative stress [6–10]. Such inflammatory microenvironments correlate with disease progression and may promote epithelial proliferation or create conditions favorable to additional cyst-initiating events in adjacent tissue [11–13].

The proteins encoded by PKD1 and PKD2—polycystin-1 (PC1) and polycystin-2 (PC2)—form a membrane-bound polycystin complex with many functions, including regulation of intracellular calcium homeostasis and the downstream signaling pathways that restrain cyclic AMP accumulation, limit epithelial proliferation, and preserve normal tubular architecture [14, 15]. The degree of functional polycystin expression—its gene dosage—is recognized as a critical disease determinant [16]. Substantial reductions in polycystin activity disrupt these regulatory pathways, promoting increased proliferation, altered polarity, cAMP elevation, and fluid secretion. These pathogenic features intensify as polycystin levels decrease, explaining why cells with near-complete loss of polycystin function acquire a cyst-initiating phenotype.

Most cases of ADPKD are initiated by heterozygous germline loss-of-function mutations in PKD1, with the remainder arising primarily from mutations in PKD2, although a small percentage of cases arise due to germline mutations in other genes [17]. Cyst formation initiates from a somatic second mutation in the remaining functional allele, further reducing or eliminating polycystin expression within an individual tubular epithelial cell [18–24]. This biallelic loss—or near-complete reduction—of protein function leads to the proliferative, polarity-defective, and fluid-secretory phenotype characteristic of cyst initiation. Each cyst, therefore, represents an independent clonal lineage [21, 22, 25]. The defining paradox of ADPKD is that such somatic mutations occur repeatedly and independently within the same kidney, even among individuals carrying the same inherited mutation [26–28]. Why PKD1 is so frequently and specifically targeted for somatic mutation, and why the number and timing of cyst-initiating events vary so widely between individuals, remain central unresolved questions in the field. Although somatic second-hit formation occurs in both PKD1 and PKD2-associated disease, here we focus primarily on PKD1.

The two-hit paradigm and the central problem in ADPKD

The genetic basis for ADPKD echoes the classical two-hit paradigm originally described for familial cancer genes, most notably RB1. It provides a foundation for understanding how cysts arise through focal, sequential loss of gene function. The two-hit model was first articulated by Alfred Knudson to explain the differing patterns of familial and sporadic retinoblastoma [29]. Retinoblastoma is a childhood cancer caused by inactivating mutations in the RB1 tumor suppressor gene, which encodes pRB, a key regulator of the G1/S transition. Loss of pRB removes an essential restraint on cell-cycle progression. Knudson observed that familial cases occurred earlier, were more often bilateral, and followed an incidence pattern consistent with the requirement for two successive mutational events—an inherited germline mutation followed by a somatic second hit in the remaining allele [29]. This model has been subsequently extended to many autosomal dominant cancer-predisposition syndromes and other proliferative disorders involving the loss of tumor suppressors. ADPKD represents a nonmalignant but striking application of this paradigm: the disease manifests as many renal cysts arising from independent somatic second hits [14].

Here, we explore emerging evidence and conceptual models that can explain how second-hit inactivation of PKD1 occurs, drawing on established principles of DNA damage, repair, and sequence-dependent mutagenesis.

Mouse models and the origin of the somatic second hit in humans

Mouse models have been indispensable for defining the cellular and genetic requirements for cyst initiation in PKD. Germline Pkd1-null mice were embryonic lethal, demonstrating that complete loss of polycystin-1 function is incompatible with normal development [30–32]. In contrast, heterozygote rodent models are viable and initially phenotypically normal [33], indicating that a single functional Pkd1 allele provides sufficient polycystin activity—an observation consistent with the human heterozygous state. Conditional deletion studies then showed that cysts form only when Pkd1 is inactivated in proliferative or incompletely differentiated tubular epithelial cells [34–36]. These findings established two foundational principles: that a somatic second hit is required for cyst initiation, and that proliferation (DNA replication) makes the mutation permanent and permits clonal expansion. Despite these insights, mouse models have not yet recapitulated spontaneous somatic second hits characteristic of human ADPKD. Even in sensitized genetic backgrounds or injury-induced proliferative states, mice do not develop the independent cyst-initiating events routinely observed in human kidneys [37]. The striking difference in spontaneous PKD1/Pkd1 inactivation between humans and mice is not explained by differences in their overall mutation rates. In fact, mice have significantly higher somatic and germline mutation rates than humans, even after accounting for the greater lifetime number of cell divisions in humans [38], yet mouse Pkd1 rarely undergoes two-hit inactivation. This contrast raises a central mechanistic question: Why do human, but not mouse, kidneys generate repeated, spontaneous somatic mutations in PKD1 throughout life?

Comparative genomic studies may offer a plausible explanation. The human PKD1 locus contains unusually long, densely guanine-rich sequence tracts and other motifs associated with elevated mutation levels that are attributable to DNA sequence and structure [39–48]. Those features are present but much lower in the murine Pkd1 ortholog [40, 45]. As a result, the human locus may be intrinsically more susceptible to endogenous mutagenesis. Correspondingly, the mouse gene may simply lack the sequence-encoded structural features that promote spontaneous second-hit formation. Interpreting mouse studies in this context clarifies their relevance: they accurately define the cellular requirements for making mutations permanent and heritable (i.e., loss of polycystin function in a proliferative state), but they may not model some critical endogenous processes that cause mutations in human PKD1. Guanine-rich and repetitive sequences, such as those found within human PKD1, can adopt alternative non-duplex conformations that alter local DNA geometry and interfere with DNA metabolism [49]. For instance, structures like H-DNA, R-loops, guanine quadruplex (G4) DNAs, or other non-canonical structures can form during replication or transcription, when DNA is transiently denatured [50, 51]. Of particular interest here is G4 DNA (Fig. 1A), a structure that is stabilized by guanine-guanine pairings to create a four-stranded structure within or between strands. G4 DNA functionally contributes to gene expression [52–55], but unscheduled G4 DNAs cause mutagenesis [49, 56–58]. The unusual sequence architecture of the human PKD1 gene may predispose the locus to frequent independent somatic mutations that could contribute to reduced functional polycystin-1 expression and thereby promote cyst formation in ADPKD. This intrinsic susceptibility offers a plausible explanation for why human kidneys, but not mouse kidneys, accumulate the large cyst burdens characteristic of the disease.

Evidence for recurrent, locus-specific somatic mutation in human ADPKD

Human cyst sequencing studies have established that ADPKD cysts arise from a unique somatic mutation in PKD1 or PKD2. Within a single kidney, hundreds to thousands of cysts may form, each representing an independent clonal lineage [21, 22, 25]. This pattern of repeated but unrelated mutational events has been recognized for more than two decades and is now firmly supported by whole-genome and whole-exome sequencing showing that somatic variants are overwhelmingly concentrated within PKD1 and PKD2 [17, 20, 23]. These findings reinforce the view that cyst initiation reflects recurrent, locus-specific mutagenesis rather than generalized DNA repair failure. Human pathological analyses further suggest that local tissue context influences where somatic mutations arise and become stably propagated. Cysts frequently emerge in regions exhibiting chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and epithelial proliferation, while adjacent non-cystic tissue remains relatively quiescent [11–13, 59–62]. Although inflammation was once considered a downstream consequence of cyst expansion, accumulating human evidence indicates that persistent inflammatory microenvironments may actually promote the initiation of new cysts within localized areas of the kidney [14, 63, 64]. Combined with alternative DNA structures in human PKD1, this may exacerbate DNA damage risk (Fig. 1B).

Mouse conditional-deletion studies clarify the cellular context needed for somatic mutations to become permanent, even though they do not model the endogenous mutational mechanism. In mice, loss of Pkd1 or Pkd2 initiates cyst formation only when gene deletion occurs during periods of epithelial cell growth [36]. In fully differentiated, non-proliferating cells, loss of these genes rarely induces cysts [36]. These results align with the fundamental requirement for DNA replication to convert DNA lesions into heritable mutations and help explain why somatic second hits in human ADPKD appear in proliferative epithelial contexts. Taken together, these observations highlight a consistent pattern in human disease: recurrent, locus-specific mutations arise in dividing epithelial cells within inflamed, oxidatively stressed microenvironments that largely target PKD1. This pattern raises a central mechanistic question: why this particular human gene, and why so frequently? We addressed this question by considering how intrinsic features of the human PKD1 sequence architecture may interact with oxidative stress and DNA replication to promote somatic mutation.

Sequence-based vulnerabilities of the human PKD1 locus

Human PKD1 contains unusually long and numerous guanine-rich sequence tracts [43, 65], making the locus particularly susceptible to two endogenous processes that can introduce mutations during DNA replication: the oxidation of guanine and formation of G4 structures (Fig. 1A). Each process can disrupt accurate copying of DNA in distinct ways, and both are relevant to tissues experiencing oxidative stress and ongoing proliferation, as is the case in ADPKD kidneys. Together, these mechanisms provide a possible explanation for why PKD1 repeatedly acquires independent somatic mutations over the course of a lifetime.

In ADPKD, oxidative stress is a hallmark of cystic kidneys, where inflammatory signaling elevates the levels of reactive oxygen species [66, 67]. Consistent with this oxidative environment, transcriptomic analyses of human ADPKD kidneys revealed increased expression of genes involved in cellular responses to oxidative DNA damage, and cyst-lining epithelial cells displayed elevated DNA-damage–associated signaling [68]. Further, human ADPKD epithelial cells also showed increased resistance to hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), a commonly used oxidative stressor [69]. Guanine is the most oxidation-sensitive of the DNA bases, which means that guanine-rich regions—like those abundant in human PKD1 [45, 65]—would be especially prone to mutagenesis. Once oxidized to form 8-oxoguanine, the base can mispair during DNA replication and result in base substitutions when the lesion is copied instead of being corrected beforehand [70]. Importantly, the murine Pkd1 ortholog does not have a guanine-cytosine bias like human PKD1 [40, 45], thus considering sequence content alone the human gene is intrinsically more vulnerable to oxidative modification and its mutagenic consequences.

During DNA replication, the guanine-rich sequence architecture of human PKD1 creates opportunities for secondary DNA structures to form that can interfere with DNA copying and promote mutagenesis. Replication of G/C-rich regions transiently exposes guanine-rich DNA sequences capable of folding into G4 structures—alternative DNA conformations stabilized by stacked guanine tetrads (Fig. 1A). Human PKD1 resides within a highly G/C-rich region of chromosome 16 and contains numerous guanine-rich tracts distributed across the locus, providing repeated opportunities for transient G4 formation during DNA replication [45, 46, 65]. Alternative DNA conformations, such as G4 structures, can interfere with DNA replication by altering local geometry, destabilizing the alignment of the template and nascent strand, or causing the polymerase to pause at the structure and reinitiate at a downstream position [49, 51, 58, 71]. When copying resumes out of register, the intervening sequence is skipped, producing insertions or deletions whose size depends on the distance between the pause site and realignment point. Because G4–forming tracts are distributed across PKD1 [45], such events can arise at multiple positions throughout the gene rather than at a single hotspot. Thus, G4-associated misalignment during DNA replication can generate repeated, independent somatic mutations across the PKD1 locus [72]. Experimental observations support this model: unusual sequence elements within the human PKD1 locus have been shown to impede DNA polymerase progression [47], and human ADPKD kidneys display increased cellular signals associated with disrupted DNA replication, specifically within cyst-lining epithelial cells [69]. Together with the mutational consequences of guanine oxidation described above, these findings highlight sequence-based vulnerabilities within the human PKD1 locus that promote the accumulation of somatic mutations through direct disruption of DNA replication.

In addition to directly disrupting DNA replication, G4 can also promote mutagenesis when it arises during transcription and interferes with replication fork progression [50, 51]. During transcription through G/C-rich sequences, strand separation transiently exposes guanine-rich DNA capable of folding into G4 structures. As discussed in the previous paragraph, these structures alone can interfere with DNA replication. A feature unique to transcription is the formation of RNA–DNA hybrids known as R-loops, in which the nascent RNA anneals to the C-rich template strand while the displaced G-rich strand remains single-stranded. During transcription of a C-rich template strand, this exposed G-rich strand can fold into G4 structures while the template strand participates in the RNA–DNA hybrid that forms the R-loop. Experimental work has shown that failure to resolve G4/R-loop structures promotes conflicts between transcription and DNA replication [73], disrupting replication fork progression and creating conditions capable of causing DNA damage and compromising faithful DNA replication [74]. This mechanism may be particularly relevant for PKD1, which is highly transcribed in tubular epithelia [32] and contains repeats capable of forming G4 and R-loop structures [45, 46].

Guanine oxidation and G4 formation do not operate independently; rather, they reinforce one another in ways that increase mutational risk specifically at guanine-rich regions of PKD1 [75]. Oxidized guanines weaken local base pairing, making strand separation easier and thereby increasing both the likelihood that exposed segments will fold into G4 structures and the likelihood that those structures will persist once formed [76, 77]. When a G4 structure forms, oxidized guanines within or directly adjacent to the folded region become less accessible to repair than in standard duplex DNA, allowing these damaged bases to persist until DNA replication [78, 79]. Persistent oxidized bases are more likely to be copied into mutations [70], while persistent G4 structures increase the chance of misalignment and deletion formation [71]. Oxidative stress due to the inflammation characteristic of ADPKD amplifies both mutational mechanisms—oxidative guanine damage and G4 formation—thereby increasing their combined contribution to somatic inactivation of PKD1 [80].

When oxidized guanines and G4 structures persist under these conditions, they can also recruit enzymes that bind to non-canonical DNA bases and structures and act on DNA in ways that introduce additional forms of DNA damage. In 8-oxoguanine repair, DNA structure formation can alter the fidelity of repair, increasing the risk of mutations [80]. In addition, when G4 structures form, the opposite C-rich strands are left unpaired or occupied by RNA in an RNA-DNA hybrid (R-loop), and vulnerable to various DNA-targeting enzymes that can result in DNA breaks in the immediate vicinity of the G4 structure [51, 81–83]. While these enzyme-mediated events are normally DNA-repair intermediates, they create short-lived forms of DNA damage that represent independent opportunities for sequence alteration. Imperfect repair of these intermediates, particularly when encountered during subsequent rounds of replication in proliferating epithelia, increases the likelihood that enzyme-mediated damage is converted into a stable somatic mutation.

The mechanisms described above predict a mutational spectrum that includes base substitutions arising from replication across oxidized guanine, small insertions or deletions caused by local misalignment during replication around G4 structures, and occasional larger deletions resulting from more extensive downstream realignment [49]. Sequencing of human ADPKD cyst epithelia reveals a mixture of somatic variants that is consistent with these predicted mutational mechanisms, with the majority being small or single-base alterations [17, 20, 23, 84]. Moreover, mutations in PKD1 are distributed widely across the gene rather than clustering at a single hotspot [84–86]. This pattern is consistent with the presence of multiple guanine-rich tracts capable of forming transient mutagenic structures during replication. The observed heterogeneity of somatic mutations therefore supports the view that PKD1’s intrinsic sequence composition, rather than a site-specific or enzymatic mechanism, drives recurrent inactivation.

Human PKD1 has about 10 times more G4 sequences than the murine Pkd1 gene, providing a plausible explanation for why spontaneous second hits are common in humans but not in mice [45]. In ADPKD kidneys, the elevated oxidative stress can increase guanine oxidation [66, 87], while ongoing epithelial proliferation supplies the replication cycles necessary for both oxidation-induced mispairing and G4-associated misalignment events to be captured as heritable mutations. The combination of intrinsic sequence susceptibility, oxidative conditions, and repeated rounds of replication yields a biological environment in which multiple independent somatic inactivation events can arise over time. These mechanistic features align with hallmark characteristics of human ADPKD, including the clonal origin of individual cysts and the broad mutational heterogeneity across the PKD1 gene [20–23].

Reframing the cystogenesis mechanism in human ADPKD

The classical two-hit model describes ADPKD as a process in which a germline loss-of-function mutation in PKD1 or PKD2 is followed by a somatic second hit in a single tubular epithelial cell. This paradigm correctly establishes that biallelic inactivation is required for cyst initiation but does not fully explain several defining features of human disease: the disproportionately high frequency of somatic mutations in PKD1 relative to the rest of the genome, the variable expressivity among individuals carrying the same inherited mutation [26, 88], or the tendency for cysts to arise in spatial clusters [89]. Considered together, those observations suggest that second-hit formation in human ADPKD may not be purely stochastic but instead reflect biological factors that influence how frequently PKD1 becomes somatically inactivated.

The sequence composition of human PKD1, which contains numerous guanine-rich tracts capable of adopting transient non-canonical structures, such as G4 DNA, presents a potential intrinsic source of mutational susceptibility. These features are much less prominent in the murine Pkd1 ortholog [40] and may help explain why spontaneous somatic inactivation is readily observed in human kidneys but rarely in mouse models [33]. The G-rich architecture of PKD1 provides opportunities for guanine oxidation, mispairing during replication, and occasional misalignment events during transient structure formation. Collectively, these properties offer a plausible foundation for the unusually high rate of somatic mutation observed in the human locus.

Cysts generate a localized inflammatory and oxidative milieu that can increase the likelihood of mutational events in neighboring epithelium. Oxidative stress raises the rate of guanine oxidation and may stabilize G4 structures or prolong their persistence, while inflammatory signaling can support epithelial proliferation, creating more opportunities for lesions to be encountered during replication. Although the quantitative contributions of these processes remain to be defined, inflammation represents a potential amplifier of the intrinsic mutational tendencies encoded by the PKD1 locus [88, 89].

The observations discussed above can be integrated into a conceptual model in which intrinsic PKD1 sequence architecture, epithelial proliferation, and the local inflammatory microenvironment may cooperate to promote recurrent somatic second-hit formation in human ADPKD. A rare, sporadic somatic mutation in PKD1 produces the first cyst. The unusual sequence architecture of the human PKD1 locus, including its guanine-rich and structure-prone regions, may increase the susceptibility of this specific locus to mutagenesis during replication and oxidative stress. The oxidative and proliferative conditions associated with cyst-associated inflammation in ADPKD kidneys [90] could then act on nearby tubules to increase mutational susceptibility at the PKD1 locus, thereby promoting somatic events that further reduce functional polycystin-1 expression. Over time, these localized effects could contribute to the spatial clustering of cysts frequently observed in human ADPKD. This model may explain why the mutagenic potential of the human PKD1 locus is realized predominantly in ADPKD kidneys, where chronic oxidative stress and epithelial proliferation provide a permissive environment for recurrent somatic second-hit formation.

Somatic mutations identified in human ADPKD cyst epithelia include base substitutions, small insertions and deletions, and occasional larger deletions. These lesion types are the same classes of mutations known to arise from guanine oxidation and from replication interference caused by transient G4 structures, mechanisms that have been well documented in other systems. Importantly, the somatic mutations observed in ADPKD are distributed broadly across PKD1, a pattern consistent with the dispersed distribution of G/C-rich sequences [42, 43, 47] with potential to form G4 structures throughout the gene [45, 72]. The mutational processes discussed here—including replication errors and enzyme-mediated DNA processing at oxidized or structured DNA—can produce a broad range of allelic outcomes, from silent variants to missense substitutions with reduced protein activity, to truncating mutations, to larger deletions (Fig. 2). Despite their diversity, these outcomes share a common biological consequence: they diminish the amount of functional polycystin-1 available to the cell. In this respect, these mutagenic mechanisms are not alternatives to the gene dosage framework but instead provide a possible explanation for how gene dosage becomes progressively reduced in individual epithelial cells and thereby contributes to cyst initiation.

Conclusions

The observations summarized above support a conceptual framework that integrates established principles of DNA damage, sequence-dependent mutagenesis, and guanine-rich DNA structure biology with current models of ADPKD cystogenesis to explain how repeated somatic inactivation of PKD1 may occur in human disease. First, the G-rich, structure-prone sequence architecture of human PKD1, combined with its high transcriptional activity, may create a baseline vulnerability to sporadic mutation. These intrinsic features could allow a rare, isolated somatic second hit to occur even in the absence of inflammation, providing one plausible route for the initial cyst to form. Second, once that cyst forms, the oxidative stress and epithelial proliferation associated with local inflammatory responses may heighten mutational susceptibility at the PKD1 locus in nearby tubular cells, potentially accelerating the rate at which new cysts arise over time. This model extends current frameworks of ADPKD cystogenesis by incorporating potential roles for sequence-dependent mutational susceptibility at the human PKD1 locus. Direct experimental investigation of these mechanisms may help clarify how sequence-dependent mutagenesis contributes to human ADPKD pathophysiology and its divergence from murine models.

One important consideration is that while the extensive guanine-rich sequences in PKD1 are present in every cell, relatively few second hits reduce polycystin-1 expression enough to give rise to the renal cysts in ADPKD. Moreover, the second hit must be combined with the inherited pathogenic allele, which is present in every cell. Although the cystic phenotype can be striking, these multiple requirements mean that the absolute number of successful second-hit events is remarkably small. This small number of successful second-hit events must be considered in the context of the enormous number of renal cells within the human kidney, which is approximately 1 million nephrons in a human kidney (ranging from 200,000 to 2.5 million), each composed of thousands of cells [91]. Because each cyst originates from a single cell, even extensive cystic disease reflects only thousands of cyst-initiating second-hit events. Similarly, in individuals without ADPKD, cyst formation would require independent inactivating mutations in both PKD1 alleles within the same cell, making such events rare.

The perspective offered here is integrated from the existing literature and suggests new opportunities to experimentally investigate and potentially target the mechanisms underlying cyst-initiating second hits in ADPKD. In this context, oxidative DNA damage and alternative DNA structures within human PKD1 emerge as possible mechanistic contributors to mutational susceptibility, perhaps even explaining simple cysts in otherwise normal kidneys. That appealing model awaits validation, but the mutagenic processes described here may represent experimentally tractable targets for reducing cyst-initiating somatic mutagenesis. Indeed, experimental studies have shown that lowering oxidative stress can attenuate cyst progression and improve disease-associated phenotypes in ADPKD models [90]. Small molecules capable of modulating G4-DNA structures may likewise offer new approaches for manipulating sequence-dependent mutational susceptibility at the PKD1 locus [92]. However, directly investigating PKD1 mutagenesis mechanisms in ADPKD remains experimentally challenging. Mutational analysis of PKD1 is complicated by extensive sequence repeats and genomic duplications [93], while the murine Pkd1 locus appears relatively resistant to spontaneous second-hit formation [33]. Humanized systems, including transfer of human G4-rich sequence elements into the more tractable mouse locus, may help overcome some of these experimental limitations. Although the sequence-based mutagenesis model developed here is primarily focused on PKD1, an important unresolved question is whether analogous mechanisms contribute to somatic PKD2 inactivation in ADPKD. Defining the extent to which sequence architecture, oxidative stress, and local tissue environment influence PKD2 mutagenesis will be important for determining how broadly these concepts apply across ADPKD genetics. Ultimately, understanding how sequence architecture and local tissue environment influence somatic second-hit formation may reveal new opportunities to limit cyst progression in ADPKD.
