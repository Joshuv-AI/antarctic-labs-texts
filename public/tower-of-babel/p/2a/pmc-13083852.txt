The AHEAD-NLR score: a novel inflammatory-comorbidity integrated tool for predicting long-term mortality in patients with heart failure after acute myocardial infarction
Authors: Guoli Lin, Caizhi Dai, Wen Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe AHEAD score (Atrial fibrillation, Hemoglobin, Elderly age, Abnormal renal function, and Diabetes) has proven valuable for risk stratification in acute heart failure. However, its prognostic utility specifically in the distinct and high-risk population of patients who develop heart failure after an acute myocardial infarction (AMI) remains to be clarified. Current risk assessment tools in this setting often underrepresent the significant role of systemic inflammation, a key driver of adverse remodeling and poor outcomes post-AMI. To address this gap, we incorporated the Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), a robust and readily available marker of inflammatory response. The modified AHEAD-NLR score aims to integrate established clinical risk factors with inflammatory activity, thereby addressing a critical limitation of prior tools and potentially offering a more comprehensive prognostic model. A total of 840 patients with heart failure after AMI were enrolled in this study (age 62.55 ± 11.89 years, 81.90% men), with a median follow-up duration of 954 days. The predictive efficacy of the AHEAD score for all-cause mortality was evaluated using Cox regression analysis. The mean AHEAD score of study was 1.05 ± 1.04. After adjusting for age, gender, red cell distribution width (RDW), uric acid, serum potassium, D-dimer, Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and Left ventricular ejection fraction(LVEF), the AHEAD score was still significantly associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality (hazard ratio and 95% confidence interval: 1.270, 1.019–1.582). In addition, when we constructed a new score, AHEAD-NLR, by incorporating the NLR (≥ 8.16) into the AHEAD score, the net reclassification improvement for predicting all-cause mortality was increased by 19.9%. AHEAD score is an independent predictor of all-cause mortality in patients with HF after AMI. Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a severe and terminal stage of cardiovascular diseases, characterized by high disability and mortality rates. With accelerating global population aging, the prevalence of heart failure continues to rise, making it a major public health challenge1. Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is one of the most common and important causes of heart failure worldwide2. Although timely revascularization has significantly reduced AMI mortality, the incidence of heart failure secondary to AMI remains high, ranging from 14 to 36% across different studies, with the highest risk occurring in the first few months post-infarction3–5.

The pathophysiology of post-AMI heart failure is complex. Beyond myocardial necrosis and mechanical complications, the inflammatory response triggered by cardiomyocyte death plays a pivotal role in disease progression6. Necrotic cardiomyocytes release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), activating innate immunity and triggering robust inflammation. Sustained inflammatory responses expand tissue damage, impair cardiac function, and ultimately promote heart failure7. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), a cost-effective and widely available inflammatory biomarker, reflects the balance between pro-inflammatory neutrophils and anti-inflammatory lymphocytes and has demonstrated significant prognostic value in both MI and heart failure populations8–10.

Several comorbidities contribute to the development and progression of post-AMI heart failure. Atrial fibrillation (AF) increases susceptibility to heart failure following MI11,12. Anemia independently predicts adverse short- and long-term outcomes13, with pathophysiological mechanisms involving hypoxia-induced myocardial damage and a pro-inflammatory state14. Advanced age reduces cardiomyocyte number and function, portending poorer prognosis15. Renal impairment, resulting from reduced cardiac output diminishing renal perfusion, nearly doubles HF risk with each 0.4 mg/dL increase in serum creatinine16. Diabetes mellitus (DM), affecting approximately 30% of hospitalized AMI patients, promotes thrombogenesis and exacerbates coronary vasoconstriction, doubling mortality risk17–19.

The AHEAD scoring system (A: Atrial fibrillation; H: Hemoglobin; E: Elderly; A: Abnormal renal parameters; D: Diabetes) is a simple clinical prognostic model for patients with acute heart failure. Each 1-point increase in the AHEAD score is associated with a 10% increase in annual mortality20. However, this score was developed in general acute heart failure populations and does not account for the inflammatory cascade that plays a prominent role in post-AMI HF outcomes. This gap restricts its predictive accuracy in the specific context of post-infarction heart failure.

The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) was incorporated into the AHEAD scoring system, thereby forming a new risk stratification tool (referred to as AHEAD-NLR). This improved tool takes into account both the burden of chronic comorbidities and the dynamic, inflammation-driven pathophysiological state reflected by NLR. This dual assessment aligns well with the known multi-factor pathogenesis of post-myocardial infarction heart failure, thus suggesting that it will perform better in prognosis assessment than AHEAD.

The prognostic value of the AHEAD score in patients with heart failure after acute myocardial infarction has not been fully studied. Moreover, to date, no research has attempted to optimize this specific population by integrating the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) into the clinical score. Therefore, the main objective of this study is to develop and validate the AHEAD-NLR score and evaluate whether it can provide superior long-term all-cause mortality prognostic performance in patients with AMI secondary to HF compared to the original AHEAD score. We aim to fill this critical gap through rigorous evaluation of the comprehensive model and ultimately aim to provide a more accurate and pathophysiological-based prognostic tool for more precise risk stratification.

Methods

Ethical approval

This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Putian University. Given the retrospective nature of the research and the use of anonymized data, the requirement for obtaining informed consent from individual patients was waived by the same Ethics Committee.

Study population

Consecutive patients hospitalized with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) complicated by heart failure (HF) in the Department of Cardiovascular Medicine, Affiliated Hospital of Putian University, between January 2017 and January 2022, were screened for inclusion. AMI included ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) and non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI). AMI diagnosis adhered to the criteria outlined in the 2023 ESC Guidelines for the Management of Acute Coronary Syndromes21. The diagnostic criteria for heart failure after acute myocardial infarction included: the presence of clinical symptoms of heart failure (dyspnea) and/or signs of HF (pulmonary rales, jugular vein distension, bilateral lower extremity edema, pulmonary edema) during hospitalization after acute myocardial infarction, along with NT-proBNP ≥ 125 pg/ml, and/or cardiac color Doppler ultrasound indicating abnormalities in cardiac structure and/or function22. Patients with missing data on renal function or hemoglobin, or those lost to follow-up, were excluded. A total of 979 patients met the inclusion criteria, among whom 85 were lost to follow-up and 54 had missing data. Finally, 840 subjects with complete hematological, biochemical, and echocardiographic data were included.

Data collection

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics (including age, gender, hypertension, diabetes, and HF status) were retrieved from the hospital’s electronic medical record system.

Endpoint outcome

The primary endpoint was all-cause mortality, assessed over a maximum follow-up period of 4 years.

Statistical analysis and model performance

Continuous variables were expressed as mean and standard deviation, and categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages. For the comparison of continuous variables between groups, one-way analysis of variance or Kruskal–Wallis H test was used as appropriate, and the chi-square test was used for categorical variables. Survival analysis based on scores was performed using the Kaplan–Meier method, and comparisons were made using the log-rank test. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were constructed to evaluate the hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CI) of the AHEAD score for all-cause mortality. After confirming that there were overall differences in survival rates among the groups of the AHEAD score, we conducted post hoc pairwise comparisons to further clarify which specific groups had significant differences. Based on the Kaplan–Meier survival curve, we performed Log-rank tests on all possible pairings of groups (a total of 15 pairs). To control the risk of Type I error due to multiple comparisons, we adjusted the P-values using the Bonferroni correction method, setting the adjusted significance level as α* = 0.0033 (0.05/15). A two-tailed test was used, with the significance level set at 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS software version 22.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL) and R software.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

The final cohort comprised 840 patients (mean age 62.55 ± 11.89 years; 81.90% male). Comorbidity prevalence was high: hypertension (61.90%), diabetes mellitus (41.42%), and atrial fibrillation (5.95%). The mean AHEAD score for the overall population was 1.05 ± 1.04. As shown in Table 2, a higher AHEAD score was associated with more severe clinical impairment and multi‑system dysfunction.Patients with higher scores exhibited significantly worse cardiac function, as indicated by lower LVEF and higher NT-proBNP levels. Regarding renal function, while creatinine and BUN tended to increase with score, the most severe group (score 5) showed a slight variation in creatinine levels, possibly due to sample size limitations. Metabolic and inflammatory profiles also deteriorated, manifested as lower hemoglobin, higher uric acid, and an increased neutrophil‑to‑lymphocyte ratio (NLR). Regarding pharmacological therapy, higher scores were associated with significantly greater use of diuretics and β-blockers, whereas the prescription rate of renin‑angiotensin system inhibitors (RASi) did not differ significantly across groups. These findings indicate that the AHEAD score effectively identifies patients with greater severity in terms of cardiac load, end‑organ injury, and systemic inflammatory response.

Prognostic values of AHEAD score

During a median follow-up period of 964.5 days (IQR 598.5–1350.5 days), 131 patients (15.59%) died. Kaplan–Meier survival curve analysis showed that with the increase of AHEAD score, the mortality rate of the study population during the 4-year follow-up period increased significantly (Fig. 1). In the univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis, the AHEAD score emerged as a robust predictor of all-cause mortality (hazard ratio [HR] = 2.21, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.83–2.67). Additionally, a panel of clinical and laboratory biomarkers were identified to be significantly correlated with the risk of death in the study cohort.

Notably, higher left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) (HR = 0.93, 95% CI 0.90–0.95) and elevated hemoglobin levels (HR = 0.98, 95% CI 0.96–0.99) were associated with a reduced risk of all-cause mortality, highlighting the crucial role of preserved cardiac systolic function and adequate oxygen-carrying capacity in improving patient prognosis. In contrast, elevated levels of biomarkers linked to inflammation, hypercoagulability, and metabolic imbalance-including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) (HR = 1.07, 95% CI 1.06–1.09), D-dimer (HR = 1.18, 95% CI 1.10–1.27), and serum potassium (HR = 2.00, 95% CI 1.19–3.36)-were significantly correlated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality. These findings underscore the adverse prognostic impacts of systemic inflammation, enhanced thrombotic potential, and electrolyte disturbances in this patient population.As an indicator of cardiac function in acute myocardial infarction patients,a higher Killp class signifies worse cardiac performance. Compared with Killp-I, Killp-II (HR = 2.453), Killp-III (HR = 10.733), and Killp-IV (HR = 11.402) were all associated with a significantly increased risk of all-cause mortality. Patients with STEMI had a significantly higher mortality risk than those with NSTEMI (HR = 1.533), suggesting a relatively worse prognosis for ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction.As coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) was not available at our center, primary percutaneous coronary intervention (pPCI) was compared with medication therapy alone. The results showed that pPCI significantly reduced the risk of all-cause mortality (HR = 0.639), supporting the adoption of this interventional approach for eligible patients to improve outcomes (Table 3).

After adjusting for age, gender, RDW, uric acid, serum potassium, D-dimer, NLR, and LVEF, the AHEAD score remained associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality in the population (1.270, 1.019–1.582) (Table 4). However, the overall test could not specify which groups had differences. Therefore, we conducted post-hoc pairwise comparisons after Bonferroni correction (Table S1). The analysis showed that there was a significant difference in survival rates between the low AHEAD score group (0–2 points) and the medium–high score group (3–5 points) (the majority of comparisons had corrected P < 0.0033), while the differences within the same risk level (such as 0 vs. 1, or 3 vs. 4) did not reach statistical significance. Specifically, the difference between scores of 2 and 3 was at the borderline level after correction (corrected P = 0.060), but this trend still supported that ‘3 points’ might be an important risk demarcation point.

Based on the analysis of the multivariate Cox proportional hazards model, the NLR level is independently associated with clinical outcomes. To determine the optimal cutoff value of NLR for predicting all-cause mortality, we performed ROC curve analysis. The area under the curve (AUC) of NLR for predicting all-cause mortality was 0.680 (95% CI 0.608–0.753). The optimal cutoff value determined by the Youden index was 8.06, with a sensitivity of 52.2% and a specificity of 78.6%. Notably, this cutoff value is very close to the previously reported value of 8.16 in the literature10. Given the minimal numerical difference and the high clinical concordance between these two values, we elected to retain the published cut-off of 8.16. We combined the NLR index with the AHEAD scoring system to construct the AHEAD-NLR index. Table 5 provides a direct comparison of the predictive performance between the AHEAD and AHEAD-NLR models.The likelihood ratio test confirmed that the AHEAD-NLR model had a significantly better goodness-of-fit than the original AHEAD model (χ2 = 19.92, P = 0.002). This finding was further supported by the lower Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) of the AHEAD-NLR model (791.48) compared with the AHEAD model (811.41), indicating improved model parsimony and predictive accuracy. Compared with the AHEAD score alone, the AHEAD-NLR score yielded a significant net reclassification improvement (NRI) of 0.261 (95% CI 0.108–0.413; P = 0.007), indicating that 26.1% of patients were more accurately reclassified into their correct risk strata. The integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) was 0.042 (95% CI 0.009–0.074; P = 0.007), reflecting a meaningful enhancement in the model’s discriminatory capacity. The C-index also increased significantly by 0.036 (95% CI 0.011–0.060; P = 0.005), further confirming the superior discriminative ability of the AHEAD-NLR model.These results demonstrate that the incorporation of NLR into the AHEAD score significantly enhances its predictive performance for all-cause mortality.

Discussion

In this study cohort, we first verified the correlation between the AHEAD score and the long-term prognosis of patients with heart failure after acute myocardial infarction (AMI); More importantly, we have demonstrated that the newly developed AHEAD-NLR score—incorporating NLR into the original AHEAD score framework—achieves a more powerful prognostic stratification than the AHEAD score alone. The AHEAD score maintains a consistent relationship with mortality. Data indicate that each 1-point increase in the AHEAD score corresponds to a 21% higher risk of death (HR 1.21) over 4 years.Our previous research indicated that in patients with acute myocardial infarction, an elevation in the NLR index is associated with an increased risk of death10. Given that NLR is related to clinical outcomes and is independent of the AHEAD score, we incorporated the NLR index to construct the AHEAD-NLR score. In terms of improving the risk stratification of patients with heart failure after acute myocardial infarction, the AHEAD-NLR index performs significantly better than the AHEAD index by correctly reclassifying 19.9% of the study subjects into the all-cause mortality group, and the AHEAD-NLR score demonstrates superior predictive efficacy compared with the AHEAD score.The pairwise comparisons after Bonferroni correction indicated that the discriminatory power of this score was mainly reflected in differentiating the low-risk (0–2 points) group from the medium–high-risk (3–5 points) group. This finding has significant clinical implications, suggesting that clinicians can consider ‘3 points’ as a practical risk threshold for quickly identifying patients who require enhanced monitoring or intervention. Moreover, further subdivision of the score within the same risk category (such as within the medium–high-risk group) did not result in significant differences in survival prognosis in this cohort, which helps simplify the clinical decision-making process.

Although clinical risk scores exist for predicting mortality in hospitalized heart failure (HF) patients—such as the Acute Decompensated Heart Failure National Registry (ADHERE) risk score23 and the Cardiac and Comorbid Conditions HF (3C-HF) risk score24, but their clinical utility remains limited. These scores incorporate continuous variables requiring calculators or flowcharts, compromising practical bedside application.Spinar et al. developed the AHEAD score, a simplified risk stratification tool based on comorbidities to estimate short-and long-term prognosis in patients hospitalized with acute heart failure20. Notably, no dedicated model currently predicts all-cause mortality specifically in post-myocardial infarction heart failure patients. An accessible mortality prediction tool for this population could facilitate point-of-care clinical decision-making, ultimately improving healthcare quality.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design inherently introduces the risk of selection bias and residual confounding. Although we adjusted for multiple potential confounders in the regression models, unmeasured or unknown factors may still affect the observed associations. Additionally, reliance on existing medical records and the exclusion of patients with missing baseline data could further limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, for clinical feasibility considerations, certain established predictors of acute heart failure (AHF) prognosis—such as active cancer and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease25 were not incorporated into the prognostic model. Third, the AHEAD-NLR score has not undergone external validation in independent cohorts. Internal validation, while informative, cannot fully address the potential for overfitting or ensure that the score performs consistently across different clinical settings, patient populations, or healthcare systems. External validation is essential to confirm the robustness and generalizability of the AHEAD‑NLR score before it can be recommended for routine clinical use.Finally, our conclusions are based solely on the data available from this study, and the prognostic performance of the AHEAD‑NLR score should be interpreted with caution. The score represents a promising tool for risk stratification in patients with acute heart failure, but further prospective and external validation studies are needed to confirm its clinical value and to support its integration into routine clinical practice.

Conclusion

In summary, the AHEAD score demonstrated favorable performance in predicting long-term mortality in heart failure patients following acute myocardial infarction. Furthermore, the AHEAD-NLR score may further enhance risk stratification capabilities beyond the foundational AHEAD score.
