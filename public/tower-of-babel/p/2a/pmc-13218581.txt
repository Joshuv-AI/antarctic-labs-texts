Improving Inference in Air Pollution Epidemiology: The Case for Rethinking Multipollutant Adjustment
Authors: Hong Chen, Matthew Quick, Jay S. Kaufman, Chen Chen, Jeffrey C. Kwong, Juwel Rana, JinHee Kim, Aaron van Donkelaar, Randall V. Martin, Michael Tjepkema, Tarik Benmarhnia, Richard T. Burnett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Air quality regulations and programs are vital for protecting the public from harms caused by air pollution. To support these actions, numerous epidemiological studies have sought to identify the pollutants most responsible for adverse outcomes. These studies often used statistical adjustments for copollutants in outcome regression models, a practice also commonly applied to assess interactions between copollutants. Here, we highlight possible pitfalls of multipollutant analyses. Indiscriminate copollutant adjustment can induce noncausal associations through collider adjustment, distorting effect estimates for individual air pollutants. We describe the underlying mechanisms and provide empirical evidence on how such bias may realistically influence the relationships between air pollution and health outcomes from a well-characterized Canadian national cohort alongside a simulation study. Additionally, we discuss strategies to mitigate the impact of this bias. Given the widespread interest in multipollutant approaches among the scientific and policy communities, greater caution is needed when conducting and interpreting research on multiple pollutants.

Ambient air pollution, comprising harmful particulates and gases, is one of the greatest environmental threats to human health.1 To date, a wide spectrum of adverse health outcomes, ranging from the development of major diseases to premature mortality, has been linked to air pollution.2–4 In response, governments worldwide have developed major air quality regulations and programs. For example, the United States and Canada introduced acid rain programs to limit sulfur dioxide (SO2) and nitrogen oxides5,6 and, alongside many other countries, have implemented national ambient air quality standards for pollutants such as fine particulate matter (PM2.5).7–9 These regulations are largely grounded in the epidemiological research linking individual pollutants to human health risks.2–4

As the scientific and policy communities seek to refine these regulations and determine which air pollutants or mixtures pose the greatest threats, a key challenge is disentangling true causal effects from biased associations. Controlling for confounders—factors associated with both exposure and outcome that can distort results—has been a longstanding priority in this field. With access to data on air pollution exposure rapidly expanding,10 researchers have increasingly incorporated multiple pollutants in epidemiological analyses to reduce confounding by coexposures or to explore interactions within pollutant mixtures.2–4

This practice is now widespread across epidemiological studies examining long-term exposure to established pollutants such as PM2.5, ozone (O3), or nitrogen dioxide (NO2),2,3,11,12 as well as in investigations of novel pollutants including ultrafine particles, airborne metals, PM2.5 components (e.g., black carbon), volatile organic compounds, and source-specific pollutants (e.g., wildfire-specific PM2.5).13–17 Moreover, coexposures like environmental noise, neighborhood greenness, and walkability have increasingly been included as covariates in air health studies and vice versa.18–21

While these adjustments are intended to strengthen causal inference, recent insights from the application of causal graphs theory in the context of environmental mixtures suggest that they may inadvertently create new biases.22 Specifically, bias may arise due to adjustment for a coexposure that is influenced both by the main exposure (or its unmeasured causes, e.g., shared emission sources) and by unmeasured causes of the outcome.22 In these situations, conditioning on the coexposure could induce a noncausal association between the main exposure and outcome.23 This is also known as collider bias—a concept well-characterized in other areas of epidemiology, where there is a consensus that more adjustment is not always beneficial.24 However, this bias remains largely unexplored in air pollution epidemiological research. In the accompanying eFigures 1 and 2; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C316, we illustrate the underlying mechanism and provide several examples where this phenomenon may occur.

Controlling for coexposures has become routine in epidemiological research on air pollution and other environmental factors, but this practice is rarely questioned.22 To shed light on its unintended implications, we conducted a series of simulations and empirical studies to examine how this issue may realistically influence air pollution-health associations. We began with a simulation study considering various scenarios involving copollutant-outcome associations, residual confounding, and interpollutant correlations, drawing on data from a well-characterized Canadian national cohort of 2.7 million adults followed from 2007 to 2016. We then performed two real-world cohort studies using this Canadian population. The first empirical study analyzed the associations of long-term exposure to PM2.5, NO2, and O3 with the risk of nonaccidental mortality. The second study assessed the associations between major PM2.5 components—a growing focus in air pollution research—and nonaccidental mortality. Finally, we discussed potential strategies to mitigate this issue in air pollution epidemiological research.

METHODS

Study Population

The Canadian Census Health and Environment Cohort (CanCHEC) is a nationally representative cohort developed by Statistics Canada through individual-level linkage of mandatory Canadian long-form census data with national health administrative databases and income tax files. Details of the cohort have been described elsewhere.25 CanCHEC collects detailed information on residential history, ethnocultural and socioeconomic characteristics, and health status,25 and has been widely used to investigate the health impacts of air pollution.26,27

This study included participants from the 2006 wave of the CanCHEC. Eligibility criteria required individuals to be aged 30–79 years and to have lived in Canada for >5 years on 1 January 2007 (baseline). Individuals with incomplete data on exposure and covariates were excluded. The outcome of interest was death from natural causes (eAppendix; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C316). Cause and date of death were obtained from the Canadian Vital Statistics Death Database between baseline and 31 December 2016 (end of follow-up). The study received approval from the Research Ethics Board of Health Canada and Public Health Agency of Canada.

Simulation Study

To illustrate the plausibility of collider bias arising from adjustments of copollutants in the presence of confounding, we carried out a simulation study. We simulated 100 Monte Carlo samples, with a sample size of 2 million subjects each.

Our simulations were performed following previous studies.28,29 Briefly, the data-generating mechanism is structured as follows: O = (L, A, Y, U, W), where L denotes a vector of two measured baseline confounders (L1, L2), A encodes two time-varying exposure variables (A1, A2), Y denotes the outcome indicating whether an individual died by time t (up to 10), and U encodes three unmeasured variables: a shared cause of A1 and A2 (U0) representing common emission sources, a confounder for A1 (U1), and a confounder for A2 (U2). W is a descendant of U0. The temporal orderings of these variables are depicted in eFigure 3; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C316. We assumed that the outcome Y was influenced by both exposures (A1, A2), measured and unmeasured confounders (L1, L2, U1, U2), and t. The estimates of our interest are the individual effects of A1 and A2 on Y.

We generated distributions of confounders, exposures, and the outcome based on observed data in the 2006 CanCHEC (referred to as the base scenario; see eAppendix; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C316). For example, L1 and L2 approximated age (mean = 51 years) and sex (50% men) in the cohort. Exposure means were 6.5 for A1 and 40.0 for A2, reflecting mean annual PM2.5 and O3 levels. The outcome Y had an 8% probability over 10 time intervals, matching the cohort’s 10-year death risk. The true effects (on the log scale) were set to 0.01 for A1 and 0.005 for A2, corresponding to 1% and 0.05% increases in Y, respectively, per unit increase. We assumed log-linear exposure-outcome relationships, with no censoring, interaction, or misclassification.

In each Monte Carlo sample, we estimated the individual effects of A1 and A2 on Y using pooled logistic regression—a discrete-time hazards model. We considered six model specifications: (1) the correct model, which matched the aforementioned data-generating mechanism used in simulation (ground truth); three two-exposure models that omitted one or both of the two unmeasured confounders (i.e., (2) omitting U1, (3) omitting U2, or (4) omitting both), but adjusted for all other confounders including the coexposure to mimic real-world situations; and two single-exposure models that omitted the other coexposure (i.e., (5) omitting A1 or (6) omitting A2) but adjusted for all measured and unmeasured confounders (L1, L2, U1, U2, t) (eAppendix; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C316). We designed specifications 2–4 to evaluate collider bias arising from adjusting for a confounded coexposure, and specifications 5–6 to illustrate confounding arising from omission of a coexposure.

To explore the magnitude of collider bias, we considered 18 scenarios across the six model specifications, with varying the effects of A1 and A2 on Y, varying degrees of unmeasured confounding by U1 and U2, and varying A1–A2 correlations (eTable 1; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C316). Last, we evaluated whether adjusting for W may reduce collider bias (measured as percent change in the log-scale coefficients for A1 and A2) in the two-pollutant analyses (under specifications 2 and 3), relative to the correct model (specification 1). The last analysis was repeated across 100 scenarios with varying correlations between A1 and A2, and between W and U0.

Two Empirical Studies

In the first empirical study, we used the 2006 wave of the CanCHEC to quantify the associations of long-term exposure to PM2.5, O3, and NO2 with nonaccidental mortality. Annual PM2.5 estimates (version V4.NA.02.MAPLE) were developed by the Washington University Atmospheric Compositional Analysis Group by combining satellite retrievals, GEOS-Chem chemical transport model outputs, and ground-based observations and calibrating these initial results to ground-based observations via geographically weighted regression.10,30 Annual O3 estimates were derived using a fusion-based approach that refined outputs from the GEM-MACH chemical transport model with ground-level monitoring data.31 Annual NO2 estimates were obtained from a national land-use regression model.32 These exposure estimates have been validated and shown to have strong agreement with fixed-site monitor measurements (e.g., R2 = 0.73 for PM2.5).10,30–32 Further details of these exposure data are available elsewhere.10,30–32 Annual exposures to these pollutants were assigned to all participants based on their postal code addresses between 2004 (3 years before baseline) and 2016 (end of follow-up).

We used pooled logistic regression to estimate the odds of mortality associated with each interquartile range (IQR) increase in the 3-year rolling mean exposure to PM2.5, O3, and NO2. We analyzed each pollutant separately (single-pollutant models) and together (three-pollutant model). We adjusted all models for covariates that are either established or suspected confounders of the air pollution–mortality relationship, including baseline age, sex, race/ethnicity, Indigenous identity, immigration status, marital status, education, occupation, employment status, time-varying annual family income, four neighborhood-level marginalization measures (residential instability, material deprivation, dependency, and ethnic concentration), urban form (representing neighborhood-level characteristics of active commuting and transit-use), community size, annual meteorological conditions (temperature and relative humidity), and Canadian region (British Columbia, Prairie, Ontario, Quebec, Atlantic, and Territories).27

To assess collider bias, we considered a covariate for Canadian airshed (Western, Prairie, West Centre, East Centre, Southern Atlantic, and Northern region) used previously.26 While similar in geography, a key distinction between Canadian region and Canadian airshed is that the East Centre airshed combines Ontario and Quebec. Although Ontario and Quebec had similar annual PM2.5 and NO2 levels, they differed in annual O3 levels. In addition, they differed in mortality rates. Hence, adjusting for Canadian airshed rather than Canadian region would confound the O3-mortality association. To illustrate how adjustment for the confounded O3 might distort estimates for copollutants like PM2.5, we compared models adjusting for Canadian airshed versus Canadian region, assuming the causal structure in eFigure 1B; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C316. All pollutants were modeled linearly for practical reasons and in accordance with common practice in the literature.11

In the second empirical study, we further sought to quantify the long-term effects of six major PM2.5 components on mortality. The components included black carbon, organic matter, sulfate (SO42−), ammonium (NH4+), nitrate (NO3−), and sea salt. Sea salt was a negative control exposure for which no association with mortality was expected.33–35 We estimated annual mean concentrations of these PM2.5 components (version V5.NA.04.02) by partitioning total PM2.5 mass into its chemical composition using the GEOS-Chem chemical transport model and calibrating these outputs to ground-based observations, following a similar methodology used for aforementioned annual PM2.5 estimates (version V4.NA.02.MAPLE).36 Details of these estimates were described elsewhere.36 Covering North America at a 1 × 1 km resolution, these component estimates exhibited good agreement with cross-validation sites in 2000–2022, with the strongest cross-validation performance observed for SO42− (R2 = 0.88), NO3− (R2 = 0.85), and NH4+ (R2 = 0.78), followed by black carbon (R2 = 0.67), organic matter (R2 = 0.63), and sea salt (R2 = 0.43).36

Similar to the first empirical study, we modeled PM2.5 components as 3-year rolling means using pooled logistic regression, adjusting for all available covariates (e.g., age, income, and Canadian region). Each component was analyzed individually and also alongside black carbon in two-pollutant models, yielding odds ratios (ORs) per 1-μg/m3 in each component.

All simulations and analyses were conducted in R 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

RESULTS

Study Population

The 2006 wave of the CanCHEC comprised 2,663,645 participants with 25.7 million person-years of observation. The average age at baseline was 50.9 years, 48.6% were men, 10.5% were visible minorities, 20.4% had less than high school education, and 65.5% were employed (eTable 2; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C316). From 2007 to 2016, a total of 213,886 participants died, with an annual mortality rate of 0.8%.

Simulation Study

In the base scenario, the correct model (specification 1)—adjusting for all measured and unmeasured confounders, including coexposures—accurately estimated the effects of A1 and A2, with median ORs of 1.01 and 1.005 per unit increase across 100 Monte Carlo samples (eFigure 3; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C316 for the data-generating mechanism used). As expected, omitting either coexposure (specifications 5 and 6) introduced confounding into the effect estimate for the other. However, adjusting for a coexposure while omitting an unmeasured confounder for that exposure also evidently distorted the effect estimate for the other coexposure. For example, in the two-exposure analyses, adjusting for A2 while omitting U2 (specification 3) biased A1’s OR toward 0.99, even with adjustment for A1’s own unmeasured confounder U1, making A1 appear null or even inversely associated with Y. Similarly, adjusting for A1 while omitting U1 (specification 2) biased A2’s estimate, as did omitting both U1 and U2 (specification 4).

In scenarios where we varied coexposure correlations or confounding structures, collider bias became more pronounced. Consider the following examples using specification 3 (adjusting for A2 while omitting U2). When the correlation between A1 and A2 increased (r = 0.6 vs. 0.4 in the base scenario), adjusting for A2 while omitting U2 biased A1’s OR further downward to 0.97. Similarly, when U2 was a stronger confounder for A2, the same adjustment for A2 while omitting U2 also lowered A1’s OR. Even when A2 had no effect on Y and was thus not a confounder for A1, adjusting for A2 while omitting U2 still biased A1’s OR, despite such an adjustment appearing innocuous.

Results from the four scenarios described above (the base scenario and three scenarios under specification 3, which adjusted for A2 while omitting U2: (1) A2 was more strongly correlated with A1, (2) stronger confounding of U2 for A2, and (3) no effect of A2 on Y) are shown in Figure 1.

Similar bias patterns were evident in the remaining 15 scenarios, which further varied the effects of A1 and A2, the extent of confounding by U1 and U2, and coexposure correlations (eFigures 4–8; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C316). Importantly, in some cases, adjusting for a confounded coexposure reversed the association of the other exposure with Y or inflated the estimate away from the null. For instance, when U1 acted as a negative confounder for A1 (e.g., by setting U1 to −0.15), adjusting for A1 while omitting U1 (specification 2) almost doubled the OR for A2.

When W—a descendant of U0, a shared cause of A1 and A2—was perfectly associated with U0 (r = 1.0), adjusting for W eliminated collider bias in the two-exposure analysis (Figure 2). In more realistic settings, bias reduction depended on both the W–U0 correlation and the A1–A2 correlation. For example, with a modest A1–A2 correlation (r = 0.40), adjusting for W strongly associated with U0 (r = 0.8) halved the bias for A1, whereas a weaker W–U0 correlation (r = 0.3) yielded minimal reduction (4%). Similar trends held for A2 and other scenarios.

Associations of PM2.5, O3, and NO2 With Mortality

The 2006 CanCHEC cohort was exposed to, on average, 3-year rolling mean concentrations of 7.0 µg/m3 PM2.5, 37.3 parts per billion (ppb) O3, and 10.0 ppb NO2 over the follow-up. PM2.5 displayed a strong correlation with NO2 (r = 0.62) and a moderate correlation with O3 (r = 0.49). O3 and NO2 were slightly correlated (r = 0.11).

In single-pollutant analyses adjusted for all covariates (using airshed instead of region to introduce O3-mortality confounding), all three pollutants were positively associated with mortality. The largest OR per IQR increase was observed for O3, 1.069 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.055, 1.083; IQR = 9.0 ppb), followed by PM2.5, 1.027 (95% CI: 1.015, 1.038; IQR = 3.3 µg/m3) and NO2, 1.015 (1.002, 1.028; IQR = 8.7 ppb) (Table). In the three-pollutant analysis, however, the PM2.5-mortality association became null (OR = 0.997), while the O3 association slightly elevated (1.071) and the NO2 association slightly attenuated (1.014).

Associations of PM2.5 Components With Mortality

The 2006 CanCHEC cohort was also exposed to, on average, 3-year rolling mean concentrations of 0.5 µg/m3 black carbon, 4.3 µg/m3 organic matter, 1.4 µg/m3 SO42−, 0.7 µg/m3 NO3−, 0.6 µg/m3 NH4+, and 0.1 µg/m3 sea salt. Black carbon exposure was positively correlated with all other PM2.5 components, with Pearson correlation coefficients ranging from 0.82 (with organic matter) to 0.09 (with sea salt).

In the single-pollutant analysis, black carbon showed an implausible inverse association with mortality (OR = 0.924, 95% CI: 0.886, 0.964 per µg/m3), despite adjustment for many covariates including race/ethnicity, income, socioeconomic position, and Canadian region (Figure 3). Conversely, sea salt—a negative control exposure—was unexpectedly associated with higher mortality (OR = 1.108; 95% CI: 1.049, 1.170 per µg/m3). When both components were included in a two-pollutant analysis, black carbon’s inverse association became exacerbated, and sea salt’s positive association was amplified.

For other PM2.5 components examined, single-pollutant analyses showed positive associations for some and no associations for others: OR = 1.029 (95% CI: 1.010, 1.050) for SO42−, 1.036 (1.001, 1.071) for NH4+, 1.015 (0.995, 1.036) for NO3, and 1.001 (0.996, 1.006) for organic matter per µg/m3 with mortality (Figure 3). Strikingly, mutually adjusting for black carbon amplified these estimates—doubling the ORs for SO42−, NH4+, and NO3, and increasing OR for organic matter ten-fold. In these two-pollutant analyses, black carbon’s inverse association was also exacerbated (e.g., OR lowered to 0.882 in the two-pollutant model with SO42−). These patterns mirrored those in the analyses on black carbon and sea salt, as well as findings from the first empirical and simulation studies.

DISCUSSION

Most scientific evidence guiding air quality regulations comes from epidemiological research.7–9,37 With greater available volumes of exposure data on air pollution and many other environmental factors, researchers have increasingly incorporated coexposures as covariates to isolate the individual effects of pollutants and to assess interactions. In a Canadian cohort, we found that when a pollutant’s association with mortality contained a noncausal path, adjusting for this pollutant in multipollutant analyses distorted the estimated effects of other pollutants. These distortions were sometimes substantial—for example, the well-established PM2.5-mortality association vanished entirely in the multipollutant analyses. We observed similar bias with PM2.5 components. Our simulation study further demonstrated that, in the presence of confounding, copollutant adjustment could greatly impact effect estimates, doubling them or even reversing their direction.

In the 2006 CanCHEC, the implausible harmful effect of sea salt and the similarly implausible protective effect of black carbon on mortality observed in the single-pollutant analyses likely reflect noncausal associations, despite our best efforts to control for many covariates. Although the exact reason is unknown, it is plausible that these biases may be due to unmeasured confounding, exposure misclassification, or both. Notably, even with their weak correlation (r = 0.09), mutually adjusting for both black carbon and sea salt in the two-pollutant analysis made black carbon appear more protective and sea salt appear more harmful. Analogously, adjusting for black carbon in the analyses of other PM2.5 components artificially strengthened their positive associations with mortality, contradicting expectations that such an adjustment would either render no effect (if black carbon had no independent effect) or attenuate the associations (if it were truly harmful). We observed the most pronounced increase for organic matter, whose association with mortality increased ten-fold after adjusting for black carbon, likely due to the strong correlation between organic matter and black carbon (r = 0.82). This finding underscores that, in addition to biases that attenuated associations in the PM2.5-mortality study, adjusting for a copollutant that acts as a collider can result in bias that exaggerates the associations between other copollutants (e.g., SO42− and NO3) and mortality.

The biases we observed above likely arose from the causal structure described by Weisskopf et al.,22 where an exposure acts as a collider on the path between other coexposures and outcome. Using causal graphs theory, these authors demonstrated that if residual confounding affects the exposure, adjusting for that exposure (now a collider) can bias estimates for other exposures.22 Beyond confounding, we propose that dependent measurement errors in a copollutant could produce similar bias in a multipollutant analysis (eFigure 9; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C316). An imperfectly measured pollutant can be associated with the outcome through its causal effect. Dependent measurement errors in this pollutant can also link it to the outcome via hidden confounders. For example, in epidemiological research on chronic exposure to air pollution, measurement errors in chronic exposure may be spatially associated with the outcome through unmeasured geographic factors beyond commonly considered contextual variables like deprivation. Adjusting for such a mismeasured pollutant can therefore induce collider bias. In our analysis of PM2.5 components, component-specific measurement errors (e.g., R2 = 0.43 for sea salt and 0.67 for black carbon) may have contributed to the biases observed. Because observational research cannot empirically disentangle whether a collider bias arises from confounding or from outcome-dependent measurement errors, we focused our discussion on the mechanism via confounding as it is better understood.

Omitting a copollutant may introduce confounding for other exposures, whereas adjusting for a confounded pollutant may instead induce collider bias. To address this issue, we offer four suggestions. First, it is important to recognize that if it is possible to eliminate confounding for all copollutants involved, the collider issues could be mitigated. Because the minimum adjustment set can differ across pollutants in a mixture, potential confounders should be considered for all copollutants, not just the primary pollutant. As with eliminating confounding in single-pollutant research, investigators must collect sufficient data on relevant covariates during study design, guided by subject-matter knowledge. Causal diagrams are valuable for this purpose, as they depict both causal relationships and associations, allowing potential bias to be identified before analysis.38 While complete elimination of confounding may be unrealistic, domain knowledge of the causal structure can help address confounding as much as possible. In some cases, spurious associations emerging during analysis can signal confounding for a copollutant, as in our study on PM2.5, O3, and NO2. Here, incorrect adjustment for Canadian airshed introduced confounding for O3, which in turn entirely obscured the well-established PM2.5-mortality association in the multipollutant analysis. Correcting for this confounding not only reduced the inflated O3-mortality association but also mitigated the resulting collider bias for PM2.5, producing an estimate at least directionally consistent with other findings in the literature.11

Second, because collider bias from copollutant adjustment depends on the degree of interpollutant correlations, investigators can attempt to reduce these correlations. One approach is to collect data on an auxiliary variable that descends from the common cause of the pollution mixture. Subject-matter knowledge is once again essential in identifying such variables. As shown in our simulation, adjusting for W reduced collider bias in the multipollutant analysis to a degree depending on the strength of its association with the common cause (Figure 2). In practice, finding a perfectly associated auxiliary variable is often challenging, making it most prudent as a supplementary strategy. This approach can be appealing, however, when the structural origins of bias from copollutant adjustment are conceptually difficult to pinpoint. Even when the bias structure is identifiable, it may still be beneficial—for instance, if exposure errors are a concern but improving exposure assessment is impractical due to time, financial, or technical constraints.

Additionally, instrumental variable analysis is another potential way to mitigate collider bias by eliminating the need to adjust for copollutants (alongside other confounders), as discussed by Weisskopf et al.22 Because standard instrumental variable estimation has more restrictive applicability than other methods, it is often used to answer causal questions about time-fixed exposures, rather than time-varying exposures like air pollution.38 Nonetheless, if implemented successfully, instrumental variable estimation is a complementary way to assess the sensitivity of effect estimates, as it relies on a different set of assumptions.

Third, when studying multiple pollutants, a degree of healthy skepticism is essential. Because we cannot empirically verify that collider bias is eliminated, methods such as negative controls should be considered more routinely. Negative controls are a powerful technique for detecting biases like confounding and measurement bias,39–41 both of which can contribute to collider bias from copollutant adjustment. In our first empirical analysis, we observed that PM2.5, an established risk factor for mortality, appeared protective after adjusting for O3, indicating bias from this adjustment. In our study on PM2.5 components, adjusting for black carbon exacerbated the paradoxical association between sea salt and mortality, further signifying this structural bias.

Another approach to detect bias from copollutant adjustment is quantitative bias analysis. By systematically conducting analyses under different bias assumptions, this approach helps explore whether other plausible causal structures may explain observed associations.42,43 These analyses typically assess a range of conditions, from near-perfect exchangeability to moderate unmeasured confounding or dependent exposure misclassification. While small biases may not meaningfully alter study conclusions, large biases—arising from strong confounding or substantial exposure misclassification—warrant careful investigation.

Finally, in the face of uncertainty in identifying the individual effects for pollutants, we must balance the trade-off between copollutant confounding and collider bias from copollutant adjustment when deciding whether to adjust for a copollutant. A good practice is to make the reasoning and assumptions about the adjustment strategy as transparent as possible to allow other investigators to evaluate and challenge components of the strategy. In some scenarios, it can be more meaningful to shift the target estimand from individual pollutants to the overall mixture effect.44 This approach might potentially reduce collider bias if biases in individual pollutants happen to offset one another; however, this is not assured. Quantifying the combined effects of pollution mixtures is an active area of research.44 Future work is warranted to examine how the collider bias structure may influence such studies.

Our study has limitations that contextualize our findings. Although we found evidence of collider bias from copollutant adjustments in the 2006 CanCHEC, there is no guarantee that a bias of similar magnitude would occur elsewhere. However, if some uncontrolled confounding is present and interpollutant correlations are modest to strong, this bias is expected to arise in multipollutant analysis. Our simulation study supports its plausibility across diverse scenarios. Notably, replacing statistical adjustment with other analytic approaches (e.g., stratification, restriction, or matching) would not eliminate this bias.

Additionally, our effect estimates from the 2006 CanCHEC, particularly the study on PM2.5, O3, and NO2, are intended for tutorial purposes only, as they were likely influenced by other threats to validity such as additional confounding. For instance, the region adjustment factor was not necessarily a true risk factor for mortality but rather a proxy for some unmeasured risk factors (e.g., smoking or healthcare access) or imperfectly measured ones (e.g., socioeconomic status). Therefore, these results should not be interpreted causally without additional assumptions. Despite these simplifications, our empirical studies are well-suited to illustrate the real-world implications of collider bias arising from copollutant adjustment.

While our focus was on relatively simple causal structures, other structures may also exist. For example, if a copollutant lies on the path between the primary pollutant and the outcome and its association with the outcome is also influenced by uncontrolled confounding, both over-adjustment bias and collider bias could arise, further distorting the association between the primary pollutant and the outcome. Copollutant adjustment may also lead to bias amplification, which operates by amplifying preexisting confounding in one exposure when adjusting for another correlated exposure.22 This phenomenon is analogous to Z-bias, which occurs when inadvertently adjusting for an instrumental variable.45,46 Unlike collider bias, however, bias amplification may sometimes reduce confounding bias,38,47 making its overall direction less predictable. Future work should better characterize the implications of bias amplification for air pollution epidemiological research.

Because the true effects of air pollutants on mortality must be unknown in empirical research, we applied subject-matter knowledge to guide selection of negative and positive controls (e.g., sea salt).33–35 Last, our simulations used only 100 Monte Carlo samples for each scenario; our intent was not to provide an exhaustive evaluation of bias from copollutant adjustment, but to highlight its practical relevance.

Despite these limitations, this study demonstrates the real-world implications of collider bias through simulations and two empirical studies using nonlinear models common in air pollution epidemiology. This is a bias that, to our knowledge, remains insufficiently understood but has important policy implications. This study also elaborates on how other mechanisms can lead to collider bias in multipollutant analysis, such as the presence of dependent measurement error, and proposes practical strategies to mitigate this bias.

CONCLUSION

In air pollution epidemiological research, collider bias from copollutant adjustment can pose a special threat to the key inferential goal of identifying causal effects of air pollutants. Given the growing interest in multipollutants within the scientific and policy communities, greater caution is needed when conducting research on multiple pollutants and interpreting results.
