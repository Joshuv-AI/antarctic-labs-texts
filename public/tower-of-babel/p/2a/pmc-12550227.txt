Genetic Deficiency of Interleukin-4 and Interleukin-13 Exacerbates Alcohol- and Cigarette Smoke-Associated Chronic Pancreatitis via the Pancreas–Intestine Axis
Authors: Jingyuan Wang, Pasha Reza Shams Azar, Minoti V Apte, Stephen J Pandol, Sohail Z Husain, Bomi Lee
Published: 2025

Alcohol consumption and cigarette smoking are independent risk factors for chronic pancreatitis (CP).1,2 These 2 environmental factors have synergistic pathogenic effects on pain and exocrine pancreatic insufficiency.3 Despite this, only about 5% of individuals exposed to these substances develop pancreatitis,4 suggesting that additional factors contribute to disease initiation and progression. Meanwhile, emerging evidence highlights roles of immune signals in CP pathogenesis, offering novel mechanistic clues and therapeutic targets.5 However, the synergistic effect of combined alcohol and cigarette smoke exposure on CP immunopathology remains unclear.

Current animal models of CP face challenges in replicating complex scenarios observed in patients,6 such as a combined exposure to alcohol and cigarette smoke. Thus, we recently developed a CP mouse model (Figure A1A) involving chronic alcohol feeding, cigarette smoke inhalation, and cerulein injections (ASC model). The combined exposure for a 13-week duration makes this model closely mimic the clinical CP pathology, including pancreatic shrinkage, inflammation, and fibrosis. This model serves as a novel tool for investigating alcohol and smoking-associated CP pathology.

Interleukin (IL)-4 and interleukin (IL)-13 signaling induces alternative activated macrophages (AAMs), which have been shown to predominate in both mouse and human CP.7 Additionally, our analysis of IL-4 and IL-13 mRNA levels in a large number of pancreatic samples from human CP revealed that IL-4 expression is increased in CP patients, particularly among those alcohol-related cases (Figure A1B). IL-13 expression is also upregulated, although not significantly (data not shown). It has been reported that blocking IL-4 and IL-13 signaling mitigates fibrosis in the cerulein-only–induced CP mouse model.7 However, the role of IL-4 and IL-13 signaling in alcohol- and smoking-induced CP models has not been explored. In the current study, we aimed to investigate the role of IL-4 and IL-13 signaling in CP pathology using the novel ASC model, which better represents the clinical CP cases.

To study the role of IL-4 and IL-13 signaling, we compared Il4 and Il13 double-knockout (DKO) mice with wild-type (WT) controls. After the 13-week ASC model, WT mice exhibited expected pancreatic atrophy (Figure 1A) and fibrosis, along with a loss of normal acinar architecture in histological analysis (Figure 1B). Surprisingly, the ASC-induced DKO (ASC-DKO) mice showed a more severe loss of normal acinar architecture (Figure 1B) and a significant increase in fibrosis (Figure 1C and D). Moreover, the ASC-DKO mice developed lymphoma-like pathology in the pancreas, as evidenced by the large aggregates of lymphocytes (Figure 1B) and increased pancreas weight (Figure 1A) due to the very solid and fibrotic tissues. In the analysis of gene expression and immunohistochemical staining, T cell marker Cd3 significantly aggregated in the ASC-DKO mice pancreas (Figure 2A and B), but not B cell markers Cd19 or Cd20 (Figures A1C and A2A). Moreover, the significantly elevated T cell lymphoma markers Cd30 and Icos (Figure 2C), and cell proliferation marker Ki67 (Figure A1D) expression in the ASC-DKO mice were also signs of T cell lymphoma.

To further characterize this unexpected phenotype, we analyzed the expression of immune cell markers in the pancreas. Indeed, we observed significantly increased infiltration of immune cells in the ASC-DKO mice, as indicated by the increased Cd45 expression (Figure A1E). Since IL-4 and IL-13 signaling is known to be critical to induce AAM, we also assessed the expression of macrophage and AAM marker genes F4/80 and Mrc1. Contrary to our expectation of downregulated AAM markers due to the loss of Il4 and Il13 (Figure A1F), neither of the genes was significantly changed in the ASC-DKO mice (Figure A1G). The result suggests IL-4 and IL-13–independent pathways for AAM activation in the ASC-DKO mice. Based on a severe increase in T cell infiltration, we further evaluated the mRNA expression of several transcription factors associated with helper T cells. Only the Th2 cell development regulator Gata3 was significantly upregulated (Figure 2D), indicating a substantial increase in Th2 cell infiltration in the pancreas of the ASC-DKO mice. The type 2-inducing cytokines, Il10 and Tgfβ, were elevated in the ASC-DKO mice (Figure A1H), which could account for the IL-4 and IL-13-independent AAM and Th2 cell differentiation as a potential compensatory mechanism. Along with an increase in Th2 cell infiltration, there was a notable elevation of the T cell exhaustion marker Pdcd1 in the ASC-DKO mice (Figure 2D). Furthermore, the ASC-DKO mice exhibited a massive decrease in circulating immune markers in the serum compared to the ASC-WT mice (Figure 2E), suggesting both locally and systemically compromised immune responses.

Given that alcohol consumption and cigarette smoking are known to compromise the mucus layer and increase gut permeability,8,9 thereby promoting bacterial translocation into extraintestinal organs, we further investigated the bacterial load in the pancreas. The total pancreatic bacterial amount, represented by the 16s rRNA expression level (Figure 2F) and histological Gram staining (Figures 2G and A1I), was significantly higher in the ASC-DKO mice compared to ASC-WT mice, suggesting that the pancreatic bacterial load, mainly Gram-negative species, was increased due to the loss of IL-4 and IL-13 in the ASC model. Moreover, the expression of the cathelicidin antimicrobial peptide gene in the pancreas was elevated in the ASC-DKO mice (Figure 2H), indicating an increased bacterial load. To determine if the increased gut permeability contributes to bacterial translocation in the pancreas, we assessed gut permeability by measuring plasma levels of lipopolysaccharide, a key component of Gram-negative bacteria. An increased systemic bacterial load by the ASC model was observed in both WT and DKO mice (Figure A1J). However, there were no significant changes in lipopolysaccharide levels between WT-ASC and DKO-ASC mice, indicating that, in addition to increased gut permeability and systemic circulation, other routes may contribute to the significantly elevated pancreatic bacterial load in DKO-ASC mice.

In summary, using the novel ASC-CP model, we demonstrated that loss of IL-4 and IL-13 exacerbated pancreatitis and fibrosis, ultimately leading to lymphoma-like pathology in the pancreas. Strikingly, the ASC-DKO mice exhibited significantly elevated Gram-negative bacterial translocation in the pancreas. Overall, the pathologies in the ASC-DKO mice appear to arise from a combination of the increased bacterial invasion from the gut and the compromised systemic and pancreatic immune protection. This also suggests a crosstalk between the pancreas and gut in alcohol- and smoking-induced CP pathogenesis. Given that the bacterial load in circulation did not differ between WT-ASC and DKO-ASC mice, it is possible that bacteria may translocate through reflux into the pancreatic duct or via the lymphatic pathway,10 which will be further investigated. These novel findings contrast with the previously observed phenotype in the cerulein-only–induced CP model,7 suggesting that the same genetic model can react differently to various CP scenarios. Our findings in DKO mice will be validated by pharmacological inhibition of IL-4 and IL-13 signaling under ASC induction. Further investigations are warranted to explore the detailed mechanisms by which IL-4 and IL-13 signaling regulates pancreatic immune responses and gut bacterial translocation in alcohol- and smoking-associated CP, as well as how these mechanisms differ from CP with other etiologies.
