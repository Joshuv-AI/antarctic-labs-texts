Light grazing promotes the accumulation of soil microbial necromass carbon in a desert steppe
Authors: Xin Ju, Bingying Wang, Tingting Shen, Zhiguo Li, Guodong Han, Qian Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGrazing, as one of the most important methods of utilizing natural grasslands, can significantly impact the accumulation and stabilization of soil organic carbon within grassland ecosystems. Soil microbial necromass carbon (MNC), including fungal necromass carbon (FNC) and bacterial necromass carbon (BNC), is an important source of soil organic carbon (SOC) and plays a critical role in the formation and stabilization of SOC. However, the effects of grazing intensity on soil MNC and its underlying drivers remain unclear.MethodsWe investigated differences in soil MNC, FNC, BNC, plant and soil factors following a grazing experiment with four grazing intensities (control, 0 sheep units ha−1 a−1; light grazing, 0.91 sheep units ha−1 a−1; moderate grazing, 1.82 sheep units ha−1 a−1; heavy grazing, 2.71 sheep units ha−1 a−1) in the Stipa breviflora desert steppe of Inner Mongolia, China. The main objective was to explore the effects of different grazing intensities and soil depths on soil MNC and its contribution to soil organic carbon.ResultsCompared with the control, light grazing significantly increased MNC, FNC, and BNC by 10.55%, 10.59%, and 10.48%, respectively, in the 0–30 cm soil layer, whereas heavy grazing significantly decreased MNC and FNC by 9.26% and 10.94%, respectively. Compared with the 0–10 cm soil layer, MNC and BNC in the 20–30 cm soil layer significantly decreased by 6.45% and 16.24%, respectively. FNC had consistently greater contributions to SOC than did BNC in desert steppe soils. Soil nitrogen associated nutrients (soil total nitrogen, ammonium nitrogen, and microbial biomass nitrogen) and phosphorus associated nutrients (soil total phosphorus and available phosphorus) were direct drivers of MNC and FNC accumulation, while phosphorus associated nutrients (soil total phosphorus and available phosphorus) were the main drivers of BNC accumulation.ConclusionsThis study highlights that light grazing can increase soil MNC accumulation, potentially contributing to organic carbon sequestration in desert steppe ecosystems. The grazing-induced decrease in soil phosphorus is a key factor regulating soil MNC, FNC and BNC in the desert steppe, while the decrease in soil nitrogen is the main driver of MNC and FNC. These findings provide a theoretical basis for optimizing grazing management to enhance soil organic carbon storage in arid grassland ecosystems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07237-8.Keywords: Grazing intensity, Microbial necromass carbon, Soil nutrients, Stipa breviflora desert steppe

Introduction

Grasslands account for 41% of terrestrial ecosystems, significantly influencing the global carbon cycle [1]. They store 34% of total terrestrial carbon stocks, with most of it stored as soil organic carbon (SOC) [2]. Studies on grassland SOC have primarily focused on stocks [3], density [4], spatial distribution [5], and stability [6], but research on the sources of SOC has been limited. These sources include plant litter, root exudates, animal residues, and microbial necromass [3, 7]. Most studies emphasize plant-derived carbon’s contribution to the soil carbon pool [8]. In contrast, the microbial carbon pump mechanism emphasizes the contribution of microbially-derived carbon to SOC formation and stabilization [9]. Although living microbial biomass carbon (MBC) contributes less than 5% to the total SOC formation [10], microbial necromass carbon (MNC), which consists of microbial metabolic products and dead microbial biomass, accounts for approximately 50% of SOC [11], which is more stable and less easily decomposable than plant residues. Consequently, contributions from MNC to SOC exceed those from plant residues carbon [11]. Gaining insight into the soil MNC and its role in SOC within grasslands is essential for forecasting future carbon budgets and the conditions of these ecosystems.

The relative size of contributions from MNC to SOC varies significantly across ecosystems [9, 12], with various biotic and abiotic factors influencing MNC accumulation [13]. Most research on MNC has concentrated on forest ecosystems [14, 15] and wetlands [16], with research on the drivers of soil MNC in grassland ecosystems remaining limited. Previous studies have explored the impact of various grassland types [17], but the factors influencing the soil MNC remain poorly understood. Factors such as climate change [18], vegetation biomass [19], litter input [20], soil nutrients [21], soil depth [22], and human activity (e.g., grazing intensity, grazing type, and grazing duration) [23, 24] may all impact soil MNC in grassland ecosystems. Therefore, further investigation into the drivers of MNC in grassland ecosystems is necessary to understand the mechanisms that underlie SOC formation and stabilization.

Grazing, as one of the most important methods of utilizing natural grasslands, ecosystems, with a substantial impact on the stability of productivity and the functions of carbon cycling [25, 26]. Grazing livestock can reduce grassland productivity and plant diversity through selective foraging and trampling, thereby indirectly affecting the accumulation of MNC in the soil [27, 28]. In addition, grazing modifies soil physicochemical properties and microbial activity, which in turn influence SOC turnover, leading to changes in both total SOC stocks and its constituent fractions [29]. However, other research has found that moderate grazing enhances carbon cycling and increases carbon stocks [23, 30]. Moreover, soil depth plays a crucial role in determining soil MNC. The MNC to SOC ratio in grasslands rises with increasing soil depth [9]. Research on alpine ecosystems has found that increased plant carbon inputs result in a slightly higher contribution of topsoil MNC to SOC compared to that in the subsoil [22]. Currently, the effects of different grazing intensities on soil MNC at different soil depths remain unclear. Investigating the variation of MNC at different soil depths under different grazing intensities contributes to a better understanding of the formation of soil organic carbon and the mechanisms underlying its stability and maintenance.

There is continuing debate regarding optimal grazing management practices to promote SOC sequestration in grasslands, and the sources of soil carbon and the factors influencing it under different grazing intensities remain unclear [31, 32]. In this study, we explore these factors in a desert steppe dominated by Stipa breviflora, we investigated the mechanisms driving MNC accumulation under varying grazing intensities. We measured the soil MNC in the 0–30 cm soil layers, soil physicochemical properties, and above- and belowground biomass. We aimed to address the following scientific questions: (1) How do different grazing intensities affect soil MNC (including FNC and BNC), and how does their contribution to SOC vary under different grazing treatments and soil depths? (2) What are the intrinsic factors drive changes in soil MNC, and do the factors that impact FNC and BNC differ? To address these questions, we propose the following two hypotheses: (1) Compared with the control, light grazing may increase MNC and its contribution to SOC by enhancing soil nitrogen and phosphorus availability, whereas opposite effects may occur under moderate grazing and heavy grazing [28, 33]. (2) The contribution of MNC to SOC may increase with soil depth, possibly due to the reduction in plant root density and differences in soil nutrient availability [22, 34].

Methods

Study site

The study area was located in Siziwang Banner, Inner Mongolia, at the Comprehensive Experiment and Demonstration Center, part of the Inner Mongolia Academy of Agriculture and Animal Husbandry Sciences, China (41° 46′ 43″ N, 111° 53′ 42″ E, elevation 1456 m). The site represents a typical desert steppe ecosystem, dominated primarily by Stipa breviflora, Cleistogenes songorica and Artemisia frigida. The subordinate species included Convolvulus ammannii, among others. (Table S1). The soil is predominantly sandy loam with low nitrogen, phosphorus, and organic matter content and relatively high potassium levels. Throughout the experimental period (2004–2023), average annual temperature was 3.94 °C and average annual precipitation was 224.60 mm, with most rainfall occurring between June and August. Growing season air temperature and precipitation data for the collection year (2023) are available in Fig. S1.

Experimental design

In the Stipa breviflora desert steppe, herders primarily graze livestock during the plant growing season, with a stocking rate of approximately 1.0 sheep unit ha−1, which represents the typical local grazing intensity [35]. Based on local grazing practices and the grassland productivity of the study site, and referring to the theoretical stock capacity of Stipa breviflora desert steppe and the grazing design proposed by Wei et al. [36], we established different grazing intensity treatments. The grazing experiment was initiated in 2004 using a randomized block design, with grazing enclosures divide the study area into different treatment plots. Four grazing intensity treatments were applied within each block: no grazing (control, CK; 0 sheep units ha−1 a−1), light grazing (LG; 0.91 sheep units ha−1 a−1), moderate grazing (MG; 1.82 sheep units ha−1 a−1), and heavy grazing (HG; 2.71 sheep units ha−1 a−1). Three blocks were established to provide replicates. Each year, from May to October, treatment plots were stocked with 2-year-old Mongolian wether sheep. Sheep were moved to grazing plots at 6:00 am and returned to pens by 6:00 pm during grazing period.

Soil sample collection

Soil samples were collected in August 2023 to analyze amino sugars and soil physicochemical properties. In each grazing treatment plot, samples were taken from ten randomly selected points using a cylindrical soil auger with a diameter of 3 cm, extracting soil from the 0–10 cm, 10–20 cm, and 20–30 cm depths. Soil cores from five points within the same layer were combined into a single composite sample. Therefore, 72 soil samples were collected. Gravel, roots, and organic matter were removed, and the samples were sieved through a 2 mm mesh before being divided into two portions. A portion of the collected soil samples was stored at 4 °C to measure microbial biomass carbon (MBC), microbial biomass nitrogen (MBN), nitrate nitrogen (NO3−–N, NN), and ammonium nitrogen (NH4+–N, AN). Another portion was air-dried in a ventilated space for the assessment of amino sugars and other soil indicators.

Soil physical and chemical properties determination

Soil samples underwent analysis for total carbon (TC) and total nitrogen (TN) using an elemental analyzer. Total phosphorus (TP) determination was determined using ultraviolet spectrophotometry via sodium hydroxide fusion. The carbon-to-nitrogen ratio (C:N), carbon-to-phosphorus ratio (C:P), and nitrogen-to-phosphorus ratio (N:P) were calculated based on the ratios of SOC to TN, SOC to TP, and TN to TP, respectively. Soil organic carbon (SOC) measurement employed the potassium dichromate external heating method. Flow analyzer quantification determined NN and AN. Available phosphorus (AP) assessment followed the sodium bicarbonate-molybdenum antimony colorimetric method. Chloroform fumigation extraction determined MBC, MBN. Soil moisture (SM) was measured using the aluminum box drying method, and soil pH was determined with a digital pH meter (model pHS-3G, INESA Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd.). Soil temperature (ST) was recorded for each sampling point using a digital thermometer (model TP3001, Zhengzhou Boyang Instruments and Meters Co., Ltd., China).

Plant biomass measurement

We measured plant aboveground biomass (AGB) and litter biomass (LB) in August 2023. To assess plant community biomass, ten 1 m2 quadrats (a total of 120 quadrats) were randomly selected in each grazing plot. Within each quadrat, we first collected all litter lying on the soil surface, as well as clearly dead but still standing plant parts. Care was taken during collection to avoid including soil or fresh green plant material. Subsequently, the fresh green aboveground vegetation was classified by species and clipped at the soil surface. Both litter and fresh plant samples were oven-dried at 65 °C for 48 h and then weighed to determine LB and AGB, respectively.

We measured plant belowground biomass (BGB) in August 2023. In each grazing plot, BGB was sampled at ten randomly selected points. 10 samples from 0 to 30 cm soil layers were collected using a 7 cm diameter root auger. Roots were extracted, washed, air-dried, and weighed following the same process used to measure aboveground biomass.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted in R version 4.0.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). A linear mixed-effect model was performed using the “nlme” package in R to examine the effects of grazing intensity, soil depth and their interaction on the soil MNC, soil nutrient variables, soil properties, and plant biomass. For this, grazing intensity and soil depth as fixed factors and block as a random effect. Additionally, we used Tukey’s post-hoc tests to compare grazing intensity effects on soil MNC, plant and soil factors. We used P < 0.05 for significance.

To examine the influence of soil and plant factors on soil MNC, we ranked the impact of each factor on soil MNC using Pearson correlation analysis. Based on this analysis, we used Partial Least Squares Path Modeling (PLS-PM) to assess the effects of plant and soil factors on soil MNC in the 0–30 cm soil layer. Specifically, we evaluated the contributions of plant and soil factors to soil MNC, removed non-significant pathways to streamline the model, and examined the direct and indirect impacts of these factors. Path coefficients were used as a measure of the direction and strength of each effect. The PLS-PM modeling was conducted with the “plspm” package [41] in R software. Figures were generated using Origin 2023 software (OriginLab Corporation).

Results

The effects of grazing intensity on the soil MNC, FNC, BNC, and their contributions to SOC

The LG treatment significantly increased MNC, FNC, and BNC in the 0–30 cm and 0–10 cm soil layers, whereas the HG treatment significantly decreased MNC and FNC compared with CK (Fig. 1a–c, P < 0.05). In the 10–20 cm layer, HG significantly reduced MNC and FNC (P < 0.05), while grazing had no significant influence on microbial necromass in the 20–30 cm layer. The FNC/BNC, FNC/MNC, and BNC/MNC ratio did not differ significantly among grazing intensities within the 0–30 cm soil layer. The FNC content in soil microbial necromass was higher than BNC in the desert steppe soil. Moreover, MNC, BNC and BNC/MNC also declined significantly with increasing depth (Fig. 1a, c, i, P < 0.05), and the FNC/BNC, FNC/MNC increased significantly with soil depth (Fig. 1g–h, P < 0.05). Significant main effects of month and grazing intensity were observed for most soil physicochemical properties, whereas their interaction was significant only for a few variables, such as TN, SOC, and NN (Table 2). As shown in Fig. 2a–o, in both the 0–30 and 0–10 cm soil layers, LG significantly increased TP, MBC, MBN, SOC, AP, and pH compared with the CK (P < 0.05). In contrast, in the 0–30 cm layer, MG significantly decreased TC, C:P, N:P, AN, AP, and SM, whereas HG significantly decreased TC, TN, SOC, AN, NN, AP, and SM (P < 0.05). In the 10–20 cm layer, LG significantly increased MBN compared to CK, MG significantly decreased AP and SM, and HG significantly decreased SOC, AP, and SM; MBC and pH significantly increased under all grazing intensities (P < 0.05). In the 20–30 cm layer, LG significantly increased MBC, MBN, SOC, and AP, whereas HG decreased TN, SOC, AN, AP, and SM (P < 0.05). Moreover, pH significantly increased under all grazing treatments, whereas NN significantly decreased (P < 0.05). Overall, with increasing soil depth, TC, TN, C:P, N:P, SOC, AN, NN, and SM declined significantly, whereas C:N, MBC, and pH increased significantly (P < 0.05).

Based on the Partial Least Squares Path Model (PLS-PM), grazing had an indirect negative impact on soil MNC, FNC, and BNC. Specifically, grazing decreased soil MNC by reducing soil moisture, soil temperature, plant biomass, which in turn N- and P-associated soil nutrients (Fig. 5a). Similarly, grazing reduced N- and P-associated soil nutrients by lowering soil moisture, soil temperature, and plant biomass, thereby affecting soil FNC (Fig. 5c). Discussion

Effects of grazing intensity on the soil MNC and its contribution to SOC

The soil MNC is a major source of SOC and a key factor influencing carbon sequestration [13]. Grazing impacts MNC by altering plant communities and the soil environment [42]. Qu et al. [27] found that grazing decreases soil MNC by reducing plant aboveground biomass and root deposition. However, our findings differ, likely because our study involved different levels of grazing intensity. Although the soil MNC, FNC, and BNC were significantly lower under high grazing intensity, these factors were significantly higher under low grazing intensity (Fig. 1a–c). This suggests that low stocking rate may increase soil MNC content, this is consistent with our first hypothesis and aligns with the intermediate disturbance hypothesis [43]. Similarly, Zhao et al. [28] demonstrated that light grazing in a desert steppe did not reduce soil MNC levels. Thus, the increase in MNC we observed under light grazing may be the result of selective foraging, trampling, and nutrient recycling via livestock excretion. This promotes compensatory plant growth and enhances carbon and nitrogen availability [44, 45], providing nutrients for soil microbes, thereby promoting microbial biomass accumulation [46] and increasing MNC [19, 34, 44]. We also found that SOC, MBC and MBN increased significantly under light grazing (Fig. 2g–i), further supporting our findings. In summary, moderate livestock disturbance promotes soil nutrient input and transformation, both of which contribute to the accumulation of soil MNC.

We found that heavy grazing reduced the contribution of MNC to SOC, consistent with findings by Zhao et al. [28]. Legacy effects from long-term heavy grazing may reduce plant biomass and root exudate input, decreasing organic matter and suppressing microbial activity, thereby reducing the contribution of MNC to SOC [25, 26, 42, 47]. However, the contributions of soil MNC, FNC, and BNC to SOC under LG treatment were not significantly decrease compared to CK treatment, likely due to increased nitrogen availability from livestock trampling and nitrogen inputs [44]. In addition, we found that contributions by soil MNC to SOC in the top 30 cm of soil ranges from 28.52 to 31.00% (Fig. 1d). This study’s MNC contribution to SOC is lower than previous reports of up to 50% [11, 48]. This discrepancy may stem from the low precipitation in our study area (224.60 mm in 2023). A rainfall manipulation experiment at the same location as our grazing plots showed that reduced precipitation significantly lowered productivity [49]. This can inhibit soil microbial activity and biomass, thereby indirectly impacting MNC [50].

In the desert steppe, The soil FNC content was higher than soil BNC content and that FNC contributed more to SOC, the FNC:BNC ratio remained relatively high across all grazing treatments (Fig. 1g), consistent with the findings of Zhao et al. [28] and Qu et al. [27], which may be attributed to the fact that fungal communities in the desert steppe are relatively stable compared with bacterial communities [51]. Fungi tend to be the dominant group in most ecosystems [52]. Additionally, fungal cell walls are more difficult to decompose and fungi typically exhibit higher substrate use efficiency compared to bacteria. These characteristics allow them to produce more biomass from the same amount of substrate, underlying their promotion of SOC storage [53]. However, we found no significant differences in the FNC:BNC ratio among different grazing intensities, possibly because grazing did not alter the microbial community structure, and both fungi and bacteria responded similarly to grazing disturbances [51, 54].

Factors driving soil MNC, FNC, and BNC

We found a significant positive correlation between N-associated soil nutrients and soil MNC under grazing (Fig. 4a), with a direct effect of 0.65 (Fig. 5a–b), which is consistent with our first hypothesis. This is because soil nutrients can serve as energy sources to support the growth and development of living microorganisms [55]. Nitrogen, as a key nutrient for microbial metabolism and growth, directly determines microbial nitrogen use efficiency [56], with AN serving as a substrate for microbial nitrogen metabolism. Microbial nitrogen cycling is more efficient with increasing nitrogen availability, driving an increase in soil MNC. Further supporting our findings, Liao et al. [57] reported that nitrogen availability plays a primary role in MNC regulation, and Ma et al. [58] used nitrogen deposition experiments to show than nitrogen addition promotes the formation and accumulation of microbial necromass. We also found that both soil MNC and MBN were significantly higher under light grazing intensity compared to CK. This can be attributed to increased nitrogen inputs to the topsoil under moderate grazing resulting from the deposition of livestock dung and urine [59, 60], which directly affects microbial growth and necromass accumulation. Therefore, nitrogen is an important factor regulating the accumulation of MNC [61]. Additionally, in this study, the responses of soil FNC and BNC to soil nitrogen are different (Fig. 5c, e), which may be attributed to their distinct nutrient requirements and ecological strategies. Fungi primarily decompose complex organic substrates, and their decomposition processes are often associated with high nitrogen demand [62]. In contrast, bacteria possess strong nitrogen-fixing abilities and exhibit high environmental adaptability [63]. Under nitrogen-limited conditions, bacteria tend to preferentially decompose microbial necromass to release available nitrogen, thereby meeting their substrate demands and reducing dependence on external nitrogen inputs [64, 65].

Phosphorus plays a vital role in the accumulation of soil MNC, and we found that P-associated soil nutrients were directly and positively correlated with MNC, FNC, and BNC (Fig. 5). Microbial nitrogen cycling is driven by metabolic activity, which is dependent on phosphorus [66]. Additionally, phosphorus and nitrogen often work synergistically in soil nutrient cycling. Adequate phosphorus availability enhances microbial nitrogen use, particularly in terms of nitrogen assimilation and transformation. Previous research also demonstrates that increases in both soil nitrogen and phosphorus drive higher use efficiency for both nutrients, thereby promoting soil MNC accumulation [67].

Although we found that C-associated nutrients were significantly correlated with soil MNC (Fig. 4), path analysis showed that they did not directly influence soil MNC (Fig. 5), which contrasts with the findings of Deng et al. [68]. This may be attributed to the fact that, although carbon is an essential energy source for microbes, it can only become the primary limiting factor for microbial growth when nitrogen and phosphorus are sufficiently available [69]. However, in desert steppes where nitrogen and phosphorus are more limited and play a dominant role in microbial metabolism, the effect of carbon may be masked [70]. Furthermore, our PLS-PM analysis showed that the C:N and C:P ratios did not have a direct effect on MNC, whereas the N:P ratio exerted a significant direct influence, further supporting this perspective. The N:P ratio, along with nitrogen and phosphorus availability, has been identified as one of the key factors influencing MNC accumulation, which is closely related to the environmental characteristics of desert steppes and the effects of grazing disturbance. In arid ecosystems, MNC is co-regulated by the absolute availability of nutrients and their stoichiometric ratios [68], among which nitrogen and phosphorus play a more dominant role in regulating microbial activity [71]. As grazing intensity increases, both nitrogen and phosphorus contents in the desert steppe decline, with nitrogen showing a greater reduction than phosphorus. Under such nutrient-limited conditions, microbes rely more heavily on sufficient phosphorus supply to enhance nitrogen use efficiency, the imbalance between nitrogen and phosphorus impairs microbial activity, ultimately affecting MNC accumulation [72]. Our previous studies in the research area also demonstrated that grazing significantly exacerbated microbial nitrogen and phosphorus limitations [33, 73], further supporting the findings of this study.

Plant carbon inputs are also an important drivers of soil MNC [3, 7]. We found that grazing-induced reductions in plant biomass, especially litter biomass, indirectly influenced soil MNC, FNC, and BNC by affecting soil nitrogen and phosphorus nutrients, with plant factors having the largest indirect effect (Fig. 5b, d, f). Consistent with our conclusions, Liao et al. [57] demonstrated that plant biomass positively affects soil MNC accumulation but is not the primary driver. This is because aboveground plant parts (i.e., leaves and stems) capture and store carbon in their biomass. Long-term grazing reduces aboveground biomass and leaf area, thereby disrupting carbon and nitrogen balances throughout the entire ecosystem [74, 75]. When these parts return to the soil as litter (e.g., fallen leaves), their carbon content influences microbial activity and nitrogen cycling. Furthermore, plant roots (belowground biomass) secrete exudates (e.g., carbohydrates and organic acids) that serve as carbon sources for soil microorganisms, regulating soil MNC accumulation [76].

Additionally, He et al. [22] emphasized soil depth as an important factor influencing soil MNC, a finding supported by our results. We observed significantly higher MNC in the top 10 cm of soil, with values declining with depth. This trend is consistent with Wu et al. [61] and is likely driven by greater organic inputs in the topsoil, such as root biomass, litter, and livestock manure, which provide nitrogen and enhance microbial activity [77, 78], thereby promoting MNC accumulation. In contrast, the subsoil receives fewer fresh organic inputs, and intensified nitrogen competition between roots and microbes may restrict MNC accumulation. Although MNC content is lower in the subsoil, our results indicate that it makes a relatively greater contribution to SOC at deeper depths, which is consistent with our second hypothesis. In the desert steppe, limited water availability and the surface concentration of livestock excreta reduce the input of plant-derived carbon to the subsoil [79, 80]. In contrast, microbially derived carbon has become an important source of soil organic carbon. Microbial necromass more readily binds with soil minerals, forming stable and less decomposable MNC [13, 81]. Moreover, the subsoil is less affected by climatic and biological disturbances, making its physicochemical properties, especially higher mineral content and stronger binding capacity, more influential [82]. Due to its strong mineral-binding ability, MNC facilitates the formation of mineral-associated organic carbon (MAOC) [83], and plays a crucial role in stabilizing and preserving SOC in the subsoil over the long term. Notably, we found that the FNC:BNC ratio increased significantly with soil depth, indicating a greater contribution of fungi to necromass carbon in deeper soil layers. This may be due to the cell wall and hyphal structures of fungi, which facilitate their association with mineral particles or aggregates, soil minerals tend to retain more FNC in stable forms [84, 85]. Therefore, Soil MNC, particularly FNC, represents an important and stable component of SOC in the subsoil.

Conclusions

In this study, we assessed the impact of different grazing intensities on soil MNC in a desert steppe and evaluated the direct and indirect factors influencing it. Our results revealed that the FNC (2.27–3.77 g/kg) in the desert steppe was greater than the BNC (1.25–2.15 g/kg). Compared with the 0–10 cm soil layer, MNC in the 20–30 cm soil layer significantly decreased by 6.45%, whereas its contribution to SOC significantly increased by 9.54%. Light grazing significantly enhanced MNC and FNC by increasing nutrients associated with nitrogen (total nitrogen, ammonium nitrogen, and microbial biomass nitrogen) and phosphorus (total phosphorus and available phosphorus). Light grazing also elevated BNC by increasing phosphorus-related nutrients (total phosphorus and available phosphorus). Additionally, litter biomass was an indirect driver for MNC, fungal necromass carbon, and bacterial necromass carbon. Our research highlights that light grazing can promote the accumulation of microbial necromass carbon, thereby aiding in the sequestration of soil organic carbon in desert steppe ecosystems, a process primarily regulated by soil nutrients related to nitrogen and phosphorus. The findings offer new insights into the relationship between MNC and soil nutrients and provide a reference for grazing management decisions and the study of grassland carbon sequestration potential.
