ITGA8 suppresses proliferation and metastasis of lung adenocarcinoma through the inhibition of glycolysis
Authors: Shuai-Jun Chen, Xiao-Lin Cui, Qian Li, Ling-Yan Xiao, Shan-Shan Rao, Yu-zhi Lu, Hong-Feng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most prominent subtype of non-small cell lung cancer characterized by high morbidity and mortality. While integrins are known regulators of tumor progression, most function as oncogenes; however, the role of ITGA8 in LUAD remains distinct and poorly understood. Here, we integrated bioinformatics analyses across multiple cohorts (TCGA, GEO) with experimental validation using clinical samples, in vitro assays, and in vivo xenograft models to investigate the function and mechanism of ITGA8. Unlike upregulated integrin members (e.g., ITGA2, ITGA11), ITGA8 was significantly downregulated in LUAD tissues, and its low expression correlated with advanced TNM stage, lymph node metastasis, and poor prognosis. Functionally, ITGA8 overexpression potently inhibited LUAD cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and xenograft tumor growth. Mechanistically, ITGA8 acted as a metabolic gatekeeper by suppressing aerobic glycolysis (decreasing ECAR, glucose uptake, and lactate production) via the activation of the AMPK signaling pathway and subsequent inhibition of the mTOR/S6K/4EBP1 axis. Notably, treatment with an AMPK inhibitor reversed the ITGA8-mediated suppression of glycolysis and malignant phenotypes. Furthermore, analysis of the tumor microenvironment revealed that ITGA8 expression was positively correlated with stromal score and regulatory T cells (Tregs) but robustly negatively correlated with the infiltration of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). Collectively, our findings establish ITGA8 as a novel tumor suppressor in LUAD that constrains malignancy by reprogramming glycolytic metabolism and remodeling the immune microenvironment, suggesting its potential as a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40678-1.

Introduction

Lung cancer ranks as the most prevalent and deadly malignancy globally. Among its subtypes, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) predominates, with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) being the most common histological variant1. Current therapeutic strategies include surgical resection, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy. Despite significant progress in these modalities, the prognosis for NSCLC patients remains unsatisfactory. Precision medicines targeting EGFR mutations and EML4-ALK fusions have significantly improved outcomes, yet they benefit only a specific subset (~ 30%) of LUAD patients2,3. Similarly, although immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs)—such as atezolizumab and pembrolizumab—have revolutionized lung cancer treatment, nearly half of patients exhibit primary resistance or develop acquired resistance during therapy4–6. Therefore, it is imperative to unravel the underlying molecular mechanisms of tumorigenesis and identify novel therapeutic targets for LUAD.

Integrins are essential transmembrane receptors that orchestrate intercellular adhesion, signal transduction, and modulation of the tumor microenvironment (TME)7–10. Aberrant expression of specific integrin subunits has been implicated in the malignant progression of lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), promoting metastasis and therapeutic resistance10–12. In LUAD, several family members, including ITGA3 and ITGB4, are typically upregulated and function as oncogenes promoting invasion and metastasis11,13,14. In stark contrast, the function of ITGA8 in LUAD tumorigenesis is poorly understood. Although bioinformatic analyses indicate that ITGA8 expression is often suppressed in tumor tissues15,16. Experimental validation and mechanistic insights are currently lacking. Consequently, this study aims to elucidate the potential mechanistic role of ITGA8 in LUAD.

High-throughput sequencing has revolutionized biomedical research by enabling the comprehensive profiling of the cancer genome and transcriptome11,17,18. However, the vast quantities of high-dimensional data generated by this technological leap necessitate sophisticated interpretation19. Consequently, advanced bioinformatics algorithms have emerged as indispensable tools for mining and integrating these high-dimensional datasets20. By leveraging these computational strategies, researchers can systematically elucidate the complex mechanisms of tumorigenesis and identify robust biomarkers for patient diagnosis and prognosis21–23. For instance, integrated bioinformatic analyses have been successfully applied to pinpoint therapeutic targets in non-small cell lung cancer24,25 and to establish predictive paradigms for risk stratification in epithelial ovarian cancer26, highlighting the translational potential of data-driven oncology.

Through a combination of bioinformatics analysis and experimental validation, we demonstrated that ITGA8 is significantly downregulated in LUAD tissues. Its reduced expression correlates with advanced Tumor-Node-Metastasis (TNM) stage, higher tumor grade, and a favorable prognosis in patients. Functionally, ITGA8 overexpression suppresses the proliferation, migration, and invasion of LUAD cells in vitro and inhibits tumor growth in vivo. Mechanistically, we revealed that ITGA8 is negatively associated with glycolysis-related pathways, suggesting that it exerts its tumor-suppressive effects by attenuating these signaling cascades. Collectively, our findings highlight the crucial role of ITGA8 in LUAD pathogenesis and suggest it may serve as a promising therapeutic target.

Downregulation of ITGA8 in LUAD tissues

To characterize the transcriptional landscape of integrin alpha (ITGA) subunits in LUAD, we analyzed their expression profiles using the TCGA dataset (535 tumor vs. 59 normal samples). As shown in Fig. 1A, ITGA2 and ITGA11 were upregulated, whereas ITGA8, ITGA10, ITGAL, and ITGAX were downregulated. Notably, ITGA8 exhibited profound downregulation in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues in the TCGA cohort (Fig. 1B, C). This expression pattern was validated externally using the GSE30219 and GSE31210 datasets (Fig. 1D, E). Furthermore, experimental verification via qRT-PCR, Western blot, and immunohistochemistry (IHC) confirmed significantly lower ITGA8 mRNA and protein levels in LUAD tissues compared to matched adjacent controls (Fig. 1F–H). Together, these multi-level analyses confirm the downregulation of ITGA8 in LUAD.

The diagnostic and prognostic significance of ITGA8 expression in LUAD

To explore the clinical significance of ITGA8 expression in LUAD, correlation of ITGA8 mRNA levels with clinical-pathological features was conducted. Based on the ITGA8 expression level, patients were classified as high (upper 50th percentile) and low (lower 50th percentile) expression groups. As shown in Fig. 2A-C, the survival analysis showed that low expression of ITGA8 predicted poor overall survival (OS) and PFS. Next, we analyzed the association between ITGA8 and clinical data. ITGA8 expression was significantly correlated with T stage (P < 0.01), Clinical stage (P < 0.05), lymph node metastasis (P < 0.01), distant metastasis (P < 0.01), gender (P < 0.01), and survival status (P < 0.01) (Fig. 2D-I). In addition, ITGA8 expression level could effectively differentiate LUAD from normal lung tissue (Fig. 2J-M).

To further evaluate the clinical significance of ITGA8 in LUAD, we analyzed the baseline clinicopathological characteristics of patients with LUAD from the TCGA cohort. Based on the median ITGA8 expression levels, patients were categorized into low (n = 264) and high (n = 264) expression groups. The associations between ITGA8 expression and clinical features are summarized in Table 1. Chi-square tests revealed significant correlations between ITGA8 expression and gender, T stage, and clinical stage. Specifically, high ITGA8 expression was more frequently observed in female patients (60.6% vs. 47.3%) and was associated with early T stage (T1: 40.2% vs. 25.0%), consistent with its potential tumor-suppressive role. However, no statistically significant differences were observed regarding age (P = 0.368) and smoking history (P = 0.266). Thus, ITGA8 may serve as a potential diagnostic and prognostic biomarker for LUAD.

ITGA8 overexpression inhibits cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in lung adenocarcinoma cells

To investigate the biological function of ITGA8 in lung adenocarcinoma, we established stable ITGA8-overexpressing A549 and H1299 cell lines via lentiviral transduction (transduction efficiency is verified in Supplementary Fig. 1A). We then assessed cell proliferation using CCK-8, colony formation, and EdU assays. The results demonstrated that the overexpression of ITGA8 significantly suppressed cell proliferation (Fig. 3A-C). Furthermore, ITGA8 overexpression markedly impaired the migratory and invasive capabilities of A549 and H1299 cells (Fig. 4A, B). Collectively, these results indicate that ITGA8 acts as a tumor suppressor by inhibiting LUAD progression.

ITGA8 suppresses LUAD proliferation and metastasis by regulating glycolysis

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying ITGA8 function, we conducted Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) on TCGA LUAD groups categorized by ITGA8 expression. The analysis revealed a significant enrichment of metabolic signatures, including the citrate cycle (TCA cycle), electron transport chain, and glycolysis pathways, in patients with low ITGA8 expression (Fig. 5A, B). Mechanistically, we observed that ITGA8 overexpression markedly elevated p-AMPK levels while suppressing the phosphorylation of mTOR, S6K, and 4EBP1 in LUAD cells (Fig. 5C). Notably, the protein levels of HIF-1α and p-AKT remained unchanged (Fig. 5D). To directly assess the impact of ITGA8 on glycolytic metabolism, we performed functional metabolic assays. As anticipated, ITGA8 overexpression significantly attenuated the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR), a surrogate marker for glycolysis, and concomitantly reduced glucose uptake and lactate production (Fig. 5E, F). These data provide direct evidence that ITGA8 functions as a negative regulator of aerobic glycolysis in LUAD cells. Collectively, these findings suggest that ITGA8 inhibits the progression and metastasis of LUAD by suppressing glycolysis.

Inhibition of AMPK reverses the tumor-suppressive effects of ITGA8

We utilized the AMPK inhibitor Compound C to verify the involvement of the AMPK pathway. In ITGA8-overexpressing cells, Compound C treatment counteracted the inhibitory effects on cell migration and invasion (Fig. 6A). Western blot analysis indicated that the ITGA8-mediated suppression of the mTOR/S6K/4EBP1 axis was blocked by Compound C (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, the reduction in ECAR, glucose uptake, and lactate production observed in ITGA8-overexpressing cells was effectively reversed following AMPK inhibition (Fig. 6C, D). Thus, ITGA8 acts as a tumor suppressor in LUAD largely by inhibiting glycolysis through AMPK activation.

ITGA8 overexpression inhibits xenograft tumor growth

To further investigate the effect of ITGA8 on LUAD cell proliferation in vivo, A549 cells were transduced with empty vector or ITGA8-overexpressing lentiviruses and subsequently injected subcutaneously into nude mice. Tumor volumes were monitored every four days. On day 32, the mice were sacrificed, and the tumors were excised, imaged, and weighed. As shown in Fig. 7A–C, tumors in the ITGA8-overexpressing group were significantly smaller and weighed significantly less than those in the control group. Consistently, tumor tissues derived from the ITGA8-overexpressing group exhibited weaker Ki-67 staining compared to the control group (Fig. 7D). These findings indicate that ITGA8 significantly suppresses LUAD tumorigenesis in vivo.

ITGA8 expression correlates with tumor microenvironment remodeling and immune infiltration in LUAD

Integrin family members, including ITGA8, profoundly influence the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) by mediating cell-extracellular matrix (ECM) interactions and facilitating crosstalk with immune cells. Emerging evidence indicates that integrins participate in tumor initiation, progression, and immune evasion through multifaceted mechanisms, such as regulating the tumor microenvironment (TME), modulating immune cell infiltration and function, and orchestrating key signaling pathways27,28. To further investigate the role of ITGA8 in the TME of LUAD, we performed a comprehensive correlation analysis using the TIMER 3.0 database29. Our results revealed that ITGA8 expression was strongly and positively correlated with the stroma score (rho = 0.536, P < 0.001) and negatively correlated with tumor purity (rho = -0.403, P < 0.001), suggesting that ITGA8 is a key player in stromal remodeling within the TME of LUAD (Figure S2A, B).

Regarding immune infiltration, ITGA8 showed a significant positive association with the abundance of resting CD4 + memory T cells (rho = 0.470, P < 0.001) and regulatory T cells (Tregs; rho = 0.414, P < 0.001), but was inversely correlated with activated CD4 + memory T cells (rho = -0.310, P < 0.001), Th1 cells (rho = -0.519, P < 0.001), and Th2 cells (rho = -0.485, P < 0.001). Consistently, ITGA8 expression was positively linked to the T cell dysfunction score (rho = 0.341, P < 0.001). Interestingly, a robust negative correlation was observed between ITGA8 and the infiltration of MDSCs (rho = -0.389, P < 0.001) (Figure S2A, B). These findings indicate that while ITGA8 is associated with a more “dormant” or dysfunctional immune profile (characterized by high resting T cells and Tregs), it may simultaneously exert its anti-tumor effects by significantly inhibiting the recruitment of pro-tumorigenic MDSCs, thereby contributing to a unique immune-modulatory landscape in LUAD.

Discussion

Integrins are canonical mediators of cell–ECM interactions, and their dysregulation is a well-established driver of tumor initiation, progression, and metastasis30. Within the landscape of LUAD, the prevailing view has largely focused on the oncogenic reliance of tumor cells on specific integrin subunits. For instance, ITGA2 and ITGA5 are known to potentiate invasion and chemoresistance, while ITGA11 drives cancer-associated fibroblast activation28,31–33. However, our findings challenge this uniform narrative by characterizing ITGA8 not as a promoter, but as a crucial restraint on LUAD progression. This posits a complex, context-dependent functional dichotomy within the integrin family, where ITGA8 functions in stark contrast to its pro-tumorigenic counterparts.

The identification of ITGA8 as a potential tumor suppressor in our study aligns with emerging evidence in other malignancies, where its silencing correlates with aggressive phenotypes33,34. However, distinct from previous reports that primarily attribute integrin functions to physical adhesion or cytoskeletal reorganization, our data underscore a systemic impact on cell survival. The strong correlation we observed between suppressed ITGA8 and poor patient survival suggests that ITGA8 loss is not merely a passenger event but a pivotal driver of the malignant trajectory in LUAD. By restoring ITGA8, we effectively curtailed the proliferative and invasive capacity of LUAD cells, reinforcing the concept that the balance of specific integrin subunits determines the net outcome of tumor behavior.

Integrins, as crucial cell surface receptors, mediate cell-ECM and cell-cell interactions, playing a pivotal role in tumor metabolic reprogramming11,30. While our study establishes ITGA8 as a key regulator of glycolysis in LUAD, it is essential to contextualize this finding within the broader integrin family, where ITGA8 shares common regulatory principles with other subunits but also exhibits distinct mechanistic characteristics. Mechanistically, a commonality among integrin members is their ability to transduce bidirectional signals (“inside-out” and “outside-in”), converting external stimuli into intracellular biochemical responses that drive the Warburg effect35. Classically, integrins such as αvβ3, αvβ5, β1, β3, α2β1 and α5β1 engage with ECM ligands to activate Focal Adhesion Kinase (FAK) and Src. This cascade primarily activates the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway, a central hub that upregulates glucose transporter (GLUT1) expression and activates glycolytic enzymes (HK2, PFK2) to support rapid tumor proliferation11,30. Additionally, integrins (e.g., via α5β1) can modulate the MAPK/ERK pathway, further reinforcing the glycolytic phenotype36. These represent the shared, canonical mechanisms by which the integrin family generally promotes anabolism. Despite these commonalities, different integrin subunits utilize unique mechanisms to fine-tune metabolism depending on their specific ligands and downstream effectors. For instance, in terms of ligand-dependent regulation, ITGA3 specifically interacts with cancer cell-derived Type I Collagen (Col1) to activate the FAK/PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis in an autocrine manner, specifically upregulating HIF1α and c-Myc to drive glycolysis in pancreatic cancer37. Regarding target specificity, ITGB4 has been shown to enhance glycolysis in triple-negative breast cancer by specifically upregulating the lactate transporter MCT1 via the FAK-AKT- HIF1α axis, facilitating the lactate loop38; furthermore, ITGB4 can act intercellularly by transferring proteins via exosomes to Cancer-Associated Fibroblasts (CAFs), inducing BNIP3L-dependent mitophagy and glycolysis in the stroma39. In the context of mechanical sensing, ITGB1 distinguishes itself by sensing ECM stiffness and activating Rac to modify actin structure and release Aldolase A, thereby mechanically tuning glycolytic flux to adapt to environmental physical cues40. Our data provide novel insights suggesting that ITGA8 acts as a metabolic gatekeeper. The observed activation of the mTOR/AMPK/S6K signaling axis upon ITGA8 depletion implies that this integrin normally functions to maintain metabolic homeostasis. This finding expands the current understanding of integrin signaling beyond structural support to include the fine-tuning of intracellular energetics, offering a molecular explanation for the aggressive nature of ITGA8-low tumors.

Building upon the established role of integrins in regulating the TME and ECM remodeling41,42, our study uncovers a distinct stromal profile associated with ITGA8 in LUAD. We observed a robust positive correlation between ITGA8 expression and the stroma score, alongside a negative correlation with tumor purity. This suggests that ITGA8 is likely expressed by stromal components or acts as a key organizer of the stromal architecture. Unlike the osteopontin-integrin axis described in other contexts that promotes a malignant, invasive stroma43, the high-stroma phenotype in ITGA8-high tumors appears to function as a constraint on tumor cellularity. This aligns with the notion that specific integrin-mediated ECM assemblies can act as physical barriers or “restraining tissues” rather than permissive soils for tumor outgrowth. A striking finding of our immune infiltration analysis is the specific immunomodulatory landscape orchestrated by ITGA8. Consistent with the role of integrins in regulating T cell retention. such as the formation of resident memory phenotypes44, we found that ITGA8 correlates with resting CD4 + memory T cells and regulatory T cells (Tregs), while being inversely associated with activated Th1/Th2 subsets. At first glance, the enrichment of Tregs and T cell dysfunction scores might suggest an immunosuppressive environment. However, when viewed in conjunction with ITGA8’s tumor-suppressive role, this profile may reflect a state of “immune quiescence” or tissue homeostasis rather than active tumor-induced immune evasion. ITGA8 may help maintain a physiological stromal barrier that limits the excessive inflammatory activation often required for rapid tumor expansion.

Currently, the therapeutic exploitation of integrins is a focal point of precision oncology45. Strategies focusing on overexpressed subunits, such as radiolabeled peptides targeting αvβ6 in pancreatic cancer46 or blockade of the α6β4-FAK/YAP/TAZ axis in NSCLC47, have demonstrated favorable pharmacokinetics and efficacy in preclinical models. Moreover, addressing integrin-mediated resistance—such as that observed in glioma stem cells48—has driven the development of peptide-drug conjugates (e.g., targeting αvβ3) to improve delivery and minimize toxicity49,50. However, distinct from these strategies that aim to inhibit oncogenic integrins, our findings with ITGA8 present a different therapeutic paradigm: addressing the loss of a tumor suppressor. Since restoring ITGA8 protein directly is technically challenging, targeting the downstream metabolic consequences of its loss becomes paramount. Our data suggest that ITGA8-low tumors exhibit hyperactive mTOR signaling and glycolytic dependency; thus, these patients might derive particular benefit from mTOR inhibitors or glycolysis-targeting agents—a stratified approach that warrants precise clinical validation.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations of our study must be acknowledged. First, while we validated our findings in clinical tissue samples, the sample size remains relatively limited, necessitating validation in larger, multi-center cohorts. Second, our mechanistic exploration relied primarily on in vitro and in vivo models; the precise molecular interaction by which ITGA8 engages the mTOR/AMPK complex remains to be fully crystallized through structural biology or co-immunoprecipitation assays. Finally, as integrins significantly influence the TME51,52, our current study focused largely on tumor-intrinsic effects. Future investigations should extend to how ITGA8 loss influences immune infiltration and stromal interactions in the complex lung TME.

In summary, this study conceptually advances the field by defining ITGA8 as a distinct tumor suppressor in LUAD that constrains malignancy through the modulation of metabolic signaling. By delineating the ITGA8-mTOR-glycolysis axis, we provide a rationale for targeting metabolic dependencies in ITGA8-defective tumors, highlighting a novel therapeutic strategy for this aggressive subset of lung cancer.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and processing

Publicly available gene expression data and corresponding clinical annotations were retrospectively collected from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) databases. Level 3 RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) data (read counts) and clinical information of LUAD patients were obtained from the TCGA portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). The ENSEMBL gene IDs were mapped to gene symbols using the appropriate annotation file for subsequent analysis.

Two independent LUAD microarray datasets were downloaded from the GEO database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo). GSE30219: Comprising 293 lung tumor samples and 14 non-tumor (normal) samples. GSE31210: Consisting of 226 patients with pathological stage I–II lung adenocarcinoma and 20 normal lung tissue samples. All data were processed and normalized prior to differential expression and survival analyses.

Clinical samples

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Review Board of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Approval No. TJ-IRB20210222). All methods involving human participants were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. Fifty treatment-naive LUAD patients from Tongji Hospital were enrolled, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study. Paired tumor tissues and adjacent non-cancerous specimens were collected, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 °C until further analysis.

Materials and reagents

The primary antibodies used in this study (anti-ITGA8, anti-p-mTOR, anti-p-AMPK, anti-p-S6K, anti-p-4EBP1, anti-mTOR, anti-AMPK, anti-S6K, and anti-4EBP1) were all purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA). The RNA Extraction Kit and the Reverse Transcription and qPCR Kit were both sourced from Yeasen (Shanghai, China).

Cell culture and transfection

The human LUAD cell lines A549 and H1299 were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA). All cells were cultured in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C. To construct the ITGA8 overexpression plasmid, full-length human ITGA8 cDNA (NM_003638.3) was amplified by PCR and cloned into the pLVX-Puro expression vector (Genomeditech, Shanghai, China). To generate stable cell lines, A549 and H1299 cells were transduced with the lentiviral vectors and selected with puromycin according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

CCK8, colony formation, and 5-ethynyl-29-deoxyuridine (EdU) assays

Cell proliferation was evaluated through the CCK8, colony formation, and EdU assays. In CCK8 assay, 4 × 103 cells were seeded in each well of 96-well plates, and then incubated under identical conditions. 10 µl CCK-8 reagent (Topscience, Shanghai, China) was added to each well and incubation continued for 2 h. The absorbance of every well was detected by a microplate reader at 450 nm. For the colony formation assay, A549 and H1299 were grown in 35-mm dishes (500 cells each well) overnight for cell attachment. Cells were cultured for 2 weeks and medium containing 10% FBS was changed every 3 days. The colonies were fixed with methanol, stained with 0.01% crystal violet solution for 1 h, and then washed with water. An EdU Cell Proliferation Assay Kit (Topscience, Shanghai, China) was used for the CCK8 assays. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates. Following incubation with 50 mM EDU for 6 h, cells were fixed, permeabilized, and stained with EDU, as described by the manufacturer. Cell nuclei were stained for 20 min with DAPI at a concentration of 1 mg/ml. Positive cells were determined by fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Cell migration and invasion assays

Cell migration and invasion were examined using Trans-well assays. A549 and H1299 cells (1 × 105 cells per insert) cultured in 200 µl serum-free DMEM medium were plated on the upper insert (8-µm diameter pore size, Corning, NY, USA), while 800 µl DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS was added to the bottom chamber. After 24 h, the migrated cells were fixed with 95% ethanol and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Under a light microscope, cells were photographed and counted in five randomly selected optical fields. Cell invasion assay was conducted in a similar way to cell migration assay except for the pre-coating of the Transwell membrane with Matrigel.

Wound-healing assay

The wound scratch assay was used to determine the migration capabilities of LUAD cells. A549 and H1299 cells (8 × 105) were cultured for 24 h in 6-well plates until confluence was reached (> 90%). A scratch was made using a 10-µl pipette tip on the midline of the culture well. Cells were washed with PBS and incubated in growth medium. Images of wound width was captured at 0 and 24 h to assess the migration of cells and the healing rate.

Xenograft assays in nude mice

All animal experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Approval No. 3509). All procedures were performed in strict accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, and the study is reported in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org). Six-week-old BALB/c nude mice purchased from Weitong Lihua Limited Company (Beijing, China) were used for our studies. A total of 2 × 10⁶ A549 cells, transduced with either empty vector or ITGA8-overexpressing lentiviruses, were injected subcutaneously into the flanks of nude mice (n = 5 per group). Tumor growth (a: the longest diameter of tumor; b: the shortest diameter of tumor) was regularly monitored and measured with Vernier caliper, and the tumor volume (V) was calculated following formula: V = a×b2/2. Before tumor collection, mice were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) to minimize pain and distress. At the end of the experiments (32 days post-injection), mice were humanely euthanized by overdose of sodium pentobarbital (150 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) in accordance with institutional animal welfare protocols. The xenograft tumors were then harvested, photographed, and used for subsequent experiments.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Xenograft tumors and LUAD samples were fixed in 4% formalin overnight at room temperature, immersed in 70% ethanol, and then embedded in paraffin. The samples embedded in paraffin were cut into 4 mm sections and affixed to slides. After deparaffinization in xylene and rehydration in graded ethanol, antigen unmasking was carried out in 10 mM citrate buffer (pH = 6). The slides were blocked with 5% bovine albumin at room temperature for 1 h, followed by an overnight incubation with primary antibody at 4 °C. Next day, UltraSensitive™ SP IHC Kit (MXB biotechnologies, Fuzhou, China) was applied to incubate the secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Following the incubation with 3,3’-diaminobenzidine, the slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and sealed. Images were acquired with an Olympus microscope system (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and subsequently quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.52; NIH, USA).

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

H&E staining was performed according to a standard protocol. Deparaffinized and rehydrated sections were first stained with hematoxylin solution (Beyotime, Wuhan, China) for 5 min. Slides were then differentiated by 5 dips in 1% acid ethanol (1% HCl in 75% ethanol) and rinsed thoroughly with distilled water. Subsequently, sections were counterstained with eosin solution (Beyotime, Wuhan, China) for 3 min. Finally, the slides were dehydrated through a graded alcohol series, cleared in xylene, and coverslipped. All sections were visualized and photographed using an Olympus optical microscope (BX51, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA from tissues or cells was extracted with RNA extraction kit (Yeasen, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturers’ instruction. The RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA employing the Hifair® III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Yeasen, Shanghai, China). In a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, CA, USA), mRNA expression was determined using qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen, Shanghai, China). An endogenous GAPDH mRNA standard was used to normalize mRNA amounts. Calculations of gene expression levels were performed using the 2-ΔΔCt method. The primers used were the following. For human ITGA8, forward primer: 5’-caccctccgcagattgttct-3’; reverse primer: 5’-ttcatcccgggcagcgaaag-3’. For human GAPDH, forward primer: 5’-ttccacccatggcaaattcc-3’; reverse primer: 5’-tggttcacacccatgacgaa-3’.

Western blot

Cells and xenograft tissues were homogenized and lysed with RIPA lysis buffer that contains protease inhibitors (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) and phosphatase inhibitor (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). The protein of each lysate was measured by BCA assay (Thermo, USA) and mixed with loading buffer (Bio-Rad, Shanghai, China). The equal amounts proteins were loaded on 6%–12% gradient SDS-PAGE and then transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking membranes with 5% milk in TBST for 1–2 h, primary antibodies were applied overnight at 4 °C in antibody diluent. Next day, the membranes were washed and incubated for one hour with a secondary antibody. Immunoreactive bands were detected using ECL reagents (Biosharp, Guangzhou, China) and images were captured on ChemiScope6100 chemiluminescence imaging system (Clinx, Shanghai, China).

XF Glycolysis Stress Test (Glycolysis experiment)

ECAR was measured using the Seahorse XF Glycolysis Stress Test Kit. Cells were incubated in unbuffered glycolysis assay medium (DMEM without phenol red, 143 mM NaCl, 2 mM glutamine, pH 7.4) for 1 h at 37 °C in a CO2-free incubator. After measuring non-glycolytic acidification, sequential injections were performed to determine key glycolytic parameters: glucose (10 mM) for basal glycolysis, oligomycin (1 µM) for glycolytic capacity, and 2-Deoxyglucose (2-DG, 100 mM) to inhibit glycolysis. The glycolytic reserve was defined as the difference between maximal capacity and basal glycolysis. Data were normalized to a seeding density of 10,000 cells per well.

Glucose consumption and lactate production measurement

Cells were seeded into six-well plates. Upon adherence, the culture medium was replaced with serum-free medium. Following a 24-hour incubation, the culture supernatants were collected for analysis. Glucose consumption and lactate production were quantified using a Glucose Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and a Lactic Acid Assay Kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China), respectively, following the manufacturers’ protocols. All results were normalized to the total protein content of the corresponding cells.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA) and expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean. To detect differences between two or more groups, t-tests and one-way ANOVA tests were used. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was used to assess the overall survival (OS) of LUAD patients expressing ITGA8. The significance level was set at p < 0.05. A minimum of N = 3 biological replicates and n = 3 technical replicates were used in all experiments. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001. Availability of data and materials.
