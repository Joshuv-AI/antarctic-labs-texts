Characterizing models for delivery of pharmacogenomic testing: a scoping review
Authors: Zeina Waheed, Mary Bunka, Louisa Edwards, Chad Bousman, Alison Hoens, Jehannine (J9) Austin, Stirling Bryan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPharmacogenomic (PGx) testing can help guide medication prescribing for a wide range of health indications. The objective of this scoping review was to understand how PGx testing has been clinically implemented and learn from these experiences. Research questions guiding this work were: (1) what different models for delivery of PGx testing have been employed? (2) what are the characteristics of each delivery model? and (3) what are the reported facilitators and barriers associated with each delivery model? A total of 134 articles reported on 125 PGx initiatives spanning 19 countries. Four unique delivery models were identified: sole prescriber-led (n = 45), prescriber-led within an interdisciplinary care team (n = 34), community pharmacist-led (n = 16), and PGx consultation service (n = 30). The unique combination of characteristics, and reported facilitators and barriers yielded distinct strengths and challenges for each identified delivery model. Findings from this review can help inform future implementation planning or expansion of PGx initiatives by presenting different delivery models that may be employed and the corresponding considerations for each approach. This information can help inform future implementers in the selection of one or more approaches that may be most suitable based on their unique contextual needs, and available infrastructures or resources.KEYWORDS: pharmacogenomics, pharmacogenetics, implementation, healthcare delivery models, prescribing optimization, precision medicine, scoping review

Introduction

Pharmacogenomic (PGx) testing analyzes variations in genes which can affect how certain medications are metabolized or transported within the body [1]. PGx testing can guide selection of a medication, medication class or dosage that may be more effective or better tolerated by the individual [1–3]. Genotyping methods can be applied preemptively or reactively. Preemptive testing involves examining a variety of genetic variations prior to there being any specific indication to do so, and results are stored in an individual’s electronic health record (EHR) to be used at a future date when a relevant medication may be prescribed. Reactive testing involves conducting testing directly prior to prescribing a medication or in response to the individual’s experience with a previous medication [4,5]. PGx testing has emerged as a promising tool to help guide medication selection and dosing for a wide range of health indications including cardiovascular and psychiatric conditions, and cancer treatment [6–10]. PGx testing has been incorporated into clinical care since the early 2000s, and its use in medication prescribing now spans the globe, with PGx initiatives established across North America, Europe, Asia, and Oceania [5,11–16]. Most studies have suggested that adoption of PGx-guided treatment would be cost-effective or cost-saving for a variety of commonly prescribed medications, including clopidogrel, carbamazepine, and antidepressants [17,18].

Given the broad range of clinical applications of PGx-guided prescribing, implementation can be undertaken within a variety of different healthcare settings (e.g., hospitals, specialized care centers, primary care clinics, pharmacies) and involve diverse healthcare professionals (HCPs), clinical workflows, and resources. Luczak et al., previously reviewed PGx clinical programs and presented a generalized two-appointment clinical ambulatory workflow structure for PGx testing [5]. Furthermore, Medwid et al., recently described three models of PGx delivery based on the setting in which PGx testing was applied: hospital or specialist, family or primary care practice, and direct-to-consumer [19]. However, in addition to considering the setting in which PGx testing is delivered, it is also important to think about the delivery models through which testing can be provided, for example, involving different workflows and/or types of HCP (e.g., physicians, nurses, pharmacists and/or genetic counselors) [12,20,21]. A broader overview of PGx delivery models and the factors that have affected successful implementation can guide future implementers in selecting the most suitable approach. An important precursor to successful implementation is understanding the relevant facilitators and barriers. Previous reviews examining facilitators and barriers to clinical implementation of PGx testing have not focused on delivery models, but rather on a specific genotyping method (e.g., preemptive PGx testing) [22], healthcare setting (e.g., primary care) [23], health condition (e.g., psychiatry) [24,25], or any approach to PGx implementation [26].

Therefore, the objective of this scoping review was to understand the different delivery models through which PGx testing has been clinically implemented across healthcare settings. The research questions guiding this work were: (1) what different models for delivery of PGx testing have been applied? (2) what are the characteristics of each delivery model? and (3) what are the reported facilitators and barriers associated with each delivery model?

Methods

A scoping review was conducted following a combined framework informed by Arksey and O’Malley [27], the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) statement and checklist [28], PRISMA flow diagram [29], and guidance from the JBI (formerly the Joanna Briggs Institute) [30]. This approach was chosen to better understand and characterize different delivery models for PGx testing, recognize facilitators and barriers associated with each model, and identify knowledge gaps [28]. The inclusion criteria consisted of English language journal articles published from 2000 onwards, reporting on the clinical implementation of PGx testing for any health condition and in any healthcare setting. This review considered experimental and quasi-experimental study designs (e.g., randomized controlled trials, non-randomized controlled trials), analytical or descriptive observational studies, qualitative studies, opinion pieces, and institutional profiles. Studies were included if they provided sufficient details describing the delivery model for PGx testing including the HCPs involved in the delivery and their roles, clinical workflows, testing logistics, approaches/strategies adopted, and resources/infrastructures used. Systematic reviews were excluded.

An experienced research librarian was consulted to develop the search strategy comprising search terms identified from MeSH subject headings based on the concepts of pharmacogenomics AND healthcare AND implementation (detailed search strategies can be found in the supplementary material). Four databases were searched (MEDLINE, CINAHL, PsycINFO, EMBASE), with an initial search conducted in January 2023 and updated in November 2024. Additional records were also identified via citation searching. Following the searches, citations were collated and uploaded into EndNote Version 20 [31] and duplicates were automatically removed. Abstract and title screening was conducted by one author (ZW). Full-text articles were screened by three independent reviewers (75% by ZW, and 12.5% by MB and LE each). Prior to the full-text review, each reviewer fully assessed inclusion of 10 randomly selected articles to ensure congruence on the understanding of the inclusion/exclusion criteria. Any uncertainty regarding eligibility was discussed and agreed upon between the reviewers prior to initiation of the full-text review. Data extraction was completed by two authors (ZW and MB). Data extracted included descriptive characteristics (geographical location, healthcare setting, program start date), clinical characteristics (health indication for PGx testing, medication class), workflow (HCPs and their roles, process for identifying eligible patients, decision-making following actionable results), logistics (ordering test, sample collection and format, testing location, results storage/access), and lessons learned (facilitators, barriers). Data extraction was supplemented with additional sources (e.g., PGx program website) as needed for clarifications such as healthcare setting and geographical location.

Given the research questions and data extracted, a narrative synthesis approach was used following guidance by Popay et al. [32]. The initiatives identified through the review were used to classify different delivery models for PGx testing. Classification was based on the type(s) of HCP(s) involved in the delivery of PGx testing, and the roles and workflows involved in the delivery to patients. Characteristics of the initiatives were then tabulated and presented by frequency and percentage for each delivery model.

Facilitators and barriers reported by the studies were organized based on the domains presented by the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) 2.0 [33,34]. The CFIR is a commonly used framework that provides a structure for identifying factors that may influence the implementation of an intervention. The CFIR consists of five domains: (1) Innovation (i.e., PGx testing) characteristics – features of the innovation that may influence implementation, (2) Outer Setting (e.g., health system) – the wider economic, policy and social context in which the intervention is implemented, (3) Inner Setting (e.g., healthcare setting) – the structural, organizational and cultural context in which the intervention will be implemented, (4) Individuals – the attributes, attitudes and beliefs of the individuals involved in implementation, and (5) Process – the way in which implementation is actively progressed. The CFIR was used to guide data organization to aid analysis and allow comparison with previously published studies. Given that the CFIR is a commonly used tool to inform and evaluate implementation, it also allowed us to identify and group which important aspects of implementation were addressed by the included articles. Barriers and facilitators related to research activities were excluded. Ideas webbing was used to identify facilitators and barriers that were commonly reported across all the delivery models and are presented separately [32,35]. Current knowledge gaps were identified by critically assessing the literature to uncover topics that were underreported (e.g., different types of HCPs) or questions that were largely unanswered (e.g., accessibility for patients outside of metropolitan locations).

Results

Overview of identified PGx initiatives

A total of 134 articles met the inclusion criteria and were included in the review (Figure 1). One hundred and twenty-five different PGx initiatives were identified from the included articles. The majority of the PGx initiatives were based in the United States (N = 84, 67%). The remaining initiatives spanned across Europe (N = 24, e.g., Netherlands, Spain, Switzerland), Canada (N = 11), Asia (N = 4), and Australia/New Zealand (N = 2).

Preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) flow diagram [29]. An initial search and citation searching was conducted in January 2023 with an update in November 2024.

Different PGx delivery models

The 125 PGx initiatives identified were organized into four main delivery models that were classified based on the HCP leading the delivery of PGx testing (i.e., HCP who drives the majority of the PGx service, including identifying patient eligibility, test ordering, results interpretation), involvement of other HCPs, and the overall clinical workflow: (1) sole prescriber-led (n = 45), (2) prescriber-led within an interdisciplinary team (n = 34), (3) community pharmacist-led (n = 16), and (4) PGx consultation service (n = 30) (Figure 2). A full list of included initiatives is presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Models of delivery for pharmacogenomic (PGx) testing.

Sole prescriber-led

This model consists of the prescriber, most often a physician (or, in one case, a nurse practitioner [36]), providing all PGx-related services to the patient, including recommending and ordering the test, and using the results to guide medication prescribing. For some initiatives in specialized centres/clinics (for example, for cardiovascular or psychiatric conditions), PGx testing was ordered as part of a standard care plan [36–39]. In this model, the prescriber is largely supported by guidance from clinical decision support (CDS) tools built into the electronic health record (EHR) which may alert them to offer PGx testing prior to prescription of a PGx-relevant medication, and/or actionable recommendations for medication prescription. In some cases, an on-call pharmacist with subject matter expertise was available to answer PGx-related questions the prescriber may have, either as part of the practice or through a third-party testing facility [40,41].

Prescriber-led within interdisciplinary team

This model consists of a physician working collaboratively with an interdisciplinary team to offer PGx testing services to the patient during their care. The distinction between this model and the sole prescriber-led model is that within this model other HCPs in the care team also directly interact with the patient to provide PGx-based care. The team will most often include an integrated clinical pharmacist who may already be providing Medication Therapy Management services [42]. Additional team members may include genetic counselors, clinical pharmacologists, nurses, and/or medical geneticists [43,44]. In general, the physician first discusses PGx testing with the patient, and the test is ordered either immediately by the physician or after consultation with the pharmacist to confirm that testing is appropriate. PGx results are returned to a member of the team (e.g., pharmacist, genetic counselor or clinical pharmacologist) who develops recommendations based on their interpretation of the results, as well as the patient’s history and concurrent medications. These recommendations are shared with the physician (and often discussed with the whole care team) before the physician makes the final decision regarding medication prescription. Results are also shared with the patient in a follow-up visit with the physician, pharmacist, or genetic counselor.

Community pharmacist-led

This model consists of a pharmacist working in a community pharmacy setting to provide most of the PGx-related services to the patient, including recommending PGx testing, ordering the test, and receiving the PGx results. PGx testing is generally suggested by the pharmacist during the patient’s visit to the pharmacy for prescription filling or renewal. However, in some initiatives, patients can also request PGx testing themselves or be referred by their primary care provider [45,46]. After the pharmacist receives the PGx results, they interpret the findings and develop final prescribing recommendations which are communicated to the patient’s prescriber. The prescriber reviews the recommendations and decides whether any changes to the medication or dosage will be made. The pharmacist also shares the results with the patient in a follow-up appointment.

PGx consultation service

This model consists of an interdisciplinary team that is most often led by a clinical pharmacist; (less commonly: a clinical pharmacologist [47], genetic counselor [48], or clinical PGx specialist [2]) which provides a referral-based PGx consultation service. Other individuals who may be part of the service team include molecular pathologists, medical geneticists, nurses, and/or administrative staff. Patients are referred via phone or through the EHR for a consultation by their physician. The consultation service lead meets with the patient to assess whether PGx testing is appropriate, collects patient history, and orders the PGx test, if suitable. In some cases, the decision to order the PGx test is discussed with the entire consultation team prior to ordering. The referring physician receives a consult note/report comprised of the final recommendations based on the PGx results and the patient’s medication history. The consultation team also often discusses the PGx results with the physician, as well as with the patient during a follow-up visit.

Characteristics by delivery model

Approximately half (59/125, 47%) of the PGx initiatives were based in a hospital or medical center (Table 1). Additional common healthcare settings were primary care clinics (27/125, 22%) or specialized centers/clinics (e.g., cancer centers, psychiatric clinics, retirement communities) (25/125, 20%). For identifying eligible patients for PGx testing, the sole prescriber-led model frequently had supportive resources, such as PGx testing recommendation alerts within the EHR (8/45, 18%) and standard care plans that include PGx testing (13/45, 29%). Patient self-referral for PGx testing was also present in some capacity in community pharmacist-led (3/16, 19%) or PGx consultation service models (4/30, 13%). The exact process for ordering PGx tests was infrequently reported ( < 15% of initiatives), but some methods included direct ordering by the HCP from a third-party company (for sole prescriber- or community pharmacy-led models) or requesting a PGx test through the EHR. For initiatives that involved a pharmacist or PGx expert, particularly the prescriber-led within an interdisciplinary team and PGx consult service models, an appointment with the patient was arranged to obtain their history and list of concurrent medications prior to ordering the PGx test (8/34, 24% and 17/30, 57%, respectively).

Sample collection was most often completed within a healthcare appointment (e.g., during the initial visit, next follow-up, or during routine bloodwork). However, all models did have some instances of using at-home self-collection kits that were mailed to the patient (most common in community pharmacist-led or PGx consultation service initiatives). Buccal swabs were more often used than saliva tubes across all delivery models, but especially in community-pharmacist led initiatives (9/16, 56% vs 5/16, 31%, respectively). Blood draw was also present in all delivery models, except community pharmacist-led initiatives. PGx testing was either conducted by a third-party testing facility (e.g., commercial laboratory) or in-house (e.g., within the hospital laboratory). However, community pharmacist-led initiatives primarily used third-party facilities (11/16, 69%).

PGx test results were commonly accessed within the EHR for prescriber-led (46/79, 58%) or PGx consultation service initiatives (21/30, 70%), but this was generally not possible for community pharmacist-led initiatives. Results were also often sent to the HCP who ordered the PGx test through mail or fax, which was the most common method for community pharmacist-led initiatives (11/16, 69%). Accessing results through a third-party company’s online portal was relatively rare (7/125, 6% of all initiatives).

Recommendations for actionable PGx results for prescriber-led (33/79, 42%) and PGx consultation service initiatives (13/30, 43%) commonly made use of CDS tools with an alert system built into the EHR. Most initiatives, except those that were sole prescriber-led, also included a review of the patient’s history and concurrent medications by a pharmacist to help inform the final prescribing recommendations. For initiatives that consisted of team-based care (i.e., prescriber-led within an interdisciplinary team and PGx consultation service), the task of sharing PGx results with the patient was distributed between the prescriber (15/64, 23%), pharmacist (20/64, 31%) and genetic counselor (8/64, 13%). A small portion of all four of the different delivery models also reported that patients received a copy of their PGx results during a follow-up appointment, but this occurred most frequently in the community pharmacist-led model (5/16, 31%).

Facilitators and barriers reported across all delivery models

Facilitators for clinical implementation of PGx testing that were reported by all delivery models mapped to the Outer and Inner Settings CFIR domains and included institutional support, personalized PGx education/training for HCPs and support staff, and CDS tools built into the EHR (Table 2). Institutional support included leadership creating an environment that is open to translating new discoveries and innovations into routine patient care, and collaboration across the organization, as well as essential financial backing allowing the set-up of required infrastructures and resources, and reimbursement for PGx testing and PGx-related services provided by HCPs and/or support staff [21,47,49–51]. Recommended strategies include a commitment from leadership, establishment of a multidisciplinary PGx oversight committee to oversee implementation, and financial grants to fund the initiative [49,50]. Strategies for providing impactful, personalized PGx training included the use of multiple formats that were tailored to the appropriate audience (e.g., in-person meetings/workshops, on-demand virtual resources, printed materials/tip sheets), and patient-centered case-based educational materials [12,36,52,53]. Although active CDS tools built into the EHR was commonly reported as a facilitator for successful implementation, the CDS along with the associated alert system needs to be carefully designed to allow user-friendly incorporation of PGx results and prevent alert fatigue [54]. Furthermore, engagement with on-site developers familiar with the existing EHR infrastructure, and collaboration between local experts (e.g., clinicians, bioinformatics specialists) was deemed essential [4,55].

Common barriers reported across all delivery models spanned Innovation, Individuals, and Process domains of the CFIR. These included uncertainty surrounding the clinical utility of PGx testing, the need for PGx education/training for HCPs and support staff, lack of clear clinical guidelines, and difficulty integrating PGx results into the EHR (Table 3). Although most initiatives reported the Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium and Dutch Pharmacogenetics Working Group guidelines as important resources, these guidelines mainly provide recommendations on how PGx test results can be used for clinical practice. There remains the need to develop guidelines (or add to existing clinical practice guidelines such as those developed by the Canadian Network for Mood and Anxiety Treatments, or the American Psychiatric Association) that provide information on when PGx testing should be ordered and how to order testing. Apprehension from HCPs regarding the clinical utility of PGx testing stemmed from a lack of clarity on the translation of the genotype results to the patient’s phenotype, especially if concomitant medications are involved or medications whose function may also be affected by additional pharmacodynamic factors [12,56]. Limitations in the PGx evidence base, particularly for certain ancestry groups and pediatric patients, also produced some skepticism about the potential benefits of PGx testing for all populations [11,15]. An additional challenge to overcome was the time and resources required to plan and execute PGx training programs, and development of clear clinical guidelines [57]. However, these were important investments to enable HCPs to effectively deliver PGx testing by providing guidance on identifying eligible patients, using PGx results and managing patient concerns/expectations. The challenge of integrating PGx results into the EHR was also an important barrier to overcome, given that many EHR systems do not support the data formats needed to integrate genetic test results [58]. Strategies to mitigate this barrier included incorporating additional time and resources during implementation planning, establishing coded standards for genotyping results, and collaboration between relevant experts (e.g., IT, clinicians, laboratory personnel) [59,60].

Facilitators and barriers by delivery model

Facilitators reported by the sole prescriber-led model were primarily within the Individuals and Process CFIR domains, with a relatively lower number reported within the Inner Setting domain (Table 4). An important facilitator for successful implementation of PGx testing for initiatives adopting a sole prescriber-led model was a positive attitude toward the clinical utility of PGx testing by prescribers [61]. Positive attitudes were encouraged through establishing clinician champions [53–55,59], creating a multidisciplinary leadership team [59,62–64], and employing an awareness campaign prior to implementation on the clinical utility of PGx testing [64]. Furthermore, ensuring that prescribers have appropriate supports and resources available such as clinical guidelines [40,60], clear and concise recommendation reports [13], and access to personnel who are knowledgeable about PGx testing were key [40]. Additional facilitators for this model included completion of a PGx knowledge assessment prior to implementation to ensure any gaps are addressed [54], as well as a staggered or pilot approach towards implementation to allow for evaluation and modifications as needed [64]. Ensuring a fast turnaround time for PGx results was reported as an important facilitator to enable timely clinical action [38,40]. However, the resources and time needed to set-up appropriate infrastructure [60], proper lab accreditation requirements [38,65], and high cost of testing (e.g., if patients must pay out-of-pocket for a commercial test) [11,39,54,58,66] were reported as barriers that can affect timely access to results (Table 5). Additional barriers included insufficient time to discuss PGx results with the patient [40] and difficulty scheduling follow-up appointments [65]. Overall, the barriers reported by this model were primarily within the Inner Setting and Individuals CFIR domains.

Facilitators reported for the prescriber-led within an interdisciplinary team model primarily spanned across the Outer Setting, Individuals, and Process CFIR domains. Similar to the sole prescriber-led model, this model also reported positive attitudes toward PGx testing clinical utility [67], fast results turnaround time [40,68], and a pilot approach [68] as facilitators. A key facilitator for this model was the integration of a clinical pharmacist within the patient’s care team which further improved the perceived clinical utility of PGx testing given that pharmacists are more readily equipped to interpret PGx results [8,44,69–71]. Furthermore, incorporating PGx as an extension to services that pharmacists already provide, such as comprehensive medication reviews or chronic disease management, was also reported as a facilitator to optimize resources [72]. Pharmacists were also seen as appropriate candidates to lead PGx implementation, such as completing an assessment of the target patient population and cost-analyses to guide decision-making on which drug-gene pairs to include in the initiative [68,73]. Barriers reported by this model mapped mainly to the Outer Setting, Inner Setting, and Process CFIR domains. These include communication between HCPs regarding PGx results [68], unclear allocation of responsibilities between HCPs [44], differentiating between inpatient and outpatient clinical workflows [46], and ensuring consistent and steady identification of patient eligibility [70,73].

Community pharmacist-led initiatives reported many facilitators within the Individuals domain, including a positive attitude toward PGx testing [74], and confidence in their ability to deliver and lead implementation [75,76]. Pharmacists also reported a minimal learning curve and time commitment toward offering PGx testing [75,77]. However, this model was often limited by the lack of necessary infrastructures that would aid communication with other HCPs [77–80], access to the EHR for a more comprehensive patient assessment [75], and PGx test ordering being outside of their scope of practice [78]. This is reflected by the numerous barriers reported within the Inner Setting domain, and lack of facilitators reported in the Process domain.

Compared to the previous models, the PGx consultation service model can provide the most comprehensive and structured PGx service through the collaboration of a range of experts such as pharmacists, genetic counselors, clinical pharmacologists or medical geneticists. This includes processes that incorporate concomitant medications and patient history within prescribing recommendations [51], and management of any potential incidental findings [49]. As such, several facilitators were reported within the Inner Setting, Individuals, and Process CFIR domains. However, given the complexity in communication and workflows for initiatives that involve collaboration between different HCPs, many barriers were also reported within the Individuals and Inner Setting domains. These were often due to lack of clarity in the HCP roles and responsibilities, including who communicates the results to the referring prescriber and the patient [57,81]. Additional barriers resulting from the referral-based structure of this model were ensuring a steady referral rate [51,82–84], and prescriber nonadherence to the recommendations provided in the consultation note [83].

Discussion

This comprehensive scoping review identified four unique delivery models for PGx testing: (1) sole prescriber-led, (2) prescriber-led within an interdisciplinary care team, (3) community pharmacist-led, and (4) PGx consultation service. The prescriber-led and PGx consultation service delivery models were implemented across a broad span of healthcare settings, including hospitals, medical centers, primary care clinics, and specialized centers/clinics.

Reporting for all delivery models suggested feasibility in their effective provision of PGx-based care to patients; however, each delivery model exhibited a unique set of facilitators and barriers. While prescribers were able to successfully deliver PGx testing to patients through a sole prescriber-led model, they often relied on standard care plans, and CDS tools with built-in alert systems to aid patient eligibility identification and clearly provide actionable results. As such, this model requires the development and establishment of clear clinical guidelines and infrastructures prior to implementation to support successful and effective delivery of PGx testing [53,85]. Some prescribers also indicated that they would be more likely to order a PGx test if they were provided with assistance from a pharmacist, geneticist or genetic counselor [40]. Furthermore, both prescriber-led models reported challenges in discussing PGx results within the time allotment of a follow-up visit, as well as in scheduling the follow-up appointment [65,68,86,87]. Suggested strategies to overcome these challenges include scheduling a follow-up appointment shortly after the initial visit, and obtaining support from pharmacists who can follow up with patients either in-person or by telephone [68,87].

Although integration of a pharmacist or other HCPs with PGx expertise improved practical usefulness and provided a more comprehensive patient profile (e.g., concomitant medications) to guide prescribing, models relying on collaboration between different HCPs (i.e., prescriber-led within an interdisciplinary team and PGx consultation service) also resulted in higher complexity with respect to clinical workflows. This, in turn, resulted in the need for coordinated communication methods and clear role assignments amongst the different HCPs [61,88]. An additional challenge faced by these models was ensuring a steady patient flow or referral rate. For the PGx consultation service model, prescriber nonadherence was also reported as a challenge. Although it is possible that prescribers may have other clinical reasons not to comply with the recommendations provided, this barrier was primarily reported as a reflection of the prescriber’s perception of PGx-based medication prescribing. As such, strategies that were successful in mitigating this barrier included continuing education programs to inform prescribers of the utility of PGx testing, development of an internal website containing additional PGx information and relevant clinical guidelines, and a pharmacist on-call program for additional consultation [83].

Community pharmacists generally report that they are confident in their ability to deliver PGx testing, especially if it was added onto services that they were already providing, such as Medication Therapy Management [77]. However, challenges faced in providing PGx testing included time-consuming workflow, lack of clinician buy-in, communication with prescribers, and inability to access to the patient’s full medical record. Additionally, test ordering was often outside of their scope of practice, although this can be mitigated by establishing a collaborative practice agreement (which allows pharmacists to order a PGx test) or providing a recommendation to the patient’s primary care practitioner to order the test [46].

The unique combination of characteristics, and reported facilitators and barriers indicate distinct strengths and challenges for each model, summarized in Table 6. Strengths of the sole prescriber-led model include its accessibility to patients and a relatively simple workflow given that only the prescriber is involved in the delivery of PGx testing. However, challenges for this model include the need for extensive PGx training and education for prescribers prior to implementation, and ongoing PGx supports and resources to aid prescribers. An additional key challenge reported by prescribers included a lack of sufficient time to discuss PGx results with patients within standard appointments.

A major strength of the prescriber-led within interdisciplinary team model was that additional HCPs who are part of the patient’s care team can support the prescriber in the delivery of PGx testing, including interpreting PGx results to inform prescribing decisions and completing follow-up appointments with the patient to discuss their results. This model was particularly strengthened by the inclusion of a clinical pharmacist within the care team due to the minimal PGx learning curve for pharmacists and that pharmacists can offer PGx testing services as an extension of existing medication review services. However, a team-based approach to provide PGx testing can also add complexity to the workflow in terms of ensuring effective communication between HCPs, and clear assignment of roles and responsibilities.

Strengths of community pharmacist-led models include the ease of accessibility for patients and the minimal PGx learning curve for pharmacists. Pharmacists also saw PGx testing fitting in smoothly as an extension to existing services they provide, such as medication reviews. However, common challenges reported by pharmacists for this model included lack of infrastructures that allow ordering of tests, access to patient medical records for a comprehensive review of patient history, and timely and effective communication with prescribers.

A key strength of the PGx Consultation Service model is that it enables a comprehensive and structured service provided by a variety of experts such as pharmacists and genetic counselors. These additional HCPs can also take on tasks that support the prescriber in providing PGx testing to the patient (e.g., patient history, follow-up appointment to explain results). However, challenges related to this referral-based model include the need to maintain a steady referral rate, clear communication and assignment of roles/responsibilities between HCPs within the service team, and effective and timely communication with the referring prescriber.

Common facilitators for implementation reported by all four delivery models included the need for institutional support (especially financial backing), development of personalized PGx education and training, and user-friendly CDS tools built into the local EHR system. These facilitators fell within the Outer and Inner Setting CFIR domains, reflecting the importance of ensuring the contextual factors of the environment align and signal readiness for implementation, regardless of the delivery model. Barriers common across all four delivery models included uncertainty about the clinical utility of PGx testing, need for PGx education/training, lack of clear clinical guidelines, and difficulty integrating PGx results into the EHR, which is mapped to the Innovation, Individuals, and Process CFIR domains. Similarly, Luczak et al. also found challenges around EHR integration, lack of PGx evidence, and lack of PGx knowledge/education to support clinical use as barriers [5]. A recent review by Fahim et al. also examined barriers associated with PGx implementation and found similar results, including cost and testing reimbursement as additional common barriers [26].

Two additional recent reviews have also examined facilitators and barriers associated with clinical implementation of PGx. Youssef et al. used the Theoretical Domain Framework to guide their analysis which primarily focusses on individual HCP behaviors and determinants of those behaviors (unlike the CFIR which provides a broader view including factors beyond the individual level) [89]. They uncovered four overall themes from the facilitators and barriers reported by the included studies: IT infrastructure, effort (cognitive, physical and emotional), “rewards” or positive outcome to PGx testing, and “unknown territory” or a general lack of knowledge of PGx testing and how to use it. McDermott et al., on the other hand, also used the CFIR to guide their review; however, only studies that employed preemptive genotyping methodology were included [22]. Nonetheless, they also found that a positive institutional culture and leadership engagement were frequently reported facilitators. Additional facilitators included engaging interested/affected individuals or groups and use of clinical champions. Commonly cited barriers included clinician self-efficacy, lack of stakeholder knowledge, and PGx costs.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this review include its breadth across health jurisdictions, medical conditions, and healthcare settings. Compared to previously conducted reviews which only report on a subset of PGx initiatives, we have included a comprehensive number of PGx initiatives and summarized the approaches into four main types of delivery models. To our knowledge, this is the first review to present and characterize these different delivery models, along with the facilitators and barriers associated with each model. Finally, this review mapped findings to the CFIR 2.0 domains which can allow future works to replicate this process to enable easier identification of facilitators and barriers and their associated strategies.

Limitations of this review include the fact that the included papers reported on some of the delivery models more frequently than others and, therefore, had a larger pool of literature to derive information. However, the sole prescriber-led model, which was most commonly reported on by the included articles, did not show an overwhelmingly higher number of reported facilitators and barriers. Due to the broad range of study designs included in this review, we acknowledge that not all articles provided the same level of detail with respect to different characteristics of the initiative. Characteristics that were commonly underreported included the platform and process for test ordering, and details on the patient follow-up visit (e.g., which HCP shares the PGx results with the patient). Additionally, as expected in a scoping review, a range of different study designs were included in the analysis. This methodological heterogeneity may have impacted the consistency and comparability of the reported barriers and facilitators. Furthermore, we also included initiatives conducted as a clinical trial which may differ from clinical practice.

Another limitation is that most of the studies included in the review reported findings based primarily on the implementation experience as perceived by the authors of the article (i.e., not necessarily on data collected from a representative group of individuals involved in the initiative), potentially limiting a full understanding of the initiative and related experiences, particularly from the patient perspective. Additionally, many initiatives reported the importance of evolving the initiative following evaluation and feedback. Therefore, it is possible that the details provided by the published literature may not be fully reflective of currently existing initiatives. It is also possible that there are publication or reporting biases, in which successfully implemented PGx initiatives were more likely to be documented. In other words, the literature may not reflect the true scope of PGx initiatives that have been executed, including those that may have been unsuccessful.

Finally, the focus of this review was to analyze and present reported facilitators and barriers by frequency and commonalities between delivery models, rather than a process of “weighting” them to indicate relative importance or influence on implementation. Future work assessing the level of impact of reported facilitators and barriers on implementation would be a useful addition to the field. An additional important area for future work would be to assess whether these delivery models differ in terms of performance or quality indicators, such as patient satisfaction.

Gaps in the literature

Gaps identified from the literature include PGx testing for rural locations, limitations in the PGx evidence base, and underreporting of genetic counselor and nurse practitioner involvement in PGx initiatives. The initiatives identified were primarily located in large, urban areas that often had fully equipped facilities nearby to support PGx testing (either through a third-party or in-house). The need for additional efforts to support PGx testing in rural areas is emphasized by recent findings from the Pharmacogenomic Testing for Veterans (PHASER) program showing that individuals living in a rural residence were more likely to receive a PGx test order [90]. This highlights the need for more reporting of PGx initiatives that help ensure equitable access to PGx testing, particularly considering that there may be substantive differences in workforce and workflow planning between urban and rural settings. For example, at-home sample collection kits have been employed to accommodate patients who are unable to make a follow-up visit. However, it is not clear whether this is also an appropriate and feasible method for rural patients [15]. Telemedicine is another viable method that is currently being used by an interdisciplinary PGx clinic in the United States to provide PGx services both in-person and virtually across four midwestern states for individuals who are not in close geographical proximity to the clinic [91]. Lastly, a recently developed PGx training program offered by the University of Minnesota College of Pharmacy is being offered to provide clinical PGx training to pharmacists who serve rural populations across Minnesota [92].

Another common gap identified was the lack of a strong evidence base for certain groups of patients (e.g., pediatric, non-European ancestry) [6,15,52]. Research initiatives are currently underway, such as the All of Us Research Program [93] and 1000 Genomes Project [94], to address the lack of diversity in ancestry gap. Previous PGx studies have been conducted on pediatric and adolescent populations [95,96] with additional studies ongoing such as the PGx-Guided Antidepressant Prescribing in Adolescents (PGx-GAP) [97] and PGx-Supported Prescribing for Kids (PGx-SParK) [98] projects. However, other than the need for additional research, there was limited discussion on how potential inequities that may occur following implementation would be addressed or prevented in the first place.

One final gap that is important to highlight is that PGx initiatives involving nurse practitioners and genetic counselors were relatively underrepresented in the literature with only one initiative led by a nurse practitioner and only eight reporting a clear role for genetic counselors (Table 1). This is surprising because nurses and genetic counselors are generally well-suited for obtaining patient/family histories during a PGx pre-visit due to their extensive experience with patients [51]. For example, genetic counselors in a multidisciplinary PGx clinic based in Pennsylvania have played an important role in PGx delivery consisting of completing the initial patient interview/intake form to determine if PGx testing is needed, and subsequently arranging a visit with the clinic’s pharmacist and physician [99].

Conclusion

This scoping review was designed to identify and characterize different delivery models that have been used for the clinical implementation of PGx testing. Four different models were identified, each with their own unique set of facilitators and barriers. Common facilitators and barriers across all delivery models were also uncovered. These findings can help inform future implementation planning or expansion of PGx initiatives by presenting different delivery models that may be employed and the corresponding considerations for each approach. Given that reporting for all four of the delivery models suggested feasibility for effective delivery of PGx-based medication prescribing, at present it is not possible to definitively say if one delivery model is superior. However, this information can help inform future implementers in the selection of one or more approaches that may be most suitable based on their unique contextual needs and/or available infrastructures/resources. The number of clinical PGx initiatives continues to increase at a rapid rate, therefore, it is imperative that appropriate descriptions of initiatives and lessons learned through these experiences are reported and compared to help inform future PGx implementation.
