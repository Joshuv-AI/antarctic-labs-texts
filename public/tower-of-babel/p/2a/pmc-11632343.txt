Re-do Commando procedure with bioprosthetic mitral valve and aortic homograft for mitral valve dehiscence from prior Commando procedure

Central Message

We present a true re-do Commando for mitral valve dehiscence using mitral bioprosthesis and aortic homograft with excess anterior mitral leaflet tissue for aortomitral curtain reconstruction.

The Commando procedure, aortic and mitral valve replacement with aortomitral junction reconstruction, first described by Tirone David in 1997, is indicated in infective endocarditis with abscess, severe mitral annular calcification, lack of annular tissue to sew to in the setting of reoperation, and to prevent patient-prosthesis mismatch. Here we report a true re-do Commando procedure for mitral valve dehiscence using a bioprosthetic mitral valve and aortic homograft.

Case Report

A 29-year-old male patient with bicuspid aortic valve initially presented in 2014 with aortic and mitral valve endocarditis, aortomitral junction destruction, aortic root abscess, and purulent pericarditis, and underwent a modified Commando procedure, which we later published in 2020.1 The patient provided written informed consent for this case report as part of our institution's consent for surgery; institutional review board approval was not required. Following median sternotomy, purulent pericardial fluid was evacuated. The entire aortic root, aortomitral junction, mitral valve anterior and posterior leaflets were involved with endocarditis and were resected through the aortic root (Figure 1). Through a transaortic approach, a #33 St Jude Epic bioprosthetic mitral valve was implanted in a supra-annular position. In contrast to other groups, the neoaortic root was reconstructed with a #27 Magna bioprosthetic aortic valve (Carpentier-Edwards) fashioned inside a 30-mm Hemashield straight graft (Getinge) using the mitral sewing ring and healthy left ventricular outflow tract tissue as an anchor and coronary buttons were reimplanted onto the straight graft. Postoperative transesophageal echocardiogram demonstrated minimal perivalvular leak. Ten years later, in 2024, the patient presented with progressive shortness of breath. He reports that he had done well over the past decade. Transesophageal echocardiography demonstrated bioprosthetic mitral valve replacement with severe intravalvular and paravalvular regurgitation (mean gradient, 15 mm Hg) and severely thickened leaflets with restricted motion (Video 1). The patient was taken for urgent re-do Commando procedure (Video 2).

Groin lines were placed prior to re-do median sternotomy and cardiopulmonary bypass was initiated in the standard bicaval fashion after sternal re-entry. The aorta was transected and dissected down to the root, mobilizing both right and left coronary buttons. The bioprosthetic aortic valve was explanted. The superior vena cava was transected to further mobilize the right atrium and fully expose the left atrial dome and facilitate resection of the aortomitral junction. The paravalvular dehiscence was easily identified. The prior bioprosthetic mitral valve was explanted. Pledgeted sutures were placed around the posterior mitral annulus for approximately two-thirds of the annulus in a supra-annular fashion, from ventricle through atrium then through the sewing ring of a #33 St Jude Epic bioprosthetic mitral valve that was then seated and the sutures were tied down. Next, the anterior third of the reconstruction was performed with nonpledgeted sutures placed first through the mitral valve sewing ring and then through the dome of the left atrium. Pledgets were added on the tissue side and then sutures were tied.

The aortic root replacement pledgeted sutures were placed to create a neoaortic annulus that was attached in part to the anterior sewing ring of the mitral valve prosthesis, left ventricular outflow tract, and residual aortic annulus. These sutures were then placed through a #27 aortic homograft. A significant portion of the anterior leaflet of the mitral valve was left on the homograft to help fill the gap between aortomitral prosthesis and aortomitral junction and additional sutures were placed to help reinforce this area. The coronary buttons were then reimplanted in orthotopic position. The aortic graft was anastomosed to the distal aorta using running 4-0 Prolene suture, the heart was de-aired and the crossclamp was removed. The heart was reperfused and atrial and ventricular pacing wires were placed. The superior vena cava was reapproximated with running 5-0 Prolene suture while de-airing the right heart. The patient had significant pulmonary hypertension and mild biventricular dysfunction preoperatively, so an intra-aortic balloon pump was placed to assist with weaning from cardiopulmonary bypass. The patient was then weaned from cardiopulmonary bypass with excellent hemodynamics. Postoperative transesophageal echocardiogram demonstrated a well-seated mitral valve with no regurgitation or paravalvular leak and aortic homograft with no regurgitation or stenosis. Hemostasis was obtained and the chest was closed in the usual fashion.

Discussion

Here we present a case report of a true re-do Commando procedure. The patient initially presented with extensive infective endocarditis and initially underwent a Commando procedure and presented 10 years later with bioprosthetic mitral valve dehiscence and septal paravalvular leak and received another Commando procedure. The re-do Commando procedure was performed with bioprosthetic mitral valve replacement and aortic homograft, using excess anterior mitral leaflet homograft tissue to reconstruct and reinforce the aorto-mitral curtain.

Nearly 3 decades after it was described, the Commando procedure remains a challenging surgery with high mortality. As surgeons gain more experience with performing the Commando procedure and more patients survive their surgery, there will be an increasing need for reintervention of prior Commando. Reported outcomes following the Commando procedure include 16% mortality rate, 50% to 92% long-term survival (at least 3 years), and 79% to 91% freedom from reintervention. Reasons for reintervention following the Commando procedure include prosthetic valve endocarditis, prosthetic valve failure, and patch or valve dehiscence.2,3 The aortomitral curtain reconstruction is a crucial part of the operation and was initially described with bovine pericardium, which can calcify putting it at risk for leak or pseudoaneurysm. Subsequently, the hemi-Commando was described using aortic homograft and anterior mitral leaflet but it depends on homograft availability. Our group described a modified Commando procedure using the mitral valve sewing ring as the anchor for the neoaortic root, which simplifies the reconstruction but is only suitable when the intertrigonal tissue and mitral annulus retain enough structural integrity. When it comes to survival or freedom from reinfection following surgery for infective endocarditis, there are no differences between homograft, bioprosthetic, and mechanical valves4; rather, outcomes are more dependent on complete eradication of infective endocarditis.5 Selection of conduit and method for aortomitral curtain reconstruction should be carefully considered based on indication for surgery and patient factors.
