Unveiling the unexpected: Skeletal sarcoidosis with an extraosseous soft-tissue mass in the ligamentous portion of sacroiliac joint
Authors: Eitan Lovat, Madeeha Gulzar, Anoushka Seneviratne, Ashish Bhagat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sarcoidosis is a chronic granulomatous, multisystem disease of uncertain etiology that affects various organ systems, although musculoskeletal involvement is rarely the dominant feature. We present the case of a woman in her 50s with a history of pulmonary sarcoidosis who developed new lower back and hip pain. Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated an asymmetric soft-tissue mass in the ligamentous left sacroiliac joint, along with osseous involvement of the sacrum and ilium. These findings were suspicious for malignancy. An 18F-FDG PET/CT subsequently confirmed FDG-avid disease in this soft-tissue mass and the surrounding bone. However, a CT-guided biopsy of the lesion revealed non-caseating granulomas. This case highlights a rare presentation of skeletal sarcoidosis with an extraosseous mass, a diagnostic pitfall that can closely mimic malignancy or inflammatory sacroiliitis.

Background

Presentation of sarcoidosis

Sarcoidosis usually manifests in adults under the age of 50, with 70% of cases occurring between 25 and 40 years of age [1]. The disease remits within 3 years in most individuals, although 10%-30% of patients will require ongoing treatment [2]. In symptomatic patients, signs of systemic inflammation may be accompanied by symptoms from the affected organ(s). Virtually every organ can be involved in sarcoidosis although lung and lymph node enlargement are seen in over 90% of patients. Extra-pulmonary manifestations of sarcoidosis have also been reported involving the eyes, skin and liver in 15%-30% of cases and central nervous system in 2%-10% of cases [3,4].

Sarcoidosis can also present with skeletal involvement in between 1% and 13% of cases [5] and most commonly affects the phalanges [6] and axial skeleton [7,8]. Although osseous involvement is often asymptomatic, it may present as arthralgia, which is seen in 12% of cases [9]. The commonest joints reported to have been involved in descending order of frequency are the ankles, knees, wrists, and elbows [9]. Sarcoid arthritis usually presents with hilar lymphadenopathy and erythema nodosum and is self-limiting. On imaging, studies have reported skeletal involvement of sarcoidosis to manifest as hypermetabolic bone lesions on PET imaging, or T1 hypointense lesions on MRI. There have also been reports of sarcoidosis presenting as lytic lesions [10]. Involvement of the axial skeleton is rare, and often asymptomatic [11] although some studies have reported an association with spondyloarthritis [12]. The most common soft-tissue complication associated with osseous sarcoid is swelling [11].

Diagnosing sarcoidosis

Several biomarkers have been proposed for the diagnosis of sarcoidosis and the monitoring of its progression, but none have been widely accepted in practice [13]. Elevated serum levels of ACE have been intensively studied since 1975, and this is the most frequently used laboratory test to date [14].

Although sarcoidosis is a multisystem disease, much of the morbidity is in relation to the pulmonary manifestations [15]. High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) is the mainstay imaging modality for identifying the lung manifestations which usually presents as bilateral, symmetrical, hilar lymphadenopathy (in up to 80% of patients). In addition to HRCT, nuclear imaging has been used to identify sites of disease. Gallium-67 (67Ga) scintigraphy, for example was found to have a sensitivity of 60%-90% [16]. Somatostatin scintigraphy has also been shown to be of benefit, particularly in identifying thoracic disease [16]. 18F-fluroudeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET) is a radioactive tracer used to identify metabolically active sites of disease, especially in oncological imaging. The role of 18F-FDG PET in sarcoidosis was first demonstrated in 1994 [17] and has now been shown to be superior to 67Ga, especially in combination with CT [16].

Histological characteristics of sarcoidosis

Histologically, sarcoidosis is characterized by noncaseating granulomatosis with epithelioid histiocytes and multinucleated giant cells, often surrounded by a rim of lymphocytes [18]. Necrosis is rarely present, and this helps distinguish sarcoidosis from infectious granulomatous conditions. In soft tissue sarcoidosis, biopsy findings may also demonstrate fibrosis [19].

Anatomy of the sacroiliac joint

The sacroiliac joint (SIJ) is the complex articulation between the axial skeleton and pelvis. It is a diarthro-amphiarthrodial joint due to its 2 components. The anterior joint is a true synovial joint, and is made up of a joint capsule, synovial fluid, and hyaline cartilage on the sacral surface with fibrocartilage on the iliac surface. The posterior component of the sacroiliac joint is a fibrous syndesmosis (amphiarthrosis) [20,21].

The joint is remarkably stable due to an extensive network of ligaments which are part of the posterior SIJ, with the posterior sacroiliac ligament complex being one of the strongest in the body [22,23]. Case presentation

Clinical presentation

We report the case of a female patient in her 50s, with a rare and interesting presentation of sarcoidosis. She was initially diagnosed with pulmonary sarcoidosis in her mid-20s, confirmed via mediastinoscopy at a tertiary care center, and subsequently followed up in a local chest clinic. Due to long standing stable disease, she was discharged from follow up several years later.

In early 2022, she was referred to our respiratory service on a 2 week wait pathway following an ultrasound of the neck performed for an isolated right sided lymph node in her neck. Around the same time, she also developed anterior uveitis, for which she was managed with steroid eye drops by the ophthalmology team at a tertiary care center. A CT performed in the subsequent months demonstrated reactivation of her pulmonary sarcoidosis, with enlarged right mediastinal, bilateral hilar and supraclavicular lymph nodes, consistent with Stage I Sarcoidosis, but without any evidence of parenchymal lung involvement. Further investigations included an echocardiogram which was unremarkable, and pulmonary function tests which showed preserved spirometry and lung volumes but a mildly reduced gas transfer. Her serum ACE levels were elevated while her calcium levels remained normal.

Three months later, at a respiratory clinic review, she reported a history of problematic exertional breathlessness and a nonproductive cough along with new musculoskeletal symptoms, involving the back, right hip, buttock, and small joints of the hands. At this point, she was referred to Rheumatology for further assessment of her musculoskeletal symptoms.

Investigations

Given her symptoms and mildly reduced gas transfer, a CT pulmonary angiogram (CTPA) was performed in mid-2022 to rule out pulmonary embolism. The scan showed no pulmonary embolism, and no interval change in lymphadenopathy compared to previous imaging. A couple of months later, she underwent EBUS bronchoscopy that confirmed non-necrotizing granulomas consistent with a diagnosis of sarcoidosis. TB culture was negative. Repeat lung function tests at that time showed no interval change and her persistent nonproductive cough was attributed to laryngopharyngeal reflux, likely due to recent weight gain and she commenced proton pump inhibitor therapy. Throughout this period, she was followed closely by a respiratory team, with repeat imaging and pulmonary function tests.

In late 2024, she reported worsening arthralgia particularly affecting her right hip, right ankle, neck and back and was urgently referred to Rheumatology. Assessment in the rheumatology clinic revealed widespread osteoarthritic changes in the small joints of the hands, severe right hip pain limiting examination and marked stiffness in the lower lumbar spine. A subsequent X-ray of the hips showed preserved joint spaces, normal sacroiliac joints, and spondylosis at L5/S1.

The patient was then referred for an MRI spine in late 2024 for severe lower back pain querying a disc prolapse. Imaging showed “irregular areas of T1 and T2 hypointensity, with corresponding STIR high signal noted in the visualized lumbosacral spine and iliac bones” (Figs. 2 and 3). In the ligamentous (posterior) aspect of the left sacroiliac joint, there was asymmetrically increased soft tissue, which, combined with the marrow hypo-intensity, raised the suspicion of marrow infiltration, possibly malignant (Fig. 3). Within 3 weeks of the MRI, the patient underwent an 18F-FDG PET/CT study which showed FDG-avid soft tissue in the same location (Fig. 4), along with heterogenous bilateral sacral, pelvic and thoracolumbar FDG-avidity with subtle sclerosis on the CT. Treatment and follow-up

Towards the end of 2024, she started a 30 mg prednisolone reducing regime, along with a conventional synthetic DMARD, methotrexate at the dose of 12.5 mg, once weekly with folic acid. Her prednisolone regime was 30 mg for 2-weeks followed by 20 mg for 2 weeks, then 10 mg for 2 weeks reducing thereafter by 1mg every fortnight. Her regime finished in early 2025.

This was followed by an interval CT scan of the chest in early 2025 that showed improvement in lymphadenopathy, likely secondary to steroid therapy. In the spring of 2025, the patient’s treatment was adjusted from an oral to subcutaneous form of methotrexate due to the fairly common gastrointestinal side effects including nausea and vomiting, with the latter preparation demonstrating enhanced gastrointestinal tolerability as well as systemic bioavailability.

A repeat MRI lumbosacral spine study in late 2025 (1 year following her initial study) demonstrated mild improvement in the infiltrative bone marrow changes at the sacral ala, but no significant interval changes in the appearance of the soft tissue mass within the left sacroiliac joint. A clinical review in early 2026 showed the patient still had back pain and difficulty rising from a seated position, and as such her methotrexate dose was increased to 15 mg, and she remains under close monitoring with the aim of achieving sustained remission.

Discussion

Sarcoidosis is a systemic granulomatous disease of unknown etiology that typically affects the pulmonary system and lymph nodes. Musculoskeletal involvement in sarcoidosis predominantly affects the small joints of the appendicular skeleton, or the bones themselves, as seen in skeletal sarcoidosis. To our knowledge, although there are reported cases of joint disease in sarcoidosis [[24], [25], [26]], and lytic/sclerotic axial/appendicular skeletal lesions, there are no reported cases of sarcoidosis manifesting as unilateral soft-tissue infiltration along with marrow infiltration of a larger joint such as the sacroiliac joint.

Our case study describes a novel presentation of extraosseous extension of skeletal sarcoidosis. Although the initial MRI raised the suspicion of malignancy—specifically due to the atypical and asymmetric increase in soft tissue associated with the ligamentous portion of the left sacroiliac joint—the follow-up PET/CT study and subsequent biopsy confirmed the presence of granulomatous soft tissue rather than a neoplastic process. Whilst skeletal sarcoid, sarcoid-lymphoma syndrome, and lymphoproliferative disease were actively considered following the initial MRI study, these differentials were ultimately thought to be less likely based on clinical and temporal factors. It has been cited in historical literature, such as the landmark study by Brincker [27], that patients with sarcoidosis are 11 times more likely to develop lymphoma [27], and there have been published case reports of these 2 diseases co-occurring [28,29]. However, this figure has been moderated by more recent large-scale epidemiological data. A comprehensive cohort study of 8541 patients by Askling et al. [30] demonstrated a much more modest overall relative risk for cancer of 1.3 [30]. While the authors found the risk for non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma to be roughly doubled, this risk was highest during the first decade of follow-up and returned to baseline levels after 10 years. Our patient’s presentation aligns with these more recent risk assessments; our patient had no secondary indications of lymphoma such as B-symptoms (fever, night sweats, and weight loss), generalized lymphadenopathy, cutaneous changes, or organomegaly. Additionally, our patient had lived with sarcoidosis for decades. This stands in contrast to the typical “sarcoidosis-lymphoma syndrome” profile, where the median interval between the onset of lymphoma and the initial diagnosis of sarcoidosis is approximately 24 months [31]. Askling et al. further suggests that the high risk-ratios reported in early literature may be partially attributed to “detection bias” or the misdiagnosis of prevalent lymphomas as sarcoidosis during the first year of clinical follow-up. In this case, as is often so with atypical presentations, histological confirmation was key in confirming the final diagnosis

The management of extrapulmonary sarcoidosis is typically guided by the severity and site of organ involvement as well as response to less aggressive therapies. In our patient immunosuppressive therapy was used in the form of a reducing regimen of corticosteroids alongside disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drug (DMARD). The clinical improvement following treatment initiation highlights the potential for disease modulation with appropriate therapy.

A notable limitation in the workup of our patient was the absence of postgadolinium contrast-enhanced MRI. While T1-weighted and STIR sequences provided clear evidence of marrow infiltration and a soft-tissue mass, dynamic contrast enhancement sequences could have offered deeper insights into the vascularity and washout pattern of the lesion. Having this data potentially could have aided in the characterization of the lesion earlier in the diagnostic pathway and potentially obviated the need for a follow-up FDG-PET/CT.

Our case highlights the importance of considering sarcoidosis in the differential diagnosis of extraosseous soft tissue involvement of a joint in patients with known active sarcoid disease. This case also demonstrates the importance of using a multidisciplinary approach and procuring histology, whilst maintaining a high index of suspicion when investigating patients with atypical musculoskeletal presentations.
