Latent profile analysis of neurocognitive development in preschool children with cancer: associations with anxiety and sleep disorders
Authors: Zefang Chen, Lifang Xu, Jing Yu, Lin Mo, Rongli Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study aims to identify latent profiles of neurocognitive development and their associated influencing factors in preschool children with cancer through latent profile analysis (LPA), thereby providing evidence for precise interventions addressing neurocognitive developmental disorders.MethodsLatent profile analysis (LPA) was conducted using continuous Ages and Stages Questionnaires (ASQ) scores to classify neurocognitive developmental profiles, while Preschool Anxiety Scale (PAS) and Sleep Disturbance Scale for Children (SDSC) scores were examined post-hoc. Parsimonious multinomial logistic regression was employed to examine factors associated with the identified profiles.ResultsA three-profile model was determined to be optimal (Entropy = 0.8548). Preschool children's neurocognitive development was classified into three profiles: the "Normally-Developed Group" (59.24%, n = 93), which exhibited the highest neurocognitive scores and lowest anxiety and sleep disturbances; the "Subclinical Developmental Concerns Group" (15.29%, n = 24), with intermediate scores; and the "Clinically Significant Developmental Concerns Group" (25.48%, n = 40), which displayed the poorest neurocognitive outcomes alongside severe anxiety and sleep issues. Parsimonious multinomial logistic regression showed that rural residence (OR = 5.80, P = 0.001) and extended daily internet use (OR = 3.19, P = 0.009) significantly increased the likelihood of being in the "Clinically Significant Developmental Concerns Group", whereas higher maternal education (OR = 0.47, P = 0.011) and older age (OR = 0.35, P < 0.001) were protective factors.ConclusionNeurocognitive development in preschool children with cancer demonstrates substantial heterogeneity, closely associated with factors such as family environment, gender, and disease stage. Tailored interventions targeting different subgroups are necessary to improve neurocognitive development, mitigate psychological and sleep-related issues, and enhance social adaptability and overall quality of life.Keywords: Neurocognitive Development, Preschool Children, Cancer, Latent Profile Analysis, Anxiety, Sleep Disorders

Introduction

Between 2018 and 2020, 121,145 children were diagnosed with cancer in China, with an average incidence rate of 126.48 per million, exhibiting a steadily increasing annual trend [1]. The current 5-year survival rate for children with cancer exceeds 80% [2]. However, the long-term adverse effects of cancer treatments can significantly impair survivors’ quality of life, while imposing substantial burdens on families and society [3]. Among these adverse effects, neurocognitive developmental delays are particularly common and critically affect the long-term quality of life of cancer survivors. Neurocognitive development during the preschool years lays the foundation for future intelligence, behavior, and social adaptability [4–6]. It encompasses multiple dimensions, including communication skills, gross motor skills, fine motor skills, problem-solving abilities, and personal-social skills, all of which develop rapidly during the preschool years. For preschool children with cancer, however, the disease itself and treatment-related factors, such as chemotherapy and radiation, may result in significant delays or abnormalities in neurocognitive development [7, 8].

Previous studies have demonstrated that cancer treatments can adversely impact children’s neurocognitive development through mechanisms such as neurotoxicity, prolonged immune suppression, and reduced opportunities for social interaction resulting from hospitalization [9–11]. Such delays may manifest as impairments in cognitive function, motor coordination, and emotional regulation. Notably, psychological and physiological factors, including anxiety and sleep disorders, are increasingly acknowledged as critical factors associated with neurocognitive development in children with cancer. Mechanistically, chronic stress and anxiety can adversely affect hippocampal maturation, while disrupted sleep architecture can impair memory consolidation and emotional regulation during early childhood [12, 13].

Anxiety is among the most prevalent psychological challenges encountered by children with cancer [14]. Symptoms may be aggravated by the uncontrollable nature of the disease, the physical discomfort caused by treatments, and fears related to survival [15, 16]. Excessive anxiety can adversely affect children’s emotional well-being, compromise attention, and hinder the development of memory and social skills [17]. Additionally, sleep disorders—characterized by difficulty falling asleep, frequent awakenings, or circadian rhythm disturbances—represent another significant challenge for children with cancer [18]. Such sleep disturbances can interrupt critical restorative processes in brain development, consequently impairing learning abilities, memory, and emotional regulation [19].

Existing studies have demonstrated a bidirectional relationship between anxiety and sleep disorders: anxiety may compromise sleep quality by causing difficulty falling asleep or triggering nighttime awakenings, while sleep disorders, in turn, can exacerbate anxiety by increasing fatigue and impairing cognitive performance [20]. These interactions are likely more pronounced in children with cancer than in healthy peers, as they experience additional stressors related to their disease and treatments [21]. However, research in this area has largely concentrated on individual psychological or physiological factors, with limited exploration of the joint impact of anxiety and sleep disorders on neurocognitive outcomes. LPA is a widely used method in pediatric oncology for identifying psychological and developmental subgroups, such as anxiety and cognitive impairments [22, 23]. This approach offers a foundation for investigating individual differences and designing tailored interventions, aligning with our aim of classifying neurocognitive profiles and associated risk factors [24, 25].

This study aims to systematically classify neurocognitive development profiles using latent profile analysis (LPA) based on ASQ dimensions. Subsequently, it compares anxiety (PAS) and sleep disorder (SDSC) scores across the identified profiles, and identifies demographic and clinical correlates of profile membership. The findings are anticipated to inform the development of tailored interventions addressing neurocognitive development.

Materials and methods

Study participants

Between September 2023 and August 2024, preschool children with cancer hospitalized at the Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University were recruited using a convenience sampling method. As a result of this sampling approach, only children whose parents provided informed consent and agreed to participate were included in the study. Most children hospitalized at this institution were accompanied by their parents, facilitating both recruitment and data collection. The sample size was justified based on clinical feasibility during the study period. A total of 180 eligible children were approached; 23 declined participation, yielding a final enrolled sample of 157 (response rate 87.2%), which minimizes potential non-response bias. Due to the face-to-face questionnaire administration and real-time cross-checking by trained healthcare professionals, the proportion of missingness for all primary clinical variables and standardized scales was 0%. Consequently, a complete-case analysis approach was naturally applicable, and advanced imputation techniques were not required.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Institutional Review Board, reference number [2023 No. 270]). Ethical approval was granted by the Department of Scientific Research at the Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University, China. All methods were conducted in strict compliance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The study also followed the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) Statement to ensure rigorous reporting standards.

Participation in the study was entirely voluntary, with oral and written informed consent obtained from all participants or their legal guardians. Participants also provided consent for the study findings to be published in academic journals and related publications. Confidentiality was rigorously maintained throughout the study, and no private information, including the child’s name, was disclosed. The primary caregiver signed an informed consent form before participation, and all surveys were conducted anonymously to ensure the protection of participants’ identities.

Outcomes and measurements

General information

The general information questionnaire was developed based on a review of relevant literature, discussions within the research team, and consultations with pediatric oncologists. The questionnaire collected sociodemographic data, including the child’s gender, age, number of hospitalizations, age at first diagnosis, educational level of the primary caregiver, place of residence (urban or rural), and parenting style (authoritarian, authoritative, or indulgent, with clear explanations provided for each category to facilitate accurate responses). Additionally, the questionnaire captured disease- and treatment-related data, such as whether the child underwent surgical interventions, received radiotherapy, or intrathecal chemotherapy.

Neurocognitive function

The Chinese version of the Ages & Stages Questionnaires, Third Edition (ASQ-3), was employed to evaluate neurocognitive development in preschool children with cancer. The Chinese ASQ-3, adapted and validated by Bian et al. [26], is specifically tailored to assess developmental behaviors in children aged 36 to 66 months. As a widely recommended screening tool for early childhood development, particularly endorsed by the United Nations International Children's Emergency Fund (UNICEF), the ASQ-3 has demonstrated robust reliability and validity across various studies. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the Chinese version is reported at 0.8, with sensitivity and specificity values of 87.50% and 84.48%, respectively, establishing it as an effective tool for identifying developmental delays. It is important to note that the ASQ-3 is a screening instrument rather than a comprehensive neuropsychological diagnostic battery; thus, results indicate suspected developmental delays rather than formal clinical diagnoses. Urban normative data were applied universally as they represent the most robustly validated national standards available, though we acknowledge this may introduce slight classification variance for rural participants.

The ASQ-3 consists of five dimensions: communication, gross motor, fine motor, problem-solving, and personal-social. Each dimension includes six items, with three response options: “Yes,” indicating the child can successfully complete the task (10 points); “Sometimes,” indicating partial completion (5 points); and “No,” indicating failure to complete the task (0 points). To assess neurocognitive function in this study, scores from each dimension were compared to normative data for urban children in mainland China. Based on the screening criteria, children whose scores were close to or below the threshold in any domain were classified as having suspected neurocognitive impairment, whereas those with scores above the threshold were considered to have normal functioning.

Anxiety

The Chinese version of the Spence Preschool Anxiety Scale (PAS) [27] was employed to evaluate anxiety levels in preschool children with cancer. The PAS is a comprehensive instrument comprising 28 core items that assess anxiety across five dimensions: Generalized Anxiety (GNA), Social Phobia (SP), Obsessive–Compulsive Disorder (OCD), Fear of Physical Injury (FPI), and Separation Anxiety (SPA). In addition to these core items, the scale incorporates five supplementary items designed to screen for post-traumatic stress disorder, bringing the total item count to 33. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0–4), where higher scores indicate greater severity of anxiety symptoms. The PAS has shown strong internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.87, demonstrating its reliability for use in Chinese children.

Sleep disorders

The Sleep Disturbance Scale for Children (SDSC) was employed in this study to assess sleep disorders in preschool children with cancer [28]. The SDSC comprises 26 items, each rated on a 5-point Likert scale according to the frequency of occurrence: 1 = never, 2 = 1–2 times per month, 3 = 1–2 times per week, 4 = 3–5 times per week, and 5 = always. The total score is derived by summing all item scores, where a score above 39 indicates the presence of sleep disorders. The scale is specifically designed to identify common sleep disturbances in children and assesses six key dimensions: Disorders of Initiating and Maintaining Sleep (DIMS), Sleep Breathing Disorders (SBD), Disorders of Arousal (DA), Sleep–Wake Transition Disorders (SWTD), Disorders of Excessive Somnolence (DOES), and Sleep Hyperhidrosis (SHY). The SDSC has shown good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.79, establishing it as a reliable tool for assessing sleep-related problems in this population.

Data collection

Trained healthcare professionals conducted data collection and administered the ASQ screening for this study in a hospital setting. After obtaining informed consent from parents, healthcare professionals guided the primary caregivers to complete the scale appropriately during the screening process. Scores were calculated based on the screening criteria. The test lasted 15 min, and all questions were asked without hints or demonstrations, strictly adhering to the questionnaire guidelines.

Statistical methods

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Pearson’s chi-square test and independent-sample t-tests were employed to compare categorical and continuous variables, respectively. Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) was conducted using Mplus (version 8.3) to classify participants based exclusively on the five continuous dimension scores of the ASQ. PAS and SDSC scores were not entered into the latent model but were examined post-hoc as external variables to compare differences across the identified profiles. The LPA used the robust maximum likelihood (MLR) estimator with 500 random starts and 100 final stage optimizations to ensure convergence. The optimal number of profiles was determined using AIC, BIC, sample-size adjusted BIC (aBIC), entropy, the Lo-Mendell-Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMR), and the Bootstrapped Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT).

Latent profiles of neurocognitive development in preschool children with cancer

Exploratory latent profile models with 1 to 10 profiles were developed using the five dimensions of the Ages and Stages Questionnaire (ASQ) — communication, gross motor skills, fine motor skills, problem-solving abilities, and personal-social skills — as observed indicators. The 3-class model was identified as the optimal solution based on model fit indices, including AIC, BIC, and entropy. As shown in the AIC/BIC curve (Fig. 1), although AIC, BIC, and aBIC values decreased with an increasing number of classes, the reduction in BIC values plateaued significantly at the 3-class model. Additionally, statistical tests for model comparison confirmed the superior fit of the 3-profile model relative to others, supported by an entropy of 0.8548, BLRT (P < 0.001), and LMR-LRT (P = 0.002). Furthermore, the average posterior probabilities for the three profiles were highly acceptable (all > 0.80), indicating strong classification accuracy. The minimum class size was 15.29% (n = 24), which exceeds the typical threshold recommended for mixture modeling to ensure reliable profile representation. From a clinical perspective, the 3-profile solution provided the most interpretable classification of developmental risk compared to higher-class models, which suffered from overfitting and produced fragmented, clinically indistinct subgroups of very small sizes due to our modest sample size (Table 5).

Characteristics of the identified latent profiles

Although 51.6% of children were suspected of impairment based on strict screening thresholds, the LPA approach identified 59.24% in the "Normally-Developed Group." This divergence occurs because threshold-based screening isolates deficits in any single dimension, whereas LPA evaluates the holistic multidimensional profile of the child. After establishing the 3-profile model, the characteristics of the three latent profiles were analyzed in detail. The "Normally-Developed Group" exhibited the highest proportion of normality across ASQ dimensions (Fig. 2), the highest overall scores in neurocognitive development, and the lowest levels of anxiety and sleep disturbances (Fig. 3). In contrast, the "Clinically Significant Developmental Concerns Group" showed the lowest proportion of normality, the most severe anxiety and sleep issues, and the poorest neurocognitive outcomes across all dimensions (Fig. 4). The "Normally-Developed Group," representing 59.24% of the sample, exhibited the highest scores across all ASQ dimensions, including communication (mean = 57.31) and gross motor skills (mean = 52.85), as well as the lowest scores in PAS and SDSC dimensions, such as general anxiety (GNA = 4.59) and Disorders of Initiating and Maintaining Sleep (DIMS = 11.00). The "Subclinical Developmental Concerns Group," comprising 15.29% of the sample, demonstrated intermediate levels across ASQ, PAS, and SDSC dimensions, including a communication score of 51.67 and a general anxiety score of 5.00. The "Clinically Significant Developmental Concerns Group," representing 25.48% of the sample, recorded the lowest scores in ASQ dimensions, such as communication (mean = 47.38) and gross motor skills (mean = 40.63), and the highest scores in PAS and SDSC dimensions, including general anxiety (GNA = 5.17) and sleep difficulties (DIMS = 27.00). These findings underscore clear differences in neurocognitive development, anxiety, and sleep disorders among the three latent profiles, offering valuable insights into targeted interventions for these groups.

Multivariate regression analysis of latent profiles of neurocognitive development in preschool children with cancer

To examine the robust predictors of neurocognitive development profiles while avoiding sparse data bias, a parsimonious multinomial logistic regression was conducted. The "Normally-Developed Group" served as the reference category, and theory-driven predictors (age, residence, maternal education, and daily internet usage) were included. The results indicated that rural residence significantly increased the likelihood of belonging to both the "Subclinical Developmental Concerns Group" (OR = 6.57, P = 0.001) and the "Clinically Significant Developmental Concerns Group" (OR = 5.80, P = 0.001). Furthermore, for the "Clinically Significant Developmental Concerns Group," extended daily internet usage was a significant risk factor (OR = 3.19, P = 0.009), whereas higher maternal education (OR = 0.47, P = 0.011) and older age (OR = 0.35, P < 0.001) served as protective factors. Discussion

Neurocognitive developmental profiles and implications

This study identified three distinct latent profiles of neurocognitive development among preschool children with cancer: the "Normally-Developed Group," "Subclinical Developmental Concerns Group," and "Clinically Significant Developmental Concerns Group." These classes represent a spectrum of neurocognitive outcomes shaped by the severity of disease, treatment burden, and individual resilience factors. The "Normally-Developed Group," representing 59.24% of the children, exhibited normal to above-average neurocognitive abilities, indicating their resilience in maintaining development despite the challenges of cancer treatment. The "Subclinical Developmental Concerns Group" (15.29%) displayed intermediate outcomes, reflecting a balance between protective and risk factors. The "Clinically Significant Developmental Concerns Group" (25.48%) exhibited significant impairments across all ASQ dimensions, particularly in communication, gross motor skills, and problem-solving, alongside heightened anxiety and severe sleep disturbances.

These findings underscore the complexity of neurocognitive outcomes in children with cancer, highlighting how development is shaped by both biological vulnerabilities and environmental stressors. Children in the "Clinically Significant Developmental Concerns Group" are likely to experience cumulative effects of neurotoxicity from treatments and disruptions in their social environment. Furthermore, delays in gross motor and fine motor skills observed in this group are consistent with prior studies, which indicate that prolonged treatment courses and limited physical activity exacerbate motor deficits [9–11]. Similarly, the pronounced communication and problem-solving difficulties in this group highlight the strong associations between developmental concerns, anxiety, sleep disorders, and inadequate cognitive stimulation during critical periods of brain development [29]. These profiles underscore the importance of proactive, individualized intervention strategies to address both neurocognitive and psychosocial needs. Furthermore, synthesizing the anxiety and sleep disorder patterns across the profiles reveals a compounding burden: the Clinically Significant Developmental Concerns Group not only displayed the lowest neurocognitive scores but also exhibited the highest severity in sleep initiation (DIMS) and generalized anxiety (GNA). This suggests that severe developmental delays co-occur with disrupted sleep architecture and emotional dysregulation, likely creating a negative feedback loop. Interestingly, treatment-related variables such as radiotherapy and chemotherapy did not emerge as significant independent predictors in our cohort. This null finding suggests that environmental and sociodemographic factors (e.g., residence and maternal education) may exert a relatively heavier weight in shaping short-term neurocognitive profiles during the preschool years than the specific modality of cancer treatment.

Determinants of neurocognitive development and high-risk vulnerability

The parsimonious multinomial logistic regression analysis identified several robust predictors of neurocognitive developmental outcomes. Specifically, rural residence, younger age, prolonged internet usage, and lower maternal education levels emerged as key factors associated with membership in the "Clinically Significant Developmental Concerns Group." Among these, younger age was identified as a critical factor, as early childhood is a period of rapid brain maturation, making younger children more susceptible to the neurotoxic effects of treatments and the overarching stressors of the disease. This aligns with broader pediatric oncology research, such as findings by Sleurs et al. [30] and Armstrong et al. [31], which indicate that younger children exhibit more severe cognitive vulnerabilities. Furthermore, rural residence emerged as a strong risk factor in our cohort. This disparity likely reflects differences in healthcare accessibility, early childhood educational resources, and socioeconomic support between urban and rural areas, which compound the developmental challenges posed by cancer.

Maternal education was identified as a key determinant of neurocognitive outcomes, with lower levels significantly correlated with poorer development. This finding is consistent with Kesler et al. [32], who emphasized the positive impact of caregivers' education on children’s brain structure and cognitive performance through greater access to learning resources and supportive environments. In the Chinese cultural context, where mothers often serve as the primary caregivers [33, 34], maternal education assumes an even more pivotal role, shaping children’s exposure to cognitively stimulating activities as well as the emotional and psychological support they receive during treatment [35]. Mothers with higher education levels are more likely to prioritize structured cognitive activities, pursue quality educational opportunities, and offer effective support for their children’s neurodevelopment [36, 37]. Conversely, lower education levels may restrict the ability to mitigate the adverse effects of the disease and its treatment on cognitive development, thereby exacerbating developmental delays in children [38].

Prolonged internet usage was also identified as a variable significantly associated with the high-risk profile, potentially interacting with the displacement of structured cognitive and physical activities, both of which are essential for neurodevelopment [39]. Excessive screen time has been associated with impaired sleep quality, reduced attention spans, and diminished opportunities for engaging in interactive learning experiences [40–42]. For children undergoing cancer treatment, screen time may act as a coping mechanism for prolonged hospitalizations; however, excessive usage can exacerbate the psychological and cognitive burden caused by the disease [43, 44]. Moreover, dietary habits and participation in cognitive activities were identified as protective factors, while deficits in these areas were associated with the "Subclinical Developmental Concerns Group." Proper nutrition supports brain growth by supplying essential nutrients for neurological development [45, 46], while regular participation in cognitive activities promotes neuroplasticity and enhances cognitive function [47]. Children lacking access to balanced diets or failing to engage in regular cognitive activities may exhibit reduced problem-solving ability, slower communication development, and delayed motor skill acquisition. These findings underscore the importance of promoting structured cognitive engagement, minimizing unproductive screen time, and ensuring adequate nutritional support to optimize the neurocognitive outcomes of preschool children with cancer.

Limitation

The limitations of this study include its relatively small sample size and single-center design, which may limit the generalizability of the findings, as well as its cross-sectional nature, which precludes causal inferences. Additionally, certain variables, such as parental education and internet usage duration, were based on caregiver-reported data, potentially introducing recall bias. Furthermore, the study did not investigate the underlying biological mechanisms, such as the neurotoxic effects of treatment, that influence neurocognitive outcomes. Future research involving larger, multi-center cohorts, longitudinal designs, and mechanistic investigations is necessary to further validate and expand upon these findings. Furthermore, the study did not account for pre-cancer neurocognitive developmental status, nor did it control for potential environmental confounders that could influence cognition, such as attendance at early childhood education programs versus remaining at home for family care. Third, the relatively modest sample size restricted our ability to explore more complex latent profiles without risking overfitting, and necessitated a parsimonious regression approach to avoid unstable parameter estimates. Fourth, neurocognitive data were derived from caregiver-reported screening tools rather than direct comprehensive neuropsychological assessments, which may introduce respondent bias. Fifth, although we included generalized treatment variables in our initial univariate analyses, our modest sample size and sample heterogeneity limited our ability to comprehensively control for nuanced clinical factors such as specific central nervous system involvement, detailed chemotherapy intensity, and precise cumulative treatment duration. These treatment-related confounders heavily influence neurodevelopment and should be explicitly isolated in larger, well-powered future cohorts. Finally, the clinical heterogeneity of the cancer sample, including various tumor types and treatment protocols, limits our ability to isolate disease-specific mechanisms.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates significant heterogeneity in the developmental profiles among preschool children with cancer, identifying three distinct subgroups that also differ markedly in their anxiety and sleep burdens. Factors such as rural residence, younger age, lower maternal education, and extended screen time were strongly associated with clinically significant developmental concerns. These findings highlight the need for tailored, family-centered interventions. However, larger, longitudinal studies incorporating direct neuropsychological assessments are necessary to confirm these predictors and inform targeted early intervention strategies.
