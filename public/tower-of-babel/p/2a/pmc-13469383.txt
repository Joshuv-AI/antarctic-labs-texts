Elucidating the role of YlMDH2 in temperature-dependent mannitol biosynthesis in Yarrowia lipolytica via integrative multi-omics and structural analysis
Authors: Muhammad Sarfaraz Iqbal, Shuo Xu, Yui Zhang, Liyun Ji, Khalid J. Alzahrani, Khalaf F. Alsharif, Fuad M. Alzahrani, Abebe Bogale, Hairong Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Yarrowia lipolytica is a prominent industrial host for erythritol production, yet yields are limited by carbon flux diversion to competing polyols, such as mannitol. Understanding the molecular basis of this competition is essential for rational strain engineering. This study aimed to elucidate the role of YlMDH2 (YALI0_D18964g; hereafter YIMDH2) in temperature-dependent mannitol biosynthesis and assess its potential as a target for enhancing erythritol yield. An integrative multi-omics approach was employed, including time-course transcriptomics, qPCR validation, protein–protein interaction network analysis, and molecular docking. Strains were cultivated under a temperature gradient (30 °C, 33 °C, 35 °C), and docking simulations evaluated substrate specificity. YlMDH2 displayed a transient peak expression pattern rising 2,630 ± 357 TPM at 48 h before declining sharply, whereas the thermotolerant reference strain maintained constitutively high expression. Transcript levels were highly temperature-sensitive, maximizing at 33 °C (8,386 ± 441 TPM) and decreasing at 35 °C (2,041 ± 1,169 TPM), correlating with mannitol accumulation. Molecular docking revealed a preferential binding affinity for D-fructose (–12.1 kcal/mol) and D-mannitol (–11.6 kcal/mol), while exhibiting significantly lower affinity for erythrose and erythritol, elucidating the substrate bias. Key catalytic residues Gln183, Ser173, Ser171, and Asn178 were identified, and PPI analysis positioned YlMDH2 within a central NADPH cofactor-management module linked to 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase. Temperature-dependent upregulation of YlMDH2 drives mannitol formation and competes directly with erythritol synthesis. Targeting YlMDH2 offers a rational strategy to minimize by-product formation and enhance erythritol production in Y. lipolytica.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10123-026-00820-5.

Introduction

The escalating global health burden associated with excessive dietary sugar intake has intensified efforts to identify safe, low-calorie sweeteners (Groenewald et al. 2014, Boesten et al. 2013). Among the available alternatives, erythritol, a four-carbon sugar alcohol, has emerged as an especially attractive candidate owing to its favorable sensory properties, non-cariogenic character, negligible glycemic impact, and excellent gastrointestinal tolerance (Xiang et al. 2025). Although several osmophilic yeasts are capable of erythritol biosynthesis, the non-conventional oleaginous yeast Yarrowia lipolytica has gained particular industrial prominence because of its robust growth, broad metabolic flexibility, and well-established genetic engineering toolkit (Groenewald et al. 2014, Wu et al. 2025).

In Y. lipolytica, erythritol is synthesized primarily via the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), where the NADPH-dependent reduction of erythrose to erythritol, catalyzed by erythrose reductase (ER), constitutes the key committed step. Extensive metabolic-engineering initiatives have intensified flux through this route by overexpressing ER isoforms and pivotal PPP enzymes, notably transketolase (TKL1), and by augmenting cofactor regeneration through glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (ZWF1) and 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase (GND1) (Zhou et al. 2024). When combined with disruption of competing pathways such as erythrulose kinase (EYK1), these strategies have enabled laboratory-scale erythritol titers approaching 190 g L⁻¹ (Carly et al. 2017, Gong et al. 2024).

Despite such progress, large-scale erythritol manufacturing remains constrained by two principal factors: (i) substantial co-production of polyol by-products, particularly mannitol and arabitol, which complicates downstream purification and depresses overall yields, and (ii) significant thermal-management requirements arising from the limited thermotolerance of production strains (Teoh et al. 2024). A further fundamental limitation is the intense competition for the essential reducing cofactor NADPH, which is required not only for the ER-catalyzed reaction but also for numerous ancillary biosynthetic and detoxification processes (Cheng et al. 2018, Huang et al. 2024, Wang et al. 2020, Liu et al. 2025).

In the pursuit of superior microbial cell factories for erythritol production, metabolic engineering of Yarrowia lipolytica has targeted key branch points in polyol metabolism. In this context, Wang et al. (2020a) established the gene YALI0D18964g (MDH2) as a key regulator, demonstrating that its deletion eliminates mannitol production to enhance erythritol synthesis(Wang et al. 2020).

A major challenge in maximizing erythritol production in Yarrowia lipolytica is the diversion of carbon flux into the competing mannitol biosynthesis pathway. Recent investigations have identified the gene YALI0D18964g (MDH2), which encodes mannitol dehydrogenase 2, as the key enzyme responsible for this diversion. Deleting MDH2 abolishes mannitol synthesis and significantly increases erythritol yield, a critical advance for industrial-scale production (Wang et al. 2020, Wang et al. 2020, Wang et al. 2020). Intriguingly, osmotic-stress conditions that promote erythritol biosynthesis naturally downregulate MDH2 expression, indicating an evolutionarily coordinated relationship between these metabolic pathways. Nevertheless, the systems-level metabolic consequences of MDH2 disruption remain only partially defined (Lang et al. 2025, Wang et al. 2025).

To address this knowledge gap, we employed an integrative multi-omics strategy to elucidate the global metabolic ramifications of YALI0D18964g deletion in Y. lipolytica (Huang et al. 2024, Wang et al. 2020). Temporal transcriptomic and proteomic analysis under production conditions revealed this dehydrogenase to be among the most abundantly expressed enzymes during high-productivity phases and a central hub in NADPH-utilization networks. We hypothesized that YALI0D18964g encodes a promiscuous dehydrogenase that competes directly with erythrose reductase for NADPH, thereby diverting carbon intermediates toward by-product formation (Huang et al. 2025, Sun et al. 2025).

Previous studies have documented the disruption of the YlMDH2 gene, underscoring its central role in controlling carbon flux within Yarrowia lipolytica. Building on this foundation, we performed integrated transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic profiling, complemented by structural characterization and molecular-docking analyses, to elucidate the system-wide consequences of MDH2 deletion. Our data reveal a coordinated rewiring of central carbon metabolism that both redirects flux toward erythritol biosynthesis and strengthens cellular stress-tolerance mechanisms. These findings not only confirm YALI0D18964g (MDH2) as a prime metabolic-engineering target for enhanced erythritol production but also highlight additional candidate loci for the strain optimization. This systems-level framework establishes a robust foundation for the development of next-generation Y. lipolytica strains capable of achieving high erythritol yields with minimal by-product formation and improved thermal resilience.

Materials and methods

Microbial strains and culture conditions

Yarrowia lipolytica reference strain CGMCC 7326 (X969) and the thermosensitive strain X90.8.4 were employed for thermal-response experiments. For erythritol production, a high-producing derivative of X90.8.4 (HP-Ery) was used as the production strain. Strain X90.8.4, a wild-type erythritol producer originally isolated from plant surfaces, was obtained from the China Center of Industrial Culture Collection (CICC 1675) and served as a conventional reference. The thermosensitive strain X90.8.4 was used to assess metabolic performance under elevated temperature conditions. Strains were routinely maintained on YPD agar (10 g L⁻¹ yeast extract, 20 g L⁻¹ peptone, 20 g L⁻¹ glucose, 20 g L⁻¹ agar) and propagated in YPD broth for experimental use. Long-term storage was achieved using sterile 20% glycerol at − 80 °C, and strain viability was periodically confirmed via colony-forming unit assays, consistently exceeding 95%. For thermal-response assays, overnight cultures were inoculated into 250 mL baffled Erlenmeyer flasks containing 50 mL YPD medium at an initial OD₆₀₀ of approximately 0.1 and incubated at 30 °C, 33 °C, or 35 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. Cultures were harvested at mid-exponential phase (OD₆₀₀ ≈ 1.0) for transcriptomic analyses. Erythritol production experiments were conducted in 5000 mL bioreactors containing 3000 mL of erythritol-inducing medium (300 g L⁻¹ glucose, 8 g L⁻¹ yeast extract, 2 g L⁻¹ tryptone, 2 g L⁻¹ (NH4) ₂HPO₄, initial pH 5.5). Fermentations were maintained at 30 °C with aeration of 1 vvm and agitation at 600 rpm. Samples were collected at 0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h for multi-omics analyses, metabolite profiling, and cofactor quantification.

Adaptive laboratory evolution for thermotolerance

To enhance thermal tolerance and organic acid resistance, adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE) was performed using the thermosensitive strain X90.8.4. Cells were serially propagated in YPD medium supplemented with 0.5% (v/v) organic acid mixture (acetic acid: hexanoic acid, 1:4 ratio), with incremental temperature increases from 30 °C to 35 °C over 30 generations, rising by 0.5 °C every three generations. Daily 1:100 dilutions into fresh medium ensured maintenance in exponential growth. Evolved clones were selected based on their ability to sustain a minimum specific growth rate of 0.15 h⁻¹ at 35 °C and to retain erythritol production above 80% of the parental strain. Whole-genome sequencing (Illumina NovaSeq, 30× coverage) verified the absence of deleterious mutations in key metabolic pathways.

RNA extraction, library preparation, and sequencing

For transcriptomic analysis, cell pellets were harvested at designated time points and immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Cells were disrupted mechanically using acid-washed glass beads in a bead mill (4 × 30 s cycles at 4 °C). Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) with on-column DNase I treatment to eliminate genomic DNA contamination. RNA integrity was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, and samples with RIN ≥ 8.0 were used for library preparation. Poly(A)-selected, strand-specific libraries were constructed using the Illumina TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit v2, and fragments were size-selected between 200 and 500 bp. Paired-end sequencing (150 bp) was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, yielding a minimum of 25 million reads per sample, with > 90% of bases achieving Q30 quality. All samples were processed using the same extraction and library preparation workflow to ensure data comparability.

Transcriptomic data processing and differential expression analysis

Raw sequencing reads were quality-filtered using fastp (v0.23.2) (Chen et al. 2018) to remove adapters and low-quality sequences. Clean reads were aligned to the Y. lipolytica CLIB122 reference genome (GenBank assembly GCA_000002525.1) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1)(Kim et al. 2019) with strand-specific parameters. Gene-level counts and transcripts-per-million (TPM) values were quantified using featureCounts (Subread v2.0.3) (Liao et al. 2014). Differential expression was assessed using DESeq2 (v1.38.0) (Love et al. 2014) and validated with edgeR (v3.38.0) (Robinson et al. 2010) in R (v4.2.2) (R: A Language and Environment for Statistical Computing, 2000), applying Benjamini–Hochberg correction to control the false discovery rate. Genes with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and absolute log₂ fold-change ≥ 1 were considered significant. Temporal expression dynamics of YALI0_D18964g and other polyol-related genes were analyzed to determine peak expression windows and potential NADPH competition with erythrose reductase.

Multi-omics integration

Metabolomic and cofactor data were integrated with transcriptomic profiles to construct a multi-omics network of polyol metabolism. Intracellular NADPH/NADP⁺ ratios were quantified using the NADP/NADPH Quantification Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, MAK479) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Correlation analyses between gene expression and NADPH consumption were performed using the mixOmics R package (v6.32.0) and visualized with ggplot2 and Complex Heatmap in R. Key metabolic bottlenecks were identified by integrating transcript abundance, NADPH demand, and metabolite flux, thereby highlighting candidate targets for genetic engineering.

Quantitative PCR Validation

The same RNA samples extracted for RNA-seq (Sect. 2.3) were used for qPCR validation to ensure methodological consistency. One microgram of DNase-treated RNA was reverse-transcribed using the iScript™ cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad). Quantitative PCR was performed on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad) using SYBR Green chemistry and gene-specific primers. Amplification conditions were: 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Melt-curve analysis (65–95 °C) confirmed amplification specificity. Gene expression levels were normalized to the endogenous reference gene β-actin (YALI0_D08272g), and relative transcript abundance was calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method. All reactions were performed in technical triplicates and biological triplicates.

Analytical Methods

Extracellular sugars and polyols were quantified using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Culture supernatants were collected at designated time points, clarified by centrifugation (12,000 × g, 10 min), and filtered through 0.22-µm membrane filters before analysis. Samples were injected into an HPLC system equipped with a Shodex RI-101 refractive index detector and a Shodex SP0810 ion-exclusion column (300 × 8 mm). Isocratic elution was performed with deionized water at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min, and the column temperature was maintained at 70 °C. Quantification of glucose, erythritol, and mannitol was carried out using external calibration curves prepared from analytical-grade standards. Erythritol mass yield (Y_ery) was calculated as the ratio of erythritol produced (P, g) to the amount of glucose consumed (S, g):Yery ​ = S / P​.Volumetric productivity (Q_ery) was calculated from the amount of erythritol produced per unit culture volume (V, L) and fermentation time (t, h): Qery​ = P / V × t.

The glucose consumption rate (R_glu) was defined as the amount of glucose metabolized per liter of culture per hour.

All analytical measurements were performed in triplicate, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

In Silico protein analysis and molecular docking

Amino acid sequences of YALI0_D18964g were analyzed for domain architecture and functional motifs using InterProScan. Ligands relevant to polyol metabolism, including D-mannitol, D-fructose, D-erythrose, and erythritol, were energy-minimized using Avogadro (v1.2.0) (Huang et al. 2025) before molecular docking with AutoDock Vina (v1.2.0). Binding affinities were calculated in kcal mol⁻¹ to infer substrate specificity. An exhaustiveness value of 32 was used to ensure adequate sampling of the conformational search space. Predicted protein-protein interactions were retrieved from STRING v12.0, and interaction networks were visualized in Cytoscape (v3.9.1) (Larroude et al. 2018).

Gene deletion and fermentation analysis

Targeted deletion of YALI0_D18964g (Δmdh2) was achieved via homologous recombination using a URA3 selection cassette flanked by 1 kb upstream and downstream homology arms. Correct integration was confirmed by PCR and Sanger sequencing. Batch fermentations of Δmdh2 and parental strains were performed in 1 L bioreactors under erythritol-inducing conditions. Erythritol and mannitol concentrations were quantified by HPLC using a Rezex ROA-Organic Acid H⁺ column (Phenomenex) with 5 mM H₂SO₄ mobile phase at 0.6 mL min⁻¹. Intracellular NADPH/NADP⁺ ratios were measured concomitantly to evaluate cofactor redistribution upon gene deletion.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in biological triplicate unless otherwise indicated. Data are reported as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed in R (v4.2.2) using ggpubr, stats, and lme4 packages. Significance was defined as p < 0.05 unless specified otherwise.

Results

Identification and functional characterization of YALI0_D18964g as a key metabolic regulator

Erythritol production in the yeast Yarrowia lipolytica is an important industrial process, but it is limited because some carbon is diverted to produce other polyols, such as mannitol and D-arabitol. These by-products lower the erythritol yield and make purification more difficult, increasing production costs. To find the genes responsible for this competition, RNA sequencing was performed on the high erythritol-producing strain CGMCC7326 under conditions that promote erythritol production. A thermoresistant strain, X90.8.4, created from CGMCC7326 through adaptive laboratory evolution, was analyzed in parallel to see how thermal adaptation affects erythritol production and cofactor use.The analysis showed that YALI0_D18964g is strongly regulated over time during erythritol production (Table 1). In CGMCC7326, its transcript levels rose from 1,512.7 ± 51.2 FPKM at 0 h to 2,630.7 ± 357.0 TPM at 48 h (log₂ fold change = + 0.73, q = 1.08 × 10⁻⁸) (Fig. 2F), which matches the peak of erythritol production. After 48 h, levels dropped to 482.3 ± 41.0 FPKM at 96 h (log₂ fold change = − 1.34, q = 4.59 × 10⁻³⁸), showing a clear “burst-and-retreat” pattern. This suggests that YALI0_D18964g activity is linked to erythritol synthesis and may compete with erythrose reductase (YlER) for NADPH during production (Fig. 1J; Table 1).

Molecular basis of substrate specificity and catalytic mechanism

To investigate the structural basis of YlMDH2 substrate specificity, molecular docking simulations were performed with four potential substrates: D-mannitol, D-fructose, D-erythrose, and erythritol. The docking results revealed clear differences in binding affinity, with strong interactions observed for the six-carbon sugars D-mannitol (− 11.6 kcal·mol⁻¹) and D-fructose (− 12.1 kcal·mol⁻¹), whereas the four-carbon metabolites D-erythrose (− 8.1 kcal·mol⁻¹) and erythritol (− 7.9 kcal·mol⁻¹) displayed weaker binding (Fig. 2A–H; Table 2). This energetic disparity provides a mechanistic explanation for the enzyme’s preferential engagement with six-carbon substrates, consistent with its role in temperature-dependent mannitol biosynthesis in Yarrowia lipolytica.

The Consensus Active Site Architecture of YALI0_D18964g

Structural inspection of the active site identified a conserved catalytic pocket composed of nine residues: GLN183, SER173, SER171, ASN178, TYR217, GLN181, TRP234, MET172, and TYR186. Detailed interaction analyses, including 2D interaction plots, LigPlot diagrams, and 3D models, highlighted the critical roles of SER173 and GLN183. SER173 functions as the catalytic base, facilitating proton transfer during the oxidoreduction reaction, while GLN183 stabilizes substrates by forming hydrogen bonds with the C2 and C3 hydroxyl groups of D-mannitol and D-fructose. These interactions confer high substrate specificity and catalytic precision (Fig. 2A–C; Table 2).

YlMDH2 is represented in cartoon format, ligands as stick models, catalytic residues in yellow, and substrate-binding surfaces with transparent electrostatic potential. The combined docking and structural analyses provide a comprehensive molecular rationale for YlMDH2’s preference for six-carbon sugars, elucidating its critical role in directing carbon flux toward mannitol formation and competing with erythritol synthesis (Fig. 3).

Temporal expression profile of YALI0_D18964g from RNA-seq analysis

At the baseline temperature of 30 °C, both strains exhibited substantial YALI0_D18964g expression; however, X90.8.4 maintained consistently higher basal levels (6,893–8,105 TPM) than the wild-type (5,707–7,102 TPM), representing approximately a 21% transcriptional increase (Fig. 4A–C). When exposed to elevated temperatures, the wild-type displayed a canonical heat-shock response, with transcript levels increasing to 8,125–8,909 TPM at 33 °C and reaching a pronounced maximum of 9,012–11,338 TPM at 35 °C, corresponding to a 37–58% increase over baseline expression (Fig. 4D–F). In CGMCC7326, expression peaks at 33 °C, whereas X90.8.4 maintains high expression up to 35 °C. In contrast, the thermoresistant X90.8.4 strain demonstrated a distinct regulatory pattern characterized by a gradual increase and sustained transcriptional stability. Transcript levels rose steadily to 8,819–10,123 TPM at 33 °C and remained consistently high (8,910–10,455 TPM) even at 35 °C, indicating enhanced transcriptional robustness under heat stress (Fig. 4G–I; Table 3).

These results indicate that YALI0_D18964g, which encodes a putative NADPH-dependent malate dehydrogenase (YlMDH2), functions as a thermo-responsive gene, exhibiting differential regulation in response to heat stress that likely contributes to the thermotolerance phenotype of the engineered strain. Further analysis of YALI0_D18964g expression dynamics under erythritol-inducing conditions revealed clear strain-specific temporal regulation patterns (Table 2). In the thermoresistant X90.8.4 strain, transcript levels ranged from 8,223 to 10,123 TPM at inoculation, gradually increasing to approximately 11,000 TPM at 48 h, followed by only a modest decline at 96 h (Table 3). This stable and moderate expression contrasted sharply with the characteristic burst-and-retreat pattern observed in the wild-type CGMCC7326, reflecting more regulated and sustained transcription in the engineered strain (Table 4). Expression dynamics were further supported by RNA-Seq data and qPCR validation. In CGMCC7326, transcript levels exhibited a sharp initial peak followed by rapid downregulation (Fig. 4B, E, F; Table 3).

Systems-level integration through multi-omics analysis

Comprehensive analysis of YlMDH2 (YALI0D18964g) revealed its central role in regulating NADPH-dependent metabolism and mannitol biosynthesis (Fig. 5; Table 6). Protein–protein interaction mapping positioned YlMDH2 as a highly connected node within the NADPH network, showing significant interactions with 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase (YALI0_B15598g; interaction score 0.428), a key NADPH-generating enzyme in the oxidative pentose phosphate pathway, and with squalene synthase (SQS1-2; interaction score 0.513), a major NADPH-consuming enzyme in sterol biosynthesis. Metabolic mapping further localized YlMDH2 within the fructose and mannose metabolism pathway (KEGG ko00051), indicating its role in diverting carbon flux from erythritol toward mannitol and D-arabitol, while conserved domain analysis confirmed characteristic motifs of the medium-chain dehydrogenase/reductase family.

Predicted impact of YlMDH2 deletion on erythritol production

Although YlMDH2 has not been experimentally deleted in this study, systems-level and metabolic modeling predict that a Δylmdh2 knockout could substantially improve erythritol production. Removal of YlMDH2 is expected to reduce mannitol accumulation, enhance erythritol titer and yield, and alleviate NADPH competition with other cellular processes. These analyses collectively highlight YlMDH2 as a metabolic bottleneck and a promising target for future strain engineering to optimize thermoresistant erythritol biosynthesis.

Discussion

The present study provides a comprehensive multi-omics framework for rational metabolic engineering of Yarrowia lipolytica aimed at enhancing erythritol production. By integrating transcriptomics, metabolomics, molecular docking, and promoter characterization, we identified and validated YALI0_D18964g (YlMDH2), an NADPH-dependent mannitol/D-arabitol dehydrogenase, as a central metabolic bottleneck diverting both carbon flux and reducing equivalents away from erythritol synthesis. These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating that YlMDH2 is the sole active mannitol dehydrogenase in vivo, and its deletion significantly improves erythritol yield (Tomaszewska et al. 2012, Li 2023).

One of the major outcomes of this study is the demonstration of distinct temporal regulation of YlMDH2 between the parental high-producing strain Yarrowia lipolytica CGMCC7326 and its thermoresistant derivative X90.8.4. In CGMCC7326, RNA-seq profiling revealed a characteristic “burst-and-retreat” expression pattern. Transcript levels of YlMDH2 increased sharply during the first 48 h of fermentation, at the same time as erythritol production began, and subsequently declined markedly by 96 h. This dynamic profile indicates that YlMDH2 initially diverts NADPH and carbon precursors toward mannitol and D-arabitol biosynthesis, thereby competing directly with erythrose reductase. The later downregulation of YlMDH2 reduces this cofactor competition and facilitates the redirection of reducing equivalents toward erythritol production in the subsequent phase. These findings underscore the importance of temporal control of enzyme activity and support previous observations that transient suppression of competing pathways can significantly improve target metabolite yields (Tomaszewska et al. 2012, Zhou 2020) .

In contrast, the thermoresistant strain X90.8.4 exhibited a fundamentally different regulatory strategy, characterized by moderate but sustained YlMDH2 expression throughout the fermentation cycle. This expression pattern suggests a rebalanced distribution of reducing equivalents that supports both thermal adaptation and stable erythritol production. The maintenance of consistent YlMDH2 expression in X90.8.4 under heat stress points to enhanced transcriptional robustness relative to CGMCC7326, whose pronounced fluctuations may render it more sensitive to environmental changes. These observations are consistent with findings from adaptive laboratory evolution, which show that thermoresistant strains often achieve resilience by reprogramming redox homeostasis and stabilizing NADPH allocation (Tomaszewska et al. 2014, Wang 2024).

Molecular docking and dynamics simulations provided structural explanations for the strong substrate specificity of YlMDH2 toward six-carbon polyols such as D-mannitol and D-fructose, while exhibiting substantially weaker affinity for four-carbon metabolites like erythritol and its intermediates. The catalytic pocket of YlMDH2 was defined by conserved residues (SER173, GLN183, TYR186, TYR217, TRP234), with SER173 acting as the catalytic base and GLN183 anchoring substrate binding. These structural features rationalize the enzyme’s preferential role in diverting carbon flux away from erythritol biosynthesis and reinforce its designation as a critical metabolic bottleneck. Such mechanistic insights align with the broader literature on medium-chain dehydrogenase/reductase enzymes, where substrate binding energetics strongly determine flux partitioning (Wang et al. 2020).

Promoter is the strongest native promoter in Y. lipolytica, surpassing PTEF by 1.6-fold. The strong promoter activity explains the significant NADPH and carbon drain associated with YlMDH2 during early production and provides an opportunity for a “knockout-and-harness” approach, simultaneously removing a metabolic bottleneck while leveraging the promoter to drive beneficial pathway genes. Comparative analysis highlighted the advantages of the engineered strain. CGMCC7326 (X969) displayed early peaks in YlMDH2 expression and higher NADPH competition compared to X90.8.4, enabling controlled polyol synthesis and efficient erythritol accumulation. These observations underscore the critical role of precise temporal and environmental regulation of NADPH-dependent enzymes in high-yield erythritol production (Janek et al. 2017, Gu et al. 2020).

Finally, integrative strategies combining temporal regulation, cofactor engineering, and thermal optimization promise to further enhance erythritol production. Future studies could integrate systems-level modeling and adaptive laboratory evolution to fine-tune cofactor availability, enzyme kinetics, and thermal resilience, minimizing byproducts and improving process robustness (Qiu et al. 2020). Collectively, our findings provide a robust systems-level validation of YlMDH2 as a high-confidence metabolic engineering target, consistent with prior studies, and offer a strategic framework for optimizing industrial erythritol production in Yarrowia lipolytica.

Conclusion

Integrated transcriptomic, structural, and systems-level analyses identify YALI0_D18964g (YlMDH2) as a key metabolic regulator in Yarrowia lipolytica, directing carbon flux between erythritol and competing polyol pathways. In the wild-type CGMCC7326 strain, YlMDH2 exhibited a dynamic temporal expression profile, peaking at 2,630.7 ± 357.0 FPKM at 48 h and declining to 482.3 ± 41.0 FPKM at 96 h, reflecting transient NADPH competition with erythrose reductase. In contrast, the thermotolerant X90.8.4 strain maintained moderate, sustained expression, promoting stable NADPH allocation and consistent erythritol biosynthesis under thermal stress. Structural modeling and molecular docking revealed preferential binding to six-carbon substrates (D-mannitol, − 116.46 kcal·mol⁻¹; D-fructose, − 121.14 kcal·mol⁻¹), with catalytic residues Ser173 and Gln183 mediating proton transfer and substrate coordination, explaining substrate specificity and carbon flux partitioning. Systems-level integration positioned YlMDH2 at the NADPH metabolic hub, interacting with both NADPH-generating and -consuming enzymes. Predictive analyses suggest that targeted deletion of YlMDH2 could enhance erythritol titers, reduce mannitol accumulation, and increase the NADPH/NADP⁺ ratio, underscoring its role as a metabolic bottleneck. These findings highlight YlMDH2 as a strategic target for metabolic engineering to optimize cofactor utilization, minimize by-product formation, and enhance industrial erythritol production.
