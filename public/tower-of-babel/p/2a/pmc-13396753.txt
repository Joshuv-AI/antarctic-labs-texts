Climate hazards and mental health hospitalizations in China: socioeconomic disparities, demographic drivers, and adaptation – a nationwide population-based study
Authors: Hanxu Shi, Teng Wang, Chaobo Bai, Peng Wang, Fiona Kwok, Weiwen Li, Lu Jing, Qijiong Zhu, Rizhen Wang, Jing Chen, Huining Yang, Jingtao Ding, Murray Ramanason, Shyam Biswal, Haojun Fan, Yujun Shen, Minghui Ren, Bin Wang, Junliang Yuan, Zhenyu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundMental and behavioural disorders account for a large and growing share of the global disease burden, yet evidence on hazard-specific mental health risks and adaptation remains limited. We aimed to systematically evaluate disease-specific hospitalization risks and attributable burdens following multiple climate hazards in China, and examined whether socioeconomic conditions, demographic change, and historical hazard experience modified these risks.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective observational study in 955 counties in China (2016–2023) using 392,924 daily disease-specific hospitalizations for mental and behavioural disorders from the Chinese Multiple County (CMC) Hospital Network. Storm, flood, and tropical cyclone events were linked to hospitalizations, and propensity score matching aided difference-in-differences (PSM-DID) models were used to estimate post–hazard excess relative risks across eight diagnostic categories. We then estimated hazard-attributable mental health hospitalizations and used counterfactual decomposition analysis to quantify the contribution of population aging and growth to hazard-attributable burden.FindingsSchizophrenia and delusion, mood disorders, and stress-related disorders showed stronger associations with climate hazards. Floods were associated with broader and longer-lasting increases in mental health hospitalizations, with elevated admissions persisting for 6–8 weeks after events. Averaged across 2016–2023, storms, floods, and tropical cyclones collectively generated approximately 117,717 (93,667, 147,158) additional mental and behavioural hospitalizations per year in China, 41.05% of which occurred in children, adolescents, and older adults. Population aging and growth accounted for 1.20–5.81% of hazard-attributable hospitalizations, depending on hazard type. Socioeconomically developed counties (quartile 4) experienced 16.18–24.96% lower hazard-attributable hospitalization risks than less developed counties (quartiles 1–3). Counties with the highest historical hazard frequency (quartile 4) showed 11.30–14.29% lower impacts from storms and tropical cyclones than counties with lower historical frequency (quartiles 1–3), whereas no such adaptive advantage was observed for floods. This adaptation observation was more pronounced in socioeconomically developed settings.InterpretationStorms, floods, and tropical cyclones imposed substantial and inequitably distributed mental health burdens. Climate adaptation policies should prioritize vulnerable populations and less developed regions, leveraging both socioeconomic development and hazard preparedness to promote mental health equity and sustainability under escalating climate threats.FundingNational Natural Science Foundation of China, National Key Research & Development Program of Ministry of Science and Technology of China, Beijing Municipal Ecology and Environment Bureau.Keywords: Climate hazards, Mental health, Socioeconomic disparities, Demographic drivers, Adaptation

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Mental and behavioural disorders represent a major source of global health burden, yet climate hazard-mental health outcomes remain inadequately quantified, limiting evidence-based adaptation strategies under climate change. Prior to conducting this study, we searched PubMed and Web of Science for articles published up to July 2025 using the search terms (climate hazards OR extreme weather disasters OR storm OR flood OR tropical cyclone) AND (mental disorders OR mental hospitalizations OR mental hospitalization rates) AND (adaptation capacity OR adaptation policy). Most existing studies focused on a narrow range of hazard-mental health outcomes, often relying on cross-sectional designs, self-reported measures, or geographically restricted samples. Few studies compared multiple hazards within the same analytic framework, characterized post–hazard risk trajectories, or provided disease-specific estimates based on routinely collected hospitalization data. Evidence on adaptation was particularly limited. Additionally, while existing studies increasingly discussed broad adaptation concepts, they rarely offered comprehensive, quantitative evidence in real-world, county-scale settings that connect sustainable, health-oriented development with climate-mental health adaptation needs.

Added value of this study

To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to provide a comprehensive, nationwide, disease-specific assessment of the mental and behavioural health impacts of multiple types of climate hazards in China. By integrating daily cause-specific hospitalization records with county-level socioeconomic data, we quantified excess hospitalization risks and attributable burdens across eight categories of mental and behavioural disorders; and examined health inequity across diverse socioeconomic and demographic groups. Our findings demonstrate that children, adolescents, and older adults bearing disproportionate burdens, and highlight the potential importance of socioeconomic development in shaping planned (structural) and autonomous (historical hazard experience-related) adaptation capacity that might reduce future mental health impacts under climate change.

Implications of all the available evidence

Climate hazards pose substantial, prolonged, and inequitable mental health burdens on the public. Adaptation planning should move beyond generic post–hazard mental health messaging and incorporate hazard-specific time courses and local vulnerability profiles. Policies should priorities children, adolescents, and older adults, as well as socioeconomically disadvantaged counties. Adaptation interventions may require rapid surge capacity after storms and tropical cyclones, and longer follow-up after floods. Strengthening healthcare access, emergency response capacity, and social protection, alongside community preparedness, may help reduce mental health inequities in the face of climate change challenges.

Introduction

Climate hazards, including storms, floods, and tropical cyclones, can affect mental health through direct trauma, interruption of psychiatric care, and disruption of housing and livelihoods.1, 2, 3 This can increase demand for mental health care beyond the capacity of available services. While previous studies have reported that specific hazards can increase the risk of depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD),4, 5, 6 it remains unclear why these hazards lead to varied mental health burdens across different places and populations. Addressing this question requires multi-hazard evidence considering temporal patterns, socioeconomic conditions, adaptation capacity, and demographic composition at a national scale.7,8

The first challenge is to determine when the mental health consequences will emerge following climate hazards. Psychiatric consequences are unlikely to follow a single trajectory after hazard onset. Instead, immediate shock, prolonged displacement, and delayed restoration of health services may increase risks at different periods in the following weeks or months.9 These temporal patterns may also vary by hazard type. For example, after Hurricane Katrina, the occurrence of post-traumatic stress disorder peaked within the first two months.10,11 By contrast, floods in England were associated with elevated psychiatric distress and anxiety that persisted for years.12,13 However, most studies relied on cross-sectional surveys, self-reported symptom measures, or single-hazard case studies,13, 14, 15 which provide limited evidence on the timing of risk trajectories and clinical health care.

The second challenge is to understand how adaptation mediates the impacts of climate hazards on mental health. Two types of adaptation are particularly relevant.16,17 Planned (or structural) adaptation18 refers to the social and material conditions that facilitate protection and recovery, including public services, financial resources, social networks, and local institutional capacity.19 Autonomous (or experiential) adaptation15 refers to the adaptive behaviours and preparedness that individuals and communities developed through previous hazard exposure.20 Planned adaptation is generally expected to provide more reliable and equitable protection,21 but its effectiveness in reducing hazard-related mental health burden remains poorly quantified. The effects of autonomous adaptation are not well characterized as well: repeated hazard exposure may improve preparedness, but it may also accumulate stress and worsen responses to later events.15 For example, prior hurricane experience in the southeastern United States was associated with increased preparedness for later storms,22 whereas repeated natural disaster exposure in Australia was associated with cumulative psychological distress.15 It remains unclear whether and under what conditions prior hazard experience strengthens adaptation capacity for mental health.

Demographic transitions, particularly population aging and growth, may amplify the mental health burden associated with climate hazards by increasing the size and vulnerability of the exposed populations. Population aging increases the proportion of older adults in exposed populations, a group at higher risk of hazard-related mental health burden.23 Population growth increases the number of people residing in high-hazard counties, especially in urban and coastal areas, where people are more frequently affected by floods and tropical cyclones.24 Currently, few studies have quantified the extent to which these demographic shifts altered the post–hazard mental health burden. Disentangling the contribution of demographic change from that of changing hazard profiles is essential for accurate attribution and for designing population-oriented adaptation strategies.7

To address these challenges, we integrated climate hazards records of storms, floods, and tropical cyclones from the EM-DAT25 disaster database with daily cause-specific mental health hospitalizations from the Chinese Multiple County (CMC) Hospital Network between 2016 and 2023. Then, we estimated the excess hospitalization risk and attributable burdens across hazard types and disease categories.26,27 Next, we examined whether advanced county-level socioeconomic conditions were associated with lower burden and identified the determinants of socioeconomic dimensions underlying this variation. Subsequently, we assessed whether greater historical hazard exposure experience was associated with lower subsequent hospitalization risks, as a proxy for autonomous adaptation.24 Finally, we estimated the contributions of population aging and growth to hazard-attributable burden in China. Together, these analyses provide national scale evidence on how socioeconomic context, adaptation, and demographic change influenced mental health hospitalizations attributable to climate hazards.

Methods

Climate hazards and observation windows

We obtained data on storms, floods, and tropical cyclones (including hazard types, geographic locations, and start and end dates) from the EM-DAT database. Hazards were classified according to the 2014 Integrated Research on Disaster Risk (IRDR) Peril Classification and Hazard Glossary28 and included according to entry criteria29 (i.e., events meeting at least one of: ≥10 deaths, ≥100 people affected, declaration of a state of emergency, or call for international assistance; Supplementary Text 1). The finest spatial resolution available was county-level. In total, we analysed 324 county-level tropical cyclones (i.e., county–hazard combinations), 521 storms, and 1959 flooding events occurring between 2016 and 2023 (Supplementary Fig. S1a–c).

To assess temporal changes in hazard-related mental health impacts, we constructed sequential 2-week observation windows after each hazard, extending to 8 weeks, beyond which no significant changes in hospitalizations were observed (see Results). We also defined a 2-week pre-hazard “warning window” to detect any differential trends between hazard-affected and control counties, such as those arising from forecast-driven anxiety or pre-hazard changes in healthcare access that could confound post–hazard estimates (Supplementary Table S1). For each post–hazard window, a 2-week pre-period immediately preceding the warning window served as the DID comparison pre-period. See Supplementary Text 2 for detailed window settings.

Moreover, to avoid contamination when selecting control counties for the DID analysis, we applied strict temporal exclusions to remove potential lag-related bias (Methods: statistical analysis: control county pre-selection). Additional details on EM-DAT selection, inclusion criteria, and potential dataset biases are provided in Supplementary Text 1.

Daily cause-specific mental health hospitalizations

We examined daily mental health hospitalizations defined as inpatient admissions with a principal discharge diagnosis in the ICD-10 F chapter (Mental and behavioural disorders, codes F00–F99). Hospitalization data were obtained from the CMC Hospital Network, which recorded over 200 million all-cause inpatient encounters across most Chinese counties between 2016 and 2023 (Supplementary Fig. S1d). The dataset includes detailed information on patient demographics, diagnoses, admission routes (emergency department (ED), outpatient referral, and transfers), hospitalization charges, payment methods, Diagnosis-Related Group (DRG) data, insurance coverage, and out-of-pocket payments. Sex was recorded as male or female based on the patient's biological sex. Gender identity was not separately collected in the hospital information system. We present sex-stratified analyses throughout this study and use the term “sex” to refer to this biologically based administrative classification. Ethnicity data was not included in the de-identified dataset provided under the data-use agreement with the CMC Hospital Network.

To reduce potential selection bias30 and ensure sufficient statistical power in DID estimation,26 we excluded hospitals showing systematic underreporting, defined as consecutive periods exceeding one month with zero recorded admissions inconsistent with their typical patient volumes. Besides, counties were included only if constituent hospitals collectively achieved ≥95% daily data completeness. The final analytical dataset comprised 392,924 mental health hospitalizations. In the Chinese hospital system, each hospitalization record contains a single principal discharge diagnosis determined by the attending physician based on the condition primarily responsible for admission and the majority of treatment resources consumed during the stay. We treated each hospitalization as an independent event. Individuals with multiple admissions during the study period contributed multiple observations, with each admission classified according to its principal diagnosis.

We included all eight major diagnostic subgroups within the ICD-10 F chapter to capture the full spectrum of climate hazard impacts on mental health: Organic, including symptomatic, mental disorders (F00–F09), psychoactive substance use (F10–F19), schizophrenia and delusion (F20–F29), mood (affective) disorders (F30–F39), stress-related disorders (F40–F49), behavioural syndromes (F50–F69), intellectual disability (F70–F89), and other mental health conditions (F90–F99). This comprehensive inclusion was motivated by accumulating evidence demonstrating that environmental stressors operate through diverse neurobiological, psychological, and social pathways to precipitate or exacerbate multiple psychiatric conditions.31 Hereafter, “hospitalizations” refer exclusively to inpatient admissions with principal mental health diagnoses, not all-cause hospitalizations.

Notably, our data source comprises inpatient records rather than emergency department logs. This limitation means our estimates may underestimate the total acute mental health service utilization if climate hazards disproportionately increase emergency visits not requiring admission.

Statistical analysis

We employed a PSM-DID framework26,27,32 to estimate the causal impact of climate hazards on mental health hospitalizations. A simple within-county before-and-after comparison would be vulnerable to temporal confounding such as seasonal fluctuations, concurrent policy changes, evolving healthcare-seeking behaviours, and secular trends in psychiatric admissions that co-occur with hazard events. By incorporating matched comparable control counties, the DID framework uses the temporal change observed in non-exposed counties as the counterfactual trajectory, representing what would have occurred in hazard-affected counties absent the hazard, thereby isolating the hazard effect from concurrent temporal confounders.26,27

Compared to conventional exposure-response models (e.g., distributed lag nonlinear model33), this quasi-experimental study design offers two distinct advantages: (1) PSM eliminates confounding from measured baseline characteristics by creating statistically balanced comparison groups, mimicking the randomization achieved in experimental designs; (2) DID controls for both time-invariant unmeasured confounders and secular trends by comparing the changes in hospitalization rates between matched hazard-affected (treatment) and non-affected (control) counties. This double-robustness approach isolates the causal effect of hazard exposure from confounding in both cross-sectional and temporal dimensions.

The framework comprised four sequential steps: (1) control county pre-selection to identify geographically and temporally valid comparison counties; (2) covariates balancing through PSM to eliminate observable confounding; (3) parallel trends validation34 through event-study design to rule out residual confounding factors; and (4) DID estimation using generalized linear mixed-effects models to quantify hazard-mental health impacts.

Control county pre-selection

To identify valid controls, we matched each hazard-affected county with counties that had comparable baseline characteristics. First, potential controls were restricted to counties within the same climate type35 (Supplementary Fig. S2). Second, adjacent counties (defined as those sharing any boundary segment or vertex with the hazard-affected county) were excluded to avoid spillover contamination.36,37 Third, counties that experienced other climate hazards within four months preceding the current hazard were excluded to eliminate confounding from post–hazard effects. This temporal criterion was determined by the quantified hazard lag effects (i.e., within eight weeks, see Results section), also comparable with existing literature.26,27 Fourth, we calculated Moran's I across candidate control counties and hazard-affected counties to address potential spatial autocorrelation that could violate the independence assumption underlying our statistical models38 (Supplementary Text 3).

Observable covariates balance through PSM

Following geographical and temporal filtering, we employed the PSM approach to balance covariates between hazard-affected and control counties. Propensity score was estimated using a logistic regression model including county-level socioeconomic indicators (gross regional product per capita and growth rate, household saving, employment rate, and education), environmental characteristics (urbanization, greening ratio, transportation), healthcare characteristics (healthcare system including hospital density, physician-to-population ratio, and health expenditure), and demographics (age structure, sex ratio, and population density). Each hazard-affected county was matched to four nearest neighbour controls, a ratio that balances statistical efficiency against match quality.39 Covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD), with values below 10% indicating adequate balance40 (Supplementary Fig. S3). Kernel density plots of propensity score distributions before and after matching demonstrated substantial improvement in overlap (comparability) between hazard-affected and non-affected groups, visually confirming the elimination of observable confounders (Supplementary Fig. S4). Specifically, before matching, propensity score distributions showed visible separation between treated and control counties, reflecting systematic baseline differences. After matching, the distributions became nearly identical, indicating that after PSM, all observed covariates included in the propensity model were effectively balanced between groups, thereby eliminating observable confounding. The number of matched hazard-affected and control counties was summarized in Supplementary Table S2.

Parallel trends validation for unobservable confounder control

While PSM addressed confounding from measured baseline characteristics, unmeasured time-varying factors may still bias estimates. To address this threat, we examined the parallel trends assumption using an event-study design,26,41,42 which statistically evaluates pre-hazard trend similarity while distinguishing systematic differences from random fluctuations. Full results (Supplementary Figs. S5–S7) showed no significant pre-hazard differential trends across all mental health conditions. This validation provided strong evidence against confounding from unobserved time-varying factors, such as local policy changes, community-level stress dynamics, or gradual shifts in healthcare-seeking behaviour.43

County classification and adaptation capacity

To examine whether adaptation capacity (i.e., manifested as attenuated hospitalization responses to hazards24) differs according to structural resources and experiential learning, we conducted stratified analyses comparing counties with varying levels of socioeconomic development and historical hazard exposure frequency. Here we distinguished two complementary patterns of adaptation: (1) structural adaptation capacity, conferred by socioeconomic advancement; and (2) experience-based adaptation, autonomously developed through repeated historical exposure. Here, socioeconomic development was quantified through principal component analysis (PCA) of multiple indicators, classifying counties as developed (top quartile) or less developed (1–3 quartiles) (Supplementary Fig. S8; Supplementary Text 4). Historical hazard exposure was determined from EM-DAT records during the baseline period 2000–2015, categorizing high- (top quartile) and low-frequency (1–3 quartiles) counties by frequency of prior events.

Health burden and demographic drivers

Furthermore, to translate epidemiological effect estimates into actionable public health metrics, we calculated the attributable fractions (AF) and estimated the population-level mental health hospitalizations caused by multiple climate hazards (Supplementary Text 5). Additionally, we performed counterfactual-based decomposition analysis24 to disentangle the contribution of the relatively long-term demographic transition (including population aging and growth) from climate hazard exposure itself in driving hazard-mental health impacts (Supplementary Text 6).

Sensitivity analyses

Finally, comprehensive sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of our findings. First, to assess whether results were sensitive to the specification of county-level heterogeneity, we re-estimated the PSM-DID models replacing county fixed effects with random effects. After extensive pre-selection and PSM, treated and control counties are comparable at baseline, making the random effects exogeneity assumption44 plausible. Concordance between the two specifications therefore serves as additional verification that matching achieved sufficient balance and that unobserved county-level confounding does not meaningfully influence hazard effect estimates. Second, we performed sensitivity analysis excluding 2020–2021, the period with substantial pandemic-related mobility restrictions and healthcare-seeking disruptions in China. Third, to examine whether repeat admissions influenced our DID estimates, we re-estimated all PSM-DID models by retaining only the first admission per individual for the same principal diagnosis during the study period and excluding subsequent readmissions. Fourth, we used a Monte-Carlo randomization approach to validate the robustness of our PCA-aided data-driven aggregation approach for county's socioeconomic development rankings under diverse weighting schemes.

Model adequacy was systematically evaluated across four diagnostic dimensions for all DID specifications. First, influential observations were identified through Cook's distance,45,46 with conventional thresholds of 4/n (conservative) and 1 (permissive). Second, multicollinearity among predictors was evaluated via variance inflation factors (VIF), with values below 5 indicating absence of concern.47 Third, residual serial dependence was assessed via the Durbin–Watson statistic.48 Fourth, model specification was examined through correlations between Pearson residuals and fitted values.49 Full diagnostic results are reported in Supplementary Table S3.

Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, and all effect estimates were reported with 95% confidence intervals. Analyses were performed using R 4.4.1, ArcGIS Pro 3.2, and MATLAB 2024b.

Results

Hospitalization risks following hazards

During 2016–2023, the CMC Hospital Network (Supplementary Fig. S1d) recorded a total of 421,586 mental and behavioural disorder hospitalizations. After applying data quality exclusions (see Methods), 392,924 hospitalizations (93.21%) were retained for analysis (Table 1), most of which were schizophrenia and delusion (24.33%), stress-related disorders (16.53%), mood disorders (14.91%), mental retardation (13.86%), and psychoactive substance use (9.44%). Individuals aged 45–64 years accounted for 36.67% of admissions, and 69.36% were admitted through EDs. Of the total included hospitalizations, 262,069 (66.70%) were first admissions from unique individuals and 130,855 (33.30%) were repeat admissions. The proportion of repeat admissions varied across diagnostic categories, being highest for mental retardation (52.99%) and schizophrenia and delusion (50.72%), and lowest for substance-related disorders (11.73%) (Supplementary Table S4).

Socioeconomic disparity and health inequity

We observed substantial disparities in hazard-related hospitalization risks between socioeconomically developed counties and less developed counties. Developed counties showed 16.18–24.96% lower cumulative excess risks across all hazard types than less developed counties. These disparities persisted across all post–hazard windows (e.g., storms: 1–2 weeks: developed: 5.54% (3.46, 7.61), less developed: 6.54% (4.93, 8.15); floods: 3–4 weeks: developed: 1.79% (1.33, 2.26), less developed: 2.36% (2.04, 2.69); Table 4).

Hospitalization burdens and demographic drivers

Extrapolating hospitalization excess relative risk estimates from the CMC Hospital Network to all hazard-affected counties, we estimated that storms, floods, and tropical cyclones collectively caused approximately 117,717 (93,667, 147,158) excess mental and behavioural hospitalizations annually (Fig. 1a). Notably, although individuals aged ≤19 and ≥ 65 years represented 35.58% of at-risk populations (residents of counties that experienced at least one hazard during 2016–2023), they bore a disproportionate 41.05% of the total hazard-attributable disease burden, with 2.99–6.19 and 9.86–23.30 additional hospitalizations per 100,000 population, respectively (Fig. 1b). Floods accounted for the largest share of burden (66.12%) (Supplementary Fig. S1b; Table 2).

Additionally, counterfactual decomposition analysis (Supplementary Text 6) showed that population aging and growth collectively explained 1.20–5.81% of excess hospitalizations across all hazard types (Fig. 1c). Specifically, for tropical cyclones, population aging contributed 3.05% of excess hospitalizations and population growth 2.76%. Counties with frequent tropical cyclone exposure were mainly located in eastern coastal regions, which showed faster population aging and higher migration-related population growth than inland regions (Supplementary Figs. S1c and S9). For floods and storms, the corresponding contributions were 2.11% and 1.03% for aging, and 0.69% and 0.17% for population growth. These flood- and storm-affected areas were predominantly in central and south-central inland China, where population growth rates were lower than in coastal regions (Supplementary Fig. S1a and b, and S9).

Adaptation capacity mobilised from historical events

Hazard frequency-stratified analyses showed that counties with more frequent historical hazard exposure may develop experience-related adaptation capacity, with relatively lower excess hospitalization risks than those with infrequent exposure. Specifically, among less developed counties, low-frequency counties (mean hazard frequency: storms: 0.24/year; tropical cyclones: 0.28/year) exhibited 11.30% and 14.29% higher excess risks following storms and tropical cyclones, respectively, compared with high-frequency counties (storms: 0.59/year; tropical cyclones: 1.57/year) (Table 4). In developed counties, differences widened, with cumulative excess relative risk disparities of 18.16% (storms) and 26.56% (tropical cyclones) between low- and high-frequency exposure groups.

However, our findings indicate that such experience-related adaptation capability was hazard-specific and may be less effective than structural adaptation conferred by socioeconomic development. For floods, no apparent adaptive advantage (i.e., reductions in risk) was observed from historical flood experience, with high-frequency counties (1.87/year) showing excess relative risks 12.19–17.71% higher than those in low-frequency counties (0.60/year). In contrast, socioeconomic development continued to provide a protective effect on health outcomes, with developed counties maintaining roughly 20% lower excess relative risks than less developed counties, regardless of exposure history.

Sensitivity analysis

Results remained consistent across multiple sensitivity analyses. First, DID models using only county-level random effects produced similar estimates (Supplementary Table S6). Second, exclusion of the pandemic period did not materially change hazard-attributable hospitalization patterns (Supplementary Table S7). Third, restricting analyses to first admissions only yielded DID estimates closely comparable to the primary analysis across all hazard types, disease categories, and post–hazard observation windows (Supplementary Table S8). Fourth, Monte Carlo simulations confirmed the stability of the socioeconomic development index under alternative weighting schemes (Supplementary Fig. S10).

Discussion

In this nationwide study, we found that storms, floods, and tropical cyclones in China were associated with clear and temporally extended increases in hospitalizations for specific mental and behavioural disorders, particularly schizophrenia and delusional disorders, mood disorders, stress-related disorders, and behavioural syndromes. These excess risks were not evenly distributed: children, adolescents, and older adults experienced the highest relative risks and accounted for a disproportionate share of the estimated burdens; socioeconomically less developed and inland counties consistently had higher hazard-attributable hospitalization risks than more developed coastal counties; and population aging and growth measurably amplified these burdens across hazard types. We also observed that counties with more frequent prior exposure to storms and tropical cyclones had relatively lower excess hospitalization risks during subsequent events, whereas no such adaptation pattern was evident for floods.

The differential hazard impacts and disease sensitivities observed in this study underscore the need for targeted adaptation interventions. Specifically, storms and tropical cyclones generated concentrated mental health demands that required swift and intensive healthcare responses, whereas floods produced more sustained elevations in excess hospitalization risks, highlighting the need for prolonged psychosocial support and healthcare engagement.6,50 Schizophrenia, mood disorders, stress-related conditions, and behavioural syndromes exhibited heightened sensitivity to climate hazards, with post–hazard impacts persisting for approximately two months. The observed disease-specific patterns may be explained by distinct vulnerability mechanisms. Schizophrenia and delusional disorders showed the highest excess risks, consistent with the neural diathesis-stress model in which acute psychosocial stressors trigger dopaminergic dysregulation and precipitate psychotic episodes in individuals with pre-existing neurobiological vulnerability.51 Mood disorders showed similarly elevated sensitivity, aligned with established evidence that the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis (i.e., the central biological mediator of depression and anxiety) is activated by both acute stressors and sustained adversity such as hazard-related displacement and financial loss.52 Stress-related disorders, which include post-traumatic stress disorder and acute stress reactions, are by definition responsive to environmental traumatic events,53 explaining their marked sensitivity. In contrast, organic mental disorders showed comparatively modest excess risks, likely because their predominantly neurodegenerative or cerebrovascular aetiology is less immediately responsive to psychosocial stressors.54 Substance-related disorders showed moderate increases, likely because progression from increased use to hospitalization-level severity typically requires longer timeframes than our 8-week observation windows,55 and acute intoxication presentations may be preferentially managed in general medical rather than psychiatric settings.

Our burden estimates showed that children, adolescents, and older adults experienced a disproportionate share of the burden from climate hazards. This disproportionate burden distribution reflects both their biological and social vulnerability as well as the baseline prevalence and disability weights associated with these conditions. Our estimates were consistent with previous findings,2,24 indicating that climate hazards may amplify pre-existing mental health inequities, disproportionately affecting individuals with lower adaptive capacity, limited social support, and barriers to accessing care.56,57

Furthermore, by combining DID estimates with counterfactual demographic scenarios, we quantified the contribution of population aging and growth to hazard-related mental health impacts. For tropical cyclones, the amplification effects of aging and population growth were particularly evident in rapidly aging and fast-growing coastal regions, where tropical cyclone exposure was frequent. For storms and floods, demographic amplification was more strongly driven by aging in central and south-central inland regions, where population growth was comparatively slower. These findings suggest that projections of future climate-related mental health burden, and the planning of service capacity in both coastal and inland areas, should explicitly incorporate local demographic trajectories.58 Notably, China's demographic transition is accelerating: the proportion of the population aged ≥65 years is projected to exceed 30% by 2050, while the total population has already begun declining.59,60 Given that older adults experienced the highest hazard-attributable hospitalization risk in our study, population aging alone could substantially amplify climate-mental health burdens in the coming decades, even if hazard frequency remained constant. Furthermore, continued rural-to-urban migration concentrates populations in coastal cities where tropical cyclone exposure is more frequent,61 potentially compounding aging-related vulnerability with increased hazard exposure. These demographic projections underscore the need for proactive investment in mental health services targeting rapidly aging and urbanizing regions.

Intriguingly, we observed “experience-associated” adaptation capability—that is, counties with more frequent historical climate hazards may exhibit lower health impacts during subsequent events. This observation aligned with previous observations in literature15,24 and was consistent with the social learning concept,62 by which communities adapt to emergency disruptions through formal mechanisms (e.g., improved infrastructure, hazard preparedness training) and informal processes (e.g., collective memory, behavioural adaptation).63 However, our results also indicated that such autonomous adaptation capacity might be hazard intensity dependent.2 Repeated exposures to severe hazards are likely associated with higher and prolonged mental health pressure. This phenomenon might be explained by destructive disruption exceeding the maximum adaptation limit.64 Thus, climate hazard-mental health adaptation is not guaranteed by autonomous adaptation alone. Instead, it requires sustained, proactive investment and multi-sectoral support to improve socioeconomic development and healthcare.

Importantly, our empirical evidence demonstrates that regional socioeconomic developments have profound population-level benefits for mitigating hazard-related mental health impacts. Socioeconomically advanced counties experienced attenuated excess risks, suggesting that robust social infrastructure, poverty reduction, and inclusive health services can buffer populations against the psychological toll of environmental shocks. These findings are consistent with policy frameworks that emphasize health equity and resilient communities, including relevant SDGs.65

Our findings also underscore the necessity for urgent climate change mitigation. The mental health burden estimated in this study was attributable to climate hazards, whose frequency and intensity are projected to increase under continued greenhouse gas emissions.66 Our observations suggest that adaptation alone, neither conferred from socioeconomic development nor experiential health benefits mobilised from historical hazard exposure, cannot eliminate the impacts of extreme weather disasters. Therefore, limiting global warming through rapid emission reductions remains a fundamental strategy for attenuating future climate-related mental health impacts, through which the avoided health burden from reduced hazard frequency and intensity constitutes a critical co-benefit of decarbonisation.67

Several limitations warrant attention. First, although the PSM-DID framework controls for measured baseline confounders and time-invariant unmeasured factors, it cannot fully eliminate confounding from time-varying unmeasured factors (e.g., concurrent local economic shocks), though our event-study analyses suggest such confounding is likely to have minor influence on effect estimates. Second, our DID framework focused on discrete hazard events and did not explicitly capture compound or cascading disasters. Third, data constraints in the EM-DAT database precluded stratification by hazard intensity, potentially obscuring dose–response relationships. Fourth, although cross-validation with Dartmouth Flood Observatory68 (DFO) and Geocoded Disaster Database69 (GDIS) supported that EM-DAT reliably captures the dominant spatial patterns of hazard exposure in China, the use of alternative hazard databases with different compilation methodologies or inclusion criteria may yield minor variations in hazard effect estimates. Fifth, our reliance on diagnosed hospitalizations likely underestimates the total burden, as individuals with subclinical or less severe conditions managed in outpatient settings, those with mental health comorbidities recorded under other primary diagnoses,70 and populations facing healthcare access barriers71 were not captured. Sixth, our sex variable was administratively recorded based on biological sex as a binary classification. Therefore we were unable to distinguish biological sex effects from gender-related social determinants (e.g., gendered caregiving roles, economic dependence, and differential healthcare-seeking behaviours). Seventh, although the CMC Hospital Network covers diverse climatic zones and socioeconomic contexts across China, it may underrepresent remote western and southwestern regions where ethnic minority populations are concentrated. Ethnicity data were unavailable in our dataset, precluding assessment of whether hazard-related mental health burdens differed across ethnic groups. Our county-level socioeconomic stratification may partially capture ethnicity-related structural inequities, as ethnic minority-concentrated regions tend to have lower socioeconomic development indexes. However, unmeasured confounders such as culturally shaped mental health literacy and language barriers in healthcare access may contribute to ethnic disparities not captured by our county-level stratification. Importantly, our finding that socioeconomically developed counties were associated with attenuated hazard-related mental health risks suggests that targeted investment in healthcare infrastructure and social services in underserved regions may promote mental health equity across China's diverse communities. Future studies integrating outpatient visits, psychotropic medication prescriptions, validated gender identity assessments, and individual-level sociodemographic and ethnicity data would better characterize the full spectrum of mental health impacts and enable more equitable identification of at-risk populations following climate hazards.

In summary, this nationwide study shows that storms, floods, and tropical cyclones in China were associated with substantial and temporally extended increases in hospitalizations for specific mental and behavioural disorders, with disproportionate impacts on children, adolescents, older adults, and residents of socioeconomically disadvantaged regions. Demographic change, regional socioeconomic development, and historical hazard exposure jointly shaped these impacts. These findings provide an empirical basis for incorporating mental health into climate-health policy and for prioritising resources towards highly exposed and structurally vulnerable populations as climate hazards intensify.
