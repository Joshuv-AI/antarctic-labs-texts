METTL3-mediated m6A modification of TNFAIP3 promotes endothelial to mesenchymal transition and atherosclerosis via the NF-κB/SNAIL signaling pathway
Authors: Jingquan Chen, Ruirui Yang, Qiang Tan, Kun Lai, Haifei Wang, Zhilong Chen, Sixuan Li, Jianghua Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEndothelial-mesenchymal transition (EndMT) is crucial in atherosclerosis (AS) development, but its molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood. TNFAIP3 is a crucial negative regulator that plays a central role in inflammatory responses and immune responses by inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway. This study aims to investigate the molecular mechanisms by which TNFAIP3 regulates EndMT and the progression of AS, as well as to elucidate the role of METTL3-mediated m6A modification in this process. Through both in vivo and in vitro assays, we analyzed the expression of TNFAIP3 in endothelial cells during AS and EndMT. In vitro, we inhibited TNFAIP3 expression in endothelial cells to observe its impact on EndMT. In vivo, we investigated the effects of endothelial cell-specific TNFAIP3 deficiency on EndMT and AS progression. Furthermore, we investigated whether this process is mediated through the NF-κB/Snail signaling pathway and observed the mitigating effect of inhibiting the NF-κB pathway on ox-LDL-induced EndMT. Finally, we studied the mechanism by which METTL3-mediated m6A modification regulates TNFAIP3 expression in endothelial cells. TNFAIP3 expression is downregulated in endothelial cells during AS and EndMT. Inhibition of TNFAIP3 expression in endothelial cells exacerbated EndMT induced by TGFβ or ox-LDL in vitro. Moreover, endothelial cell-specific deletion of TNFAIP3 increased the expression of mesenchymal markers and exacerbated AS progression. This process is mediated through the NF-κB/Snail signaling pathway. Suppression of the NF-κB pathway alleviated ox-LDL-induced EndMT. Additionally, the reduction in TNFAIP3 expression in endothelial cells is mediated by METTL3/YTHDF2-dependent m6A modification. In AS, the expression of METTL3 is upregulated in endothelial cells, accelerating the degradation of TNFAIP3 mRNA via YTHDF2-dependent m6A modification, thereby downregulating its expression levels. This process activates the NF-κB/Snail signaling pathway, which in turn promotes EndMT. This finding highlights the involvement of epigenetic mechanisms in AS, providing a theoretical basis and potential therapeutic targets for inhibiting EndMT and AS progression.Supplementary key words: TNFAIP3, NF-κB/Snail, METTL3, Endothelial to mesenchymal transition, Atherosclerosis

Atherosclerosis (AS), a chronic inflammatory disease, serves as one of the pathological foundations for CVDs. It is characterized by abnormal accumulation of lipids, fibrous tissue, and calcium within the walls of large and medium-sized arteries, ultimately forming plaques that restrict blood flow and lead to vascular obstruction (1, 2). Endothelial cells, which form a monolayer barrier on the inner wall of blood vessels, are the first to be affected by various risk factors such as hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, and smoking during the initiation and progression of AS. These factors cause structural and functional abnormalities, thereby regulating vasoconstriction, dilation, inflammation, and thrombosis (3, 4). Therefore, gaining a deep understanding of the specific roles and molecular mechanisms of endothelial cells in AS is of paramount importance for the prevention and treatment of this condition.

Endothelial to Mesenchymal Transition (EndMT) refers to the process in which endothelial cells gradually lose their unique characteristics and acquire mesenchymal cell properties under specific stimulatory factors, such as transforming growth factor β (TGF-β), oxidative stress, or acidic microenvironments (5, 6). Studies have found that in the context of AS, when subjected to local inflammatory factors, changes in mechanical stress, or high concentrations of lipids in the blood, endothelial cells can undergo EndMT, leading to the disruption of intercellular junctions, loss of cell polarity, and the onset of expression of mesenchymal markers such as α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and vimentin (7, 8, 9). These transformed cells not only migrate to deeper layers of the vessel wall but also participate in the formation of the fibrous cap and calcification processes of AS plaques, thereby affecting plaque stability and potentially increasing the risk of cardiovascular events. Nevertheless, the molecular mechanisms of EndMT in AS have not yet been fully elucidated.

Tumor necrosis factor alpha-induced protein 3 (TNFAIP3), also known as A20, is a critical regulatory protein widely expressed in various cell types (10, 11). TNFAIP3 serves as a potent negative feedback regulator that inhibits the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway through its deubiquitinating and E3 ubiquitin ligase activities, thereby participating in the regulation of inflammatory responses and immune responses in the body (12). For instance, in psoriasis, polymorphisms in the TNFAIP3 gene are closely associated with disease severity (13). Aberrant expression of TNFAIP3 has also been linked to the occurrence, development, and prognosis of multiple types of cancer (14, 15). Moreover, NF-κB signaling is known to play a key role in EndMT. Activation of NF-κB can induce the expression of a series of genes related to EndMT, such as the transcription factors Snail, Twist, and Slug, which collectively act to promote the transition of endothelial cells into mesenchymal cells (8, 16). However, in AS, the mechanism by which TNFAIP3 influences EndMT is not yet well understood.

As an important epigenetic modification, N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modifications are ubiquitous in messenger RNA (mRNA), long non-coding RNA (lncRNA), and some tRNA and rRNA of eukaryotes (17). It participates in regulating multiple aspects of RNA, including stability, splicing, transport, and translation, thereby playing a crucial role in cellular biological processes (18). Methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3) is a widely present RNA methyltransferase in eukaryotes, primarily responsible for catalyzing the formation of m6A modifications (18, 19). Recent studies have revealed that METTL3 participates in the regulation of AS progression (20, 21). Our previous research indicated that METTL3-mediated m6A modification contributes to phenotypic transition of vascular smooth muscle cells and may exacerbate AS progression through the miR-375-3p/PDK1 axis (21). However, its endothelial-specific roles in atherogenesis are less clear. Critically, although METTL3-mediated m6A modification of TNFAIP3 was recently reported to promote NLRP3 inflammasome activation in gingival fibroblasts during periodontitis by accelerating TNFAIP3 mRNA decay (22), its role in vascular endothelial biology is unknown. Notably, this prior study demonstrated TNFAIP3 degradation enhances NEK7 ubiquitination to drive inflammasome assembly in oral mucosa, establishing a disease-specific mechanism distinct from vascular pathologies. Herein, we investigate whether endothelial METTL3 regulates TNFAIP3 through m6A modification during atherosclerosis-related EndMT. Furthermore, studies have demonstrated that METTL3 directly or indirectly regulates the expression of genes related to EndMT by affecting the level of m6A modification on specific mRNAs. For instance, METTL3 can enhance the mRNA stability of transcription factors such as Snail and Twist. Therefore, it is meaningful to explore whether METTL3 regulates TNFAIP3 expression through m6A modification, thereby participating in the development of EndMT in AS, and to elucidate its potential molecular mechanisms.

In this study, we report that METTL3-mediated m6A modification plays a pivotal role in the process of lipid peroxidation-induced EndMT by regulating TNFAIP3 expression. During this process, METTL3 expression is elevated, and it accelerates the degradation of TNFAIP3 mRNA in endothelial cells through YTHDF2-dependent m6A modification, thereby reducing its expression. This subsequently activates the NF-κB/Snail signaling pathway, inducing EndMT and contributing to AS. Additionally, genetic knockout effectively reduced TNFAIP3 expression in endothelial cells, further exacerbating EndMT-induced atherosclerosis. This finding implicates epigenetic mechanisms in AS, offering potential therapeutic targets to suppress EndMT and AS progression.

Materials and methods

Animal models

Eight-week-old Apoe−/− mice were purchased from the Model Animal Research Center of Nanjing University. The TNFAIP3 knockout (TNFAIP3fl/fl) mice and endothelial cell-specific (Cdh5Cre) mice on a C57BL/6J background were obtained from the Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. TNFAIP3fl/fl mice were crossed with Cdh5Cre mice to obtain TNFAIP3fl/flCdh5Cre mice. Subsequently, TNFAIP3fl/flCdh5Cre mice were crossed with Apoe−/− mice to generate TNFAIP3fl/flCdh5CreApoe−/− mice. Control mice were TNFAIP3fl/flApoe−/− mice obtained by crossing TNFAIP3fl/fl mice with Apoe−/− mice.

TNFAIP3fl/flCdh5Cre Apoe−/− male mice (n = 6) and TNFAIP3fl/fl Apoe−/− male (n = 6) were housed in a specific-pathogen-free (SPF) environment with a maintained temperature of 22–25°C, and a 12-h light/dark cycle. Mice were fed either a normal diet (ND) or a high-fat diet (HFD) ad libitum. Eight-week-old mice were fed under these conditions for 14 weeks to establish control groups and atherosclerosis models. All experimental protocols involving mice were approved by the Theoretical Committee of the Institute of Animal Science of Affiliated Hospital of North Sichuan Medical College, adhering to the guidelines outlined in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Histological analysis of atherosclerotic lesions

The aortas were dissected from the mice. Specimens were then embedded in OCT compound and sectioned. To evaluate collagen content and lipid accumulation in the tissue, we employed various staining methods: hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining (Beyotime, C0105M), Sirius red staining (Phygene, PH1099), Masson's trichrome staining (Leagene, DC0032), and Oil Red O lipid staining (Sigma, 102419). For the Oil Red O staining procedure, frozen sections were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, washed with distilled water for 10 min, and stained following the manufacturer’s instructions. HE, Sirius red, and Masson's trichrome staining were performed according to the manufacturers' protocols. After staining the tissue sections, microscopic images were captured using the bright-field mode of an OLYMPUS BX53 microscope (Olympus Corporation) at magnifications of 200× and 400× to evaluate the histological characteristics of atherosclerotic lesions in the aortic root.

Blood lipid assay

Blood lipid levels were assessed based on the instructions provided with the kits for total cholesterol (CHO, A111-1-1), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL, A113-1-1), triglycerides (TG, A110-1-1), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL, A112-1-1), all of which were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute.

Cell culture and treatment

Human coronary artery endothelial cells (HCAECs) were obtained from ScienCell and maintained in Endothelial Cell Medium enriched with 5% fetal bovine serum, 1% endothelial cell growth supplement, and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin Solution (ScienCell, 1001). To model atherosclerotic endothelial injury, HCAECs were exposed to human oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL, Solarbio, IO1300), at a concentration of 50 μmg/ml for 24 h. EndMT was induced in vitro by treating HCAECs with 10 ng/ml of Transforming Growth Factor β2 (TGFβ2, Gibco, 100-35B) in the culture medium for 7 days, with the medium being refreshed daily. Cells treated with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) served as the control group.

In separate experiments, HCAECs were treated with a CTL peptide (25 μM, BML-P608-0500, Enzo), an NBD peptide (25 μM, ALX-163-011-1, Enzo), and ox-LDL (50 mg/ml) for 24 h.

Crystal violet assay

HCAECs were seeded at a density of 18,000 cells per well into 24-well plates. Following treatment, cells were fixed using cold 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 10 min and subsequently stained with a 0.5% crystal violet solution (Sigma, C3886) for 10 min. After staining, cells were rinsed with tap water to remove excess dye. Images were captured using an inverted microscope (Leica DMi8).

Cell proliferation assay

HCAECs were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 3,000 cells per well. Following treatment, cell proliferation was measured using a BrdU Cell Proliferation Kit (Roche, 11647229001) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. The cells were exposed to BrdU for 10 h, after which the absorbance was read at 370 nm using a SpectraMax M5 microplate reader.

Tubule formation assay

The treated HCAECs were trypsinized, counted, and seeded onto 96-well plates coated with growth factor-deficient Matrigel (CB-40230A, Fisher Scientific) at a density of 6,000 cells per well. The cells were then placed back into the incubator for 4 h. Images were captured using a phase-contrast microscope (Leica DMi8), and the number of tubule branching points was quantitatively analyzed.

Immunofluorescent staining

For tissues, frozen sections were allowed to thaw at room temperature and subsequently rinsed with PBS to eliminate OCT compound. For HCAECs, treated cells are fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized using Triton X-100. The samples are then blocked using a solution of 10% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS. Subsequently, the samples were then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies diluted in 3% BSA in PBS. After incubation, the slides are washed three times with PBS and incubated with the appropriate secondary antibodies for one hour at room temperature. They are washed three times with PBS again, counterstained with DAPI solution, mounted, and visualized using a confocal microscope (Oberkoche). The antibodies used in this study are listed in supplemental Table S1. Fluorescence intensity and double-positive cell quantification were analyzed using ImageJ software (NIH). Images were processed under identical threshold settings, and the mean fluorescence intensity was measured within defined regions of interest. For co-localization analysis, overlapping signals from different channels were identified, and the proportion of double-positive cells was calculated relative to total DAPI-positive nuclei.

siRNA transfection

GenePharma designed the small interfering RNAs (siRNAs). Transfections of these siRNAs were carried out utilizing Lipofectamine RNAiMAX reagent (Invitrogen, 13778100), following the manufacturer's guidelines. Following various treatments, cells were harvested at specified time points for subsequent experimental analyses. The siRNA sequences used in this study are as follows: siTNFAIP3, 5′-AGACACACGCAACTTTAAA-3′; sip65, 5′-GCCTTAATAGTAGGGTAAGTT-3′; siMETTL3, 5′-GCTGCACTTCAGACGAATTAT-3′; siYTHDF2, 5′-TCTGGATATAGTAGCAATTAT-3′.

Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation-PCR (MeRIP-PCR) assay

The EpiQuik CUT&RUN m6A RNA Enrichment Kit (Epigentek, P-9018-24) will be used, and the manufacturer's instructions will be strictly followed. HCAECs will be collected and lysed using a specific lysis buffer. The m6A antibodies will be conjugated to coated magnetic beads, and the cell lysate will be incubated with this antibody-bead complex under rotation at room temperature for 2 h. After the incubation period, proteinase K buffer will be used to digest proteins in the supernatant. The RNA bound to the antibody-coated beads will then be isolated through a phenol-chloroform-isoamyl alcohol extraction procedure. Finally, the isolated RNA samples will undergo analysis via RT-qPCR.

Luciferase reporter assay

The wild-type and mutant dual-luciferase reporter gene plasmids were constructed by Promega. The adenine in the highest m6A modification score site of TNFAIP3 (i.e., GGACU) was mutated to cytosine to create the Mut-TNFAIP3 fusion gene reporter plasmid. HCAECs subjected to various treatments were transfected with either the WT-TNFAIP3 or Mut-TNFAIP3 fusion gene reporter plasmids in 6-well plates. After 48 h, luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

TUNEL assay

After the treatment of HCAECs, the cells were fixed with pre-chilled 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 10 min. Subsequently, the In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit (Sigma, 12156792910) was used according to the manufacturer's instructions. Visualization was performed using a confocal microscope (Oberkoche).

RT-qPCR

Extraction of total RNA from mouse aortic tissue and HCAECs was accomplished using TRIzol reagent, followed by reverse transcription employing the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, RR047A). Real-time PCR assays were conducted using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ (TaKaRa, RR420A). The relative expression levels of mRNA were quantified via the 2ˆ-ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH serving as the internal reference for normalization. The specific primer sequences utilized are listed in supplemental Table S2.

mRNA stability assay

The treated HCAECs were plated in 6-well plates and exposed to Actinomycin D (5 μg/ml, Sigma, 114666) for various durations. RNA extraction was then performed using TRIzol reagent (Life Technologies), followed by RT-qPCR analysis.

Western blot assay

Aortic tissue and HCAECs were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, P0013B) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were quantified, and each sample was resolved on SDS-PAGE gel before being transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. The membranes were blocked for 2 h using 5% non-fat milk in PBS, followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. Following incubation with the appropriate secondary antibodies, the blots were visualized and analyzed using the Odyssey Infrared Imaging System (LI-COR). The antibodies used in this study are listed in supplemental Table S1.

GEO datasets

We obtained three transcriptomic datasets, GSE224939, GSE236730, and GSE118446, from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gds). The GSE224939 dataset profiled human umbilical artery endothelial cells (HUAECs) subjected to normal culture conditions, TGF-β stimulation, or Acss2 knockdown. The GSE236730 dataset included RNA-seq data from primary human endothelial cells, including HUVECs, HAECs, and HPMVECs, cultured under control conditions or stimulated with TGF-β1 and IL-1β for four days, with or without acetate treatment. The GSE118446 dataset investigated transcriptional changes in HUVECs and HPAECs exposed to TGF-β2, IL-1β, or their combination for seven days. Differential expression analysis was performed for each dataset using the GEO2R tool (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/geo2r), applying thresholds of |log2 fold change| > 1 and adjusted P-value < 0.05. Genes consistently differentially expressed across the three datasets were selected for further analysis. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using KOBAS 3.0 (http://kobas.cbi.pku.edu.cn/) to identify significantly enriched signaling pathways. False discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism software was employed for statistical analysis. All results are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean and are based on data from at least three independent experiments. The normality of all data was verified using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For comparisons between two groups with normally distributed data, a two-tailed Student's t test was employed. In cases involving three or more groups, statistical significance was determined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Statistical significance was set at a P-value < 0.05.

Results

In the context of AS and EndMT, the expression of TNFAIP3 in endothelial cells is significantly downregulated

To further validate the role of EndMT in atherosclerosis pathogenesis, we analyzed three independent human endothelial cell transcriptomic datasets modeling EndMT in vitro. The GSE224939 dataset comprised RNA-seq profiles of human umbilical artery endothelial cells (HUAECs) subjected to TGF-β stimulation or Acss2 knockdown, the GSE236730 dataset included RNA-seq data from primary endothelial cells (HUVECs, HAECs, and HPMVECs) treated with TGF-β1 and IL-1β in the presence or absence of acetate, and the GSE118446 dataset profiled HUVECs and HPAECs exposed to TGF-β2 and/or IL-1β for seven days. Differential expression analysis was performed using GEO2R with thresholds of |log2FC| > 1 and adjusted P-value < 0.05. To identify shared transcriptional signatures, we focused on common differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across these datasets and subjected them to KEGG pathway enrichment analysis using KOBAS 3.0. The analysis revealed significant enrichment of the TNF signaling pathway (FDR < 0.05) across all datasets, a pathway that is functionally linked to the regulation of TNFAIP3. Notably, TNFAIP3 was consistently downregulated under EndMT-inducing conditions (Fig. S1A–C). Furthermore, previous studies have reported that the expression of TNFAIP3 is downregulated in endothelial cells under ox-LDL stimulation (23). Consequently, we hypothesized that TNFAIP3 might be involved in the EndMT process in AS (Fig. 1A). Next, to further explore the role of TNFAIP3 in AS and EndMT, we first examined TNFAIP3 expression levels in the arterial tissues of Apoe−/− mice fed a high-fat diet (HFD) (n = 6, all male mice per group). The results showed that TNFAIP3 expression was downregulated in the arterial tissues of Apoe−/− mice fed a HFD compared to controls (Fig. 1B, C). Next, we performed immunofluorescence staining using specific antibodies against TNFAIP3 and the endothelial cell marker CD31 (n = 6, slides were randomized and quantified by investigators blinded to group assignment). We observed that TNFAIP3 expression was reduced in the endothelial cells of the arteries of AS mice compared to normal mice (Fig. 1D). In vitro, we induced mesenchymal transition in Human Coronary Artery Endothelial Cells (HCAECs) using transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) and established an in vitro atherosclerosis model by treating the cells with oxidized Low-Density Lipoprotein (ox-LDL) (n = 3). The results show that both TGF-β and ox-LDL stimulation lead to a decrease in TNFAIP3 expression in endothelial cells (Fig. 1E, G). These data suggest that the downregulation of TNFAIP3 is associated with EndMT and may contribute to the progression of AS through this process.

Knockdown of TNFAIP3 significantly enhances EndMT of HCAECs in AS

To further confirm the role of TNFAIP3 in AS and EndMT, we silenced TNFAIP3 expression in HCAECs using small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting TNFAIP3 (supplemental Fig. S2A, B). We found that in TGFβ-induced EndMT (n = 3), the expression of endothelial cell markers ZO-2, ICAM2, and CD31 was significantly decreased, while the expression of mesenchymal markers N-cadherin, α-SMA, and Vimentin increased. These effects were further enhanced upon silencing of TNFAIP3 (Fig. 2A, B). Similar trends were observed in the expression of endothelial and mesenchymal markers in HCAECs induced by ox-LDL (Fig. 2A, B). In the rescue experiment, overexpression of TNFAIP3 reversed the downregulation of endothelial markers such as CD31 and the upregulation of the mesenchymal marker N-Cadherin induced by its knockdown, thereby suppressing the phenotypic features of EndMT (supplemental Fig. S2C). This indicates that TNFAIP3 regulates the EndMT process in AS. Next, we investigated its impact on endothelial cell phenotype. The results of crystal violet staining and BrdU cell proliferation assays showed that cell proliferation decreased under TGFβ or ox - LDL stimulation. Similarly, silencing TNFAIP3 further inhibited cell proliferation (Fig. 2C, D). Although we did not observe differences in endothelial cell apoptosis (supplemental Fig. S2D). Tubule formation is a characteristic of endothelial cells that decreases during EndMT (24). We found that consistent with the trend of mesenchymal transition, the tubule formation capacity of HCAECs was reduced after induction by TGFβ or ox-LDL, and this effect was further promoted by the silencing of TNFAIP3 (Fig. 2E). These results suggest that silencing TNFAIP3 promotes the transformation of endothelial cells into a mesenchymal phenotype, thereby contributing to the progression of AS.

Endothelial cell-specific TNFAIP3 knockout exacerbates EndMT and AS

Given that the aforementioned in vitro data showed that silencing TNFAIP3 enhances EndMT, we next investigated whether TNFAIP3 regulates EndMT in vivo and influences the progression of AS. Firstly, we crossed TNFAIP3fl/fl mice with Cdh5Cre mice to obtain endothelial cell-specific TNFAIP3 knockout (TNFAIP3fl/flCdh5Cre) mice, using TNFAIP3fl/fl mice as control mice. We further crossed these mice with Apoe−/− mice to obtain TNFAIP3fl/flCdh5CreApoe−/− mice and TNFAIP3fl/flApoe−/− mice. In TNFAIP3fl/flApoe−/− mice, we clearly identified TNFAIP3 expression in endothelial cells, whereas almost no TNFAIP3 expression was observed in TNFAIP3fl/flCdh5CreApoe−/− mice (supplemental Fig. S3A). After HFD feeding (n = 6, all male mice per group), there were no significant differences in body weight or lipid levels between TNFAIP3fl/flApoe−/− and TNFAIP3fl/flCdh5CreApoe−/− mice (supplemental Fig. S3B, C). However, we found that the expression of N-cadherin, α-SMA, and Vimentin was increased in the endothelial cells of TNFAIP3fl/flCdh5CreApoe−/− mice (Slides were randomized and quantified by investigators blinded to group assignment) (supplemental Fig. S3D–F). Furthermore, immunofluorescence analysis revealed a marked enhancement in the co-localization of N-cadherin, α-SMA and Vimentin with the endothelial marker CD31, as indicated by increased fluorescence intensity in double-positive cells (Fig. 3A–C). This indicates that the absence of TNFAIP3 in endothelial cells exacerbates EndMT. By Oil Red O staining, we observed an increased severity of atherosclerosis throughout the aorta in TNFAIP3fl/flCdh5CreApoe−/− mice (Fig. 3D). Additionally, there was a significant increase in lesion area and lipid burden and a decrease in collagen content in the aortas of TNFAIP3fl/flCdh5CreApoe−/− mice (Fig. 3E–G). These results indicate that endothelial cell-specific deletion of TNFAIP3 promotes the transformation of endothelial cells into a mesenchymal phenotype, thereby aggravating AS.

Inhibition of TNFAIP3 exacerbates ox-LDL-induced endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT) by activating the NF-κB/Snail signaling pathway

Studies have shown that TNFAIP3 acts as a negative regulator of NF-κB, and the abnormal activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway is closely related to the occurrence of EndMT (8, 12). Therefore, we further examined changes in NF-κB signaling in AS and ox-LDL-induced HCAECs, which were reflected by the phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of p65, degradation of IkBα, and expression of downstream products such as VCAM-1. In vivo experiments revealed a significant increase in the phosphorylation of p65 and the expression of VCAM-1, along with accelerated degradation of IκBα and upregulated expression of the key EndMT transcription factor Snail in the arterial tissues of HFD-fed mice (n = 6, all male mice per group) (Fig. 4A). This phenomenon was also observed in HCAECs induced by ox-LDL (n = 3) (Fig. 4B). Additionally, we observed an increase in nuclear localization of p65 following ox-LDL treatment (Fig. 4C, D). This suggests that TNFAIP3 may regulate the development of EndMT in AS through the NF-κB/Snail signaling pathway. To further validate our hypothesis, we knocked down the expression of p65 (a core molecule in the NF-κB signaling pathway) in HCAECs using p65 siRNA (supplemental Fig. S4B) or inhibited NF-κB activation using the NF-κB-specific inhibitor NBD peptide. We found that the elevated expression of p65 and Snail in HCAECs induced by TNFAIP3 silencing under ox-LDL stimulation was attenuated by p65 siRNA or NBD peptide (Fig. 4E, supplemental Fig. S4C). Subsequently, we examined the expression levels of endothelial and mesenchymal markers in the cells. The results showed a decrease in the expression levels of ZO-2, ICAM2, and CD31, along with an increase in the expression levels of N-cadherin, α-SMA, and Vimentin in HCAECs following ox-LDL treatment, which were rescued by p65 siRNA or NBD peptide (Fig. 4F, supplemental Fig. S4D). Additionally, we explored their effects on the endothelial cell phenotype. We found that both sip65 and NBD peptide treatments reduced the ox-LDL-induced decrease in cell density and cell proliferation (Fig. 4G, H, supplemental Fig. S4E, F). sip65 and NBD peptide treatments also partially rescued the ox-LDL-induced decline in the tube formation ability of endothelial cells (Fig. 4I, supplemental Fig S4G). These data indicate that the reduced expression of TNFAIP3 in AS promotes the occurrence and development of EndMT by activating the NF-κB/Snail signaling pathway.

METTL3 downregulates TNFAIP3 expression through m6A modification

Studies have reported that METTL3 plays a crucial role in the development of AS. Furthermore, METTL3-mediated m6A modification regulates the expression of downstream molecular mRNAs (18, 19). Therefore, to investigate whether METTL3 regulates TNFAIP3 expression levels in AS, we first predicted m6A modification sites on TNFAIP3 using the SRAMP website. The results showed the presence of multiple m6A modification sites on TNFAIP3 (Fig. 5A, supplemental Fig. S5A, B). Next, we examined the expression levels of METTL3 in mouse arterial tissues. Compared with Apoe−/− mice fed a ND the expression level of METTL3 was significantly elevated in the arterial tissues of mice fed a HFD (n = 6, all male mice per group) (Fig. 5B, C). Notably, the expression of METTL3 in endothelial cells of aortic tissues from HFD-fed mice also showed an upregulation trend (n = 6, slides were randomized and quantified by investigators blinded to group assignment) (Fig. 5D). In ox-LDL-induced HCAECs, the expression of METTL3 also increased (n = 3) (Fig. 5E, F). This suggests that in AS, the increased expression of METTL3 in endothelial cells may downregulate the expression of TNFAIP3. To verify this, we reduced the expression of METTL3 in HCAECs using siRNA (supplemental Fig. S5C, D). The results showed that silencing METTL3 rescued the ox-LDL-induced decrease in TNFAIP3 expression (Fig. 5G). Next, to clarify whether METTL3 regulates TNFAIP3 expression through m6A modification, we performed MeRIP-qPCR experiments. We found that silencing METTL3 decreased the m6A enrichment level of TNFAIP3 mRNA (Fig. 5H). Subsequently, we mutated adenine to cytosine in the recognition sequence (i.e., GGACU) of the site with the highest m6A modification prediction score for TNFAIP3 (supplemental Fig. S5A, B) to construct a mutant TNFAIP3 (Mut-TNFAIP3) dual-luciferase reporter plasmid, thereby reducing the m6A modification level of TNFAIP3. The results showed that silencing METTL3 increased the luciferase activity of the wild-type TNFAIP3 (WT-TNFAIP3) reporter gene but had no effect on the luciferase activity of Mut-TNFAIP3 (Fig. 5I). Moreover, METTL3 silencing slowed the degradation rate of TNFAIP3 mRNA (Fig. 5J). In other words, in AS, the elevated expression of METTL3 in endothelial cells accelerates the degradation of TNFAIP3 mRNA through m6A modification, thereby downregulating TNFAIP3 expression and affecting the progression of EndMT.

METTL3 reduces TNFAIP3 mRNA stability in a YTHDF2-dependent manner

Numerous studies have reported that YTHDF2, as a "Reader" molecule in m6A modification, promotes the degradation of target mRNAs by recognizing m6A modification sites (18, 25). Therefore, we investigated whether YTHDF2 is involved in regulating METTL3-mediated TNFAIP3 mRNA stability. We observed that the expression of YTHDF2 was upregulated in the arterial tissues of Apoe−/− mice fed a HFD (n = 6, all male mice per group) (Fig. 6A). Similarly, the expression levels of YTHDF2 were significantly increased in ox-LDL-induced HCAECs (n = 3) (Fig. 6B). Next, we silenced the expression of YTHDF2 in HCAECs (Fig. 6C, D) and investigated its effect on TNFAIP3 expression. The results showed a significant increase in TNFAIP3 mRNA expression levels in HCAECs after silencing YTHDF2 (Fig. 6E). RIP-qPCR verified the interaction between the YTHDF2 protein and TNFAIP3 mRNA (Fig. 6F). Silencing YTHDF2 also enhanced the luciferase activity of the WT-TNFAIP3 reporter gene but did not affect the Mut-TNFAIP3 luciferase activity (Fig. 6G). Actinomycin D assay results also demonstrated that silencing YTHDF2 slowed down the degradation of TNFAIP3 mRNA (Fig. 6H). This indicates that in AS, YTHDF2-dependent m6A modification participates in the regulation of TNFAIP3 mRNA degradation, thereby downregulating its expression levels. In summary, our results suggest that elevated levels of METTL3 in endothelial cells accelerate the degradation of TNFAIP3 mRNA through YTHDF2-dependent m6A modification, leading to downregulation of TNFAIP3 expression. This further activates the NF-kB/Snail signaling pathway, promoting EndMT and thus exacerbating AS (Fig. 6I).

Discussion

In the field of cardiovascular disease research, EndMT has emerged as a novel research focus, gradually unveiling its pivotal role in the development of the cardiovascular system and the pathogenesis of related diseases. EndMT is a cellular biological process that can be triggered by various factors. When these stimulate factors are present, endothelial cells may undergo EndMT, leading them to detach from the vessel wall and transform into cells with characteristics of fibroblasts or myofibroblasts (6, 26). Numerous studies have demonstrated that EndMT plays a critical role in a multitude of cardiovascular diseases, including atherosclerosis, pulmonary hypertension, and heart valve diseases (7, 27, 28). Particularly in AS, endothelial cells within the arterial wall acquire a more active state through EndMT, enabling them to secrete greater amounts of extracellular matrix proteins and other pro-inflammatory mediators, which contribute to plaque formation and development (29). Dong Y et al. confirmed that epsins increase TGF-β signaling by promoting the internalization and degradation of fibroblast growth factor receptor-1, thereby enhancing EndMT during the development of atherosclerosis (30). Cai Y et al. were the first to discover that in AS, endothelial adenosine receptor 2A (ADORA2A) upregulates ALK5 expression via the cAMP/PKA/CREB axis, leading to activation of the TGFβ-Smad2/3 signaling pathway, thus promoting EndMT (31). Thus, the crucial role of EndMT in the progression of AS is evident.

During the process of EndMT, TGF-β is recognized as the primary inducer (32). By binding to TGF-β receptors on the cell surface, they activate Smad proteins, which then enter the nucleus to regulate the expression of target genes (33). Additionally, multiple intracellular signaling pathways, including Wnt, Notch, and NF-κB, are also involved (26, 33). NF-κB is typically bound to inhibitory protein IκB in an inactive state and resides in the cytoplasm. Upon exposure to cytokines such as TNF-α, thrombin, or physical stimulate factor, IκB is degraded, allowing NF-κB to be released and enter the nucleus, where it activates a series of target genes related to EndMT (8, 26). These genes include but are not limited to TWIST1, SNAIL, and CDH2, which collectively regulate cell migration and acquisition of a mesenchymal phenotype. Notably, NF-κB activation is not confined to the classic inflammatory pathway. During EndMT, NF-κB can also be activated through non-canonical pathways that may involve different signaling molecules and receptors, such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and other NF-κB activators (5, 34). This complex interplay of signals makes the regulatory mechanisms of NF-κB in EndMT particularly intricate and diverse. TNFAIP3, as a negative regulator, specifically targets IκB kinase in the NF-κB signaling pathway, modifying it through deubiquitination to prevent auto-phosphorylation and activation of IκB kinase, thereby inhibiting the activation of NF-κB (12). However, little is known about the regulatory mechanisms of TNFAIP3 in AS. Our studies indicate that in mouse models of atherosclerosis, the expression of TNFAIP3 in endothelial cells is downregulated. In vitro experiments have demonstrated that both the induction of EndMT with TGFβ and the stimulation of HCAECs with ox-LDL result in reduced expression of TNFAIP3 in endothelial cells. When TNFAIP3 is silenced, endothelial cells exhibit obvious EndMT characteristics. More importantly, this phenomenon has been confirmed in vivo. Mice with endothelial cell-specific TNFAIP3 deficiency display heightened mesenchymal phenotypes and severe atherosclerotic lesions, accompanied by larger plaque areas. However, both siRNA and gene knockout models may still have a certain degree of off-target effects. Therefore, future studies should combine shRNA-based stable knockdown systems, CRISPR-mediated gene knockout, and complementary rescue experiments to further confirm the role of TNFAIP3 in atherosclerosis.

During the EndMT process, multiple phenotypic changes are regulated at the DNA level by transcription factors. The Snail family of transcription factors (mainly comprising Snail1 and Slug/Snail2) is one of the core molecules regulating this process. These proteins can directly bind to the E-box sequence in the promoter regions of target genes, inhibiting the expression of epithelial markers such as CD31 while activating the expression of mesenchymal markers such as N-cadherin and vimentin (8, 33, 35). Additionally, Snail can increase the expression of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), promoting extracellular matrix degradation and further supporting cell migration (36, 37). Studies have shown that activated NF-κB can increase the stability of Snail mRNA and elevate its protein levels by enhancing the transcriptional activity of the Snail gene (38). Moreover, Lee JH et al. found that TNFAIP3 promotes TGF-β1-induced epithelial-mesenchymal transition in breast cancer cells through multi-monoubiquitylation of Snail1 (39). Therefore, it is reasonable to believe that NF-κB and Snail play significant roles in TNFAIP3-regulated EndMT. Our study shows that in AS, endothelial cell NF-κB signaling pathways and Snail expression are elevated. The NF-κB family primarily consists of five proteins, p65 (RelA), RelB, c-Rel, p50, and p52. These proteins can form homodimers or heterodimers and regulate target gene expression by binding to specific DNA sequences. Among them, the p65 subunit is one of the most active components of the NF-κB signaling pathway (40, 41). The NBD peptide (NF-κB Blocking Peptide) is a small molecule peptide that specifically inhibits the NF-κB signaling pathway by blocking the nuclear translocation of the p65 subunit, thereby preventing NF-κB activation (8). This study found that under conditions of TNFAIP3 silencing, the expression of Snail was significantly upregulated, and this upregulation could be attenuated by silencing p65 or inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway based on the NBD peptide. Furthermore, both sip65 treatment and NBD peptide administration significantly rescue the expression changes of EndMT markers induced by ox-LDL, while also preserving the basic functions of endothelial cells, including maintaining angiogenic potential.

Epigenetics refers to a series of molecular mechanisms that influence the traits and functions of organisms by regulating gene expression without altering the DNA sequence. These regulatory methods include DNA methylation, RNA methylation, histone modifications and the role of non-coding RNAs (42). The m6A modification is regulated by a specific set of "writer," "eraser," and "reader" proteins, which are responsible for catalyzing the addition of m6A, removing it, and recognizing it, respectively. METTL3 serves as a core catalytic subunit in the m6A modification process, forming a methyltransferase complex with other proteins like METTL14 to catalyze the transfer of a methyl group from S-adenosylmethionine to the N6 position of adenosine in RNA, resulting in m6A modification (43, 44). Our previous studies have already confirmed the crucial role of METTL3 in the initiation and progression of AS by modulating the phenotype of vascular smooth muscle cells and destabilizing AS plaques (21). Consistent with this, we have also observed high expression of METTL3 in endothelial cells. Studies have shown that in periodontitis, METTL3-mediated m6A modification targets TNFAIP3 transcripts and accelerates their degradation, leading to NLRP3 inflammasome activation and increased pyroptosis (22). While its role in vascular endothelial dysfunction—specifically in atherosclerosis-related EndMT—remains unexplored. Crucially, unlike prior models focused on immune cells or tumor microenvironments, endothelial-specific METTL3/TNFAIP3 dysregulation may drive plaque instability through unique mechanisms involving mesenchymal transition. In our study, elevated expression of METTL3 in endothelial cells accelerates the degradation of TNFAIP3 mRNA through m6A modification. However, this process depends on the recognition by "reader" proteins, including YTH domain-containing proteins and others such as IGF2BP family proteins and heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoproteins (HNRNPs). Among them, YTHDF1 and YTHDF3 can recognize and bind to m6A-modified mRNAs, promoting their translation in the cytoplasm, while YTHDF2 binds to m6A and promotes mRNA degradation (18, 43). We found that YTHDF2, as an m6A modification "reader" molecule, plays a crucial role in regulating METTL3-mediated TNFAIP3 mRNA stability. Therefore, we believe that the METTL3/TNFAIP3/NF-κB/Snail axis affects the progression of atherosclerosis by regulating endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition. However, it is important to note that the expression regulation of TNFAIP3 exhibits a complex, multilayered network characteristic, where the interactions among post-transcriptional modification mechanisms warrant in-depth exploration. Current studies have shown that inflammation-related microRNAs can form RNA-induced silencing complexes (RISCs) by directly binding to conserved sites within the 3′UTR region of TNFAIP3 mRNA, thereby inhibiting its translation or triggering degradation (45). This regulatory mode displays dynamic characteristics that enable rapid responses to inflammatory signals. For instance, in LPS-stimulated macrophages, the upregulation of miR-155 expression shows a significant negative correlation with the decrease in TNFAIP3 protein levels (46). Meanwhile, RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) can regulate mRNA stability and translation by binding to AU-rich elements (AREs) (47). As one type of post-transcriptional modification, m6A modification may interact with the aforementioned mechanisms in multiple ways. Future studies should systematically evaluate whether there are functional synergies or antagonisms between m6A modification and other post-transcriptional modifications, as well as how these mechanisms are integrated into key signaling pathways such as NF-κB.

Additionally, the TGF-β/Smad pathway, as one of the core regulatory pathways for EndMT, has been extensively demonstrated to promote the transformation of endothelial cells into mesenchymal cells by inducing the expression of transcription factors such as Snail and Slug (48). METTL3, as an m6A methyltransferase, may, at the epigenetic level, affect TGF-β signaling by modifying the mRNA stability of TGF-β receptors or downstream signaling molecules. This modification may enhance the activity of the TGF-β pathway, thereby exacerbating the EndMT process (49). Meanwhile, as a negative regulator of NF-κB, the silencing of TNFAIP3 may lead to persistent activation of NF-κB. However, our current study has not directly verified whether METTL3 directly modifies mRNAs related to the TGF-β pathway, nor has it explored whether the activation of NF-κB crosstalks with the TGF-β pathway to influence the EndMT process. This represents a limitation of our study, and elucidating the specific contribution of the TGF-β pathway in the regulation of EndMT by the METTL3/TNFAIP3 axis will be an important direction for future research. Currently, a small number of small-molecule compounds have been reported to target the catalytic active site of METTL3, such as STM2457 (50). These compounds have demonstrated certain pharmacological activities in leukemia or stem cell differentiation models. Therefore, using these small-molecule compounds to target METTL3 may offer a novel strategy for anti-atherosclerosis therapy.

In this study, we demonstrated that the elevated expression of METTL3 in endothelial cells accelerates the degradation of TNFAIP3 mRNA through YTHDF2-dependent m6A modification. This epigenetic regulatory process leads to the downregulation of TNFAIP3 expression in endothelial cells, subsequently activating the NF-κB/Snail signaling pathway, resulting in EndMT and thereby promoting the development of AS. Among these, TNFAIP3 plays a pivotal role, as its silencing exacerbates the process of EndMT and plaque formation in AS. This study suggests a potential mechanism underlying AS and may inform theoretical foundations while highlighting potential targets for future therapeutic strategies.
