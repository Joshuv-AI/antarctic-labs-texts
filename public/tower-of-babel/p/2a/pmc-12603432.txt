Construction and identification of a prognostic and therapeutic signature based on ferroptosis-related oxidative stress genes in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Da Yao, Feng Jin, Zhaohua Xia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-associated deaths globally, with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) being the predominant type. Ferroptosis, a unique form of iron-dependent programmed cell death that results from lipid peroxidation, plays a critical role in the initiation and progression of LUAD. In this study, we aimed to construct and identify an effective prognostic model that could serve as a potential therapeutic target for LUAD.MethodsThe expression of ferroptosis-related genes obtained from FerrDb and oxidative stress-related genes collected from MSigDB were analyzed using data from The Cancer Genome Atlas-LUAD cohort. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator Cox regression, CoxBoost, random survival forest, and stepwise Cox analyses were performed to screen for suitable gene signatures for modeling. Finally, we developed and verified a novel ferroptosis-and oxidative stress-related risk model.ResultsAn 11-gene-based prognostic model, comprising ERO1A, BTK, SPHK1, TRPA1, BAK1, PPIA, SLC1A1, HGF, ECT2, PNPT1, and GPR37E, was built. This model showed good performance while predicting survival of patients with LUAD. This study indicated that different risk score groups exhibited different tumor mutation burden, immune microenvironment cell infiltration, immune response, differentially expressed genes, functional characteristics, correlations with immune checkpoint genes, and drug sensitivity.ConclusionsWe established a novel ferroptosis-related oxidative stress gene-related model that possesses potential predictive value for prognosis of patients with LUAD, immunotherapy response, and chemotherapeutic drug sensitivity, and indicated its potential applicability in clinical practice.

Introduction

Lung cancer has the highest global incidence and mortality rate among cancers, with 2.5 million new cases and 1.8 million lung cancer-related deaths in 2022, accounting for 12.4% and 18.7%, respectively, among all cancers and ranking second and first, respectively (1). Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most common histological subtype of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and is the predominant type of lung cancer (2,3). Despite considerable advancements in tumor treatment, such as chemotherapy and immunotherapy, advanced-stage patients with LUAD still have a relatively poor prognosis, with a 5-year overall survival rate of <30% (4,5). Therefore, developing a prognostic model using biomarkers to predict the occurrence and development of LUAD remains crucial.

Ferroptosis, discovered as a type of iron-dependent programmed cell death, can cause the accumulation of iron-dependent lipid peroxides and the imbalance of redox, ultimately resulting in cell membrane rupture and oxidative cell death (6,7). Growing evidence has shown that ferroptosis induction may be an attractive strategy for chemotherapy and immunotherapy (8-10). However, the effect of ferroptosis on the prognosis of patients with LUAD remains unclear. Previous studies have indicated that ferroptosis is closely associated with the redox systems. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated by nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidases, lipid peroxidation resulting from redox activation, and redox producers of oxidative phosphorylation in mitochondria might disrupt intracellular redox homeostasis and induce ferroptosis (11,12). Therefore, developing a prognostic model based on ferroptosis-related oxidative stress genes in LUAD can be highly valuable.

In this study, we constructed a comprehensive prognostic model for LUAD based on 11 ferroptosis-related oxidative stress genes and explored the differences in prognosis, immunotherapy response, and chemotherapeutic drug sensitivity among the subtypes. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-854/rc).

Methods

Data source

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)-LUAD dataset (https://www.cancer.gov/ccg/research/genome-sequencing/tcga) was used to obtain RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), clinical, and survival data (13). In total, 537 LUAD and 59 normal samples were included in this study, of which 524 samples with complete prognostic information were included in the subsequent model construction and analysis. As a subsequent model validation dataset, GSE30219 (218 samples) (14), GSE31210 (226 samples) (15), GSE37745 (196 samples) (16), and GSE41271 (275 samples) (17) with prognostic information of cancer tissues were downloaded from the National Center for Biotechnology Information Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The corresponding annotation information for the probe was downloaded simultaneously. The probes were converted into gene symbols, different probes were mapped to the same gene, and the maximum value was used as the gene expression value. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Differential expression analysis

To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from the RNA-seq data, we screened high-expression genes (average read count >5) using the Deseq2 package (18) (Version 1.40.2) in R and set two thresholds [adjusted P value <0.05, |log2 fold change (FC)| >0.5] for statistical and biological significance.

Screening of related genes

For oxidative stress-related genes, we downloaded the gene collection from the MSigDB database (https://igordot.github.io/msigdbr/) (19) using the Msigdbr package (Version 7.5.1) in R and screened 436 oxidative stress-related genes for redundancy. Furthermore, we retrieved ferroptosis-related genes from the latest version of the FerrDb database (http://www.zhounan.org/ferrdb) (20) and collected 538 ferroptosis-related protein-coding genes. Subsequently, we screened genes that showed significant differences between normal and tumor tissues in TCGA dataset and analyzed the relationship between differentially expressed oxidative stress and ferroptosis genes using Pearson correlation coefficients. Finally, 197 differentially expressed oxidative stress genes with high expression correlations with differentially expressed ferroptosis genes were screened using thresholds (|cor| >0.3, P<0.05).

In TCGA-LUAD samples, 46 prognosis-related oxidative stress genes were screened using the survival (version 2.41-1) R package based on the above genes and combined with clinical survival prognosis information.

Construction and evaluation of the ferroptosis-related oxidative stress genes Riskscore model

It has been reported that a combination of multiple methods yields a better model than a single method (21). Based on the above ferroptosis-related oxidative stress-related genes, we first screened 18 genes as variables using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox model of the glmnet (version 2.0-18) R package and then obtained variables for seven and 15 genes using the CoxBoost (version 1.4) and randomForestSRC packages (version 3.2.2), respectively, in R. Finally, by combining the 20 genes screened by the above three methods, we simplified the genes using the My.stepwise package (version 0.1.0) in R and obtained a total of 11 genes that were most important for prognosis, and used them to construct Riskscore models.

To determine the accuracy of the Riskscore model, we calculated the Riskscore for each sample according to the gene expression level in the GEO dataset. Furthermore, with the median or quantile of the Riskscore, these samples were grouped into high- and low-Riskscore groups, which were used to evaluate the correlation with prognosis using the Kaplan-Meier analysis of survival package (Version 2.41-1) in R. The median of Riskscores was used as the threshold for TCGA-LUAD and GSE30219, whereas the top 40% and bottom 40% of the Riskscore were grouped into high- and low- Riskscore groups, respectively, for GSE41271, GSE31210, and GSE37745. Finally, based on TCGA samples, Kaplan-Meier curve analysis was used to calculate the association between high- and low-gene expression levels and survival prognosis (TCGA-LUAD, GSE41271, GSE31210) or disease recurrence (GSE30219, GSE37745). The cutoff value was obtained using the survminer package (version 0.4.3) in R based on expression, survival time, and survival status.

Independent analysis of Riskscore models

To determine whether the above Riskscore model is a significant independent prognostic factor, we included sex and stage in multivariate Cox regression analysis, and further screened out the variables (P<0.05). The clinical factors identified by Riskscore model and multivariate Cox regression analysis were then integrated, using the R package rms to build a nomogram to predict 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival. Decision curve analysis (DCA) curves were drawn to evaluate the effectiveness of the nomogram.

Mutation analysis between different Riskscore groups

To compare the differences in tumor mutation burden (TMB), we identified genes with significant differences in mutation frequency between the different groups using the maftools (22) (version 2.160) R package. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare TMB differences.

Association of the immune microenvironment between different Riskscore groups

Expression based on TCGA STAD tumor samples was analyzed using cell-type identification by estimating relative subsets of RNA transcripts (CIBERSORT) (23), which quantified the abundance of 22 immune cells. In addition, we used estimation of stromal and immune cells in malignant tumor tissues using expression data (ESTIMATE) (24) to calculate the StromalScore, ImmuneScore, and ESTIMATEScore, which reflect the number of stromal components around the tumor, the number and activity of immune cells in the tumor microenvironment, and the overall characteristics of the tumor microenvironment combined with the StromalScore and ImmuneScore. Differences in the immune microenvironment were compared based on the above scores using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and the Spearman correlation between the above scores and the risk score was calculated.

Association of the immune response between different riskscore groups

For immune response analysis, we analyzed the potential Riskscores for sample immunotherapy response using Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) (25) based on the tumor immune microenvironment. In addition, we assessed cytokine (CYT) scores by calculating the geometric mean of GZMA and PRF1 (26). The immune prognostic signature (IPS) scores of the samples were downloaded from The Cancer Immunome Atlas (TCIA) database (https://tcia.at/home) (27). Tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) (28) was obtained by calculating the geometric means of CCL2, CCL4, CCL5, CCL8, CCL18, CCL19, CCL21, CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11, and CXCL13.

Functional analysis of the differential genes between different Riskscore groups

For Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes signaling pathway analyses, differential genes between high- and low-Riskscore were analyzed using the clusterProfiler (29) (Version 3.1.7) R package.

Drug sensitivity between Riskscore groups

To compare the sensitivity to chemotherapy drugs, the Wilcoxon test was conducted using the pRRophetic (30) (version 0.5) R package.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyzes were based on R software 4.2.2. A Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to assess the correlations. Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to analyze the difference between groups. Statistically significance was defined as P value < 0.05.

Identification of key genes

A flowchart of this study is shown in Figure 1. First, in the differential expression analysis, we found 219 DEGs among the 538 ferroptosis-related genes, including 132 upregulated and 87 downregulated genes in tumor tissues, and 208 DEGs among the 436 oxidative stress-related genes, including 116 upregulated and 92 downregulated genes in tumor tissues (Figure 2A,2B). Based on these DEGs, we screened 197 ferroptosis- and oxidative stress-related genes using Pearson’s correlation coefficient analysis. Finally, a total of 43 prognostic ferroptosis- and oxidative stress-related genes were screened using Kaplan-Meier and univariate Cox regression analyses.

Construction and validation of the Riskscore model

Based on the 46 aforementioned genes, LASSO Cox regression, CoxBoost, and randomForestSRC analyses identified 18, 7, and 15 genes, respectively (Figure S1A-S1C). After redundancy, 24 genes were screened using these three methods (Figure S1D). Further stepwise Cox analysis and multivariate Cox regression coefficients identified 11 optimal genes as prognostic biomarkers for constructing the Riskscore model (Figure 3A). Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed significant differences between the high- and low-Riskscore groups. Patients with LUAD having a low Riskscore had a higher probability of survival (Figure 3B,3C). The AUC for 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival predictions were 0.70, 0.69, and 0.73, respectively (Figure 3D). Additionally, the prognostic value of the Riskscore model was further confirmed using survival probability (GSE41271 and GSE31210) and no recurrence probability (GSE30219 and GSE37745), which exhibited significant differences between groups based on the Riskscore model (Figure 4).

Independent prognostic factors of Riskscore

Cox analysis indicated that the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) stage and Riskscore, instead of sex and age, were significant independent prognostic factors for LUAD (Figure 5A, Figure S2). Furthermore, we assessed the predictive abilities of the nomogram model based on these prognostic factors. The calibration curve showed that the nomogram exhibited perfect predictive ability, as it closely matched the predicted recurrence rates at 1-, 3-, and 5-years (Figure 5B,5C). The DCA curves suggested that Riskscore performed well in clinical benefit (Figure 5D). These findings suggested that the 11-genes signature showed independent prognostic factors for LUAD patients.

Mutation features in the different Riskscore groups

To compare differences in TMB, we used the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, which suggested that TMB in the high-Riskscore group was significantly higher than in the low-Riskscore group and that TMB was significantly positively correlated with Riskscore (Figure 6A). Furthermore, comparing the mutation frequency between the two groups, we identified 128 genes with significant differences (P<0.05) and 29 genes with the most significant differences (P<0.005) (Figure 6B,6C).

Characteristics of immune microenvironment between different Riskscore groups

There were 11 significantly different immune cells between the high- and low-Riskscore groups. Compared to the low-Riskscore group, M1 macrophages and activated CD4 memory T cells were significantly higher in the high-Riskscore group (Figure 7A). Furthermore, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test confirmed a difference in the individual scores based on the ESTIMATE between high- and low-Riskscore groups (Figure 7B).

To elucidate the relevance and variability of Riskscore prediction for immunotherapy response, TIDE, CYT, IPS, and TLS score were conducted and showed that the TIDE score of the high-Riskscore group was higher (Figure 7C, Figure S3), CYT, IPS, and TLS score of the high-Riskscore group was lower (Figure 7D), which indicated a low potential to benefit from immunotherapy.

Functional analysis of the differential genes between different Riskscore groups

A total of 856 DEGs were identified between the high- and low-Riskscore groups, of which 434 were upregulated and 422 were downregulated in the high-Riskscore group. GO enrichment analysis showed that the aforementioned DEGs that were considerably enriched were involved in the antimicrobial humoral response, cornified envelope, and structural constituents of the skin epidermis (Figure 8A). Additionally, gene set enrichment analysis (GESA) analysis based on Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) revealed 15 pathways significantly related to DEGs, including B cell receptor signaling pathway and hematopoietic cell lineage (Figure 8B).

Differential immune checkpoint genes in the different Riskscore groups

Considering the correlation between the Riskscore and immune checkpoint genes, the Spearman’s correlation coefficient analysis showed that the Riskscore was negatively correlated with multiple genes of the HLA family (Figure 9A).

Differential drug sensitivities in the different Riskscore groups

Furthermore, we elucidated the role of the Riskscore in predicting the therapeutic response to 237 common chemotherapeutics, and of these, cisplatin, docetaxel, erlotinib, gemcitabine, paclitaxel, and vinorelbine exhibited significant differences between the two groups (Figure 9B).

Discussion

Recent studies have widely established ferroptosis signatures based on a single algorithm for predicting the survival of patients with LUAD (31,32). However, a combination of multiple methods can yield a better model than a single method (21). In this study, we established and verified a novel ferroptosis- and oxidative stress gene-related risk model based on the LASSO Cox, CoxBoost, randomForestSRC, and stepwiseCox analyses. In our Riskscore model, the high-Riskscore group had a worse survival rate (Figure 3C). The external validation results also verified that our Riskscore model had a high predictive accuracy (Figure 4). Finally, we compared the differences in TMB, immune microenvironment cell infiltration, immune response, DEGs, functional characteristics, correlation with immune checkpoint genes, and drug sensitivity between the different Riskscore models.

The 11 genes involved in the Riskscore model were ERO1A, BTK, SPHK1, TRPA1, BAK1, PPIA, SLC1A1, HGF, ECT2, PNPT1, and GPR37E, and of these ERO1A, BTK, SPHK1, BAK1, and PPIA were common genes screened using LASSO Cox, CoxBoost, and randomForestSRC analyses, and are associated with cancer progression. For example, ERO1A, an endoplasmic reticulum-resident oxidase, induces immunosuppression and resistance to programmed death 1 (PD-1) blockade. Higher ERO1A expression is associated with a higher risk of recurrence after neoadjuvant immunotherapy in NSCLC (33). Moreover, BTK expression is negatively associated with survival and TNM stages in LUAD (34). However, due to the existence of a distinct isoform of BTK, p65BTK levels are significantly higher in LUAD with EGFR-wild type (35). Therefore, BTK may play a dual role in LUAD. Increasing evidence has demonstrated that knockdown of SPHK1 in NSCLC can inhibit the cell cycle and tumor growth in vivo and in vitro (36). Moreover, the study also indicated that the role of SPHK1 is associated with EGFR phosphorylation and protein kinase B (AKT)/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) activation (37). These studies indicate the important roles of these 11 genes in the mediation of cancer, especially LUAD, and suggest the robustness of the Riskscore model.

In this study, we observed differential TMB and immune microenvironments between different Riskscore, indicating the crucial role of the immune response in LUAD. It has been reported that TMB is a reliable biomarker for predicting the efficacy and prognosis of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in solid tumors (38). However, TMB has limitations for the treatment of NSCLC with ICI. Combined biomarkers may help implement a good prediction model (39,40). Immune cells play important roles in elimination, equilibrium, and escape in the tumor microenvironment. Our results demonstrated that TMB and immune cells positively correlated with the Riskscore. Additionally, the expression of HLA-related genes, which are important components of the immune system, was lower in the high-Riskscore group than in the low-Riskscore group. These results further indirectly demonstrate the excellent prognostic performance of the Riskscore in LUAD. Finally, we also explored the role of the Riskscore in predicting chemotherapeutic response and found that the cisplatin, docetaxel, erlotinib, gemcitabine, paclitaxel, and vinorelbine responses were significantly different in the high- and low-Riskscore groups, which showed the potential significance of the Riskscore in the context of clinical practice.

Despite these strengths, there are some limitations in this study. Previous studies have demonstrated the key role of Riskscore model [e.g., ERO1A, BTK, SPHK1 (33-37)] in immune evasion and tumor growth in LUAD progression, however the prognostic value of our Riskscore model must be further validated experimentally and in a clinical context.

Conclusions

We constructed a novel and reliable ferroptosis- and oxidative stress gene-related risk model for patients with LUAD, which performed well for predicting the prognosis of LUAD and was associated with TMB, immune microenvironment, immune response, and drug sensitivity, aiming to shed new light on the potential application of this model in clinical practice.
