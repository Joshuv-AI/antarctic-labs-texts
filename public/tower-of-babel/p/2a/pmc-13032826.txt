Continuous phylogeography reveals shifting environmental drivers of highly pathogenic avian influenza H5 spread in Italy, 2016–23
Authors: Diletta Fornasiero, Paolo Mulatti, Alice Fusaro, Isabella Monne, Fabiana Gámbaro, Simon Dellicour, Francesca Scolamacchia, Luca Martelli, Giulia Faustini, Mariette F Ducatez, Claire Guinat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe ongoing global spread of highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) continues causing major impacts on poultry, wildlife, and public health. Among the most affected countries in Europe, Italy experienced three major epidemic waves in 2016–23, caused by 2.3.4.4b H5N8 and H5N1 subtypes. Yet, the underlying spatio-temporal dispersal dynamics and drivers of these epidemics remain to be elucidated. Here, we combined viral genome sequencing with continuous phylogeographic inference to reconstruct the evolutionary and spatio-temporal history of these epidemics. By combining genetic data with landscape, poultry-related variables, and wild bird abundances, we explored how the environment was associated with viral diffusion in space and time. We identified distinct diffusion patterns and environmental associations across the three epidemics. The first two waves (2016–17 and 2021–22) were dominated by local poultry-to-poultry transmission in high-density farming areas, with agricultural areas being significantly associated with a relatively higher viral lineage dispersal velocity in 2021–22. In contrast, the most recent wave (2022–23) showed evidence of mid- to long-distance dispersal events positively associated with wetlands, waterbodies, and gull abundances, pointing to a larger role of wild birds in HPAI spatial dynamics. The estimated weighted diffusion coefficients and spatial wavefront distances revealed a shift from localised poultry-driven to long-distance spread, likely related to wild birds. Our findings highlight a progressive shift from poultry-driven epidemics to more complex environmental transmission dynamics involving wild birds and natural habitats. These changes underscore the need to adapt surveillance and control strategies to an evolving and ecologically diverse viral landscape.Keywords: HPAI, phylogeography, environmental drivers, viral lineage spread location, viral lineage spread velocity

Introduction

Highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) is a severe, highly transmissible transboundary viral disease caused by avian influenza A viruses of subtypes H5 and H7. These viruses can cause massive mortality in susceptible bird species and have severe consequences for poultry production systems, wildlife conservation, and may pose a significant threat to global public health due to their zoonotic potential. Outbreaks in poultry typically lead to significant economic losses due to high mortality, culling policies, trade and movement restrictions, while wild bird infections threaten biodiversity and contribute to the maintenance and dissemination of the virus across wildlife and to spillover events in poultry (Hagerman and Marsh 2016).

In recent years, following the introduction of the clade 2.3.4.4b viruses into Europe in 2016 (Fusaro et al. 2024), the continent has faced an unprecedented shift in the ecology and epidemiology of HPAI. The virus rapidly established endemic circulation in wild birds (Adlhoch et al. 2022b), triggering widespread and recurrent outbreaks across the continent. This viral lineage has shown remarkable adaptability, expanding its host range to diverse mammal species (Agüero et al. 2023, Leguia et al. 2023, Food and Agriculture Organisation of the United Nations 2025), and spreading to new geographic areas (Madslien et al. 2021). Its persistence is supported by ongoing genetic reassortments and sustained circulation in wild bird reservoirs (European Food Safety Authority et al. 2023, Fusaro et al. 2024), allowing the virus to remain active year-round, including during summer months (Xie et al. 2023, Couty et al. 2026). These changes have disrupted previously predictable and long-standing ecological and seasonal patterns and raised significant concerns for both animal and public health. Understanding the mechanisms and pathways driving these changing dynamics has become an urgent priority for animal and public health across Europe.

Italy ranks among the most affected countries in Europe, with five HPAI epidemic waves reported since 2016. Among these, the three largest Italian HPAI epidemics—2016–17 (H5N8), 2021–22 (H5N1), and 2022–23 (H5N1)—together led to 441 poultry outbreaks and 290 recorded wild bird cases, resulting in the loss of ~20 million birds, although the true number of wild birds affected is likely being underestimated. Most poultry outbreaks occurred in the northern regions, particularly Veneto, Lombardia, Emilia-Romagna, and Piemonte, a high-density poultry area (HDPA) characterised by large-scale indoor operations with high stocking densities that hosts >70% of the country’s poultry production (EFSA Panel on Animal Health and Animal Welfare (AHAW), European Union Reference Laboratory for Avian Influenza et al. 2023). This region also overlaps with ecologically significant wetlands located along migratory bird routes, creating a high-risk interface for virus introduction and spread. While HPAI circulation has continued with seasonal outbreaks during the 2023–24 and 2024–25 epidemic waves, the three preceding waves remain the most impactful in terms of spatial scale, affected species, and burden on the poultry industry. Notably, during the 2021–22 epidemic, broiler was the most affected species after fattening turkey (Adlhoch et al. 2022a), whereas during the 2022–23 wave, mass mortality events in wild birds were observed, particularly in gulls (Adlhoch et al. 2023a). These recurring HPAI epidemics in Italy have shown different patterns over time and across regions, underscoring the growing unpredictability of the virus in terms of timing, affected species, and geographical extent. A large number of viral genome sequences were generated during these epidemics, providing a rich and representative dataset, which offers a unique opportunity to reconstruct transmission patterns and to explore the drivers underlying observed differences between epidemic waves. While viral evolution, via reassortment and host adaptation, is a recognized driver of viral circulation dynamics, the contribution of ecological and anthropogenic factors remains poorly understood (Runstadler and Puryear 2024).

In this study, we investigated the evolutionary and dispersal history of the three most significant HPAI epidemics in Italy over a 7-year period (2016–23), using a continuous phylogeographic approach. By leveraging an extensive and representative dataset of viral genome sequences and integrating it with environmental data, we also aimed to investigate the association of key drivers with the observed HPAI diffusion across space and time. These insights would provide a valuable opportunity to move beyond case-based analyses and to generate new hypotheses about the mechanisms that drive HPAI dynamics. They could ultimately inform more targeted and effective strategies for risk assessment, surveillance, and control of this complex and rapidly evolving disease.

Materials and methods

Data collection and preparation

Epidemiological data on Italian poultry and non-poultry bird outbreaks were collected for the HPAI H5N8 epidemic (2016–17), and two HPAI H5N1 epidemics (2021–22 and 2022–23). Specifically, outbreak data—including dates of symptom onset, suspicion, confirmation, and extinction, as well as geographical coordinates, species, and production types—were retrieved from on-field epidemiological investigations and the Animal Disease Information System (ADIS, European Commission 2021). Poultry farm and non-poultry bird outbreaks were defined and reported according to official ADIS notifications to ensure consistency and preserve the correct total number of outbreaks.

Italian HA segment sequences of H5 subtype, clade 2.3.4.4b, from the three epidemic events were collected from the GISAID EpiFlu database (Shu and McCauley 2017) on 1 February 2024, along with 45 newly released sequences generated at IZSVe using Illumina MiSeq, as previously described (Mulatti et al. 2018, Fornasiero et al. 2023, Fusaro et al. 2024), and which have been made publicly available as part of this study. European H5 sequences of clade 2.3.4.4b, corresponding to outbreaks confirmed during the same period as the Italian epidemics, were retrieved from GISAID on 29 March 2024 (Supplementary Table 1). All sequences were aligned using MAFFT 7 (Katoh and Standley 2013) and manually trimmed in AliView 1.28 (Larsson 2014). Duplicate records (i.e. identical isolate names and nucleotide sequences) and sequences with HA lengths <1700 bases or low quality were removed. To ensure computational efficiency while maintaining dataset representativeness, Italian sequences were subsampled based on phylogenetic relationship and epidemiological information: within each group of highly similar sequences (>99.5% nucleotide identity), a single representative was retained per outbreak (ADIS number), collection date, and species. European non-Italian sequences were similarly filtered based on country and high genetic similarity (>99.5%). The curated datasets underwent maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic analysis in IQ-TREE 2 (Minh et al. 2020) to identify monophyletic Italian groups. Branch support was assessed using ultrafast bootstrap (UFBoot) with 1000 replicates (Hoang et al. 2018) and Shimodaira-Hasegawa-like approximate likelihood ratio test (SH-aLRT) values (Minh et al. 2020). The ML trees were further analysed in TempEst 1.5.3 (Rambaut et al. 2016) to detect outliers and assess the temporal signal.

A total of 22 raster variables related to landscape features, poultry, and wild bird abundance were compiled from various data sources (see Supplementary Table 2 for details and variables’ description). These variables were selected to capture key environmental factors potentially influencing the dispersal dynamics of HPAI lineages in Italy. Landscape data comprised land cover types (CORINE Land Cover—CLC—classes including: artificial surfaces—CLC1, agricultural areas—CLC2, forest and semi-natural areas—CLC3, wetlands—CLC4, and water bodies—CLC5), topographic and anthropogenic features (elevation, human footprint, landscape fragmentation, and fine particulate matter concentration—PM2.5), and proximity to ecological sites (distance from wetlands and Important Bird Areas—IBAs). Natural resources availability was described using vegetation phenology metrics derived from the Medium Resolution Vegetation Phenology and Productivity dataset, including the start, end, and duration of the growing season. Poultry-related factors included the number of poultry farms and the Simpson diversity index, which describes the heterogeneity of poultry species composition. Wild bird species abundance was represented by six species of epidemiological interest for HPAI: mallards (Anas platyrhynchos), cattle egret (Bubulcus ibis), black-headed gull (Larus ridibundus), little egret (Egretta garzetta), Mediterranean gull (Larus melanocephalus), and common pheasant (Phasianus colchicus). All rasters were harmonised to a common coordinate reference system (EPSG:4326, WGS 84—geographic coordinates), spatial resolution (0.0449°), and extent covering Italy. Raster resampling was performed on QGIS 3.38.1 (QGIS Association 2026) using nearest neighbour or bilinear interpolation methods, for categorical or continuous data, respectively. Categorical variables were transformed into a continuous format by calculating the percentage of each categorical class per pixel, enabling their inclusion in the phylogeographic analyses.

Identifying introduction events and monophyletic Italian groups

Prior to phylogenetic reconstruction, all HA sequences were screened for recombination using the Recombination Detection Program (RDP5, v5.58) (Martin et al. 2021), and no evidence of recombination was detected in any of the epidemic waves considered. Discrete trait analyses were then performed to identify introduction events into Italy and monophyletic Italian groups based on ancestral state reconstructions. To achieve this, a binary location trait (Italy versus other) was assigned to each sequence, and discrete trait analyses were performed separately for each epidemic wave in two steps using BEAST 1.10.4 (Suchard et al. 2018) with BEAGLE 3 (Ayres et al. 2019) library to enhance computational performance. We used a HKY + Γ4 nucleotide substitution model, an asymmetric discrete trait substitution model, an uncorrelated relaxed clock model with a lognormal distribution (Drummond et al. 2006), and a coalescent Bayesian skygrid tree prior (Drummond et al. 2002, Gill et al. 2013). Markov chain Monte-Carlo (MCMC) chains were run for 1 billion iterations, sampling every 105 iterations. The first 10% of samples was discarded as burn-in, and convergence and mixing properties were assessed in Tracer 1.7.2 (Rambaut et al. 2018), ensuring that effective sample size (ESS) values exceeded 200 for all parameters. Maximum clade credibility (MCC) trees were reconstructed using TreeAnnotator v1.10.4 (Suchard et al. 2018) and visualized using the R package ‘ggtree’ (Yu et al. 2017).

Reconstructing the spatio-temporal diffusion of HPAI

Continuous (i.e. spatially explicit) phylogeographic analyses were performed using precise geographical coordinates and collection dates to reconstruct the spatial and temporal diffusion of HPAI in Italy. Analyses were implemented in BEAST 1.10.4 using relaxed random walk (RRW) diffusion models (Drummond et al. 2006), with branch-specific in dispersal velocity drawn from an underlying gamma or lognormal distribution, an HKY + Γ4 nucleotide substitution model, an uncorrelated lognormal relaxed clock model, and a coalescent Bayesian skygrid tree prior. All MCMC chains were run for 1 billion iterations, sampling every 105 chains. The first 10% of samples were discarded as burn-in, and convergence and mixing properties were assessed in Tracer 1.7.2, ensuring ESS values >200. MCC trees of the spatially explicit phylogeographic reconstruction were generated by extracting the estimated dates and geographical coordinates of internal nodes. From the posterior distributions, 1000 post-burn-in trees were randomly selected and analysed using the R package ‘seraphim’ (Dellicour et al. 2016b) to estimate two statistics: the weighted diffusion coefficient (WDC) and the evolution through time of the spatial wavefront distance. The WDC quantifies lineage diffusivity and represents the velocity at which inferred lineages spread across a two-dimensional space (Trovão et al. 2015, Dellicour et al. 2024). The spatial wavefront distances were computed for each monophyletic group containing at least 10 sequences, representing the temporal evolution of the maximal distance between the inferred geographic origin of each group and the furthest location reached by its lineages over time (Dellicour et al. 2016a). For these monophyletic groups, sequences were additionally assigned to viral genotypes as previously described by Mulatti et al. (2018) for the 2016–17 epidemic wave, or by Fusaro et al. (2024) for the 2021–23 epidemics. For the latter two epidemic waves, genotype assignment was performed using the Genin2 tool (https://github.com/izsvenezie-virology/genin2), which identifies epidemiologically relevant clade 2.3.4.4b genotypes detected in Europe since 2020. Relevant genotypes are defined as those detected in at least three viruses collected from at least two different countries, following on the classification and nomenclature described in Fusaro et al. (2024).

Investigating the impact of environmental factors on the dispersal locations of viral lineages

The 1000 post-burn-in trees were also analysed to investigate whether HPAI viral lineages tended to preferentially circulate within areas associated with specific environmental conditions. This was assessed by estimating the E metric (Dellicour 2020), defined as the average value extracted on an environmental raster at the inferred tree node locations (Eestimated), and which was computed for the 1000 trees sampled from the posterior distribution. The posterior distribution of these values was then compared to a null distribution generated by randomizing tree branch locations while preserving tree topology, branch lengths, and root location (Erandomised). While Eestimated > Erandomised indicated a tendency of sampled lineages to preferentially circulate within specific environmental conditions (i.e. in areas associated with relatively higher values for the tested environmental factor), Eestimated < Erandomised indicated a tendency of sampled lineages to avoid circulating in areas associated with relatively higher values for the tested environmental factor. Bayes factor (BF) support was used to quantify the strength of these associations between local environmental conditions and lineage dispersal location, with BF values >20 indicative of strong support (Kass and Raftery 1995).

Investigating the impact of environmental factors on the dispersal velocity of viral lineages

The influence of specific environmental factors on the dispersal velocity of HPAI viral lineages was investigated based on the analysis of the 1000 post-burn-in trees sampled from the posterior distribution of the continuous phylogeographic inference (see above). This was done by estimating environmental distances, defined as the spatial distance weighted according to the values of the underlying raster for each phylogenetic branch, using two alternative path models: the least-cost path model, which calculates the path of minimum cumulative cost between branch start and end points, based on a cost surface raster (Dijkstra 1959), and the Circuitscape path model, which incorporates stochasticity by applying circuit theory to consider multiple possible paths (McRae 2006). To investigate the potential influence of these environmental factors, we computed the Q statistic defined as the difference between the coefficients of determination obtained from two linear regressions: (i) branch durations regressed against environmental distances computed on the tested environmental raster, and (ii) branch durations regressed against environmental distances computed on a null raster (i.e. a raster where all accessible cells have a value of 1). The Q statistic hence measures to what extent environmental heterogeneity can explain some variability in lineage dispersal velocity as compared to a uniform ‘null’ raster, where computed environmental distances will only be a proxy for geographic distances. For each tree, a Q statistic was estimated and, as for the E metric, statistical support was then evaluated by comparing the Q distribution from inferred trees to that from randomized trees obtained following the above-described randomization procedure of phylogenetic branches. We again interpreted BF values >20 as strong support. Raster variables were tested both as potential ‘conductance’ (facilitating movement) and ‘resistance’ (impeding movement) factors. The scaling factor (set to 10, 100, and 1 000) was used to test different levels of raster influence on lineage dispersal velocity (Dellicour et al. 2020).

Results

Genetic sequence data and Maximum Likelihood trees

After data curation (Supplementary Table 1), the final datasets comprised 176 sequences for the 2016–17 epidemic (93 Italian and 83 non-Italian), 483 sequences for the 2021–22 epidemic (316 Italian and 167 non-Italian), and 442 sequences for the 2022–23 epidemic (181 Italian and 261 non-Italian) (listed in Supplementary Table 3). ML trees were inferred to identify the presence of monophyletic Italian groups during each epidemic wave (Supplementary Material Fig. 1). In 2016–17, despite multiple introduction events, a single monophyletic group was observed (UFBoot = 98%, aLRT = 82.2%). In contrast, in the 2021–22 and 2022–23 epidemics, multiple groups were detected (support values ranging from 62% to 100% for UFBoot, and from 84% to 94% for aLRT), reflecting repeated incursions into the country leading to further internal dissemination of viral strains.

Identifying HPAI introduction events in Italy

Time-scaled phylogenetic reconstructions per epidemic are shown in Supplementary Material Fig. 2. The MCC tree confirmed a single well-supported monophyletic group during the 2016–17 epidemic, comprising 84 (76 poultry and 8 non-poultry birds) Italian sequences (posterior probability [PP] = 0.83; ancestral state probability [ASP] = 0.95). The time for the most recent ancestor was estimated to be 10 January 2017 (95% highest posterior density [HPD]: 7 September 2016–22 January 2017). For the 2021–22 epidemic, one monophyletic group of 227 sequences (222 poultry and 5 non-poultry birds; PP = 0.07; ASP = 0.99) was dated on 29 August 2021 (95% HPD: 4 August–23 September 2021). In addition, six further monophyletic groups (ranging from 2 to 35 sequences; PP = 0.30–1; ASP = 0.98–0.99), encompassing a total of 73 poultry and 9 non-poultry bird outbreaks, emerged over a short period between late October and early December 2021. In the 2022–23 epidemic, the earliest monophyletic group involved 40 outbreaks (12 poultry and 28 non-poultry birds) and was dated to 7 July 2022 (95% HPD: 13 May–26 August 2022; PP = 0.31; ASP = 0.99). Five additional monophyletic groups (2–10 sequences; PP = 0.17–1.00; ASP = 0.99–1.00) emerged in September 2022, jointly accounting for 17 poultry and 9 non-poultry bird outbreaks. The largest monophyletic group, comprising 87 sequences (10 poultry and 77 non-poultry birds; PP = 1; ASP = 0.98), originated on 11 December 2022 (95% HPD: 19 November–25 December 2022). The most recent monophyletic groups (PP = 0.76-0.90; ASP = 0.99), involving only non-poultry bird outbreaks (4 and 22 sequences, respectively), emerged in January and March 2023.

Analysing the dispersal dynamics of HPAI lineages introduced in Italy

The first wave (December 2016–17), driven by the H5N8-A/wild duck/Poland/82 A/2016-like genotype, unfolded in two distinct phases, affecting both domestic poultry and wild birds. The first phase (December 2016–June 2017) involved 16 poultry outbreaks and six wild bird detections, mostly concentrated in the northern regions of Veneto, Lombardia, and Emilia-Romagna, at the periphery of the HDPA. The second phase (July–December 2017) followed a more typical epidemic curve with successive peaks as transmission moved through the population, resulting in 67 poultry outbreaks—mainly in the core of HDPA—and eight additional wild bird detections. The continuous phylogeographic analysis was conducted on 84 Italian HPAI H5N8 sequences isolated from 80 outbreaks, representing 82% of all confirmed outbreaks (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Material Fig. 3, Supplementary Tables 4 and 5). The inferred MCC tree mirrored this two-phase temporal structure, showing two well-separated subclades (Fig. 1B). The viral lineage WDC showed two peaks during the first phase, in mid-January at 409 km2/week (95% HPD: 0–3 052 km2/week), and mid-March 2017 at 607 km2/week (95% HPD: 0–1965 km2/week), then dropped between April and September to an average of 79 km2/week (95% HPD: 1–769 km2/week). The highest peak in the epidemic diffusion occurred in late October, with a WDC of 1 288 km2/week (95% HPD: 19–1880 km2/week. Supplementary Material Fig. 4). The epidemic wavefront estimate shows that the viral lineages spread up to ~270 km (95% HPD: 175–358 km) from its location of origin in about 8–9 months, without further expansion until late October in correspondence of the WDC peak, reaching ~320 km (95% HPD: 249–446 km, Fig. 1C). The inferred spatial trajectories revealed substantial uncertainty regarding the early location of epidemic diffusion, estimated near the Veneto-Lombardia border (Fig. 1D). During the first phase, viral dispersal was characterised by long-distance dispersal events, predominantly along the east–west axis. The second exhibited short-distance transmission events, north–south spread with lower uncertainty.

The second wave (October 2021–April 2022) was characterised by rapid and intense spread. It peaked in early December 2021, with up to 51 poultry outbreaks reported in a single week, primarily affecting the Veneto and Lombardia regions. After the peak, outbreak frequency declined, with sporadic cases continuing in rural farms in Toscana and Emilia-Romagna regions between mid-March and April 2022. In total, 317 poultry outbreaks were reported across nine regions, along with 23 detections in non-poultry birds, including seagulls, birds of prey, owls, and herons (Supplementary Material Fig. 3, Supplementary Tables 4 and 5). The continuous phylogeographic reconstruction was performed using 316 Italian HPAI H5N1 sequences from 308 outbreaks, representing 91% of all confirmed outbreaks (Fig. 2A). The inferred MCC tree showed one dominant monophyletic group and a few other monophyletic groups that emerged in rapid succession, reflecting the wave’s intensity (Fig. 2B). The viral lineage WDC increased rapidly until mid-December 2021, peaking at 1306 km2/week (95% HPD: 272–3253 km2/week), before sharply declining to 2 km2/week (95% HPD: 0–667 km2/week) by mid-January. During the final phase of the epidemic, it fluctuated averaging around 50 km2/week (95% HPD: 0–253 km2/week. Supplementary Material Fig. 4). The maximum spatial wavefronts reached ~30 km (95% HPD: 22–44 km) and 50 km (95% HPD: 14–62 km) for the monophyletic groups ‘b’ (genotype EA-2020-C) and ‘c’ (genotype EA-2021-AD), respectively. In contrast, group ‘a’ (genotype EA-2020-C) expanded to ~220 km (95% HPD: 97–231 km) by early January 2022, roughly 6 months after its emergence (Fig. 2C). The inferred spatial spread suggested that after the first introduction event, the epidemic wave most likely developed and rapidly intensified in the HDPA (Fig. 2D). This early phase showed low spatial uncertainty, consistent with localised and dense lateral transmissions. In contrast, the later phase was mainly driven by long-range movements along the east–west axis, which were associated with higher spatial uncertainty, reflecting a more diffuse pattern of viral spread.

The third wave (September 2022–August 2023) showed a different dispersal dynamic pattern compared to previous waves, with limited impact on poultry but widespread infection in wild birds. A total of 41 poultry outbreaks were reported, while 253 wild bird outbreaks were confirmed across 30 species. Black-headed gulls (L. ridibundus) were particularly affected, experiencing large-scale mortality events (Supplementary Material Fig. 3, Supplementary Tables 4 and 5). The epidemic unfolded in three main peaks: mid-September 2022–January 2023, February–May 2023, and mid-June–late August 2023. Continuous phylogeographic analysis included 181 HPAI H5N1 sequences from 174 outbreaks, covering 59% of all confirmed cases (Fig. 3A). Despite the lower proportion compared to previous epidemics, the available dataset still captured the main spatio-temporal patterns of the epidemic, as many unsequenced detections likely represented repeated findings within the same local populations over short time intervals. The inferred MCC tree revealed multiple monophyletic groups consistent with distinct introduction events, reflecting the trend of the epidemic curve (Fig. 3B). The viral lineage WDC showed three peaks: the first in late September 2022, reaching 1 137 km2/week (95% HPD: 170–4484 km2/week); the second in mid-February 2023, peaking at 2332 km2/week (95% HPD: 862–3984 km2/week); and the third in June 2023, with a peak of 1240 km2/week (95% HPD: 182–2470 km2/week. Supplementary Material Fig. 4). Monophyletic groups ‘c,’ ‘d’ and ‘e,’ all predominantly characterised by EA-2021-AB genotype, spatially expanded over the same period (late 2022), reaching maximum wavefronts of 83 km (95% HPD: 59–113 km), 668 km (95% HPD: 601–758 km) and 214 km (95% HPD: 160–264 km), respectively, potentially representing distinct diffusion dynamics that occurred simultaneously. The remaining two monophyletic groups (a and b), consisting of the EA-2022-BB genotype, emerged later, from January 2023, reaching maximum spatial expansions of ~138 km (95% HPD: 109–159 km) and 170 km (95% HPD: 138–194 km), respectively, indicating more localised spread despite the large number of reported outbreaks (Fig. 3C). Spatial reconstruction identified the border area between Veneto and Emilia-Romagna as the most probable origin of the epidemic, though uncertainty extended from the Venetian Lagoon to Lombardia (Fig. 3D). Early viral spread featured mid to long-range-distance movements in multiple directions, later shifting to short-range movements towards northern areas typically unaffected by HPAI.

Testing the association between environmental factors and the dispersal locations of inferred viral lineages

The landscape phylogeographic analyses investigating the association between environmental factors and the dispersal locations of HPAI viral lineages revealed both recurrent patterns and shifts across the three epidemic waves (Fig. 4). During the 2016–17 epidemic, HPAI viral lineages tended to circulate in areas with a high number of poultry farms (BF = 166), greater diversity in poultry production types (Simpson’s diversity index, BF > 999), and elevated PM2.5 concentration (BF = 35). For the 2021–22 epidemic, we found positive associations between the dispersal locations of sampled viral lineages and the same three environmental factors (number of poultry farms, BF > 999; Simpson’s diversity index of poultry production types, BF = 332; and PM2.5 concentrations, BF = 21) along with human footprint (BF = 70). Moreover, for the 2021–22 epidemic, our analyses highlighted that sampled viral lineages tended to avoid circulating in areas associated with a relatively higher altitude (BF = 29) and the presence of forests and semi-natural areas (BF = 55). In contrast, for the 2022–23 epidemic, we further found strong associations with a broader range of variables, inferred viral lineages tended to circulate within artificial areas (BF > 999), wetlands (BF = 249), water bodies (BF > 999), as well as areas associated with a relatively higher human footprint (BF > 999), PM2.5 concentrations (BF = 26), and wild bird species abundances estimated for two out the six considered gull species (L. melanocephalus, BF = 199; L. ridibundus, BF > 999). For this epidemic wave, we also found that sampled viral lineages tended to avoid circulating in areas associated with a relatively higher elevation (BF = 142), agricultural areas (BF = 28), forests and semi-natural areas (BF = 26), areas distant from wetlands (BF > 999), and in landscapes with a high degree of fragmentation (BF = 22). Finally, for the 2022–23 epidemic, we also found a tendency for sampled viral lineages to have preferentially circulated in areas associated with earlier start (BF = 31), longer duration (BF = 249), and later end (BF = 31) of the vegetation growing season, possibly due to longer periods of ecological suitability and resource availability for wild birds. Contrary to the two previous epidemic waves, the number of poultry farms (BF = 142) was now identified as an environmental factor with a negative association with viral lineage dispersal locations, which could potentially suggest a shift in outbreak locations from poultry-dense areas towards zones more linked to wild bird activity.

Association between environmental factors and the dispersal velocities of viral lineages

Our analyses on the association between environmental factors and the dispersal velocity of inferred viral lineages revealed only one strong association during the 2021–22 epidemic, identifying the agricultural areas as a potential conductance factor (BF = 24; Supplementary Table 6).

Discussion

Our study provides new insights into the evolutionary and viral lineage dispersal dynamics of the three most impactful HPAI epidemic waves that occurred in Italy between 2016 and 2023, revealing substantial changes in viral diffusion patterns, ecological drivers, and host involvement over time. Following introduction into the Italian country, each epidemic wave likely began spreading from a common geographical area within the HDPA region, yet displayed distinct temporal and spatial dispersal dynamics. The 2016–17 H5N8 epidemic unfolded in two phases, with H5N8-A/wild duck/Poland/82 A/2016-like virus circulation reported throughout the year and included an atypical persistence during summer months. The spatial expansion observed during the first phase of the epidemic, reaching a wavefront of ~270 km by March 2017, is consistent with long-distance transmission events that triggered sporadic and geographically dispersed outbreaks in domestic and rural poultry farms, and in wild birds at the margins of the HDPA (Mulatti et al. 2018, Harvey et al. 2021). In contrast, until October 2017, the second phase was characterised by a lower WDC (~79 km2/week) and a stationary wavefront, suggesting a more localised transmission pattern. During July–October 2017, outbreaks in poultry farms occurred predominantly within the HDPA and were associated with short-distance dispersal events, likely facilitated by indirect transmission mechanisms such as fomites, human activities, or shared equipment. After this period, both the WDC and the spatial wavefront increased again, indicating a further expansion driven by long-distance dispersal events into previously unaffected areas. The 2021–22 H5N1 epidemic was the shortest of the three waves, lasting from mid-October 2021 to mid-April 2022, and was largely confined within the HDPA, with only a limited number of sporadic outbreaks occurring outside the HDPA at a later stage. The epidemic wave was characterised by a massive involvement of poultry farms, with very few wild bird cases reported, possibly under-represented due to less intensive surveillance in wildlife. Three monophyletic groups were identified: the largest group, consisting of EA-2020-C sequences, began spatial expansion earlier and reached a maximum wavefront of ~220 km by January 2022, whereas the other two groups, corresponding to the EA-2020-C and EA-2021-AD genotypes, expanded later and over shorter distances. The observed spatial wavefront distances and the relatively low WDC are consistent with lateral spread events between farms, driven by direct and indirect contacts (Fornasiero et al. 2023). Similar to the 2016–17 waves, the 2022–23 H5N1 epidemic spanned nearly 1 year. The initial phase involved outbreaks in both commercial and rural poultry farms, along with a major involvement of wild bird populations, particularly seabirds, during spring and summer 2023. At the onset of the epidemic, two distinct diffusion dynamics were observed for sequences of the EA-2021-AB genotype, each corresponding to different monophyletic groups. Two groups included a limited number of cases and remained relatively localised within ~200 km (group ‘c’ and ‘e’), whereas the third emerged concurrently and expanded rapidly, reaching a maximum wavefront of over 650 km (group ‘d’). This unusually large wavefront was linked to a series of epidemiologically related outbreaks reported in areas far from the HDPA. Starting on December 2022, the epidemic progressed with the emergence of two additional monophyletic groups (‘a’ and ‘b’), which expanded with spatial wavefronts aligned with those observed in previous epidemics, primarily affecting the HDPA and surrounding areas. The overall number of affected poultry farms in 2022–23 (n = 41) was considerably lower than the previous epidemic wave in 2021–22 (n = 317). This was likely due to: (i) reduced susceptible poultry populations, as many poultry holdings were yet not restocked by September 2022, following the previous epidemic (Adlhoch et al. 2023b); (ii) improved biosecurity awareness; and (iii) the earlier implementation of stricter control measures, such as the establishment of a Further Restricted Zone (Delegated Regulation (EU) 2020/687, Article 21(1)(c) 2020). A shift in the eco-epidemiology of the H5N1 viruses was observed in the second part of the epidemic, when outbreaks became more spatially dispersed across northern Italy, and entire Black-headed gull colonies were affected. This change was associated with large-scale mortality events in Laridae linked to the gull-adapted EA-2022-BB genotype (Adlhoch et al. 2023a, Fusaro et al. 2024), revealing a spatial pattern no longer confined to the HDPA. Contextually, the larger number of cases in wild birds (especially compared to the 2016–17 epidemics) might be largely attributed to enhanced surveillance efforts following strengthened collaborations with wildlife and environmental institutes, wildlife rescue centres, and hunting associations. These partnerships significantly improved case identifications in wild birds through active surveillance activities and offered a more comprehensive picture of the epidemic.

Our investigations of the impact of environmental factors on the dispersal dynamics of viral lineages showed a potential shift from poultry-driven outbreak dynamics in earlier epidemics (2016–22) to a more wildlife and landscape-driven pattern in 2022–23, despite all outbreaks remaining largely confined in high-density farming areas. During the 2016–17 and 2021–22 waves, viral dispersal location seemed to be primarily positively associated with poultry-related factors, especially the higher number of poultry farms and a higher differentiation in terms of production types and reared species. Landscape variables related to human activity, such as human footprint and elevated PM2.5 concentrations, also appeared positively associated with dispersal locations of inferred viral lineages. These findings are consistent with previous studies highlighting that intensive farming systems can amplify viral pressure and sustain local transmission events (Hogerwerf et al. 2010, Dhingra et al. 2018, Dupas et al. 2025). Furthermore, we identified that agricultural areas, such as croplands and pastures, could have acted as a conductance factor increasing viral lineage dispersal velocity during the 2021–22 epidemic, potentially favouring movements of wild birds seeking foraging and roosting sites due to the abundance of food availability (McDuie et al. 2022, Martelli et al. 2023). Consistent with this, Liang et al. (Liang et al. 2020) identified cropping density as a consistent risk factor for HPAI outbreaks, positing that non-migratory wild birds may facilitate viral dissemination by bridging wetlands and poultry farms, even in the absence of long-distance dispersal events.

A shift in risk factors importance potentially occurred during the 2022–23 epidemic, during which HPAI mainly affected wild birds: in our analyses, wetlands and water bodies then emerged as environmental factors positively associated with the dispersal locations of inferred viral lineages, indicating that such natural habitats for waterfowl could have become more central to viral circulation in wild birds. We also found strong support for negative associations between inferred lineage dispersal locations and the following environmental factors: distance from wetlands, landscape fragmentation, agricultural areas, as well as forest and semi-natural areas. Interestingly, while we identified that inferred viral lineages tended to preferentially circulate in areas associated with a higher number of poultry farms during the previous epidemic waves, we found support for the opposite association during the latest 2022–23 epidemic wave. However, our analysis also revealed a supported tendency for a preferential circulation of lineages in areas with artificial surfaces and high PM2.5 concentrations, pointing to a potential dual pattern of viral lineage circulation that occurred in both natural and anthropogenically influenced habitats. This duality aligns with the ecology of the most affected wild bird species, and our results highlight a positive association between the inferred dispersal locations of HPAI lineages and the estimated abundances of L. ridibundus and L. melanocephalus. These gull species are well-adapted to both natural wetlands and human-modified environments, such as urban parks, garbage dumps, and industrial coastlines (Soldatini et al. 2008, Méndez et al. 2020), and likely act as ecological links between these habitats, supporting viral persistence across diverse landscapes. These findings are consistent with Martelli et al. (Martelli et al. 2025), who also identified gulls as key species associated with HPAI occurrence. However, unlike Martelli et al., who reported herons and mallards among the species associated with a higher risk of HPAI introduction events in poultry during the 2016–17 epidemic, our study found no robust associations between the dispersal location of inferred viral lineages and the abundances of A. platyrhynchos, B. ibis, and E. garzetta. These differences may stem from diverse methodological approaches and scopes of the two studies. Specifically, Martelli et al. focused on the risk of HPAI introduction in poultry exploiting species favourability maps, which indicate the potential habitat suitability where a species can be present. In contrast, our study examined the overall viral lineage spread across both wild and domestic hosts, using species’ abundance data. By not distinguishing between wild and domestic outbreaks, our approach may have captured different ecological signals, possibly explaining the weaker associations observed for some species. A comprehensive understanding of HPAI circulation among wild bird populations remains limited and calls for increased research efforts and targeted surveillance strategies (Burns et al. 2012). Reported cases likely represent only a fraction of the actual viral circulation within wild bird communities and tend to involve mainly some species (mostly Anseriformes and Charadriiformes), potentially overlooking others that may serve as bridge species of the infection. In fact, such species could play a critical role in maintaining local viral circulation, also facilitating its transmission to poultry (Caron et al. 2009, 2014, Shriner and Root 2020). Understanding the role of bridge species remains one of the key challenges in HPAI epidemiology, and further targeted investigations are needed to clarify the ecological roles of different species in HPAI spread and persistence. For the 2022–23 epidemic wave, we also identified a supported positive association between vegetation variables from the Plant Phenology Index (i.e. start and end of the growing season, and season length) and the dispersal location of viral lineages, suggesting that extended growing season—starting earlier and ending later—could increase habitat suitability for wild birds by providing food and shelter. These findings are consistent with previous studies (Si et al. 2010, Si et al. 2013), which found that plant availability and vegetation type influence HPAI occurrence in wild birds. Conversely, altitude and forest and semi-natural areas (CLC3) were identified as strong repelling factors in the 2021–22 and 2022–23 epidemics, possibly due to limited habitat suitability for waterfowl, which reduces opportunities for aggregation, and lower presence and density of poultry farms. These findings are further in line with previous studies (Si et al. 2010, Si et al. 2013, Min and Yoo 2023), which reported negative associations between altitude and HPAI outbreaks, possibly due to increased geographical isolation and local ecological conditions (Mannelli et al. 2006, Gilbert and Pfeiffer 2012). Overall, the estimated associations between environmental factors and HPAI viral lineage dispersal dynamics suggest that waves occurring in areas with ecological conditions favourable to wild bird presence, such as breeding and feeding habitats, may reflect incursions of avian influenza from wild birds into domestic poultry. In contrast, waves associated with ecological profiles are more strongly linked to human activities, or their proxies could be more consistent with patterns of farm-to-farm transmission.

This study presents a series of limitations that warrant consideration. First, the analyses were based solely on the HA gene segment, as using whole-genome sequences would have resulted in prohibitive computational demands in terms of runtime and memory usage. Moreover, despite the high genomic coverage achieved, some transmission events may have gone undetected due to missing sequences, particularly from wild birds, which are likely underrepresented in the dataset. For example, several outbreaks were also reported in geographically distant regions (Lazio, Friuli-Venezia Giulia, Emilia-Romagna, Puglia, and Campania), but the lack of available sequences from most of these areas precluded their inclusion in the phylogenetic analyses, potentially biasing the reconstruction of certain transmission pathways. All environmental variables were analysed in relation to both wild bird and poultry outbreaks combined, without distinguishing between the two populations, partly because the methods do not allow population-structured analyses. This prevented the identification of species-specific roles, such as potential reservoirs and bridge hosts, prompting for further investigation through host-level phylodynamic analyses. Our phylogeographic analyses were limited to univariate testing of environmental variables, preventing assessment of the combined effect of multiple environmental drivers. Additionally, there were some temporal mismatches between the available environmental variables and the epidemic wave periods, which may have reduced the inference precision. However, the temporally closest variable to those periods was selected to minimise this impact. Another caveat concerns the influence of sampling patterns on hypothesis testing related to the association between environmental factors and the viral lineage dispersal (Dellicour et al. 2019). Since these analyses focus on the environmental values extracted at the tree nodes, and more than half of these nodes are tip nodes whose locations are directly determined by the sampling effort, this hypothesis testing approach remains notably dependent on the sampling pattern. While the poultry dataset is broadly representative of the Italian epidemics, and wild bird surveillance has remained stable over the study period, which help limit potential bias, these results should still be interpreted with caution, considering the environmental context represented by the available sequences. It is important to note that surveillance in wild birds in Italy has predominantly relied on passive measures. Consequently, infections in asymptomatic or subclinically infected individuals, which are common within key host species in the multi-host and dynamic ecosystems that characterise avian influenza, may have gone undetected. This could potentially bias our understanding of viral circulation, leading to an underestimation of viral persistence or dispersal routes mediated by wild birds. Finally, as some key environmental variables were identified as factors positively associated with lineage dispersal locations, their integration into targeted surveillance strategies should be further explored to support early detection and risk-based prevention measures.

Similar patterns of shift from poultry-driven to wild bird-driven epidemics have been reported in other European countries, suggesting that the mechanisms sustaining HPAI persistence and spread are changing at a continental scale (Jung Kjær et al. 2023). The continuous evolution of HPAI ecology raises important questions about the effectiveness of current surveillance and control strategies, which need adjustment to address the changing dynamics of HPAI transmission and evolution. In this context, disentangling the drivers of viral spread in wild birds from those affecting virus introduction into poultry, and subsequent farm-to-farm transmission, represents a highly relevant and promising direction for future research, with important implications for both risk assessment and targeted control measures. For example, wild bird surveillance efforts currently rely on both passive mortality reporting and targeted active monitoring programmes on selected species. However, the latter are often costly and logistically complex, limiting their feasibility for large-scale or continuous application. To better address the increasing complexity of HPAI evolution, future efforts should focus on the development of complementary strategies, such as environmental sampling and standardized protocols and laboratory methods for virus detection in the environment. Building long-term capacities and partnerships with relevant stakeholders involved in active surveillance of HPAI viruses in wild birds will also be crucial to improving surveillance scheme to reflect more widespread occurrence of AI in non-waterfowl birds including large mortality in several colonial seabirds such as gannets, gulls and terns, and other diverse groups such as shorebirds, pelicans, spoonbills, cranes and geese, together with detections in raptors and carrion-feeding birds and a wide diversity of mammals. Additionally, a deeper understanding of wild bird ecology, including species distribution, movement patterns, and inter-species interactions, is needed to better anticipate high-risk scenarios and enable timely, targeted interventions.
