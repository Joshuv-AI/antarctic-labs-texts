The impact of perceived social support on anxiety and depression: the chain mediating effect of internet addiction and insomnia
Authors: Jiahui Guo, Weina Zhang, Changhong Jia, Xin Zhang, Libo Ai, Yan Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnxiety and depression as important factors affecting college students’ physical and mental health have received more and more social attention. Though a growing literature have confirmed significant correlations among perceived social support, Internet addiction, insomnia, anxiety and depressive symptom, there is a paucity of evidence focused on the sequential mediating roles of Internet addiction and insomnia in these relationships. This empirical study attempted to investigate the chain mediating role that Internet addiction and insomnia played together in the relationship between perceived social support and negative emotions in the population of college students.Methods3484 undergraduate students aged 18–23 were recruited with cluster sampling methods and investigated by the Perceived Social Support Scale, the Internet Addiction Test, the Insomnia Severity Index, the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 and the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 items. Data were analyzed with SPSS 26.0 and Amos 24.0.ResultsPerceived social support was negatively correlated with Internet addiction (r=-0.156, p < 0.01), insomnia (r=-0.217, p < 0.01), anxiety (r=-0.251, p < 0.01), and depression (r=-0.276, p < 0.01). In contrast, Internet addiction was positively associated with insomnia (r = 0.441, p < 0.01), anxiety (r = 0.474, p < 0.01), and depression (r = 0.518, p < 0.01). Furthermore, insomnia was positively linked with anxiety (r = 0.658, p < 0.01) and depression (r = 0.707, p < 0.01).ConclusionBoth Internet addiction and insomnia partially mediated the relationship between social support and negative emotions among college students. Internet addiction and insomnia played a chain mediating role in the relationship between perceived social support and negative emotions. These findings highlight the importance of enhancing social support, promoting healthy Internet use and improving sleep quality in interventions targeting mental health in young adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03195-w.

Introduction

In recent years, due to the impact of the epidemic, the economy has declined. Employment, academic pressure, family relationship, sleep disorders, Internet addiction, etc., all have become sources of anxiety and depression for college students. Persistent anxiety and depression are the reasons behind the occurrence of complications [1]. A cross-sectional study in Guangdong Province showed that the incidence rates of problematic Internet use, depression, anxiety, and stress among adolescents were 16.32%, 30.16%, 35.97%, and 18.80%, respectively [2]. College students are easily affected by anxiety and depression, which can lead to impaired learning status, reduced physical activity, increased risk of dropping out, heightened instances of unsafe sexual behavior, elevated levels of smoking, increased alcohol and drug addiction, and even increased risk of suicide [3].

Social support is the information of being loved, cared and valued [4], which can reduce negative emotions in response to unexpected events and improve overall mental health [5, 6]. This study follows the social support buffering model, which states that social support buffers and prevents various negative emotional experiences caused by stress, including anxiety and depression, while increasing positive emotional experiences [7]. Tinajero et al. showed that there are gender differences in social support [8]. Wan suggested that there is a significant difference in the subjective support dimension of social support by gender and that there is no significant difference in social support by grade level and whether the child is an only child or not [9]. Li suggested that, on the contrary, there is a difference in social support in terms of grade level, and there is no significant difference in terms of whether or not they have only one child [10]. Liao et al. showed that non-only children had higher levels of social support than only children [11]. The possible reason for this is that Liao was different from his study group, which was nursing college students. Wang suggested that there is a significant difference between the utilised dimension of social support and the subjective support dimension in terms of whether or not they are single parents [12]. In addition, social support is affected by psychological factors such as Internet addiction, insomnia, anxiety and depression. Studies have shown that perceived social support could help to alleviate anxiety, depression, and other negative psychological conditions [13, 14]. Sufficient social support contributes to lower level of Internet addiction [15]. Results of a meta-analysis show that social support is highly associated with insomnia [16].

The phenomenon of Internet addiction has received attention across various fields such as psychology and neuroscience after Young proposed it in 1996. Different words like “Internet addiction” and “compulsive Internet use” have been used to describe this problem [17]. With the progress of scientific research, it has been confirmed that Internet addiction shares the similarity to other addictive behaviors such as drug addiction and alcohol addiction. Prolonged Internet use activates the “reward center” of the brain, accounting for the release of dopamine, the source of happiness. The process builds tolerance in individuals, forcing them to pursue more stimuli to achieve the same level of excitement [18]. Therefore, researchers have defined Internet addiction as a problematic Internet use with excessive, uncontrollable, withdrawal symptoms and accompanied by physical and mental impairments [19]. Over the past three years of the epidemic, with more people forced to “stay at home” in a fixed area of activity, recreational activities have become monotonous. Increased time spent working and studying at home leads to increased access to the Internet, which objectively contributes to the phenomenon of Internet addiction [20, 21]. The Bio-Psycho-Social Model followed in this study emphasizes biological orientation, psychological factors, social factors, etc., to explain the causes of Internet addiction from multiple perspectives [22]. That is to say, Internet addiction gets a short-lived, high-intensity euphoria through the Internet and brings about a series of physiological and psychological changes. Psychologically, they are torn between euphoria and pain, and they are affected by interpersonal indifference in the social environment, which exacerbates Internet addiction and leads to increased social separation. Some studies have shown that Internet addiction is influenced by factors such as gender, grade level, whether single parent and whether only child [23, 24]. Mei et al. suggested that males and single-parent families had a greater number and degree of Internet addiction. Still, no significant effect was detected on the factor of being an only child [25]. In addition to this, Internet addiction is highly positively correlated with insomnia [26], anxiety [27], and depressive symptoms [28].

Insomnia is a sleep disorder that affects individuals’ daytime activities due to difficulty in falling asleep or maintaining, resulting in insufficient sleep duration or decreased sleep quality [29]. It is reflected in the disruption of work and study status, affecting physical and mental social functions, and increasing the risk of mental illness [30]. Influenced by uncontrollable factors, insomnia among college students is related to environmental isolation, anxiety, depression, and greater academic and employment pressure, and so on [31, 32]. Sleep disorders are often considered as an important problem or complication caused by Internet addiction, and could lead college students to spend more time online due to sleep problems [33]. As such, this has already become a critical public health issue in China [32]. Internet addiction is accompanied by chronic sleep deprivation and daytime sleepiness, as well as memory loss and decreased learning ability among college students, seriously influencing their academic performance, safety and health. Lucena et al. showed that women showed more insomnia symptoms compared to men [34]. However, the results of a polysomnographic study of insomniacs and healthy controls showed no significant difference in sleep quality by gender [35]. Zheng suggested significant differences in sleep quality by gender and grade [36]. In contrast, Xu suggested that none of the differences were significant [37]. Therefore, there is no uniform conclusion on whether there is a significant difference in insomnia in terms of gender and grade. In addition to gender and grade, insomnia was also associated with single-parent families [38] and only children, and the detection rate of insomnia was higher in non-only children [39].

Anxiety is an intense and persistent emotional state of worry and fear that arises when an individual is exposed to objective stimulate [40]. This emotional state is accompanied by palm sweating, lack of attention and inattention, which influence people’s external attention, thought processes, interpersonal relationships, and so on [41]. As a common mood disorder, depression is an emotional experience and one of the psychological problems people would face. The main clinical feature is persistent low mood, accompanied by negative emotions such as anxiety, fear, compulsion, and pessimism [42]. Teenagers suffering from anxiety or depression may experience various adverse consequences, such as academic difficulties, dropouts, maladaptations in social relationships, increased risk of drug abuse, and suicide [43]. The detection rates of mental health problems among university students, in descending order, were sleep problems (23.5%), depression (20.8%), anxiety (13.7%) [44]. Anxiety and depressive symptoms among college students were related to gender [45], grade level, whether they came from single-parent families [28] and whether they were only children, and the proportion of anxiety and depressive states was higher among college students who were only children than those who were not [46].

For the relationship between social support, Internet addiction, insomnia, anxiety, and depression, multiple studies have proposed that Internet addiction is positively correlated with social support and depression, while social support is negatively correlated with depression. Internet addiction mediates the relationship between social support and depression. In addition, Internet addiction was significantly associated with the prevalence of major depressive symptoms and excessive daytime sleepiness, while pathological Internet use patterns were associated with individuals with lower levels of social support [47]. In addition, another study also showed that Internet addiction was directly associated with depression and indirectly predicted depression through the knock-on mediating effect of social support and sleep quality among college students [48].

Based on the above, it seems necessary to explore the influencing factors affecting college students’ anxiety and depression symptoms, as well as to propose the prevention and interventions. Currently, most of the related studies analyze the relationship between two or three of these factors, and less often discuss the relationship between the four. In terms of demographic variables, it was not possible to draw uniform conclusions as to whether there was a correlation between social support and grade level, being an only child, Internet addiction, insomnia and gender, and grade level. Therefore, gender, grade level, whether or not a single-parent family, and whether or not an only child were included in this study to test whether these four demographic variables affect perceived social support, Internet addiction, insomnia, anxiety, and depressive symptoms. With college students’ anxiety and depression symptoms as the dependent variable and comprehending social support as the independent variable, two mediating variables, Internet addiction M1 and insomnia M2, were added to innovatively explore the interlocking mediating roles of Internet addiction and insomnia in the effects of perceived social support on anxiety and depression in terms of the content of the study. The specific pathways of the effect of perceived social support on anxiety and depression were determined by constructing and testing a chain mediation model (Fig. 1). Demographic variables were analysed for differences through independent samples t-tests and one-way ANOVA. In addition, the study aims to investigate the correlations between variables and explore whether Internet addiction and insomnia act as chain mediators between perceived social support and anxiety and depression symptoms. By examining the relationships between these variables, we hope to identify the factors that influence negative emotions in college students, thereby providing richer ideas for preventing anxiety and depression.Thus, we predicted the following:

Hypothesis 1

Differences in perceived social support, Internet addiction, insomnia, anxiety, and depressive symptoms among college students by gender, grade level, single parent or not, and only child or not.

Hypothesis 2

A two-by-two correlation between perceived social support, Internet addiction, insomnia, anxiety and depression.

Hypothesis 3

Perceived social support not only directly predicted college students’ anxiety and depression symptoms, but also indirectly predicted college students’ anxiety and depression symptoms through the separate partially mediated effects of Internet addiction and insomnia, as well as indirectly predicted anxiety and depression symptoms through the chain mediation of Internet addiction and insomnia.

Methods

The methodology of this study is a correlation study of path analysis types. In two universities in Hebei Province, a cluster sampling method was used to distribute questionnaires to the college students. The reasons for adopting this sampling method for its efficiency in organization and reduced demand on manpower. A total of 4845 questionnaires were distributed, and after exclusion and screening, a total of 3484 valid questionnaires were obtained, with an effective response rate of 71.91%. Selection criteria were students of Tangshan Polytechnic University and North China University of Science and Technology, aged 18 to 23 years old, with exclusion criteria being inability to answer the questionnaire in its entirety, and a self-reported history of mental disorders. The demographic variables include gender, grade, whether it is a single parent family, and whether it is an only child. The scale used in this study was released through the Questionnaire Star platform(https://www.wjx.cn/)and was answered using a mobile client. In order to obtain informed consent from the participants, it was briefly explained to them that the data collected would be used for research purposes only. If participants agreed to participate in this survey, they answered the questions according to the questionnaire guidelines; otherwise, they clicked on the opt-out link. The study was conducted in accordance with the guiding principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, informed consent was obtained from the participants for all data collected, and approval was obtained from the Medical Ethics Committee of the North China University of Science and Technology (protocol code sdnu-2021037).

Measures

Perceived Social Support Scale (PSSS)

Based on the Multidimensional Social Support Scale (MSPSS) developed by Zimet et al. in 1988 [49], Jiang et al. translated, revised and developed the Perceived Social Support Scale (PSSS) in 1999 [50]. This study used the Chinese version of the Perceived Social Support Scale, which has been widely used in previous research [51–54]. In the measurement of Chinese adolescents’ perceived social support, the scale showed good reliability and validity, with Cronbach’s α of 0.927. And Cronbach’s α of 0.818, 0.875, and 0.846 for the three dimensions, respectively [55]. The Perceived Social Support Scale consists of 12 items, including three dimensions of perceived support from family (items 3, 4, 8, 11), friends (items 6, 7, 9, 12), and significant others (items 1, 2, 5, 10). It adopts a 7-level scoring method (1 point = “strongly disagree”, 7 points = “strongly agree”). The higher the total score, the higher the individual’s subjective perception of social support. 12–36 points indicate low level of perceived social support, while 37–60 points indicate a moderate level, and 61–84 points indicate a high level of perceived social support. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the total scale of perceived social support was 0.966, and the Cronbach’s α coefficients for the three dimensions were 0.926, 0.945, and 0.915.

Internet Addiction Test (IAT)

The Internet Addiction Test (IAT) is based on the 8-item Internet Addiction Screening Scale compiled by Young in 1996 with reference to the American Classification and Diagnostic Manual of Mental Disorders IV, including 12 new items and a total of 20 items. Each item is scored at a 5-level. The total score range of this scale is 20–100, and the higher the score, the higher the degree of Internet addiction [56]. This study used the Chinese version of the Internet Addiction Test, which has been used in Chinese studies of influencing factors and interventions on the Internet [26, 57], and the relationship between Internet addiction and anxiety [58]. In a study of Internet addiction among adolescents in Wuhan, China, the Clonbach α coefficient for this questionnaire was 0.759 [59]. More than 50 points indicates to have Internet addiction [22]. The Cronbach’s ɑ reliability coefficient in this study is 0.946.

Insomnia Severity Index (ISI)

The Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) was developed by Morin in 1993 [60], with an evaluation period of less than two weeks. This study used the Chinese version of the Insomnia Severity Index, which is used in a study on the improvement of insomnia in Chinese clinics with low impedance ideation therapy [61] and toning and tranquillising acupressure techniques [62]. The results of a reliability study of the Insomnia Severity Index Scale for college students in Guangzhou showed that the Clonbach’s α coefficient was 0.843 [63]. The higher the score, the severer degree of insomnia. It consists of 7 items with 4-level scoring system and a total score range of 0–28 points. Less than 7 points indicates no insomnia. From 8 to 15 points indicates sub insomnia. From 16 to 21 points indicates insomnia. More than 21 points indicate severe insomnia [64]. The Cronbach’s α reliability coefficient of this scale in this study is 0.905.

Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7)

The 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7) was developed by Spitzer in 2006 [65] to assess the level of individual’s anxiety level. The Chinese version of the Generalised Anxiety Disorder Scale was applied in this study. This version of the scale also been emplyed in a longitudinal study to investigatethe relationship between Internet addiction and anxiety [27], as well as in study on anxiety and the inability to tolerate uncertainty among Chinese university students [66]. In a study examining the relationship between anxiety, perceived stress and tolerance among college students in Guangdong Province, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of this scale was 0.94, with good reliability and validity [67].The total score range from 7 to 28 points, with 7 points indicating no anxiety and 28 indicating severe anxiety level. Previous research has shown this scale could effectively evaluate the anxiety level of college students [68]. The Cronbach’s α of this scale in this study is 0.947, confirming its strong reliability.

Pacient Health Questionnaire-9 items Scale (PHQ-9)

PHQ-9 has 9 items to evaluate the level of individual’s depression within 2 weeks [69]. The Chinese version of the PHQ9 was used in this study, which has good reliability in the Chinese population [70]. A study conducted to investigate the relationship between daytime sleepiness and depression in three Guangdong universities, this scale showed Cronbach’s α of 0.86, 0.86, 0.89 and 0.89 across four measurement points, indicating stable internal consistency [71]. The total score ranges from 9 to 36 points, with 9 indicating no depression, and 36 indicating severe depression. This scale was confirmed can effectively measure the depression status among college students [68]. The Cronbach’s α of this scale in this study is 0.916.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 and AMOS 24.0 [72]. The normality of the quantitative data was determined by kurtosis, skewness and P-P plot data trend. Descriptive statistics of demographic variables were examined using independent sample t-test and one-way analysis of variance. Pearson product difference correlation was computed to explore the relationship among the variables. A structural equation model was constructed in Amos 24.0 and Model 6 was selected using the PROCESS macro. Samples were repeated 5000 times, and a default 95% confidence interval was used to test the chain mediating effect of Internet addiction and insomnia.

Common Method Bias Testing

This study used a questionnaire survey for data collection and Harman’s one-factor test was conducted to test common method bias. The results showed six factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, with the variance contribution rate of the first principal component accounting for 34.01%, indicating that there was no common method bias in this study. In addition, the test results of kurtosis, skewness and P-P plot showed that the data followed a normal distribution.

Discussion

Differences in Perceived Social Support, Internet Addiction, Insomnia, Anxiety and Depression Across Demographic Variables

Through independent samples t-test, this study found significant gender differences in perceived social support [8], with female students reporting higher level of perceived social support than male students [73]. This may be attributed to different roles of female and male, wherein females are often socialized to express emotions and seek help more openly, while males are encouraged to be independent and less self-disclosing. Notably, only social support from friends and significant others in this study showed statistically significant gender differences, which differs from Wan’s finding-perceived social support exhibited significant gender differences [9]. In addition, perceived social support did not vary significantly by grade level or only-child status, which is consistent with Wan’s study [9]. Furthermore, this study found that the dimensions of family support and friend support differed significantly by single-parent status, which constracts with Wan’s finding-utilization of support and subjective support were the dimensions showing significant differences [12]. These discrepancies may be partly explained by sociocultural gender role expectations [8].

Regarding Internet addiction, the results revealed significant differences by gender and only-child status [23, 24] with females reporting higher scores on perceived social support than males. This finding distinguishes from Mei’s study, which reported no significant difference in internet use between only-child and non-only-child groups [25]. No significant differences were observed in internet adediction with respect to grade level or single-parent family status.

For depression, female students scored significantly higher than male students, and there were differences in the core symptom network between males and females [74]. At the academic level, the results showed that higher grade levels were associated with increased anxiety among college students [28]. As students progressed to sophomore and junior years, academic demands and workload became heavier compared to freshmen [75]. Lower academic satisfaction scores were significantly correlated with depression and anxiety [76].

In terms of family background, students from single-parent families reported lower perceived social support but higher levels of insomnia, anxiety and depression compared with those from intact families [52]. Children in two-parent households tend to adopt positive coping strategies, exhibit stronger adaptability to the environment, and have better skills in managing and regulating negative emotions with greater ease [77]. Conversely, students from non-single-child families scored higher in Internet addiction, insomnia, anxiety and depression compared to single-child families [78]. Only-child students were more likely to receive sufficient care and attention from their original families without concerns about sharing emotional resources with siblings, which potentially leading to better parent-child relationship [79]. Due to the limited resources provided by the family for growth, only child may hold an advantage in terms of emotional and material resources, which in turn positively reinforces their mental health. Indeed, only-child adolescents demonstrate better better mental health than non-only-child adolescents [80].

Chain mediation effect

The results of this study showed that both Internet addiction and insomnia could partially mediated the relationship between perceived social support and anxiety or depression among college students. Specifically, perceived social support exerted not only a direct effect on anxiety and depression, but also indirect effects through the sequential mediating roles of Internet addiction and insomnia [48, 81].

Furthermore, this study verified a significant negative correlation between perceived social support and anxiety. Participants with higher levels of perceived social support reportedlower levels of anxiety and depression [82, 83]. Perceived social support plays a crucial role in maintaining and regulating mental health, buffering individuals against negative emotions caused by external adverse events. Those with higher level of perceived social support tend to receive unconditional and positive attention from their families [17], experience harmonious interpersonal relationships, receive positive social evaluations, and exhibit more mature and stable personalities. On the contrary, individuals with lower level of perceived social support would be more prone to experience negative emotions such as anxiety, depression, and helplessness when faced with setbacks and failures due to conditional love and unstable attention. To cope, they may adopt maladaptive defense mechanisms, such as lowering self-esteem to reduce the needs for social support, leading to loss of the confidence and initiative in seeking for social support.

The study also found a significant negative correlation between perceived social support and Internet addiction. In another words, students with higher level of perceived social support were less prone to problematic Internet use [15]. According to the social compensation hypothesis, individuals with high level of perceived social support are more likely to perceive attention and care from others. Unburdened interpersonal relationships satisfy their sense of security and emotional experience, which encouraging them to focus on safe exploration in the real world. In contrast, individuals with insufficient perceived social support, lacking stable affection and unconditional support, may compensate by turning to the Internet as a substitute to establish emotional fulfilling relationship that are absent in their real lives. Therefore, low level perceived social support reduces their sense of security in the real world, prompting them to rely on virtual platforms such as short video apps or online games to express themselves and seek a sense of value. By viewing the Internet as a “good medicine” to escape from reality and compensate for the sense of deficiency in reality, these individuals would be more likely to experience self-doubt and may easily become addicted to the Internet through repeated reinforcement [84]. However, the temporary and illusory sense of happiness gained from Internet could not solve underlying real-life problems. Once back to reality, individuals often experience heightened anxiety and depression. Another results confirmed that Internet addiction was positively correlated with anxiety and depression. The more severe the degree of Internet addiction [20], the higher the levels of anxiety and depression [81, 85, 86]. Adolescence often seek recognition from others in this period, being afraid of rejection and competition failure, some students become more addicted to their own world and refuse to actively interact with others due to the reasons like family relationship or external environmental factors. When their spiritual needs can’t be satisfied in the real world, individuals who are more prone to engage in Internet addiction would seek refuge in the virtual world. Yet the transient pleasure brought by avoidance cannot offset the frustration and helplessness of real-life struggles, so thus intensifying anxiety and depression [87]. Additionally, surfing the Internet has become a part of their lives, college students who suffer from Internet addiction may even blur the boundary between the virtual and real world and feel empty, anxious and unease when unable to access the Internet. Consistent with Dong’s research, this study showed that Internet addiction plays a partial mediating role between perceived social support and anxiety and depression [47]. Notably, Dong et al. reported that such mediation emerged primarily at higher levels of psychological resilience.

Beyond the perceived social support → Internet addiction → anxiety/depression pathway, perceived social support also affected anxiety and depression through insomnia. That is, this study supported the pathway of perceived social support → insomnia → anxiety/depression pathway. Results showed a negative correlation between perceived social support and insomnia, indicating that individuals with higher level of perceived social supporters experienced fewer insomnia symptoms. In particular, adequate social support an individual perceived was associated with fewer night awakening and better sleep quality [82, 88]. Sleep quality, however, is influenced by many psychological and physiological factors, including physical conditions, emotional states, life events, and environmental contexts. When individuals lack material and spiritual support to cope with life challenges and emergencies, their sense of sleep safety decreases, making them more prone to anxiety and depression [88]. According to the social buffering model, social support plays a dual role in maintaining health and preventing diseases [7]. Adequate support can mitigates the impact of stressful life events, maintain physical and mental health, enhance adaptability to life stressors, and reduce negative impact on sleep quality. Consistent with this, this study found that insomnia was positively correlated withanxiety and depression. Individuals with greater insomnia usually experienced higher level of anxiety and depression [89]. Poor sleep impaired individual’s emotional regulation, making insomnia an important risk factor for anxiety and depression. Among college students, irregular schedule and lifestyle habits leads to delayed bedtimes, insufficient sleep and sleep deprivation. This, in turn, reduced positive emotional experience, impaired concentrate, decreased in academic performance, and negative evaluations from themselves and others, further exacerbating negative emotions. From the psychoanalytic perspective, insomnia may reflect inner conflict, and when anxiety caused by this conflict is not appropriately handled, insomnia may occur.

This study also found that Internet addiction was significantly and positively related to insomnia. In other words, individuals with higher level of Internet addiction were more prone to suffer from insomnia [85, 89]. In the era of informatization, college students rely heavily on the Internet for study, daily life and entertainment. However, lack of self-control often leads to excessive use, manifesting in delayed bedtime and shortened sleep duration at night [90]. There is no doubt that such a bad habit imposes a great burden on individual’s sleep quality. Short videos and different games provided by mobile phones, tablets and computers provide people with sensory excitement. Prior research has showed that adolescents’ use of online media during nighttime correlated with their poor sleep quality [91]. What’s more, Internet addiction has been found to be contagious among peers [92]. On the basis of the negative predictive effect of perceived social support on Internet addiction, users with high level of Internet addiction often become obsessed with their phones due to the strong interest they developed in the online world, increasing their time of use. In addition, the blue light emitted by electronic devices such as phones could reduce their desire for sleep [93]. This light may interfere with the melatonin secretion, thereby inhibiting and delaying sleep, and significantly increase the level of insomnia physiologically [94]. Staying up late and spending excessive time online leads to a serious lack of sleep time. Insomnia negatively impact on an individual’s immune function, emotional state, and cognitive process. It manifested as physical and mental exhaustion, difficulty concentrating, diminished interest in learning, reduced interaction with others, deteriorating interpersonal relationships, and exacerbated anxiety and depression. Ji et al. demonstrated that Internet addiction, insomnia, and depression are highly interrelated. However, their study focused primarily on the indirect effect of Internet addiction on insomnia through depression, using depression as a mediating variable to explore the relationship among the three factors [95]. In contrast, the present study expolored the effects of perceived social support on anxiety and depression through the chain-mediating roles of Internet addiction and insomnia. The level of perceived social support affects an individual’s degree of Internet addiction, which in turn indirectly influence anxiety and depression through insomnia. This aligns with the pathway: perceived social support → Internet addiction → insomnia → anxiety and depression.

Conclusion

In conclusion, as the results showed, perceived social support was significantly and negatively correlated with Internet addiction, insomnia, anxiety and depression. Conversely, Internet addiction showed positive correlations with insomnia, anxiety and depression. Mediation analyses further revealed that perceived social support contributed to emotional well-being indirectly through Internet addiction and insomnia. Specifically, higher level of perceived social support was associated with better psychological outcomes, while internet addiction and insomnia were related to poorer emotional states. Moreover, perceived social support was found to influence anxiety and depression through the sequential mediating effects of Internet addiction and insomnia.

By constructing a chain mediation model involving perceived social support, Internet addiction, insomnia, anxiety and a depression, this study provided important implications for interventions addressing anxiety and depression among college students in China. Firstly, perceived social support emerged as key predictor of emotional health. Students with lower levels of perceived social support are more vulnerable to anxiety and depressive symptoms. Therefore, in the actual prevention and intervention work should prioritize identifying such students, cultivating their interpersonal skills and encouraging them to actively seek support from family, friends, and significant others. Secondly, attention was paid to demographic variations, such as gender and single-parent family status in perceived social support; grade level and only-child status in internet addiction and psychological distress. Thirdly, the confirmed mediating roles of Internet addiction and insomnia highlighting the importance of behavioral and lifestyle factors in intervention. Therefore, in addition to fostering stronger perceived social support, students should be guided to develop healthier internet use habits, improve sleep quality, and challenge irrational self-belief. Active participation in social and extracurricular activities can also strengthen real-world sources of support, reduce reliance on transient online gratifications, and promote more sustainable mental well-being.

Limitations

Firstly, there is a lack of objective data on sleep quality, self-reported scale may cause bias in the estimation of participants’ sleep duration and sleep patterns. Moreover, individuals have varying expectations on sleep. For those with lower sleep expectations may still experience positive mental state and be less vulnerable to get affected by negative emotional distress caused by insomnia, even when their sleep duration falls below the normative range. Secondly, the Perceived Social Support Questionnaire used in this study was unable to distinguish between real and online sources of perceived social support, which may limit the precision of the findings. Finally, given the consideration of cross-sectional design, causal relationships among the variables cannot be established. The findings suggest associations only, without clarifying directionality or temporal effects. Future longitudinal or experimental studies are needed to confirm the causal pathways observed. Additionally, future research should consider integrating targeted methodological improvements and more refined data-collection strategies to better support the translation of research findings into practice.
