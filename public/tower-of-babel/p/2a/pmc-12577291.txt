Measuring empowerment to address chronic diseases in mexico: validation of the american short form patient activation measure (PAM13)
Authors: Miguel A González-Block, Emilio Gutiérrez-Calderón, Hortensia Reyes-Morales, Joel Rodríguez-Saldana, Sugeidi Avendaño-Villavicencio, Sandra Patricia Díaz-Portillo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe empowerment of persons facing chronic conditions in Mexico can support health care in the context of vertical and fragmented institutions. The American short form Patient Activation Measure (PAM) is a 13-item instrument designed to assess patient knowledge, skills and confidence for self-management. PAM-13 was translated into Mexican Spanish, its psychometric properties were established for both sick and healthy persons, and its external validity assessed against health service utilization.MethodsThe translation was carried out according to WHO guidelines. The PAM 13-Mexico was applied in a nationally representative household survey to healthy persons and to persons living with diabetes and hypertension, equally divided within a sample of 2,400 persons. The instrument was also applied to an opportunistic sample of 136 women living with breast cancer in two large cities. Internal consistency and cross-validation against care variables were computed. Results were compared to similar data for other high and upper middle-income countries.ResultsThe psychometric properties of the PAM 13-Mexico were generally good. The level of internal consistency is high (α = 0.88; McDonald’s omega coefficient = 0.88) and item-rest correlations are moderate to strong. The Mexican mean PAM score (77.1) is greater than the American (61.9) and the Danish (64.2). The association with health service utilization and quality was weak to strong.ConclusionsThe PAM-13 Mexico is a reliable instrument to measure empowerment of persons to address chronic diseases. The validity of PAM-13 needs to be researched against a wider set of health care variables and with diverse populations.Key words: Empowerment; Validation; Chronic disease; Mexico; Activation

Background

Empowerment according to Paulo Freire is the capacity of people to critically address their reality and discover how they can participate in its transformation [1]. For Amartya Sen empowerment is the excercise of agency defined as the freedom people enjoy attaining desired objectives [2]. The World Health Organization defines empowerment as the process by which persons understand their role and attain the knowledge and skills to perform health-seeking behaviors within an environment that recognizes community and cultural differences and that encourages participation [3]. Judith Hibbard and collaborators operationalized empowerment as the activation of persons towards self-management of their care in collaborating with providers, and for maintaining health [4]. The empowerment of persons living with illness has been found to improve treatment outcomes, patient satisfaction and health, as well as service provider productivity [5],

In Mexico, chronic diseases represent the main public health problem, with persons over 20 years of age reporting in 2023 a prevalence of diabetes in 18.4%, and of hypertension in 29.9% [6],[7]. The incidence of cancer in Mexico for the same year was 150 cases per 100,000 inhabitants, with breast cancer standing out among women at 46 and prostate cancer among men at 44 per 100,000 inhabitants [8]. The population with diabetes tend to normalize and accept low quality health services and frequently abandon treatment [9] and, among people living with breast, cervical and prostate cancer, a low level of comprehension of survival prevails, understood as simply being alive, as a confirmation of the end of treatment, or as the attainment of a cure [10].

Community interventions for the self-measurement of blood pressure levels have been found to be cost-effective among persons living with hypertension in the USA [11],[12] and for the prevention of strokes in Thailand [13]. Moreover, activation through educational and empowerment strategies has also proven effective for diabetes control in 10 Latin American countries [14]. In the context of cancer, empowerment is especially relevant given the importance of early detection for survival and adherence to improved treatments, as well as the importance of patient participation in facing the physical and mental challenges that intensive treatment and surveillance implies for prolonged periods of time [15].

Angelmar and Bermann separate empowerment in four components: the understanding of the patient role, knowledge acquisition to interact with provideers, patient skills, and a facilitating environment [16]. Following a similar approach, Hibbard et al. [17],[18] developed and optimized the Patient Activation Measure (PAM) consisting of a 13-item instrument to assess the person’s self-reported knowledge, skills and confidence for self-management of health conditions as a reliable and valid measure capable of segmenting consumers based on their capacity to engage in productive health behaviors. Knowledge, skills and confidence indicators enables PAM-13 to measure the range of factors that have proven to be critical to change patient behavior through self-management involving the family as members of the care team [4].

PAM classifies persons into one of four increasingly higher activation levels according to the association of knowledge, skills and confidence indicators based on a measurement model calibrated to increasingly hard-to-attain health behaviors [4]. In the first stage, activation involves understanding the patient’s role and acquiring basic knowledge about their health condition and its treatment. This stage involves areas such as knowledge of medications and lifestyle changes that are necessary to cope with the disease and prevent its complications, as well as the belief that active participation in health care is important. In the second stage of activation, patients gain confidence to identify when medical care is needed, follow treatment recommendations, and manage symptoms on their own. Later stages of activation include the ability to maintain necessary lifestyle changes, to have confidence to handle new situations or problems, and to prevent chronic diseases from interfering with one’s life [17].

Studies across diverse countries and health conditions have shown that a higher PAM-13 score is positively associated with preventive care and healthy lifestyle behaviors, information seeking and use, healthcare use and outcomes, quality of the patient-provider relationships and self-management, and health literacy [18–21]. Activation is associated with improved self-esteem, greater knowledge of the disease and its treatment, greater self-efficacy in relation to the disease and treatments, and greater perception of control over treatments. Activation is also associated with better interaction and collaboration with health care teams, better self-management of the disease, greater emotional control and, more generally, higher levels of satisfaction, better health status and better quality of life.

A research project was performed in Mexico to translate the American short form Patient Activation Measure (PAM 13) [18] into a Mexican Spanish version, to establish the psychometric properties of the Mexican version; and to validate the Mexican version of the PAM in a panel of persons living with hypertension and diabetes and breast cancer as well as among health persons needing to address health promotion and disease prevention. The external validity of the Mexico PAM 13 was assessed by analyzing the association between activation and key health service utilization variables.

Methods

PAM 13 translation and adaptation process

Translation and adaptation of the PAM 13 followed systematic procedures including forward and backward translation, expert panel meeting to assess the translation, pre-testing, and consensus about the final version. Two independent Mexican translators performed the forward translation. The translators, along with two researchers with an expertise in chronic care and research in chronic care, met as an expert panel to resolve discrepancies between the translations. The instrument was translated back into English, finding minor discrepancies between the original and the backward translations. The instrument was found to be fit-for-purpose among both ill and healthy individuals. Pre-testing of PAM 13 was done in a sample of 20 persons living with diabetes, hypertension, breast cancer, and healthy adults. Analysis of results did not lead to significative changes to the instrument.

Survey methods

The PAM 13-Mexican Spanish was applied through a stratified random household survey of urban populations representative at the national level to samples of 800 adults living in each case with hypertension, diabetes or not reporting any chronic health condition, for a total of 2,400 individuals. An additional sample of 136 women living with breast cancer was surveyed in the premises of Fundación Salvati AC, a not-for-profit health care provider offering survivorship support to women ascribed mostly to public health care institutions in Mexico City and Guadalajara, the two largest cities in the country. The survey was applied between July and August 2023.

To select individuals for the application of the household survey, adults were initially asked to respond if they had received a prior diagnosis among a list of 14 chronic diseases, including “other”. The diseases listed were: tuberculosis, asthma, chronic bronchitis or emphysema, heart disease, rheumatic fibrosis, stroke, arthritis, osteoarthritis, gastritis or gastric ulcer, colitis, kidney disease, depression or anxiety, cancer or tumors other than breast, and HIV/AIDS. Those responding positively to either hypertension or diabetes were selected and if they responded positively to both diseases they were assigned randomly to one group. Persons with no prior diagnosis of any of the 14 chronic diseases were selected for the healthy group.

The research protocol and survey instruments were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Health Sciences of Anahuac University, with the approval number 202229. Signed informed consent was requested for each participant.

PAM level

Patient activation was measured using the PAM 13- Mexican Spanish, which consists of 13 items (Table 1). Categories are answered in a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from totally disagree to totally agree. The instrument measures four stages of activation in a progressing difficulty according to the order of the items: level 1 (patients believe that their role is important; items 1 and 2), level 2 (patients have confidence and knowledge to take action; items 3–8), level 3 (taking action; items 9–11) and level 4 (staying on course under stress; items 12 and 13) [18]. PAM scores were produced by Insignia Health through a proprietary methodology where the Rasch model is applied to convert the original data into scores with a continuous linear behavior through a process that involves the Logit transformation and then rescaled to a user-friendly range of 0 to 100 (0 = lowest activation, 100 = highest activation).

Participant socioeconomic, demographic and health insurance status were measured as background variables. Education was divided in six categories; (illiterate, primary education, junior high-school; high-school, university studies, and postgraduate education.

Psychometric properties

To establish the psychometric properties of the PAM 13-Mexican Spanish the data quality and internal consistency of the instrument were assessed. For data quality, the mean (with standard deviation) and median were assessed. Internal consistency was measured as the Cronbach’s α, the McDonald’s omega coefficient and using the inter-item and item-rest correlations (Pearson’s r). Item-rest correlations are the correlations between an item and the scale that is formed by all other items. Following Rademakers et al. [17], correlation ≥ 0.50 is considered strong, r ≥.30 moderate and r ≥.10 weak. The distribution of levels of activation and mean overall scores in the sample were described by gender, age, educational level and access to health insurance.

Construct validity

The questionnaire was constructed to measure the association of PAM 13 with health care access and quality of care for adults with prior diagnosis of hypertension, diabetes, and breast cancer, and adults in the general population without these diagnoses (Table 2). All participants were asked about quality of interactions and participation, and the satisfaction of health rights. Persons with prior diagnoses were asked to report the time elapsed between diagnosis and treatment, and participation in educational and support groups. Persons with diagnoses of hypertension and diabetes were asked about common complications for each disease and hospitalization and emergency medical service use were assessed [22],[23]. Persons with hypertension were asked the frequency of measurement of blood pressure and persons with diabetes were asked regarding access to 12 interventions to prevent secondary complications [24]. Access, quality and support variables were categorized, numerical values were assigned to each category with the higher number for positive responses, and scales were standardized in 0-100 scale.

Health care access, utilization and support factors included for all groups were the following: contact with a health provider by type of resource and institution during the last year; ease of access; perceived quality of the provider-patient relationship; access to information regarding health rights and compliance with same; support received by third parties; action taken with regard to unsatisfied demand; knowledge of medical history; and support by family in seeking care and with domestic activities. In the case of persons living with a chronic disease the survey included the following questions: time elapsed between first symptoms, preliminary diagnosis, confirmatory diagnosis and entry to treatment; access to support and educational groups; topics addressed in the support and education sessions; and satisfaction with the support and education received. The analyses of independent variables were performed separating groups of persons according to health situation and integrating the subsamples.

The associations between the PAM level and measures of health care utilization, access and support were analyzed through Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), assessing significance.

The PAM scale was processed by Insignia and basic scale properties were assessed by fitting Rasch Rating Scale Models.

Analyses were performed with IBM-SPSS V.27 and R packages, mainly “eRm”, to assess the Rasch model fitted.

Results

Participants

Household survey

In this study 2 400 questionnaires were completed, 800 for each of persons with a prior diagnosis of hypertension or diabetes and for those not reporting a previous chronic disease diagnosis. A total of 211 individuals had a prior diagnosis of both diseases (Table 3). Average age for persons with hypertension was 52.7 years (51.8–53.6), for those with diabetes 53.4 years (52.5–54.3), and for those not reporting chronic diseases 38.3 years (37.3–39.2). Females corresponded with 56.5% of the sample with hypertension, 57.5% with diabetes and 55% among the healthy individuals. The diabetes and hypertension groups showed no significant differences between them in terms of education, access to health insurance or employment status but had lower education levels and lower access to health insurance than the breast cancer and healthy groups. Women with breast cancer had significantly higher education and access to health insurance than the other groups, and healthy persons were more frequently economically active than persons living with a chronic disease.

Data quality was assessed by establishing the mean PAM score (with standard deviation) and the median. By design, the questionnaire did not allow for missing data. Overall, the mean scores on the items varied between 3.42 and 3.67. In general, the last items have a lower mean score compared to the earlier items in the questionnaire.

Internal consistency was measured as the Cronbach’s α, the McDonald’s omega coefficient and inter-item correlations. Cronbach’s α and the McDonald’s omega coefficient for the sum scale was 0.88. This Cronbach’s α is like the Danish and Dutch (0.89, 0.88) [19],[20] and, along with McDonald’s omega, can be considered a good level of internal consistency. The inter-item correlations ranged from 0.20 to 0.54 (mean = 0.36). Item-rest correlations per item to the sum scale were moderate (for item 1) to strong (for all other items) and varied between 0.41 and 0.62.

A factor analysis was performed on the PAM-13 items to identify underlying dimensions and provide evidence for construct validity. Using principal components, two factors with eigenvalues > 1 were identified, explaining 49.8% of the total variance. Following varimax orthogonal rotation, two distinct item groups emerged. Items 1–7 (excluding item 5) formed one group, while items 5 and 8–13 formed the second. This natural grouping is consistent with expectations: one group where most of participants adheres, and another where a smaller proportion adheres.

Construct validity

To establish construct validity, associations between the PAM 13-Mexican Spanish levels and access, quality and support indicators were examined. PAM level showed no significant association with quality of hypertension care, complications from hypertension or diabetes, nor with stigma and discrimination (Table 7). No associations were found for each of the variables of the indicators of complications for hypertension and diabetes, except for loss of vision in the case of diabetes, where persons with PAM level 1 had significantly higher frequencies than the persons with higher activation. For all other indicators, groups with higher activation were associated with positive access, quality and support indicators. Associations were particularly strong for more timely access to diagnosis and treatment, access to interdisciplinary care, quality of interactions with providers and quality of participation in care decisions. PAM level was also strongly associated with quality of diabetes care, the perception of satisfaction of health rights by provider institutions and, in the case of those insured, utilization of their assigned providers.

Discussion

The PAM-13 Mexican Spanish is a reliable instrument to measure patient activation. The level of internal consistency is good and item-rest correlations are moderate to strong. Compared to the data for other countries, the internal consistency as measured by Cronbach’s α (and in some cases also by the McDonald omega coefficient) is comparable in the Mexican sample. Though the individual item sequence in our study did not match exactly the original US scale, in general the progressing difficulty of the items is reflected in the mean scores. Further validation in other Mexican population samples and more specific statistical (Rasch) analyses will have to confirm whether the sequence of the original US scale will require readjustment in Mexico. For the ongoing validation of the PAM-13, it is also suggested that future studies assess the instrument’s sensitivity to detect real and significant longitudinal changes in the latent trait (activation) at individual or group levels. Such evaluations are essential when determining the effectiveness of interventions or training programs.

The Mexican Spanish mean PAM score of 77.1 is higher than the USA (61.9), Danish (64.2) and Dutch (61.3) scores [18–20]. The results for Mexico were higher than expected given lower levels of health insurance and health service access and particularly given the low participation and choice that persons are afforded within vertically integrated institutions. This contrasts with the greater educational levels and the role given to patient choice and empowerment in comparator countries, particularly in the Netherlands and in the USA [25],[26].

The construct validity of the PAM-13 Mexican Spanish was assessed by examining the cross-validation with the score levels for access, quality of care and support data. The association between the PAM and variables was moderate to strong, except for the frequency of measurement of blood pressure in the case of persons living with hypertension. Access to interventions with proven benefits for the prevention of complications from diabetes was strongly associated with PAM levels. Furthermore, PAM levels were strongly associated with the report of compliance by institution with the persons’ expectations regarding their health rights. However, it is not possible in this cross-sectional study to establish cause and effect between the two sets of variables. Activation levels are associated with most of the access, quality and support indicators studies, but it may also be the case that institutional compliance and improved quality lead to higher levels of activation. Therefore, further studies with the PAM 13-Mexican Spanish are required to establish how activation is specifically associated with access, quality and support indicators.

The association of the PAM-13 Mexican Spanish with access to interdisciplinary care and with improved quality indicators, particularly in the case of persons with diabetes, is in line with other studies. Mosen et al. [27] found in a study conducted in the United States that each increase in the PAM score was associated with improvements in self-management behaviors and medication adherence as well as with satisfaction. When compared to level 1 of the PAM, participants with a score at level 4 were approximately 3 times more likely to report high medication adherence. Likewise, compared to level 1, those at level 4 had the highest score for a self-management behavior index. Hibbard [17],[18] found also for the United States that persons with high activation are significantly more likely to exercise regularly, follow a low-fat diet, and eat more fruits and vegetables. Additionally, they are significantly more likely to inquire about the qualifications of a new provider and to be persistent in asking their health care provider for information and directions until they are satisfied with the information at hand. Self-management behaviors associated with the chronic health condition are also significantly associated with activation levels.

The positive associations found in Mexico for quality of care and support, and for access to interdisciplinary care and to secondary prevention in the case of diabetes, suggest that the PAM 13-Mexican Spanish instrument can be reliably utilized to assess the capacity of persons to participate in treatment decisions and in support groups.

The finding that PAM 13-Mexican Spanish was not associated with the frequency of blood pressure measurements in the context of positive associations with other quality indicators, particularly in the case of diabetes, is unexpected. Hibbard [17] identified that hypertensive patients in the USA increased their likelihood of adhering to treatment at a first level of activation, followed by knowledge of desirable blood pressure parameters, weekly blood pressure monitoring, and, with greater activation, keeping a blood pressure diary.

The poor or low association of the PAM-13 Mexican Spanish with age and diagnoses is contrary to what other studies have found. Genevieve [21] found in a population-based study in Australia during the pandemic that younger participants (18–24 years) were twice as likely to have a PAM level 1 compared to people aged 25–44 years (p <.001). When comparing the population with and without chronic diseases, they found that those who did not have a chronic condition were twice as likely to have a PAM level 1 score compared to those who had chronic diseases (p <.001). These findings indicate that there is an opportunity to strengthen patient activation among persons living with chronic conditions in Mexico.

The lack of association of PAM 13-Mexican Spanish score levels with the most common secondary complications of hypertension and diabetes differs from what Hibbard [4] found for the United States, where people with chronic diseases with high levels of activation had lower rates of visits to the doctor’s office, emergency room visits and nights in the hospital. Further research is needed in Mexico to establish if the same indicators, rather than the more complex, aggregate indicators used, are also associated with PAM levels.

The validation of PAM in various countries and its predictive findings allow for recommending behaviors that are consistent with their level of activation. Hibbard [17] showed that reaching different levels of empowerment can be a facilitator or even a requirement for the success of strategies based on information or incentives to action. Thus, at intermediate levels of activation, a greater search for information is observed, followed by the search for information on physician accreditation, and finally, knowledge of clinical practice guidelines at higher levels of activation. Monitoring activation thus allows identifying which information strategies can be developed more successfully and gradually.

PAM-13 forms part of a cohort of tools measuring generic chronic diseases self-management competencies, among which the most cited and with robust validations among Spanish-speaking populations are the Health Partner Scale (HPS) [28] and the Self-Efficacy Scale for Chronic Disease Management (CDMS) [29]. These tools offer the possibility of measuring and monitoring patient activation in different settings– individual, group and community. PAM-13 is unique across these tools in allowing for the segmentation of the population based on activation levels that can be followed by the execution of activation strategies focused on patients with higher clinical risk and lower activation. In a quasi-experimental study patients living with diabetes that were coached with an approach tailored to their activation levels showed significant gains in activation in comparison to a control group [17]. Other benefits of measuring activation levels are the allocation of resources to increase access to programs based on personalized actions among the less activated population, while in the case of the most activated, they can be approached with less expensive methods such as providing them with digital information [17].

The strengths of this study include a high response rate with no missing values given the household survey strategy. The sample size was similar or larger in comparison to similar studies [17, 19–21]. However, the sample for women with breast cancer was limited due to the sampling at two health care centers that may have over-represented women with social security. Future studies need to broaden the diversity of the samples of persons living with specific diseases, particularly males living with an oncological condition. Given the observational design of the study, the associations found between activation levels and sociodemographic, service demand and quality, and social support variables cannot be interpreted as causal relationships.

Measuring empowerment with PAM-13 and setting strengthening goals can be recommended at the time persons are diagnosed with a chronic disease as this day is life-changing and can be viewed as a disaster, a burden, or a problem but also possibly as a challenge or an opportunity [30],[31]. Patient empowerment has been recognized as a critical need to address the chronic disease syndemic in Mexico. A study undertaken in parallel with the validation of PAM-13 Mexican Spanish identified the feasibility for implementing the tool among public health institutions catering for the non-insured as well as among civil society organizations supporting patient care (unpublished data). Monitoring empowerment, diagnosing the situation, understanding the factors that affect it, and recommending how to stratify patients according to their activation promises to facilitate the design and scale-up of chronic disease prevention and control programs.

Conclusions

The PAM-13 Mexican Spanish is a valid instrument to measure patient activation. Measuring activation of persons living with chronic diseases as well as among the healthy can support policies and programs aimed to empower the population for health promotion and chronic disease control.
