FDG-PET anterior cingulate cortex hypometabolism as a marker of cerebral small vessel disease in Alzheimer's and vascular cognitive impairment and dementia
Authors: Sarah T Thomas, Logesh Palanikumar, David G Darby, Amy G Brodtmann
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) hypometabolism is a non-specific finding in many brain disorders.•ACC hypometabolism was more common in vascular cognitive impairment and dementia (VCID) than Alzheimer dementia (AD).•ACC hypometabolism correlated with cerebral small vessel disease markers.•ACC hypometabolism may be associated with motor stereotypies in VCID.•FDG-PET patterns of hypometabolism may identify vascular contributions to cognitive impairment.Keywords: Vascular cognitive impairment, Alzheimer’s disease, PET, Small vessel disease, Anterior cingulate cortex

Introduction

Vascular cognitive impairment and dementia (VCID) represents a spectrum of cognitive impairments caused by cerebrovascular injury, including ischaemia, infarction, and haemorrhage [1,2]. When these deficits impede functional performance, vascular cognitive impairment progresses to vascular dementia [3]. VCID is the second commonest cause of dementia, comprising 15–30 % of all-cause dementias [4,5]. Diagnosis typically relies on clinical phenotype integrating vascular risk factors, a cognitive profile of retrieval based memory deficits, slowed speed of processing, and executive dysfunction [6] and supportive neuroimaging findings (MRI or CT). These include ischaemic or haemorrhagic strokes, and cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) markers such as white matter hyperintensities (WMH) of presumed vascular origin, cerebral microbleeds and enlarged perivascular spaces [7]. Diagnostic precision can be further complicated by the coexistence of cerebrovascular disease with other neuropathologies such as Alzheimer disease, which is the norm in later life [8]. The presence of mixed pathologies correlate with worse disease severity in neuropathological studies of late-life dementia [7,9].

Fluorodeoxyglucose-positron emission tomography (FDG-PET) is frequently employed for clinical evaluation of dementia syndromes, especially Alzheimer dementia (AD) and frontotemporal dementia (FTD), but its role in VCID and CSVD is less defined [2]. This may reflect the reliance on MRI CSVD biomarkers in VCID, and because FDG-PET imaging signatures have been well described in other dementias. For example, precuneus and posterior cingulate hypometabolism is regarded as pathognomonic of AD, as is left anterior temporal hypometabolism in semantic dementia, and so on [10]. FDG-PET findings may augment MRI interpretation when examining for co-pathologies [10].

One cortical region that may be differentially affected by vascular disease burden is the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC). This medial prefrontal region is critical for normal motivation, emotion regulation, and executive function [11]. This region has extensive reciprocal connections with the hippocampus, amygdala, and anterior insula, forming a hub within the salience and cognitive control networks [12]. Pathological processes affecting the ACC and its connections are associated with apathy, slowed processing speed, and behavioural rigidity, commonly observed in VCID patients [13,14]. The ACC is enriched with von Economo neurons, large, spindle-shaped projection neurons, thought to support rapid inter-regional communication and complex social cognition, which may be selectively vulnerable in frontal-predominant neurodegeneration [15].

Despite these functional associations, ACC hypometabolism has been reported across various dementia subtypes, including AD and behavioural variant FTD, limiting its specificity for VCID [16]. Whether ACC hypometabolism reflects underlying vascular injury, shared network dysfunction, or co-morbid neurodegenerative pathology remains unclear. We aimed to investigate whether qualitative assessment of FDG-PET ACC hypometabolism correlated with a diagnosis of VCID versus AD.

Methods

Setting and participants

We performed a retrospective cross-sectional study to identify people referred to the Eastern Cognitive Disorders Clinic (ECDC, Melbourne, Australia) between January 2009 and October 2024 with a diagnosis of VCID or AD. Diagnoses were made in a multidisciplinary specialist memory clinic setting in accordance with published consensus criteria on the basis of detailed clinical assessments, cognitive and behavioural screens, neuropsychological assessments, MRI and FDG-PET imaging where available [17,18].

Data collection

Demographic and clinical data were entered into a secure REDCap database. All patients completed a detailed demographic questionnaire (Melbourne Life Questionnaire, copyright John Hodges 2010). Informants completed the Cambridge Behavioural Inventory – Revised (CBI-R), which includes questions on motivation and apathy, motor stereotypies and emotional changes [19]. Cognitive screening tests included the Mini Mental State Examination (MMSE) [20] and from 2011 the Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination – Revised Edition (ACE-R) [21] where possible. Demographic details extracted included age, gender, years of education, vascular risk factors including a history of hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, smoking, prior stroke, and depression were documented. Sex and gender were self-reported for our database, with options for cis- and trans-gender, non-binary and self-identification.

Imaging assessment

Available clinical MRI and PET scans were checked against Eastern Health network electronic medical records. External images were transferred, if possible, for visual inspection. Clinical scans could not be anonymised as they are viewable only by patient name. MRI scans were independently rated for white matter hyperintensities (WMH) using the overall Fazekas score by two raters trained in cognitive and stroke neurology (ST & LP). The presence or absence of ACC hypometabolism was determined on available FDG-PET from a visual review of global cortical FDG-PET maps by two raters (ST & LP). Disagreements were resolved either by consensus or a third rater (AB).

Statistical approach

Dichotomous variables were reported as n (%) and compared using Chi-squared tests. Continuous variables were summarised as medians (IQRs) or means (SD) and analysed using t-tests or Mann-Whitney-U tests where appropriate. Subgroup analyses examined for differences between individuals with and without ACC hypometabolism, both within and across disease groups (VCID and AD). We also explored the association between WMH severity and ACC hypometabolism using binary logistic regression with Fazekas scores and patient groups (VCID and AD) serving as independent variables. Odds ratios with 95 % confidence intervals were calculated. Predicted probabilities were derived from the model.

Standard protocol approvals, registrations, and patient consents

The study was approved by the Eastern Health Research Ethics Committee (LR27–1213 15 February 2013). All data were fully anonymised prior to conducting statistical analyses for the current study. The need to obtain participant consent was waived.

Results

Participants

We identified 98 people diagnosed with VCID and 281 with AD (Table 1). All participants identified as cis-gender. VCID and AD patients were of similar ages (Median (IQR) age VCID: 71 (63–77), AD: 72 (66–79) years). More men had a diagnosis of VCID (67 %) and more women of AD (53 %). People with VCID had more vascular risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes, previous stroke, and smoking history, than those with AD. People with VCID were also more likely to be prescribed antidepressants, have fewer years of education (Mean (SD) VCID: 11.9 (2.6), AD: 13.8 (4.3)) and higher Fazekas scores, reflecting greater WMH severity (Table 1). People with VCID had higher scores on both cognitive screening tools compared to those with AD (Mean (SD) ACE-R score VCID: 81.2 (10.4), AD: 62.8 (18.1); MMSE score VCID: 25.7 (3.2), AD: 20.1 (5.9)).

Imaging

There was 97 % inter-rater reliability for rating overall MRI Fazekas scores, and 100 % inter-rater agreement for rating the presence or absence of ACC hypometabolism on FDG-PET, noting that raters were not blinded to the diagnosis (see Fig. 1 for representative images).

The Z-score scale (right) indicates standard deviations from the mean SUVR from a normative control database. Blue represents values within 0–1.5 SD (normal range) and progressively warmer colours (green–yellow–red) corresponding to greater hypometabolism (up to >6 SD below the reference mean).

ACC hypometabolism was identified in 28 (70 %) people with VCID. ACC hypometabolism was associated with higher WMH severity: mean (SD) Fazekas scores were 2.3 (0.6) for those with ACC hypometabolism compared with 1.8 (0.7) with normal ACC metabolism. In contrast, 46 (34 %) people with AD with an available FDG-PET exhibited ACC hypometabolism. The presence of ACC hypometabolism was significantly associated with greater composite vascular risk factors, especially T2DM, in people with AD. Fazekas scores were generally lower in people with AD (mean (SD): 1.2 (0.83)). Higher Fazekas scores were observed in those with ACC hypometabolism (mean (SD): 1.5 (0.8)) compared with those who had visually normal ACC metabolism (mean (SD): 1.0 (0.7), p < 0.001) (Fig. 2).

When comparing ACC-hypometabolic subgroups across VCID and AD diagnoses, VCID patients had significantly higher Fazekas scores (2.3 vs 1.5, p < 0.001), more vascular risk factors, and better cognitive performance (ACER 80.1 vs 60.4, p < 0.001; MMSE 25.3 vs 19.4, p < 0.001) than their AD counterparts.

ACC hypometabolism and cognitive performance

In people with VCID, the presence of ACC hypometabolism was associated with higher mean (SD) scores both on the ACE-R (80.1 (8.5) vs 72.6 (9.5), p = 0.04) and MMSE (25.3 (3.7) vs 23.0 (2.6), p = 0.04). The presence of ACC hypometabolism did not appear to be associated with cognitive screening scores among AD patients compared with those who had normal ACC metabolism (60.4 (14.9) vs 64.3 (19.5), p = 0.2) and MMSE (19.4 (5.3) vs 20.2 (6.1), p = 0.43).

Carer-reported behavioural scores and ACC hypometabolism

Completed CBI questionnaires were available in only 52 (14 %) people, therefore, the findings warrant cautious interpretation. Among those with VCID, the presence of ACC hypometabolism was associated with motor stereotypies and routine-bound behaviours (Supplementary Table 2). No differences were detected between the groups in other behavioural sub-domains including mood, self-care, and apathy. In people with AD, the presence of ACC hypometabolism was not associated with a distinct behavioural profile. Disturbances in eating behaviour were more pronounced in AD than in VCID, but there were no significant differences across other CBI sub-scales.

Discussion

We examined whether ACC hypometabolism on FDG-PET was associated with a diagnosis of VCID and the severity of WMH on MRI. We found that ACC hypometabolism was more frequent but not specific to VCID and more associated with radiological markers of WMH severity. This suggests that ACC hypometabolism may represent a downstream functional correlate of vascular injury and damage to frontal-subcortical circuits.

Apathy and depression are common neuropsychiatric symptoms of VCID and reported in ACC-related pathologies [22]. Its role in regulating mood and motivation, supporting goal-directed behaviour and integrating salient emotional and cognitive inputs has made it a promising neuro-modulatory target in treatment-resistant depression and severe apathy [23]. While the VCID group was more likely to be prescribed antidepressants (p = 0.004) compared with AD patients, we were not powered to detect an association between apathy/depression and ACC hypometabolism, particularly with the screening tools used in the clinic. The association with stereotypic behaviours and ACC hypometabolism points to ACC’s putative role in cognitive flexibility.

The observation that ACC hypometabolism was associated with higher cognitive scores in VCID was unexpected. This may be partly influenced by the limited power of the PET subgroup. It also underscores the limitations of cognitive screening tools like MMSE and ACER in capturing executive or motivational deficits linked to ACC dysfunction and may be subject to floor effects in patients with lower scores. Compensatory mechanisms within neural networks may also contribute to the preservation of general cognitive performance despite focal ACC changes. ACC hypometabolism in AD did not correlate with cognitive or vascular measures likely reflecting broader network disintegration. Altogether, this lack of specificity of ACC hypometabolism as a stand-alone biomarker highlights the need to interpret imaging findings in clinical context.

An important consideration in this study is that the cohort reflects a real-world case mix with the heterogeneity typical of clinical populations where overlapping cerebrovascular and Alzheimer features are frequently observed and strictly “pure” cases are uncommon. Patients were classified as AD or VCID according to consensus clinical criteria, but many displayed elements of both, reflecting the complexity of diagnosis in practice. This strengthens the generalisability of our findings, but we acknowledge that the accuracy of such classifications may shift as biomarker-driven approaches become more widely implemented.

This study had several limitations. Fewer people with VCID had available FDG-PET than those with AD for various reasons. In Australia, FDG-PET is available on universal healthcare only for a suspected diagnosis of Alzheimer’s disease. High diagnostic certainty coupled with physician experience underlies the decision to not refer for FDG-PET in 42 (67 %) patients, while 19 (30 %) did not return for further investigation, and two (3 %) declined further testing. The retrospective, non-randomised study design, and modest sample sizes may limit the generalisability of our findings. ACC hypometabolism was also visually rated by two clinicians who were not blinded to the clinical diagnosis at MRI and FDG-PET review, and while there was high inter-rater agreement, this may have introduced bias in imaging interpretation. WMH severity was also assessed using the Fazekas scale, which is the most consistently available and clinically validated marker of WMH burden but does not capture the full spectrum of CSVD features. WMH in older adults may arise from both vascular and Alzheimer-related processes, including cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) and secondary white matter degeneration. Disentangling the relative arteriosclerotic and CAA-related contributions to WMH remains an interpretative challenge, especially in advanced stages. Future studies integrating other MRI features of CSVD, such as cerebral microbleed counts, lacunes, and perivascular spaces, may yield more granular insights into how CSVD and amyloid pathology influence the vulnerability of specific networks [7]. Finally, the cognitive and behavioural tests administered within the clinic setting may not fully capture ACC-relevant domains.

Nevertheless, the study is novel in exploring the interpretive value of ACC metabolism in both AD and VCID cohorts when combined with other imaging and clinical data. ACC hypometabolism in VCID appears to map more closely to SVD markers than to cognitive severity, offering a potential imaging correlate of frontal network involvement. While the finding is non-specific, identifying this imaging pattern could support clinicians in recognising vascular contributions to cognitive syndromes, particularly when FDG-PET is performed for a suspected neurodegenerative condition. Clinicians are encouraged to probe vascular risk factors, subtle dysexecutive symptoms, and behavioural changes more carefully. Future prospective studies using quantitative PET metrics and executive task batteries are needed to validate and expand upon these findings.
