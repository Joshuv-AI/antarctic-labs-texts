A seven-year monitoring on fish community around the Qigu Lagoon located on the southwest coast of Taiwan
Authors: Yi Fang, Chun-Yu Hsieh, Meng-Chi Hung, Li-Yung Hsieh, Mei-Li Hsueh, Wen-Kai Tsai, Pei-Hsiang Tsai, Yu-Jen Su, Wen-Yang Fang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEstuaries and lagoons are recognized as vital nursery habitats for coastal ecosystems, providing essential ecosystem services to human societies while also facing significant threats from human activities and climate change. However, long-term monitoring data for these ecosystems remain scarce. This dataset presents the occurrences and morphological measurements of fish collected from the Qigu Lagoon over a seven-year period (2015–2021). Additionally, seven water quality parameters which are basic to aquatic life over four years (2018–2021) are included. A total of 15,009 individuals representing 190 species were collected. Among the families, Leiognathidae, Clupeidae, and Mugilidae were the most abundant, while the dominant species included Eubleekeria splendens, Leiognathus equulus, Planiliza macrolepis, Nematalosa japonica, and Nuchequula mannusella. This dataset serves as a baseline for understanding interannual changes in fish assemblages in relation to environmental changes. It may provide valuable references for researchers in related fields and for managers of estuaries and coastal lagoons.Subject terms: Population dynamics, Community ecology

Background & Summary

Coastal wetlands are complex socio-economic systems that provide essential ecosystem services to human societies1. In recent decades, the growing global population and economic development have significantly increased environmental pressures on coastal areas2,3, leading to the degradation and loss of coastal wetlands4,5.

Among the various types of coastal wetlands, coastal lagoons and estuaries are recognized as some of the most productive ecosystems, serving as habitats and nurseries where marine life can forage, inhabit, grow, and breed6. However, they are also vulnerable and face significant environmental pressures from sources such as pollution, fishing, and tourism5,7–12. These anthropogenic factors can diminish the resilience of coastal lagoons and estuaries, especially when combined with the impacts of climate change9,13,14. Despite the substantial threats they encounter, our understanding of the ecosystems of coastal lagoons and estuaries remains limited and fragmented12,15. To effectively manage and conserve these ecosystems, which hold significant socio-economic and ecological value, a clearer understanding of the status and change mechanisms of communities within coastal lagoons and estuaries is essential. In this context, long-term data on the dynamics of fish assemblages are indispensable, as they can illuminate the mechanisms of change within fish assemblages and the consequences of biodiversity conservation measures16.

Previous studies of fish assemblages in coastal lagoons and estuaries have examined habitat utilization, as well as seasonal and annual variations in community structure and dynamics in relation to environmental factors17–28. However, these studies include relatively few long-term monitoring datasets29,30, particularly in tropical regions such as Taiwan. In Taiwan, although there have been investigations into the long-term dynamics of fish assemblages in rocky tidepools31,32, research on those in coastal lagoons and estuaries along the southwestern coast remains limited. Specifically, only a few studies have been conducted on coastal lagoons, such as the Qigu Lagoon in Tainan County and Dapeng Bay in Pingtung County33–37, as well as estuaries like the Tanshui River and the Puzih River38,39, most of which are not long-term studies.

This dataset presents the results of a long-term fish investigation conducted in the Qigu Lagoon, the largest coastal lagoon in Taiwan, and the surrounding estuarine areas. The regions comprise both natural wetlands, such as mangrove forests and mudflats, and artificial wetlands, including fish ponds and salt fields. The terrestrial inputs of nutrients, combined with the topography of the semi-enclosed shallow lagoon, foster a vital coastal wetland ecosystem. Local residents rely on this ecosystem for their livelihoods through activities such as fishing, aquaculture, and ecotourism40. Fish diversity in the Qigu Lagoon is notably high, with approximately 50 families and over 100 species identified in the lagoon and its surrounding waters. Among the collected individuals, the major families include Mugilidae, Terapontidae, Leiognathidae, Gerreidae, and Clupeidae33–37.

The topography of the Qigu Lagoon is continually changing due to both natural and anthropogenic factors41,42. In recent decades, its extent and average depth have gradually decreased as a result of siltation in tidal inlets and coastal erosion40,43. These alterations have subsequently affected the physicochemical properties of the water; for instance, the water exchange capacity between the Qigu Lagoon and the outer sea has decreased by approximately 50% since the closure of the inlet between Qingshan Port Barrier Island and Wangzailiao Barrier Island40. Changes in water quality can significantly affect fish assemblages. For instance, the richness of fish assemblages may increase with dissolved oxygen, and their composition may also vary with alterations in salinity and temperature44,45. However, the nature of this relationship in the Qigu Lagoon remains not fully understood, highlighting the need for further monitoring and analysis.

Therefore, this dataset integrates the occurrences and morphological measurements of fish with water quality parameters collected from 2015 to 2021. It serves as a baseline for understanding long-term changes in the community structure of fish assemblages in tropical coastal lagoons and estuaries in relation to environmental changes, providing a valuable reference for researchers to explore and for managers to conserve these ecosystems.

Methods

The Qigu Lagoon, located on the southwest coast of Taiwan, spans approximately 9 km in length from north to south and 5 km in width from east to west. It covers an area of roughly 1,000 ha (23°6′14.4″N to 23°9′7.2″N, 120°2′9.6″E to 120°5′11.7″E). The composition of the sediment in the lagoon ranges from very fine sand to silty clay loam. The Qigu Lagoon generally has an average depth of approximately 1 metre42. The Qigu River, which flows into the lagoon from the southeast, serves as the primary source of freshwater for the Qigu Lagoon. Due to the influx of freshwater from the Qigu River and surrounding ditches, the lagoon’s water typically fluctuates between brackish and saline, depending on tidal and meteorological conditions. There are three sand barrier islands situated between the Qigu Lagoon and the Taiwan Strait, along with a tidal inlet in the southwestern part of the Qigu Lagoon. The water surrounding the sand barrier islands adjacent to the tidal inlet has depths ranging from 2 to 3 metres42 and is saline due to its exchange with the Taiwan Strait. Dense nets and oyster racks are present in the Qigu Lagoon and the ditches surrounding the Qigu River.

This study collected fish from 6 sampling stations in the Qigu Lagoon and the estuary of the Qigu River (Fig. 1 and Tables 1, 2). Collections were conducted every three months from February, 2015 to January, 2017 (February, May, August, and November in 2015; January, April, July, and October in 2016), and every two months thereafter (January, April, June, August, October, and December in 2017; January, March, May, July, September, and November during 2018–2020; February, April, June, August, October, and December in 2021).

Details of the water sampling stations.

Pound nets were employed to collect samples at the sampling stations located in the estuary and in the shallow-water area of the lagoon (ST3, ST3-1, ST2, and ST4) (Fig. 2). The pound nets consisted of several cylindrical hoop nets designed to capture fish (mesh size: 1 cm × 1 cm), along with a lead component (height: 2.1 m, mesh size: 1.5 cm × 1.5 cm). This lead component comprises a series of bamboo stakes and netting. When deployed, the netting was arranged in a V-shape or a Π-shape, with an opening facing the direction opposite to the outgoing tide and several hoop nets positioned at the edges. This configuration allowed fish to enter the hoop nets with the flow of water. Inside the hoop nets, funnel-shaped nets effectively prevented fish from escaping. The width of the opening of pound nets at ST2, ST3, ST3-1, and ST4 were 120 m, 30 m, 50 m, and 70 m, respectively. The gill nets (length: 151.5 m, width: 2.4 m, mesh size: 5.5 cm × 5.5 cm) were secured to floating oyster farming platforms or rafts and deployed in the surrounding water (ST3-7 and ST4-1) (Fig. 3).

Photographs of sampling stations with gill nets. (a) ST3-7 and (b) ST4-1.

Local fishers were commissioned to set the nets and haul them in after a minimum of one tidal period. The collection durations typically ranged from 12 to 24 hours. Those species whose colouration changes after death were photographed upon collection for identification purposes. The collected fish samples were kept refrigerated during transport to the laboratory, where they were subsequently identified, counted, and measured. The identification of the samples was performed by several surveyors in accordance with The Fish Database of Taiwan46, Fishes of Taiwan47, and Fishes of Japan with Pictorial Keys to the Species48. Those belonging to taxa that are difficult to identify (e.g., Gobiidae or Nematalosa) were assigned serial numbers, frozen, and subsequently identified based on micromorphological traits. After the collected fish had been identified, their morphological characteristics, including body length, total length, body depth, and body weight, were measured.

The seven water quality parameters of surface water—dissolved oxygen, electrical conductivity, redox potential, salinity, pH, water temperature, and turbidity—have been measured at seven sampling stations using a multiparameter meter (HANNA HI9829) since 2018. Water quality measurements were conducted during both low and high tide periods at the sampling stations located in the estuary and along the coast of the lagoon (ST2_WQS, ST3_WQS, ST3-2_WQS, ST3-8_WQS, and ST4_WQS). In contrast, measurements in the deep-water area of the lagoon were conducted only during high tide periods, coinciding with fish sampling, due to the availability of rafts.

The raw data for both fish and water quality parameters were initially recorded on paper, digitized using Microsoft Excel, and stored as XLSX files. The digitized raw data were subsequently converted to the Darwin Core data format49 using Microsoft Excel and R for uploading to GBIF.

Data Records

This dataset comprises occurrence and individual morphological measurements of 15,009 fish belonging to 190 species from 6 sampling stations (Table S1, see supplementary XLSX file) and water quality parameters from 7 sampling stations in the Qigu Lagoon and the estuary of the Qigu River between 2015 and 2021 inclusive. It adheres to the Darwin Core data format49 and includes an Event core, an Occurrence extension, and a MeasurementOrFact extension, all provided in CSV files accessible at GBIF50.

The Event core comprises 588 rows (sampling events) and 19 columns, which include identifiers (eventID and parentEventID), date fields (year, month, and day), samplingProtocol, sampleSizeValue, sampleSizeUnit, location data (locationID, locality, decimalLatitude, decimalLongitude, geodeticDatum, verbatimCoordinateSystem, coordinatePrecision, county, country, and countryCode), and eventRemarks. The Occurrence extension consists of 3,465 rows (species recorded during each sampling event) and 10 columns, including identifiers (occurrenceID and eventID), basisOfRecord, taxonomic data (family, genus, scientificName, taxonRank, and vernacularName), individualCount, and occurrenceStatus. The MeasurementOrFact extension contains 66,422 rows (the morphological measurements of the collected fish and the water quality parameters) and 10 columns, which include identifiers (measurementID, occurrenceID, and eventID), measurementType, measurementValue, measurementAccuracy, measurementUnit, measurementMethod, measurementDeterminedDate, and measurementRemarks. A detailed explanation of each column can be found on the Darwin Core Quick Reference Guide website51.

This dataset will be continuously updated as the investigation progresses.

Technical Validation

Sampling stations selection

The habitats of the Qigu Lagoon and the estuary of the Qigu River are diverse. The sampling stations were selected based on a range of environmental types, including the mangrove areas of the river, as well as the deep and shallow waters of the lagoon, and the tidal inlet areas adjacent to the open sea.

Occurrence and morphological measurements of fish

When converting the raw data to the Darwin Core data format49, the scientific names of fish were validated using NomenMatch52. Most of the names confirmed to be synonyms were updated to their valid counterparts, while those with undetermined valid names were retained. Erroneous morphological measurements, such as those that obviously exceeded the normal range of size or body lengths that were longer than the total length of the same individual, were detected using R and corrected with reference to the paper records.

In the period from 2015 to 2021, fish collections were conducted 38 times at ST2, ST3, ST3-7, and ST4. From 2016 to 2021, there were 97 fish collections at ST3-1; this frequency is attributed to the fact that collections at this station were typically conducted over more than three consecutive days. Between 2018 and 2021, 26 fish collections were conducted at ST4-1. Additionally, during the same period, 38 water quality measurements were taken at ST2_WQS, ST3_WQS, ST3-2_WQS, ST3-8_WQS, and ST4_WQS, along with 19 measurements at ST3-7_WQS and ST4-1_WQS, which were only measured during high tide. The number of species per sample ranged from 1 to 35, with an average of 12.60 ± 5.90 (s.d.). The number of individuals per sample ranged from 1 to 1,205, with an average of 54.58 ± 84.08 (s.d.). The family Leiognathidae accounted for the largest proportion of individuals collected during 2015–2021 at 27.38%, followed by Clupeidae at 13.98% and Mugilidae at 11.47%. Collectively, these three families make up over half of the total samples (Fig. 6). In the families Leiognathidae, Clupeidae, and Mugilidae, the most abundant species are Eubleekeria splendens, Nematalosa japonica, and Planiliza macrolepis, accounting for 48.98%, 48.81%, and 65.33% of their respective families.

Water quality parameters

The pH and conductivity probes of the multiparameter meter were calibrated monthly using the “quick calibration mode” with a calibration solution. The calibration of the dissolved oxygen probe was based on the saturated vapor pressure at the temperature of each sampling day. Given the significant variation in environmental conditions, the water quality parameters were measured in triplicate at each sampling station to ensure the reliability of the data. Major limitations of the investigation

The sampling gear varied between two types of sampling stations—shallow waters and deep waters—resulting in a lack of comparability in the fish assemblage structure between these stations. The duration of fish collection was not strictly controlled due to weather conditions and the schedules of the fishers; therefore, the data must be standardized by time during analysis.
