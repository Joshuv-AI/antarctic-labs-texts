Divergent Inflammatory and Bioenergetic Metabolomic Signatures Distinguish Methamphetamine‐Associated From Idiopathic Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension
Authors: Hannah K. Robertson, Emma Yuguchi, Ian Gurholt, William C. Nichols, Nick H. Kim, Timothy Fernandes, Atul Malhotra, Mona Alotaibi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMethamphetamine‐associated pulmonary arterial hypertension (Meth‐APAH) is an increasingly recognized cause of pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) and is associated with worse outcomes than idiopathic PAH (IPAH). Although both share histopathologic features of pulmonary vascular remodeling, the molecular basis for their clinical divergence remains unclear, particularly at the metabolic level. We performed high‐throughput, non‐targeted plasma metabolomic profiling in patients with Meth‐APAH and matched IPAH controls enrolled from the Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension Biobank. Multivariate and regression analyses adjusted for demographics, hemodynamic severity, and PAH therapies were used to identify distinguishing metabolites. Among 98 patients (49 Meth‐APAH and 49 IPAH), baseline hemodynamics and functional status were similar, although Meth‐APAH patients had higher smoking prevalence and lower PAH therapy use. Of 757 metabolites passing quality control, 55 differed significantly between subtypes, including 22 enriched in Meth‐APAH. Meth‐APAH was characterized by elevations in amino acid–derived metabolites and pro‐inflammatory eicosanoids, implicating dysregulation of the kynurenine pathway and arachidonic acid metabolism. IPAH demonstrated relative enrichment of branched‐chain amino acids, antioxidant metabolites, and specialized pro‐resolving mediators. Exploratory survival analyses identified associations between subtype‐discriminating metabolites and outcomes. These findings reveal metabolic heterogeneity across PAH etiologies and support biologically distinct pathobiology in Meth‐APAH and IPAH.

Introduction

Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a progressive and life‐threatening disease characterized by pulmonary vascular remodeling, increased pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), and right ventricular failure. Among the various etiologies of PAH, methamphetamine‐associated pulmonary arterial hypertension (Meth‐APAH) has emerged as a significant public health concern, with its incidence rising in parallel with the global surge in methamphetamine use [1, 2]. As of the sixth World Symposium on Pulmonary Hypertension, methamphetamine is now recognized as a definite cause of PAH, reflecting the growing body of evidence linking chronic methamphetamine exposure to severe pulmonary vascular disease [3]. While historically concentrated in the Western United States, Meth‐APAH is increasingly observed across diverse geographic regions, accounting for a notable proportion of PAH cases presenting to expert centers nationwide [4, 5].

Compared to idiopathic PAH (IPAH), Meth‐APAH presents with a more severe clinical phenotype. Patients with Meth‐APAH demonstrate worse functional class, worse hemodynamic derangements—including higher right atrial pressure (RAP) and lower cardiac index (CI)—and impaired health‐related quality of life [1, 4, 6]. Critically, Meth‐APAH is associated with substantially worse survival outcomes, with event‐free survival rates of 47.2% and 25% at 5 and 10 years, respectively, representing more than double the risk of clinical worsening or death compared to IPAH [1]. Recent studies have also demonstrated that Meth‐APAH patients exhibit more severe right ventricular dysfunction and greater impairment in right atrial and ventricular mechanics, suggesting potential direct cardiotoxic effects of methamphetamine beyond pulmonary vascular injury [7]. While Meth‐APAH shares the hallmark histopathologic features of pulmonary vascular remodeling with other PAH subtypes including medial hypertrophy, intimal proliferation, and plexiform arteriopathy, distinct patterns have also been described, including proliferative capillaries reminiscent of pulmonary capillary hemangiomatosis and intravascular microcrystalline deposits from drug adulterants [1, 2]. At the molecular level, proposed mechanisms unique to methamphetamine's vascular toxicity include dysregulated serotonin signaling, catecholamine‐mediated vasoconstriction, and mitochondrial injury through HIF‐1α destabilization. These mechanisms may overlap with but are not identical to classical IPAH pathobiology [1, 2, 8].

Despite the shared histopathologic features of pulmonary vascular remodeling between Meth‐APAH and IPAH, the worse clinical outcomes in Meth‐APAH suggest underlying pathobiological differences that remain incompletely understood. Metabolomics has emerged as a powerful tool for characterizing molecular heterogeneity in PAH, revealing distinct metabolic fingerprints that distinguish disease subtypes and identify dysregulated pathways associated with disease severity and outcomes [9]. However, the metabolomic differences between Meth‐APAH and IPAH have not been systematically examined. In this study, we applied high‐throughput, non‐targeted metabolomic profiling across multiple biochemical platforms to comprehensively compare Meth‐APAH and IPAH in a well‐matched cohort.

Methods

Study Cohort and Sample Preparation

Patients with Meth‐APAH and IPAH were enrolled from the Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension Biobank (PAHB), a multicenter NIH‐supported repository that includes deeply phenotyped PAH patients with linked clinical data and biospecimens. Meth‐APAH was defined based on documented methamphetamine exposure and fulfillment of contemporary hemodynamic criteria for PAH at the time of diagnostic right heart catheterization (RHC). Diagnosis of PAH was defined by RHC measurements including mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) > 25 mmHg, pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP) ≤ 15 mmHg, and PVR > 3 Woods Units (WU), based on the prevailing diagnostic criteria for PH at the time of cohort recruitment [1]. IPAH controls were selected from the same biobank and matched on age, sex, and mPAP, and use of methamphetamine or known PAH‐related toxins was a formal exclusion criterion for the IPAH group. Clinical data were abstracted from standardized case report forms and included demographics, comorbidities, PAH‐directed therapies, and invasive hemodynamic measurements (Figure 1). All studies were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, V edition (2000). All participants provided informed consent, and the study was approved by the institutional review boards of participating centers.

Study overview.

Metabolomics Analysis

Peripheral venous blood was collected into EDTA tubes at the time of clinical evaluation. Samples were processed according to standardized protocols, and plasma aliquots were stored at −80°C until analysis. Directed untargeted, high‐throughput metabolomics was performed on plasma samples to measure metabolites spanning polar metabolites and bioactive lipid mediators as previously described [9]. Metabolite profiling was conducted at UCSD using liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS)–based platforms optimized for coverage of central carbon metabolism, amino acids, fatty acids, eicosanoids and other bioactive lipid species. Metabolites were identified using in‐house authentic standards, with rigorous quality control procedures applied across batches. Metabolite concentrations were log‐transformed and scaled prior to analysis to reduce skewness and improve comparability across metabolite classes. Samples and metabolites failing predefined quality thresholds were excluded from downstream analyses.

Statistical Analysis

Patients with Meth‐APAH were matched to IPAH controls to reduce confounding. Matching was performed using a nearest‐neighbor approach without replacement, based on age, sex, and mPAP from the baseline diagnostic RHC. Balance between groups after matching was assessed by standardized mean differences and visual inspection of covariate distributions. Baseline characteristics were summarized by PAH subtype. Continuous variables are reported as median with interquartile range, as appropriate based on distribution. Categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages. Group comparisons for clinical variables were performed using Student's t‐test or Wilcoxon rank‐sum test for continuous variables and χ 2 or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. Metabolite concentrations were log‐transformed to improve normality and reduce right‐skewness. Metabolites with more than 70% missingness were excluded from analysis. Following transformation, metabolite levels were standardized (z‐score–scaled) to enable comparison of effect sizes across metabolite classes.

To identify metabolites distinguishing Meth‐APAH from IPAH, both partial least squares‐discriminant analysis (PLS‐DA) and multivariable logistic regression models were applied. PLS‐DA was performed on normalized metabolites and variable importance in projection (VIP) scores were calculated to rank metabolites contributing to group separation. Logistic regression models were fitted for each metabolite individually with PAH subtype (Meth‐APAH vs IPAH) as the dependent variable. The primary model adjusted for age, sex, and BMI, and the secondary full model additionally adjusted for disease duration, baseline mPAP, aspirin use, statin use, and PAH medication class. Effect estimates are presented as adjusted standardized differences in metabolite levels between Meth‐APAH and IPAH with corresponding 95% confidence intervals and p‐values. Metabolites meeting p value threshold < 0.05 were included in the secondary analysis. To reduce false‐positive findings while integrating multivariate and regression‐based approaches, metabolites were classified according to combined criteria. A metabolite was considered a confirmed candidate when the VIP score exceeded 1.0 and the logistic regression p‐value was less than 0.05. Metabolites with VIP ≥ 0.8 and logistic regression p‐value less than 0.05 were considered potential candidates.

To assess subtype‐specific prognostic associations, metabolites prioritized from the primary analysis were further evaluated in time‐to‐event analyses stratified by PAH subtype. Cox proportional hazards models were constructed separately within Meth‐APAH and IPAH cohorts to examine associations between individual metabolites and the composite outcome of all‐cause mortality or lung transplantation adjusting for age, sex and BMI. Time‐to‐event was defined as the interval from plasma collection at enrollment to the occurrence of the event or censoring. Hazard ratios are reported per one standard deviation increase in metabolite level with corresponding 95% confidence intervals and p‐values. Metabolites were subsequently classified as subtype‐specific prognostic markers if significant in one PAH subtype but not the other, or as exhibiting divergent prognostic effects if associations differed in direction or magnitude between Meth‐APAH and IPAH. All analyses were conducted using R (version 2023.03.0).

Results

Study Population

A total of 98 patients were included in the analysis, comprising 49 individuals with Meth‐APAH and 49 matched IPAH controls (Table 1). The two groups were well balanced with respect to age, sex, and BMI. Measures of pulmonary hypertension (PH) severity at the time of diagnostic RHC were comparable between Meth‐APAH and IPAH, including mPAP, PVR, RAP, PCWP, and CI. The median interval from diagnostic RHC to blood draw was 3.0 years in the Meth‐APAH group and 4.9 years in the IPAH group (p = 0.103). Functional class distribution and 6‐min walk distance (6MWD) were also similar between groups. PAH‐directed therapies differed modestly between subtypes, with a lower proportion of Meth‐APAH patients receiving pulmonary vasodilator therapy compared with IPAH. Other clinically relevant medications were similar between groups as well (Supplemental Table 1). No amphetamine use was documented in any IPAH patient in this cohort (Supplemental Table 2). As expected, a higher prevalence of prior smoking history was observed among patients with Meth‐APAH. Other comorbidities, including renal insufficiency and cirrhosis, were infrequent and did not differ significantly between groups.

Global Metabolomic Profiling Distinguishes Meth‐APAH from IPAH

PLS‐DA demonstrated separation between Meth‐APAH and IPAH based on circulating metabolite profiles (Figure 2). While some overlap was observed, the first two PLS components captured distinct multivariate patterns corresponding to PAH subtype, supporting biological differences between groups.

Differential Metabolic Signatures Distinguishing Meth‐APAH and IPAH

757 metabolites were profiled across polar and bioactive lipid platforms, similar in number to other large‐scale metabolomics studies in PAH [9, 10, 11], and of these PLS‐DA identified 259 metabolites with VIP scores > 1.0. In parallel, multivariable logistic regression analyses identified 63 metabolites differentially abundant between Meth‐APAH and IPAH. All metabolites meeting regression significance were evaluated against PLS‐DA findings. Fifty‐five metabolites satisfied combined criteria based on multivariate importance and regression significance, including 51 classified as confirmed candidates (VIP > 1.0 and p < 0.05) and 4 as potential candidates (VIP ≥ 0.8 and p < 0.05) (Supplemental Table 3; Supplemental Figure 1), including 22 enriched in Meth‐APAH (Figure 3A). These metabolites spanned multiple biochemical classes, indicating broad metabolic divergence between PAH subtypes. Meth‐APAH was characterized by elevations in amino acids and amino acid–derived metabolites, including histidine, and proline, consistent with perturbations in amino acid metabolism (Figure 3B; Supplemental Table 4). Additionally, compared to IPAH patients, Meth‐APAH demonstrated enrichment of classic inflammatory eicosanoid metabolism including elevations of leukotriene B4 (LTB4) metabolite 12‐oxo‐LTB4 and 13,14‐dihydro‐15‐keto‐prostaglandin D1, a prostaglandin D1 (PGD1) metabolite implicating dysregulation of arachidonic acid metabolism. Patients with Meth‐APAH also demonstrated enrichment of hydroxykynurenine, a downstream metabolite in the kynurenine pathway, one element of the catabolism of tryptophan which has been of recent interest in PAH pathobiology. There were no significant differences between groups in kynurenine or downstream metabolites of hydroxykynurenine.

In contrast, IPAH controls demonstrated marked enrichment of branched chain amino acids (BCAAs) which are essential components of energy metabolism and protein synthesis, as evidenced by a relatively higher abundance of leucine, valine, and ketoleucine (Figure 3C). IPAH controls also exhibited selective enrichment of omega‐3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) and their downstream metabolites including anti‐inflammatory specialized pro‐resolving mediator (SPM) Maresin 2, as well as alpha‐tocopherol and rosmarinate, two anti‐oxidant metabolites.

Exploratory Integration of Differential Abundance and Mortality Associations

To explore whether metabolites distinguishing Meth‐APAH from IPAH also exhibited subtype‐specific prognostic relevance, we performed an exploratory integration of logistic regression analyses of disease subtype with Cox proportional hazards models stratified by PAH etiology. Among metabolites enriched in Meth‐APAH, 20‐hydroxydocosahexaenoic acid (20‐HDoHE) and N‐acetylasparagine demonstrated concordant associations with increased mortality risk within Meth‐APAH, suggesting alignment between differential abundance and adverse outcomes in this subgroup (Figure 4; Supplemental Table 5). However, beyond these observations, most differentially abundant metabolites showed attenuated, absent, or discordant associations with mortality across PAH subtypes. Overall, differential abundance alone did not consistently translate into subtype‐specific prognostic associations, indicating limited specificity of individual metabolites for mortality risk.

Discussion

In this study, we performed comprehensive plasma metabolomic profiling across polar, and bioactive lipid platforms to compare Meth‐APAH with IPAH in a well‐matched cohort. Despite similar demographic characteristics, hemodynamic severity, functional status, and overall disease burden at the time of sampling, Meth‐APAH and IPAH demonstrated distinct global metabolomic profiles, as evidenced by unsupervised and multivariate analyses. Multivariate and regression analyses identified 55 metabolites distinguishing Meth‐APAH from IPAH, spanning multiple biochemical classes including amino acid metabolism, carbohydrate pathways, arachidonic acid metabolism, and omega‐3 PUFAs. Meth‐APAH was characterized by enrichment of amino acid–derived metabolites, and pro‐inflammatory eicosanoids, whereas IPAH demonstrated relative enrichment of BCAAs and omega‐3 fatty acids with downstream specialized pro‐resolving mediators—a pattern suggesting divergent inflammatory resolution capacity between subtypes. Exploratory integration of differential abundance with Cox proportional hazards models demonstrated that a subset of subtype‐discriminating metabolites exhibited associations with mortality.

The distinct metabolomic profiles observed between Meth‐APAH and IPAH reveal divergent patterns of inflammatory signaling and energy metabolism, despite comparable hemodynamic severity and functional status. The enrichment of kynurenine pathway metabolites in Meth‐APAH is particularly notable, as kynurenine pathway activation is mediated through IL‐6/IL‐6Rα signaling and promotes pulmonary vascular remodeling via indoleamine 2,3 dioxygenase (IDO1) and tryptophan 2,3 dioxygenase (TDO2) activation [12, 13, 14]. Elevated kynurenine metabolites correlate with higher pulmonary vascular resistance, worse right ventricular function, shorter 6MWD, worse functional class, and reduced survival in PAH patients [8, 12]. The concurrent enrichment of pro‐inflammatory eicosanoids in Meth‐APAH, including LTB4 metabolite 12‐oxo‐LTB4 and a prostaglandin D1 metabolite suggests a shift in the prostacyclin‐thromboxane A2 balance toward vasoconstriction, proliferation, and thrombosis [15]. These findings are consistent with the known pro‐inflammatory effects of methamphetamine on pulmonary vasculature through macrophage activation, oxidative stress, and NLRP3 inflammasome activation, as well as methamphetamine‐induced mitochondrial dysfunction through inappropriate enhancement of oxidative phosphorylation and impaired electron transport [2, 16].

To our knowledge there are no other large‐scale metabolomics studies comparing metabolic signatures of Meth‐APAH and IPAH to reference. When considering the metabolic effects of methamphetamine outside of PAH, prior metabolomic studies have demonstrated perturbations in amino acid metabolism including elevated circulating histidine and alterations in arginine‐proline metabolism [17, 18, 19]. Additionally, humans with methamphetamine use have been shown to have higher circulating amounts of toxic metabolites of the kynurenine pathway including 3‐hydroxykynurenine and quinolinic acid consistent with IDO/TDO‐mediated tryptophan catabolism [20] —lending systemic context to the kynurenine pathway enrichment observed in our Meth‐APAH cohort. Our study cannot fully separate the direct metabolic effects of methamphetamine from the downstream consequences of methamphetamine‐induced pulmonary vascular disease, however, most of our Meth‐APAH group had formerly used methamphetamine and for those who reported active use, methamphetamine and its metabolites are generally cleared within 3–4 days, suggesting that the metabolomic differences observed in this study likely reflect downstream biological consequences of chronic methamphetamine exposure rather than the presence of the drug or its direct metabolites [21, 22]. Thus these results suggest that the disproportionate kynurenine pathway activation and pro‐inflammatory eicosanoid production may contribute to the distinct pathobiology and worse clinical outcomes of Meth‐APAH irrespective of their proximate mechanism when compared with IPAH.

In contrast, IPAH demonstrated relative enrichment of anti‐inflammatory SPM mediators, specifically Maresin 2. An SPM in the same family, Maresin 1, has been shown to reverse pulmonary vascular remodeling through inhibition of pulmonary arterial smooth muscle cell proliferation and improvement in mitochondrial function in animal models [23, 24]. The relative deficiency of these pro‐resolving pathways in Meth‐APAH compared with IPAH may contribute to impaired resolution of vascular inflammation and worse clinical outcomes observed in methamphetamine‐associated disease. These findings suggest that Meth‐APAH and IPAH differ fundamentally in their capacity for inflammatory resolution, with potential implications for subtype‐specific therapeutic approaches targeting these pathways [25]. While both conditions share features of metabolic dysfunction characteristic of PAH—including increased glycolysis and impaired mitochondrial respiration—the specific patterns of bioenergetic alterations may differ, potentially reflecting distinct mechanisms of pulmonary vascular injury [8, 26, 27].

Two metabolites, 20‐HDoHE (a downstream metabolite of omega‐3 fatty acid docosahexaenoic acid (DHA)), and N‐acetylasparagine, demonstrated concordant associations with increased mortality risk within Meth‐APAH, suggesting alignment between metabolite enrichment and adverse outcomes in this PAH subgroup. N‐acetylasparagine, an N‐acetylated amino acid derivative, has not specifically been implicated in PAH but may reflect perturbations in asparagine/aspartate metabolism consistent with dysregulated amino acid metabolism in PAH [28]. 20‐HDoHE has not been characterized in detail within pulmonary hypertension or cardio‐inflammatory disorders but the omega‐3 fatty acid derivative DHA on the other hand has been investigated in PH and has been shown to mitigate disease by acting as a vasodilator, inhibiting pulmonary vascular remodeling, and preventing RV hypertrophy in murine models [29, 30, 31]. The contrary finding of an association between this metabolite and higher mortality in our study could possibly be a severity marker of increased DHA mobilization, however, this finding requires validation.

This study has several limitations. The cross‐sectional design limits inference on temporal or treatment‐related metabolic changes. Regarding data sampling, our hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry (HILIC‐MS) compound library covers endogenous metabolites and does not include xenobiotics such as methamphetamine or its breakdown products as reference standards; future work including targeted drug metabolite assays would provide a more complete characterization. Additionally, matching was performed on hemodynamic values from diagnostic RHC while plasma sampling occurred at a separate enrollment visit, such that hemodynamic status may not have precisely reflected matching values at the time of metabolomic profiling. Mortality analyses were exploratory given the small sample size, and circulating metabolomic profiles reflect systemic metabolism and may not directly capture tissue‐specific processes within the pulmonary vasculature or right ventricle. Finally, the lack of an external validation cohort warrants confirmation of these findings in independent populations.

Overall, our findings suggest that Meth‐APAH and IPAH each have distinct metabolic phenotypes, with two Meth‐APAH enriched metabolites also demonstrating increased risk of mortality. Divergent profiles regarding amino acid metabolism, omega‐3 PUFAs, and bioactive eicosanoids may suggest fundamentally different underlying pathophysiologic processes or differences in response to various insults to the pulmonary vasculature. Further research is needed to elucidate whether distinct metabolomic signatures contribute to worse clinical outcomes or could serve as therapeutic targets in Meth‐APAH.
