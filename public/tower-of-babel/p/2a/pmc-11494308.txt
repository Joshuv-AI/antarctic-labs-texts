Deep silicification–assisted long-term preservation of structural and genomic information across biospecies: From micro to macro
Authors: Ke Liang, Liang Zhou, Qi Lei, Ting Ruan, Minmin Zhang, Jimin Guo, C. Jeffrey Brinker, Wei Zhu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Significance“The concurrent preservation of morphological, structural, and genomic aspects in biological samples provides researchers with comprehensive data to comprehend internal tissue architecture, cellular morphology, and genetic information dynamics, thereby fostering a holistic understanding of biological phenomena and disease mechanisms. Presently, only cryopreservation, chemical reagent fixation, and bio-plasticization are capable of simultaneously preserving these critical aspects. Here, we introduce ‘deep silicification’, which operates at room temperature without the requirement for fixation, enabling long-term preservation of structural and morphological integrity alongside genomic information across biospecies ranging from microorganisms to macroscopic entities. With its simplicity and reliability, “deep silicification” represents a paradigm shift in biological sample storage, holding promise for mitigating species extinctions, fostering biodiversity, and potentially reversing such trends in the future.”

Preserving morphological and structural attributes within biological samples furnishes researchers with crucial materials for delving into the intricate details of internal tissue architecture, cellular morphology, and organ functionality across organisms (1–4). Meanwhile, the genome serves as the conduit for genetic information within organisms, and its preservation enables scientists to scrutinize the structural intricacies, functional dynamics, and regulatory mechanisms inherent to genes (3, 5–7). Concurrent preservation of the morphological, structural, and genomic aspects of biological samples yields a more comprehensive and multidimensional dataset, thereby facilitating a holistic understanding of biological phenomena and the underlying mechanisms of diseases (8–11).

Currently, methods capable of concurrently preserving the morphology, structure, and genomic information of biological samples over extended periods primarily include the following (12, 13): 1) Cryopreservation—freezing biological samples at low-temperature to decelerate their metabolic processes (14). However, this approach consumes relatively high energy, and repetitive freezing/thawing cycles may damage DNA structure (12); 2) Chemical reagent fixation—using chemical agents like formaldehyde to fix proteins in biological samples, followed by encapsulation through paraffin embedding (12, 15, 16). Nonetheless, formalin fixation may induce DNA fragmentation and lesions, reducing the yield of extracted DNA and leading to undesired sequence artifacts that interfere with DNA profiling (17); 3) Bioplasticization technology—by substituting water molecules within tissue cells with liquid polymer compounds, facilitating comprehensive tissue structure retention and long-term storage (18–20). However, this technique is intricate and costly, and the integral heat treatment process may compromise molecular information. To date, there are still limited methodologies that possess the capability to concurrently safeguard the morphology, structure, and genomic data of biological specimens. Therefore, there arises an urgent need to innovate novel technologies that can deliver prolonged, stable, and high-fidelity storage of both morphological/structural features and genetic information under ambient conditions, all while remaining environmentally sustainable and economically viable.

Natural fossilization represents a complex process requiring the alignment of optimal conditions over long time scales, accomplished through intricate geological mechanisms (21–23). A pivotal breakthrough emerged in 2006 with the successful extraction of ancient DNA from Neanderthal and mammoth fossils dating back hundreds of thousands of years (24). Conversely, our preceding investigations led to the development of a self-limited, protein-catalyzed silicification technology designed to emulate the natural fossilization process (25). This process entails the initial generation of neutrally charged monosilicic acid under low pH conditions (~pH 3.0), subsequent replacement of silicic acid with hydrogen-bonded water at intra- and extracellular interfaces, and eventual condensation of silicic acid into amorphous silica throughout the entire cell, facilitated by proximal proteins (25, 26). While successfully replicating biological sample structures, this method encounters challenges stemming from the prefixation step employing formalin and unavoidable DNA degradation catalyzed by nucleases during prolonged silicification (27–29). Furthermore, we pioneered cryosilicification technology to achieve the long-term preservation of whole blood DNA for over one thousand years (thermally equivalent) (30). This approach entails the formation of nanosized ice crystals during the freezing stage to facilitate the enrichment and deposition of silicic acid within cells. Nonetheless, this technique proves unsuitable for macroscopic-scale samples (e.g., tissue, organ) due to reliance on cryogenic equipment and the risk of cryoinjury.

To address the limitations inherent in current silicification methodologies, we introduce an alternative technology termed “deep silicification,” which operates at room temperature without the need for fixation. The key innovation lies in the synergy between ethanol and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) during the self-limited, protein-catalyzed silicification process (31). Ethanol serves to immobilize biosamples without disrupting the silicification process, effectively suppressing enzyme activity and microbial metabolic reproduction. Concurrently, a small proportion of DMSO dramatically enhances the penetration and accumulation of silicic acid within bioorganisms, resulting in a 30-fold increase in silica concentration within cells, and thus significantly reinforces the interaction between inorganic silica and bioorganisms. Moreover, the versatility of deep silicification technology enables comprehensive preservation of organismal structure and morphology across various scales, encompassing microorganisms (e.g., bacteria and cells) to macroscopic entities (e.g., mollusks and mammalian organs) (Fig. 1 A and B). Accelerated aging experiments demonstrate the ability to store genomic information in biological samples at room temperature for thousands of years, exhibiting a substantial enhancement by factors exceeding 4,723-fold compared to control samples. Whole-genome sequencing analyses confirm virtually complete preservation of genetic integrity, with nearly 100% fidelity observed before and after silicification. Characterized by its simplicity, reliability, and cost-effectiveness, the “deep silicification” strategy holds immense potential to revolutionize the storage of biological samples.

Results

Intracellular Deep Silicification.

First, 20 μL of 1 M HCl was added to 20 mL of 0.9% saline (154 mM NaCl) solution to prepare pH 3 saline solution. Then, TMOS was added to pH 3 saline solution. After full dissolution, absolute alcohol and DMSO were added to achieve silicification solution. At the cellular scale, the specific operational procedure involved dispersing approximately 2 × 106 murine fibroblasts (L929) in 1 mL of the silicification solution and incubating them for 3 h. Subsequently, two washes with a 1 × PBS solution were conducted. The silicified cells were then placed in a constant temperature and humidity box at 70 °C with 60% relative humidity (RH) and maintained for 24 h after rinsing. The protective efficacy of varying ethanol concentrations (v/v) in the silicification solution on DNA within the silicified cells was assessed. Gel electrophoresis revealed that at ethanol concentrations of 60% or lower, DNA extraction from the cells exhibited significant degradation. Conversely, at an ethanol concentration of 80%, both the integrity and yield of DNA extracted from silicified cells closely matched those from untreated cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Therefore, for our experiments, an 80% ethanol concentration was considered optimal.

To characterize the deposition of silica on the cells, Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) were employed. Following deep silicification at room temperature, additional bands emerged in the FTIR spectrum at 1,067, 959, 797, and 452 cm−1, attributed to asymmetric stretching of Si-O and Si-OH (32), symmetric stretching of Si-O, and bending vibration of Si-O-Si, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Notably, the bands at 1,651 and 1,533 cm−1 corresponded to the stretching modes of Amide I and II associated with protein amino acids remained completely unchanged, verifying that the proteinaceous network remains largely unperturbed and substantiating the role of proteins as catalysts. SEM images depicted a rough surface of silicified cells, highlighting the presence of amorphous silica deposits (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) images further affirmed the uniform distribution of carbon, oxygen, nitrogen, and silicon in the cells (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Quantification of silica content by ICP-OES spectroscopy elucidated that higher silica precursor concentrations result in higher silica contents of silicified cells, and that a concentration of 50 mM results in ~30.03 mg silica per mL of cell (Fig. 2 B and C).

To assess the effectiveness of the deep silicification method in DNA preservation, L929 cells were subjected to preservation using formalin, cryosilicification, and deep silicification, respectively, followed by an accelerated aging treatment at 70 °C with 60% RH. After 24 h, cells preserved in formalin solution exhibited no discernible DNA bands in the gel electrophoresis lane (33). The DNA bands of cells preserved using cryosilicification displayed a pronounced trailing phenomenon, whereas those of cells preserved by the deep silicification method appeared clear, intact, and similar to those of fresh cells (Fig. 2D). Following a 7-d aging process, deep silicified cells, despite exhibiting some trailing DNA bands, showed notably higher overall position and significantly less degradation than cells stored by cryosilicification (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Furthermore, ICP-OES data showed that cells preserved through deep silicification exhibited significantly higher silicon content per cell (27.71 pg) relative to those preserved using cryosilicification (0.91 pg), indicating a ca. 30-fold increase in silicon content (Fig. 2E). These findings substantiate the superior DNA protection afforded by deep silicification compared to conventional formalin and previously reported cryosilicification methods.

The 3D confocal fluorescence microscopy images depicted nuclei, stained blue, enveloped by a green fluorescent silica matrix, resulting in a dense intracellular silica structure. Z-stack confocal fluorescence imaging revealed the presence of these green silica signals intranuclearly (Fig. 2F and SI Appendix, Fig. S8). Furthermore, cells subjected to deep silicification were sectioned using a microtome and subsequently examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Notably, a substantial amount of amorphous silica encased the nuclei centrally, with nano-silica particles adeptly traversing the nuclear envelope. EDS imaging further elucidated the intracellular distribution of nitrogen and silicon (Fig. 2 G and H).

Control cells stored in PBS, unsilicified cells (characterized by the absence of TMOS compared to the deeply silicified group), and deeply silicified cells were concurrently exposed to an environment of 70 °C with 60% RH to assess the stability of DNA. On day 1, severe DNA degradation was observed in the control and unsilicified cells, while clear DNA bands were still evident in the deeply silicified group (Fig. 2I). As time progressed, DNA degradation became inevitable. By day 7, none of the three groups exhibited characteristic DNA bands. However, significantly greater integrity of DNA fragments was observed in the deeply silicified protected cells compared to the other two groups. Apparently, DNA degradation was effectively inhibited by the inorganic silica network structure present both inside and outside the cell nuclei, akin to a “fossil” (34, 35). Moreover, target gene fragments of DNA encoding the small subunit of mice ribosomes (18S rDNA) were amplified from extracted DNA using PCR. Four distinct primer pairs were designed to generate DNA fragments of lengths 241, 532, 883, and 1,072 bp, denoted as M1 through M4. DNA fragmentation was further confirmed by gel electrophoresis. Interestingly, despite pronounced DNA degradation in the control and unsilicified groups on both day 1 and day 7, the yield of target genes M1 through M4 amplified by PCR was comparable to that of deeply silicified samples. By day 14, the M3 and M4 bands of the control and unsilicified groups became blurry or disappeared, whereas the gene yield for all target genes in the deep silicification group remained above 88% (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). On day 21, a yield of 54.16% for M4 was still maintained in the deeply silicified group. All the results highlight the potential of the deep silicification method for sustained DNA protection within cellular specimens (Fig. 2 J and K).

Bacterial Silicification.

Bacteria play crucial roles in disease prevention, development, and treatment (36, 37). To investigate the potential protective effect of deep silicification on bacterial DNA, Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) was selected (Fig. 3A). As depicted in Fig. 3 B and C, TEM images revealed that EcN exhibited a rod-like structure with well-defined boundaries, whereas after silicification, an obvious silica shell was observed. The preservation characteristics of silicified EcN were again evaluated through an aging test. In Fig. 3D, Gel electrophoresis results demonstrated that DNA signature bands from the silicified sample remained observable on the initial day, while DNA from the other groups had undergone significant deterioration. By the 14th day, silicified EcN exhibited evident degradation, whereas DNA from the other groups was nearly obliterated (Fig. 3D). Next, PCR was employed to amplify DNA target gene segments encoding ribosomal subunits (16S rDNA) of EcN. The amplified fragments, designated as E1 through E4, had lengths of 179, 233, 742, and 1,032 base pairs (bp), respectively. On the 7th day, the control group exhibited a minimal yield of 12.45% for the E3 fragment, with the E4 fragment undetectable. Conversely, the group lacking silicification demonstrated yields of 73.76% and 48.58% for the E3 and E4 fragments, respectively. However, the silicified group exhibited distinctly superior yields of 91.72% and 74.33% for the E3 and E4 fragments, respectively (Fig. 3 E and F). By the 14th day, all three groups showed the absence of the E4 gene band. In the control E. coli sample, only fragments of the E1 gene were detected, resulting in a modest proportion of 21.52%. Silicified EcN revealed a 2.5-fold increase in the production of the E3 gene segment compared to the unsilicified counterpart (Fig. 3G).

Note that in this instance, although deep silicification is effective, its efficiency in preserving bacterial DNA seems less pronounced than that in eukaryotic cells. Two key factors account for this: 1) The dense bacterial cell wall impedes the permeation of silicic acid into the bacterial interior (38, 39); 2) Unlike eukaryotes, bacterial DNA lacks histone protein shielding (40, 41), being directly situated in the cytoplasm, thereby limiting the catalytic process of silica condensation onto chromatin structures.

Deep Silicification of Macroscopic Organisms.

To assess the potential versatility of deep silicification across varying scales, we employed earthworms as biological specimens to examine its preservation effects on macroscopic organism structures and DNA. In contrast to microscopic cell and bacterial samples, macroscopic organisms exhibit heightened complexity, featuring epithelial tissues, connective tissues, and protective skin organs acting as barriers against external stimuli (42). Given this intricacy, we extended the silicification duration to 2 wk and elevated the concentration of DMSO to 20% v/v to facilitate the penetration of the silica precursor into the biological specimens. Furthermore, we investigated appropriate concentrations of ethanol and TMOS for macroscopic organism samples. Based on gel electrophoresis results, we utilized a silica precursor solution containing 60% ethanol and 50 mM TMOS in subsequent experiments (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 A and B).

Analogous to silicified cells, additional bands emerged in the FTIR spectrum at 1,015, 950, 788, and 452 cm−1, attributed to the asymmetric stretching of Si-O and Si-OH, the symmetric stretching of Si-O, and the bending vibration of Si-O-Si, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). To quantitatively assess the silica content in silicified earthworm samples, we evaluated the fresh weight and postdrying mass of earthworm specimens. Surprisingly, the residual mass of the silicified group was 17.15% greater than that of the unsilicified group, indicating a mass increase attributable to silica deposition (SI Appendix, Fig. S12). Subsequently, earthworm samples with a mass of ca. 200 mg were used for different preservation methods. After drying, significant shrinkage was observed due to water loss in the control and unsilicified groups. Conversely, the silicified group exhibited visible silica deposition on the surface and maintained an intact structure with clear segments (SI Appendix, Fig. S13). SEM images revealed abundant silica material deposition (Fig. 4 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S14), which provide excellent support and protection for the overall structure. To assess the preservation effect on the structure of earthworm samples, silicified samples were calcined to remove all organic matter, which serves as a template for silica deposition in a tube furnace at 550 °C for 10 h, forming silica replicas. As shown in Fig. 4 C and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S15, longitudinal sections exhibited well-preserved hierarchical muscle tissue, while fiber replicas with a diameter of around 180 nm were visible in transverse sections. Interestingly, 3D reconstructed images from Micro-CT scans showed a higher density of silicified earthworms compared to the control group, with well-defined boundaries and distinct structures of segments (Fig. 4 F and H).

Moreover, silicified earthworms were subjected to accelerated aging test. As shown on day 1, both the control and unsilicified groups showed a lack of prominent bands and significant degradation in DNA. In contrast, the silicified group exhibited an intact main band, with DNA quality comparable to fresh samples. From day 7 onward, although the DNA of the silicified group showed varying degrees of degradation, its quality remained superior to the other groups (Fig. 4I). To further confirm DNA fragmentation, PCR amplification was performed on target gene fragments encoding earthworm 18S rDNA with lengths of 196, 494, 791, and 1,085 bp, designated Q1 to Q4. On day 14, gene bands Q1 to Q4 from the control group were all blurred, with yields of 15.22%, 25.42%, 17.32%, and 9.57%, respectively (Fig. 4 J and K). In contrast, the silicified group exhibited significantly higher yields of Q1 to Q4 gene fragments: 52.99%, 95.17%, 86.15%, and 85.52%, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S16). By day 21, the Q1 to Q4 gene yields in the silicified group were markedly elevated, surpassing those of the control group by 76%, 63%, 71%, and 60%, respectively.

To quantitatively evaluate the degradation rate of macroscale biological sample DNA, qPCR was utilized to analyze the Q1 target gene in each sample on days 1, 7, 14, and 21. On day 1, the silicified group exhibited a detection rate of 67.8% for Q1, whereas the control and unsilicified groups displayed rates of only 1.2% and 28.1%, respectively. Remarkably, even on day 14, the silicified group retained 34.2% of Q1 (Fig. 4L). Note that in the initial stages, while the samples remain in a wet state, the degradation rates of control samples increase significantly due to the synergistic effects of enzymatic activities, primarily DNase, and environmental factors such as temperature fluctuations and oxidative stress. However, within the silicified matrix, these degradative agents are effectively sequestered, and their activities are inhibited. Employing a thermodynamics-driven first-order degradation kinetic model (43–45), the decay rate constants (k) for each sample group at 70 °C were calculated, resulting in values of 1.44 × 10−6, 8.74 × 10−7, and 3.26 × 10−7 for the control, unsilicified, and deeply silicified groups, respectively (SI Appendix, Table S1). Encouragingly, the deeply silicified group demonstrated degradation rates merely 25.97% and 42.79% of those observed in the control and unsilicified groups, respectively, suggesting that amorphous silica deposition within the organism can effectively inhibit DNA degradation. To extrapolate the accelerated aging data to realistic storage temperatures, Arrhenius-type activation energies (EA) were computed based on the temperature dependence of the decay rates for the control, unsilicified, and deeply silicified groups: 49.17, 78.23, and 167.44 kJ/mol, respectively (Fig. 4M and SI Appendix, Table S2). The extrapolated storage half-life for the silicified earthworm at 20 °C was approximately 1,347.4 y, whereas the half-lives for the control and unsilicified groups were only 0.3 y and 2.6 y, respectively. Overall, the stability of DNA in the silicified samples exhibited a significant enhancement by factors of 4,723-fold and 517-fold, respectively, compared to the control and unsilicified groups (Fig. 4N).

Deeply Silicified Organs.

Mammalian organs possess more complex structures and internal microenvironments (46, 47). To evaluate the effectiveness of deep silicification for preserving mammalian macroscopic samples, we subjected the hearts and livers of BABL/c mice to silicification. SEM images of the heart surface and cardiac profile revealed that the control group exhibited a smooth surface with erythrocytes accumulating around the cardiac blood vessels, displaying a biconcave shape with a smooth surface. In contrast, in the silicified group, the heart surface exhibited distinct roughness, with surface erythrocytes tightly wrapped by silica (Fig. 5 A and B). Moreover, we examined the degree of silicification of the inner tissues by dissecting the BABL/c mice hearts horizontally. Valvular structures between the chambers of the heart were found covered with abundant “stone” silica deposits. Under magnification, the silicified heart valve structure was clearly visible, retaining blood cells encapsulated in nanoscale silica (SI Appendix, Fig. S17). SEM images of the silica replicas of the heart revealed intact preservation of myocardial trabeculae and blood cell silicon replicas on the columnar structures (Fig. 5 C–E), primarily attributed to the deposition of amorphous silica in cells and tissues, which successfully immobilized and preserved the micro- and macroscopic structures

Paraffin-embedded and H&E-stained sections of unsilicified and deeply silicified mice liver samples were compared and analyzed. The results revealed that the hepatocytes in the sections of the unsilicified samples were well-defined, with visible nuclei and cytoplasm. Conversely, the tissue sections of the deeply silicified samples appeared blurred due to the abundance of white silica particles deposited on the hepatocytes (SI Appendix, Fig. S18). Importantly, the blurred tissue sections observed in the silicified group can be attributed to the infiltration of the silicification solution into the organ, resulting in the deposition of dense silica materials between the cells and tissues. Furthermore, genomic DNA extracted from the silicified liver samples underwent gel electrophoresis, showing clear and intact DNA bands without significant degradation (SI Appendix, Fig. S19).

The DNA gel electrophoresis outcomes from the liver sample aging experiment mirrored those observed in the silicified earthworm samples. On day 1, DNA from the control and unsilicified groups displayed no discernible band and suffered severe degradation, while the silicified group showcased a more intact primary band, akin to that of freshly extracted sample DNA (Fig. 5F). To further assess DNA fragmentation, PCR amplification was conducted on target gene segments M1 through M4. By day 1, the control group exhibited significant loss of the M4 gene fragment, with only a small portion of the short M1 fragment gene remaining by day 14. Similarly, the unsilicified group lost M3 and M4 gene fragments by day 14, with only the M1 fragment remaining distinctly visible, whereas the silicified group maintained the ability to amplify the large M4 fragment gene (Fig. 5G). Quantitative analysis revealed that by day 7, M3 and M4 yields in the control and unsilicified groups were merely 3%, whereas those in the silicified group were 91.78% and 53.48%, respectively. Deep silicification of liver samples preserved a greater number of large fragment genes (Fig. 5H).

To ensure the stable preservation of genetic information in biological samples, whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was conducted on freshly obtained, deeply silicified liver samples. Following deep silicification, the percentage of bases compared to the reference genome remained unchanged, with the coverage of the silicified group at 98.34% compared to 98.53% in the fresh sample (Fig. 5I). These findings confirm that deep silicification treatment does not alter the whole-genome information of the samples. Additionally, the silicified samples underwent an aging experiment at 70 °C and 60% RH for 7 d (equivalent to nearly a century of storage at room temperature). The silicified samples exhibited a gene coverage rate of ≥1×, representing approximately 97.02% of the coverage rate of the fresh samples (compared with the reference gene, the coverage of fresh samples ≥1× was 95.49%, and the coverage of silicified samples was 92.64%) (Fig. 5J).

Discussion

In summary, “deep silicification” effectively emulates natural fossil formation processes, enabling the in situ encapsulation and long-term preservation of genomes within organisms. This method involves introducing inorganic siloxane precursors into cells without disrupting cellular structures or intracellular DNA, and controlling precursor diffusion, hydrolysis, and polycondensation using ethanol and a small amount of the permeable solvent DMSO. Consequently, this results in complete encapsulation of cellular structures and DNA.

The process achieves “in situ preservation” of biological samples and their genetic information through a simple, one-step silicification at room temperature. This approach enhances protection, eliminates the need for extensive freezing equipment, reduces preservation costs, and facilitates outdoor collection and storage of biological samples. Stability assessments, whole-genome sequencing, and accelerated aging studies demonstrate that deep silicified samples are robust against external stressors, such as prolonged exposure to high temperatures and humidity. Accelerated aging experiments show the potential to store genomic information at room temperature for thousands of years, with preservation enhancement exceeding 4,723-fold compared to control samples. Whole-genome sequencing confirms near-complete genetic integrity, with nearly 100% fidelity observed pre- and postsilicification.

Moreover, deep silicification provides consistent protection across various scales, achieving “fossil-like” encapsulation and preservation from micrometer-sized bacteria and cells to macroscopic soft-bodied animals and mammalian organs. This technique preserves sample morphology and structure while enabling “in situ encapsulation and on-demand extraction” of genomic material. Compared to various previous methods such as cryopreservation, formalin fixation, DNA extraction and preservation, and cryosilicification, our present study demonstrates significant advantages in DNA preservation, structural integrity maintenance, protein conservation, and considerations of time, effort, toxicity, and energy efficiency (SI Appendix, Table S3). Looking forward, the preservation of structural and genomic information through deep silicification offers significant potential for mitigating species extinctions, and fostering biodiversity conservation in the future.

Materials and Methods

A full description of materials and methods is available in SI Appendix.

Silicification Solution Preparation.

To create a pH 3 saline solution suitable for microbiological samples, the process involves adding 20 μL of 1 M HCl to 20 mL of 0.9% saline (which corresponds to 154 mM NaCl). After this, 750 μL of TMOS is mixed into the 20 mL of the adjusted saline solution. Once the TMOS is fully dissolved, 80 mL of absolute alcohol and 1 mL of DMSO are added to the mixture, producing a 50 mM silicification solution. For handling organs and larger biological samples, the same procedure is followed, except that after the TMOS dissolution, 60 mL of absolute alcohol and 20 mL of DMSO are added instead, again resulting in a 50 mM silicification solution.

Biological Samples Silicification.

500 µL E. coli or 2 × 106 L929 cells were added to a tube, rinsed with 1 × PBS for 2 times, and then added 1 mL silicification solution. Then the tube was placed on the rotary mixer and rotated at 15 rpm for 3 h at room temperature. Organs, earthworms, or larger scale biological samples were added to a tube containing silicification solution at room temperature for 2 wk. The volume ratio of larger scale biological samples to silicification solution was kept at more than 1:10 (biological samples: silicification solution; v/v). Meantime, renew silicification solution every 3 d.

Desilication Treatment.

The silicified E. coli or L929 cells were immersed in 1 mL of etching solution (BOE: 1 × PBS = 1: 9; v/v) for 5 min to etch the silica. The desilicified E. coli or cells were rinsed with 1 × PBS for 2 times. The silicified organs or earthworm samples were ground with a mortar, and weighed m mg samples into a tube according to the following equation:M015=M1m,

where 15 is the extraction mass of fresh organs or earthworms, M0 is the mass of original organs or earthworms and M1 is the mass of silicified organs or earthworms. Then, 1 mL etching solution is added to the tube for 5 min to etch the silica. The desilicified organs or earthworm samples were rinsed with 1 × PBS for 2 times.

PCR Assays.

PCRs were performed using the Bio-Rad C1000 Touch™ PCR System with the primer sequences listed in SI Appendix, Table S4. The reaction protocol was as follows: 25 μL of Taq PCR Master Mix (2×), 4 μL of primer mixture, 2 μL of template, and 19 μL of water were added to the reaction tubes. The specific parameters of the PCR program are listed in SI Appendix, Table S5.

Accelerated Aging Test.

The accelerated aging test was performed according to previous studies with slight modifications. Cells or E. coli samples were transferred into Eppendorf tubes with open lids, and earthworm or mice organs were placed in glass dishes and stored at 70 °C and 60% RH in a CTHL-150 Temp&Humidity Chamber (STIK, China). To better mimic the natural aging process, the samples undergo direct air-drying immediately after silicification without any subsequent drying treatments. After different storage times, the DNA from each sample was extracted for further analysis.

Biological Sample Calcination.

Referring to previous studies, the silicified samples were placed in high temperature resistant glass dishes and calcined in a tube furnace at 550 °C for 10 h. Air was continuously introduced during the calcination process. The heating process of the tube furnace was controlled at 5 °C/min, and the cooling process was not required.

qPCR Assays.

The qPCR was performed to accurately quantify the content of the target gene fragments at different time points and the results were analyzed using the Bio-Rad CFX Connect Real-Time PCR system. The extracted sample DNA was diluted 100-fold with Milli-Q water and used as DNA template during qPCR amplification. The qPCR system consisted of the following composition of reagents: 10 µL of BeyoFast™ SYBR Green qPCR mix (2×), 1 µL of 10 µM primer mix (Q1, Forward primers 5′-3′: CCGTAACTCTGATGACTCTGG, and Reverse primer 5′-3′: CTGCCTTCCTTGGATGTG), and 9 µL diluted DNA template. Degradation Kinetics of DNA Storage.

The intricate process of DNA degradation over time can be mathematically modeled through the equation (43–45):-dCdt=kC,

where C represents the DNA concentration and k is the decay rate constant. Integrating this equation yields, providing a framework to quantify the rate of DNA degradation:lnC=-kt+lnC0.

To empirically validate this model, qPCR (quantitative PCR) was employed to analyze the concentration of a specific DNA fragment, Q1 (196 bp), encoding for earthworm 18S rDNA, during an accelerated aging study. By plotting the logarithm of qPCR-measured DNA concentrations against time and applying linear regression analysis, the decay rates were accurately determined.

Further delving into the temperature-dependent nature of DNA degradation, the Arrhenius equation was utilized:kT=Ae-EART,

where EA is the activation energy, kT is the degradation kinetic constant at a given temperature T, and R is the universal gas constant:lnkT=-EART+lnk0.

This equation was fitted to the kinetic data, enabling the calculation of the activation energy.

With this information, the degradation kinetic constant kT for any given temperature T could be predicted using the Arrhenius equation:kT=Ae-EART.

Moreover, the half-life (t1/2) of the DNA at a specific temperature was calculated using the formula, offering insights into the longevity of DNA under varying conditions:t1/2=ln2kT.

Whole-Genomic Sequencing (WGS).

To gain a comprehensive understanding of the genomic changes that occur during the silicification process, WGS was performed on genomic DNA extracted from both fresh and deeply silicified mice liver samples. The BALB/cJ mouse genome assembly version, sourced from the NCBI database, served as the reference sequence for this analysis.
