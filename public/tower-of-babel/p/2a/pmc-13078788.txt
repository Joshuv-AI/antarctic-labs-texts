Case Report--Late Presentation of Shone’s Complex With Severe Left Ventricular Dysfunction
Authors: Refal Mahrouqi, Asayil Almarjan, Ahmed Sultan, Sheeren Khaled, Elnazeer Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundShone’s complex is a rare Congenital Heart Disease (CHD), often including supravalvular mitral ring, parachute Mitral Valve (MV), subaortic stenosis, and coarctation of the aorta. Variants of this complex have been reported with considerable anatomical and clinical heterogeneity.Case presentationA 14-year-old girl with severe cachexia and cardiac failure, referred for CHD evaluation. Echocardiography and CT revealed a bicuspid Aortic Valve (AV), severe valvular and subvalvular aortic stenosis, a subaortic membrane, a muscle ridge causing Left Ventricular Outflow Tract (LVOT) obstruction, dilated LV with EF 25–30%, and a ventricular septal defect.InterventionMV replacement, Konno procedure with LVOT and annular enlargement, AV replacement, and Tricuspid Valve (TV) repair. She had an uneventful ICU course.

Introduction

Shone’s complex, first described, refers to a spectrum of left-sided obstructive cardiac lesions. The complete form includes supravalvular mitral ring, parachute mitral valve, subaortic stenosis, and coarctation of the aorta [1]. Incomplete forms, like in our patient, are more common. Despite advances in surgical strategies, patients with multi-level obstruction and ventricular dysfunction remain at high risk [2]. Recent studies continue to highlight the variability in presentation and the role of tailored surgical management [3].

2.2. Postoperative course

The patient was transferred to the CSICU in stable condition. She was extubated within 24 hours, and chest tubes were removed on postoperative day 2. The CSICU stay was 4 days. Postoperatively, she developed left bundle branch block (Fig. 4), followed by complete heart block on postoperative day 10, necessitating permanent pacemaker implantation. She was subsequently discharged in good condition. The patient completed two months of structured postoperative follow-up in both the cardiac surgery and anticoagulation (INR) clinics at our institution. During this period, she remained clinically stable, with well-functioning prosthetic valve parameters on echocardiography and consistently therapeutic INR levels without complications. As a non-Saudi Umrah patient, she returned to her home country (Yemen) after completing the early critical postoperative phase. Although long-term follow-up at our center was not feasible due to her return abroad, she was discharged in stable condition with clear medical instructions and arrangements for continued care in her local healthcare system.

Discussion

Shone’s complex represents a spectrum of obstructive lesions affecting the left heart, and most patients present in childhood with variable severity depending on the number of obstructive components [3,4]. Our patient demonstrated an incomplete variant consisting of a parachute mitral valve, supravalvular band, subaortic membrane, bicuspid aortic valve with severe stenosis, and associated VSD. The absence of coarctation distinguished it from the classical full form.

Late presentation in adolescence with severe LV dilation and reduced ejection fraction is uncommon. Most published reports describe earlier detection due to symptomatic heart failure or incidentally during evaluation for murmurs [3,5]. The degree of LV dysfunction in our case (EF 25–30%) reflects the long-standing obstructive physiology and underscores the importance of timely diagnosis.

Surgical management of Shone’s complex is challenging and depends on the specific constellation of lesions. While some authors recommend staged repair [6,7]. Combined procedures are often necessary when patients present later with multi-level obstruction. In our patient, simultaneous mitral valve replacement, Konno procedure with aortic valve replacement, and tricuspid valve repair provided satisfactory anatomical and functional correction. Similar complex reconstructions have been reported with good short-term survival, though long-term outcomes remain guarded [8].

The occurrence of complete heart block requiring permanent pacemaker implantation has been reported in patients with Shone’s complex, either in the early postoperative period following complex LVOT and septal interventions or as a late-onset conduction disturbance, reflecting the vulnerability of the conduction system in this condition [9]. This highlights the need for long-term rhythm surveillance in such patients. Furthermore, systematic reviews have emphasized the high incidence of postoperative complications in Shone’s complex surgery, underscoring the importance of close follow-up [3,10].

Conclusion

Shone’s complex remains a challenging congenital anomaly with significant anatomical variability. Our case underscores the importance of early recognition, multimodal imaging, and individualized surgical strategies. Despite low EF and multiple valve pathologies, complex reconstruction can lead to favorable outcomes.
