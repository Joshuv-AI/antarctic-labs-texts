Hydrochlorothiazide with salt restriction in nonsurgical hypoparathyroidism: a placebo-controlled single-blinded randomized crossover trial assessing efficacy and safety
Authors: Vivek Jha, Soham Mukherjee, Sanjay Kumar Bhadada, Nancy Sahni, Sant Ram, Pinaki Dutta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this single-center, randomized, placebo-controlled, single-blinded, crossover trial (Clinical Trial Registry-India: CTRI/2024/08/090041), we evaluated the efficacy and safety of low-dose hydrochlorothiazide (HCTZ) in adults with nonsurgical hypoparathyroidism (excluding autoimmune and obvious syndromic forms). After stabilization on fixed doses of calcium carbonate and calcitriol under a controlled low-sodium diet, 26 participants received HCTZ (12.5-25 mg/d) or placebo for 7 d, with a 15-d washout before crossover, followed by an optional 4-wk open-label HCTZ extension. The primary outcome was change in serum calcium; secondary outcomes included change in 24-h urinary calcium (UCa), fractional calcium excretion, urinary sodium, and bone turnover markers (β-CTX and P1NP). Compared to baseline (8.61 ± 0.32 mg/dL), serum calcium increased significantly with HCTZ at day 5 (9.01 ± 0.46 mg/dL) and day 8 (9.04 ± 0.52 mg/dL; p < .001), while no significant change occurred with placebo. Hydrochlorothiazide reduced 24-h UCa by −26.3% at day 8 (vs +4.7% with placebo; p < .001). These effects remained robust in sensitivity analyses adjusting for urinary sodium and when expressed per kg body weight. Fractional calcium excretion fell by 29.9% with HCTZ (p < .001). A small but statistically significant decline in serum potassium was observed at day 8 (from 4.18 to 3.95 mEq/L; p < .001), though values remained within the normal range, no participants developed hypokalemia; renal function and intact PTH (iPTH) were stable. During the extension phase, serum calcium levels rose while UCa excretion declined significantly, without any reported adverse effects. These findings indicate that low-dose HCTZ with sodium restriction safely improves calcium homeostasis in nonsurgical hypoparathyroidism—raising serum calcium and reducing calciuria—with good tolerability. Hydrochlorothiazide offers a cost-effective adjunct to standard therapy and warrants further investigation for long-term outcomes.Keywords: nonsurgical hypoparathyroidism, hydrochlorothiazide, urinary calcium, fractional calcium excretion, thiazide diuretics, calcium balance

Introduction

Idiopathic hypoparathyroidism is an uncommon endocrine disorder defined by low serum calcium, elevated phosphate levels, and a deficient or undetectable concentration of PTH.1 This biochemical imbalance can manifest with a range of neuromuscular and systemic symptoms, such as muscle cramps, paraesthesia, tetany, seizures, laryngospasm, and potentially fatal cardiac arrhythmias.2

While postsurgical hypoparathyroidism, particularly after thyroid or parathyroid surgery, accounts for most adult cases, idiopathic forms remain distinctly rare. A registry from India estimated the overall incidence of hypoparathyroidism, including idiopathic cases, at 2.6 per 100 000 person-years.3 Global data similarly attribute nearly three-quarters of cases to surgery, with autoimmune and idiopathic etiologies comprising the rest.1,4,5

Management of hypoparathyroidism typically includes calcium supplements and active vitamin D analogues (such as calcitriol or alfacalcidol) to maintain low-normal serum calcium, limit phosphate build-up, and keep urinary calcium (UCa) below 300 mg/d in men and 250 mg/d in women.6,7 However, this regimen does not replicate PTH’s physiological effects and may result in long-term renal complications due to hypercalcemia.

Persistent hypercalciuria is a common complication in hypoparathyroidism, contributing to nephrolithiasis, nephrocalcinosis, and progressive renal dysfunction.8 To mitigate this, current guidelines recommend thiazide diuretics in combination with dietary sodium restriction to reduce UCa excretion.9 While thiazides are effective in idiopathic hypercalciuria, evidence supporting their use in nonsurgical hypoparathyroidism is sparse.10,11

A few studies have focused on postsurgical cases and are characterized by small sample sizes, variable results, and methodological heterogeneity. Porter et al. evaluated chlorthalidone (50 mg/d, longer half-life than hydrochlorothiazide [HCTZ]) combined with sodium restriction in a small group of predominantly postsurgical patients and without concomitant vitamin D therapy, which limits its applicability to modern practice, where calcitriol is standard.12 Brickman et al. used very high-dose HCTZ (up to 200 mg/d) with variable responses in mixed cohorts, including only a few idiopathic cases.11 Similarly, Parfitt et al. primarily examined the physiological interaction of thiazides with calcium metabolism in the absence of PTH rather than their clinical utility, and the study was limited to postsurgical patients.13 These shortcomings highlight that evidence is limited, heterogeneous, and largely postsurgical. Our trial therefore adds incremental knowledge by focusing exclusively on nonsurgical hypoparathyroidism, using pragmatic low-dose HCTZ under standardized sodium restriction with UNa verification, and employing a randomized, placebo-controlled crossover design.

Given the limited representation of nonsurgical hypoparathyroidism in prior studies and the inconsistent impact of thiazide diuretics on calcium homeostasis, there remains a critical need to evaluate their efficacy and safety specifically in this subgroup. We hypothesized that low-dose HCTZ, combined with sodium restriction, would enhance serum calcium and reduce UCa excretion in patients with nonsurgical hypoparathyroidism, even in the absence of endogenous PTH. Because sodium intake itself strongly influences calciuria, sodium restriction was standardized and applied in both HCTZ and placebo periods. This ensured that HCTZ was the only element varied by randomization beyond sodium restriction. Better understanding their utility could aid in managing hypercalciuria more effectively, reduce dependence on high-dose calcium and active vitamin D, minimize renal risks, and offer a more sustainable treatment approach, especially in low-resource healthcare settings, where recombinant PTH (rhPTH) therapy is inaccessible to the majority of patients due to cost constraints.

This study aims to generate clinically relevant evidence to support more targeted and accessible treatment strategies for this underserved group.

Materials and methods

This study was a single-center, randomized, single-blinded, placebo-controlled, crossover clinical trial conducted at a tertiary care academic hospital in northern India between April 2024 and May 2025. The trial was designed to assess the efficacy and safety of HCTZ therapy in adult patients with nonsurgical hypoparathyroidism. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institute Ethics Committee, and all participants provided written informed consent prior to enrolment.

Eligible participants included adults aged ≥18 yr with biochemically confirmed hypoparathyroidism of nonsurgical origin. Patients with surgical, autoimmune, or syndromic causes were excluded. Comprehensive genetic testing was not performed in all participants; therefore, rare genetic etiologies cannot be excluded. For this reason, we describe the study cohort as patients with nonsurgical hypoparathyroidism with exclusion of obvious syndromic features. A detailed list of inclusion and exclusion criteria is provided in Table S1.

After screening, each participant underwent a 4-6 wk of stabilization phase during which serum calcium levels were maintained to a target range of 8.0-9.0 mg/dL using individualized titration in oral calcitriol doses. Calcitriol was modified in increments or decrements of 0.25 μg/d, with a maximum dose of 3 μg/d. Throughout the study period, participants received and maintained a fixed dose of elemental calcium (1-1.5 g daily, administered as calcium carbonate) and calcitriol. Weekly monitoring of serum calcium and phosphate levels was conducted until the target calcium range was consistently achieved for a consecutive period of 2 wk.

Following stabilization, participants entered a 4-5 d dietary control period, during which all individuals were provided a standardized diet containing 600-1300 mg/d of elemental calcium and 100-200 mEq/d of sodium. This controlled dietary intake was implemented to minimize dietary variability and establish a uniform baseline for subsequent comparisons. Daily dietary calcium intake was assessed using a validated semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire specifically developed for common Indian food items14 and compliance with sodium restriction was reinforced by dietitian counseling and objectively verified using 24-h urinary sodium excretion.

Upon completion of the control period, baseline clinical assessments included systolic and diastolic blood pressure, heart rate, and body weight. Baseline biochemical parameters comprised serum calcium (corrected for albumin), phosphate, sodium, potassium, magnesium, creatinine, albumin, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), plasma 25OHD, plasma intact PTH (iPTH), and fasting plasma glucose, along with bone turnover markers: plasma C-terminal telopeptide of type I collagen (β-CTX) and procollagen type 1 N-terminal propeptide (P1NP). Urinary parameters included 24-h UCa, sodium, and phosphorus excretion, as well as fractional UCa excretion. Albumin-adjusted serum calcium was calculated using the standard formula, and these values were used for all calcium-related analyses.15 The doses of oral calcitriol and calcium carbonate were maintained constant throughout the study and were not modified during either treatment period, as per study protocol.

Participants were then randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to 1 of 2 treatment sequences using computer-generated block randomization. Group A received HCTZ 12.5 mg/d with a low-sodium diet (50-100 mEq/d) during the first phase, while Group B received a placebo with the same dietary restriction for 7 d. Adherence to the prescribed low-sodium diet was monitored and reinforced with the support of the Department of Dietetics. Prior to randomization, all participants underwent individualized dietary counseling by trained clinical dietitians to standardize sodium intake. All participants were admitted during the dietary run-in and the first intervention phase (HCTZ or placebo), during which they received standardized meals with controlled sodium content (100-200 mEq/d) prepared under the supervision of the Department of Dietetics. Dietitians conducted in-person counseling to verify adherence and provide ongoing support. This sodium restriction was applied uniformly in both HCTZ and placebo periods, ensuring that exposure to HCTZ was the sole randomized intervention. Following a 15-d washout period, participants crossed over to the alternate treatment, ensuring that each participant received both interventions in a randomized sequence. During the outpatient crossover and extension phases, adherence to sodium restriction was reinforced through dietitian-led follow-up and objectively verified using 24-h urinary sodium measurements at each assessment. These combined measures—inpatient dietary control, structured counseling, and biochemical verification—ensured consistent sodium restriction across all study phases.

Each intervention phase spanned 7 d. On day 5 of each phase, participants underwent clinical and biochemical evaluations similar to the baseline assessment, excluding bone turnover markers. Participants received HCTZ 12.5 mg once daily for the first 4 full days, and the dose was escalated to 25 mg/d after completion of the day 5 assessments, provided the lower dose was well tolerated. The first 25 mg dose was therefore administered immediately after the day 5 evaluation, and participants continued 25 mg once daily from day 5 to day 7 under identical sodium restriction. Final assessments were performed on day 8, marking the end of each treatment period.

Day-5 assessments reflect early pharmacodynamic effects at 12.5 mg; day-8 assessments capture the incremental effect at 25 mg under identical sodium restriction. Compliance with HCTZ and placebo administration was ensured through direct supervision during the inpatient phase, with all doses dispensed by nursing staff and recorded in treatment logs. During the outpatient crossover and 4-wk extension phases, adherence was monitored by weekly telephonic follow-ups, pill count verification during each visit, and review of participant diaries. Overall compliance exceeded 95% across all treatment periods.

Study medication and placebo manufacturing

The HCTZ used in this trial was the commercially marketed Aquazide (Hydrochlorothiazide IP), 12.5 and 25 mg tablets (Sun Pharma Laboratories Ltd.), licensed for clinical use and compliant with Indian Pharmacopoeia standards.

Placebo tablets were prepared by the hospital pharmacy, using inert excipients and film-coated to match Aquazide in appearance, size, hardness, and packaging. Both active and placebo tablets were dispensed in identical coded containers prepared by an independent pharmacist not involved in patient care or analysis. Because Aquazide is a licensed commercial preparation, no additional pharmacokinetic testing was required; the placebo contained no active diuretic component.

Blinding

This was a single-blinded study in which participants were blinded to treatment allocation, while treating investigators were aware of the treatment sequence to permit protocol-defined dose escalation (12.5 → 25 mg on day 5) and to ensure close biochemical and safety monitoring during the inpatient phase. Laboratory personnel and statisticians were blinded to the treatment sequence, and all primary and secondary outcomes were objective biochemical measures, minimizing the risk of bias. Participants were informed only that they would receive 2 treatments in random order but were unaware which was active or placebo during each phase.

After completion of the crossover phase, all participants were offered an open-label extension phase during which they received HCTZ at a dose of 25 mg/d, based on tolerability, for an additional 4 wk following a 2-wk washout period. Final clinical and biochemical evaluations were performed at the end of the extension period to assess both short-term and sustained effects of HCTZ therapy. In addition to the standard assessments performed during day 5 and day 8 of each intervention phase, plasma bone turnover markers—β-CTX and P1NP—were also measured at the conclusion of the extension phase to evaluate the impact on bone remodeling.

The primary outcome of the study was the change in albumin adjusted serum calcium concentration following treatment with HCTZ. Secondary outcomes included changes in 24-h UCa, sodium, and phosphorus excretion; changes in fractional UCa excretion; alterations in bone turnover markers (plasma β-CTX and P1NP); and changes in plasma iPTH levels. All blood samples were collected under fasting conditions. Urine was collected over 24 h using acid-washed containers, and adequacy was confirmed through urinary creatinine excretion. The crossover phase was designed to evaluate short-term efficacy and safety; longer-term stability at 25 mg/d was assessed in the 4-wk open-label extension.

Biochemical measurements were performed in the Department of Endocrinology and the Central Clinical Laboratory at the institute using validated methods. Total serum calcium was measured using the Arsenazo III colorimetric method and adjusted for serum albumin. Serum phosphorus, sodium, potassium, and magnesium were analyzed using ion-selective electrode or colorimetric methods. Plasma iPTH and 25OHD levels were measured using electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) on the Roche Cobas e601 analyzer. Bone turnover markers (P1NP and β-CTX) were also measured using ECLIA. All assays were performed on fasting morning samples to minimize diurnal variation. Fractional calcium excretion was calculated using the standard formula.16

Study design

This study employed a crossover design wherein each participant acted as their own control, thereby improving statistical power and minimizing inter-individual variability. Sample size estimation was based on prior data,12 which reported a mean serum calcium change of 1.4 mg/dL with a SD of 1.0 mg/dL. Assuming a 2-sided alpha of .05 and a power of 80%, the minimum required sample size was three participants per group. To accommodate the crossover structure and account for potential dropouts, a total of 30 participants (15 per treatment sequence) were enrolled to ensure adequate power and robustness of results.

Type of study

This interventional human trial followed CONSORT 2010 guidelines, with the completed checklist available in the Supplementary material. It was prospectively registered with the Clinical Trials Registry-India (CTRI/2024/08/090041). No experimental drugs or procedures were used; all participants received standard hypoparathyroidism care, and HCTZ was administered within approved therapeutic limits.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0 (IBM Corp.). Statistical tests were selected based on variable type, distribution, and the crossover design.

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± SD for normally distributed data and median with IQR for skewed data. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, histograms, and Q-Q plots. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages.

Within-treatment period (during HCTZ or placebo treatment phase) changes over time were analyzed using repeated measures ANOVA for normally distributed data (eg, body weight and eGFR), and the Friedman test for non-normal data (eg, heart rate and electrolytes). For the open-label extension (baseline vs day 30), paired t-tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used for parametric and nonparametric data, respectively. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons included Tukey’s test (ANOVA) and Nemenyi test (Friedman).

Between-treatment (HCTZ vs placebo) comparisons during the crossover phase were assessed using paired t-tests for normal variables and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for non-normal variables.

Generalized estimating equations with an exchangeable correlation structure were used to evaluate overall treatment effects across time, accounting for within-subject correlations. Link functions were selected according to outcome distribution. Sensitivity analyses were also performed with 24-h UCa normalized for urinary sodium excretion (UNa, included as a covariate in mixed-effects models) and indexed to body weight (mg/kg/24 h). In addition, within-HCTZ contrasts were examined to compare the effect of 12.5 mg vs 25 mg dosing.

To assess potential carryover and period effects in the 2-treatment, 2-period crossover design, the Grizzle method and 2-way repeated measures ANOVA were applied.

All p-values were 2-sided, with significance set at p < .05. Where applicable, 95% CIs were reported. Analyses followed a per-protocol approach, and missing data were not imputed.

Results

A total of 68 individuals were screened during the study period. Of these, 38 were excluded due to ineligibility or refusal to participate. The remaining 30 patients were enrolled in the study. Four participants were lost to follow-up for nonstudy-related reasons (2 relocated and 2 withdrew consent), and the final analysis was performed on 26 individuals who completed the study as per protocol. This was a crossover study; hence, all 26 participants received both the HCTZ and placebo sequentially as per the study protocol. Thirteen participants received the HCTZ first followed by placebo, while the remaining 13 received placebo first followed by the HCTZ. Ultimately, all 26 participants were exposed to both HCTZ and placebo during different phases of the study. Therefore, baseline characteristics of the 26 participants were compared separately for the HCTZ and placebo phases. All 26/26 participants escalated to 25 mg/d at day 5; 0/26 remained at 12.5 mg/d.

Baseline characteristics

A total of 26 participants (mean age: 40.19 ± 13.94 yr; median: 40 yr; IQR: 28.5-52 yr; 53.8% female) were enrolled, with no significant baseline difference during HCTZ and placebo treatment periods in clinical, biochemical, or urinary parameters, confirming successful randomization and group comparability (Table 1).

Primary outcome

Baseline albumin-adjusted serum calcium levels were comparable during the HCTZ and placebo treatment periods (p = .099). By day 5, albumin-adjusted serum calcium increased significantly with HCTZ, showing a mean percent change from baseline of 4.7% ± 2.5% (p < .001 within-treatment period), whereas during placebo treatment, it showed a nonsignificant decline (−1.2% ± 1.3%; p = .214 within-treatment period). The between-treatment difference (HCTZ vs placebo) at day 5 was highly significant (p < .001).

At day 8, with HCTZ treatment, the participants maintained a significant improvement (5.0% ± 2.9%; p < .001 within-treatment period), during placebo treatment, they again showed no significant change (−1.6% ± 2.4%; p = .188 within-treatment period). The between-treatment difference (HCTZ vs placebo) at day 8 remained highly significant (p < .001) (Table 2; Figure 2). At baseline, 10 participants (38.5%) met the criteria for hypercalciuria (≥300 mg/d in men or ≥250 mg/d in women). Following HCTZ treatment, UCa excretion declined in all participants, and none remained hypercalciuric at day 8 assessment. The magnitude of reduction in UCa with HCTZ was positively correlated with baseline UCa levels (r = 0.46, p = .018), indicating that participants with higher baseline excretion derived greater absolute benefit from treatment.

Secondary outcomes

Change in 24-h urinary calcium excretion over time

As shown in Table 3 and Figure 3, 24-h UCa decreased significantly during HCTZ treatment, with a mean percent reduction of −19.4% ± 9.3% at Day 5 (p < .001 within-treatment period) and −26.3% ± 12.9% at day 8 (p < .001 within-treatment period). In contrast, during placebo treatment, the participants showed no significant changes (+4.1% at day 5, p = .198; +4.7% at day 8, p = .186). Between-treatment differences (HCTZ vs placebo) were highly significant at both timepoints (p < .001).

Change in fractional urinary calcium excretion

As shown in Table 4, Table S5, and Figure 2, fractional calcium excretion declined significantly during HCTZ treatment, with mean percent reductions of −21.6% at day 5 and −29.9% at day 8 (both p < .001 within-treatment period). In contrast, during placebo treatment, the participants showed no significant changes (+7.5%; p > .2). Between-treatment difference (HCTZ vs placebo) was highly significant at both timepoints (p < .001).

The decline in UCa was driven primarily by a reduction in fractional calcium excretion, with minimal contribution from changes in filtered load, as GFR remained stable and the rise in serum calcium was modest.

Change in 24-h urinary sodium excretion over time

Urinary sodium excretion was comparable between treatment periods at baseline (HCTZ: 150.4 ± 53.4 vs placebo: 154.8 ± 50.8 mmol/d; p = .76) and declined modestly by day 8 in both arms (HCTZ: 126.2 ± 59.2 vs placebo: 130.7 ± 53.5 mmol/d; p = .78), with no significant between-treatment differences (HCTZ vs placebo) at any timepoint.

Intact PTH (iPTH)

Intact PTH levels remained low and stable across both HCTZ and placebo treatment periods. Baseline values were comparable (HCTZ: 5.0 ± 2.5 vs placebo: 5.7 ± 3.7 pg/mL; p = .76). By day 8, levels showed no significant within-treatment period changes (HCTZ: 5.3 ± 3.6, p = .66; placebo: 5.1 ± 2.5, p = .09) and no significant between-treatment differences (HCTZ vs placebo) (p = .73).

Adverse effects

As shown in Tables S6 and S7, HCTZ had no significant effect on serum sodium or eGFR at the end of 7 days (all p > .05). However, serum potassium declined significantly during HCTZ treatment by day 8 (3.95 ± 0.25 vs 4.18 ± 0.24 mEq/L; p < .001), though no hypokalemia occurred in any study subject. No serious adverse events occurred. These findings suggest good overall tolerability with mild potassium reduction.

Extension phase (end of 1 mo)

In the extension phase, all participants received HCTZ 25 mg/d for 30 days, and day 30 outcomes were compared with respective baseline values from the intervention phase. At the end of 1 mo, serum calcium increased significantly (p = .003 vs HCTZ baseline; p < .001 vs placebo), while 24-h UCa, urinary sodium, and fractional calcium excretion decreased significantly (all p < .001). Serum potassium showed a modest, nonsignificant decline (p = .2121). Although a transient drop was noted at day 8 (p < .001), potassium levels remained within the physiological range throughout, indicating preserved homeostasis.

Discussion

Managing nonsurgical hypoparathyroidism remains challenging due to the lifelong absence or dysfunction of PTH, a key regulator of calcium homeostasis. The lack of PTH impairs renal calcium reabsorption, hinders the conversion of 25OHD to its active form, and limits skeletal calcium mobilization, leading to persistent hypocalcaemia and hyperphosphatemia. Conventional therapy involves long-term supplementation with oral calcium and active vitamin D analogues, which helps correct serum calcium but fails to address the underlying hormonal deficit. Consequently, many patients continue to experience UCa wasting, predisposing them to nephrolithiasis and nephrocalcinosis. Thiazide diuretics, which inhibit the sodium-chloride cotransporter in the distal tubule and enhance calcium reabsorption, have shown benefits in disorders, such as idiopathic hypercalciuria, postsurgical hypoparathyroidism, and osteoporosis. Their adjunctive role in nonsurgical hypoparathyroidism is increasingly recognized, especially in resource-limited settings when combined with sodium restriction and regular monitoring.17 However, most available evidence is based on studies involving postsurgical hypoparathyroidism,11–13 limiting its applicability to idiopathic cases.

To our knowledge, this is the first randomized controlled trial specifically conducted in individuals with nonsurgical hypoparathyroidism, and the first to employ a crossover design to rigorously evaluate the short-term efficacy and safety of HCTZ in this population. It also represents the largest cohort of nonsurgical hypoparathyroidism patients studied to date for this intervention.

Our 12.5 → 25 mg step-up prioritized safety in nonsurgical hypoparathyroidism and targeted early pharmacodynamic readouts (days 5 and 8), when thiazide-induced changes in renal calcium handling typically emerge. Participants received 12.5 mg once daily for the first 4 full days, and the first 25 mg dose was administered immediately after day 5 assessments. The greater magnitude of change observed by day 8 compared with day 5 likely reflects the pharmacodynamic impact of dose escalation from 12.5 to 25 mg/d, rather than duration alone. The 4-week 25 mg/d extension demonstrated sustained hypocalciuria and stable electrolytes, addressing dose relevance for clinical use.

Comparative evaluation: serum calcium, 24-h urinary calcium excretion, and fractional calcium excretion in nonsurgical hypoparathyroidism treated with hydrochlorothiazide

This randomized crossover trial is the first to exclusively evaluate the efficacy of HCTZ in individuals with nonsurgical hypoparathyroidism. Using a controlled, low-sodium dietary protocol, we demonstrated that low-dose HCTZ (12.5-25 mg/d) led to a consistent rise in serum calcium and a marked reduction in both 24-h UCa and fractional calcium excretion (FeCa%)—all without altering background calcitriol or calcium supplementation. These effects were achieved without any electrolyte imbalances, reinforcing the safety of this approach even in the absence of endogenous PTH. Importantly, the hypocalciuric effect of HCTZ was independent of sodium intake, as demonstrated by sensitivity analyses adjusting for urinary sodium and indexing UCa to body weight, both of which confirmed the robustness of our findings.

Prior studies by Porter et al.,12 Brickman et al.,11 and Parfitt et al.13 used higher thiazide doses in small, mixed populations (mostly postsurgical hypoparathyroid population) and showed variable or short-lived responses. Notably, none of the prior reports focused specifically on idiopathic (nonsurgical) hypoparathyroidism, and sodium intake—a critical modifier of thiazide response—was not standardized or objectively verified. In addition, the doses studied historically were often much higher than those used in current clinical practice, such as HCTZ 100-200 mg/d in Brickman et al.9 or chlorthalidone 50 mg/d in Porter et al.,8 raising concerns about tolerability. By contrast, our trial incorporated strict inclusion criteria, standardized dietary sodium restriction with UNa verification, and pragmatic low-dose HCTZ (12.5-25 mg/d). This approach yielded reliable and sustained biochemical improvements, without adverse events, and provides clinically relevant data directly applicable to long-term management even with low dose. In contextualizing these studies, it is important to note that Worcester and Coe10 examined physiology in the absence of PTH rather than clinical management; Brickman et al. used very high-dose HCTZ (100-200 mg/d) with inconsistent results; and Porter et al. employed chlorthalidone in largely postsurgical patients without calcitriol. None of these reports evaluated nonsurgical hypoparathyroidism under conditions of standardized sodium restriction with UNa verification, nor did they test modern low-dose HCTZ relevant to clinical practice. Our trial therefore provides incremental, disease-specific, and practice-relevant evidence beyond these classic studies.

Mechanistically, thiazide-induced inhibition of the sodium-chloride cotransporter (NCC) in the distal convoluted tubule enhances calcium reabsorption by promoting favorable electrochemical gradients. This action increases the activity of calcium transport proteins such as TRPV5, calbindin-D28K, and PMCA1b—whose expression is maintained through systemic calcitriol and sodium regulation, even in the absence of PTH.18–22 Our findings provide clinical validation for this mechanism, suggesting that thiazide diuretics can improve calcium balance independently of PTH.

The mean rise in albumin-adjusted serum calcium with low-dose HCTZ (~0.47 mg/dL) should be interpreted in the context of our population and design. In hypoparathyroidism,1 the absence of PTH alters tubular calcium handling, so modest improvements in distal reabsorption can yield proportionally larger serum changes. Concomitant standardized sodium restriction (verified by UNa) likely potentiated the hypocalciuric effect,23 and patients were stabilized at 8.0-9.0 mg/dL, where small absolute increments translate into a larger relative shift. Notably, albumin-adjusted calcium remained within the target range and no hypercalcemia occurred.

The significant drop in FeCa%, a sensitive index of tubular calcium reabsorption, further supports improved renal calcium conservation. The apparent paradox of a large fall in UCa despite a small increase in serum calcium is explained by a marked reduction in fractional calcium excretion rather than changes in filtered load. The mechanisms include enhanced distal tubular calcium transport (via TRPV5, calbindin-D28K, and NCX1/PMCA1b) and a modest proximal shift due to extracellular volume contraction.15–18 Standardized sodium intake and sensitivity analyses confirmed that this effect was independent of urinary sodium excretion. While previous studies rarely reported FeCa%, similar reductions have been noted with recombinant PTH therapies like TransCon PTH in the PaTHway trial.24 However, such treatments are expensive and less accessible in resource-limited regions. In contrast, HCTZ remains a low-cost, widely available alternative with a favorable safety profile.

The modest, nondifferential decline in UNa across both arms is consistent with the trial design, where dietary sodium restriction was standardized and enforced equally during HCTZ and placebo phases. This decline therefore reflects adherence to the uniform restriction protocol rather than a HCTZ effect. As expected, no between-treatment difference (HCTZ vs placebo) emerged because both periods were subjected to the same sodium intake targets, and compliance was verified with 24-h UNa measurements.

In summary, our findings establish low-dose HCTZ as a safe, physiologically sound, and economically viable adjunct to standard therapy in nonsurgical hypoparathyroidism. Its benefits—supported by mechanistic insight and clinical efficacy—provide a practical approach to improving calcium homeostasis and minimizing UCa loss, especially in resource-constrained environments.9,25,26 Given its favorable safety profile, cost-effectiveness, and impact on key biochemical outcomes, this strategy holds promise to be considered as a first-line adjunct alongside conventional therapy in future guidelines, with the potential to prevent long-term complications such as nephrocalcinosis and renal impairment at the earliest.

Adverse effect

In our study population with nonsurgical hypoparathyroidism, low-dose HCTZ was well tolerated, with no clinically significant electrolyte disturbances observed. A mild, transient reduction in serum potassium was noted by day 8, yet levels remained within the physiological range and normalized spontaneously by the end of the extension phase. None of the participants required medical intervention, reinforcing the safety of this regimen. These findings align with prior data from cardiovascular trials, where thiazide-induced hypokalemia was generally mild and seldom treatment-limiting.27,28

Renal function remained stable throughout, and the consistent decline in urinary sodium across both treatment arms reflected strong adherence to dietary sodium restriction. This dietary control, when combined with HCTZ, likely facilitated enhanced calcium reabsorption through TRPV5 and sodium-calcium exchanger pathways, independent of PTH activity.19,20 The placebo-controlled design in our study allowed us to clearly attribute the additional hypocalciuric effect to HCTZ, beyond what was achieved with sodium restriction alone.

Importantly, no adverse effects, such as dysglycemia or symptomatic hypotension, were reported. HCTZ, being affordable, orally administered, and widely accessible, offers a practical and scalable adjunctive therapy for managing nonsurgical hypoparathyroidism, particularly in resource-limited settings.19

Because thiazides can potentiate hypercalcemia in the presence of active vitamin D, we maintained fixed doses of calcitriol and calcium, targeted serum calcium to the 8.0-9.0 mg/dL range, and monitored frequently. No episodes of hypercalcemia occurred during the crossover or the 4-wk open-label extension, but this caution remains important for clinical practice.

Mechanistic synergy between thiazide diuretics and sodium restriction in enhancing calcium conservation in nonsurgical hypoparathyroidism

In this study involving patients with nonsurgical hypoparathyroidism and ongoing high UCa loss, we found that low-dose HCTZ effectively reduced calcium excretion in the urine and led to a modest increase in blood calcium levels. Thiazide diuretics work by blocking the NCC in the distal part of the kidney tubule. This action causes a slight reduction in fluid volume outside the cells, which increases sodium and calcium reabsorption in the proximal tubule. Since calcium is reabsorbed along with sodium in this part of the kidney, this helps reduce the amount of calcium lost in urine.29

At the distal tubule level, blocking NCC lowers the sodium concentration inside kidney cells. This improves the activity of the sodium-calcium exchanger (NCX1), allowing more calcium to move from the kidney cells into the blood. The drop in intracellular calcium also stimulates the expression of TRPV5 channels and calbindin-D28K, which help transport calcium through the cells. Together, these effects enhance calcium reabsorption and support better calcium balance in the body.30,31

All participants in our study followed a controlled low-sodium diet, which further improved the effect of HCTZ. A reduced sodium intake decreases the amount of sodium delivered to the distal tubule, increasing both sodium and calcium reabsorption in the proximal tubule. Additionally, low dietary sodium has been shown to increase TRPV5 expression, further supporting calcium retention.32

This combination of low-dose HCTZ and dietary sodium restriction led to a consistent reduction in 24-h UCa excretion and modest improvements in serum calcium, even in the absence of PTH. These findings suggest that targeting both dietary sodium restriction and renal calcium handling can improve management of nonsurgical hypoparathyroidism.

Strengths and limitations

This randomized, single-blinded, crossover study was designed to reduce individual variability by allowing each participant to serve as their own control, thereby improving internal validity. To our knowledge, this is the first randomized controlled crossover trial conducted exclusively in patients with nonsurgical hypoparathyroidism, enhancing disease-specific relevance. Standardized intake of calcium, calcitriol, and dietary sodium across both treatment phases helped minimize potential confounding factors. Importantly, sodium restriction was held constant across both arms, allowing the incremental effect of HCTZ to be isolated. Sensitivity analyses adjusting for urinary sodium and indexing UCa to body weight confirmed the robustness of this effect. Frequent biochemical assessments and a 30-day extension phase enabled evaluation of short-term effectiveness and safety.

However, the limited duration of the trial did not permit assessment of long-term outcomes such as kidney stone formation or changes in bone mineral density. Genetic testing was not done in all the patients. Accordingly, although surgical, autoimmune, and syndromic causes were excluded, rare genetic causes cannot be entirely ruled out. We have therefore described the cohort more conservatively as nonsurgical hypoparathyroidism with exclusion of syndromic features. Measures of quality of life were not captured. The single-blinded design, chosen to ensure safety monitoring and dose escalation, represents a limitation; however, this was mitigated by objective biochemical outcomes, allocation concealment, laboratory/statistical blinding, and nonsignificant carry-over effects. Finally, although this study included the highest number of nonsurgical hypoparathyroidism cases reported to date, its single-center design and modest sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings to wider populations.

Clinical relevance and future implications

This trial provides direct evidence supporting the use of low-dose HCTZ to enhance calcium conservation in nonsurgical hypoparathyroidism. The intervention was well tolerated and led to measurable biochemical improvements under controlled conditions. These results justify integrating thiazides into individualized treatment regimens, particularly when UCa remains elevated despite optimized conventional therapy.

Further studies are needed to assess the durability of response, long-term renal outcomes, and potential for dose reduction of active vitamin D and calcium supplementation.

Conclusion

This randomized crossover trial demonstrates that low-dose HCTZ (12.5-25 mg/d), combined with sodium restriction, safely improves calcium balance in patients with nonsurgical hypoparathyroidism by increasing serum calcium and reducing UCa loss. The treatment was well tolerated, with no serious adverse effects or electrolyte disturbances, supporting its safety profile. This approach offers a cost-effective adjunct to conventional therapy. In clinical practice, low-dose HCTZ may allow subsequent, monitored reductions in calcium and calcitriol requirements, potentially improving cost-effectiveness while lowering long-term hypercalciuria risk; this hypothesis warrants prospective evaluation. Further studies are needed to evaluate long-term outcomes and explore its integration with PTH-based treatments.
