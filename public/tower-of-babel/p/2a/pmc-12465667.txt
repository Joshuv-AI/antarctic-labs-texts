Loneliness, social isolation and stroke risk in men and women with and without diabetes
Authors: Xiu Wu, Yuanhao Zu, Xuan Wang, Yilin Yoshida
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWe evaluated the association between loneliness, social isolation, and stroke risk in individuals with and without diabetes and assessed sex differences in these associations.Research design and methodsFrom the UK Biobank Study, we included 40,684 participants with diabetes (mean age 51.25, 12.5% women, 88.65% white) (self-reported diabetes diagnosis, diabetes medication, HbA1c > 6.5%, or ICD codes) and those without (1:1 matched on sex, race, age, and center). We used Cox regression to examine the association between loneliness, social isolation, and stroke risk and estimated the absolute risk reduction (ARR) of strokes in diabetes and euglycemic groups separately. We also performed Cox regression to evaluate the excess stroke risk associated with diabetes (vs. euglycemic controls) across levels of loneliness and social isolation.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 12 years, within the diabetes cohort, lower levels of loneliness and social isolation were associated with reduced stroke risk (Hazard ratio [HR] for loneliness 0.73, 95% CI 0.62–0.86; social isolation 0.83, 0.72–0.97), and such associations were significantly stronger in men than women (HR for loneliness 0.63, 0.52–0.77 in men, 0.96, 0.72–1.29 in women, p-interaction = 0.02; HR for social isolation 0.79, 0.65–0.95 in men, 0.93, 0.72–1.22 in women, p-interaction = 0.05). ARR related to improved loneliness and social isolation was 1.3% and 0.4% in the diabetes group (equivalent to 13 and 4 stroke cases per 1000 people with diabetes, respectively, during the 12-year period), respectively. As the index of loneliness and social isolation went from highest to lowest, the adjusted stroke risk decreased by 65% and 8%, respectively, in people with diabetes vs. those without.ConclusionsImproved loneliness and social isolation may mitigate excess stroke risk in people with diabetes, particularly in men with diabetes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24458-6.Keywords: Loneliness, Social isolation, Stroke, Diabetes, Sex differences

Introduction

Diabetes is a well-established risk factor for stroke [1]. Individuals with diabetes face a two-fold increased risk of stroke compared to those without diabetes [2, 3]. Loneliness and social isolation, key factors reflecting social connectedness and mental well-being, are particularly impactful in individuals with diabetes, influencing their disease management and outcomes [4]. The U.S. Surgeon General described loneliness and social isolation as an epidemic, noting that these psychosocial risk conditions are associated with more than 30% increased risk of stroke and stroke-related death [5, 6]. Given the significant adverse impact of loneliness and social isolation in individuals with diabetes [7], the relationship between loneliness and social isolation and stroke risk in this patient group may be particularly pronounced yet explored in depth [6, 8–10].

Previous studies have shown sex differences in cardiovascular risk associated with loneliness and social isolation [11]. It has been reported that loneliness and social isolation are more strongly associated with the risk of stroke and cardiovascular mortality in men compared to women [12, 13]. This difference can be attributed to sex-based variations in social support-seeking behaviors and coping mechanisms. For example, men are less likely to seek emotional support and maintain strong social connections, which may contribute to increased stress, inflammation, and other cardiovascular risk factors, while women often engage in more active social interactions and utilize support networks that can buffer against these risks [14–16]. Previous research also has shown sex differences in stroke risk in people with or without diabetes. In the general population, men have a higher burden of stroke compared to women [17], whereas in populations with diabetes, women appear to have a higher risk of stroke [18]. A better understanding of sex differences in susceptibility and responses to loneliness and social isolation in people with diabetes will enable more tailored, sex-based psychosocial assessments and care to reduce stroke risk in this population.

In this study, we sought to examine the association of improvement of social isolation and loneliness with the risk of stroke in men and women with diabetes. We also compared people with diabetes with matched control participants without diabetes to investigate whether the diabetes-related excess risk of stroke could be attenuated or eliminated through improving loneliness and social isolation indexes.

Methods

Study population & sample selection

The UK Biobank is a large-scale, population-based cohort study, recruiting over 500,000 participants aged 37 to 73 from 22 assessment centers across England, Wales, and Scotland between 2006 and 2010. Participants provided written informed consent, and the study was approved by the North West Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee. All participants provided written informed consent at recruitment. The UK Biobank resource is available to bona fide researchers upon application (https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/enable-your-research). Due to data access restrictions, individual-level data are not publicly available. However, analytic code and derived variables used in this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines. From all participants, we excluded 19,516 participants with baseline CVD, 1,522 baseline CKD, 2,598 with missing data on loneliness and social isolation, 1,007 with gestational diabetes, and 47,408 with prediabetes. We identified a total of 40,684 participants with diabetes (i.e., diabetes diagnosis, use of diabetes medication, HbA1c > 6.5%, ICD codes for diabetes), and an age-, sex-, race-, and center-matched euglycemic group (1:1) (see Figure S1).

Definition of loneliness and social isolation

In the UK Biobank, the loneliness scale was assessed with two questions: (I) “Do you often feel lonely?” and (ii) “How often can you confide in someone close to you?” The most loneliness was regarded as feeling lonely and having someone to confide in less than once a month. The social isolation scale was estimated with three questions: (a) “How many people, including yourself, live in your household?”; (ii) “How often do you visit or receive visits from friends or family?”; (iii) “Which of the following social or leisure activities do you participate in at least once a week?” Most social isolation was described as living alone, receiving or making social visits less than once a month, and not engaging in social activities at least once a week. Participants with elevated risk factors were assigned a code of 1, while those with lower risk factors were assigned a code of 0. The loneliness or social isolation scale was computed by summing the individual scores from two or three relevant factors, respectively, yielding a possible range of 0 to 2 or 0 to 3. Higher scores corresponded to greater levels of loneliness or social isolation [4, 13, 19–21]. A complete scoring algorithm can be found in Table S1.

Outcome

The primary outcome of this study are ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes based on the International Classification of Disease (ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes). Ischemic stroke was from ICD-10 codes (I63-I64) and ICD-9 (433–434). Hemorrhagic stroke was from ICD-10 codes (I60-I62) and ICD-9 (430–432). Follow-up duration was measured from the baseline assessment date to the earliest of the following events: diagnosis of an outcome, death, or the censoring date (May 23, 2021). (The ICD codes are shown in Table S1).

Covariates

Common clinical risk factors included Body Mass Index (BMI) > 30 [22–24], blood pressure (BP; systolic and diastolic BP levels), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c), and HbA1c. LDL-c was assessed using enzymatic assays on the Beckman Coulter Clinical Chemistry Analyzer AU5800 [25, 26]. HbA1c was measured via high-performance liquid chromatography [27]. These methodologies are detailed in the UK Biobank’s biomarker assay quality procedures document [28].

Sociodemographic variables were assessed through a touch-screen questionnaire, including age, sex, race, and the Townsend Deprivation Index. The touch-screen data also captured physical activity (measured in minutes of moderate or vigorous activity per week or an equivalent combination) [29, 30], alcohol consumption, healthy diet score [31–33], and smoking status [34]. Additionally, information on medications, including antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, and anti-diabetes medications (classified as insulin use, oral medications, or none), was collected using medication codes. We also encompass psychological comorbidities, such as depression and anxiety, which were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-4 (PHQ-4) [35]. Depression was assessed through two questions regarding the frequency of depressed mood and lack of enthusiasm or interest over the past two weeks. Anxiety was evaluated with questions on the frequency of tenseness/restlessness and tiredness/lethargy in the past two weeks. Participants reporting symptoms “more than half the days” or “nearly every day” were classified as having depression or anxiety [36]. Information on the time of diabetes diagnosis and diabetes type was obtained from inpatient health records. Definitions of all covariates can be found in the supplemental materials (Table S1).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables with a normal distribution were presented as means with standard deviations (SD), while those without a normal distribution were reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Categorical variables were summarized as counts and corresponding percentages. We used Cox proportional hazard models to examine the associations between loneliness, social isolation, and stroke risk in diabetes and euglycemic cohorts separately. The group with the highest index was set as the reference group. The Schoenfeld residuals test was conducted to assess the proportional hazards assumption. Three adjusted models were implemented: Model 1 adjusted for age, sex, and race. Model 2 expanded the adjustments to include the Townsend Deprivation Index, smoking status, physical activity, BMI, LDL-c, BP, HbA1c, use of hypertension and high Cholesterol medication, use of diabetes medication, age at diabetes diagnosis, and diabetes type. Model 3 further included anxiety and depression in addition to the covariates in Model 2. Unadjusted Cox model (Model 1) were first applied to estimate the crude associations between loneliness, social isolation, and stroke risk before adjusting for covariates (Model 2–3). Continuous covariates were retained in their original scale to preserve information and maximize model precision, unless otherwise noted. Missing data were handled using a complete-case approach; participants with missing data in key covariates were excluded from the analyses. The overall proportion of missingness was low (< 10%).We included a sex-exposure interaction term in each model to test the sex-modifying effect on the association of loneliness, social isolation, and stroke risk. We then stratified the sex group and estimated the sex-specific risk of stroke associated with loneliness and social isolation [37]. Additionally, we used the same Cox models when comparing people with diabetes with matched control participants across levels of loneliness and social isolation. We constructed ARR to quantify the reduction in stroke risk associated with improved loneliness or social isolation, ARR provides a metric for assessing the potential intervention effectiveness [38, 39]. We conducted sensitivity analysis for ischemic stroke and hemorrhagic stroke, separately. Additionally, we also further accounted for living with any family member in the analysis for the association between loneliness and stroke risk to evaluate the influence of living conditions. Statistical analyses were carried out with SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc. Cary, NC, USA) and all P-values were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (Table 1).

Results

Characteristics of participants

In individuals diagnosed with diabetes, the mean age was 58.9 years. Men and women with diabetes reported similar percentages of loneliness (8.7% and 9.2%, respectively, for the highest loneliness score [score = 2]) and social isolation (12.9% and 12.8%, respectively, for the highest isolation score [score ≥ 2]). Women with diabetes had higher percentages of depression and anxiety (15.2% and 20.1%, respectively, compared to 11.4% and 14.5% in men). Men with diabetes (vs. women) had higher percentages of current smokers (14% vs. 11%), frequent drinkers (40.8% vs. 19.7%), and poor or moderate diet quality (76.6% vs. 65.2%). Men also had higher percentages of using antihypertensive (71.9% vs. 69%), lipid-lowering (64.5% vs. 61.1%), and oral anti-diabetes medications (29.2% vs. 25%), as well as a lower BMI (30.8 vs. 32), compared to women (Table 1).

The prevalence of loneliness, social isolation, psychosocial comorbidities, and the use of medications was lower in the euglycemic group compared to the diabetes group. In the euglycemic group, the mean age was 55.7 years. Euglycemic men had higher percentages of loneliness and social isolation than women (5.8% for the highest loneliness score [score = 2]) and social isolation (9.0% for the highest isolation score [score ≥ 2]). Euglycemic women had higher percentages of depression (7.3% vs. 6.3%) and anxiety (11% vs. 7.8%). Euglycemic men had higher percentages of current smokers (11.5% vs. 8.2%), frequent alcohol users (20% vs. 34.33%), poor or moderate diet quality (74.1% vs. 61.2%), and a higher BMI (27.2 vs. 26.5). Euglycemic men also had higher percentages of use of hypertension medications (27.2% vs. 23.6%) and use of lipid-lowering medications (14.5% vs. 9.5%) (Table 1).

Loneliness and social isolation and stroke risk among individuals with diabetes

Over a median follow-up period of 12 years, we identified 2,616 incident strokes, with 1755 cases in the diabetes cohort. Results from model 1 demonstrated that lower loneliness (score 0 vs. 2) was significantly associated with a reduced risk of stroke (Hazard Ratio [HR] and 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.64, 0.55–0.75) in individuals with diabetes. The association remained significant in model 2 (HR 0.70, 95% CI 0.60–0.82), further adjusting for clinical and behavioral risk factors (Table S2), and in model 3 with additional adjusting anxiety and depression (HR 0.73, 95% CI 0.62–0.86) (Fig. 1A. and Table S2). Men consistently exhibited a higher significant reduction of stroke risk associated with a lower loneliness index than women (men HR: 0.63, 95% CI:0.52–0.77; women HR: 0.96, 95% CI: 0.72–1.29, p-interaction = 0.02 in model 3) (Fig. 1A). ARR associated with low loneliness (score 0 vs. 2) was 1.3% in all people with diabetes, 0.9% for men with diabetes, and 0.7% for women with diabetes (equivalent to 13 stroke cases per 1000 people with diabetes, 9 per 1000 men with diabetes, and 7 per 1000 women with diabetes, respectively, during the 12-year period). The association between loneliness and stroke risk was not significant among euglycemic individuals (Figure S2A and Table S2).

Social isolation was significantly associated with reduced stroke risk in individuals with diabetes (model 1, HR and 95% CI 0.75, 0.65–0.86). The association remained significant in model 2 (HR 0.82, 95% CI 0.7–0.95) (Table S3) and in model 3 (HR 0.83, 95% CI 0.72–0.97) (Fig. 1B and Table S3). Men also showed a higher significant reduction of stroke risk associated with a lower social isolation index than women (men HR: 0.79, 95% CI:0.65–0.95; women HR: 0.93 95% CI: 0.72–1.22, p-interaction = 0.04 in model 3) (Fig. 1B and Table S3). ARR associated with low social isolation (score 0 vs. 2) was 0.4% in all people with diabetes, 0.6% for men with diabetes, and 0.1% for women with diabetes (equivalent to 4 stroke cases per 1000 people with diabetes, 6 per 1000 men with diabetes, and 1 per 1000 women with diabetes, respectively, during the 12-year period). The association between social isolation and stroke risk was not significant among euglycemic individuals (Figure S2B and Table S3).

We also evaluated the excess stroke risk in the diabetes group (vs. euglycemic group) across levels of loneliness and social isolation (Table 2). Compared to euglycemic individuals, the stroke risk among individuals with diabetes attenuated with reduced levels of loneliness and social isolation (Table 2). The results were consistent across both the unadjusted and fully adjusted models. In model 3, the HR for stroke risk decreased by 65% and 8% in individuals with diabetes versus those without diabetes when the loneliness and social isolation indices shifted from highest to lowest. The attenuation in excess stroke risk in diabetes (vs. euglycemic group) with the decreased loneliness levels was particularly evident in men. The fully adjusted HR and 95% CI from highest to lowest loneliness were 2.57 (1.55, 4.26), 1.09 (0.84, 1.40), and 1.00 (0.84, 1.2), respectively in men and 1.06 (0.54, 2.1), 1.38 (0.99, 1.94), and 1.53 (1.2, 1.96), respectively in women, p-sex interaction = 0.04, 0.26, and 0.01, respectively (Table 2). This sex difference was not observed for excess stroke risk in the diabetes group across social isolation levels (Table 2).

In stroke-type stratified analysis (ischemic stroke, n = 1445 in the diabetes group and n = 671 in the euglycemic group; hemorrhagic stroke, n = 310 in the diabetes group and n = 210 in the euglycemic group), the findings are, in general, consistent with the overall analysis. Specifically, we found the association between loneliness (score 0 vs. 2) and ischemic stroke in men with diabetes was more pronounced than that in women with diabetes (men HR: 0.64, 95%CI 0.52–0.8 and women HR: 0.91, 95% CI 0.66–1.24, respectively, p-sex interaction = 0.12)(Figure S3A and Table S4). Results for social isolation (score ≥ 2 vs. 0) and ischemic stroke also mirrored the same trend (men HR: 0.84, 95%CI 0.64–1.03 and women HR: 0.94, 95% CI 0.7–1.25, respectively, p-sex interaction = 0.12), but the statistical significance was not evident (Figure S3C and Table S5). In the diabetes group, hemorrhagic stroke risk appeared to be lower with improved levels of loneliness and social isolation in male patients. However, the results were not statistically significant (Figure S4 A and C and Table S5). In the euglycemic group, the association between these two exposures and stroke outcome was not statistically significant (Figure S3 B and D and Table S5; Figure S4B and D and Table S5).

When further adjusted for living with family (or not) in the analysis for stroke risk associated with loneliness, we continued to observe that men with diabetes had more risk reduction compared to their female counterparts (men HR: 0.64, 95% CI 0.52–0.78, and women HR: 0.97, 95% CI 0.73–1.31, respectively; p-sex interaction = 0.03 in model 3) (Figure S5A and Table S6). This association again was not in the euglycemic group (Figure S5B and Table S6). We also conducted additional sensitivity analyses stratified by anxiety and depression status to assess potential effect modification. As shown in Tables S7 and S8, there were no statistically significant interactions between anxiety or depression and diabetes status in the associations of loneliness or social isolation with stroke risk (all P for interaction > 0.05).

Discussion

This study examined the association between loneliness, social isolation, and stroke risk among individuals with or without diabetes, with new insights into sex heterogeneity in these relationships. Our findings reveal that lower levels of loneliness and social isolation are associated with a reduced risk of stroke in individuals with diabetes, particularly among male patients with diabetes. In contrast, these associations were not observed in the euglycemic group. These findings underscore the potential importance of addressing psychosocial health as part of comprehensive cardiovascular risk management in individuals with diabetes.

Our results are consistent with previous studies, which have shown that loneliness and social isolation are associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular outcomes in people with diabetes or obesity, including cardiovascular mortality [4, 8]. For example, a large meta-analysis by Valtorta et al. (2016) reported that poor social relationships were linked to a 29% higher risk of coronary heart disease and a 32% increase in stroke risk, emphasizing the broader cardiovascular impact of social disconnection [11]. Similarly, Bu et al. (2020) found that loneliness significantly predicted cardiometabolic morbidity and mortality in individuals with obesity and type 2 diabetes, even after adjusting for traditional risk factors [40]. Our study builds upon these findings by focusing specifically on stroke and by differentiating the effects of loneliness and social isolation in diabetes versus euglycemic populations.

Men with chronic diseases, including diabetes, may experience heightened vulnerability to the negative effects of loneliness and social isolation [41, 42]. Our findings showing a stronger stroke risk associated with these two conditions in men with diabetes were consistent with prior research [41, 42]. There are several potential explanations. First, men are less likely to seek emotional support and discuss their feelings openly, leading to internalization of stress. This behavior can result in greater physiological impacts, such as elevated inflammation, dysregulated stress hormone levels, and heightened cardiovascular reactivity, all of which are linked to increased stroke risk [43–46]. Second, men are more likely to depend on a spouse or primary relationship for emotional support, whereas women tend to maintain broader social networks that offer resilience against isolation and loneliness. This reliance makes men particularly vulnerable if their primary support network is disrupted [47]. Third, men are more prone to engaging in unhealthy behaviors under stress, such as poor dietary habits, physical inactivity, and reduced adherence to diabetes management regimens [11, 45]. These maladaptive behaviors exacerbate the metabolic and vascular worsening of diabetes, thereby increasing stroke risk [11, 45]. These findings reinforce the need for clinicians to recognize and assess psychosocial vulnerability—particularly in men with diabetes. Interventions such as social prescribing, peer-support programs, and psychological counseling may be effective strategies to mitigate loneliness and reduce stroke risk in participants with diabetes.

Our study also showed a relatively stronger effect of loneliness (vs. social isolation) on stroke risk in people with diabetes. Previous research has indicated the significant role of loneliness in cardiometabolic health [4]. Bu et al. (2020) indicated that loneliness, characterized by one’s subjective perception of social disconnection, may lead to prolonged stress responses, elevated blood pressure, and impaired vascular function, all of which are major risk factors for stroke [40]. Valtorta et al. (2018) suggested that loneliness is a predictor of adverse cardiovascular outcomes, even after adjusting for measures of social isolation. They speculated that the emotional distress associated with loneliness can trigger dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and chronic inflammatory responses. Both pathways are known contributors to atherosclerosis and thrombosis, increasing stroke risk [11]. Despite these plausible explanations, the underlying pathological mechanisms driving the significant adverse cardiovascular effect of loneliness, especially in a sex-specific context, require further research. The differential impact observed between loneliness and social isolation supports the notion that subjective emotional experiences may have more deleterious biological effects than objective social connectivity. Therefore, public health and clinical interventions should not only aim to increase frequencies of social interaction but also integrating strategies to increase the quality of the relationship.

This study has several strengths. First, it benefits from a large sample size, a long follow-up period, and the use of validated scales to measure loneliness and social isolation. Second, the matching of diabetes participants with euglycemic controls and describing men and women separately provides a comparative view of the effects of loneliness and social isolation on stroke risk across subgroups. However, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, the observational nature of this study limits the ability to draw causal inferences. Although we adjusted for common cardiovascular risk factors and psychosocial comorbidities, residual confounding cannot be ruled out. Second, the generalizability of the findings may be restricted to populations with demographic and clinical characteristics similar to those of the UK Biobank cohort. Additionally, reverse causality may be possible, such that those with strokes may be more vulnerable to loneliness and social isolation. However, we excluded individuals with a history of stroke at baseline and conducted a prospective analysis. This, to some degree, minimize the possibility of reverse causality. Future research should investigate sex-specific mechanisms driving these associations between loneliness, social isolation, and stroke risk in diabetes populations, including the roles of inflammation, stress, and behavioral factors [13]. Future studies should also explore whether reducing loneliness or social isolation through behavioral or social interventions can lead to measurable reductions in stroke incidence among high-risk populations, particularly men with diabetes. Moreover, the use of repeated psychosocial assessments over time could better capture dynamic changes and their relationship with health outcomes.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated the significant role of loneliness and social isolation in stroke risk among individuals with diabetes, with a more pronounced impact in men. Our findings suggest the potential public health benefit of addressing these two psychosocial risk conditions in the growing population with diabetes. Incorporating sex-tailored psychosocial support into diabetes care may mitigate excess stroke risk in populations with diabetes.
