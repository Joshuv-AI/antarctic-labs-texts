Comparing immune-checkpoint inhibitor-mediated inflammatory arthritis to polymyalgia rheumatica: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Alice Tison, Deanna Jannat-Khah, Laura C Cappelli, Maria E Suarez-Almazor, Noha Abdel-Wahab, Pankti Reid, Jeffrey A Sparks, Tawnie J Braaten, Alexa Meara, Cassandra Calabrese, Tamiko Katsumoto, Nilasha Ghosh, Kyle Ge, Alain Saraux, Valérie Devauchelle-Pensec, Ami A Shah, Clifton O Bingham, Anne R Bass
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)-related inflammatory arthritis occurs in approximately 6% of ICI-treated patients, and is often long-lasting. The clinical presentation is heterogeneous, and often loosely resembles either rheumatoid arthritis (ICI-IA) with peripheral presentation, or polymyalgia rheumatica (ICI-PMR) with predominant bilateral shoulder and hip pain and stiffness. Our objective was to compare clinical features, immunosuppressive treatment, and cancer outcomes between ICI-IA and ICI-PMR.MethodsData were extracted from the Rheumatology Adverse Events Due to Immunotherapy Observational Study multicenter prospective registry, regarding patients with rheumatologist-diagnosed ICI-IA or ICI-PMR and no preexisting primary IA/PMR. Clinical features were collected at the first visit in the registry. Multivariable logistic regression was used to compare second-line immunosuppressive drug utilization between groups. Kaplan-Meier curves were constructed to compare time of prednisone tapering to a dose of (1) 10 mg and (2) 5 mg from the baseline registry visit. Cox proportional hazard models were used to compare progression-free survival (PFS). Date of first cancer progression from arthritis onset was extracted based on documentation of imaging and/or pathology in the medical record. Patients with ICI-PMR were also compared with an independent cohort of patients with primary PMR.ResultsWe analyzed 490 patients: 418 with ICI-IA and 72 with ICI-PMR. Compared with patients with ICI-IA, patients with ICI-PMR were older (70 vs 63 years old, p<0.001), more likely to be male (p=0.034), had shorter onset after ICI initiation (87 vs 125 days, p=0.011) and had less peripheral synovitis (15% vs 72%, p<0.001). Time to a prednisone dose of 10 and 5 mg/day was comparable between groups. Patients with ICI-IA were more likely to receive a second-line immunosuppressive drug (OR 3.51 (95% CI 1.50 to 8.21, p=0.004)). PFS was comparable between groups. Compared with primary PMR (n=189), the sex ratio was inversed in ICI-PMR, but there was a similar proportion with peripheral arthritis (15%).ConclusionsPatients with ICI-IA were more likely to require a second-line immunosuppressive drug than patients with ICI-PMR, but PFS was the same. Future therapeutic clinical trials in ICI-IA/PMR should stratify patients according to phenotype.

Background

The broad use of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) to treat a wide range of cancers has led to the emergence of new rheumatic inflammatory entities, termed rheumatic immune-related adverse events (irAEs). Initially under-reported in clinical trials or non-specifically referred to as myalgia or arthralgia, numerous case reports and series have progressively enabled a better characterization of rheumatic irAEs. ICI-mediated joint inflammation occurs in 4%–7% of patients and is heterogeneous, with two dominant phenotypes: a peripheral arthritis presentation resembling rheumatoid arthritis (RA), and a polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR)-like phenotype with pain and stiffness in the shoulder and pelvic girdles, the latter of which the incidence has been reported as 2%.1 2 Spondyloarthritis-like presentations have also been reported.3 4

Unlike some other irAEs, ICI-mediated joint inflammation tends to be long-lasting, can be disabling, and alter patients’ quality of life.5 6 Although resembling primary inflammatory arthritis, differences have been highlighted between ICI-induced peripheral inflammatory arthritis (ICI-IA) and RA, such as seronegativity for rheumatoid factor and anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP), and in ICI-PMR, a higher proportion of cases with normal C-reactive protein (CRP) and with peripheral synovitis compared with primary PMR.3 7 8 This suggests that ICI-IA/PMR represent new conditions, and this is supported by translational studies.9 However, whether ICI-IA and ICI-PMR belong to the same continuum of toxicity or should be distinguished is unknown. Overlapping forms have indeed been reported.4 ICI-inflammatory arthritis classification criteria are currently being developed by the American College of Rheumatology (ACR)/European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) to enable homogeneous high-quality research, and ICI-IA and ICI-PMR are being considered as one entity.10 However, whether these phenotypes should be used to stratify patients in future clinical trials or whether specific clinical trials should be dedicated according to phenotype is open to debate.11

The efficacy of interleukin (IL)-6 receptor inhibitors (IL-6Ri) has been retrospectively reported in ICI-IA and in few ICI-PMR.12 Two prospective trials are under way to test the efficacy and safety of IL-6Ri and tumor necrosis factor inhibitors (TNFis) in patients with ICI-IA exclusively (EUCT: 2022–501130-33-00, NCT06037811). While IL-6Ri are approved for both primary RA and PMR, TNFi are not efficacious in primary PMR which raises the possibility that therapeutic responses may also differ between ICI-IA and ICI-PMR. Thus, characterizing the disease trajectories, immunosuppressant requirements and outcomes, for ICI-IA and ICI-PMR individually is crucial to the design of future clinical trials.

Thus, the aim of this study was to compare ICI-IA to ICI-PMR regarding demographic and clinical features, glucocorticoid (GC) and second-line immunosuppressive drug requirements, and cancer outcomes. Characteristics of patients with ICI-PMR were also compared with a cohort of patients with primary PMR.

Methods

RADIOS registry

The Rheumatology Adverse Events Due to Immunotherapy Observational Study (RADIOS) is a prospective multicenter rheumatic irAE registry in the USA. The registry is comprised of patients diagnosed with cancer and treated with at least one ICI, who developed rheumatologic irAE and were seen by a rheumatologist. Participating centers include Johns Hopkins University (JHU) with patients included since 2015, and eight other sites enrolling since 2023 including the Hospital for Special Surgery (HSS), MD Anderson, University of Chicago, Brigham and Women’s Hospital, University of Utah, The Ohio State University, Cleveland Clinic, and Stanford University. Study data are collected and managed using the software Research Electronic Data Capture hosted at the JHU coordinating site.13 14

Inclusion criteria

Patients enrolled in the RADIOS Registry, aged ≥18 years old with ICI-IA or ICI-PMR were included. In the absence of classification criteria for rheumatic irAEs, ICI-IA and ICI-PMR diagnosis was based on the investigating rheumatologist’s diagnosis. We did not use the primary PMR ACR/EULAR 2012 classification to classify ICI-PMR due to missing data.15 A total of 126 patients with ICI-IA from JHU who have been previously described in two publications16 17 were rolled into the RADIOS registry and are included in the cohort.

Exclusion criteria

Patients with ICI-arthralgia and patients with a history of preexisting IA or PMR (prior to ICI) were excluded. Patients were also excluded if their symptoms started more than 1 year after the last ICI infusion as their condition was less likely to be ICI-mediated.

Patient characteristics

“Baseline” clinical features, including IA/PMR characteristics, ICI type, cancer type and stage, other irAEs and laboratory findings were extracted from the first RADIOS registry visit (not at the onset of arthritis). Joint involvement was assessed using a 28 and 66 tender/swollen joint count worksheet. Arthritis severity was graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE): grade 1: mild pain, stiffness and/or weakness, grade 2: moderate pain limiting instrumental activities of daily living (ADL), grade 3: severe pain limiting self-care ADL. Information on periarticular manifestations was also included. Enthesitis was assessed using the Spondyloarthritis Research Consortium of Canada (SPARCC) Enthesitis Index. Dactylitis and tenosynovitis were also reported on the appreciation of the clinician. Data on steroid and second-line immunosuppressive drugs were identified at baseline and during follow-up. GCs were defined as methylprednisolone and prednisone. Hydrocortisone, used for adrenal replacement and dexamethasone, often used for cancer-related manifestations, were not considered in the analysis. Cumulative GCs utilization for ICI-IA/PMR in a 6-month period from the time of onset was available for a subset of patients.

Patients with primary PMR

To more deeply characterize patients with ICI-PMR, they were compared with patients with primary PMR enrolled in three French clinical trials.1820 Primary PMR was defined by a classical PMR occurring out of an ICI treatment setting. 37 and 39 patients eligible for the ALORS (Abatacept in Early-Onset Polymyalgia Rheumatica Study) and BACHELOR (Baricitinib in Early Polymyalgia Rheumatica) trials respectively were included.18 19 Inclusion criteria for those trials were a recent onset primary PMR (≤6 months), fulfilling the ACR/EULAR 2012 classification criteria, without GCs at baseline (for at least 4 or 2 weeks respectively), with a CRP PMR activity score >17 (CRP PMR-AS). 113 patients eligible for the Safety and Efficacy of Tocilizumab versus Placebo in Polymyalgia Rheumatica with Glucocorticoid Dependence (SEMAPHORE) trial20 were also included. Inclusion criteria for SEMAPHORE, were the fulfillment of the Chuang criteria for PMR, with GCs-dependent primary PMR, defined by a CRP PMR-AS >10 when a prednisone dosage below 10 mg/day was reached, with a taper made according to the international recommendations.

Statistical analysis

Categorical data were compared using Fisher’s exact test or Pearson’s χ² test and continuous data were compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum test or t-test, as appropriate. A multivariable logistic regression was used to compare second-line immunosuppressive drug utilization in ICI-IA versus ICI-PMR, adjusted for arthritis severity (maximum IA CTCAE grade up to and including the time of the baseline RADIOS registry visit), cancer type, cancer stage, recruitment institution, ICI regimen (monotherapy vs combination ICI), and age. Regarding second-line immunosuppressive drugs, only immunosuppressive disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs)—methotrexate, leflunomide and/or biologic/targeted synthetic (b/ts) DMARDs—were retained for the analysis, since they could potentially impact the tumor responses to ICI. Patients whose first DMARD was prescribed before the onset of IA/PMR or who had a DMARD prescribed for concomitant colitis were excluded from the DMARD analyses. Adjustment for concurrent chemotherapy was not possible owing to missing data.

Kaplan-Meier curves were constructed for survival analyses and groups were compared using log-rank tests. Time to a prednisone equivalent dosage of (1) 10 mg/day or (2) 5 mg/day was measured, starting from the first GC dose received at or after the baseline registry visit. A Cox proportional hazards model was performed, adjusting for maximum IA/PMR grade, type of ICI regimen (monotherapy vs combination ICI), ICI-IA/PMR duration at baseline (defined as time from ICI-IA/PMR diagnosis to baseline registry visit), and cancer type. Progression-free survival (PFS) was measured, defined as the time to the first cancer progression or death, starting from the onset of ICI-IA/PMR. We retrospectively identified cancer progression dates that occurred after ICI initiation but prior to the baseline (first) visit in the registry (through medical record review) and prospectively for subsequent dates of cancer progression. Patients whose cancer progressed before the arthritis onset were excluded from this analysis. The Cox model adjusted for cancer type, cancer stage, type of ICI regimen (monotherapy vs combination ICI), and time from ICI initiation to ICI-IA/PMR onset (continuous or categorized as <3 months, 3–6 months, and >6 months). Proportional hazard assumptions were tested using Schoenfeld residuals in each Cox model. A two-sided p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Pair-wise deletion was used in case of missing data. In multivariable models, adjustment variables were selected based on clinical relevance, such as those that were statistically significant in univariable analysis. All the analyses were performed using Stata software, V.18.0. Data for the study was downloaded on April 17, 2025.

Results

Patient characteristics

Demographic characteristics and cancer history of patients with ICI-IA and ICI-PMR are presented in table 1. 490 patients were included in the analysis, n=418 with ICI-IA and n=72 with ICI-PMR. The cohort as a whole was predominantly white and non-Hispanic. Mean (SD) age at baseline registry visit was higher in patients with ICI-PMR compared with patients with ICI-IA (70 (10) vs 63 (13) years, p<0.001). Patients with ICI-PMR were also more likely to be male than patients with ICI-IA (61% vs 48%, p=0.034). The most frequent cancer types were melanoma (31%), non-small cell lung cancer (15%), and renal cell carcinoma (14%), and most were stage 4 (65%), without significant differences between groups. Most patients had received an anti-programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) (67%), while 25% received combination ICI. Half of patients had another irAE during follow-up. Median (IQR) duration of follow-up was 119 (0–378) days from the baseline RADIOS registry visit and 358 (127–709) days from IA/PMR onset, comparable between groups.

ICI-IA and ICI-PMR characteristics

Inflammatory arthritis characteristics at the baseline RADIOS registry visit (first visit in the registry) are presented in table 2. The median (IQR) time from ICI initiation to ICI-IA/PMR onset was 125 (36–267) days in ICI-IA versus 87 (28–192) days in ICI-PMR (p=0.011). Maximum CTCAE grade up to and including the baseline RADIOS registry visit did not differ between groups, with a predominance of grade 2 (50%). While the majority (72%) of patients with ICI-IA had at least one peripheral swollen joint at baseline visit (35% of patients with ICI-IA were on GCs at baseline) peripheral joint swelling was only observed in 15% of patients with ICI-PMR (52% were on GCs at baseline). Among patients with ICI-IA with swollen peripheral joints, 69%, 48% and 57% had swollen hand joints, wrist and/or knee respectively. Among patients with ICI-PMR with at least one swollen joint, 78%, 25% and 33% had involvement of hand joints, wrist and/or knee respectively. None of the patients with ICI-PMR had ankle or foot swelling while 30% and 23% of patients with ICI-IA did respectively. The median 28 swollen joint count was higher in patients with ICI-IA than patients with ICI-PMR, as was the CDAI (14 vs 7.5, p<0.001). Tenosynovitis was only reported in 43 patients (10% of the whole cohort), all but one of them in the ICI-IA group. Enthesitis was also rarely reported (14%), virtually all of them (96%) in the ICI-IA group. RF and/or anti-CCP positivity was reported in 48 patients (15%) without a significant difference between groups. Regarding Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System health measures, the median T-score for pain was slightly higher in ICI-IA than ICI-PMR (60 vs 57, p=0.039) while the median T-score for physical function was similar (39 vs 40, p=0.086). At the baseline RADIOS registry visit, a normal CRP was reported in 41% and 54% of patients with ICI-IA and ICI-PMR, respectively.

Glucocorticoid utilization

GC utilization in ICI-IA and ICI-PMR is presented in table 3. 129 (35%) patients with ICI-IA and 35 (52%) of patients with ICI-PMR were receiving GCs at their baseline RADIOS registry visit (p=0.014). While the median maximum daily GCs dose at any time was slightly higher in patients with ICI-IA than patients with ICI-PMR (20 mg vs 15 mg, p=0.050), the median (IQR) maximum dose prescribed after baseline registry visit was similar (10 (10–20) mg/day and 11 (10–20) mg/day respectively). The cumulative GC dosage in the first 6 months after ICI-IA/PMR onset was tracked and documented in a subset of patients (n=125 ICI-IA, and n=36 ICI-PMR, figure 1). The average daily GCs dose at each time point until 6 months did not differ between groups. The overall median (IQR) prednisone equivalent dose in the first 2 weeks of treatment was 15 (10–26) mg/day and was down to a median (IQR) 9.3 (4.7–18) mg/day during the second month of treatment. Kaplan-Meier curves demonstrating the time to achieve a prednisone equivalent dose of (1) 10 mg/day and (2) 5 mg/day from first GC prescription at or after baseline registry visit were similar between groups (p=0.51 and p=0.69 respectively, figure 2A,B). Similar results were found in the Cox model after adjustment for confounders (online supplemental table 1).

Second line immunosuppressive drug utilization

Second line immunosuppressive drugs (methotrexate, leflunomide and/or any b/ts DMARD) were more commonly prescribed to patients with ICI-IA than to ICI-PMR (42% vs 26%, p=0.016, table 3). The difference in immunosuppressive DMARDs use between ICI-IA and ICI-PMR remained significant in the multivariable logistic regression modeling after adjusting for confounders (online supplemental table 2), with an OR of 3.51 (95% CI 1.50 to 8.21), p=0.004). Maximum CTCAE grade was also associated with second line immunosuppressive drug use in this model (OR 11.62 (95% CI 3.23 to 41.87) p<0.0001), and a negative association (less immunosuppressive DMARD use) was found in patients with non-small cell lung cancer compared with patients with melanoma (OR 0.31 (95% CI 0.12 to 0.80) p=0.016). Methotrexate was more commonly used in the ICI-IA group (p=0.009, table 3), while hydroxychloroquine was used in 22% of patients overall, with a similar proportion across groups. 117 patients (26%) required a biologic or a Janus kinase inhibitor, without a significant difference between groups (27% vs 19%, p=0.18). TNFis were used almost exclusively in patients with ICI-IA (68/69) whereas IL-6R inhibitors were used in a similar proportion in both groups (14% in ICI-IA vs 16% in ICI-PMR). ICI was temporarily held (for any irAE) in 69% of patients, and permanently discontinued (for any irAE) in 25%, without difference between groups.

Progression-free survival

PFS from onset of ICI-IA/PMR was comparable between ICI-IA and ICI-PMR in log rank testing (p=0.97, figure 3A). This finding was confirmed in a multivariable Cox model (adjusted HR (aHR) for ICI-PMR: 0.91 (95% CI 0.53 to 1.57), p=0.739, online supplemental table 3). Patient age was removed from the model since it violated the proportional hazard assumption, and it was not significant in the univariable nor the multivariable model for PFS. Time from ICI initiation to ICI-IA/PMR onset was categorized since the continuous variable violated the proportional hazard assumption. A sensitivity analysis of PFS was performed in the subgroup of patients with melanoma and also showed no difference between ICI-IA and ICI-PMR groups (p=0.65, figure 3B). Due to the small number of progression events in this subgroup, the Cox model was only adjusted on time from ICI to IA/PMR onset (kept as a continuous variable since respecting the proportional hazard assumption in this model) and cancer stage, with an aHR for progression or death of 0.90 for ICI-PMR compared with ICI-IA (95% CI 0.30 to 2.67), p=0.846 (online supplemental table 3).

Comparison of ICI-PMR to primary PMR

Given the demonstration of some demographic and clinical differences between ICI-IA and ICI-PMR, we investigated the degree to which ICI-PMR resembled primary PMR using an independent cohort of patients with primary PMR (n=189) as shown in table 4. Regarding the sex ratio, a female predominance was observed in primary PMR (females, 64%), while male predominance characterized ICI-PMR (males, 61%). The mean (SD) age at disease onset was slightly lower in primary-PMR, without statistical significance (67 (8.2) years in primary PMR versus 69 (10) years in ICI-PMR, p=0.055). While anti-CCP positivity was reported in 11 patients with primary PMR (6%) none of the patients with ICI-PMR were CCP positive. RF positivity was similar between groups (6.8% in primary PMR and 7.3% in ICI-PMR). 15% of patients with primary PMR had at least one swollen peripheral joint (excluding the shoulders and hips), with a similar proportion for ICI-PMR (15%), mainly involving the hand, with the metacarpophalangeal joints being the most affected. Further data on hip/shoulder pain and range of motion was only available in a subset of patients with ICI-PMR (n=20–29) and are presented in online supplemental table 4 in comparison to patients with primary PMR, broken down by the clinical trial in which the patients with primary PMR were enrolled. Despite half of the ICI-PMR receiving GCs at baseline, shoulder involvement was comparable with GC naïve recent onset PMR, with bilateral shoulder limited range of motion reported in 60% and 71% of the patients respectively. Six out of 20 patients (30%) had upper extremity elevation <90°, which was similar to 23/76 (30%) in recent onset primary PMR. Bilateral hip pain was reported in nearly half of the patients with ICI-PMR (48%). Bilateral limited hip range of motion was found in 32% compared with 43% of the primary recent onset PMR. Back pain was reported only in 2 patients with ICI-PMR (10%) compared with 24 recent-onset primary PMR (32%). The median (IQR) CRP was lower in ICI-PMR compared with primary recent PMR (0.7 (0.1–2.3) mg/dL versus 2.5 (1.3–5.5) mg/dL), but comparable to GC-dependent PMR (0.9 (0.4–1.7) mg/dL).

Discussion

In this large prospective multicenter observational cohort, we highlight clinical differences in ICI-PMR compared with ICI-IA, such as a male predominance, older age of onset, and faster time of onset after ICI initiation. As expected, there was a lower proportion of patients with ICI-PMR with swollen peripheral joints compared with patients with ICI-IA at the baseline visit (15% vs 72%), but peripheral joint swelling in ICI-PMR was comparable to patients with primary PMR. There was similar GC use between patients with ICI-IA and ICI-PMR, but significantly greater utilization of second-line immunosuppressive drugs in the ICI-IA group. There was no statistical difference in PFS between patients with ICI-IA and ICI-PMR in the cohort as a whole, or in the subset of patients with melanoma.

The mean age of patients with ICI-IA and ICI-PMR in our study was consistent with previously published series.5 8 The frequency of peripheral arthritis in patients with ICI-PMR in our cohort (15%), however, was lower than previously reported. For example, de Fremont et al reported a 57% frequency of peripheral arthritis in a series of 14 patients with ICI-PMR, and Vermeulen et al reported a frequency of 53% in a series of 15 patients with ICI-PMR.21 22 Those two series had a comparator group of primary PMR that demonstrated lower rates of peripheral arthritis (n=12/43, 28% and 11/37, 30% respectively). In a recent meta-analysis of ICI-PMR cases (n=314), Hysa et al reported a 35% prevalence of peripheral arthritis.8 The difference between our series and these others could be the result of several factors. Half of patients with ICI-PMR in our cohort were on GCs at baseline registry visit, which could have led to resolution of subtle preexisting peripheral joint swelling. In addition, given the absence of specific classification criteria for ICI-PMR, the diagnosis relies on physician judgment. Thus, we cannot exclude the possibility that some patients with shoulder girdle pain and peripheral arthritis were classified as having ICI-IA in our cohort but might have been labeled as having ICI-PMR in another series. There were in fact differences observed in the proportion of patients with ICI-PMR identified across the RADIOS investigational sites, suggesting a center effect in the referral or diagnostic process. One way to limit those discrepancies in the future would be to apply the classification criteria of primary PMR to ICI-PMR.15 In one ICI-PMR series, Calabrese et al reported that only 12/20 patients (60%) fulfilled the 2012 ACR/EULAR classification criteria for primary PMR (75% when additional cases from the literature were included), while 11/14 (79%) in the series of de Fremont et al, and 10/15 (67%) in the series of Vermeulen et al, met PMR criteria.7 21 22 Owing to missing data, we could not assess the proportion of patients with ICI-PMR fulfilling the ACR/EULAR 2012 classification criteria in our study. Perhaps relevant to this issue, a recent whole-body MRI study demonstrated an equal prevalence of subclinical inflammation and erosions in patients with ICI-IA (n=25) and ICI-arthralgia (n=35), raising concerns about the performance of physical examination to classify rheumatic irAE patients.4 11

We demonstrated a higher utilization of second-line immunosuppressive drugs in patients with ICI-IA compared with ICI-PMR; however, we did not find any difference in GC use. The median average daily dose used in both groups was in accordance with the expert recommendations.2326 Regarding cancer outcomes, it is interesting to note that despite greater utilization of second-line immunosuppressive drugs in the ICI-IA group, including more methotrexate and TNFi use, we did not demonstrate any difference in PFS. While safety data on methotrexate used to treat rheumatic irAE have been mostly reassuring in small series, there are some concerns about the safety of TNFi in the ICI context. For example, in a Dutch melanoma registry, ICI-treated patients who received a TNFi±GC (n=65) for an irAE had worse overall survival compared with those treated with GCs alone (n=157). However the study did not account for immortal time bias or cumulative GC exposure.27 Similarly, in an observational cohort of 147 patients with ICI-IA/PMR, TNFi use was associated with worse PFS compared with methotrexate (aHR=3.27 (95% CI 1.21 to 8.84); p=0.019). However the small number of progressors in that study did not allow for multiple adjustments.28 In an international study, Verheijden et al demonstrated moderate worse survival in case of a second-line immunosuppressive drug utilization to treat an irAE, but without significant impairment in the subgroup of patient receiving a TNFi (n=102).29 Despite 69 patients (15%) in our cohort receiving a TNFi at any time during follow-up, 68/69 in the ICI-IA group, we did not show a difference in PFS in patients with ICI-IA compared with patients with ICI-PMR. Of important note, our survival analyses were not designed for the purpose of studying the specific impact on TNFi or any other second-line immunosuppressive drug on survival. Although overall b/ts DMARD use was similar between ICI-IA and ICI-PMR, it is interesting to note that the use of biologic in ICI-PMR was exclusively with IL-6R inhibitors, likely related to IL6-R inhibitor’s market authorization for primary PMR.

We found that maximum CTCAE grade was associated with immunosuppressive DMARD use. Additionally, patients with ICI-IA had a higher CDAI at baseline than patients with ICI-PMR, although symptom duration at baseline was comparable. Cappelli et al demonstrated that higher CDAI and a longer symptom duration at baseline were significantly associated with more DMARD use in a cohort of 126 patients with ICI-IA (ICI-PMR was excluded) (OR 1.06 (95% CI 1.01 to 1.11), p=0.017 and OR 1.06 (95% CI 1.01 to 1.12), p=0.027 respectively).16 In a latent class analysis of those same 126 patients with ICI-IA, Cappelli et al later defined two clinical phenotypes, one with higher disease activity and more upper extremity and small joint involvement. In that study, there was a trend toward more GCs use in patients with that ICI-IA phenotype, but no difference in DMARD use.17

In our study, we demonstrated some common features between ICI-PMR and primary PMR, such as the age at disease onset and the proportion and location of associated peripheral arthritis, which could potentially be explained by common pathophysiology underlying the two diseases. Hysa et al demonstrated that the elevation of serum soluble coinhibitory immune checkpoint molecules concentration, notably PD-1, PDL-1 and PDL-2, were strongly associated with primary PMR compared with healthy controls, suggesting a possible immunomodulatory feedback mechanism to control T cell hyperactivity in the disease.30 However, we also report differences between ICI-PMR and primary PMR, such as the male predominance in ICI-PMR, which has already been described8 21 and which may point to differences in the pathophysiology in ICI-PMR compared with primary PMR. (Interestingly, in one study of patients with melanoma treated with anti-PD1, men had an increased risk of developing vitiligo31). Vermeulen et al suggested ICI-PMR could be a distinct entity from primary PMR characterized by less inflammation, given a lower frequency of weight loss and morning stiffness, and milder positron emission tomography (PET) fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) uptake in the spinal regions, greater trochanters and ischial tuberosities (although the 18F-FDG-PET Leuven score was still higher than in controls).22

Our study’s limitations include its medium size and the heterogeneity of the cohort; however, it is the largest prospective cohort available so far. Having multiple investigative sites enabled us to account for different practices, both diagnostically and in the prescription of second-line immunosuppressive drugs. To account for heterogeneity, we adjusted for investigative center in the multivariable analyses. Another limitation concerns the definition of ICI-IA and ICI-PMR cases, which was based on the physician diagnosis in relation to the corresponding primary inflammatory diseases, with site-to-site variation. Nevertheless, the differences highlighted in our study based on these case definitions mean that rheumatologists still identified clinically meaningful distinct groups. The proportion of patients receiving GCs at the baseline registry visit (38%) was another limitation which could have impacted the swollen joint counts at the baseline visit. Furthermore, we cannot ascertain that the tender and swollen joint count was done as thoroughly for patients with ICI-PMR as for patients with ICI-IA. Importantly, CDAI does not capture disease activity as well for ICI-PMR as it does for ICI-IA, and the PMR activity score was available for only a subset of patients with ICI-PMR. Finally, two limitations come from the recruitment settings of the patients. First, the fact that RADIOS is an academic registry could have led to the inclusion of more severe ICI-IA/PMR phenotypes. Second, the ICI-PMR and primary PMR cohorts drew from different populations (geographic areas, timeline periods, academic vs clinical trial setting) which could have influenced the results, such as the inclusion of younger patients to receive a biologic DMARD in the primary PMR clinical trials. However, the gender differences we identified were unlikely to have been influenced by study setting.

Conclusion

This is the largest study comparing the clinical features and disease trajectory of ICI-IA versus ICI-PMR. ICI-PMR was characterized by an older age, an earlier onset after ICI initiation and male predominance compared with ICI-IA. Despite comparable GCs utilization, more second-line immunosuppressive drugs were prescribed for ICI-IA. However, PFS was similar between groups. Our results suggest enough in the way of differences between these two entities, that future clinical trials should stratify enrollment according to ICI-IA versus ICI-PMR phenotype, since the former is more severe. This is particularly important because the ACR/EULAR ICI-IA classification criteria are likely to group ICI-IA and ICI-PMR as a single entity. It is notable that although more patients with ICI-IA required second-line immunosuppressive drugs than patients with ICI-PMR, it was still less than half of them (42%). This suggests that better approaches are needed to identify patients who will need a second-line immunosuppressant, in order to minimize prolonged patient discomfort and GC exposure, and to optimize the design of clinical trials.
