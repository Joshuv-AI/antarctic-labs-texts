Challenges in radioecology following the new trends in UAE’s agriculture and environmental changes: a review
Authors: Jan Mihalik, Houda Chelaifa, Mouza Alzaabi, Ahmed K. Alkaabi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The development of the nuclear industry in the countries of the Arabian Gulf demands an investigation of its potential impact on the environment and human activities. It should involve routine monitoring of radionuclide from existing nuclear facilities as well as modelling of accidental release of radioactivity. Agriculture is usually considered the human activity which is the most endangered with radioactive pollution. Although the traditional concept of low-developed agriculture in the United Arab Emirates has been already described from a radioecological point of view, herein, the recent trends of agriculture are recorded. Due to climatic changes and population increase, the use of saline lands will be more pressing. Apart from it, the risk assessment for nuclear events, which was formed mainly for temperate zones, neglected another aspect of arid zones which is underground water reservoirs refilled with episodic heavy rains. The sandy or saline soils are not an effective barrier for the migration of radionuclide deposited in topsoil. A hypersaline environment could prevent the adsorption of radionuclide on soil particles and allow their high bioavailability for halophyte plants. The new principal challenges in radioecological research in arid zones are impacts on (i) saline agriculture, (ii) underground water reservoirs, and (iii) proposal for countermeasures in order to minimise the impact of radioactive contamination.

Introduction

The development of nuclear technology in the United Arab Emirates (UAE) opened the tasks of environmental monitoring, assessment of the environmental impact in the course of the standard operation and in the emergency situations as well as the strategy for preparedness in the case of uncontrolled radioactive discharge. Since the nuclear power plants (NPPs) in UAE are the first and only built in extremely arid conditions, the number of radioecological studies is limited.

Thanks to circumstances the region of the Arab peninsula has not seriously suffered from any previous radiation event. Even the global nuclear fallout left hardly recognisable traces of 137Cs in the environment of UAE (Raj et al. 2022). Unfortunately, the long-term records of radioactive concentration in the environment as a consequence of global nuclear fallout were not provided.

The target radionuclides are 36Cl, 90Sr, 129I, 131I, 134Cs, and 137Cs which could be released during nuclear accidents in nuclear power plant (NPP) or from nuclear waste facilities. The fate of radionuclides in the environment is naturally different due to their different chemical and physical characteristics. The most important is valency which pre-determine their speciation in solution. Another important characteristic is the physical form in which they are present in the environment: some forms are more soluble than others. Notwithstanding, the physical form of contaminants could change under the environmental conditions due to weathering, acidification, interaction with biota, etc. However, the chosen radionuclides are characteristic of high bioavailability (Weller et al. 2019; Bytwerk and Higley 2009).

When the nuclear accident leads to uncontrolled radioactivity discharge to the air, apart from the gas radionuclides, others will be in the form of aerosol because they are released together with water from the primary circle (Koo et al. 2014) in the case of a feed-and-bleed process or hydrogen explosion. Depending on the atmospheric layer which aerosol achieves and atmospheric conditions, they could be deposited in various distances from an NPP. In the case of Fukushima, the radius of the most contaminated area was 40 km (Kato et al. 2019). The arid conditions lead to assume that radionuclide will be eliminated from the air as aerosol deposits formed in a humid atmosphere. At the same time, the surface layer of the desert will be intensively disturbed by wind.

The arid conditions are similar to the conditions in the Sahara Desert in Algeria where the nuclear tests were carried out in the 1960s (Danesi et al. 2008); however, the more detailed information of the radioecological situation in this locality is unknown. Forty years later, the highest activities were found in vitrified sand and lava, and there were also observed places with activities of 239, 240Pu, 137Cs, and 90Sr in the range of 50 Bq/kg, 3.5 kBq/kg, and 12 kBq/kg, respectively, deponed in sand and soil (Danesi et al. 2008).

One of the principles of nuclear safety is to be prepared for the worst scenario which is uncontrolled release of radioactivity into the environment. Preparedness for such situation has to include models of contamination transport, potential impacts, and countermeasures in order to eliminate the adverse effects. A specific environment calls for a specific solution. Especially, if that environment has added value: it is rare, unique, and fragile. The goal of emergency preparedness plans is firstly protection of people, but the environmental protection should be the next. Preparedness will shorten the time to make decisions and realise in situ activities.

The goal of the review is to reveal specifics of the local environment and agriculture which have not been considered until now for radioecology purposes. The consideration of them will help to adjust models of radionuclide distribution in the environment and to estimate the potential risk for saline agriculture from radioactive contamination. The general information about radionuclide bioavailability and the effect of salinity are linked with the real situation in regional agriculture and specific environmental conditions.

Environmental conditions

Potential interaction of nuclear industry and environment in the Arabic peninsula

Installation of nuclear power plants in hyperarid conditions arose the attention of the environment in this region. NPP lies on the skirt of the second largest water table in the peninsula, Sabkha Matti (Fig. 1) in altitude of 5 m a.s.l. The atmospheric dispersion model (Lee et al. 2014) showed that it is predominantly south-east surrounding NPP which could be adversely contaminated with radioactive fallout. Therefore, special attention should be paid to the underground water present in aquifers in this region. In temperate zones, this issue is naturally eliminated because water replenishing the aquifer is filtered through developed soil in the top layers. Clay, Fe/Mn minerals and organic matter (Mihalik et al. 2018) could adsorb dissolved radionuclide ions from the soil solution. In that case, the radionuclides are mainly retarded in the uppermost 10 cm of soil (Jagercikova et al. 2015). The situation is different in soil poor of adsorptive particles or with porous structure (Al-Rayyes et al. 2007). Upon these conditions, contamination could reach deeper layers. Eventually, the crucial factor for the downward mobility of contaminants is the rate of water in the soil, namely permeability. Aba et al. (2018) found that the activity in dust deposited on the top soil in the North-Eastern region of the Gulf is associated mainly with fine particles.Fig. 1Distribution of sabkhas in the Arabian Peninsula (Alshenawy et al 2021; Qureshi 2017). , nuclear power plant; , coastal sabkha; , inland sabka

The challenge which is poorly considered in arid climates is episodic heavy rains (Aba et al. 2021). They are able to recharge underground reservoirs and mobilise topsoil contaminants. Considering the climate changes, the models predict increasing in rain precipitation in the UAE by up to 30% during this century (Rao et al. 2024). The expectation is that most of these rainfalls will appear as extreme heavy rains.

A model of radionuclide dispersion in the Arabic gulf after radioactivity release from the UAE’s NPPs (Perianez 2021) showed that coastal marshland and mangroves could be exposed to contamination. Whilst in the winter season, the west–east coastal currents dominate along the UAE coast; in other seasons, they are replaced with the weaker east–west currents. However, since the currents are generally weak and the water exchange between the Arabic Gulf and Oman sea is not very intensive, the interaction between contaminated sea water and coastal ecosystems will last a long period (Maderich et al. 2023). This could represent a significant risk for coastal saline agriculture once established in this area. Moreover, even the release of radioactivity from Bushehr NPP on the coast of Iran could cause the contamination of the UAE coast in the long-term perspective (Perianez 2021).

Due to global warming, the increasing of sea levels is a fact (El-Geziry 2023). The main reason is the volume expansion of water when adsorbing heat and melting of ice. It could cause significant risk to the coastal infrastructure including power plants. The model showed that the sea level will increase in the range of 0.6–1.2 m before 2100 (MOCCE 2021). Moreover, the impact could be more severe when storm surges are included. Even if the NPP will not be imminently exposed to the increasing sea level thanks to engineering barriers, the vicinity of the NPP could be seriously affected. The effects on the surroundings could be the following: (i) increasing of underground water, (ii) erosion of soil, and (iii) destroying of infrastructure (Kopytko and Perkins 2011). All these expected environmental changes make the prediction of environmental impacts of potential radioactive contamination more uncertain.

Arid soil

Surface water can penetrate soil by diffusion, percolation, or flow through cracks. In this order, the rate of soil penetration increases. Whilst dispersed sand is well passable for water, sand rock could be an effective barrier for immersion and water needs to reach cracks in the rock to descend to deeper layers. Water with contaminants is in permanent contact with soil which could be an effective filter of radionuclides if it contains absorptive matters (clay, organic matter, Fe/Mn minerals, etc.). However, the content of clay and organic matter in sand is usually very low (< 1%). Burmann (1993) showed that even such small quantities of absorptive matter could play a significant role in the elimination of radionuclide from the soil solution. On the other hand, colloid clay particles released in the water stream could be a vector for radionuclides in soil (Saiers and Hornberger 1996).

The region of the Arabic Gulf is characteristic of arid conditions which influence the soil quality throughout the region. Soils belong typically to Entisols — Sandy Desert Soils, which could contain calcerous, gypsum, and clay, and aridosols, which are water deficient and rich in dissolved salts (Shahid and Abdelfattah 2008).

Sabkha

Sabkha is the name for land with higher salinity due to the presence of salty aquifers. Coastline sabkhat (plural of sabkha) are exposed also to high tides and seawater invasions into the permeable substratum which replenishes water lost to evaporation (Saeed et al. 2021) (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Situation in saline ecosystems and potential ways of contamination: (a) mangrove with tide, (b) coastal sabkha, (c) wind, (d) aquifer with upward capillary flow and downward flow by pores, (e) water table, (f) exchange of underground water between sea and water table

Inland sabkha lays on the saline aquifer which could be the remains of a prehistoric ocean (Saeed et al. 2021). Furthermore, they are facultatively exposed to precipitation. Isotopic analyses showed that Sabkha Matti is not probably replenished from the current seas (Saeed et al. 2021). On the other hand, water in sabkha undergoes vertical circulation and even horizontal movement which should be considered in models of contaminant migration. Coastal and inland sabkhat occupied around 25% of the UAE area. Their salinity is 2 to 25 dSm−1 (Qureshi 2017). Some salt-tolerant plants are able to grow on slightly saline soil. Increasing salinity allows the growth of only halophyte plants which have mechanisms to avoid the toxic effects of salt.

On another hand, some farms use salt water for irrigation which increases the salinity of farmland. However, some special agronomic management allows plant production even on the high saline land (Qureshi 2017).

Alshenawy et al. (2021) reported significant contents of clay minerals: montmorillonite, illite, and kaolinite in sabkhat in Saudi Arabia. Especially, montmorillonite and illite are very effective adsorbers of Cs (Kwon et al. 2023). Whilst illite is a highly specific adsorber for caesium due to its interlayer structure, montmorillonite has a high adsorption capacity thanks to swelling (Kwon et al. 2023).

Sabkha soil is formed mainly of sand and gypsum. The specific feature of sabkha soils is that their minerals (anhydrite, calcite, gypsum, and salts) undergo a cycle of dissolving and precipitation during the rain episodes (Saeed et al. 2021). The aquifer of the water table in Sabkha Matti is only 1 m below the surface (Saeed et al. 2020). The combination of the subsurface localization and high-water permeability of the soil allows the replenishing of the aquifer from rain precipitation (Fig. 2).

Aridosols have usually poorly developed uppermost soil layers which are often formed of compacted calcerous, gypsum, or silica. Sand which covers an area does not fulfil the filtration function unlike clay or organic matter present in moderate climate regions. If the layers are not compacted, their high water permeability allows to form underground water reservoirs in aquifers. Rain precipitation therefore easily penetrates soil and refills reservoirs. Limited filtration ability of soil could lead to contaminant cotransport from the top layers to the aquifer (Aba et al. 2018). Contaminants present in the top layers could reach the deep aquifer through upward ground flux, rainwater flux, and diffusive transport which are usual processes also in the wetland in the Chernobyl Exclusion Zone (Bugai et al. 2022). The difference between wetlands in the temperate zone and the salt plane in the subtropical zone is in the organic content which is very low in the salt plane.

Al-Amoudi et al. (1992) carried out experiments with similar sabkha soil from Saudi Arabia. The permeability for distillate water was up to 0.11 m.h−1 which is in the fast permeability class (Gupta et al. 2024). Al-Rayyes et al. (2007) performed column experiments with soils typical in Kuwait. Whilst Cs and Pu were retained in the upper soil layers, Sr passed to the deeper layers up to 30 cm in gypsiferous soil. Thanks to unstable structure, high water permeability, and low retention capacity, the gypsiferous soils have the highest permeability also for radionuclides.

Gypsum soil represents one of the typical soils in arid conditions in the UAE (Shahid and Abdelfattah 2008). Gypsum is characteristic of low stability when exposed to water. Structural changes lead to cavitation. Moreover, the surface of the particles in gypsum soil is easily dissolved in unsaturated solution (Kuechler et al. 2004) for example rain. Rainwater passing through soil washes away dissolved anions SO42− and cations Ca2+ and Sr2+, which coprecipitated with Ca in sulphate minerals. At the same time, soil rich in gypsum has high water-holding capacity (Moret-Fernandez and Herrero 2015).

This phenomenon should be considered in the strategy of measures for remediation of a contaminated environment after a nuclear accident with radionuclide release into the environment.

Salinity

The influence of salinity on radionuclide mobility in the environment is still disputable. The majority of results showed that increasing salinity leads to decreasing adsorption and increasing desorption of radionuclides from clay (Delaval et al. 2020). The main impact of salinity on radionuclide reactions comes through isotope dilution and ion competition. When Cs is considered, the concentrations of 134Cs and 137Cs in seawater are lower than the concentration of 133Cs (0.35 ug/L), the only natural isotope of Cs. Once when isotope equilibrium state is reached, the ratio among isotopes in solution and adsorbent will be the same, however, with the domination of the natural isotope.

Ion competition in adsorption results from similarities among ions (K+, Na+, and Cs+) in their valency; however, they distinguish in radius of hydrated ions. Whilst Na+ has six molecules of water in its hydration sphere, K+ has 7 water molecules and Cs+ has eight water molecules (Persson 2022). Caesium is the weakliest hydrated alkali metal ion.

Moreover, the ions interact with each other in accordance with the specific interaction theory based on the Debye–Huckel theory (Ludwig 2022). The result is the lower concentrations of ions available for reactions than their total concentrations. Ion-pair interactions increase with ion concentrations, namely salinity.

Eventually, in natural conditions, salinity is not an isolated parameter; therefore, the final effect depends on ion speciation, the presence of different adsorbents (clay, organic matter), pH, etc.

Special attention should be paid when salinity changes. It happens (i) in the vertical profile of seawater, (ii) in the coastal area when seawater is exposed to evaporation, and (iii) when it is mixed with fresh water. Mixing of brackish groundwater with meteoric water is different during the year; therefore, the salinity of groundwater seasonally changes (Turkeltaub et al. 2024). Existing equilibriums are disturbed and establishing new equilibriums is time-dependent. Delaval et al. (2020) realised that nonequilibrium processes led to the highest desorption efficiency of Cs, up to 87%. Turkeltaub et al. (2024) carrying out experiments with U and Sr found that artificial saline groundwater increased the mobility of both elements in the environment simulated surrounding of nuclear fuel repository. On the other hand, a low ionic strength solution stabilises colloids, increasing the sorption of elements in colloids and therefore increasing their mobility (Turkeltaub et al. 2024).

Nagao et al. (2020) studied the desorption of 137Cs from the beach sand. 137Cs was deposited after the Fukushima nuclear accident. Interestingly, the high activity of 137Cs was found even in 100 cm of vertical profile. The accumulation of caesium in deeper layers could be caused by the accumulation of adsorptive silk-clay particles in these layers. Sea water with salinity around 33 could partially desorb once adsorbed Cs from sand; however, the experiment did not reach a higher desorption efficiency than 58%, rather lower (on average 40%). On the other hand, pure water which could simulate rain had a desorption efficiency of only around 1%. The question of why seawater did not desorb caesium from beach sand in the natural conditions could be elucidated through equilibrium between the adsorption/desorption process; simply, caesium does not run away from the system.

Sanial et al. (2017) showed that salinity did not have an influence on adsorption efficiency. However, it is not clear in this paper why moderate salinity should have the highest desorption efficiency. There could be another factor which influences desorption in the case of moderate salinity.

Whilst in the low salinity represented by deionised water, desorption is controlled through diffusion and potential dissolving of the particle surface; in the high salinity, it is driven by competitive ion-exchange reaction and isotope dilution. Therefore, when the conclusions of Nagao et al. (2020) and Sanial et al. (2017) are assessed, the desorption increasing with increasing salinity seems to be more plausible. Alamudy and Cho (2018) brought experimental evidence of the impact of ionic strength on caesium adsorption on montmorillonite: increasing ionic strength decreased the adsorption of caesium.

Copplestone et al. (2001) analysed sand on the beach of the Irish Sea. Surprisingly, 137Cs did not penetrate sand deeper than 10 cm. The conclusion was that biofilm on the sand surface reduces Cs (radionuclide) mobility.

The salinity of sabkha soil is up to six times higher than seawater (Alshenawy et al. 2021). It significantly limits the life present in these conditions. However, the soil surface is usually covered with biofilm and some algae, and even some higher plants are adapted to this harsh environment.

Target radionuclides

Since our attention is focused mainly on saline conditions, the target radionuclides are 36Cl, 90Sr, 129I, 131I, 134Cs, and 137Cs which have analogues in the most abundant elements in seawater.

Chlorine

36Cl is the second most critical radionuclide in nuclear waste (White and Broadley 2001). It arises during the operation of a nuclear reactor as an activation product from 35Cl. It could be released mainly during decommissioning and from nuclear waste. Despite its long half-life (3.105 years) and its belonging to β-emitters, 36Cl is significant for radioecology due to its enormous bioavailability and indispensability for organisms (Bytwerk and Higley 2009). Based on the models, 36Cl has a higher radiation impact on the terrestrial environment (excluding humans) than 129I (Sheppard et al. 1996).

Soil solution contains chlorine predominantly as a monovalent anion. Due to the negative charge, it is poorly adsorbed on inorganic soil matrices. The exemption is organic matter which could contain chlorine integrated in organic compounds. This could be a result of microorganism interaction with detritus. Vertical distribution of Cl differs in relation to the content of organic matter in soil: (i) in soil without organic matter chlorine leaches from the top layers to deeper zones, (ii) soil with organic matter usually contains chlorine in at least two different forms: less mobile organically bonded chlorine enriched in the organic layer and anionic chloride in leachate in deeper profile (Le Dizes and Gonze 2019). The affinity of chlorine to organic matter leads to enrichment of Cl in the organic layer up to 80% of the total Cl content in soil (Le Dizes and Gonze 2019). Some parts of soil chlorine could be integrated also into volatile organic compounds through microorganism metabolism. Naturally, the high mobility of 36Cl in soil represents a high contamination risk also for underground water.

Chlorine belongs to plant micronutrients. It is taken up primarily as chloride anion and in this form plays its role in plant physiology which is mainly osmoregulation, membrane potential, and electrical excitability. Thanks to the negative charge, chlorine could easily enter the plant tissue through roots and consequently be distributed in the whole body.

Another entrance for chlorine to plant bodies is via leaves. Henner et al. (2013) showed that soluble 36Cl is easily and rapidly absorbed through leaves. This could play an important role in saline coastlines where plants are exposed to regular tides.

Plants have the ability to exudate chlorine through the roots back to the soil solution. However, it naturally happens when favoured conditions occur, namely when the Cl concentration in soil solution decreases. It is not clear how plants use the ability to exudate Cl in permanently saline conditions (against the concentration gradient). Some plants (e.g., Atriplex) exudate saline on the leaf surface (Kahli et al. 2021).

Chlorine is eluted from death plant biomass very easily (Kashparov et al. 2007) probably because it is primarily in extracellular space.

Usually, the Cl concentration in plants is higher than in soil solution. This is not applicable for saline conditions when plants need to regulate internal Cl concentration (Flowers and Colmer 2015).

Iodine

Iodine has quite similar characteristics to chlorine. Whilst 131I with a short half-life (8 days) is dangerous in the first days after a nuclear accident, 129I is present in nuclear waste for a very long time (T1/2 = 15.7 × 106 years), and its main source in the environment is reprocessing of nuclear fuel. Both are easily available for organisms with high affinity to thyroid glands.

Contrary to chlorine, iodine has more stable states which makes its chemistry in the bio-geo-chemical cycle more complex. It exists primarily as IO3−, I−, and organic iodine in soil (Fuge and Johnson 2015), whilst in the air iodine, it is present in the forms of I2 and CH3I (Riyazuddin et al. 2022). Radioiodine released in nuclear accidents is considered a combination of I2, aerosols, and organic iodine, such as CH3I (Sportisse 2007). The iodine speciation depends on pH, redox potential, and metabolic processes in organisms (Riyazuddin et al. 2022). Algae are considered the main contributor to atmospheric iodine (Yeager et al. 2017) through the production of volatile halogened organic compounds especially methiodide.

Similar to chlorine, if the soil contains organic matter, they are the main pool of iodine (Seki et al. 2013). Schwehr et al. (2009) have revealed that IO3− and organic iodine have lower mobility and greater affinity for solid components of soil than I−.

The volatilisation of organic iodine compounds mostly occurs in anoxic conditions in paddy soil (Redeker et al. 2000), peat bogs (Keppler et al. 2003), and coastal salt marshes (Manley et al. 2006). Moreover, anoxic conditions generally favour the mobility of iodine in soil (Zhang et al. 2023).

Hou et al. (2003) showed that about 70% of iodine (I-129) in Chernobyl and Irish soils after the explosion of the Chernobyl nuclear power plant was associated with oxides and organic matter, and only 10–20% existed as water-soluble and exchangeable forms. Kashparov et al. (2007) provided experiments in which after 269 days of application of radioiodine isotopes to the soil, < 3% had been washed down to < 20 cm.

The internal speciation of iodine includes I− dissolved in the plant sap, and organic compounds (methyl iodide, proteins, etc.) in the organelles and cell walls (Zhang et al. 2023).

It is well known that marine algae can accumulate iodine to high levels. Fievet et al. (2023) described the concentration factor in brown sea algae at the level of 104. Hou and Yan (1998) systematically studied the iodine content of 35 species of seaweed from coastal areas of China where they found from 13 to 6000 μg/g of iodine in biomass.

Due to its physiological function, iodine is transported to xylem vessels through both apoplastic (extracellular/passive) and symplastic (cytoplasmic/active) pathways (Humphrey et al. 2019). As a solute, I− can freely diffuse in the apoplastic solution. The cell wall, with a negative charge, has little resistance to the movement of anions, creating favourable conditions for I− transport. Active transport dominates when the concentration of iodine is not sufficient. Humphrey et al. (2019) revealed that spinach plants exposed to I− and IO3− contained 90% of the iodine in the apoplast solution in organic form, and the rest was I−. Smolen et al. (2021) assumed the availability of low molecular organic compounds with iodine from soil solution to the roots.

Iodine is accumulated mainly in roots; a smaller part of this iodine flows with the xylem to leaves where the is usually the highest concentration of above-ground organs. Since phloem carries less iodine than xylem, fruits contain the lowest concentration of it (Zhang et al. 2023). However, atmospheric iodine could be absorbed through leaves in the form of I2 or methyl iodide (Riyazuddin et al. 2022).

Caesium

Caesium as a fission product is released from a reactor when the cracks in nuclear rods occur. It is represented by two radioisotopes 134Cs with 2.1 years half-life and 137Cs with 30.2 years half-life. They were massively released during tests of nuclear weapons and accidents in NPPs in Chernobyl and Fukushima. 137Cs is still well detectable in the regions impacted by those accidents. The current research deals also with 135Cs (2.3 My half-life) which ratio with 137Cs could be an important fingerprint of the sources of artificial radioactivity (Stager et al. 2023).

The abundance of stable caesium is 0.3–25 ppm in soil. It has a high absorption affinity to clay and organic matter (Mihalik et al. 2018).

Since Cs belongs to alkali metals, it is dissolved in solution as monovalent cations. Its competitive ion-exchange reactions with potassium are broadly described. However, their mutual influence on their uptake by plants is not clear. Whilst Shin and Adams (2016) presented that increasing potassium concentration in soil solution limits the uptake of caesium into plants, Burger and Lichtscheidl (2018) drew rather an ambivalent conclusion that increasing concentration of potassium could limit or enhance caesium uptake to plants depending on other conditions.

Similarly, to other monovalent ions, caesium is taken up by plants through passive transport with water solution and actively by the K+ channels.

Goddard and Jupp (2001) confirmed the accumulation of 137Cs in seaweed growing in the Arabic sea. In the genus Enteromorpha spp. the concentration of 137Cs was 2.9 Bq.kg−1 dw; however, Cheatomorpha spp. reached 7.5 Bq.kg−1 dw. In that case, the transfer factor of 137Cs from seawater to biomass is approximately 3 when the activity of 137Cs in the Arabic Sea 1.0–2.5 Bq.m−3 (Uddin et al. 2020) is considered. The same seaweeds extremely accumulated 40 K up to 5000 Bq.kg−1. The disproportion between the accumulation of 40 K and 137Cs in Goddard and Jupp (2001) could be caused by a limited pool of 137Cs and an unlimited pool of 40 K.

Strontium

It is highly abundant in seawater and, therefore, also in soil which formed sea bottom, or which is intermittently flooded. Uddin et al. (2013) presented the concentration of strontium in the seawater in the Arabian Gulf in the range of 8.9 to 9.6 ppm which is higher than the typical concentration in the ocean of 7.8 ppm. 90Sr is present in the Arabian Gulf in a low activity of 0.5–1 mBq.L−1.

Radioactive strontium is produced in nuclear reactors during fission and subsequently present in nuclear waste. It could be released from a nuclear facility during an accident, decommissioning, and from the disposal of nuclear waste. The major sources of 90Sr in the global environment were the accident in NPP in Chernobyl (1986) and Fukushima (2011), the accident in Mayak (1957), and a series of tests of nuclear weapons. The most markable radioactive isotope of strontium is 90Sr with a half-life of 28.8 years. It undergoes beta decay with an energy of electrons 0.546 MeV.

Chemical behaviour is typical for the elements of II. A group: bivalent, creating less soluble sulphates and accompanying calcium in various minerals. The forming of less soluble compounds could limit its mobility in water; however, it is entirely considered a mobile element (Burger and Lichtscheidl 2019). Since calcium minerals like gypsum undergo a process of dissolving and recrystallization when they are exposed to rainwater, both elements, Ca and Sr, are temporally released from their crystalline structures, and their mobility increases. Similarly, carbonates are dissolved in solutions with decreasing pH which leads to an increase of mobile form of Ca and Sr in solution.

It is easily bioavailable for plants. There is no known physiological function of Sr, but still, it occurs in organisms due to similar chemical behaviour to calcium. Victorio et al. (2020) found in the leaves of Avicennia schaueariana a concentration of Sr around 0.2 ppm. Ghafouri et al. (2022) investigated the strontium uptake by halophytes: Tamarix spp., Haloxylon spp., and Malcolmia Africana. The concentrations of Sr in the shoots were in the range of 200–300 ppm. Interestingly, strontium was predominantly accumulated in the shoots of Tamarix and Malcolmia. The transfer factors of Sr in Tamarix, Haloxylon, and Malcolmia were 1.37, 0.98, and 1.84, respectively. The lesson from the investigation of the environment of Chernobyl pointed out the strontium fixation on organic matter in the soil. 90Sr recirculated between organic topsoil, plants, and animals for a long time after its release to the environment. The cycle was disturbed when the organic matter disappeared from the soil for example due to fire (Bugai et al. 2022). Afterwards, the mobility of strontium increased, and it reached the aquifers.

The risk of human internal contamination with 90Sr comes from its internalisation in the same tissues as calcium: bones, muscles, etc. These tissues will be exposed to beta radiation for a long time which could result in specific cancers.

Environmental impact on agriculture and biotechnology

Use of saline environment for agriculture and biotechnology

As far as agricultural production is considered, the investigation of typical subtropical plants becomes more important. Raj et al. (2022) made the first approach to a complex description of the agriculture system in the Arab peninsula and particularly in the United Arab Emirates (UAE) including potential ways of harm during radiation situations. However, this approach follows the traditional concept of low-developed agriculture which contains mainly traditional crops like maize, alfalfa, non-leafy vegetables, and leafy vegetables. The origin of this approach comes from experiences from previous nuclear accidents which occurred in temperate climates; therefore, it did not adequately highlight the specifics present in the UAE environment and current trends in agriculture namely saline agriculture. Recently, the natural radionuclides were monitored in ghaf trees (Prosopis cineraria) (Raj et al. 2024).

Another feature of regional agriculture is that production dependent on water and special conditions is carried out mainly in greenhouses. Besides vegetables, mushrooms which are significant accumulators of radionuclides in temperate zones (Weller et al. 2019) grow also indoors. This way of cultivation allows to protect plants against contamination. On the other hand, it is a well-known symbiosis of some mycorrhizal fungi with date palms (El Hilali et al. 2022) which calls for investigation of nutrients and contaminants transfer.

Agriculture in the UAE undergoes changes, and rapid development could be expected in a few years. The traditional concept of low-developed agriculture should be extended with the recent trends which could play an important role in the future. Climate changes and increasing population pushes forward plants which were rather marginalised in the economy: halophytes growing in saline and brackish conditions on the coastal area and salt pan (Qureshi 2017). The saline environment in UAE is represented mainly by salt wetlands on the coast and inland (sabkha) and mangrove forests (Fig. 2). With regard to this, halophytes could be divided upon biotopes which they occupy.

Saline (halophyte) agriculture is intensively investigated (Hamed et al. 2014; Centofanti and Banuelos 2019). Some halophytes are already used for food or forage production (Qin et al. 2016; Centofanti and Banuelos 2019, Nikalje et al. 2022). Eventually, even date palm belongs to salt-tolerant plants (Yaish and Kumar 2015).

Another application of halophytes is the production of biofuels which was successfully realised in UAE by the Sustainable Bioenergy Research Consortium (SBRC) (Laveille et al. 2022). In the system, the seawater is pumped inland to supply the aquaculture farm where fish and/or shrimp is grown. The nutrient-rich water that leaves this subsystem is used to irrigate and fertilise halophyte, here Salicornia fields (Chelaifa et al. 2020). Finally, the effluent coming from the Salicornia fields is channelled to mangrove swamps, which filter the water further before the water is returned to the ocean.

Saline agriculture has an important ecological function when preserving coastal areas against degradation and soil transport to the sea. Similarly, dry areas are preserved against wind transport. Some plants are very effective in the desalination of soil and water (Panta et al. 2014). UAE accommodates several large-scale projects for the development of local saline agriculture (Lyra et al. 2021).

At the same time, the hybridisation of halophytes with traditional crops calls for investigation of the accumulation of heavy metals, radionuclides, and other xenobiotics in such hybrids.

However, there are more salt-tolerant plants which are used as forage (Alhaddad et al. 2021) and which call for advanced analyses of their ability to accumulate radionuclides (Table 1). Algae cultivation holds significant economic importance in the region. Specifically, the algae Dunaliella salina stands out as a distinctive green, halophilic eukaryote renowned for being the primary source of β-carotene production (Loughland et al. 2014). It is an example of saline biotechnology localised in ponds on the skirt of Abu Dhabi city or in Khiran in Kuwait. The hypersaline ponds are 15 cm deep and flooded with seawater.

Notwithstanding, the mangrove forest is also an example of using saline land. Mangrove forest creates a specific ecosystem for plants and animals. If mangrove does not have fresh water influx, the salinity of water is higher than in the surrounding sea. It is regularly exposed to tides which can bring contaminants floating along the coast. Shallow water with a slow stream and higher organic content is ideal for the accumulation of contaminants (Samara et al. 2020).

Surprisingly, until now, saline soil as well as halophytes stayed beyond interest in radioecology. With the development of agriculture in countries poor on arable land but rich in saline land, the question of the bio-geo-chemical cycle of radionuclides in this specific environment and the impact of hypothetical contamination on human activities will arise.

Survival strategy in halophyte

To understand behaviour of radionuclides in halophytes, the physiology of these plants should be described.

Halophytes represent a heterogeneous community as far as the nutrient uptake and water balance are considered. Coastal grasses have the ability to uptake nutrients directly via leaves. Plants on the desert-like saline pans belong to succulents which gives them the ability to reduce water evaporation through leaves cuticula.

(2021) recorded that Atriplex in saline conditions increased the accumulation of cadmium contrary to non-saline conditions. A comprehensive description of heavy metal accumulation in halophytes could be found in Williams et al. (1994).

Radionuclides in halophytes

With regard to the chemical behaviour of elements, there is no difference between radioactive and stable isotopes. The only exemption is hydrogen with isotopes having multiple differences in atomic weight.

Therefore, it is reasonable to assume that the most important radionuclides for future radioecological investigation have analogues among stable nuclides present in saline environments namely Cl, Sr, I, and Cs.

Usually, a high transfer factor for alkali metals is recorded in water plants which uptake nutrients through the whole surface without effective selection (Burger and Lichtscheidl 2018). In the temperate zones, the specific position as radionuclide accumulators belongs to mushrooms and wild boars (Stager et al. 2023), which are marginal in the Arabic peninsula.

The experiments with caesium showed high accumulation in S. portulacastrum (Nikalje et al. 2022) which corresponds well with findings of a high transfer factor for 40 K for S. portulacastrum: 1.5–1.6 (Kaewtubtim et al. 2017). In the case of radioactive contamination, it would be a vulnerable species.

Duarte et al. (2022) presented halophytes (H. portulacoides, S. fruticosa, A. halimus, B. maritima, I. crithmoides, and S. vera.) contain 100 × more iodine and 10 × more chlorine than common leaf vegetable. Moreover, Luque et al. (2015) found high transfer factors for uranium and thorium in Spartina densiflora and Sarcocornia perennis growing on salt marsh.

Trees and shrubs in mangrove forests are salt-tolerant with the ability to accumulate heavy metals and natural radionuclides like 40 K (Table 2). Kaewtubtim et al. (2017) recorded a transfer factor of 40 K as high as 1.8 to stems of Avicennia marina. A high transfer factor of 40 K was characteristic of most of the plants growing in the south-Asia mangrove forest. Copplestone et al. (2001) analysed facultative halotolerant grass Festuca rubra growing on the sand dune and found a transfer factor for 137Cs 0.05–0.14.

As mentioned earlier, the uptake of halophytes is not selective. Moreover, the exudation of salts from the plant body is limited and jointly with physiological mechanisms which eliminate the toxic effects of salts through their compartmentalization; they lead to increasing of their transfer factor.

Impact on agriculture

Due to limited sources of arable land and increasing demand for food self-sufficiency, efforts to exploit salty areas (coastline and salt pans) increase. In some sabkha regions, the underground water is used for irrigation. The contamination of the coastal area or surface of the sabkha could lead to contamination of the water body in the whole sabkha due to connection to the same water table.

Water sources that favour saline agriculture in subtropical zones make the system fragile in the case of its contamination.

Once when animal feeding is based on the production from coastal areas, its contamination could have an adverse impact on the whole food chain. Fortunately, forage produced from saline agriculture usually undergoes treatment in order to decrease its salt content (washing, desalination), and their content in final forage is only around 25% (El Shaer 2021). It is reasonable that the same procedures will lead to a decrease in radioactivity in biomass.

Food and forage are regulated in accordance with the limit which is 100 Bq.kg−1 in Japan (Nabeshi et al. 2022) and 600 Bq.kg−1 in European Union (Stager et al. 2023). It means that well-performed radiation monitoring could eliminate the risk coming from contaminated food. Decrease of contamination of human food through processing and preparation is not applicable; therefore, contaminated plant and animal products are eliminated from future use. However, the consequences would be the development of saline agriculture if the sources, soil and water, were contaminated.

Paradoxically, the highest impact could be expected on the natural ecosystem of mangrove forests: the radioactivity will stay for a long time in the ecosystem. The countermeasures for gentle remediation of the ecosystem are limited. At the same time, it could have an impact on the production of some seafood and fishery.

Sea is a primary source of water for biotechnologies produced, e.g. beta-carotene from the algae living in ponds on the seacoast. In the case of increasing risk of contamination of ponds, engineering barriers could be applied (e.g. replacement of fresh sea water with another source of saline water). However, algae growing in the contaminated environment could contain high concentrations of contaminants (Iwamoto and Shiraiwa 2017). The impact is mainly on commercial activities which will need to find another source of beta-carotene.

The negligible impact could be estimated on the biofuel production. The limit for food safety is not applicable.

Environmental management

Sabkha represents the unique geological heritage of our planet (Lokier 2013). It is already under strong pressure of industrial development and construction of infrastructures: roads, residences, waste dumps. During the last 60 years, the area of Abu Dhabi Sabkha decreased by 34%. Sabkha Mati is more preserved thanks to its remoteness; however, the technological impact is already visible here: highways and nuclear power plants.

Protection of the Sabkha Mati should be included in preparedness in the case of a radiation accident. Since the accident in Fukushima, the strategy for post-accident environmental rehabilitation becomes a part of emergency preparedness for nuclear accidents (IAEA 2014). Strategy for environmental management should cover a model of radionuclide transport in the system involving the following: (i) surface deposition, (ii) vertical penetration with surface water and capillarity, (iii) horizontal movement of underground water mass. At the same moment, the proper way of future environmental management should be proposed: remediation activities should be based on technics which do not destroy the fragile geological structure; therefore, the excavation of surface soil should be avoided. The potentially applicable methods are stabilisation of contamination on the surface using an adsorbent (Galambos et al. 2012). On the skirt of sabkha, the engineering barriers could eliminate the transport of water bodies out of contaminated areas. Shrubs and trees could be effective barriers against contaminant transport on the surface (Khan and Noor 2017).

However, environmental management depends on the perception of environmental values. The specific of the geo-ecological system is that the main communication highway is underground water; therefore, individual parts, horizontally or vertically, cannot be isolated. The environmental positive scenario involves the establishment of an exclusion zone and the adaptation of engineering barriers on the skirt of the zone. The negative environmental scenario of remediation measures would be based on the excavation of the contaminated area and application of engineering barriers directly on the spots: (i) drying the underground water to eliminate radionuclide transport, (ii) injection barrier underground like bitumen, clay, or concrete barriers, (iii) solidification of the surface. Whilst soil excavation in the area of Fukushima was employed (Feng et al. 2022), in sabkha, the excavation of the 5-cm top soil layer could be difficult due to the unstable surface which is drenched by underground water. The way to solidify the surface is drainage of underground water. However, heavy mechanisms, water drainage, and engineering barriers would destroy the geological system in sabkha. Adaptation of remediation methods should be based on a scientific approach and therefore be tested on a laboratory scale in order to use it effectively in emergency situations. Herein, the environmental positive scenario corresponds with increasing attention to gentle remediation methods (Purkis et al. 2022). The goal of GRM is to preserve the beneficial characteristics of the environment. This makes countermeasures sustainable and acceptable for society. The main tool for GRM is the stabilisation of contamination to prevent their transport from contaminated areas. With regard to it, the presence of clay minerals and gypsum in sabkha soil could be an effective tool for stabilisation of contamination.

Strategy for the future research

The future research agenda should comprehensively cover the typical environmental conditions and their changes caused by global changes and human activities. It is also important to consider the perspective of employing natural sources.

The future research requests a series of experiments targeted at the mobility of radionuclide in the specific ecosystems and their transfer to plants. In order to provide these experiments, the local soils need to be sampled, characterised (salinity, pH, clay minerals, other minerals, organic content, cation exchange capacity), and later examined for adsorption capacity for specific radionuclides in various salinity and their mobility.

Another research should cover also specific operations in agriculture like desalinisation, using underground water, as well as adaptation to climate changes. Plants which are traditionally used for feeding or which have perspective in saline agriculture should be investigated for transfer factors in diverse conditions. Data will serve as models of radionuclide migration.

The important research agenda is effective measures for minimising of impact of radioactive contamination. To confirm their effectiveness, the laboratory experiments need to be carried out.

Conclusion

Agriculture in arid zones is a dynamic system which undergoes rapid development. At the same time, it is fragile because all compartments are strongly dependent. Contamination of products of saline agriculture as well as underground water reservoirs could have a crucial impact on the whole system (feeding animals, watering plants).

The radionuclide 36Cl, 90Sr, 129, 131I, and 134, 137Cs are easily available to halophytic plants like their stable analogues and could be accumulated in these species. However, the lack of data calls for future investigation of radionuclide transfer factors to halophytes.

In the case of caesium, a risk of reaching the aquifer and contamination of underground water is high due to the high level of the aquifer and high ionic strength of the soil solution. The exemption is soils rich in clay minerals, especially illite and montmorillonite, which are very effective in caesium adsorption. Similar behaviour of strontium and calcium leads to an increase of Sr mobility in soil when the calcium minerals are dissolved. Ions with negative charges like Cl and I will easily reach the aquifer if the soil does not contain organic matter.

The radioecological risk for saline agriculture and underground water in arid zones as a consequence of uncontrolled radioactive discharge from nuclear installation are newly considered. Contamination could occur directly by radioactive fallout or secondarily by contaminated water or wind. Serious attention to the potential contamination of the Sabkha Matti aquifer should be paid. The challenge is to choose proper methods for remediation which will not destroy unique environmental conditions but protect humans and our activities against the adverse effects of radioactive contamination. Moreover, the impact of occurring climate changes and their future predictions should be considered also in models of radioactive contamination and they need to be frequently updated.
