How family cohesion affects adolescents’ cyberbullying: The chain mediating role of social support and self-esteem
Authors: Zhiyong Xiao, Yanhong Shao, Shigong Chen, Wenhui Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Despite the recognition of the impact of family cohesion, social support, and self-esteem on cyberbullying, the precise pathways by which family cohesion affects cyberbullying – particularly through the mediating roles of social support and self-esteem – remain unclear. This study seeks to elucidate how family cohesion affects adolescents’ cyberbullying through the chain mediating roles of social support and self-esteem. In December 2024, an anonymous paper-based survey was successfully conducted on 944 middle school students (M = 15.27, SD = 1.619) in Jiangsu and Shandong Provinces. Structural Equation Modeling using AMOS 24.0 and SPSS 24.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk) was applied to analyze the pathways. The analysis revealed a significant direct negative association between family cohesion and adolescent cyberbullying. Furthermore, family cohesion exhibited an indirect negative effect on cyberbullying through 2 distinct pathways: via social support and via self-esteem. Additionally, a sequential mediation effect was observed, indicating that family cohesion reduced cyberbullying through the chain pathway of social support and self-esteem. Notably, the strongest indirect effect emerged in the path family cohesion →social support → cyberbullying. These findings elucidate the mechanisms linking family cohesion to reduced adolescent cyberbullying, offering valuable insights for developing targeted intervention and prevention strategies.

1. Introduction

The proliferation of digital communication platforms and social media has facilitated the emergence of cyberbullying as a prevalent social phenomenon.[1,2] Cyberbullying, also known as online harm, involves targeted and aggressive behavior perpetrated through electronic communication channels, such as emails, online comments, images, or video clips, directed against individuals who are unable to protect themselves.[3] Cyberbullying encompasses 2 primary dimensions: the cyberbullying victimization and the cyberbullying perpetration.[4] Cyberbullying victimization refers to individuals being subjected to malicious attacks, insults, harassment, and other harmful behaviors in online environments, while cyberbullying perpetration refers to individuals engaging in malicious attacks, insults, harassment, and other harmful behaviors against others in online environments.[4] The impact of cyberbullying on victims is profound, leading to increased levels of stress, anxiety, depression, and even suicidal ideation.[5] Furthermore, cyberbullying can have detrimental effects on adolescents’ academic performance, affecting their motivation to learn and adapt to the school environment.[6] Therefore, gaining a deeper understanding of the factors influencing cyberbullying for adolescents is vital for devising effective intervention strategies and offering the necessary support.

Family factors are crucial in both the occurrence and prevention of cyberbullying.[7,8] Family cohesion, a key aspect of family factors, denotes the emotional closeness, unity, and sense of belonging within a family.[9] Family cohesion, characterized by open communication, mutual support, shared values, and a strong sense of belonging among family members,[10] acts as a protective factor against cyberbullying. When teenagers feel a strong sense of connection with their families and experience a supportive home environment, they are more likely to develop healthy coping strategies, which can help them resist and cope with cyberbullying.[11]

In addition to family cohesion, social support is another important factor that affects cyberbullying.[12] Social support refers to the degree of emotional and material connections that individuals have with different aspects of their social environment, including family members, friends, colleagues, and partners, as well as social institutions like the family unit, workplace, political parties, and labor unions.[13] It has been demonstrated to be a vital factor in enabling individuals to resist and cope with cyberbullying incidents.[14] In the context of cyberbullying, those who enjoy greater social support tend to possess the confidence and skills to resist and cope with cyberbullying incidents.[15]

Moreover, self-esteem is an important internal factor that affects cyberbullying.[16] Self-esteem refers to an individual’s comprehensive, subjective, and emotional assessment of their own worth and value[17] and has been shown to influence how individuals respond to cyberbullying.[18] Adolescents who possess higher self-esteem typically exhibit greater resilience and are better prepared to handle cyberbullying incidents, given their stronger sense of self-worth and confidence.[19]

Although existing literature has identified family cohesion, social support, and self-esteem as relevant factors in cyberbullying, the precise mediating mechanisms through which these variables interact to influence cyberbullying outcomes remain insufficiently understood. To address this gap, the current study aims to comprehensively examine the interplay between family cohesion, social support, self-esteem, and cyberbullying. By integrating these key variables, the study aims to provide a holistic understanding of their collective influence on cyberbullying. Moreover, the study aims to explore the mediating roles of social support and self-esteem in the relationship between family cohesion and cyberbullying among adolescents. The significance of this study lies in its contribution to bridging the existing research gap and providing a deeper understanding of the factors that shape adolescents’ cyberbullying. By investigating the interactive effects and mediating roles of these factors, the study offers valuable insights into the complex dynamics that influence cyberbullying among adolescents.

The theoretical framework underlying the construction of a mediation model in this study is the Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), proposed by Bandura.[20] SCT primarily focuses on how individuals develop, maintain, and modify their behavior within their environment.[21] This theory consists of 3 core components: environment, self-influence factors, and behavior.[22] As noted by Bandura,[23] the environment refers to the social context in which individuals operate, encompassing factors such as family, school, and peer relationships. This component has a substantial influence on individual behavior, providing positive or negative feedback to the individual. Self-influence factors refer to the internal cognition, beliefs, attitudes, and emotions of individuals, which can influence an individual’s behavior choices in a specific environment. Behavior represents the individual’s performance in a specific environment. According to SCT, family cohesion enhances students perceived social support.[24] With enhanced perceived social support, students are more capable of feeling valued and accepted by others and develop a positive self-image and higher self-esteem,[25] ultimately leading to a negative impact on their online behavior. Based on SCT, this study hypothesizes that family cohesion (as an environmental factor) positively predicts adolescents’ perceived social support (as a self-influence factor), which in turn affects their self-esteem influence factors (as a self-influence factor), ultimately reducing engagement in cyberbullying behaviors.

2. Literature review

2.1. Family cohesion and cyberbullying

Family cohesion has been extensively studied with cyberbullying, and research consistently demonstrated its significant impact on adolescents’ involvement in cyberbullying.[11] Buelga et al found that family cohesion serves as a protective factor against cyberbullying among adolescents, highlighting its crucial role in mitigating the risk of cyberbullying perpetration and victimization.[26] Zhao conducted a questionnaire survey with a total of 452 adolescents and found that family cohesion is a protective factor of cyberbullying.[27] Similarly, Martínez-Monteagudo et al revealed that a deteriorated family environment increases the likelihood of adolescents being both victims and aggressors of cyberbullying, while a positive and supportive family environment decreases this probability.[28] Moreover, Vazsonyi et al emphasized the importance of parental closeness, noting that higher levels of paternal and maternal closeness were significantly associated with lower levels of cyberbullying perpetration.[29] This suggests that a strong sense of family cohesion and parental involvement can act as a protective factor against engaging in cyberbullying behaviors. Additionally, Buelga et al demonstrated that non-open communication with the mother and avoidant communication with the father predicted the role of cyber victims among adolescents aged 12 to 18,[30] further highlighting the intricate interplay between family communication patterns and cyberbullying experiences.

2.2. Social support as a mediator

Empirical evidence consistently demonstrates that family cohesion serves as a foundational predictor of perceived social support.[31,32] Cano et al highlighted that higher family cohesion was directly associated with higher social support.[33] In general, adolescents with high family cohesion are more likely to engage in enriching emotional communication with others.[34] Besides, the process of establishing intimate relationships with family members provides adolescents with valuable lessons on how to effectively navigate interpersonal problems.[35] This, in turn, enhances their interpersonal skills and equips them with better support in school and social environments.[36] Alshehri et al also highlighted that the improvement of interpersonal cohesion between parents and children is conducive to adolescents gaining more social support.[37]

Social support has been identified as a significant factor in influencing cyberbullying, and it also plays a positive role in addressing this issue.[38] According to Fridh et al,[39] social support from peers and family offers adolescents informational assistance, equipping them with knowledge and skills related to online safety and coping with cyberbullying. Chu et al further suggest that social support provides emotional assistance to students, making them feel understood, accepted, and cared for, which in turn helps reduce the likelihood of engaging in cyberbullying behaviors.[15] Moreover, Nagar and Talwar emphasize the importance of teacher support in providing tangible help and resources to address cyberbullying issues, thereby enhancing students’ self-protective capabilities and mitigating the harm caused by cyberbullying.[40] In a study conducted by Arató et al[41] involving students aged 11 to 19, self-report questionnaires revealed that support from both family and peers significantly influences individuals’ engagement in cyberbullying. Specifically, perceived social support from both familial and peer networks functions as a robust protective factor against cyberbullying victimization. Moreover, research demonstrates that the combination of strong peer support and balanced family cohesion significantly reduces the likelihood of cyberbullying perpetration. These findings collectively highlight the critical role of multidimensional social support systems in mitigating cyberbullying risk at both the victimization and perpetration levels.

2.3. Self-esteem as a mediator

Family cohesion has been consistently found to have a positive relationship with self-esteem. Several researchers have explored this association and provided their perspectives. According to Hossein Khanzadeh and Mirzaei,[42] family cohesion positively influences an individual’s self-esteem. Specifically, a cohesive family environment, characterized by belonging, acceptance, and support, contributes to higher levels of self-esteem.[43] Wang et al conducted a study and found that family cohesion, including father-child cohesion and mother-child cohesion, plays a significant role in shaping adolescents’ self-esteem.[44] Adolescents who perceive their families as cohesive tend to have higher self-esteem due to the emotional support and positive interactions they experience within the family unit. Similarly, Shi et al conducted a study involving 2400 adolescents aged 10 to 15 and demonstrated that family cohesion is associated with higher self-esteem in adolescents.[45] Olson et al further suggested that cohesive family environments facilitate self-esteem development through enhanced emotional reciprocity and the establishment of nurturing interpersonal dynamics.[46]

Moreover, self-esteem is also believed to be a key factor influencing students’ cyberbullying.[47] Some researchers have found that students with low self-esteem have a lower likelihood of experiencing cyberbullying.[48,49] For instance, Burns conducted a study with 653 European junior high school students and discovered a significant negative correlation between self-esteem and cyberbullying.[30] Similarly, Ding et al investigated Chinese university students and found a negative correlation between self-esteem and cyberbullying.[50] It has been suggested that self-esteem plays a role in developing positive beliefs about personal skills and abilities, enabling students to cope better with interpersonal challenges and address negative interactions assertively, thus reducing their vulnerability to cyberbullying.[51] Garaigordobil and Navarro found the mediating role of self-esteem in the relationship between the father’s coercion and being a cyberaggressor.[52] These perspectives indicate that family cohesion may impact adolescents’ engagement in cyberbullying indirectly through its influence on self-esteem.

In addition, social support has been established as a significant antecedent of self-esteem development.[53] Liu et al empirically validated the dual-function hypothesis of social support, demonstrating its significant predictive utility for self-esteem development while concurrently establishing its critical mediating role in adolescent mental health promotion.[54] Notably, their findings indicate that an increase in social support is linked to elevated levels of self-esteem among individuals.[55] Social support fosters a sense of belonging and connectedness, which further contributes to individuals’ overall well-being and self-esteem.[56] Existing research consistently identifies social support as a significant antecedent of self-esteem. 2.4. The present study

Based on the aforementioned the theoretical framework and literature review, we aim to develop an integrated model illustrating the interrelations between family cohesion, social support, self-esteem, cyberbullying among adolescents, and the mediating role of social support and self-esteem. Our hypotheses were presented in the following:

H1: Family cohesion will demonstrate a significant negative association with adolescent cyberbullying.

H2: Social support will mediate the relationship between family cohesion and adolescent cyberbullying.

H3: Self-esteem will mediate the relationship between family cohesion and adolescent cyberbullying.

H4: Social support and self-esteem will sequentially mediate the association between family cohesion and adolescent cyberbullying.

In summary, the proposed model delineates a dual-pathway mechanism wherein both social support and self-esteem independently mediate, and sequentially chain-mediate, the protective association between family cohesion and adolescent cyberbullying. 3. Materials and methods

3.1. Sampling and procedure

Before administering the paper-based survey, ethical approval and endorsement were secured from the Research Ethics Committee of Liaocheng University. Several steps were taken to safeguard the privacy and confidentiality of the students. Firstly, the study’s aims and goals were explained to the participants, and their consent was secured prior to collecting any data. Secondly, the survey was designed to collect anonymous data, with no identifying information provided. Lastly, the researchers maintained strict confidentiality regarding the data and results, ensuring that individual participants’ identities remained anonymous.

This study employed convenience sampling and cluster sampling methods to conduct questionnaire surveys in Shandong and Jiangsu provinces. Prior to the survey, the research team held discussions and randomly selected 2 middle schools (one urban and 1 rural) in each target city using a random number generation method. For each grade level at the selected schools, 2 classes were randomly chosen as participant groups. Before administration, researchers briefly explained the questionnaire‘s purpose and content to obtain informed consent from both the adolescent participants and their guardian.

The sample size for this study was established in accordance with the guidelines of Structural Equation Modeling, which suggest that the sample size should be at least ten times the number of observed variables.[57] Consistent with this recommendation, a total of 1050 adolescents were selected as participants for this study through random sampling from 4 schools in Jiangsu and Shandong Provinces, China, in December 2024. The 4 selected schools reflect diversity in student backgrounds, academic achievement, and socio-economic status, enhancing the representativeness of the sample.

Given the minimal amount of missing data, this study opted for a deletion method to address missing values, which encompassed both demographic variables (such as age, gender, and place of residence) and key research variables (such as family cohesion and cyberbullying). The specific criteria for deleting cases were as follows: questionnaires with more than 10% of items unanswered were considered invalid and excluded; questionnaires with obvious response biases (e.g., consecutive identical answers for more than 15 items) were excluded. A total of 106 cases were deleted through this process. After removing cases with missing values, the remaining sample size (N = 944) remained sufficient to meet the requirements for statistical analysis. The effective response rate of the questionnaire was 89.9%. As is detailed in the data in Supplementary File 1 (Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q50), the participants’ ages ranged from 13 to 18, with a mean age of 15.27 years (SD = 1.619). The sample consisted of 463 boys (49%) and 481 girls (51%). Specifically, the valid respondents were distributed across grades as follows: 155 students (16.4%) were in the 7th grade, 196 (20.7%) in the 8th grade, 198 (21.0%) in the 9th grade, 143 (15.1%) in the 10th grade, 137 (14.5%) in the 11th grade, and 115 (12.2%) in the 12th grade. Regarding place of residence, 512 (54.2%) lived in urban areas, while 432 (45.8%) lived in rural areas. The demographic characteristics of participants are presented in Table 1.

3.2. Questionnaire design

3.2.1. Family cohesion scale

The measurement of family cohesion was conducted using the Cohesion Subscale of the Family Intimacy and Adaptability Scale, adapted from Fei et al’s scale[58] which was modified according to Olson et al’s study.[59] This subscale comprises 16 items, such as “In times of difficulty, each member will make every effort to support 1 another.” A 5-point Likert scale was employed, with scores ranging from 1 to 5, indicating “hardly ever” to “almost always,” and higher average scores reflecting higher family cohesion. The scale has demonstrated good reliability and validity, as evidenced by a Cronbach α value of 0.947. In addition, the fit indices, including χ2/df = 1.273, IFI = 0.997, CFI = 0.997, TLI = 0.997, GFI = 0.988, AGFI = 0.982, and RMSEA = 0.017, NFI = 0.988, further support the robustness of the scale.

3.2.2. Social support scale

The measurement of social support was conducted using the Adolescent Social Support Scale developed by Ye and Dai,[60] which is based on Xiao[14] social support theoretical model. This scale consists of 17 items, categorized into 3 dimensions: subjective support (e.g., “Most of my classmates care about me”), objective support (e.g., “I often receive care and support from my family and friends”), and support utilization (e.g., “When faced with difficult choices, I actively seek help from others”), The 5-point Likert scale was used, with scores ranging from 1 to 5 indicating “disagree” to “agree,” with higher average scores indicating higher social support. The reliability and validity of the scale were good. The Cronbach α value of the scale in this study was 0.924, χ2/df = 1.602, IFI = 0.994, CFI = 0.994, TLI = 0.993, GFI = 0.984, AGFI = 0.977, and RMSEA = 0.025.

3.2.3. Self-esteem scale

The Self-Esteem Scale, developed by Rosenberg,[61] was used to assess the self-esteem levels of adolescents. This scale consists of 10 items and is unidimensional (e.g., “I feel that I have a number of good qualities”). The items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale, with average scores ranging from 1 to 4, corresponding to “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.” Five of the items were reverse-scored to ensure accurate measurement, and higher average scores indicate a stronger sense of self-esteem. The scale demonstrated good reliability and validity, with a Cronbach α value of 0.917. Additionally, the fit indices, including χ2/df = 2.051, IFI = 0.998, CFI = 0.998, TLI = 0.992, GFI = 0.996, AGFI = 0.976, and RMSEA = 0.033, further supported the scale’s robustness.

3.2.4. Cyberbullying scale

The assessment of cyberbullying among adolescents was conducted using the revised Chinese version of the Cyberbullying Scale developed by You.[62] This scale consists of 2 subscales: the Cyberbullying Victimization Scale, which contains 12 items, and the Cyberbullying Perpetration Scale, which includes 8 items. Each subscale includes 3 dimensions: Cyber Verbal Bullying (posting inappropriate comments about friends online when angry or after an argument), Anonymous Identity, and Cyber Impersonation Bullying. The scale employs a 4-point rating scale, ranging from 1 (Never happened) to 4 (Frequently happened). Higher average scores suggest a greater frequency of cyberbullying incidents. This scale has been well-validated among Chinese adolescents.[63] The scale used in the study exhibited strong reliability, with a Cronbach α value of 0.949. Additionally, the fit indices, including χ2/df = 2.038, IFI = 0.987, CFI = 0.987, TLI = 0.986, GFI = 0.975, AGFI = 0.968, and RMSEA = 0.033, further support the robustness of the scale. This study operationalizes cyberbullying as a unidimensional construct (calculated as the mean score of all items across 2 subscales), primarily examining the overall involvement in core variables rather than specific role mechanisms. This approach mitigates potential multicollinearity issues associated with single-dimension analyses while enhancing model parsimony and stability. Methodologically, parallel regression was employed to address measurement errors.

3.3. Statistical analysis

The analysis of the collected data was carried out using SPSS 24.0 and AMOS 24.0. To start, the Harman single-factor test was utilized to examine common method variance (CMV). Next, descriptive statistics, reliability analysis, and Pearson correlation tests were applied to the variables of family cohesion, social support, self-esteem, and cyberbullying. In the final step, the chain mediation analysis was conducted in AMOS 24.0 using the maximum likelihood estimation technique. Bootstrap analysis was performed with 1000 samples, and 95% confidence intervals were calculated to assess the significance of indirect effects.

4. Results

4.1. Common method variance

To mitigate the potential impact of CMV stemming from the use of a single questionnaire, we applied Harman single-factor test.[64] CMV can inflate or deflate the relationships between variables, particularly in self-reported studies, leading to biased results. To assess this, we performed an exploratory factor analysis on all items from the 4 scales using an unrotated principal component analysis method. The results indicated that 10 factors had eigenvalues >1, with the first factor accounting for 28.499% of the total variance. This percentage is below the critical threshold of 40%,[65] suggesting that CMV is not a significant issue in this study. Therefore, we conclude that the observed relationships among the variables are unlikely to be substantially influenced by common method bias.

5. Discussion

Research findings indicate that family cohesion, social support, and self-esteem positively influence cyberbullying. Nevertheless, the precise pathways through which family cohesion affects cyberbullying via social support and self-esteem remain unclear. This study sought to develop a mediation model to explore whether family cohesion is indirectly linked to adolescent cyberbullying through social support and self-esteem. The findings, implications, and limitations are outlined below.

5.1. Findings

The study’s findings revealed that family cohesion is directly and negatively associated with adolescent cyberbullying. This not only corroborates Zhao[27] finding of a negative correlation between family cohesion and adolescent cyberbullying but also aligns with Martínez-Monteagudo et al’ s notion that cyberbullying is influenced by the family environment, with family cohesion being a key factor.[28] One possible reason is that the presence of strong family cohesion provides adolescents with alternative outlets for emotional expression and problem-solving. Adolescents are more likely to seek advice and support from their family members when faced with conflicts or challenges, rather than resorting to negative online behaviors.[71] This study further demonstrates that family cohesion is a predictive factor of cyberbullying.

The study’s results pinpointed social support as a significant partial mediator between family cohesion and adolescent cyberbullying. This aligns with prior research indicating a positive link between family cohesion and social support,[31,32] as well as a negative relationship between social support and cyberbullying.[15,41] These findings support the notion that family cohesion plays a crucial role in shaping the perception of social support, which, in turn, affects adolescents’ engagement in cyberbullying behaviors. The finding provides more evidence of the role of social support in the relationship between family cohesion and cyberbullying.

The study’s findings revealed that self-esteem significantly partially mediates the relationship between family cohesion and adolescents’ cyberbullying behavior. This aligns with the perspective of Garaigordobil and Navarro, who highlight the critical role of self-esteem in linking family cohesion to cyberbullying.[52] This phenomenon might be explained by the fact that good family cohesion implies close emotional bonds and support within the family, which in turn can establish stable emotional attachment for adolescents. Additionally, parents, to a certain extent, are able to provide effective guidance and support to adolescents, assisting them in developing sufficient self-esteem. As a result, adolescents experience greater confidence and self-respect, which plays a certain role in preventing cyberbullying among them.

The study’s results further indicated that both social support and self-esteem act as a chain mediating mechanism between family cohesion and adolescent cyberbullying. In essence, adolescents with higher family cohesion are more likely to experience greater social support, which enhances their overall subjective emotional evaluation of their own worth and value. This, in turn, is linked to reduced impulsivity, aggression, and a greater consideration of future consequences, thereby diminishing the likelihood of engaging in cyberbullying behaviors. This finding provides deeper insights into how environmental systems and individual factors interact to influence adolescent cyberbullying, building on previous research by clarifying how family cohesion can affect cyberbullying behaviors. It is important to note that while both the serial mediation and self-esteem as a mediator were established, their effect sizes were relatively small (0.232 and 0.191, respectively), compared to the mediating effect of social support. This suggests that social support has a more substantial impact on cyberbullying behaviors. Therefore, when designing interventions to address adolescent cyberbullying at the family level, enhancing their perception of social support should be prioritized over boosting their self-esteem.

5.2. Implications

The results of this study hold important implications for both theoretical development and practical applications in the realm of adolescent cyberbullying. From a theoretical standpoint, this research enriches the current body of literature by elucidating the chain mediating role of perceived social support and self-esteem in the relationship between family cohesion and cyberbullying. It deepens our comprehension of the intricate dynamics at play in this association and underscores the necessity of accounting for multiple factors when examining cyberbullying behaviors in adolescents. This study highlights the pivotal role of social support and self-esteem as mediating variables, thereby identifying potential targets for intervention and prevention strategies.

From a practical perspective, this study provides crucial guidance for developing effective interventions aimed at preventing and addressing cyberbullying in adolescents. Recent studies have also highlighted the significance of balanced digital engagement for adolescent development.[71] Interventions should target not only enhancing family cohesion but also fostering social support and self-esteem. To strengthen family cohesion, it is essential to promote open and honest communication among family members, encouraging them to share their thoughts, feelings, and concerns. Creating a safe and supportive environment where family members actively participate in decision-making processes and have a say in shaping family rules and expectations can also contribute to stronger family cohesion.[72] In terms of social support, adolescents should be encouraged to develop and maintain positive relationships with peers who provide emotional support, encouragement, and understanding.[73] Additionally, fostering a supportive and nurturing family environment where adolescents feel comfortable seeking support from family members is crucial.[74] Building a strong and positive teacher-student relationship is also essential. Teachers should focus on establishing trust, respect, and open communication with their students. Teachers should also create opportunities for success and growth by offering activities and tasks that match students’ abilities and providing support as needed. To foster self-esteem, it is important to teach adolescents that their abilities and intelligence can be developed through effort and perseverance. Encouraging a growth mindset and emphasizing progress, effort, and strengths rather than solely focusing on outcomes or failures can help boost self-esteem. Providing specific and constructive feedback that highlights their efforts and progress can also contribute to building self-esteem.[75]

5.3. Limitations

Several limitations need to be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional nature of this study prevents us from drawing definitive causal conclusions about the relationships among the variables. Future research should consider using longitudinal or experimental approaches to validate the causal relationships. Second, the data in this study relied exclusively on self-reports from secondary school students, which could be influenced by biases such as social desirability. Future research should strive to collect data from diverse sources, such as parents or peers, to corroborate the findings. Thirdly, the model proposed in this study was examined in a sample of 4 secondary schools in eastern China, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future studies should focus on different types of samples to increase the external validity of the research.

6. Conclusion

The conclusions of this study can be summarized as follows: family cohesion is a direct and negative predictor of adolescent cyberbullying; social support acts as a mediator between family cohesion and adolescent cyberbullying; self-esteem also serves as a mediator in this relationship; social support and self-esteem together form a sequential mediation pathway between family cohesion and adolescent cyberbullying. This study reveals the relationship and underlying mechanisms between family cohesion and adolescent cyberbullying, providing essential guidance for the development of targeted intervention and prevention measures.
