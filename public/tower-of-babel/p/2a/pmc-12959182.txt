Structures of clinical antibodies bound to IL-33 uncover two distinct epitopes underlying differential efficacy
Authors: Jing Chen, Yue Wang, Xinquan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInterleukin-33 (IL-33), an alarmin cytokine of the IL-1 family, drives type 2 inflammation through signaling via the ST2 and IL-1RAcP receptors, making it a critical therapeutic target for inflammatory diseases such as asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Current therapeutic strategies have primarily focused on antibodies that target IL-33 or ST2 to disrupt their specific interaction. However, the structural mechanisms underlying antibody-mediated neutralization of IL-33 remain poorly understood. Here, we report the structures of three antibodies in clinical trial – etokimab, itepekimab, and tozorakimab – complexed with IL-33, determined by X-ray crystallography and cryo-electron microscopy. Structural analysis reveals two distinct neutralizing epitopes on IL-33, termed Epitope 1 at IL-33/ST2 binding Site 1 and Epitope 2 at IL-33/ST2 binding Site 2. Tozorakimab, which targets Epitope 1, completely blocks ST2 engagement by sterically occluding the ST2 D1-D2 domain-binding interface. In contrast, etokimab and itepekimab, which recognize Epitope 2, interfere with IL-33 recognition of the ST2 D3 domain and thereby only partially inhibit ST2 binding. These structural and biochemical findings provide a molecular explanation for the differential efficacy of the three antibodies in inhibiting IL-33 signaling in cellular assays. Collectively, our results provide valuable insights into the molecular determinants of efficacy for existing IL-33 therapeutics and offer a structural framework for the rational design of next-generation IL-33 targeted inhibitors.

Introduction

Interleukin-33 (IL-33) was first identified in 2005 as a member of the IL-1 cytokine family.1 Like other IL-1 family members (excluding IL-18), IL-33 signals through the shared co-receptor IL-1RAcP and exhibits high specificity for its primary receptor, ST2.2–4 The full-length IL-33 protein consists of 270 amino acids and has a molecular weight of approximately 30 kDa.5 Upon cleavage by inflammatory proteases, IL-33 is processed into an ~18 kDa mature form, which exhibits significantly enhanced biological activity.1,6 Functionally, IL-33 operates through two principal mechanisms. First, as a nuclear factor, full-length IL-33 localizes to the nucleus by associating with chromatin, where it modulates gene expression through chromatin remodeling and epigenetic regulation, thereby suppressing inflammatory responses.7 Second, as an extracellular cytokine, mature IL-33 is released in response to cellular damage or infection,8,9 acting as an alarmin by engaging the ST2 receptor and initiating inflammatory cascades.2,8,10–12

As a key alarmin in innate immunity, mature IL-33 is rapidly released in response to cell stress or necrosis.8,13 Upon release, it binds to the membrane-bound receptor ST2, thereby activating immune cells and sustaining exaggerated inflammatory responses that contribute to both localized and systemic inflammatory pathologies.10 Elevated levels of IL-33 and its receptor, ST2, have been documented in various disease contexts, including skin lesions of atopic dermatitis (AD) patients and corresponding murine models,14 intestinal mucosa and serum of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) patients,15 and serum and bronchial biopsies from individuals with asthma. In asthma, IL-33 expression correlates with disease severity, and its upregulation is recognized as a pivotal driver of airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR) and disease pathogenesis.16–19 Beyond these diseases, the IL-33/ST2 signaling pathway has also been implicated in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular diseases, neurological disorders, and gout.20–25

Since the identification of IL-33 as a member of the IL-1 cytokine family, the IL-33/ST2 signaling axis has rapidly emerged as a promising therapeutic target, with several monoclonal antibodies advancing into clinical development. Among these, three representative candidates targeting IL-33 highlight the current landscape of research and development. Etokimab (ANB020), an IgG1 monoclonal antibody developed by AnaptysBio, was evaluated in Phase 2 trials in immune-mediated disorders, including AD;26 however, its clinical development was terminated. Itepekimab (REGN3500), an IgG4 antibody generated using Regeneron’s VelocImmune® platform in collaboration with Sanofi, is currently undergoing Phase 3 trials for chronic rhinosinusitis and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).27,28 Tozorakimab (MEDI3506), a humanized IgG1 antibody identified from a native phage display library by AstraZeneca, has advanced to Phase 3 clinical studies across multiple indications, including asthma, COPD, and respiratory infections.29–32 Although these antibodies advanced to clinical trials, the structural mechanisms by which therapeutic antibodies neutralize IL-33 remains unclear. Specifically, it is unknown how antibodies recognizing different regions of IL-33 engage the receptor-binding surface and how these epitope differences translate into distinct modes of receptor interference.

In this study, we sought to address these questions by determining the structures of IL-33 in complex with three clinically relevant antibodies – etokimab, itepekimab, and tozorakimab – using X-ray crystallography and cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM). Our analysis identified two structurally distinct neutralizing epitopes on IL-33: Epitope 1 at IL-33/ST2 binding Site 1 and Epitope 2 at IL-33/ST2 binding Site 2. Tozorakimab, which targets Epitope 1, completely blocks ST2 engagement by sterically occluding the ST2 D1-D2 domain-binding interface. In contrast, etokimab and itepekimab, which recognize Epitope 2, interfere with IL-33 recognition of the ST2 D3 domain and thereby only partially inhibit ST2 binding. These structural and biochemical findings provide a molecular explanation for the differential efficacy of the three antibodies in inhibiting IL-33 signaling in cellular assays.

Results

IL-33 neutralization profiles of etokimab, itepekimab, and tozorakimab in cellular assays

Etokimab, itepekimab, and tozorakimab are fully human or humanized antibodies that share a conserved framework region. Analysis of their complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) reveals that HCDR1 is relatively conserved, including conserved positions G26, T28, and F29. Additionally, itepekimab and tozorakimab share common residues at F27, S30, and A33 (Figure 1A). In contrast, the remaining five CDR loops exhibit considerable sequence diversity. Germline analysis indicates distinct genetic origins: IGHV1-46, IGHV3-23, and IGHV3-23 were used for the heavy chain, and IGKV1-9, IGKV1-12, and IGLV3-1 were used for the light chain of etokimab, itepekimab and tozorakimab, respectively. While both itepekimab and tozorakimab utilize IGHV3-23, Itepekimab shows a higher somatic hypermutation burden (88.8% VH identity) compared to Tozorakimab (93.9% VH identity). In addition, etokimab and itepekimab each contain a 15-residue heavy chain CDR3, whereas tozorakimab features an extended 19-residue CDR3 (Table S1). We expressed the mature C-terminal domain of IL-33 (residues 112–270) in a prokaryotic system and produced full-length antibodies in HEK293F cells. Biochemical characterization using surface plasmon resonance (SPR) showed that all three antibodies bind IL-33 with fast-on, slow-off kinetics and exhibit low nanomolar affinities (0.127 nM for etokimab, 0.0812 nM for itepekimab, and 0.810 nM for tozorakimab; Figure 1B). These affinities meet or exceed that of the IL-33/ST2 interaction (0.551 nM). Functional inhibition was firstly assessed using HEK-Blue IL-33 reporter cells, which stably express the human IL1RL1 (ST2) gene, enabling the monitoring of NF-κB and AP-1 pathway activation. All three antibodies effectively suppressed IL-33–induced signaling, and they exhibited similar IC50 values across different IL-33 concentrations (with 2 ng/mL IL-33, IC50 = 0.029–0.061 nM; with 30 ng/mL IL-33, IC50 = 0.657–1.158 nM). One notable difference in the inhibitory capability is at the IC90 level. At lower IL-33 concentrations, tozorakimab (IC90 = 0.075 nM) was 3.4- and 15.9-fold more potent than etokimab (IC90 = 0.26 nM) and itepekimab (IC90 = 1.189 nM), respectively. This trend was maintained at higher IL-33 concentrations, where tozorakimab (IC90 = 1.534 nM) outperformed etokimab (IC90 = 3.498 nM) and itepekimab (IC90 = 9.131 nM) by 2.3- and 6.0-fold, respectively (Figure 1C, Figure S1). In addition, we evaluated the inhibitory effects of the three antibodies on IL-33–induced signaling using primary human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). Consistent with the HEK-Blue reporter assay, Tozorakimab exhibited a more pronounced inhibitory trend in this primary cell system (Figure 1D, Figure S2), Tozorakimab (IC50 = 0.079 nM) was 13.5- and 15.3-fold more potent than etokimab (IC50 = 1.07 nM) and itepekimab (IC50 = 1.21 nM), respectively.

Structural determination of IL-33–antibody complexes

To explore the molecular mechanisms underlying IL-33 neutralization by the three antibodies, we first determined the crystal structure of the tozorakimab Fab – IL-33 binary complex at a resolution of 3.51 Å (Figure 2A, Table S2; Protein Data Bank (PDB) code 9WWH). Although co-crystallization of IL-33 with etokimab or itepekimab alone was unsuccessful, we used tozorakimab as a helper Fab to facilitate structural determination of the other two complexes. SPR competition assays indicated that tozorakimab recognizes an epitope distinct from those targeted by etokimab and itepekimab, while the latter two share overlapping binding sites (Figure S3A). Formation of ternary complexes, IL-33/tozorakimab/etokimab and IL-33/tozorakimab/itepekimab, was confirmed by size-exclusion chromatography (Figure S3B). Using cryo-EM, we resolved the tozorakimab/etokimab/IL-33 ternary complex at 2.57 Å (Figure 2A, Figure S4, Table S3; PDB code 9X0J) and the tozorakimab/itepekimab/IL-33 complex at 3.64 Å (Figure 2A, Figure S5, Table S3; PDB code 9X05). IL-33 adopts a canonical β-trefoil fold comprising a central core of 12 β-strands surrounded by solvent-exposed loops involved in receptor recognition.33,34 A segment of the β4–β5 loop (residues 168–178) exhibited high flexibility and was poorly resolved across our structures, displaying discontinuous density; consequently, this region was excluded from epitope analysis. Structural analysis revealed that both etokimab and itepekimab bind around β4-β5 and β8 strands of IL-33, with their epitopes partially encompassing the dynamic β4–β5 loop. In contrast, tozorakimab engages the N-terminal domain around β2-β3 and β6 strand region. Structural alignment confirmed that etokimab and itepekimab adopt similar binding orientations and exhibit extensive steric overlap, whereas Tozorakimab binds to a distal, non-overlapping epitope which was consistent with our SPR competition data (Figure S3A).

We previously reported the crystal structure of human IL-33–ST2 ectodomain complex (PDB: 4KC3). The IL-33/ST2 interface comprises two spatially distinct binding sites: Site 1 involves extensive hydrophilic interactions between IL-33 and the first two Ig-like domains (D1 and D2) of ST2, whereas Site 2 is mainly formed by hydrophobic contacts between IL-33 and the terminal Ig-like domain (D3) of ST2 34 (Figure 2B). Superposition of the antibody complexes with the IL-33–ST2 structure reveals that all three Fabs would sterically hinder ST2 binding: etokimab and itepekimab clash with the D3 domain of ST2 at Site 2, whereas tozorakimab clashes with the D1 domain at Site 1 (Figure 2C). These results indicate that all three antibodies would inhibit IL‑33 signaling by directly competing with ST2 binding.

Etokimab and itepekimab recognize overlapping epitopes (Epitope 2) on IL-33 at Site 2

The etokimab – IL33 interface buries a total surface area of 939 Å2, with contributions of 680 Å2 from the heavy chain and 259 Å2 from the light chain, involving 16 residues on IL33. The paratope comprises 15 residues, 11 of which originate from the heavy chain including Y32 (CDR1); Y52, R54, and N55 (CDR2); and Y101, Y102, Y103, L104, T105, and F111 (CDR3), which highlights the key role of CDR2 and CDR3 in antigen recognition. The light chain contributes four residues: T31 (CDR1), W50 (CDR2), and Y94 (CDR3) (Figure 3A). Etokimab forms several important hydrogen bonds with IL33, which from the heavy chain: Y32 with D178; Y101 with E165; Y102 with N171 and K180; and Y103, T105 with N222. And from the light chain: Y49 with E165 and Y94 with S225 (Figure 3C, Figure S6). Tyrosine residues (Y32, Y101, Y102, Y103 in the heavy chain; Y49, Y94 in the light chain) play a central role in polar interactions, thereby stabilizing the etokimab – IL33 complex. The itepekimab – IL33 interface buries a total surface area of 909 Å2 (537 Å2 from heavy chain, 372 Å2 from light chain) and engages 20 epitope residues. Its paratope consists of 16 residues: 10 from the heavy chain including T28 and R31 (CDR1), R57 (CDR2), and Y101, T102, T103, S104, W105, Y106 (CDR3), with CDR3 serving as the primary interaction site; and six from the light chain including F30, S31, W32 (CDR1), A50 (CDR2), and S93 (CDR3), which is dominated by CDR1 residues (Figure 3B). Itepekimab forms a central interaction mediated by R57, T103, S104, W105, and Y106 of heavy chain with N222 of IL33, and the light chain contributes hydrogen bonds including W32 with Y122 and Y49 with E165 (Figure 3D, Figure S6).

Structural superposition suggests that etokimab and itepekimab sterically block ST2 engagement by targeting Epitope 2, a region that overlaps with IL-33 residues at Site 2 involved in binding to the D3 domain of ST2. Etokimab shares six overlapping epitope residues with ST2 (I119, E165, K180, L182, N222, N226; 288 Å2), whereas itepekimab exhibits more extensive overlap, sharing 11 residues (I119, Y122, A124, E165, K180, M181, L182, N222, N226, L267, S268; 386 Å2) (Figure 3E). The larger shared interface suggests that Itepekimab more closely mimics the ST2 binding mode. Etokimab and itepekimab share 11 common epitope residues on IL33 (I119, T120, E165, D178, G179, K180, L182, L220, H221, N222, N226). Both antibodies employ conserved tyrosine residues – heavy chain Y101 contacting K180 and light chain Y49 contacting E165—while different residues at positions 103–105 interact with G179, L220, and N222 (Table S4). Thus, despite sequence divergence, the recurrent use of tyrosine and conserved interactions mediated by heavy chain CDR3 account for the recognition of highly similar antigenic epitopes by etokimab and itepekimab.

Tozorakimab recognizes epitope 1 on IL-33 at Site 1

Tozorakimab recognizes Epitope 1 on IL‑33 comprising 24 residues and burying a total surface area of 1149 Å2 (768 Å2 from heavy chain, 381 Å2 from light chain). The heavy chain paratope involves 18 residues, primarily from CDR1 (F27, T28, S30, S31, Y32) and CDR3 (R98, K100, F101, Q103, L104, W105, R110, Y111, Y115). The light chain contributes nine residues, mainly from CDR1 (G28, D29, K30, Y31) and CDR2 (D50, T52) (Figure 4A). Tozorakimab sterically occludes ST2 D1-D2 domains binding at Epitope 1 by clashing with six key IL-33 residues: T135, E139, Y146, E148, K151, and K152 (Figure 4B). The antibody – antigen interface is stabilized by an extensive interaction network. The heavy chain contributes hydrogen bonds and salt bridges, with the CDR3 loop playing a central role. Notable interactions include R98 and K100 forming dual hydrogen bonds and salt bridges with E139; Q103 engaging T189 and K190; W105 contacting K158, S187, and P188; and Y111 interacting with F136 and D157. The light chain contributes K30 binding to K151 and D153, and D50 establishing both a hydrogen bond and a salt bridge with K151 (Figure 4C, Figure S7). Electrostatic potential analysis reveals a complementary interface: a negatively charged region on IL‑33 centered at E139 is matched by a positively charged surface on the antibody focused around K100 (Figure 4D). These structural features suggest that the heavy chain mediates high-affinity IL-33 binding through an extensive network of polar and electrostatic interactions, while the light chain enforces steric blockade of ST2 engagement.

We systematically introduced two-amino-acid mutations into each CDR of tozorakimab’s heavy and light chains, generating four mutant heavy chains (H1–H4) and four mutant light chains (L1–L4) (Table S5). These were paired with their wild-type counterparts to produce eight antibody variants. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)-based binding assessment revealed that the heavy-chain mutants (H1–H4) exhibited severely impaired binding (Figure 5A), and consistently cell-based inhibition assays showed that H1–H4 variants completely lost neutralization activity (Figure 5B). The light-chain mutants (L1–L4) retained IL-33 binding affinity comparable to that of the wild-type antibody (EC50 = 1.327–8.314 ng/mL vs. 1.398 ng/mL), showing only a 0.95- to 5.95-fold reduction in binding. In contrast, their inhibitory function was substantially compromised, with IC50 values elevated by 56.8- to 351.3-fold (Figure 5B). Notably, the L3 mutant, which showed no significant defect in IL-33 binding, exhibited a greater than 50-fold reduction in signaling inhibition. These results further support that the tozorakimab heavy chain is critical for high-affinity IL-33 binding, whereas the light chain is important for steric blockade of ST2 engagement. ST2 competition by etokimab, itepekimab, and tozorakimab in SPR assay

Previous studies have shown that IL-33 hot-spot residues for ST2 binding are primarily located at Site 1, indicating that Site 1 is more critical than Site 2 for ST2 engagement.34 Given that etokimab and itepekimab recognize IL-33 at Site 2, whereas tozorakimab targets Site 1, we further investigated whether these three antibodies differ in their ability to interfere with ST2 engagement. We performed SPR-based competition assays to assess the binding of ST2 to IL-33 in the presence of each antibody. In these experiments, IL-33 was immobilized on a CM5 sensor chip, and antibody binding was allowed to reach saturation prior to injection of ST2. When etokimab or itepekimab was pre-bound to IL-33, subsequent injection of ST2 resulted in a clear increase in response units, indicating that ST2 was still able to associate with IL-33. In contrast, when tozorakimab was pre-bound to IL-33, no additional increase in response signal was observed upon ST2 injection, suggesting effective blockade of ST2 binding (Figure 6). These results indicate that antibodies recognizing distinct epitopes on IL-33 differ in their capacity to hinder ST2 engagement. This observation is consistent with the differentiated inhibitory profiles observed in cellular assays and provides functional support for the structural distinction between the epitopes targeted by tozorakimab and by etokimab and itepekimab. Discussion

Our structural determination of three antibody – IL-33 complexes establishes the first comprehensive epitope mapping of this cytokine. Although all three antibodies neutralize IL-33 through direct competition with receptor binding, they do so via two fundamentally distinct molecular mechanisms. Etokimab and itepekimab recognize Epitope 2 at IL-33/ST2 binding Site 2, whereas tozorakimab exclusively targets Epitope 1 at Site 1. Despite pronounced sequence divergence, etokimab and itepekimab exhibit remarkable structural convergence in their binding modes, driven by substantial epitope overlap mediated largely by conserved tyrosine residues (Y101 in the heavy chain and Y49 in the light chain) that form critical interactions with IL-33 residues E165 and K180. In contrast, tozorakimab engages a non-overlapping epitope and forms an extensive antibody – antigen interface, characterized by a buried surface area of 1149 Å2 and a dense network of hydrogen bonds, salt bridges, and electrostatic contacts. In addition, tozorakimab displays a contiguous positively charged surface (K100, K30, and R49) that complements a prominent electronegative region on IL-33 centered on E139.

Integrating our structural findings, we propose that distinct IL-33 epitopes contribute differentially to receptor interference, with Epitope 1 displaying features consistent with a more effective mode of ST2 blockade. Site 1 forms an extensive interaction surface with ST2, engages multiple regions within the ST2 D1/D2 domains, and contributes substantially to the overall binding interface, supporting a central role in receptor recognition. In addition, Site 1 presents a pronounced electronegative surface that complements a positively charged region on ST2 (Figure S8), representing a functional hotspot for receptor blockage by antibodies. Tozorakimab exploits this intrinsic electrostatic feature through complementary charge interactions, forming an interaction network that overlaps with the ST2 binding interface and thereby competes with receptor association.

Our mutagenesis experiments with tozorakimab showed light-chain mutations, while largely preserving binding, significantly impair functional potency. We propose that these light-chain mutations induce conformational rearrangements at the antibody-IL-33 interface that disrupt the steric blockade of ST2 binding. These results confirm that the heavy chain is indispensable for epitope recognition, whereas the light chain is critically involved in competing with ST2 binding, highlighting the importance of direct steric competition with ST2 for effective neutralization. This suggests a potential direction for antibody optimization: engineering light chains with enhanced affinity and optimized interface complementarity to strengthen steric hindrance, or developing high-affinity single-domain antibodies that directly target the ST2 binding interface.

A recent report characterized A12, a fully human single-domain antibody that binds the Site 2 epitope of IL-33, corresponding to the Epitope 2 region shared by etokimab and itepekimab.35 Competition and mutagenesis analyses suggested that A12 disrupts IL-33/ST2 signaling by partially occluding receptor engagement and destabilizing the IL-33/ST2 complex. In the same study, itepekimab was designated a non-competing control antibody with respect to ST2 binding. In this study, our SPR-based competition experiments also showed that pre-association of etokimab or itepekimab with IL-33 does not completely abolish subsequent ST2 binding. Structural insights from our work provide a mechanistic rationale for this phenomenon, indicating that antibodies directed against the Site 2 epitope can allow residual receptor engagement, whereas Site 1-targeting antibodies directly occlude essential regions of the ST2 interface. These findings highlight the limitations of competition assays in the absence of structural context and emphasize the value of high-resolution structural information for accurate interpretation of functional outcomes. Together, our results support targeting Site 1 as a mechanistically distinct and potentially more efficacious strategy for inhibition of the IL-33 signaling pathway.

Astegolimab (RO7187807), a fully human anti-ST2 antibody developed by Genentech/Roche, represents a leading therapeutic candidate targeting the IL-33 receptor.36 Patent descriptions indicate that astegolimab binds the ST2 D1/D2 domains, sterically hindering IL-33 binding, emphasizing the importance of direct, competitive epitope targeting for effective pathway inhibition. Innovatively, the bispecific antibody AK139 targets both IL-4 Rα and ST2. This strategy simultaneously blocks the IL-4/IL-13 and IL-33/ST2 pathways, potentially yielding synergistic efficacy, particularly in complex inflammatory diseases. Given the complexity of conditions like COPD and asthma, exploring combination therapies or bispecific approaches might substantially enhance therapeutic prospects. This is especially relevant for antibodies like etokimab and itepekimab, which show high affinity, but suboptimal clinical performance as monotherapies. A recent report indicated that combining itepekimab (anti – IL-33) with dupilumab (anti – IL-4 Rα, Dupixent®) may confer synergistic benefit in late-stage severe disease characterized by mixed inflammation and tissue remodeling.37 Furthermore, the success of targeting related IL-1 family receptors, such as the IL-1 R antagonist anakinra (Kineret®) 38 and the IL-36 R inhibitor spesolimab (Spevigo®),39 highlights the substantial potential of targeting ST2. Beyond monoclonal antibodies, future therapeutic modalities may include receptor antagonists and other innovative strategies, opening a diverse landscape for drug development.

Materials and methods

Protein expression and purification

The IL-33 (112–270) construct containing a C259S mutation to minimize dimerization was cloned into the pGEX-6P-1 vector with an N-terminal GST tag. The protein was expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3) cells induced with 0.5 mM IPTG at OD600=0.6–0.8, followed by incubation at 16°C for 16–18 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4000 × g for 15 min and resuspended in HBS buffer (10 mM HEPES, pH 7.2, 150 mM NaCl) supplemented with 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride. After lysis, the supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 2 h and applied to a Glutathione Sepharose 4B column. Following extensive washing, on-column cleavage was performed using 3C protease at 4°C for 16 h under light-protected conditions. The eluate was further purified by size-exclusion chromatography using a Superdex 200 Increase column to obtain high-purity IL-33 protein.

Human ST2 (residues 1–328) with a C-terminal 6×His tag was cloned into the pCAG vector and expressed in HEK 293F cells. Transfection was performed at a cell density of 2 × 106 cells/mL using 1 mg plasmid DNA and 4 mg polyethylenimine (PEI) per liter of culture, followed by 25 min incubation at room temperature. The supernatant was harvested 48 h post-transfection by centrifugation at 4000 × g for 20 min and purified using Ni-NTA resin (GE Healthcare), followed by gel-filtration chromatography on a Superdex 200 column (GE Healthcare) equilibrated with HBS buffer (10 mM HEPES, pH 7.2, 150 mM NaCl).

The full-length sequences of etokimab, itepekimab, and tozorakimab were obtained from Synapse. Heavy and light chains were separately cloned into the pcDNA3.4 vector, each containing a Kozak sequence (gccaccatgg) and a secretion signal peptide (MEFGLSWVFLVAIIKGVQC) at the N-terminus. Transfection of 293F cells at 2 × 106 cells/mL was carried out using 1 mg heavy chain plasmid, 1 mg light chain plasmid, and 4 mg PEI per liter of culture, with 25 min incubation at room temperature. After 6 d of expression, the supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 4000 × g for 20 min. Antibodies were purified using Protein A magnetic beads (GenScript), eluted with 0.1 M glycine (pH 2.7), and buffer-exchanged into phosphate-buffered saline (PBS).

Surface plasmon resonance binding and competition assays

The binding affinities of itepekimab, etokimab, and tozorakimab for IL-33 were determined by SPR using a Biacore 8K+ instrument (Cytiva). Antibodies were immobilized on a CM5 sensor chip in 10 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 4.0) at a concentration of 50 µg/mL to achieve a target density of ~ 5000 response units (RU). Soluble ST2 (sST2) was immobilized as a control under similar conditions (pH 5.5, 10 µg/mL). All experiments were conducted in PBS containing 0.005% Tween-20 at a constant flow rate of 30 µL/min. Serial dilutions of IL-33 (starting from 11.11 nM) were injected over the sensor surface for a 180 s association and a 300 s dissociation.

For epitope competition analysis, the IL-33 was immobilized on a CM5 sensor chip using sodium acetate buffer (pH 4.0) to a level of ~300 RU. All competition assays were performed using the same running buffer as described for affinity measurements. In each cycle, the first protein was injected for 150 s to saturate the antigen. This was followed by an 150 s or 80 s co-injection of protein 1 together with the protein 2. Binding was evaluated qualitatively: a marked increase in RU during the co-injection phase indicated binding to non-overlapping epitopes, while a negligible change in RU signified competition for the same or sterically overlapping epitopes. Data were acquired in multi-cycle kinetics mode and processed using Biacore Insight Evaluation Software.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

High-binding 96-well plates were coated with 100 µL per well of recombinant IL-33 diluted to 2 µg/mL in PBS and incubated overnight at 4°C in the dark. Plates were then blocked with 200 µL per well of blocking buffer (PBS containing 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 0.05% Tween 20) for 2 h. After washing, serial dilutions of test antibodies in dilution buffer (PBS with 0.5% BSA and 0.05% Tween 20) were added (100 µL/well) and incubated for 1 h. Plates were washed again and incubated with Anti-human-FC-HRP (Promega, cat. no. W4031) diluted 1:2000 for 1 h. Following a final wash, 100 µL of TMB substrate was added per well and incubated for 25–30 min at room temperature in the dark. The reaction was stopped with stop solution, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

HEK-BlueTM IL-33 cells assay

HEK-Blue™ IL-33 cells (InvivoGen, catalog no. hkb-hil33) were utilized for bioactivity assessment of IL-33 through monitoring NF-κB and AP-1 pathway activation. These cells are derived from HEK293 and stably express the human IL1RL1 gene. At 70–80% confluence, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well. Recombinant IL-33 was applied at concentrations of 2 ng/mL and 30 ng/mL, with antibodies serially diluted threefold from a maximum concentration of 100 nM. Following 21 h of incubation at 37°C under 5% CO2, 20 μL of supernatant from each well was transferred to a new plate and mixed with 180 μL of Quanti-Blue solution. After 30 min of protected incubation at 37°C, absorbance at 620 nm was measured. Dose – response curves were generated to determine the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) for each antibody.

Human PBMCs assay

Human PBMCs were obtained from healthy donors with written informed consent, under protocols approved by the Ethics Committee of Tsinghua University (Beijing, China). All procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and relevant regulations. PBMCs (2x106 cells/mL) were stimulated with recombinant human IL-12 (5 ng/mL) and IL-33 in the presence or absence of the indicated antibodies. Cells were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 19 h. Culture supernatants were collected, and soluble IFN-γ levels were quantified using a Human IFN-γ Valukine™ ELISA kit (Catalog No. VAL104C), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. PBMCs from three independent donors were tested with comparable results.

Crystallization, data collection, and structure determination

The tozorakimab Fab – IL-33 complex was formed by incubating the components at a molar ratio of 1:1.2, followed by purification using a Superdex 200 Increase column equilibrated with HBS buffer. The purified complex was concentrated to 15 mg/mL for crystallization trials. Initial crystal screening was conducted using the sitting-drop vapor-diffusion method at 18°C, with 0.2 μL of protein solution mixed with 0.2 μL of reservoir solution. Crystals suitable for X-ray diffraction were obtained under condition containing 0.2 M magnesium chloride hexahydrate, 0.1 M sodium citrate tribasic dihydrate (pH 5.0), and 15% (w/v) polyethylene glycol 20,000.

X-ray diffraction data were collected at the BL18U1 beamline of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility using a wavelength of 0.987 Å. All datasets were integrated and scaled using the HKL-2000 software package. Data processing statistics are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Cryo-EM sample preparation and data collection

Aliquots of [IL33]-[Itepekimab Fab]-[Tozorakimab Fab] and [IL33]-[Etokimab Fab]-[Tozorakimab Fab] (4 μL, 2 mg/mL, in buffer containing 10 mM HEPES pH 7.2, 150 mM NaCl) were separately applied to glow-discharged holey carbon grids (Quantifoil grid, Cu 300 mesh, R1.2/1.3), blotted for 2 s with 0 force in 100% relative humidity and immediately plunged into the liquid ethane using Vitrobot (FEI). Images were recorded using FEI Titan Krios microscope operating at 300 kV with a Gatan K3 Summit direct electron detector (Gatan Inc.) at Tsinghua University. The automated software (AutoEMation) was used to collect 3227 movies for [IL33]-[Itepekimab Fab]-[Tozorakimab Fab] and 2826 for [IL33]-[Etokimab Fab]-[Tozorakimab Fab] at × 810,000 magnification and at a defocus range between 1.3 and 1.8 μm with a pixel size of 1.0742 Å. The exposure time was 2.56 s, with a total exposure dose of 50 electrons per Å2 over 32 frames.

Cryo-EM data processing, model fitting, and refinement

Motion Correction (MotionCo2), CTF-estimation (GCTF) and non-templated particle picking (Gautomatch, http://www.mrc-lmb.cam.ac.uk/kzhang/) were automatically executed by using the TsingTitan.py script written by Dr. Yang Fan of Tsinghua University. Sequential data processing was performed in cryoSPARC v4.7.0. Details of the data collection and processing are shown in Supplementary Figures and Supplementary Tables . The initial models of the IL33, itepekimab Fab, tozorakimab Fab and etokimab Fab were fit into the map using UCSF Chimera v.1.15. Manual model rebuilding was performed with COOT v.0.9.2 and refined with PHENIX v.1.18.2 real-space refinement. The quality of the final model was analyzed with PHENIX v.1.18.2. The validation statistics of the structural models are summarized in Supplementary Tables.
