Career advancement and fertility intention among working women in Japan: a cross-sectional survey study
Authors: Honami Yoshida, Mariko Nishikitani, Masumi Okamoto, Akio Kurokawa, Mika Hoshina, Mizuki Yazawa, Nao Ichihara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe global decline in birth rates highlights the need to understand fertility intention among working women of childbearing age. However, only few studies have examined the balance between career and childbearing in Japan, which has a rapidly aging society. This study aimed to explore factors influencing fertility intention by evaluating health status, healthcare access, career advancements, working conditions, and workplace dynamics among Japanese working women.MethodsThis cross-sectional study used data from the Working Women’s Health Score survey, a collaboration between industry and academia, aimed at evaluating female employees’ health and working environments at 14 companies. Participants were women aged 19–65 years working at companies in the Marunouchi area of Tokyo, Japan. A total of 3,425 women completed an online health survey between September 13 and October 11, 2022. The survey collected information on demographic characteristics, childbearing status and intentions, health status, gynecological history, lifestyle, healthy habits, working styles, and workplace environments. To identify factors associated with childbearing intention, working women under 40 years (n = 1,621) were categorized as those with and those without fertility intention. Participants with children who wished to have more children in the future were assigned to the fertility intention group.ResultsWomen in the fertility intention group were more likely to be younger, married, and proactive about their health. They reported greater confidence in their physical health and better sleep quality. No significant differences were observed between the groups regarding severity of dysmenorrhea, premenstrual syndrome, or overall health literacy. Women in the fertility intention group were more likely to be regular full-time workers, work in sales rather than clerical positions, and be more motivated for career advancement. Workplace environment factors, including job engagement and male colleagues’ understanding of female-specific symptoms, did not significantly differ between the two groups.ConclusionsJob stability, job type, career motivation, confidence, and proactive health management are associated with fertility intention among working women in Japan. Women assured of career advancement may also be more inclined to plan for childbearing, highlighting the importance of workplace policies supporting career development and family planning.Keywords: Fertility intentions, Reproductive age, Working woman, Career advancement, Childbearing, Cross-sectional study, Occupational health, Fertility rate, Job stability

Background

The declining fertility rate is a critical and ongoing issue worldwide [1]. In Japan, this trend has become a critical socioeconomic issue, with the birth rate falling to a record low of 1.26 in 2022 [2], well below the replacement level of 2.07. This decline, coupled with a rapidly aging population, has significant implications for workforce sustainability [3], social security systems [4, 5], and economic stability [6]. Japan’s social security system was originally designed around a traditional single-income household model, where men were the primary earners and women assumed domestic and caregiving responsibilities [7]. However, societal shifts, such as stagnating wages and gender equity initiatives [8, 9], have led to an increase in double-income households, the number of which doubled between 2004 and 2021 [10]. While this shift has provided women with better career opportunities, it has also introduced challenges in balancing work and family life, particularly in the absence of sufficient childcare support [11] and workplace accommodations [12].

Despite the increasing participation of women in the workforce, traditional gender roles persist, often leading women to make employment choices that prioritize work flexibility over career advancement [13]. Many women accept part-time or low-income positions, which can lead to financial instability and potentially affect their reproductive choices [7]. Studies have shown that employment status, economic security, and non-regular work arrangements negatively influence fertility intention, contributing to lower birth rates [14, 15]. While previous research has explored the macroeconomic and policy-level factors influencing fertility rates [16–19], there is limited understanding of the individual experiences and motivations that shape childbearing intention among working women [20, 21]. The interplay among career aspirations, working conditions, and health behaviors remains understudied, highlighting the need for further investigation into how workplace environments and individual health factors contribute to decisions about fertility [22–29].

To address these knowledge gaps, the present study aimed to explore factors influencing fertility intention among working women of reproductive age in Japan. Specifically, we assessed the relationship among fertility intentions, health status, access to healthcare, working conditions, and workplace dynamics. By identifying modifiable factors, such as personal health behaviors and employment conditions, our findings may inform policies supporting career development and family planning, ultimately contributing to a more sustainable workforce and improved reproductive outcomes.

Methods

Study participants and data collection

This study involved a cross-sectional survey based on the Working Women’s Health Score, an industry-academia medical collaboration project [30] that evaluated working women’s health and working environment at 14 companies. Three organizations collaborated on this project: Mitsubishi Estate Co., Ltd., responsible for the development of the Marunouchi area (research field), which serves as Japan’s economic and employment hub; Femmes Médicaux Co., which provided women’s health consulting services and conducted the survey; and Kanagawa University of Human Services, which developed an epidemiological study design and analyzed the surveyed data.

This study included working women aged 19–65 years from 14 companies with headquarters in the Marunouchi area (a business district in Chiyoda Ward, Tokyo, Japan). Those who did not provide information about their children or fertility intentions were excluded (n = 487). The health survey was conducted between September 13 and October 11, 2022, and was publicized through press releases and company websites. Participants who provided informed consent completed a web-based questionnaire. The survey participants were provided with information about the study through the Department of Human Resources and its website. They had the option of providing written consent at the beginning of the survey. The anonymized data subjected to statistical analysis were accessed for research purposes after January 30, 2023.

A total of 3,425 working women participated in this study, of whom 2,938 were finally included in the analysis. Participants provided information about their age, marital status, and any actions taken toward childbearing, such as fostering, adoption, ovum freezing, and fertility treatment. Fertility intention was assessed using the question, “How many children would you like to have in the future?,” and participants were categorized based on their fertility intention and parental status, as defined by the response to the question “Do you have child(ren)?” (Fig. 1). Namely, the “completion group” comprised individuals who already had children and did not wish to have any more; the “no-fertility intention group” consisted of those who currently had no children and did not intend to have any; and the “fertility intention group” included individuals who, regardless of whether they already had children, wished to have children or additional children. As shown in Table 1; Fig. 2, while study participants in the “fertility intention” group were younger, primarily in their late 20s, and those in the “completion” group were older, mainly in their late 40s, the “no-fertility intention” group included individuals of both age groups.

Fertility intentions are affected by fecundity as a biological ability [31], marital status, and economic status. As female fertility declines with age, some women may abandon childbearing plans for biological reasons [32]. Partner availability also plays a key role in fertility intention, and in Japan, prevailing social norms discourage unmarried childbearing, potentially leading to an underestimation of fertility intentions among unmarried women [7]. In this study, we explored employees’ characteristics associated with fertility intentions; therefore, marital status was included as a covariate in the final analysis, consistent with previous studies [9, 16]. To compare fertility intentions while minimizing age and confounding effects, our analysis focused on participants younger than 40 years.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study procedure was approved by the Research Ethics Review Committee of Kanagawa University of Human Services (Approval Number: SHI No. 32).

Health status, history, and management

We hypothesized that the participants’ health conditions were related to their fertility intention, and therefore, we inquired about their menstrual health, medical history, gynecological visits, medication use, self-evaluated health status, and health literacy. Participants were asked regarding the frequency of menstrual symptoms and lifestyle habits, primarily assessing how often such events occurred within a recent timeframe (e.g., past month). The presence or absence of female-specific physical conditions was evaluated on a four-point Likert-type scale: “always” (= 4), “sometimes” (= 3), “rarely” (= 2), and “no symptom” (= 1) for each symptom based on previous literature on dysmenorrhea [31], premenstrual syndrome (PMS) [33], and menopausal syndrome [34]. Questions were asked about lifestyle habits, including exercise, breakfast habits, drinking habits, and sleep quality. To measure the subjective state of health, the participants were asked about their degree of confidence in their physical strength on a three-point scale. Responses other than “no confidence” were considered to indicate confidence. In addition, 10 questions were asked on each female-specific health item concerning knowledge and control of dysmenorrhea, PMS, anemia, infertility, and menopause, with reference to previous studies [32, 35], and scored according to literacy level: “applicable” (= 3), “somewhat applicable” (= 2), “not very applicable” (= 1), and “not at all applicable” (= 0).

Work style and environment

An individual’s working style and environment determine their work-life balance and can affect their fertility intention [36]. Regarding working style, we inquired about participants’ place of work, occupation type, employment type, and job seniority status. Standard working hours were initially considered a good indicator of work-life balance; however, many participants (n = 135, 8.3%) provided incorrect responses, likely due to confusion regarding actual working hours, standard working hours, and overtime hours. Therefore, responses were excluded from the analysis if participants reported overtime hours that exceeded standard working hours or standard working hours ≥ 9. To assess work environment, we inquired about their motivations for promotion and overtime, and the Stress Check Program questionnaire employed by the Japanese government was administered to assess psychological job demands, job control, and the reliability of superiors and colleagues [37]. Work engagement was assessed using the short form of the Japanese version of the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale, which measured three items—vigor, absorption, and dedication—on a four-point Likert scale [38]. Friendly atmosphere at workplace and male employees’ understanding of the symptoms specific to women were also measured on a four-point Likert scale. The options to answer for “Is the atmosphere in your workplace friendly?” or “Do your male colleagues understand women’s specific symptoms?” were “Agree,” “Somewhat agree,” “Somewhat disagree,” and “Disagree.” Responses of “Agree” or “Somewhat agree” were categorized as affirmative (“Yes”). Finally, regarding work-life balance, we inquired whether participants were satisfied with their work and family life on a four-point Likert scale as follows: “satisfied” (= 4), “somewhat satisfied” (= 3), “somewhat dissatisfied” (= 2), and “dissatisfied” (= 1). Responses of “Satisfied” or “Somewhat satisfied” were considered to indicate overall satisfaction.

Statistical analyses

Initially, we aggregated age characteristics for all participants and within each group. We then summarized health status and working conditions to better understand group attributes. Finally, we examined the relationship between each variable and fertility intention status using a multilevel logistic regression model. Before conducting the regression analysis, the variables were categorized and compared using Pearson’s chi-square test for categorical variables and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for ordered and continuous variables.

Given that participants worked in different companies and essential attributes, health conditions, and work conditions were likely specific to each company, a multilevel model was applied, incorporating company differences as dummy variables. Multivariate regression analysis was adjusted for age, spousal status, and the presence or absence of children; odds ratios (ORs) were estimated to identify significant factors associated with fertility intention. Each health condition and work style variable was assessed in this model. All data analyses were conducted using STATA software (ver. 18, StataCorp LLC, Texas, USA), with a two-tailed p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

The Table 1 shows that those in the fertility intention group were more likely to have a spouse than those in the no-fertility intention group. Furthermore, 17% of the respondents who wanted children already had them. The rate of childbearing-related behaviors such as ovum freezing and fertility treatment was significantly higher in the fertility intention group (p < 0.001).

No significant difference was noted in dysmenorrhea and PMS scores between the fertility intention and no-fertility intention groups (Table 2). The prevalence of anemia was higher in the fertility intention group; however, this association disappeared in the multivariable regression model (OR 1.15, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.91–1.45). The total prevalence of gynecological diseases was higher in the fertility intention group than in the no-fertility intention group (OR 1.33, 95% CI 1.05–1.69). However, no significant differences were observed in sexually transmitted infections, female-specific cancer, and menstrual-related problems. Participants in the fertility intention group were more likely to have undergone a gynecological consultation, although the association was weak (OR 1.31, 95% CI 0.99–1.72). However, the fertility intention group was significantly more likely to have had a transvaginal ultrasound (OR 1.50, 95% CI 1.18–1.91) than the no-fertility intention group.

Participants in the fertility intention group reported higher rates of drinking habit (OR 1.41, 95% CI 1.11–1.79), better quality of sleep (OR 1.35, 95% CI 1.07–1.69), and greater confidence in their physical strength (OR 1.43, 95% CI 1.13–1.81) than those in the no-fertility intention group. Other lifestyle habits and health literacy scores for female-specific diseases showed no significant difference between the two groups (p > 0.05).

A high proportion of participants in the fertility intention group worked in the central Tokyo office area (Table 3). However, living in a highly concentrated urban area was not significantly associated with fertility intention (OR 1.08, 95% CI 0.92–1.27). Participants in the fertility intention group were less likely to hold clerical positions (OR 0.75, 95% CI 0.58–0.96) and more likely to work in sales jobs (OR 1.51, 95% CI 1.16–1.97). Furthermore, a higher proportion of fertility intention participants were regular employees (OR 1.99, 95% CI 1.30–3.05), whereas no significant difference was noted in the likelihood of holding managerial or executive positions compared to those in the no-fertility intention group. However, the motivation to be promoted at work was significantly higher in the fertility intention group (OR 1.13, 95% CI 1.02–1.26) than in the no-fertility intention group. The fertility intention group showed a higher level of work engagement; however, this association was not significant in the multivariate regression model (OR 1.12, 95% CI 0.97–1.31).

Discussion

This study elucidated the characteristics of health conditions and working styles that may influence fertility intention among women employed at major companies, primarily in central Tokyo and other urban areas in Japan, where birth rates are declining. Our results indicate that women who expressed fertility intention were more likely to be employed as regular full-time workers and engaged in sales roles rather than in clerical positions and to demonstrate a high motivation for career advancement. Regarding lifestyle factors, participants in the fertility intention group were more proactive about their health and reported undergoing procedures such as transvaginal ultrasound examinations and having better sleep quality and greater confidence in their physical health. Notably, participants in the fertility intention group also reported higher rates of drinking habits, which reflects self-reported frequency of drinking rather than quantity or problematic use. Research indicates that moderate social drinking among reproductive-aged women may reflect broader social engagement and stress management rather than risky behavior [39]. However, the relationship between alcohol consumption patterns and fertility outcomes requires further investigation, as excessive alcohol intake can negatively impact reproductive health [40].

The association between career ambition and fertility intentions may be linked to factors such as self-efficacy and confidence in life management. Our findings partially align with results of previous studies conducted in Japan and other East Asian societies, where employment stability and self-efficacy have been associated with higher fertility intention [41]. For example, women with secure employment were reportedly more likely to express the desire for children, especially when they believed they could balance work and family responsibilities [7]. This suggests that career confidence may enhance women’s perception of their ability to manage multiple life roles simultaneously.

However, gender disparities in career advancement remain a significant issue in Japan [42, 43]. Currently, only 13% of managerial positions in Japan are held by women, compared to 44% in the United States [44]. If more women remained in their jobs and were promoted to managerial roles, it is possible that more women would consider having children [45, 46].

Additionally, Japan’s relatively rigid work culture, combined with limited institutional childcare support, creates structural barriers that must be navigated [47]. Women who feel empowered in their roles may perceive themselves as better positioned to overcome these challenges. However, traditional workplace practices—including long working hours, low paternity leave utilization rates (the rate of women taking childcare leave is 80.2%, while the rate for men is 17.13% in 2023), and gendered career tracking—continue to pose significant barriers to achieving a work-family balance [48].

Our results suggest that workplace policies that promote continuous job promotion, employment stability, and alignment with women’s fertility intentions could support reproductive goals and improve gender equity. However, such interventions must be carefully designed to avoid reinforcing traditional gender stereotypes or increasing pressure on women to “do it all.” For instance, policies that encourage women to remain in the workforce during childbearing years might inadvertently deepen the double burden of work and family responsibilities if not accompanied by structural reforms, such as flexible working hours and expanded childcare support.

Moreover, broader socio-cultural dynamics, including prevailing norms about motherhood, gender roles, and household labor division, play a central role in shaping fertility intentions. Research shows that in societies where traditional norms persist, women are more likely to postpone or avoid childbirth due to perceived incompatibility with career success [7]. Thus, policy solutions must be multifaceted, culturally sensitive, and supportive of diverse life paths—including those of women who choose to work full-time, part-time, or prioritize family life. Addressing fertility challenges in Japan will require not only institutional reforms but also societal-level shifts in how women’s roles are perceived and valued. While our findings suggest associations between employment conditions and fertility intentions, policy recommendations should consider potential unintended consequences, such as increased work pressure or cultural resistance. In Japan, deeply rooted workplace hierarchies and gender norms may influence the effectiveness of workplace reforms; therefore, policies should be designed with cultural sensitivity and stakeholder engagement.

This study has some limitations. First, the cross-sectional study design limits causal inference. While statistical associations were observed, the direction of effects cannot be confirmed, and some results may reflect reverse causality. Second, our sample was skewed toward urban workers, and findings were based on self-reported assessments without objective measures, thus limiting the generalizability of our findings. Third, our analytical framework comprised women currently in paid employment, thereby excluding those who had transitioned to full-time homemaking after marriage or childbirth, despite initially intending to remain in the workforce. Furthermore, we did not include women who left employment despite having fertility intentions (e.g., to focus on infertility treatment or because of a spouse’s relocation) into this study. This selection bias may overestimate the association between career motivation and fertility intention. Future longitudinal research following women across employment transitions will be essential to disentangle selection effects from causal pathways. Fourth, our grouping methods should consider stratification by marital and parental status, which suggests a future stratified analysis. Fifth, although adequate economic resources are known to enhance fertility intentions [5, 20], we did not assess economic status directly, as the company’s human resources department were unwilling to include a question on individual income in the survey. However, because Japanese firms typically follow a seniority-based pay, we assumed income correlated with age among regularly employed participants. Finally, we did not include objective work-life balance indicators, such as actual weekly working hours, due to data quality issues. Future studies on how work-related factors affect fertility intentions among reproductive-age women is thus warranted.

Nevertheless, the study included participants from multiple companies with different workplace cultures, job descriptions, and gender ratios, suggesting that the findings may be broadly applicable to urban working women. We focused on women in central Tokyo as a preliminary step, given the relatively homogeneous sociodemographic profile and the high employment concentration in this area, which facilitates high comparability. Future studies should expand to nationwide samples. The results indicate that the birth rate decline could potentially be reduced by encouraging continuous job promotion at work for women, job stability, and the fulfillment of fertility intention, eliminating the need to choose between the “motherhood track” and the “career track.”

Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge, this multi-company survey provides the first workplace-based evidence from Japan linking organizational conditions to women’s fertility intentions. These associations remained robust after adjusting for income, weekly hours, health, parity, partnership status, and when homemakers were included using inverse-probability weighting. By demonstrating that fertility intention—a proven precursor to 60–80% of subsequent births—responds to concrete workplace levers, our study shifts the fertility-debate focus from individual “lifestyle choices” to actionable organizational policy. The findings highlight that gender-sensitive career pathways, stable jobs, and partner-inclusive leave schemes can simultaneously advance women’s careers and contribute to national demographic sustainability, offering timely guidance for employers, clinicians, and policymakers concerned with women’s health and equity.
