Trazodone, melatonin, and combination therapy for insomnia, fatigue, and daytime sleepiness following traumatic brain injury: study protocol for a randomized clinical trial

Background

Sleep disorders are among the most common and significant consequences of traumatic brain injury (TBI), impacting approximately 30–70% of patients regardless of injury severity. These issues can last for months or even years after the injury [1]. They include a variety of conditions, such as insomnia, hypersomnia, circadian rhythm problems, and sleep-related breathing issues. Each of these conditions can hinder cognitive recovery, cause emotional problems, and lead to worse functional outcomes [2, 3]. Notably, disrupted sleep at night often results in severe daytime symptoms. Fatigue affects 37–73% of TBI survivors, whereas excessive daytime sleepiness occurs in 25–47% of cases [4, 5]. Because sleep issues greatly influence neuroplasticity and recovery potential, addressing these issues is crucial for TBI care. However, research on evidence-based drug treatments in this vulnerable group is still limited [6].

The causes of sleep disorders following TBI involve a mix of direct brain injury and secondary physiological changes. Key mechanisms include damage to critical brain areas that regulate sleep, such as the hypothalamus, brainstem reticular formation, and thalamic nuclei. This damage disrupts arousal and sleep‒wake cycles [7, 8]. Changes in brain chemicals, especially melatonin production and serotonin signaling, make these problems worse and lead to circadian misalignment [9, 10]. Additional factors, such as pain, depression, environmental changes in acute care settings, and the use of multiple medications, further complicate sleep disturbances and often continue into the chronic recovery stage [11, 12].

Current evidence-based management of post-TBI sleep disorders relies mainly on cognitive-behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I). This approach has shown strong effectiveness in treating primary insomnia populations [13]. However, its application in TBI groups is limited due to cognitive impairments, a lack of insight and motivation, executive dysfunction, and practical issues with maintaining treatment adherence [14, 15]. As a result, doctors often use medication even though there is little high-quality evidence to support its safety and effectiveness for TBI patients.

Among these medications, trazodone and melatonin have become preferred first-line options because of their distinct mechanisms of action and good safety records. Trazodone, a serotonin antagonist and reuptake inhibitor (SARI), has sedative effects by blocking histamine H1 and α1-adrenergic receptors while also affecting 5-HT2A/2C receptors. This action helps with falling asleep and staying asleep without the risk of dependence seen with traditional sleeping pills [16, 17]. Clinical evidence supports its ability to improve sleep quality and reduce sleep latency in various populations, although specific data on TBI-related insomnia, fatigue, and daytime sleepiness are limited [16, 17].

Melatonin, a natural regulator of the body’s sleep‒wake cycle, is especially relevant for managing sleep after TBI given the frequent disruption of suprachiasmatic nucleus function after brain injury [18]. Melatonin works by acting on MT1 and MT2 receptors, which help individuals fall asleep and align their sleep–wake rhythm, addressing common issues such as trouble initiating sleep and disturbances in sleep patterns after TBI [19, 20]. While melatonin has been shown to be effective in improving subjective sleep quality in TBI patients, its impact on daytime symptoms is mixed. For example, Grima et al. reported significant improvements in fatigue but not in daytime sleepiness, even though sleep metrics improved [6].

The idea behind using a combination of these treatments is based on how their mechanisms complement each other. Trazodone’s direct sedating effects may help with sleep initiation and maintenance, whereas melatonin’s effects on circadian rhythms could restore a better sleep cycle and improve overall sleep quality. This combined approach might be particularly useful for TBI patients, as they often experience both disrupted sleep patterns and circadian irregularities. However, no randomized clinical trial has systematically assessed how well these treatments work alone or together on insomnia, fatigue, and sleepiness in TBI patients.

This randomized, double-blind clinical trial aims to address these important gaps by comparing trazodone alone, melatonin alone, and their combination in treating insomnia, fatigue, and daytime sleepiness in patients with moderate to severe TBI. The results provide crucial evidence to guide clinical decisions and help establish a basis for sleep-focused treatments in TBI recovery.

Methods

Study design

This randomized, double-blind, parallel-group clinical trial evaluates the efficacy of trazodone, melatonin, and their combination in improving sleep quality, fatigue, and daytime sleepiness among patients with moderate to severe TBI. This is a three-arm superiority trial with a 1:1:1 allocation ratio.

The study takes place at Taleghani Hospital, Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences, Kermanshah, Iran. It follows SPIRIT 2013 guidelines and is prospectively registered with the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT20241121063793N1). The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences, Kermanshah, Iran (Approval No: IR.KUMS.MED.REC.1403.050, Date: 15 October 2023). Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants by the principal investigator (MH) or a delegated, trained physician after a complete explanation of study objectives, procedures, risks, and benefits.

This is a single-center, investigator-initiated trial. Day-to-day oversight is provided by the principal investigator (MH). No steering committee, endpoint adjudication committee, or independent data management team is required due to the small sample size and short duration. Safety oversight is performed by the institutional ethics committee.

The participants are randomly assigned a 1:1:1 ratio to receive one of the three treatments over 4 weeks. Assessments are performed before and immediately after treatment (Figs. 1, and 2). All procedures follow the Declaration of Helsinki and local regulatory standards. Participants

Eligible participants are adults aged 18 years or older with a documented history of moderate to severe TBI, defined by a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score between 3 and 12, occurring at least 6 months prior to enrollment. This threshold was selected to exclude the acute recovery phase, during which spontaneous neurological and sleep-related improvements may occur, and is consistent with prior randomized clinical trials of insomnia following TBI [21]. The participants report new sleep problems that began after their TBI, confirmed by an insomnia severity index (ISI) score of 8 or higher, indicating at least mild insomnia symptoms [22]. A board-certified sleep physician must diagnose insomnia. Participants must be able to provide informed consent and follow the study procedures.

The exclusion criteria include any sleep disorders, fatigue syndromes, or neurological conditions present before the injury; current night shift work; untreated sleep apnea or other untreated primary sleep disorders; recent use of sleep medications; or starting any pharmacological, psychological, or behavioral therapies for sleep outside the study protocol. Additional exclusions are significant cognitive impairment (defined as a Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) score ≤ 22, assessed during the screening phase [23] to ensure adequate comprehension of study procedures and valid completion of self-reported outcomes), vegetative state, pregnancy or lactation, and any medical or psychiatric condition that might risk participant safety or data validity. Screening procedures involve thorough medical examination, urine toxicology to exclude illicit substance use, and pregnancy testing for female participants.

Participants must not start any new sleep treatments during the study period. Written informed consent is obtained after a complete explanation of the study’ objectives, procedures, potential risks, and benefits.

Randomization and blinding

The allocation sequence is generated by an independent biostatistician not involved in enrollment or outcome assessment using computer-generated permuted blocks stratified by age and sex. Allocation concealment is maintained through sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. The principal investigator (MH) or a trained research physician enrolls eligible participants after screening and consent. A separate blinded research coordinator assigns participants to interventions by opening the next sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelope.

Both the participants and the investigators are blinded to the treatment assignment. The study medications are provided in identical packaging. Unblinding will only be permitted in the event of a serious adverse event that requires immediate knowledge of the treatment allocation for clinical management. In such cases, the principal investigator will request unblinding from the independent research coordinator who holds the allocation list. All unblinding events will be documented and reported to the ethics committee.

All participants receive standardized sleep hygiene counseling. Adherence is tracked weekly. Retention is promoted through weekly telephone contacts by the study coordinator to monitor adherence, troubleshoot barriers, reinforce sleep hygiene education, and remind participants of the week-4 visit. Outcome data are collected for all participants who discontinue the intervention unless consent is explicitly withdrawn.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions include intolerable adverse effects (e.g., excessive sedation or orthostatic hypotension with trazodone), participant request, pregnancy, or development of a contraindicating medical condition. All modifications/discontinuations are documented in the case report form and participants are encouraged to complete follow-up assessments unless consent is withdrawn.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome is the change in sleep quality measured by the insomnia severity index (ISI) from baseline to the end of the intervention (week 4). This is a validated seven-item questionnaire that assesses insomnia symptoms and their impact during the day. ISI scores range from 0 to 28, with higher scores indicating more severe insomnia. The Persian version of the ISI has shown strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s α > 0.8) and test–retest reliability (ICC > 0.7) in Iranian samples [24]. The secondary outcomes include changes in fatigue severity as measured by the fatigue severity scale (FSS) and daytime sleepiness as measured by the Epworth sleepiness scale (ESS). The FSS includes 9 items rated from 1 to 7; the average score indicates fatigue severity, with scores of 4 or higher suggesting clinically significant fatigue. The Persian version of the FSS has shown excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.96) and reliability [25]. The ESS is an 8-item measure of daytime sleepiness scored from 0 to 3 per item, with total scores ranging from 0 to 24. Higher scores indicate more daytime sleepiness. The Persian ESS has internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.78) and good validity with the PSQI (r = 0.42) [26]. Assessments are performed at baseline and at the end of the 4-week intervention period.

Data collection

Data entry is performed by a trained research assistant into a password-protected, encrypted Microsoft Excel database hosted on a secure KUMS server with role-based access limited to the study team. Ten percent of entries undergo double verification; range and logic checks are applied automatically. All data are backed up daily and stored in compliance with Iranian data protection regulations and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sample size

A total of 48 participants (16 per group) were targeted based on a priori power calculations from a preliminary pilot study at our center. The pilot demonstrated a moderate effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.35) for ISI change across groups. Using G*Power 3.1 for a 3 × 2 mixed ANOVA (α = 0.05, power = 80%, correlation among repeated measures = 0.5), 13 participants per group were required. The target was increased to 16 per group to accommodate 10% attrition and the exploratory nature of the trial in a challenging population.

Recruitment strategies include systematic screening of consecutive eligible patients attending the outpatient neurology and neurorehabilitation clinics at Taleghani Hospital, review of the hospital’s chronic TBI registry, and active referrals from collaborating physicians within the Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences network. Weekly monitoring of enrollment rates by the study team will allow timely adjustments if needed. Community outreach via local patient support groups will supplement clinic-based recruitment.

Statistical analysis

The analyses will follow the intent-to-treat principle. Continuous variables, such as the ISI, FSS, and ESS scores, will be checked for normality via the Shapiro–Wilk test and for homogeneity of variance via Levene’s test. A mixed-design two-way ANOVA will assess the effects of the treatment group (between-subject factor: trazodone, melatonin, combination) and time (within-subject factor: baseline, posttreatment) on the outcome scores. If normality or homogeneity assumptions are not met, we will use nonparametric alternatives such as the Friedman test or the Kruskal–Wallis test. Post hoc comparisons will be adjusted for multiple testing via Tukey’s HSD or Bonferroni correction when appropriate. Missing data will be handled with multiple imputation, assuming that they are missing at random. We will also conduct sensitivity analyses through complete-case and last observation carried forward methods to ensure robustness. A two-tailed p value of less than 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Safety monitoring

Adverse events are collected systematically at each weekly phone contact and at the week-4 visit using open-ended questions and a structured checklist. All events are assessed for causality and severity by the principal investigator, documented in the case report form, and reported to the ethics committee within 24 h for serious adverse events (SAEs). SAEs are also reported to the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials. An independent safety monitor (senior neurologist not otherwise involved) reviews cumulative safety data every 3 months.

No interim analyses are planned because of the small sample size (n = 48) and short trial duration (4 weeks). The trial will be stopped only if the ethics committee determines that continuation poses unacceptable risk to participants.

Discussion

Sleep disturbances are among the most prevalent and debilitating problems after TBI. They significantly impact recovery, social functioning, and long-term quality of life. Fatigue and excessive daytime sleepiness often occur and can worsen functional impairments and lower the potential for rehabilitation [6]. Despite how common these issues are, effective drug treatments for insomnia, fatigue, and circadian rhythm disorders after TBI are still limited. This randomized, double-blind clinical trial compares how well trazodone, melatonin, and their combination improve sleep quality, fatigue, and daytime sleepiness in patients with moderate to severe TBI.

This study addresses several important gaps in the current literature. First, trazodone and melatonin are frequently used off-label to treat sleep disturbances after TBI. However, their effectiveness when used alone or together has not been rigorously evaluated in this group of patients. Trazodone, a SARI that has sedating antihistaminergic properties, has shown benefits in starting and maintaining sleep. There is limited but promising evidence that it may also help reduce symptoms related to fatigue. Melatonin acts as a chronobiotic by activating MT1 and MT2 receptors in the suprachiasmatic nucleus, which helps align circadian rhythms with sleep onset. Notably, melatonin supplements have been shown to reduce fatigue in TBI patients, although their effects on daytime sleepiness are less clear [6]. The possible additive or synergistic effects of these medications, which target different aspects of sleep regulation and circadian restoration, need thorough investigation.

Second, this trial focuses especially on patients with moderate to severe TBI, a group that is often left out of clinical research owing to their complex conditions and additional health issues. Most previous studies have included mostly mild or mixed-severity groups, which makes the results less applicable to those with more severe TBI. By including a well-defined group and using validated assessment tools such as the ISI, FSS, and ESS, this study improves the relevance of its findings to real-world neurorehabilitation.

The decision to not include a placebo or usual-care control group is based on ethical and practical reasons. Given how serious sleep disturbances and fatigue are in this population and the common off-label use of trazodone and melatonin, it was considered unethical to withhold active treatment. Comparing three active interventions enhances the study’s relevance and feasibility. Future larger-scale, multicenter trials may include placebo or standard-care groups where ethical considerations and logistical capabilities permit.

This study has several limitations. The lack of comprehensive psychiatric assessments may lead to the absence of other conditions affecting sleep, fatigue, and daytime sleepiness. Furthermore, the relatively short duration of the intervention restricts our understanding of long-term effectiveness and safety. Self-reported measures may introduce recall bias; future trials should use actigraphy for objective sleep evaluation.

Despite these limitations, this trial is a significant step toward improving drug management for TBI-related sleep disturbances, fatigue, and daytime sleepiness. By comparing widely used treatments and assessing combination therapy, important data can be used to guide clinical decisions and future studies. Further research that includes longer follow-up periods, objective sleep assessments, biomarker analyses, and broader patient populations will be crucial for developing personalized treatment plans in this challenging area.
