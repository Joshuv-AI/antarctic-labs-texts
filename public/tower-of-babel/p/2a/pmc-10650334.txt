Optimizing contrast protocol for bone‐subtraction CT angiography of intracranial arteries in normal dogs using 160‐slice CT
Authors: Soyon An, Gunha Hwang, Rakhoon Kim, Jihye Cha, Hee Chun Lee, Tae‐Sung Hwang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBone‐subtraction computed tomography angiography (CTA) (BSCTA) is a new technique designed to overcome the limitation of three‐dimensional CTA, where the vessels surrounded by bone and calcification can be obscured. An optimal contrast CT protocol for intracranial artery visualization with BSCTA has yet to be established in dogs.ObjectivesThe purpose of this study was to determine the optimal contrast protocol of CTA for visualizing intracranial artery using an automatic bone‐subtraction technique in dogs.MethodsBrain CTA was performed four times for each of nine healthy beagle dogs to cover all the contrast protocols: two different contrast iodine concentrations (300 and 370 mgI/mL) and two different contrast media injection rates (2 and 4 mL/s). Bone removal post‐processing was performed automatically by subtracting the non‐enhanced CT data from the contrast CT data using a dedicated workstation. The bone‐subtracted intracranial vessels were analysed for quantitative and qualitative evaluation.ResultsQuantitative evaluation showed significantly higher CT attenuation values for the group with a 370 mgI/mL iodine content at a rate of 4 mL/s than the two groups with a 300 mgI/mL iodine content at the rates of 2 and 4 mL/s (p < 0.001). Qualitative assessment revealed significantly higher mean scores for the 370 mgI/mL groups than the 300 mgI/mL groups and significantly higher mean scores for the 4 mL/s groups than the 2 mL/s groups (p < 0.05).ConclusionsThe optimal contrast protocol for BSCTA suggests that high iodine material concentration and high injection rate should be used for strong arterial attenuation and great visualization of the intracranial arterial structure in dogs.Keywords: bone subtraction, computed tomography, contrast media, dog, intracranial artery

INTRODUCTION

Computed tomography angiography (CTA) is a widely used noninvasive diagnostic procedure in humans, especially in head and neck imaging, for the detection of cerebral aneurysms, arterial stenosis and other vascular anomalies in the extra‐ and intracranial arteries (Lell et al., 2006; Venema et al., 2001). In dogs, cerebrovascular accidents are becoming a more common cause of acute neurologic dysfunction, and incidental intracranial aneurysms can be detected through CTA (Arnold et al., 2020; Bertolini, 2013). While two‐dimensional CTA images can be used to evaluate vascular structures, three‐dimensional (3D) reconstruction provides more accurate anatomical information. However, a limitation of 3D CTA is that vessels surrounded by bone and calcification may be obscured (Lell et al., 2006). Bone‐subtraction CTA (BSCTA) can overcome this limitation by creating a bone model through a non‐enhanced scan that removes the bone structure from CTA data sets (Lell et al., 2006, 2007; Tomandl et al., 2006). In humans, early attempts to subtract non‐enhanced data from enhanced CT data, known as ’digital subtraction’ CTA, were based on a section‐by‐section approach. However, studies have reported improvements in the selective bone‐subtraction method using a ‘matched mask bone elimination’, (MMBE) which selectively removes bone and calcifications while maintaining soft tissue visualization (Lell et al., 2006; Venema & den Heeten 2003; Venema et al., 2001).

To maximize the clinical benefit of CTA, certain practices for CT intravenous contrast media administration must be followed (Bae, 2010). Some veterinary research has examined the optimized protocol for CT of canine brain parenchyma (Zarelli et al., 2014) and the optimal placement of the region of interest (ROI) on canine brain CTA (Park et al., 2021). Contrast protocols for the neck and brain BSCTA use a human contrast media dose for a 70 kg patient, consisting of 70–100 mL of 300–370 mgI/mL; injection rate of 4–5.5 mL/s; and saline flush of 30–80 mL with 4–5 mL/s (Cheng et al., 2015; Lell et al., 2006, 2007; Li et al., 2013; Nagayama et al., 2017; Tomandl et al., 2006). However, an optimal contrast CT protocol for intracranial artery visualization in dogs with BSCTA using 160‐slice CT has yet to be established.

The purpose of this study was to compare different CTA contrast protocols, with varying contrast iodine concentrations and contrast injection rates, using the intracranial BSCTA technique in normal dogs. We hypothesized that highest iodine material concentration and highest injection rate would result in good visual quality of the intracranial arteries using bone‐subtraction techniques in dogs. The goal was to suggest an appropriate contrast protocol for visualizing the intracranial artery.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

Nine healthy male beagle dogs were used in a crossover method for the experiments. The mean weight for all dogs was 10.6 kg (8.7–12.1 kg) and the mean age was 5.9 years (2–9 years). All dogs were screened for evidence of neurogenic disease and vascular anomaly disease in abdomen through physical and neurological examinations, thoracic and abdominal radiography and abdominal ultrasonography. To ensure safe anaesthesia, the beagle dogs were fasted for 12 h whereas they were free to drink water.

All dogs were premedicated with glycopyrrolate (0.01 mg/kg, SC, Mobinul Inj, Myungmoon Pharm.). Anaesthesia was induced with alfaxalone (2 mg/kg, IV, Alfaxan Inj, Careside.) through an 18‐ga intravenous catheter and 3‐way stopcock in the cephalic vein. General anaesthesia was maintained with isoflurane (Ifran, Hana Pharm.) in oxygen (2 L/min) via endotracheal intubation. Electrocardiography (ECG), oxygen saturation, and breathing were monitored throughout all procedures. The heart rates of the dogs were monitored throughout scanning to ensure they remained above 100 beats/min and were also recorded before induction and after the CT scan.

All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, and the dogs were cared for according to the Guidelines for Animal Experiments (GNU‐220420‐D0039).

CT image acquisition

The study was performed by a single operator (ASY) using a 160‐slice multi‐detector CT (Aquilion Lightning 160, Canon Medical Systems). The subjects were positioned in ventral recumbency on the CT table, and a scanography helical scan was performed from the head to the thorax in the rostral‐to‐caudal direction to determine the position of the bolus‐tracking site and CTA. The trigger threshold for bolus tracking was set at 50 Hounsfield unit (HU), and the mean HU of each ROI. Circular ROI was placed at the T8 level in the left pulmonary artery (Park et al., 2021). CT scanning started automatically with a 5‐s delay after the trigger when the HU of the ROI reached the threshold HU and breath‐holding was maintained during scanning (Park et al., 2021). The scanning parameters of the pre‐contrast and angiographic volume images were as follows: kilovoltage of 80 kVp, effective tube current of 200 mAs eff , field of view (FOV) of 180 mm, rotation time of 0.75 s and slice thickness of 0.5 mm. Brain CTA was automatically initiated at the occipital bone level in the caudal‐to‐rostral direction with the beginning of the injection of the contrast media, using a bolus‐tracking technique. Breath‐holding was maintained during bolus tracking and CTA scanning, and the scan duration was fixed at 6.5 s. The images were reconstructed with a 0.5 mm thickness with a 0.25 mm interval, using a head‐specific reconstruction kernel (Canon proprietary terms FC 64), a 250 window level, a 550 window width and a 180 mm FOV.

Contrast protocol

All contrast media were preheated to 37°C and administered intravenously using a dual‐head power injector (Salient, Medrad Inc.). This study used two types of contrast media: 300 mg/mL (Iohexol, Omnipaque 300, GE Healthcare) and 370 mg/mL (Iopamidol, Scanlux 370, Sanochemia Pharmazeutika AG). The contrast injections were standardized for all experiments to a total of 600 mg iodine/kg (Cassel et al., 2013; Goggs et al., 2014; Kishimoto et al., 2010). There were four groups based on the iodine concentration and the injection flow rate: group A (300 mg/mL Iodine content at a rate of 2 mL/s), group B (300 mg/mL Iodine content at a rate of 4 mL/s), group C (370 mg/mL Iodine content at a rate of 2 mL/s) and group D (370 mg/mL Iodine content at a rate of 4 mL/s). Saline injections were followed by a half volume of the contrast volume with more than 10 mL at a minimum and a fixed injection rate at 2 mL/s (Kishimoto et al., 2010). The studies were conducted using a cross‐over design with the same nine dogs used at four different times. The experiment was performed in the following order: group A–D. To ensure complete renal excretion of the contrast medium, a minimum time delay of 2 weeks was maintained between consecutive examinations in the same dog.

Image post‐processing

For post‐processing subtraction and 3D reconstruction images, the data were transferred to a software program (Xelis, Infinitt). The study used ‘MMBE’, a semi‐automatic bone‐subtraction method. A low‐dose mask of the bony skull was acquired after extracting the bone‐containing voxels from the post‐contrast images using a computer algorithm, and these data compensated for movements between scans (Venema et al., 2001). As this method avoids the subtraction of brain tissue, all soft tissue structures remained available for further evaluation in the resulting data volume (Lell et al., 2006). Two volume data of pre‐ and post‐contrast CT images were selected, and subtraction proceeded automatically (Figure 1A–C). Image filtering for brain angiography subtraction in software was selected none of remove noise. All 3D images for the maximum‐intensity projections (MIP) and volume‐rendering (VR) rendering set minimum 150 HU and maximum 2000 HU threshold. The subtraction axial images were acquired automatically with 250 window level and 550 window width. Entire data images set identical.

Quantitative image analysis

Vascular CT attenuation values at the cavernous segment of the internal carotid artery (Cho et al., 2012, 2015; Morhard et al., 2008; Nagayama et al., 2017; Waaijer et al., 2007) were measured in terms of HU values on non‐subtraction axial post‐contrast images using a Radiant digital viewer (Medixant) by one experienced veterinarian for radiology (ASY). The circular ROI was carefully drawn to be as large as possible while not approaching the edges of the internal carotid artery using a manually defined ROI with 0.3–2 mm2. The CT values were measured three times at different segments of the bilateral vessel, and the average value was used for the vascular CT value.

Qualitative image analysis

Two experienced veterinarians for radiology (HGH and RHK, Residents in the Veterinary Medical Imaging Department of the Teaching Hospital) evaluated the 3D images of BSCTA. Both evaluators had no prior knowledge of the contrast injection protocols or the clinical data. All data sets were randomized for interpretation. The experienced veterinarians evaluated the MIP images, but additional VR was optionally available to all readers on a workstation (Figure 2A–E). The MIP technique was selected as the evaluation method because it is a widely used post‐processing technique and highlights bone remnants, thus facilitating the assessment of the subtraction results (Fishman et al., 2006). To exclude areas from overlying skin and subcutaneous region, the volume of interest was defined as the cranial fossa of cerebral arteries to the caudal fossa of cerebellar arteries. The arterial enhancement, sharpness of the arterial boundary, depiction of small arterial details, venous contamination, bone remnants and overall image quality were each assessed through five‐point scales (Cho et al., 2015; Morhard et al., 2008; Nagayama et al., 2017; Tang et al., 2015). The scoring criteria details are presented in Table 1 and (Figure 3A–F). A window level of 250 HU and width of 550 HU were fixed only during the quantitative and qualitative assessments in order to compare the differences among the four groups.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted with a commercially available software package (SPSS 25.0, IBM). All data were tested to confirm a normal distribution using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Wilcoxon's signed‐rank test was used to analyse the differences between each of the groups for HU value and subject score assessment analysis. Inter‐observer variation was assessed using linear weighted kappa statistics. Kappa values under 0.20 indicate poor agreement; 0.21–0.40, fair agreement; 0.41–0.60, moderate agreement; 0.61–0.80, good agreement; and 0.81–1.00, excellent agreement (Landis & Koch, 1977). p Values less than 0.05 were considered to be statistically significant for qualitative analysis.

RESULTS

A total of 36 series of CT scans were performed, and the mean heart rates during CT scanning were 125 bpm (109–139 bpm). ECG and oxygen saturation remained stable during the experiments, and there were no complications such as contrast material paravasation. The mean duration time of contrast agent injection for groups A, B, C and D was 10.6 s (8.7–12.1 s), 5.3 s (8.3–6.0 s), 8.4 s (6.9–9.6 s) and 4.2 s (3.4–4.8 s), respectively. Immediately following the completion of contrast media injection, a 10 mL saline solution was administered to all groups at a rate of 2 mL/s for 5 s. The average reconstruction time was 3 min for BSCTA, with an average of 36 series images per scan.

Quantitative image analysis

The enhancement values measured in the internal carotid artery of groups A, B, C and D were 236.3 HU (138–338 HU), 278.6 HU (211–495 HU), 361.2 HU (259–604 HU) and 506.6 HU (168–821 HU), respectively. The HU values of group D were significantly different from those of groups A and B (p < 0.0001). Table 2 summarizes the CT values.

DISCUSSION

The aim of this study was to determine the optimal contrast protocol for achieving good arterial enhancement with no interruption of BSCTA images in healthy beagle dogs by evaluating different iodine concentrations and contrast injection rates. The CT attenuation values of all protocol groups at the cavernous segment of the internal carotid artery were quantitatively evaluated, whereas six subjects were qualitatively analysed for image quality assessment using MIP and VR images by two readers.

Various factors, including patient‐related factors, CT scan‐related factors and contrast media‐related factors, can affect contrast enhancement in CTA (Bae, 2010; Bertolini, 2017). The main patient‐related factors are patient body weight and cardiac output, and the other factors include age, sex, venous access, renal function, hepatic cirrhosis, portal hypertension and various other pathologic conditions (Bae, 2010). To control for patient factors, medium‐sized male healthy beagles were selected, and their disease status and heart rate were monitored throughout the experimental period. The mean heart rates during CT scanning ranged from 109 to 139 bpm.

Scan parameters, scan duration, scan direction, multiphasic acquisitions and scan delay are factors that can affect contrast enhancement in CTA (Bae, 2010). Kilovoltage, effective tube current, rotation time, slice thickness and bolus tracking were fixed in this study. Previous studies for cerebral CTA and BSCTA have shown that low kilovoltage can improve vascular enhancement while reducing the radiation dose (Cho et al., 2015; Nagayama et al., 2017; Ramgren et al., 2012; Tang et al., 2015; Waaijer et al., 2007). In this study, 80 kVp was selected rather than 120 kVp according to previous studies (Cho et al., 2015; Nagayama et al., 2017; Ramgren et al., 2012; Tang et al., 2015; Waaijer et al., 2007). The effective tube current was 200 mAs eff , which was set as high as possible to achieve good quality within a permissible total effective dose. The rotation time and slice thickness were chosen as low as possible to acquire lots of information on the volume data. The ROI setting was selected to fix the factors of the bolus‐tracking technique. The ROI for CT brain angiography using bolus tracking was placed in the pulmonary artery or pulmonary vein, which is expected to facilitate strong intracranial arterial attenuation and minimal venous contamination in beagle dogs (Park et al., 2021). We determined that placing the ROI in pulmonary artery could enhance intracranial arteries for BSCTA. The mean score of venous contaminant of all groups was almost higher than four, indicating that replacing the ROI in the pulmonary artery was beneficial for minimizing venous contamination in canine brain CTA.

The parameters of contrast medium, including iodine volume, concentration and injection rate, are known to affect contrast enhancement in CTA (Bae, 2010). In this study, the total iodine volume was fixed in 600 mgI/kg based on previous studies in canine CT angiography (Bertolini, 2017; Cassel et al., 2013; Kishimoto et al., 2010; Park et al., 2021). When the total iodine volume and injection rate are fixed, using a higher contrast media concentration will result in greater peak enhancement by shortening the injection duration (Bae, 2010; Bertolini, 2017). Our results showed that the image quality subject to arterial enhancement, sharpness of artery boundary and overall image quality were significantly higher in the groups with a concentration of 370 mgI/mL (groups C and D) compared to the other groups with the same injection flow velocity. Increasing the injection rate while keeping a fixed volume of contrast media also results in higher arterial enhancement (Bae, 2010; Bertolini, 2017). Our study showed that the groups with an injection rate of 4 mL/s (groups B and D) had significantly higher scores in all image subjects except for venous contaminant, which is consistent with previous studies. Therefore, we suggest using a high enough injection flow velocity to withstand the pressure of contrast injection. However, the pressure of the contrast injection in the power injector increases with the injection rate, and it is not easy clinically to apply an 18G intravenous catheter on a peripheral vein in small dogs. Our results showed no significant differences in image scores of arterial enhancement, sharpness of artery boundary, venous contaminant and overall image quality between groups B and C. Therefore, we propose using a high iodine concentration of 370 mgI/mL when applying a small intravenous catheter and a low injection velocity in dogs.

Iohexol and iopamidol are both nonionic monomer contrast media with low osmolality, although there is a small difference in osmolality and viscosity between the two agents. The Omnipaque 300 (iohexol) has an osmolality of 672 mOsmol/kg at 37°C and a viscosity of 6.3 mPas at 37°C, whereas Scanlux 300 (iopamidol) has an osmolality of 635.9 mOsmol/kg at 37°C and a viscosity of 4.5 mPas at 37°C. Scanlux 370 has an osmolality of 834.8 mOsmol/kg at 37°C and a viscosity of 9.0 mPas at 37°C (Kodzwa, 2018). The high concentration of iodine in contrast media is associated with high osmolality and viscosity (Jost et al., 2011). Moreover, the high osmolality of the high concentration agent not only leads to an increase in blood flow but also has a higher risk of renal toxicity (Andreucci et al., 2014; Kishimoto et al., 2010). As a solution to this problem, saline flush was introduced to CTA (Haage et al., 2000). Saline flushing is essential in human brain CTA because it improves arterial opacification and prolongs the arterial enhancement phase (Bae, 2010; Bertolini, 2017). In beagle dogs, the vascular volume from the site where the omobrachial vein flows into the external jugular vein to the cranial vena cava was 10 mL (Kishimoto et al., 2010). Therefore, we administered a saline dose that was half the contrast media dose, with no less than 10 mL for saline flush. As a result, the saline flush technique was applied to all groups, and none of the subjects experienced side effects.

Several human studies in this field have identified an optimal attenuation value greater than 250 HU (Ramadan et al., 2010; Tamura et al., 2010). Another study indicated that approximately 300 HU produces satisfactory 3D CT images (Hirai et al., 2005). As the attenuation value reflects 3D reformation, it is logical that the results of our quantitative and qualitative evaluations were similar (Park et al., 2021). In our study, the mean attenuation value of group B was greater than 250 HU, whereas those of groups C and D were greater than 300 HU. The mean HU value in group D was superior to those in the other groups. Qualitative analysis revealed that the image score of group D, which included arterial enhancement, sharpness of artery boundary, depiction of small arterial details and overall image quality, was significantly higher than those of the other groups. The image score of group D – which included arterial enhancement, sharpness of artery boundary, depiction of small arterial details and the overall image quality – was found to be significantly higher than those of the rest in qualitative analysis. Therefore, both qualitative and quantitative evaluations in this study provided appropriate results as the optimal contrast protocol. The mean score of venous contaminant and bone remnants was lowest in group D, but there was no significant difference from other groups. This result suggests that the high concentration of contrast media and high velocity of injection flow increase the visualization of cerebral artery while increasing bone remnants (Bertolini, 2017).

There were several limitations of this study. First, the iodine volume of contrast media was not considered a variable factor. Although increasing the contrast medium volume relates to a longer injection duration and thus results in a high level of contrast enhancement, it was difficult to acquire an image of only arterial enhancement (Bae, 2010). Therefore, standard iodine volume (600 mgI/kg) was chosen, as it is the most widely used in veterinary medicine (Bertolini, 2017; Cassel et al., 2013; Kishimoto et al., 2010; Park et al., 2021). Second, the quantitative assessments were performed by only one investigator. Moreover, the lack of quantitative analysis comparison between the contrast protocols. In human medicine for detecting intracranial vessel anomaly or arterial aneurysm, clinicians usually used 3D images with qualitative evaluation. Further study will be useful to select establish contrast protocol for BSCTA. Third, the image analysis subjects of qualitative assessments were modified by human criteria because, to our knowledge, there is no established and clearly defined scoring system for cranial artery in dogs.

CONCLUSION

This study has demonstrated that the use of a high iodine material concentration (370 mgI/mL) and a high injection rate (4 mL/s) is the optimal contrast protocol for BSCTA in beagle dogs. This protocol enables strong arterial attenuation and provides excellent visualization of the intracranial arterial structure with 3D images. The standardization of BSCTA in dogs is crucial for detecting intracranial vessel diseases, and this study serves as a significant step towards this goal. The findings of this study can provide valuable insights into optimizing imaging protocols for BSCTA in dogs and can potentially aid in improving diagnostic accuracy for intracranial vessel diseases.
