Glucocorticoids and Neutrophil Biology: Impact on the Development of Resistance to Glucocorticoid Therapy
Authors: Fabiana dos Santos Pacheco, Rayssa Garcia da Rocha Mota, Yago Amigo Pinho Jannini de Sá, Cory M. Hogaboam, Hugo Caire Castro-Faria-Neto, Vinicius Frias Carvalho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeutrophils are the most abundant leukocytes in peripheral circulation and play a crucial role in combating infections and mediating inflammatory responses. Neutrophils are produced in bone marrow, have a short half-life in the blood, and are rapidly attracted to the tissues in response to infection or tissue damage.SummaryGlucocorticoids (GCs), stress hormones, and potent anti-inflammatory agents exert complex effects on neutrophils. While they generally suppress immune responses, GCs can paradoxically enhance neutrophil survival and function under certain conditions. This duality is evident in their ability to delay neutrophil apoptosis and to induce a shift of neutrophils from the marginated to the circulating pool, increasing the neutrophil presence in the bloodstream. Th17 cells, a subset of T-helper cells, recruit neutrophils to sites of infection and inflammation. In addition, neutrophils promote Th17 cell differentiation. GCs can enhance Th17 differentiation and IL-17 production, exacerbating neutrophil accumulation. Nevertheless, in glucocorticoid-resistant diseases, including multiple sclerosis (MS), traumatic brain injury (TBI), and encephalomyelitis, Th17 cells and neutrophils contribute to persistent inflammation. This resistance complicates the treatment of autoimmune diseases and chronic inflammatory disorders, also in the central nervous system, where standard glucocorticoid therapy fails to mitigate symptoms effectively.Key MessagesIn this context, we propose a mechanism for the development of resistance to GC driving by uncontrolled TH17 response and neutrophils induced by chronic stress. Understanding the interactions between neutrophils, Th17 cells, and GCs is essential for developing targeted therapies for diseases resistant to GC, such as MS, TBI, and encephalomyelitis.

Introduction

In humans, neutrophils represent the most abundant leukocytes in peripheral circulation, playing a crucial role in the immune system [1]. Neutrophils represent 50–70% of blood leukocytes in healthy humans; however, they constitute only 10–30% of circulating leukocytes in rodents [2]. The presence of granulocytes colony-stimulating factor (G-CSFs) stimulates the differentiation of hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) into myeloid progenitors that later will differentiate in neutrophils, a process known as granulopoiesis. In homeostasis, the BM generates 1011 neutrophils per day, and this rate can increase to 1012 per day during bacterial infection [3]. Neutrophils have a short half-life in the blood, approximately 1.5 h in murine and 8 h in humans [4, 5]. Neutrophils are indispensable for the control of bacterial, fungi, and viral infections due to their different effector actions, including phagocytosis, production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and release of digestive enzymes, toxic substances, proteases, pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, and neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) [6]. Nevertheless, the impairment of neutrophil migration and function participates in the greater severity of bacterial infections, including sepsis [7]. On the other hand, excessive or dysregulated neutrophil recruitment and activation are also capable of inflicting tissue damage and exacerbating inflammation [8]. Studies have shown that neutrophils play a significant role in neuroinflammation and contribute to the dysfunction of central nervous system barriers, as observed in multiple sclerosis (MS), stroke, and Alzheimer’s disease [9].

GCs are stress hormones mostly synthesized by the adrenal glands. GCs wield robust anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive actions, influencing various aspects of immune cell functions. Nevertheless, patients can be resistant to GC therapy, such as MS [10]. It is well known that the exacerbated neutrophil recruitment and activation induced by a Th17 response is one of the factors responsible for resistance to GC therapy [11]. In this review, we will focus on neutrophil biology, from their production in the BM, migration, and functions in the inflammatory site. Moreover, we will also propose that chronic stress-induced local accumulation of neutrophils triggered by uncontrolled Th17 response as a mechanism for resistance to GC therapy in CNS diseases (Fig. 1).

GC-induced neutrophilia and GC resistance in CNS diseases: GCs promote neutrophilia and enhance the positive feedback loop between Th17 response and neutrophils, contributing to CNS diseases that are resistant to GC therapy, such as multiple sclerosis, TBI, and encephalomyelitis. Understanding these mechanisms may guide the development of new therapeutic strategies. CNS, central nervous system; GC, glucocorticoid; TBI, traumatic brain injury.

Granulopoiesis and Release of Neutrophils from BM to Peripheral Blood

The hematopoietic system is hierarchically organized, and all its cell types originated from self-renewing HSCs. Neutrophil production by HSCs occurs in BM in a process knowledge as granulopoiesis [12, 13]. In the BM, granulopoiesis produces granulocytes, which is one of the first lines of defense of innate immunity. It consists of a sound process, regulated by a network composed of transcription factors, cytokines, growth factors and receptors, and microenvironmental signals that ensure the maintenance of homeostasis and response to pathogens, inflammatory processes, and systemic stressors [14]. Granulopoiesis started with HSC differentiation in multipotent progenitor cells followed by subsequent differentiations in lymphoid-primed multipotent progenitor cells and granulocyte-monocyte progenitor cells. The granulocyte progenitor cell gradually matures under the influence of different cytokines and growth factors in the BM microenvironment into neutrophils [15]. The last stage of neutrophil differentiation in the BM is the non-proliferative stage, comprising immature and mature neutrophils. Morphologically, immature and mature neutrophils showed a band-shaped and segmented nucleus, respectively [16]. Furthermore, several surface markers are used to define the mature status of non-proliferative neutrophils since morphological and histochemical observations are subjective. The differential expression of the CXC chemokine receptor and the cluster of differentiation allowed discrimination of immature and mature neutrophils. In mice, immature and mature neutrophils are CXCR2−CXCR4+CD101− and CXCR2+CXCR4−CD101+, respectively; however, human immature and mature neutrophils express CXCR4+CD10− and CXCR4−CD10+, respectively [17]. The G-CSF is the primary growth factor involved in granulopoiesis. The lack of G-CSF or its receptor induces defects in neutrophil granulopoiesis, resulting in chronic neutropenia associated with reduced neutrophil progenitors in the BM [18, 19].

Humans and mice retain a large storage pool of mature neutrophils in BM for up to 4–6 days, with a BM-to-blood ratio of 24:1 in mice, constituting BM a reserve for mature neutrophils [20]. The stromal cells derived factor 1 (SDF-1/CXCL12):CXCR4 axis regulates neutrophil retention in BM, despite mature neutrophils lacking surface CXCR4 [21]. This is possible because mature neutrophils from BM exhibited high intracellular levels of CXCR4 that are quickly upregulated after activation with SDF-1/CXCL12 [22]. BM stromal cells express SDF-1/CXCL12 [23], while BM endothelial cells and osteoblasts express CXCR2 ligands (IL-8/CXCL1, GROβ/CXCL2) [24, 25], creating a gradient for mature neutrophil mobilization to the periphery during homeostasis. In addition, knockout mice to the membrane-proximal region of the cytoplasmic domain of G-CSFR showed a reduction of neutrophil migration from the BM into the circulation, indicating an important role of G-CSF in the neutrophil mobilization from BM into the bloodstream [26]. G-CSF reduces SDF-1/CXCL12 production by BM stromal cells and CXCR4 expression in myeloid cells [27, 28], promoting neutrophil migration from BM to blood. Furthermore, G-CSF also induced GROβ/CXCL2 expression by BM endothelial cells [29], activating CXCR2 on mature neutrophils in the BM. Bone marrow plays a fundamental role in the immune response and during high and rapid demand for neutrophils in the blood, such as bleeding or severe infection, increasing neutrophils output several-fold above steady-state levels. This phenomenon is known as emergency granulopoiesis, which is characterized by the accelerated expansion of myeloid progenitors, mobilization of hematopoietic stem cells, and abbreviated maturation of neutrophils, often resulting in the release of immature cells into the circulation. Cytokines such as G-CSF, IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, as well as stimuli derived from PAMPs and DAMPs, remodel the hematopoietic niche and activate transcriptional programs mediated primarily by C/EBPβ and STAT3. This process ensures a rapid response in containing pathogens but, when persistently activated, contributes to immunopathology in conditions such as sepsis, cancer, autoimmune diseases, and chronic stress [30, 31].

In the case of severe infection, a high demand for effector cells stimulates emergency granulopoiesis in the bone marrow, leading to the release of immature neutrophils and the expansion of myeloid progenitors. Neutrophils recognize the pathogen sensing, through pattern recognition receptors such as Toll-like receptors, switches from steady state to emergency granulopoiesis. In BM, the activation of PRRs by hematopoietic cells and non-hematopoietic cells produces elevated levels of G-CSF that induce emergency granulopoiesis in the myeloid stem cells. In addition, the activation of PRRs induces a high production of CCR2 ligands in the periphery [26]. The association of CCR2 activation in the BM neutrophils and the inhibition of the SDF-1/CXCL12-CXCR4 axis by G-CSF culminates in the high migration of neutrophils from BM into the bloodstream in the emergency granulopoiesis, resulting in blood neutrophilia.

Neutrophil Behavior in the Peripheral Blood

In the blood, neutrophils are distributed into the free-flowing pool and the marginal pool, which consists of cells not recovered by blood sampling [32, 33]. This concept was initially described in pioneering studies with healthy volunteers who received infusions of radiolabeled autologous neutrophils, where approximately 51% of the infused neutrophils disappeared from circulation immediately, suggesting the existence of a marginal pool of neutrophils [33]. Neutrophils represent the mobile and transient fraction of the innate immune response, exhibiting highly dynamic behavior, alternating between the circulating pool and the marginated pool, where they remain transiently attached to the vascular endothelium. During their brief stay in circulation, these cells continuously patrol the microvasculature, rapidly interacting with chemotactic signals and endothelial molecules that regulate adhesion, rolling, and migration [33]. The expression of integrins, selectins, and chemotactic receptors allows neutrophils to enter an activated state and be recruited to infectious sites within minutes. During acute inflammation or systemic stress, the accelerated release of immature neutrophils from the bone marrow alters the circulating profile, contributing to exacerbated inflammatory responses. Thus, peripheral blood neutrophils act as a strategic corridor where they balance immune surveillance, rapid response, and functional readiness, performing continuous patrolling of the microvasculature [34, 35].

This marginated pool represents a dynamic population of neutrophils that can be mobilized back into the circulating pool by stimuli like exercise and adrenaline [36]. The characterization of the marginated pool was further refined with the identification of two neutrophil subpopulations in the splenic red pulp: mature and motile Ly6Ghigh neutrophils and immature and immobilized Ly6GintCD117+CD49d+ neutrophils [37]. These neutrophils play distinct roles in tissue surveillance, with motile neutrophils continuously inspecting the environment and immobilized neutrophils remaining stationary in a perivascular position. This study confirmed the presence of the marginated pool and suggested specialized functions for these subpopulations. Since neutrophils are short-lived in circulation, they require proper turnover to safeguard the host from unintended inflammatory damage. During the steady state, nonlytic neutrophil death pathways predominate, promoting anti-inflammatory responses by retaining granular and cytoplasmic components within the cell. In a nonlytic neutrophil death during the steady state, neutrophils become senescent and age and then die by apoptosis [38, 39]. Under homeostatic conditions, neutrophils have high levels of L-selectin on the cell membrane (CD62L), which progressively decreases during their survival, while levels of CXCR4 increase [40]. These changes in the cell membrane allow the aging neutrophils (CXCR4+CD62L−) to migrate back to BM via the SDF-1/CXCL12-CXCR4 pathway [41]. Upon their return, senescent neutrophils undergo apoptosis and are phagocytized by stromal macrophages [39].

Macrophages in the liver and spleen also eliminate senescent neutrophils. However, the molecular mechanism involved in this elimination is still not fully understood [23]. In the liver, aging neutrophils are phagocytosed by Kupffer cells, facilitated by P-selectin expression in hepatic endothelial cells [21, 42, 43]. In the spleen, neutrophil clearance occurs in the marginal zone, where there are many circulating macrophages [38]. Intravital microscopy images of the spleen treated with AMD3100 (CXCR4 antagonist) showed an increase in the number of splenic neutrophils; however, there was no evidence of activation or changes in CXCR4 expression on neutrophils. This suggests that the clearance process of senescent neutrophils in the spleen is independent of CXCR4 [44].

Neutrophil Migration to Inflammatory Site and Immune Response

Neutrophil migration from the bloodstream into tissues is crucial for initiating inflammation after tissue injury. This process involves adhesion molecules, cytokines, and proteases, with chemoattractant molecules and G-protein-coupled receptors playing central roles. Neutrophil recruitment begins with selectin-mediated binding, resulting in endothelial cell reorganization and transient “rolling” interaction with endothelium. Subsequent activation by chemokines, cytokines, and PAMPs triggers activation of integrins such as VLA-4 (CD49D/CD29), MAC-1 (CD11b/CD18), and LFA-1 (CD11a/CD18) promote firm adhesion by ICAM-1 expressed on the endothelial surface. Once activated, neutrophils undergo morphological changes, allowing them to migrate or crawl along the endothelial surface and intercellular junctions [45–47]. After crossing the basement membrane, neutrophils respond to a chemotactic gradient produced in response to infection or tissue damage. The neutrophil chemoattractant agents can be categorized into chemotactic lipids (e.g., leukotriene B4 or LTB4), formyl peptides (e.g., N-formyl-Met-Leu-Phe or fMLP), complement anaphylatoxins (C3a and C5a), and chemokines (CXCL1 to CXCL3 and CXCL5 to CXCL8) [33, 48]. Furthermore, neutrophil adopts an “amoeboid” migration style, involving cellular deformation, actin polymerization, and structural tissue reorganization, releasing proteases to break down matrix components [45–47].

Neutrophils orchestrate innate and adaptive immune responses, contributing to acute and chronic inflammation. Their functions include pathogen recognition and phagocytosis, production of ROS, release of antimicrobial peptides and NETs, and regulation of inflammation [49, 50]. During phagocytosis, cellular debris or microbial pathogens are engulfed by phagosomes, which fuse with granules containing microbicidal proteins. The NADPH oxidase, located within the phagosome membrane, generates ROS, toxic to bacteria. Alternatively, neutrophils can release ROS, antimicrobial granule proteins, and NETs into the extracellular space [50]. NETs, composed of DNA and proteins like myeloperoxidase and neutrophil elastases, immobilize pathogens and facilitate phagocytosis [50–52].

During the resolution phase, the host microbiome contributes to the nonlytic neutrophil death, including apoptosis, preserving homeostasis. This process links microbial elimination to the accelerated clearance of neutrophils through efferocytosis [47]. Efferocytosis is dependent upon ROS and contributes to a noninflammatory death pathway that utilizes reduced oxygen tension within inflamed or infected tissue to prolong neutrophil survival to enhance pathogen consumption [50]. Another homeostatic death of neutrophils mechanism is autophagy, essential for the clearance of many internalized pathogens, including Streptococcus pneumoniae [50], Leishmania donovani [51], and adherent invasive Escherichia coli [52].

Studies highlight the involvement of neutrophils in neurodegenerative conditions such as MS, which is classically associated with autoreactive T lymphocytes and microglia/monocytes; however, clinical evidence demonstrates that neutrophils contribute to the recruitment of inflammatory cells to the CNS, disruption of the blood-brain barrier, formation of NETs, and modulation of the pro-inflammatory microenvironment, influencing disease activity, tissue damage, and possibly progression to chronic inflammation [53–55]. In this context, the CXCR2 receptor plays a critical role in directing neutrophil migration to inflammatory sites within the central nervous system during MS [56]. According to Khaw et al. [56], selective deletion of Cxcr2 gene in neutrophils in a murine model of MS (experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis) led to significant clinical improvement during both acute and chronic phases of the disease. This was accompanied by reduced neuronal damage and lower production of ROS, indicating that CXCR2 signaling is a key driver of neutrophil-mediated neurotoxicity [56].

In addition to MS, other neurological conditions characterized by inflammation and tissue damage have also been widely investigated. Among them, traumatic brain injury, where neutrophils infiltrate the meninges and parenchyma, contributing to the initial response and secondary injury, (i) promoting disruption of the blood-brain barrier via MMPs, elastase, and ROS; (ii) forming NETs that exacerbate neuronal damage, thrombosis, and compromise vascular repair; (iii) altering the systemic immune response [57–59]. Which triggers a neuroinflammatory cascade and degenerative processes following the initial insult, and encephalomyelitis, frequently employed in experimental models (such as experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis) to elucidate the immunological mechanisms underlying demyelination, stand out. Both share common features with MS, particularly regarding the involvement of neutrophils recognized as important mediators of neuroinflammation and tissue injury [57, 60, 61].

Effect of GCs on Neutrophil Biology

GCs are a class of steroid hormones, either endogenously produced or synthesized in a laboratory. Widely used in the treatment of various diseases due to their anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive capacity, their side effects and resistance to GC therapy have been widely explored [62]. GCs act by binding to their specific receptor (NR3C1) and subsequently regulating gene expression [63]. Interaction of the receptor-corticoid complex with specific regions called GC response elements results in the upregulation of anti-inflammatory proteins, repression of pro-inflammatory proteins in the cytosol, and metabolic reprogramming [64–66].

Transactivation refers to the process by which GCs activate gene expression, including annexin-1, MKP-1, and TSC22D3. The GCs diffuse through the cell membrane and bind to the GR in the cytoplasm [67, 68]. This process is crucial for the anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive actions of GCs, making them effective in treating various inflammatory and autoimmune conditions [69, 70]. The understanding of the mechanisms of transactivation and the role of GR isoforms, such as GRα and GRβ, is essential for developing targeted therapies for diseases characterized by altered GC sensitivity, such as asthma, rheumatoid arthritis, MS, and certain cancers [69, 71–73]. The GRα isoform is the classic and functional receptor, while the GRβ isoform does not bind GCs and acts as a dominant-negative inhibitor of GRα [68, 69, 71]. It is well known that the knock-down GRβ in monocytes showed enhanced steroid response to dexamethasone treatment [67]. The GRα is usually widely expressed in different cell types and tissues while GRβ has a lower expression, but in conditions such as chronic rhinosinusitis the expression of GRβ was upregulated [73]. Additional mechanism of resistance to GC includes (i) post-translational modifications of GR, including modification of S-203 alone or simultaneously with S-211, that can halt the translocation of the receptor [74, 75]; (ii) acetylation of K154, that directly impacts the stability of GR activation [76]; (iii) acetylation by CLOCK, that reduces its binding to GRE [77]; (iv) SUMOlylation at Lys-277 and Lys-293, that downregulates GR activity [78]. Furthermore, it has been observed that MS patients who are responsive to GCs treatment exhibit higher serum levels of GRα and lower levels of FKBP5, underscoring the importance of understanding the different isoforms of GR for the development of targeted therapies [79].

Transrepression is another mechanism by which GCs exert their anti-inflammatory effects, distinct from transactivation. Instead of binding directly to DNA, the GC-GR complex interacts with other transcription factors, such as AP-1 and NF-κB, which participate in promoting the transcription of pro-inflammatory genes, such as TNF, IL-1β, and NOS2. This interaction inhibits the transcription activity of these transcription factors, thereby reducing the inflammatory response [80–82]. Although GCs possess an anti-inflammatory action in most inflammatory cells, their effects on neutrophils may vary depending on the concentration and time of exposure, even leading to neutrophilia. In resting conditions, granulopoiesis is controlled by various physiological mechanisms, including daily oscillations and metabolic and cell-intrinsic aging processes [83], suggesting that endogenous GCs have an important role in the production of neutrophils in BM. In fact, mice implanted with a corticosterone tablet showed increased serum GC levels in parallel to an increase in more than 40% of neutrophils in the BM, confirming the induction of granulopoiesis by GCs, without interfering with the ability of these granulocytes to produce ROS, undergo chemotaxis or bacteria phagocytosis [84]. The induction of granulopoiesis by GCs was related to their capacity to promote the differentiation of granulocyte-macrophage progenitors into neutrophils [85, 86]. This effect can be related to the ability of GC to improve G-CSF-induced granulopoiesis by synergistic activation of STAT5 [86]. Furthermore, a single dose of dexamethasone increased the BM release of neutrophils [87]. In addition, GCs inhibited the expression of CXCR4 in the mesenchymal stem cells [88] and downregulated SDF-1/CXCL12 expression and secretion by BM stromal cells [85, 89]. Likewise, GC raises the expression of CXCR2 in human neutrophils in vitro. This evidence suggests that the inhibition of the SDF-1/CXCL12-CXCR4 axis and the increase in the expression of CXCR2 in BM neutrophils can be the mechanism related to the GC-induced neutrophil release from BM [87, 88]. Besides the effects of GCs on granulopoiesis, these hormones also shift neutrophils from the marginated to the circulating pool and increase the half-life of these cells in the circulation. It is well known that GCs increase the survival of neutrophils not by decreasing pro-apoptotic factors but rather by increasing antiapoptotic factors such as Mcl-1 and IAPs [90].

Despite direct effects of GCs over neutrophil population, intrinsic indirect effects might be related to the observed neutrophilia [91, 92]. Chronic stress reshapes innate immunity, and the importance of neutrophils in the inflammatory response is undeniable. Nevertheless, a persistent inflammatory response can act as a trigger for inflammatory stress, which induces complex and paradoxical effects on the inflammatory response. Persistent activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and the sympathetic nervous system leads to the continuous release of GC and catecholamines. Under basic conditions, a significant proportion of neutrophils are found adhered to the microvascular endothelium; however, GCs reduce the expression of molecules important for endothelial adhesion, such as ICAM-1 and E-selectin, detaching neutrophils from the endothelium and returning them to circulation [79, 80]. Consequently, increased GC led to a rapid increase in the number of circulating neutrophils. Therefore, individuals under chronic stress exhibit relative neutrophilia, not due to increased granulopoiesis but rather due to redistribution. Prolonged exposure to GC promotes desensitization of GR, dysfunction of the immunoneuroendocrine feedback system, and alteration of the balance between regulatory and effector immune cells. As a result, the body exhibits a state of low-grade inflammation, with the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, alterations in neutrophil function such as impaired chemotaxis and tissue migration, impaired inflammatory resolution, and a greater propensity for tissue damage. Thus, chronic stress not only weakens innate and adaptive immunity but also sustains an inflammatory environment that is associated with metabolic, cardiovascular, and neurodegenerative diseases [93, 94].

Although GCs exert effects on neutrophil biology, the HPA axis is regulated by immunological signals and, during stress neutrophils, can indirectly or directly influence cortisol production [95–97]. During inflammatory processes, neutrophils can migrate to adrenal tissue and locally produce inflammatory cytokines and ROS, directly modulating adrenal cell function and steroidogenesis [98–100]. Indirectly, neutrophils produce pro-steroidogenic cytokines, including IL-1β and IL-6. These cytokines increase corticotropin-releasing hormone and adrenocorticotropic hormone secretion by hypothalamus and pituitary, respectively, raise glucocorticoids production [96–98]. During chronic stress, interactions occur between nervous, endocrine, and immune systems, directly affecting HPA axis. The persistent activation of HPA axis leads to changes in the neuroendocrine set point, altering glucocorticoid secretion and the sensitivity of its receptors in peripheral tissues [99–101]. Prolonged stress promotes hyporesponsiveness of immune system, in which the neutrophil inflammatory response is attenuated. Furthermore, chronic stress-induced low-grade inflammation contributes to an increase in the negative feedback of HPA axis, promoting a reduction in the systemic glucocorticoid levels [97, 102–105]. In this context, reducing cortisol levels and resolving neutrophil inflammation could restore responsiveness to exogenous glucocorticoid therapy.

Chronic Stress and Neutrophil Activation by the IL-17 Pathway

Prolonged exposure to GC becomes neutrophil hyperresponsiveness, displaying increased survival, enhanced degranulation, and heightened release of NETs [106, 107]. GC can promote human and murine Th17 cell differentiation [108], which can lead to increased neutrophilia due to activation of Th17 cells and consequent release of G-CSF, TNF-α, and CXCL8, inducing granulopoiesis and activation and recruitment of neutrophils [108, 109]. The NET release and their histones promote Th17 cell differentiation [110], and the conditioned supernatants of neutrophils stimulated with IFN-γ plus lipopolysaccharide-induced chemotaxis of Th17 cells, which was sensitive to treatment with α-CCL2 and αCCL-20 [96]. Chronic stress-induced activation of the HPA axis and sympathetic system amplifies neutrophil recruitment to peripheral tissues, where their interactions with antigen-presenting cells further potentiate IL-23-dependent stabilization of the IL-17 pathway. Together, these mechanisms reveal how chronic stress reprograms neutrophils toward a phenotype that actively sustains IL-17-mediated inflammation, linking neuroendocrine stress responses to dysfunction of immune activation [111–113].

In some chronic diseases, including depression, asthma and MS, there is an increase in the number of Th17 cells and neutrophils and in the levels of IL-17A in the blood and/or tissue that occurs in parallel to the development of resistance to GCs, which makes treatment even more difficult [109, 114, 115]. Therefore, we might want to consider a strong feed forward loop between Th17 cell activity and neutrophils can be enhanced by GCs [114, 116]. Consequently, we can speculate that the GC-induced uncontrolled Th17 response accompanied by neutrophilia can evoke the development of GC resistance. In line with this hypothesis, sleep deprivation in mice, which is a model characterized by increasing the circulating levels of corticosterone [117], induced Th17 profile response in parallel with extensive neutrophilia in the BAL after antigen stimulation, which was insensitive to dexamethasone treatment [116, 118].

Despite our focus on Th17 cells, ILC3 also has an important source of IL-17 and could be directly involved with neutrophil accumulation and resistance to GC therapy [119]. In MS, it has been observed that CNS-infiltrating ILC3s play a crucial role in antigen presentation, promoting pro-inflammatory T-cell responses within the CNS and driving the development of MS-like disease in murine models [120]. As a disclosure, although our focus is on the immune cells loop, airway epithelial cells also produce G-CSF after stimulation with IL-1β and TNF. Moreover, the combination with dexamethasone enhances its release, highlighting additional cell-cell communication involved in the GC-induced neutrophilia [121].

We could also highlight the role of single-nucleotide polymorphisms of GR, more specifically rs37972 and rs37973, decreasing the GC-induced transcript 1 gene (GLCCI1) expression in lymphoblastoid cell lines [115]. It is not fully clear the best way to restore GC effects in patients’ resistance to GC therapy; however, some strategies have been studied. Antibody-drug conjugates designed to target neutrophils are proposed as an option for cancer patients with corticoid resistance, and ILC3 is also considered a potential target in some cases of GC resistance [119]. Indirect vitamin D supplementation was made in asthmatic patients increasing GRα and reducing IL-17 and IL-4, which could lead to the restoration of GC effectiveness [122]. Targeting IL-17 with sonelokimab, vunakizumab, or ixekizumab (anti-IL-17 antibodies) is also an alternative [118]. Preliminary studies speculate on the involvement of epigenetic mechanisms in modulating the response to GC in the context of neutrophil function. Wiencke et al. [122] demonstrated that exposure of human neutrophils to dexamethasone induces specific alterations in DNA methylation, resulting in the epigenetic index called the Neutrophil Dexamethasone Methylation Index (NDMI). Alterations observed in regions linked to transcriptional regulation and sites recognized as potential GR-binding sites, indicating that GC can modulate the neutrophilic response through epigenetic DNA remodeling [123]. Another study observed that in an experimental model of steroid-resistant neutrophilic inflammation, dysfunction of histone deacetylases is associated with loss of sensitivity to GC. This alteration in the balance between histone acetylation and deacetylation compromises the ability of the GR to repress pro-inflammatory genes, suggesting that epigenetic modifications in chromatin may contribute to resistance to GC in neutrophils, although the data are largely associative and do not conclusively demonstrate a direct pharmacological “resistance” mechanism [123].

Conclusion

Prolonged exposure to GC, whether resulting from continuous pharmacological use or persistent activation of the HPA axis in individuals subjected to chronic stress, promotes desensitization of GR, dysfunction of the immunoneuroendocrine feedback system, and disruption of the balance between regulatory and effector immune cell populations. As a result, the body develops a state of low-grade chronic inflammation characterized by the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and alterations in neutrophil function, including increased neutrophil egress from the bone marrow into the circulation, redistribution of marginated neutrophils into the circulating pool, and prolonged neutrophil lifespan. Thus, chronic stress not only weakens innate and adaptive immunity but also sustains an inflammatory environment associated with metabolic, cardiovascular, and neurodegenerative diseases.

Beyond these direct effects on the neutrophil population, indirect mechanisms also contribute to GC-induced neutrophilia. Studies show that GC promotes the differentiation of human and murine Th17 cells, modulating the release of mediators such as G-CSF, TNF-α, and CXCL8, which induce granulopoiesis, activation, and recruitment of neutrophils. Moreover, the positive feedback loop between Th17 cell activity and neutrophils is reinforced by GC. In various diseases resistance to GC therapy, including MS, a Th17-skewed response is observed in parallel with a robust accumulation of neutrophils.

In summary, in individuals experiencing chronic stress, the development of resistance to GC therapy is driven by an uncontrolled Th17 response accompanied by GC-induced neutrophilia, thereby narrowing the therapeutic window and contributing to persistent inflammation in chronic CNS diseases. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the mechanisms by which stress conditions promote this feedback loop between Th17 cells and neutrophils may aid in identifying new molecular pathways and developing innovative and effective therapeutic strategies for treating chronic CNS disorders that do not respond to exogenous GC.
