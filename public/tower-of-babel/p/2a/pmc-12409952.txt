Atypical cadherin FAT1 promotes tumorigenesis by suppressing autophagic cell death in glioblastoma under hypoxia or nutrient stress
Authors: Yakhlesh Gupta, Sanjeev Goswami, Manvi Arora, Nargis Malik, Khushboo Irshad, Archismita Kundu, Srinivas H. Gowda, Mani Kapoor, Shruti Gupta, Tapas Chandra Nag, Vaishali Suri, Ashish Suri, Parthaprasad Chattopadhyay, Subrata Sinha, Kunzang Chosdol
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAutophagy, a conserved intracellular degradation process, plays dual roles in cancer, promoting survival under stress or mediating cell death through deregulated autophagy. Atypical cadherin FAT1 functions as an oncogene or tumor suppressor in a context-dependent manner. Our previous work identifies the oncogenic role of FAT1 in glioblastoma. Deregulated autophagy has been documented in glioma. Here, we investigated the role of FAT1 in regulating autophagy and its implications for glioblastoma growth and progression.MethodsCRISPR-Cas9 mediated FAT1 knockout was generated in glioblastoma (U87MG and LN229) and other cancers such as hepatocellular carcinoma (HepG2 and HUH7) and pancreatic adenocarcinoma (MIAPaca-2 and Panc-1) cells. The cell viability and growth under hypoxia ± serum deprivation were analyzed by 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT), colony formation, and Annexin V–FITC assays. Autophagy markers were assessed by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), Western blot, immunocytochemistry (ICC), and immunohistochemistry (IHC). Autophagosomes were visualized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and puncta formation was analyzed by transfecting the cells with pEGFP-LC3. Autophagy flux was evaluated by analyzing p62/SQSTM1 levels, and the GFP/RFP ratio using pMRX-IP-GFP-LC3-RFP-LC3ΔG. In vivo, FAT1-knockout U87MG xenografts in nude mice were analyzed for tumor growth and autophagy marker expression. Surgically resected glioblastoma tumors from our hospital and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset were analyzed for autophagy marker expression and patient survival correlations.ResultsFAT1-knockout glioblastoma (U87MG and LN229) cells demonstrated reduced survival and colony numbers under normoxia and hypoxia with serum deprivation, facilitated by autophagy-dependent cell death. These cells exhibited upregulated autophagy markers, increased LC3 puncta, autophagosomes, and autophagy flux. FAT1-knockout glioblastoma cells showed decreased total and phospho-mTOR levels. FAT1-knockout xenografts showed reduced tumor progression with increased LC3II, Beclin1, and autophagosomes. Human glioblastoma tumors and TCGA glioblastoma data revealed an inverse expression correlation of FAT1 with LC3B/Beclin1, tumors with high-FAT1/low-LC3B expression were associated with poor patient survival. FAT1 also regulated autophagy in hepatocellular and pancreatic cancers.ConclusionOur findings indicate that FAT1 mediates pro-tumorigenic function by suppressing autophagic cell death in glioblastoma and other cancers. FAT1 may serve as a potential therapeutic adjuvant along with standard therapeutic regimens for treating cancers with high FAT1 expression having an oncogenic role.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11658-025-00783-x.

Background

Autophagy is a highly controlled cellular process accountable for the degradation and recycling of damaged organelles and cytoplasmic components through the formation of autophagosomes, which subsequently fuse with lysosomes for content clearance [1]. Under physiological and pathological conditions, autophagy process is critical for maintaining cellular homeostasis by inducing metabolic adaptation, particularly under stressful conditions, such as oxidative stress, hypoxia, and/or nutrient deprivation [1, 2]. Dysregulation of autophagy has been implicated in many diseases, including cancer. Autophagy exhibits dual roles in tumor biology, with both pro-tumorigenic and anti-tumorigenic properties, subject to the cellular conditions and the stage of the disease [3, 4]. Autophagy often suppresses tumor growth during the early stage of tumor development by averting the deposition of damaged organelles/proteins and helps maintain cellular homeostasis [5]. During advanced stage, the tumor cells exploit autophagy for survival by metabolic adaptation to overcome cellular stress, such as nutrient deprivation, oxidative stress, or hypoxia, and promote cancer cell survival [6]. Cell survival and tumor progression in solid tumors have been linked to autophagy with coordinated inactivation of apoptosis [7]. Studies have documented autophagy to have tumor-promoting roles in cancers, including glioblastoma. Autophagy has been documented to facilitate cancer cell survival and drug resistance in glioblastoma under cellular stress and promotes tumor progression [8]. High Beclin1 expression has been correlated with enhanced cell proliferation, resistance to apoptosis, and poor prognosis in glioma [9]. Similarly, increased LC3-II levels, reflecting elevated autophagic flux, have been observed in high-grade gliomas and are associated with resistance to therapy and increased tumor aggressiveness [10–12]. Inhibiting late-stage autophagy has been demonstrated to enhance apoptosis in glioblastoma cells owing to the accumulation of cytotoxic autophagic vacuoles [13, 14]. Both autophagy and apoptosis have a complex and interconnected functional role that influences tumor growth and response to therapy in a context-dependent manner [12, 15].

Conversely, the tumor-suppressive role of autophagy in glioma and other cancers have been documented. Studies in glioblastoma have shown that low expression of key autophagy-related genes, including Beclin1, LC3B, and ATGs, are correlated with tumor progression and poorer patient outcomes [16, 17]. This reduction in autophagy markers, and their correlation with tumor progression and poor prognosis, underscores the role of autophagy in suppressing malignancy [16]. Low autophagy protein markers have been observed in high-grade glioma compared with low-grade astrocytic tumors or normal brain tissues [16, 18]. Increased autophagy markers such as LC3 and Beclin1 were associated with better survival in glioblastoma patients with poor performance scores [19]. Suppression of proteins involved in autophagy inhibition have been associated with upregulated autophagy, leading to reduced tumor growth [20, 21].

A critical marker of impaired autophagic activity is the accumulation of p62, an autophagy substrate usually degraded during autophagy [22]. Elevated p62 levels reflect defective autophagic flux, contributing to oncogenesis. Experimental studies have demonstrated that mice deficient in autophagy-essential genes develop multiple liver cancers. These tumors display increased resistance to hypoxic stress, concomitant with p62 accumulation [23], suggesting a failure to initiate autophagic cell death under stress conditions.

Autophagic cell death has been observed during developmental stages as well as in cancers [24, 25]. This autophagic cell death is morphologically and mechanistically distinct from apoptosis and necroptosis [26]. The interplay between autophagic cell death and other cell death mechanisms highlights its complexity as a cellular process [22]. Accumulation of autophagosomes in dying cells is a hallmark feature of autophagic cell death, setting it apart from other forms of programmed cell death [27]. Inhibiting apoptotic pathways alone often fails to prevent cancer cell death, as cancer cells can rely on autophagy for survival. However, studies have shown that blocking essential autophagy gene(s) (ATGs) rescues cancer cells from death, highlighting the important role of balanced autophagy in sustaining cancer cell viability [27]. Several experimental evidences increasingly support the role of autophagic cell death in cancer cell elimination [16, 28–30]. This underscores the involvement of autophagic processes to cell death, independent of apoptosis or necrosis.

The utility of autophagy in cancer remains complex, in balancing tumor suppression and promotion. While diminished autophagic activity enables tumor progression, enhancing autophagic processes potentially sensitizes tumor cells to stress-induced death. Further research is needed to unravel the precise molecular mechanisms underlying the regulation of autophagy and its implications for therapeutic strategies targeting autophagic pathways in glioblastoma and other cancers.

Glioblastoma, the most aggressive astrocytic tumor, is characterized by poor prognosis and a median survival of only 12–15 months post-diagnosis, with less than 10% patients surviving beyond 5 years [31]. Despite decades of research, therapeutic advancements have been limited. Current treatment regimens, including temozolomide, are often compromised by drug resistance, and targeted therapies have shown minimal efficacy, providing only marginal improvements in patient outcomes. FAT1 belongs to a nonclassical cadherin protein family and plays crucial roles in developmental processes and cell–cell communication [32, 33]. High levels of FAT1 transcripts are primarily found in kidney, lungs, and eye epithelia during fetal development [34]. It is critical for regulating cell migration, tight junction integrity, and actin organization [33, 35], as well as it regulates growth and planar cell polarity [32]. In Drosophila, “fat” functions upstream of the Hippo pathway to regulate growth and influence planar cell polarity [36].

In cancer, FAT1 demonstrates context-dependent roles, acting as both a tumor-suppressor and an oncogene. Studies have demonstrated a tumor suppressor role of FAT1 in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma [37], breast cancer [38], and esophageal cancer [39].

Conversely, high FAT1 expression correlates with poor prognosis in breast cancer [40], leukemia [41], oral squamous cell carcinoma [42], pancreatic cancer [43], hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [44], and colon cancer [45]. It correlates positively with tumor grading and proliferation, indicating its importance in tumor progression. The effect of FAT1 expression on cell migration/invasion has been documented, affecting oral squamous cell carcinoma, triple-negative breast cancer, etc. [40, 42]. Our previous research identified FAT1 as a critical oncogenic driver of glioblastoma progression, promoting pro-tumorigenic inflammation [46], migration/invasion [46, 47], epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) stemness [48], immune evasion [49], and suppressing tumor suppressors PDCD10/PDCD4 [46, 50], contributing to tumor aggressiveness and poor survival outcomes [49, 50]. FAT1 also enhances hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF1α)–epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) expression, which is frequently overexpressed in glioblastoma, facilitating tumor invasiveness [47]. These findings reflect that FAT1 has an oncogenic role and is a promising therapeutic target in different cancers. Targeting FAT1 has been explored in cancers using monoclonal antibodies against FAT1 in colorectal cancer [45, 51] and use of verteporfin in suppressing FAT1 in gastric cancer [52]. Knockdown of FAT1 sensitizes oral squamous cell carcinoma cells to cisplatin [53], and knockdown of FAT1 in glioma and other cancers has been found to downregulate migration invasion and stemness of tumor cells [54].

Glioblastoma tumors exhibits significant resistance to the standard therapeutic regimens, underscoring the necessity for novel combinatorial treatment strategies. Given the observed pro-tumorigenic role of FAT1 in glioblastoma and the association of glioblastoma with reduced autophagy-related gene expression [16, 55, 56], in the current study, we examined the novel role of FAT1 in regulating autophagy in glioblastoma, and how FAT1 protects glioblastoma cells from undergoing autophagic cell death under severe cellular stress. Using CRISPR-Cas9-mediated FAT1-knockout glioblastoma cells (U87MG and LN229), we evaluated the effects of FAT1 depletion on cell survival and clonogenic potential under normoxia and severe hypoxia conditions with serum deprivation. Additionally, in vivo studies using nude mouse xenografts were done to evaluate the effect of FAT1 on tumor growth and autophagy. In support of the in vitro and in vivo studies, glioblastoma patient tumor samples were analyzed for expression correlation of FAT1 and autophagy-related genes and their correlation with the survival outcome of the patients, and the results corroborated with the TCGA dataset.

Methods

Cell culture, hypoxia, and low serum treatment

Cancer cell lines [glioblastoma cells (U87MG and LN229), hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells (HepG2 and HUH7), and pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD) cells (MIAPaca-2 and Panc-1)], and human embryonic kidney 293T (HEK293T) cells were procured from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), USA and maintained in standard culture conditions. For hypoxia (0.2% O2) treatment, cells were cultured in an Anoxomat anaerobic jar system (Advanced Instruments, USA) for 48 h and 6 days as per experimental need. Cells were collected after the appropriate time point and processed for RNA isolation using RNAiso Plus (Takara, Japan, Cat. no. 9109) and lysate preparation using Pierce radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (ThermoScientific, USA, cat. no. 89901), etc. U87MG and LN229 cells were cultured in low serum concentrations (5% serum and 1% serum) for 6 days under both normoxia and hypoxia followed by MTT assay. For autophagic modulation cells were incubated with bafilomycinA1 (Cayman, USA, cat. no. 11038; 15 nm/ml) and/or Earl’s balanced salt solution (EBSS) (Merck, Germany, cat. no. E3024) for 4 h under normoxia and hypoxia, followed by lysate preparation.

Stable knockout clone generation in cancer cells

Single guide RNA (sgRNA) oligos specific for FAT1 were designed using Optimized Crispr Design online software (www.crispr.mit.edu), and the three best-scored sgRNAs were selected (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Commercially synthesized FAT1 and control sgRNAs were annealed, phosphorylated, and cloned into lentiCRISPR v2 plasmid (Addgene-98290, depositor: Brett Stringer’s lab) (Supplementary Fig. S1B). The recombinant plasmid was confirmed by PCR and sequencing. SgFAT1-lentiCRISPR v2/control-lentiCRISPR v2 with virus packaging plasmids pMD2.G (Addgene-12259, depositor: Didier Trono’s Lab) and psPAX2 (Addgene-12260, depositor-Didier Trono’s Lab) were transfected into HEK293T cells using PEIMax transfection reagent (Polysciences Inc., USA, cat. no. 24765; 3 μl/μg of DNA). Lentiviral particles were collected and infected to DEAE-Dextran (Merck, Germany, cat. no. D9885; 4 μg/ml) preincubated (24 h) glioblastoma, HCC, and PAAD cells. After 48 h, sgFAT1-lentiCRISPR v2/control-lentiCRISPR v2 virus infected cells were treated with puromycin (Merck, Germany, cat. no. P8833; 2–4 μg/ml) for 7–10 days for selection of positive clones. Positive clones were propagated and harvested for whole-cell lysate preparation. FAT1 expression was analyzed by Western blotting.

Transfection of cells with siRNA/plasmid

Cells were transfected with pEGFP-LC3 (human) plasmid (Addgene-24920, depositor: Toren Finkel’s lab), siLC3B (CST, USA, cat. no. 6313; 150 nM/transfection) or siControl (CST, USA, cat. no. 6568; 150 nM/transfection), using Lipofectamine-3000™ reagent (Invitrogen, USA, cat. no. L3000015; 3 μl/μg of nucleic acid) in reduced serum medium, Opti-MEM (Gibco, USA, cat. no. 31985070). To check the long-term effect of siLC3B on cell viability of U87MG and LN229 FAT1 knockout (sgFAT1) and control cells, the cells were transfected twice (at 0 h and 72 h) with siLC3B, to maintain the downregulation of LC3 expression for 6 days. Cells were subjected to fluorescence microscopy/MTT assay/lysate preparation for Western blotting, RNA isolation, cDNA preparation, and expression analysis by qPCR (thermal cycler RotorGeneQ, Qiagen, Germany). The details of primers are listed in Supplementary Table S6. Images from fluorescence microscopy (Nikon ECLIPSE Ti2, Japan) were analyzed for puncta formations.

MTT assay/cell viability assay

Cell viability was analyzed by MTT assay by seeding 5 × 103 cells per well in 96-well plates. After 12–16 h of incubation, FAT1 knockout and control cells were treated with (i) medium containing normal serum (10% serum) and low-serum (5% and 1% serum) under normoxia (20% O2) and hypoxia (0.2% O2) for 6 days and (ii) siRNA (siLC3B and siControl) under normoxia (20% O2) and hypoxia (0.2% O2) for 48 h. After desired incubation time, MTT (Merck, Germany, cat. no. 475989; 0.5 mg/ml) was added for 4 h followed by DMSO, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm, and 630 nm used as reference, using an Infinite 2000 Pro multimode reader (Tecan Life Sciences, Switzerland).

Colony formation assay

Anchorage-dependent growth of normoxic and hypoxic FAT1 knockout (sgFAT1) and control cells with/without siLC3B/siControl was analyzed. The cells (FAT1 knockout and control) were seeded (2 × 105 cells/well) in six-well plates. After 12–16 h of incubation, the cells were transfected with siLC3B/siControl and incubated under normoxia (20% O2) and hypoxia (0.2% O2) for 48 h. After 48 h, 3 × 104 cells were suspended in 1 ml complete DMEM with 0.3% low-melting-point agarose (Sigma, St. Louis, USA). The agarose–cell mixture was layered on top of a 0.6% complete medium–agarose mixture in six-well plates. After 3 weeks of incubation under standard culture conditions, colonies were stained and fixed using 0.05% Crystal Violate in methanol followed by imaging in the light microscope for counting and analysis of colonies.

Cell cycle analysis

FAT1-knockout (sgFAT1) and control U87MG and LN229 cells were synchronized for 8 h and cultured under normoxia (20% O2) and hypoxia (0.2% O2) for 6 days. The cells were trypsinized and fixed using 70% chilled ethanol for 20 min, followed by RNAse (10 mg/ml, ThermoScientific, cat. no. R1253) treatment for 15 min at room temperature. Cells were stained with propidium iodide (PI) (Merck, USA, cat. no. P4170; 10 μg/ml) in PI staining buffer (4 mM sodium citrate, pH 7.4) in the dark for 30 min at room temperature, followed by acquisition by flow cytometer (FACS Canto; BD Life Sciences, USA), and data were analyzed by using FlowJo software.

Apoptosis assay

FAT1-knockout (sgFAT1) and control cells were evaluated for apoptosis by Annexin V–FITC/PI staining. The cells were synchronized for 8 h, and cultured under normoxia (20% O2) and hypoxia (0.2% O2) for 48 h. The cells were trypsinized and fixed using 70% chilled ethanol for 20 min. Fixed cells were stained with Annexin V-FITC/PI in binding buffer as per the Apoptosis assay kit protocol (BioLegend, USA, cat. no. 640914), cells were acquired using a FACS Canto flow cytometer (BD Life Sciences, USA), and data were analyzed by using FlowJo software.

Immunocytochemistry (ICC) assay

Cellular expression of LC3 and p62 was analyzed in control and FAT1-knockout (sgFAT1) cells. Anti-LC3B (ThermoScientific, USA, cat. no. PA1-46286; 1:1000) and anti-p62 (BD Life Sciences, USA, cat. no. 610832; 1:500) antibodies were used. We seeded 2 × 103 cells in glass-bottomed black-wall 96-well plates (Greiner-Bio One, Austria, cat. no. 655981) for 48 h under normoxia (20% O2) and hypoxia (0.2% O2). The cells were fixed using chilled methanol for 20 min. Blocking was done by blocking buffer (1% BSA + 0.5% Triton X-100) for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were incubated with primary antibody in blocking buffer for 2 h at room temperature, followed by secondary antibodies anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor-488 (Invitrogen, USA, cat. no. 11008; 1:5000) and anti-mouse IgG Alexa Fluor-594 (Invitrogen, USA, cat. no. 11032; 1:5000), respectively, for 1 h at room temperature in blocking buffer, followed by 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) staining. Images were documented using a confocal microscope (Nikon A1 HD25, Japan), and mean fluorescence intensity was analyzed by using ImageJ software.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Autophagosomes in control and FAT1-knockout (sgFAT1) cells were assessed by TEM at the Sophisticated and Analytical Instruments Facility (SAIF), Department of Anatomy, AIIMS, New Delhi. Firstly, 3 × 105 cells were incubated in T25 cm2 flasks under normoxia (20% O2) and hypoxia (0.2% O2) for 48 h, followed by trypsinization and fixation of cells using 2.5% glutaraldehyde overnight at 4 ℃. Post-fixation, the cells were incubated in 1% osmium tetroxide at room temperature for 1 h. The samples were then dehydrated using ethanol and embedded in epoxy resin, and ultrathin sections were produced using an ultramicrotome. Sections were mounted on copper grids, and staining was done using uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Imaging was done by transmission electron microscopy (TECNAI G20 HR-TEM, ThermoScientific, USA), and the number of autophagosomes was analyzed.

Autophagic flux detection

Autophagic flux was analyzed under normoxia and hypoxia in control and FAT1-knockout (sgFAT1) cells as previously [57] mentioned. The plasmid pMRX-IP-GFP-LC3-RFP-LC3ΔG (Addgene-84572, depositor-Noboru Mizushima’s lab) was used. GFP-LC3–RFP-LC3ΔG is cleaved by ATG4 proteases into GFP-LC3 and RFP-LC3ΔG, the mutated product RFP-LC3ΔG will remain in the cytosol as internal control, and wildtype GFP-LC3 is degraded by active autophagic flux process. The cells were transiently transfected with the above plasmid using Lipofectamine-3000™ reagent (Invitrogen, USA, cat. no. L3000015; 3 μl/μg of nucleic acid) and incubated under normoxia and hypoxia for 48 h. Images were taken using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon ECLIPSE Ti2, Japan), and GFP/RFP fluorescence was visualized and the color ratio (GFP/RFP) used to estimate the autophagic flux. Fluorescence was quantified by using ImageJ software, and optical density (OD) was analyzed.

Tumor xenograft study

The in vivo nude mice experiment was carried out at the Animal Facility, Dabur Research Foundation, Ghaziabad, UP. Experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (IAEC), AIIMS, New Delhi (file no. 974/IAEC/16) and Committee for the Purpose of Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals (CPCSEA) at Dabur Research Foundation, Ghaziabad (proposal no. IAEC/55/1015). Healthy athymic female nude mice (Mus musculus, age 8–9 weeks, Hylasco Bio-Technology, Hyderabad) were randomly grouped (n = 8 in each group), with group 1 animals denominated as Control and group 2 animals denominated as sgFAT1 (FAT1 knockout). A total of 200 µl of control and sgFAT1 U87MG cells (1.0 × 107cells/100 µl) in serum-free medium with Matrigel (Corning, USA) in equal proportions (1:1) was injected subcutaneously in the right flank region. Body weight, clinical signs, and tumor volume were recorded twice weekly. On day 40, when the xenograft tumor size/volume in control animals reached its upper limit ( − 2000 mm3, as approved by IAEC, AIIMS), animals were sacrificed and xenograft tumors were resected and processed for Western blotting, TEM, and IHC. Body weight and tumor volume data were statistically analyzed by two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Statistical significance was analyzed by using GraphPad PRISM.

SDS-PAGE and western blotting

For SDS-PAGE and Western blotting, 40 μg of lysate were loaded on the gel along with HiMark Pre-stained protein ladder (Invitrogen, USA, cat. no. LC5699), and electrophoresis was done in Tris–glycine electrophoresis buffer using a running apparatus (Bio-Rad, Mini Protean). On the basis of the molecular weight of the target proteins, different percentage of Tris–glycine polyacrylamide gels were used. For detection of high molecular weight FAT1 protein (−508 kDa), a gradient resolving gel was prepared with the upper 70% comprising 6% acrylamide and the lower 30% comprising 10% acrylamide. Electrophoresis was performed at a constant 60 V for 5 h in chilled Tris–glycine running buffer, followed by electrotransfer of resolved proteins to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (MDI, INDIA, cat. no. SVFX8301XXXX101) at a constant voltage of 30 V at 4 °C overnight. For medium-sized proteins such as mTOR (−289 kDa), a 10% resolving gel was used, and for smaller proteins (less than 70 kDA) such as LC3B, Beclin1, and p62, a 12% resolving gel was used, and electrophoresis was done at 80 V for 4 h. Electrotransfer of resolved proteins was done to PVDF membranes (MDI, INDIA, cat. no. SVFX8301XXXX101) at a constant voltage of 30 V at 4 °C overnight. Blocking was performed using 5% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA) in 1 × TBS (pH 7.4) at 4 °C for 2 h to minimize background noise and improve specificity, particularly for high molecular protein such as FAT1 and phospho-mTOR. This was followed by incubation with primary antibody anti-FAT1 (Novus Biologicals, USA, cat. no. NBP2-32275, 1:500), anti-LC3B (CST, USA, cat. no. 2775; 1:1000), anti-Beclin1 (CST, USA, cat. no. 3495; 1:1000), anti-p62 (BD life sciences, USA, cat. no. 610832; 1:1000), anti-phospho-mTOR(ser2448) (CST, USA, cat. no. 5536; 1:1000), anti-mTOR (CST, USA, cat. no. 2983; 1:1000), anti-β-actin (abcam, USA, cat. no. ab8227; 1:1000), and anti-GAPDH (abcam, USA, cat. no. ab9485; 1:1000) at 4 °C overnight. The blot was then washed with 0.05% Tween-TBS (pH 7.4) thrice, 10 min each. Next, the blot was incubated with goat horseradish peroxidase (HRP) anti-rabbit (abcam, USA, ab6721; 1:5000) or anti-mouse antibody (abcam, USA, ab97023; 1:5000) at 4 °C for 2 h to maintain high specificity and reduce nonspecific binding during secondary antibody incubation. The blot was developed using Peirce ECL Western (ThermoScientific, USA, cat. no. 32016) and documented in an Azure Gel documentation system (Azure 200, Azure BioSystems, USA). Western blot experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates. Band intensity was quantified by using ImageJ software, and the expression of each target protein was normalized to the corresponding loading control (β-actin or GAPDH).

Glioblastoma patient tumor tissue

Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institute Ethics Committee (IECPG-111/28.02.2019). After obtaining due consent from glioblastoma patients, glioblastoma tumor tissues (n = 35) were collected from neurosurgery OT, AIIMS, New Delhi, for expression analysis at mRNA (qPCR) and protein (IHC) level. Histopathological diagnosis was done by Prof. Vaishali Suri at the Neuropathology lab, AIIMS, New Delhi. As per the WHO 2021 classification of central nervous system (CNS) tumors, glioblastoma, which are the tumors with isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) wild-type status, were taken for the study. Normal human total brain RNA (Takara, Japan, Cat. no. 636530) was used as a control for qPCR analysis.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

Histological analysis of target genes in glioblastoma and xenograft tumors was performed at the Neuropathology Laboratory, AIIMS, New Delhi. Briefly, tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, and antigen retrieval was done. Next, sections were incubated with Peroxidase Block and Protein Block reagents. Primary antibodies specific to FAT1 (Merck, Germany, cat. no.HPA023882; 1:50), LC3B (ThermoScientific, USA, cat. no. PA1-46286; 1:100), Beclin1 (ThermoScientific, USA, cat. no. PA1-16857; 1:100), and ULK1 (ThermoScientific, USA, cat. no. PA1-109212; 1:50) were applied overnight at 4 °C. Further, sections were incubated with Post Primary Block reagent and horseradish peroxidase-coupled Polymer secondary antibodies, followed by a liquid DAB Substrate Chromogen system. Visualization and scoring were done by Prof. Vaishali Suri at the Neuropathology lab, AIIMS, New Delhi. Images were quantified using ImageJ software, and optical density (OD) was analyzed.

In silico dataset analysis

The TCGA cancer genomic dataset present on cBioportal for Cancer Genomics (https://www.cbioportal.org/) and R2: Genomics Analysis and Visualization Platform (https://r2.amc.nl) were assessed for Spearmen’s correlation between FAT1 and target genes, and Kaplan–Meier survival analyses in glioblastoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, and pancreatic adenocarcinoma patients were assessed.

Statistical analysis

In vitro experiments were performed as three biological replicates in triplicates. Statistical significance (*p < 0.05) between datasets were calculated using T-test and two-way ANOVA as per the requirement, and data are presented as mean ± SD. For analysis of glioblastoma tumor data, Spearman’s correlation (nonparametric, two-tailed) was done using GraphPad PRISM software.

Hereafter, sgFAT1#3 is termed sgFAT1.

Decreased cell survival and clonogenicity of FAT1-knockout glioblastoma cells exposed to low serum and hypoxia

A hypoxic core with necrosis and nutrient-deficient tumor microenvironment is a characteristic feature of glioblastoma, and core hypoxic cells are aggressive and generate resistant clones [58]. To mimic the hypoxic tumor core, FAT1-knockout glioblastoma cells (U87MG and LN229) were treated with low oxygen [severe hypoxia (0.2% O2)] and/or low serum (5% and 1%) and control cells under normoxia with 10% serum and cultured for 6 days, and cell survival was analyzed. As compared with 10% serum, there was a significant decrease in the cell numbers in serum-deprived (5% and 1% serum) cells (U87MG and LN229) under both normoxia and hypoxia. In FAT1-knockout cells, there was a further reduction in cell number compared with their respective control cells (Fig. 1D). Earlier, we reported decreased colony formation on transient FAT1 knockdown in U87MG cells [48]. Here, in FAT1-knockout cells, we observed a significant decrease in the number of colonies w.r.t. controls in U87MG (35%) and LN229 (60%) cells under normoxia and in hypoxic U87MG (25%) and LN229 (40%) cells (Fig. 1E). These observations demonstrate that FAT1 supports the survival of tumor cells in the stressful microenvironment of hypoxia and serum deprivation.

The reason for decreased cell survival and reduced clonogenicity in FAT1-knockout cells could be due to either increased apoptosis or autophagy. Annexin V–FITC assay also showed no significant difference in early and late apoptosis between FAT1 knockout and control cells under both normoxia and hypoxia (Fig. 2A and B). Both U87MG and LN229 cultured in 5% serum under normoxia and hypoxia showed decreased cell survival on FAT1 knockout. Mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) is a master regulator of autophagy, in response to cellular stress, nutrients, and hypoxia [60]. Pharmacological inhibition of mTOR has been studied for improving the management of diseases, including cancers, that have occurred owing to autophagy deregulation [61]. mTOR regulates initiation of autophagy by phosphorylating autophagy initiating kinases such as ATG1, Beclin1, ATG13, and ATG14L [62, 63]. When cells encounter stressors such as energy depletion, starvation, hypoxia, etc., the phospho-mTOR level goes down and its suppressive effect is lost, leading to activation of the autophagic process [71].

The phospho-mTOR and mTOR levels were analyzed in FAT1-knockout cells: under both normoxia and hypoxia, a decrease in both phospho-mTOR and total mTOR protein levels was observed compared with their respective controls (Fig. 3F(i); Supplementary Fig. S3B). Furthermore, the mTOR mRNA level was also downregulated in FAT1-knockout U87MG and LN229 cells under normoxia and further reduction in hypoxia (Fig. 3F(ii)). This observation reflects the possible transcriptional regulation of mTOR by FAT1, which requires further study. We have previously reported HIF1α regulation by FAT1 via the AKT–mTOR pathway under hypoxia [47], and the FAT1-mediated regulation of mTOR/phospho-mTOR holds true in further regulating autophagy as well.

Discussion

Autophagy is a highly conserved cellular process that helps degrade damaged organelles, protein cargoes, and various cellular components, contributing to the maintenance of intracellular homeostasis. It plays a protective role in cells by supplying nutrients [1, 68]. In cancer, the importance of autophagy has been highlighted by its paradoxical roles, having context-dependent effects, as both a cytoprotective and a cytotoxic role [69, 70]. Despite significant research, the roles and effects of autophagy in cancer progression and treatment remain complex and continue to pose challenges.

Recently, studies have elucidated the role of autophagy in mediating cell death either independently or in association with apoptosis [71–73]. However, conditions such as persistent oxidative stress or prolonged nutrient deprivation lead to hyperactivation of autophagic processes, which can result in excessive degradation of cellular components, disrupting cellular homeostasis and inducing autophagic cell death [26, 74]. With advancements in research, the role of autophagy in cancer has become more complex [69], with studies suggesting that it may either promote cancer cell survival or lead to cell death [69, 70].

In glioblastoma, autophagy has a dual role, acting either as a protective mechanism or as a form of programmed cell death, depending on the context [1]. Autophagy-dependent cell death involves the activation of the autophagy machinery, accumulation of autophagosomes, and an increased autophagic flux, ultimately leading to cell death. Several cellular mechanisms that play a role in inducing autophagic cell death in cancers have been identified [24, 70, 75–77].

Glioblastoma is one of the most lethal cancers among all known brain cancers with a median survival time of 12–15 months and less than 10% of patients surviving beyond 5 years post-diagnosis [31]. Cadherin proteins are well known for their involvement in cancer development, invasiveness, and epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [78]. FAT1 is an atypical cadherin, type-1 transmembrane protein, that acts either as an oncogene or tumor suppressor, in a context-dependent manner [43, 44, 79, 80]. In glioblastoma, FAT1 functions as an oncogene, playing a role in tumor cell invasion, stemness/EMT, upregulating HIF1α activity under hypoxia, and enhancing the tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment, which promotes tumor progression [46–50, 81].

Studies indicate that glioma cells trigger autophagy in response to stressors such as hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, and chemotherapy, leading to cell survival [55]. However, under certain conditions, autophagy may promote cell death by facilitating the degradation of cellular components, a process known as autophagic or type II cell death [24]. Research suggests that autophagy-mediated cell death in glioma could be harnessed as a therapeutic strategy, to overcome treatment resistance [82]. For instance, when combined with other therapies, autophagy induction can enhance the cytotoxic effects on glioma cells, potentially leading to better clinical outcomes [83]. In some instances, glioma cells exploit autophagy to survive under stress, making autophagy inhibitors an attractive target for therapeutic interventions [82]. Overall, understanding the complex role of autophagy in glioma progression and therapy resistance is critical for developing novel treatment strategies that can exploit both its tumor-suppressive and tumor-promoting effects [55].

We previously reported the oncogenic role of FAT1 in regulating multiple downstream signaling pathways in glioma under both normoxia and hypoxia, affecting clonogenicity, migration, and invasion [46–48, 50, 81]. However, the role of FAT1 in regulating autophagy in glioblastoma has not yet been investigated. In this study, we used CRISPR-Cas9-mediated FAT1-knockout glioblastoma (U87MG and LN229) cells to explore the involvement of FAT1 in autophagy-related pathways and its implications for cell death and survival. One of the characteristic features of glioblastoma tumors is the presence of a hypoxic core with nutrient deficiency due to the hyperproliferation of tumor cells [58]. Linking the study of FAT1 with autophagy may offer insights into how FAT1 regulates cellular responses to hypoxic and nutrient stress and its potential therapeutic implications in targeting autophagy to improve glioblastoma treatment.

We and others have reported that patients with high FAT1 expression in glioblastoma tumors have shorter overall survival [50, 84]. We have previously documented the expression of the FAT1 gene in glioblastoma cells, noting that its expression is significantly elevated under severe hypoxic conditions (0.2% O2) [47, 48]. In the current study, we further observed an upregulation of FAT1 expression in glioblastoma cells (U87MG and LN229) when exposed to severe hypoxia, reinforcing its potential role in hypoxia-driven glioblastoma progression.

In FAT1-knockout glioblastoma (U87MG and LN229) cells, we observed a significant reduction in cell survival under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions, in the context of serum deprivation. Furthermore, the clonogenic potential of FAT1-knockout glioblastoma cells was markedly diminished under both normoxia and hypoxia. These reductions in cell survival and clonogenicity are often linked to the activation of programmed cell death processes [85]. Programmed cell death is categorized on the basis of the involvement of specific molecules and signaling pathways, with apoptosis and autophagy being well-established pathways that influence cell survival and clonogenicity in cancer cells [86].

To investigate whether the decreased survival and clonogenicity of FAT1-knockout glioblastoma cells were mediated by apoptosis or autophagy, we conducted cell cycle analyses and Annexin V-FITC assays. These assays revealed no significant changes in the apoptotic cell population of FAT1-knockout U87MG and LN229 cells under normoxic or hypoxic conditions. Moreover, using a pan-caspase inhibitor in low-serum conditions had no impact on the survival of FAT1-knockout glioblastoma cells. These findings indicate that the observed reduction in cell survival and clonogenicity cannot be attributed to apoptosis.

There is considerable evidence in the literature suggesting the role of activated autophagy in the elimination of cancer cells, as seen in pancreatic cancer [87], gastric cancer [88], prostate cancer [89], and others. To further investigate this, we knocked down LC3 expression using siRNA in glioblastoma cells, and we observed increased survival and clonogenicity of FAT1-knockout serum-deprived glioblastoma cells (under both normoxia and hypoxia), suggesting that autophagy plays a role in promoting cell death and reduced clonogenicity in FAT1-knockout cells. A study reported an inverse correlation between Beclin1 and LC3B-II expression with disease progression in glioblastoma tumors and a positive correlation with survival time [16]. Upregulation of BNIP3 causes hypoxia-induced cell death, which was suppressed by autophagy inhibition in apoptosis-competent glioblastoma and breast cancer cells [90]. Overexpression of ULK2 inhibited anchorage-independent growth, astrocyte transformation, and in vivo tumor growth [18]. Similarly, miR-519a promoted autophagy via the STAT3/Bcl-2/Beclin1 pathway, sensitizing glioblastoma cells and xenograft tumors to temozolomide treatment [56]. Another study demonstrated radiation-induced cell death of glioblastoma cells via high autophagy, which was reversed by inhibition of ATG5 [91]. A study reported that knockdown of c-Met (receptor tyrosine kinase) promoted autophagy features and LC3 accumulation, which inhibited glioblastoma cell proliferation, reduced xenograft tumor growth, and increased murine survival [92].

To decipher the role of FAT1 in the autophagy process, we investigated the expression of autophagy-related genes such as Beclin1, LC3B, ULK1, ATG13, ATG7, and ATG5 in FAT1-knockout U87MG and LN229 cells exposed to normoxia and hypoxia. We observed significant upregulation in the expression of these autophagy markers (Beclin1, LC3B, ULK1, and ATG5) in FAT1-knockout U87MG and LN229 cells under both normoxia and hypoxia, with further increases upon exposure to hypoxia. The protein levels of lipidated LC3 (LC3II) and Beclin1 were also elevated in FAT1-knockout cells, and more so in hypoxia-treated cells. Additionally, the number of autophagic vacuoles (autophagosomes), as detected by TEM, LC3 fluorescence levels, and LC3 puncta formation, were significantly increased in FAT1-knockout cells under normoxia, which was further augmented under hypoxic conditions. These findings suggest that FAT1 knockout in glioblastoma cells increases the autophagic process, which is further enhanced by severe hypoxia and serum deprivation, contributing to decreased cell survival and clonogenicity. A recent study in AML suggested that FAT1 downregulates the autophagy level and proliferation activity of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) cells [93]. These findings support the context and tissue specificity in the functioning of the FAT1, similar to other well-established molecules such as TGF-β [94], STAT3 [95], and c-Myc [96] having a dual role in tumorigenesis depending upon varying cellular contexts.

We replicated some of the key features of our findings in cell lines from other tumor types, such as hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), where FAT1 has been shown to have pro-tumorigenic features. CRISPR-Cas9 mediated FAT1-knockout clones of HCC (HepG2 and Huh7) and PAAD (MIAPaca-2 and Panc-1) cells also exhibited increased lipidated LC3 and Beclin1. The number of autophagosomes in both FAT1-knockout HCC and PAAD cells increased.

Cloning and expression of the full-length FAT1 cDNA (−14 kb) encoding the 508-kDa FAT1 protein was technically challenging, and we could not perform the rescue experiments by re-expressing FAT1 in the FAT1-knockout cells. To further support the specificity of the role of FAT1 in regulating autophagy, we knocked down LC3B in FAT1-knockout cells, which significantly rescued the reduced cell viability and diminished colony-forming potential in glioblastoma cells mediated by FAT1 knockout. This functional rescue provided an indirect but mechanistically relevant substitute and helped overcome this limitation to a significant extent. These findings corroborate the conclusion that FAT1 regulates autophagy and autophagy-mediated cell death under cellular stress.

In nutrient-rich conditions, phospho-mTOR (the active form of mTOR) inhibits autophagy directly and indirectly [97]. Conversely, under conditions of cellular stress such as energy depletion, starvation, or hypoxia, mTOR undergoes inactivation, thereby relinquishing its suppressive effect and facilitating the activation of autophagic pathways [64, 98]. Further, PI3K has been demonstrated to mediate phosphorylation and upregulation of NFkB, which further upregulates the transcriptional activation of mTOR, in bovine mammary epithelial cells [99]. We previously reported that FAT1 regulates the expression and activity of HIF1α through the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway in glioblastoma, where siRNA-mediated FAT1 knockdown downregulated the levels of EGFR, phospho-AKT, phospho-mTOR, and phospho-S6K1 under hypoxic conditions [47]. Furthermore, we recently reported that FAT1 upregulates the expression NFκB-RelA and its downstream target, miR‐221‐3p/222‐3p, which subsequently suppresses the expression of tumor suppressor genes PDCD10 and PTEN [50]. A negative expression correlation between PTEN and mTOR have been observed in gastric cancer and prostate cancer [100, 101]. In addition, PI3K/AKT signaling stimulates mTOR transcription by inhibiting FOXO4, an ATF5 transcription repressor, in chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) [102]. Here, we observed that the phospho-mTOR and mTOR were reduced at the protein level as well as the mTOR at mRNA level in FAT1-knockout glioblastoma cells under both normoxia and hypoxia. The transcriptional regulation of mTOR at the mRNA level by FAT1 could be mediated via the NFkB/miR221-3p/222-3p/PTEN axis and/or PI3K/AKT/FOXO4 (ATF5 transcription repressor) axis. However, this requires further experimental validation and is beyond the scope of the present study.

The level of p62/SQSTM1, an autophagy substrate, serves as a readout for autophagy rate and activity in cells [65]. Accumulation of p62 reflects the failure of cells to complete the autophagic process, which may make tumor cells resistant to severe hypoxic stress and cell death [23]. We found that the level of p62 in both FAT1-knockout glioblastoma cells was significantly decreased under normoxia and further decreased under hypoxia, with the lowest levels observed in FAT1-knockout hypoxic cells. These findings indicate that exposure to severe hypoxia increases autophagy activity, which is further enhanced in FAT1-knockout cells, supporting the role of autophagy in the observed phenotypic features in glioblastoma cells (cell death and reduced clonogenicity). Moreover, in addition to the known oncogenic role of FAT1 in glioblastoma by promoting EMT, stemness, migration, and invasion, the current findings indicate that FAT1 influences the progression of glioblastoma by regulating autophagy as well.

On the basis of the literature and our findings in this study, we may state that a balanced autophagic process is necessary for maintaining the cellular homeostasis essential for tumor cells to survive under adverse/stressful cellular conditions. When the balance is lost, owing either to reduced autophagic activity and loss of important autophagic genes [103] or to hyperactive autophagic activity [74, 104], the cellular homeostasis will be lost, which would be detrimental to the tumor cells. The tumor cells may undergo cell death either by apoptotic activity [105, 106] or by activation of autophagy-dependent cell death [74, 90, 91]. Either way, the tumor cells are killed, which is beneficial for the clinical outcome of patients.

Glioblastoma is a heterogeneous tumor with a tendency to develop resistance to conventional treatments and often utilizes autophagy as a survival mechanism [55, 107]. Temozolomide-mediated cytotoxic effects in cancer cells induce autophagy, leading to reduced efficacy and promoting the survival of glioblastoma cells [108]. Autophagy inhibitors such as chloroquine (CQ) have been used in conjunction with temozolomide, which sensitizes glioblastoma cells by promoting apoptosis [109]. Studies have demonstrated that hyperactive autophagy leads to autophagy-dependent cell death, which is non-apoptotic [22, 62, 110]. Circular RNA such as Hsa_circ_0072309 increases p53 protein stability and induces autophagy, thereby enhancing the temozolomide sensitivity of p53 wild-type glioblastoma cells [111]. Targeting the EGFR/PI3K/mTOR pathway induces autophagy and sensitizes glioma cells to chemotherapeutic agent, gefitinib [112]. Rapamycin, an mTOR inhibitor, enhances autophagy and synergizes with temozolomide to induce autophagic cell death in glioblastoma by promoting mitochondrial dysfunction and activating caspase-independent pathways [113]. Furthermore, mTOR inhibition by RAD001 with temozolomide treatment enhances autophagy and also increases DNA damage and autophagic cell death [77]. Likewise, AMPK activator, metformin, augmented temozolomide-induced cytotoxicity via autophagy activation in stressed cells [114, 115]. Natural products such as resveratrol and curcumin activate autophagy, thereby enhancing temozolomide effectiveness by promoting non-apoptotic forms of cell death in glioma stem-like cells [116, 117]. Consequently, combining autophagy inducers with chemotherapeutic agents such as temozolomide offers a promising and effective adjuvant/combinatorial treatment strategy.

Our study uncovers a novel role of FAT1 in regulating autophagy in glioblastoma. FAT1 balances autophagic flux and protects cancer cells under nutrient or hypoxic stress, preventing autophagy-dependent cell death. In addition to promoting migration, invasion, and stemness in glioblastoma, FAT1 protects tumor cells from stress-induced cell death. FAT1 knockout disrupts this balance, increasing autophagic cell death and inhibiting tumor growth and progression. In addition to glioblastoma, preliminary evidence suggests that this protective role extends to HCC and PAAD, positioning FAT1 as a potential therapeutic and prognostic marker in cancers. Therefore, targeting FAT1 with standard therapeutic regimens may offer a synergistic effect with improved clinical outcomes in cancers where FAT1 functions as an oncogene and has high FAT1 expression.

Conclusions

We suggest that FAT1, in addition to its role in promoting tumor migration, invasion, and stemness in glioblastoma, also protects these cells from autophagic cell death under adverse stressful conditions. Preliminary data suggest that this role extends to HCC and PAAD. Thus, FAT1 may serve as a promising adjuvant therapeutic target when used along with standard treatment regimens in high FAT1-expressing glioblastoma and other cancers where it exhibits an oncogenic role.
