Intestinal epithelial PIEZO1 regulates macrophage-to-myofibroblast transition via epithelial-derived TGF-α signaling in Crohn’s disease
Authors: Jing Yang, Jinyan Jing, Ying Wang, Qing Zhao, Ni Ma, Danxia Liang, Zexiong Su, Yan Hu, Zhiling Li, Hanshi Zeng, Yupeng Li, Jiaoli Lan, Min Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntestinal fibrosis represents a common complication of Crohn’s disease (CD). Previous studies have shown that PIEZO1 is enriched in the colonic mucosal epithelial cells of patients with CD. However, whether epithelial PIEZO1 contributes to intestinal fibrosis in CD remains unclear. The present study investigated whether PIEZO1 in intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) regulates intestinal fibrosis by modulating macrophage-to-myofibroblast transition (MMT).MethodsWestern blotting, real-time PCR, Masson’s trichrome staining and immunofluorescence were used to evaluate the association between PIEZO1 and MMT in vivo and in vitro. Conditioned medium (CM) derived from IECs was used to assess its effects on macrophage phenotype. Co-immunoprecipitation, single-cell RNA sequencing and Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay were performed to identify the mechanism through which PIEZO1 signaling in IECs regulates MMT.ResultsIntestinal fibrosis in patients with CD was positively correlated with the MMT process. CM derived from IECs with elevated PIEZO1 activity promoted MMT. Mechanistically, elevated PIEZO1 activity in IECs was associated with increased TGF-α secretion, which was accompanied by activation of the PKA/NF-κB P65 signaling pathway in macrophages. Furthermore, in a chronic DSS-induced colitis–associated fibrosis model, IEC-specific PIEZO1 deficiency attenuated intestinal fibrosis.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that epithelial PIEZO1 regulates MMT and intestinal fibrosis in CD, potentially involving TGF-α–mediated signaling.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02941-w.

Introduction

Intestinal fibrosis is a common and debilitating complication of Crohn’s disease (CD), characterized by excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition. This pathological process drives tissue stiffening and luminal narrowing, frequently necessitating endoscopic balloon dilatation or surgical resection for symptom relief [1].

Previous studies have implicated epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT) in intestinal fibrosis progression [2, 3]. Notably, macrophage-to-myofibroblast transition (MMT), a key source of myofibroblasts, has been well documented in heart, kidney and liver fibrosis [4–6]. Recent research indicates that MMT also mediates intestinal fibrosis [7, 8]. However, the mechanisms remain poorly defined.

The intestinal epithelium forms a dynamic interface between luminal cues and mucosal immunity, constantly exposed to mechanical forces. Piezo type mechanosensitive ion channel component 1 (PIEZO1), highly expressed on the plasma membrane of intestinal epithelial cells (IECs), functions as a sensitive detector of shear stress and peristaltic strain [9–12]. Beyond its role as a mechanotransducer, PIEZO1 is increasingly recognized as a regulator of epithelial homeostasis [13]. Its activation reshapes tight junction architecture-modulating proteins such as ZO-1 and occluding-thereby influencing barrier permeability, while its localization at cell–cell contacts supports junctional organization and mechanical integrity [14, 15]. PIEZO1 activation induces Ca²⁺ influx and activates inflammatory transcriptional programs, including NF-κB signaling, linking mechanical stimuli to immune responses [9, 16]. Under physiological conditions, this signaling axis helps maintain epithelial balance and promotes tissue repair in response to normal luminal forces. In CD, however, barrier disruption, immune cell infiltration and progressive ECM stiffening generate an aberrant mechanical environment that may drive sustained PIEZO1 activation [13, 17]. Such dysregulated signaling could amplify inflammatory pathways and promote pathological ECM deposition, contributing to fibrosis progression. While PIEZO1 has been implicated in fibrosis in other tissues, its specific role in intestinal fibrosis and crosstalk with immune cells in CD remains to be fully elucidated.

Previous work provides a crucial foundation for further investigation. Specifically, compared with healthy controls, CD patients exhibit significantly elevated PIEZO1 expression in IECs, along with increased macrophage frequency in the intestinal mucosa [18]. This observation prompted the hypothesis that intestinal epithelial PIEZO1 initiates pro-fibrotic crosstalk with macrophages. By investigating this interaction, the present study aims to determine whether epithelial PIEZO1 regulates MMT and contributes to intestinal fibrosis in CD.

Materials and methods

Human intestinal specimens

Colonic mucosal tissues were derived from colonic biopsies of Crohn’s Disease (CD) patients who were in the active phase, had the fibrostenotic B2 phenotype, according to the Montreal Classification, and whose fibrotic stenosis was confirmed by medical imaging, endoscopy and/or surgery.

Cell culture and treatment

Intestinal epithelial cells Caco-2 (ATCC, USA; Cat. # HTB-37), murine macrophage cells RAW264.7 (ATCC, USA; Cat. # TIB-71), and human monocyte leukemia cells (THP-1, ATCC, USA; Cat. # TIB-202) were cultured under standard conditions.

Caco-2 and THP-1 were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640 medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; Cat.# C11875500BT) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum ( FBS; Hycyte, China; FBP-C520-1) and 1% penicillin/ streptomycin (Proteintech, China; Cat.# PR40022), whereas RAW264.7 were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; Cat.# C11995500BT) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin.

All cells were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2. For passaging, Caco-2 cells were detached using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Procell, China, Cat. # PB180226) at approximately 80% confluence. THP-1 cells were passaged every 3–4 days, while RAW264.7 were passaged by gentle pipetting using phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Servicebio, China; Cat. # G4202-500ML) without trypsin digestion.

Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs)

Murine BMDMs were generated using a procedure similar to that described previously [19]. Bone marrow cells were isolated from femurs and tibias and cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS, 20 ng/mL M-CSF (MedChemExpress, USA; Cat. # HY-P7085), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Medium was replaced every 48 h. After 7 days of differentiation, adherent macrophages were gently washed with PBS to remove residual M-CSF-containing medium and subsequently stimulated under the indicated experimental conditions in fresh complete medium.

Generation of stable cell lines

Caco-2 cells were cultured in 12-well plates (2 × 105 cells/well). Upon reaching approximately 40% confluence [20, 21], the cells were infected with PIEZO1 shRNA (h) lentiviral particles (Caco-2-shPIEZO1, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA; Cat. # sc-93227-V) or control shRNA lentiviral particles (Caco-2-shCTL, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA; Cat. # sc-108080). After 4 h, puromycin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA; Cat. # sc-108071) was used to select stable cell lines at a final concentration of 2 µg/mL for 5 days. Western blotting and real-time PCR were performed to test the expression level of PIEZO1.

Conditioned medium preparation and macrophage treatment

Conditioned medium (CM) was collected from Caco-2-shPIEZO1 (KD) or Caco-2-shCTL (CTL) cells. Briefly, cells were cultured to 100% confluence, washed with PBS and incubated with serum-free RPMI-1640 medium (without fetal bovine serum or additional growth factors) for an additional 24 h. Then, the supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 1000 g for 5 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris, and the resulting supernatant was used as CM.

For treatment, THP-1 (2 × 106 cells/well) in a 12-well plate was stimulated with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, MedChemExpress, USA; Cat. # HY-18739) (200 ng/mL) for 48 h to obtain THP-1 macrophages [22]. Differentiated THP-1 macrophages were then treated with CM collected from Caco-2-shPIEZO1 (KD) or Caco-2-shCTL (CTL) cells for 24 h.

In the PKA inhibition experiment, THP-1 macrophages were treated with CM with or without H-89 dihydrochloride (MedChemExpress, USA; Cat. # HY-15979 A) (30 nmol/L) for 24 h. In the NF-κΒ P65 activation experiment, THP-1 macrophages were stimulated with CM with or without NF-κΒ activator 1 (Selleck Chemicals, USA; Cat. # E1351) (9 nmol/L) for 24 h.

Morphological analysis of THP-1 macrophages

THP-1 macrophage morphology was assessed using phase-contrast microscopy, with at least five random, non-overlapping fields captured per condition under identical settings. Pseudopodia-positive cells-defined by clearly identifiable protrusions extending beyond the cell perimeter by more than half the cell body radius-were manually quantified using ImageJ software. At least 100 cells per condition were analyzed across five independent biological replicates (n = 5). The percentage of pseudopodia-positive cells was calculated relative to the total cell count, with the mean from each experiment serving as a single biological replicate for statistical analysis. All quantifications were conducted in a blinded manner.

TGF-α stimulation

THP-1 macrophages were treated with recombinant human TGF-α (MedChemExpress, USA; Cat. # HY-P7296), BMDMs and RAW264.7 cells were treated with recombinant mouse TGF-α (MedChemExpress, USA; Cat. # HY-P77857). All cells were cultured with TGF-α or vehicle control for 24 h.

Transwell co-culture and migration assay

Transwell inserts (Jet Bio-Filtration, China; Cat. # TCS003024; pore size 8.0 μm) were used. Caco-2 cells with PIEZO1 knockdown (Caco-2-shPIEZO1) or control (Caco-2-shCTL) were seeded in the lower chamber of a Transwell system. After the cells reached 100% confluence, they were washed with PBS and cultured in serum-free RPMI-1640 medium for 24 h before the migration assay. PMA-differentiated THP-1 macrophages were then added to the upper chamber at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells per well. After 24 h, migrated cells on the lower membrane surface were fixed with paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet. Images were captured under a microscope and quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Western blotting analysis

The total protein was extracted using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Millipore, USA; Cat. # 20–188) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Switzerland, Cat. # 04693159001) and phosphatase inhibitor (Roche, Switzerland, Cat. # 04906845001). Protein concentrations were determined via bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Beyotime, China; Cat. # P0010S). Equal amounts of proteins (20 µg per lane) were loaded and subjected to SDS-PAGE and then transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (Millipore, USA; Cat. # ISEQ00010). After blocking with 5% nonfat milk (ABclonal, China, Cat. # RM00014) for 1 h at room temperature, the membrane was incubated with specific primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with a horseradish peroxidase secondary antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Proteins were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (Advansta, USA; Cat. # K-12045-D50) and visualized with a chemiluminescence imaging system (Bio-Rad). Exposure times were carefully adjusted to avoid signal overexposure. Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ software based on non-saturated images.

The primary antibodies used for immunoblotting were rabbit anti-α-SMA (Proteintech, China; Cat. #14395-1-AP; 1:1000), rabbit anti-COL1A2 (Solarbio, China; Cat. #K004211P; 1:1000), rabbit anti-phospho-PKA (ImmunoWay, China; Cat. #YP0226; 1:1000), rabbit anti-PKA (ImmunoWay, China; Cat. #YT3749; 1:1000), rabbit anti-phospho-NF-κB P65 (Ser 536) (HuaBio, China; Cat. #ET1604-27; 1:1000), rabbit anti-NF-κB P65 (HuaBio, China; Cat. #ET1603-12; 1:1000), mouse anti-FAM38A/PIEZO1 (M1005-2, HuaBio, China; Cat. #M1005-2; 1:1000), mouse anti-GAPDH (Proteintech, China; Cat. #60004-1-Ig; 1:5000), The HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies used in this study were goat anti-rabbit IgG (HRP) (Zsbio, China; Cat. #ZB5301; 1:10000) and goat anti-mouse IgG H&L (HRP) (Zsbio, China; Cat. #ZB5305; 1:5000).

Immunofluorescence staining and quantitative analysis

After treatment, the cells were fixed with 4% polyformaldehyde (Biosharp, China; Cat. # BL539A) and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton-X100 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA; Cat. #T8787). The paraffin-embedded tissues were subjected to antigen retrieval with sodium citrate buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, China; Cat. #P0083) after deparaffinization in xylene and gradient ethanol. 10% goat serum (Solarbio, China; Cat. #SL038) was used to block for 1 h at room temperature. Then, primary antibodies were added overnight at 4 °C. The following day, the cells and sections were incubated with the secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature and covered with mounting medium and antifading (with DAPI) (Abcam, UK; Cat. #ab104139). The slides were imaged using the Leica X image analysis software (Leica, Hamburg, Germany). Images were acquired using identical microscope settings (laser intensity, exposure time, gain, and offset) across all experimental groups within each independent experiment to ensure comparability. All images were captured within the linear range of fluorescence detection and processed uniformly.

Images were analyzed using ImageJ software (NIH). For each biological replicate, at least five random, non-overlapping fields were selected per condition. Mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was measured within the defined region of interest (ROI) after background subtraction. The same threshold parameters were applied to all groups within each experiment to minimize bias. For co-localization or double-positive analysis, the percentage of double-positive cells was calculated as the number of marker-positive cells divided by the total number of DAPI⁺ nuclei in the same field and expressed as a percentage. Cell counting was performed manually in a blinded manner. More than 100 cells per condition were analyzed in each independent experiment. All experiments were performed in at least three independent biological replicates (n ≥ 3). The mean value from each experiment was considered one biological replicate for statistical analysis. Data are presented as mean ± SD.

The antibodies used for Immunofluorescent staining listed as followed: rabbit anti-α-SMA (Proteintech, China; Cat. #14395-1-AP; 1:100), rabbit anti-COL1A2 (Solarbio, China; Cat. #K004211P; 1:50), rabbit anti-phospho-PKA (ImmunoWay, China; Cat. #YP0226; 1:50), rabbit anti-PKA (ImmunoWay, China; Cat. #YT3749; 1:50), rabbit anti-phospho-NF-κB P65 (ABclonal, China; Cat. #AP0124; 1:50), rabbit anti-NF-κB P65 (HuaBio, China; Cat. #ET1603-12; 1:50), mouse anti-CD68 (Immunoway, USA; Cat. #YM3050; 1:50), rabbit anti-TGF-α (BD-PT4626, Biodragon, China; Cat. #BD-PT4626; 1:50), Goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 647) (Abcam, UK; Cat. #ab150079; 1:100) and goat anti-mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 488) (Abcam, UK; Cat. #ab150113; 1:100).

Masson’s trichrome staining

Specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin and sectioned into 3–4 μm thick slices. Masson’s trichrome staining kit (Solarbio, China; Cat. # G1340) was used to assess tissue fibrosis according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For each biological replicate, at least five random, non-overlapping fields were selected per condition. The mean value from each experiment was considered one biological replicate for statistical analysis. The percentage of the collagen-rich regions was quantified using Image J software (National Institutes of Health, MD, USA).

Chronic DSS-induced colitis–associated fibrosis model

Weight-matched 6-8-week-old male C57BL/6 mice (20–22 g) were purchased from DK Pharmaceutical (Guangdong) Co., Ltd. and fed with regular food and water before the experiments in individually ventilated cages (IVCs) in a specific pathogen-free barrier unit. The animals were randomly allocated to four groups (n = 6 per group): (i) WT CTRL: normal WT control group receiving sterile water, (ii) WT DSS: WT group receiving Dextran sulfate sodium (DSS), (iii) CKO CTRL: Villin-Piezo1−/− control group receiving sterile water, (iv) CKO DSS: Villin- PIEZO1−/− mice receiving DSS. Colonic fibrosis in a mouse model was induced using a longitudinal multi-cycle dextran sulfate sodium (DSS, colitis grade, MP Biomedicals, USA; Cat. # BD-0216011090) protocol over 59 days. Specifically, the DSS-treated groups underwent four induction cycles: Cycles 1–3: 6 days of % (w/v) DSS in drinking water, followed by a 12-day recovery period with sterile water. Cycle 4: A final induction of % DSS for 5 days, concluding on Day 59. Control groups received sterile water throughout the experimental period.

Disease activity was monitored daily by calculating the Disease Activity Index (DAI) based on body weight loss, stool consistency, and occult blood. All mice were euthanized on Day 59 for colon length measurement and subsequent histopathological analysis. All animal experiments were approved by the Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center animal committee (KYZ202216403) and performed at Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center.

Assessment of disease activity index (DAI)

Disease activity index (DAI) was evaluated during DSS treatment as previously described [25]. The DAI score was assessed based on three parameters: body weight loss, stool consistency, and the presence of fecal blood. Each parameter was scored on a scale of 0–4. The final DAI score was calculated as the average of the three parameters, obtained by summing the individual scores and dividing the total by three.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The CM, collected with up to 100% confluence of Caco-2-shPIEZO1 or Caco-2-shCTL, was analyzed for human TGF-α and TGF-β1 using ELISA kits (Bioswamp, China; Cat. # HM10354 and HM10058) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Mouse serum TGF-α was quantified with a mouse-specific ELISA kit (Bioswamp, China; Cat. # MU30305), following the same protocol. The concentrations were quantified using the corresponding standard curves. All samples were tested in duplicate.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

After treatment with CM for 3 h, THP-1 macrophages were lysed in Co-IP lysis buffer (Proteintech, China; Cat. # PR20037) for 30 min. The resulting protein lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 12,000 g for 20 min. Equal amounts of protein (~ 800 µg per reaction) were incubated with anti-PKA antibodies (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA; Cat. # sc-365615; 2 µg per reaction) or IgG isotype control (ABclonal, China; Cat. # AC011; 2 µg per reaction) at 4 °C overnight with gentle rotation. The next day, Protein A/G beads (Millipore, USA; Cat. # LSKMAGAG10) were added to the lysate mixture to incubate for another 3 h at 4 °C. The beads were washed three times with lysis buffer, and the bound proteins were eluted by boiling in 1x loading buffer for 5 min. The precipitated immunocomplexes and corresponding Input lysates were analyzed by standard Western blots.

Cell transfection

The THP-1 macrophages were cultured in 12-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well. After 48 h, the cells were transiently transfected with SignalSilence® NF-κB P65 siRNA I (Cell Signaling Technology, USA; Cat. # 6261) or control siRNA (Cell Signaling Technology, USA; Cat. #6568) using Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; Cat. # L3000015) according to the instructions. Cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection for analysis.

PIEZO1 pharmacological modulation

Cells were seeded into 12-well plates. Upon reaching approximately 50% confluence (low-density) or 100% confluence (high-density), pharmacological treatments—including the PIEZO1 agonist Yoda1 (Selleck Chemicals, USA; Cat. # SML1558) at 10 µM or the inhibitor GsMTx4 (Selleck Chemicals, USA; Cat. # S7364) at 1 µg/mL—were initiated. Cell culture supernatant was collected 24 h post-treatment, centrifuged to remove cellular debris, and utilized for subsequent ELISA analysis of TGF-α levels.

TGF-α neutralization assay

CM was collected from Caco-2 cultured to 100% confluence under serum-free conditions and incubated with a neutralizing anti–TGF-α antibody (HF007036, abinScience, China; Cat. # HF007036; 10 µg/mL) [26, 27] or isotype-matched IgG antibody (abinScience, China; Cat. # HV080207; 10 µg/mL) for 30 min prior to macrophage stimulation. Cells after 24 h of stimulation were then harvested for analysis.

RNA sequencing and transcriptomic quantification

Total RNA was extracted from Caco-2-shCTL and Caco-2-shPIEZO1 cells (n = 4 per group) using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, and RNA integrity was verified prior to library preparation. RNA sequencing libraries were constructed using a poly(A)-enriched protocol and sequenced on an Illumina platform to generate paired-end reads. Data were deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive (GSA, National Genomics Data Center, China) under accession number HRA013526.

Raw reads were subjected to quality control to remove adaptor sequences and low-quality reads. Clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome using a standard alignment pipeline. Gene expression levels were quantified as normalized read counts.

Differential expression and functional enrichment analysis

Differential gene expression analysis between Caco-2-shCTL and Caco-2-shPIEZO1 cells was performed using the DESeq2 package. Genes with an adjusted P value (FDR) < 0.05 and |log₂ fold change| ≥ 1 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Differentially expressed genes were visualized using Venn diagrams and volcano plots.

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed to categorize differentially expressed genes into biological process, molecular function, and cellular component terms. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was conducted to identify significantly enriched signaling pathways. Enrichment significance was determined based on adjusted P values (FDR < 0.05). These analyses were used to characterize functional pathways associated with PIEZO1 knockdown in intestinal epithelial cells.

Single-cell RNA-seq (ScRNA-seq) data quality control and preprocessing

This cohort included CD patients (n = 6) and healthy controls (n = 6), using data (HRA000072, HRA003028) from previous studies [18, 28]. Colonic mucosal tissues were obtained from colonic biopsies of CD patients who were in the active disease phase and presented with the fibrostenotic B2 phenotype, according to the Montreal Classification. Fibrotic stenosis was confirmed by medical imaging, endoscopy and/or surgery. Intestinal specimens from non-IBD individuals were included as controls for comparison. The raw gene expression matrix from each sample was aggregated and converted into a Seurat object via the Seurat R package (V4.0). We selected cells with a gene count (nFeature_RNA) between 200 and 5000, a total RNA count (nCount_RNA) below 30,000, and a mitochondrial gene expression percentage (percent.mt) less than 10%. To further eliminate the data of doublets, we performed the scrublet pipeline for each batch of our scRNA-seq data [29], which were expected to exclude doublets, and the expected_doublet_rate was set at 0.05. We got 28,317 cells for further analysis, including 5922 nonimmune cells and 22,395 immune cells. The gene expression matrices were normalized to the total unique molecular identifiers (UMI) counts per cell and transformed to the natural log scale. After selecting 2500 highly variable genes by the FindVariableFeatures function in Seurat, principal components analysis (PCA) was performed using these genes. The numbers of principal components (PCs) of different kinds of cells were differentially selected according to the knee point of the scree plot for each cell type to accommodate different population complexities based on the ElbowPlot function in Seurat, for cell type classification, the top 30 PCs were chosen, with a resolution set at 0.3, for epithelial cell subcluster analysis, the top 25 PCs and a resolution of 0.2 were selected.

Unsupervised cell clustering and annotation

According to the differentially expressed genes of each lineage, cells were divided into distinct cell types. Differentially expressed genes of each subset were identified using the FindAllMarkers function implemented in Seurat. Clusters were named by the highly expressed genes of each cluster. Seurat’s FindClusters function was applied with default parameters, with the exceptions of the adapted K parameter and resolution, to identify cellular subsets within the epithelial cell cluster.

Molecular interaction network analysis

CellPhone DB (http://CellPhoneDB.org) was used to analyze the interactions of the epithelial cells and macrophages.

Gene sets of the human matrisome

To systematically investigate the origin and dynamic changes of the extracellular matrix (ECM) in stenotic intestinal segments of Crohn’s disease (CD) at the single-cell level, the human matrisome gene set was downloaded for subsequent analyses. This gene set, curated by the Broad Institute of MIT and Harvard, encompasses all genes encoding ECM proteins (data available at: http://matrisomeproject.mit.edu/) [30]. These genes are further classified into three major categories: glycoproteins, collagens and proteoglycans.

Gene set score and expression analysis

The ECM gene set score was used to assess the expression of ECM-related protein-encoding genes in macrophages, which were derived from the hallmarks gene set in the MSigDB database (http://gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/). Scaled expression levels of target genes in macrophages were visualized as heatmaps using the R package pheatmap (Version 1.0.12).

Gene set variation analysis

To compare the signaling pathway enrichment of macrophages, the “ssGSEA” method was used to calculate the enrichment score of indicated gene sets.

Statistical analysis

For the experimental data, statistical analyses and graphics were performed using the GraphPad Prism (version 7.0) software package (GraphPad Software, San Diego, USA). All data are presented as mean ± SD. The sample size (n) specified in the figure legends refers to the number of independent biological replicates. Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test. Comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. For experiments involving two independent variables, statistical significance was determined using two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test.

Correlation analysis was performed using simple linear regression. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

PIEZO1+ epithelial cells and macrophages accumulate in the colonic mucosa of patients with CD

To systematically analyze the connection between epithelial cells and macrophages in patients with CD, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was performed on colonic mucosa from 6 healthy controls and 6 CD patients. Based on the analysis, the cells were partitioned into 6 groups using unsupervised graphs and labeled based on marker gene expression (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Materials 1: Fig. S1). Compared with healthy controls, CD patients exhibited increased accumulation of myeloid cells and epithelial cells (Fig. 1A). Further sub-clustering of 17,390 epithelial cells defined nine distinct subsets (Fig. 1B). Among these, epithelial cells exhibiting high PIEZO1 expression were significantly enriched in CD patients compared to controls (Fig. 1C). More importantly, cell interaction analysis revealed enhanced interactions between PIEZO1⁺ epithelial cells and macrophages in CD patients (Fig. 1D). It has been reported that macrophages participate in fibrosis across multiple organs [4, 8, 31]. Interestingly, ECM-related genes were significantly upregulated in macrophages (Fig. 1E).

To explore the impact of PIEZO1 activity in IECs on the MMT process, Caco-2 cells were cultured to full confluence to induce PIEZO1 signaling, consistent with its function as a mechanosensitive receptor (Supplementary Materials 1: Fig. S2A) [32, 33]. PIEZO1-dependent epithelial signaling regulates MMT via PKA/NF-κB P65 pathway

To further investigate the potential mechanism of PIEZO1-dependent epithelial signaling regulates MMT, several signaling pathways associated with fibrosis and inflammation were examined [34–38]. As shown in Fig. 4A, CM derived from Caco-2-shCTL cells markedly enhanced the phosphorylation of PKA and NF-κB P65 in THP-1 macrophages, whereas no appreciable changes were observed in the activation of CREB, AKT and ERK1/2 signaling pathways. Consistently, clinical colonic mucosal specimens, from CD patients, showed elevated phosphorylation levels of PKA and NF-κB P65 compared with healthy controls (Fig. 4B).

To further investigate the role of intestinal epithelial PIEZO1 on PKA and NF-κB P65 in THP-1 macrophages, co-immunoprecipitation analysis was conducted. PIEZO1 knockdown in IECs markedly diminished the interaction between PKA and NF-κB P65 in THP-1 macrophages (Fig. 4C). Given that epithelial cell density influences intercellular mechanical tension and PIEZO1 activity, CM was collected from Caco-2-shCTL cells cultured at different stages of confluence level (50% and 100%). Western blot analysis revealed that CM from 100% confluent Caco-2 cell induced a more marked upregulation of ECM-associated markers (COL1A2 and α-SMA) compared with CM from 50% confluence, indicating enhanced MMT induction (Fig. 4D). To confirm the involvement of the NF-κB pathway, THP-1 macrophages exposed to CM from 100% confluent Caco-2 cells were transfected with NF-κB P65-specific siRNA (siP65) (Fig. 4D). Silencing of NF-κB P65 markedly reduced P65 and p-P65 levels and significantly attenuated the CM-induced upregulation of COL1A2 and α-SMA, confirming a functional role of NF-κB activation in MMT. Furthermore, pharmacological inhibition of PKA using H-89 dihydrochloride decreased p-PKA levels and concomitantly decreased p-P65 expression, accompanied by reduced expression of COL1A2 and α-SMA in THP-1 macrophages (Fig. 4E). Importantly, treatment with an NF-κB activator (NF-κB activator-1) partially restored the expression of COL1A2 and α-SMA that had been suppressed by H-89, further supporting that NF-κB acts downstream of PKA (Fig. 4E). These results indicated that the PKA/NF-κB P65 signaling pathway plays a crucial role in regulating intestinal epithelial PIEZO1-driven MMT.

To identify epithelial-derived mediators underlying PIEZO1-regulated MMT, transcriptomic analysis comparing PIEZO1-activated and PIEZO1-knockdown IECs was performed. Differential expression analysis revealed multiple altered genes, among which TGFA was markedly downregulated upon PIEZO1 knockdown (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Materials 1: Fig. S3A). Notably, genes related to epithelial barrier function and redox homeostasis were also differentially regulated, suggesting broader effects of PIEZO1 signaling on epithelial homeostasis [43–45]. Pathway enrichment analyses are summarized in Supplementary Materials 1: Fig. S3B-C. Consistent with the transcriptomic findings, TGF-α secretion was increased in the supernatant of Caco-2 cells with PIEZO1-dependent mechanotransduction compared with PIEZO1-knockdown cells (Fig. 5C).

Pharmacological gain- and loss-of-function assays were performed to assess whether PIEZO1-dependent mechanotransduction regulates TGF-α production under conditions of epithelial crowding. In sub-confluent Caco-2 cultures (50% confluence), activation of PIEZO1 with Yoda1 increased TGF-α levels in the CM. In contrast, the density-dependent increase in TGF-α secretion observed at 100% confluence was markedly suppressed by the mechanosensitive channel inhibitor GsMTx4 (Fig. 5D).

These results indicated that epithelial TGF-α production was governed by PIEZO1-dependent mechanotransduction.

To determine the functional role of TGF-α in MMT, recombinant TGF-α (5 ng/mL) was added into the CM of Caco-2-shPIEZO1, which restored MMT marker expression and PKA/NF-κB P65 activation in THP-1 macrophages (Fig. 5E). Conversely, neutralization of TGF-α in CM from IECs with PIEZO1-dependent mechanotransduction attenuated MMT marker expression and pathway activation, whereas control IgG had no effect (Fig. 5F). Recombinant TGF-α further induced fibrosis-associated gene expression and PKA/NF-κB signaling activation in RAW264.7 macrophages in a dose-dependent manner, with more pronounced effects observed at higher concentrations (Fig. 5G). Consistently, TGF-α stimulation also increased fibrosis-associated marker expression and activated PKA/NF-κB signaling in primary murine bone marrow–derived macrophages (BMDMs) (Fig. 5H). Immunofluorescence analysis of colonic tissues from patients with fibrostenotic Crohn’s disease (B2 phenotype) showed increased epithelial TGF-α expression in fibrotic regions (Fig. 5I).

Collectively, these findings define a mechanistic axis in which intestinal epithelial PIEZO1 regulated MMT through enhanced TGF-α secretion and subsequent activation of the PKA/NF-κB signaling pathway in macrophages.

Intestinal epithelial PIEZO1 deficiency attenuates colonic fibrosis in a chronic DSS-induced colitis–associated fibrosis model

To better elucidate the potential therapeutic target of PIEZO1 in intestinal fibrosis, a chronic DSS-induced colitis–associated fibrosis model, a widely used experimental system for studying intestinal fibrogenesis, was constructed (Fig. 6A) [25]. Compared with control mice, IEC–specific Piezo1 knockout (Villin-Cre; Piezo1fl/fl; CKO) mice exhibited significantly attenuated body weight loss during DSS treatment (Fig. 6B-C). Consistently, the disease activity index (DAI) was significantly reduced in CKO mice. These results indicate that epithelial PIEZO1 deficiency alleviates colitis severity in this model. In line with these findings, PIEZO1 ablation attenuated colon length shortening in mice with colonic fibrosis (Fig. 6D). Notably, TGF-α expression was significantly reduced in colonic tissues from CKO mice, accompanied by a decreasing trend in circulating TGF-α levels (Fig. 6E-F and Supplementary Materials 1: Fig. S4A). Histological analyses further demonstrated that collagen deposition, as well as the abundance of CD68⁺COL1A2⁺ and CD68⁺α-SMA⁺ macrophages, were markedly decreased in the colons of CKO mice compared with controls (Fig. 6G-H and Supplementary Materials 1: Fig. S4B-D).

Discussion

Intestinal fibrosis is a common and clinically significant complication in CD, frequently leading to strictures and the need for surgical intervention [46, 47]. Although the activation and expansion of myofibroblasts are central to fibrogenesis, the cellular sources and regulatory mechanisms driving this process remain incompletely understood [1]. The present study identifies intestinal epithelial PIEZO1 as a mechanosensitive regulator linking epithelial mechanical cues to MMT.

Mechanistically, PIEZO1 activity in IECs was associated with increased TGF-α secretion, which was associated with promotion of MMT through activation of PKA/NF-κB P65 pathway. These findings broaden the functional scope of TGF-α, extending its well-established roles in tumor invasion and epithelial-mesenchymal transition to include stromal-immune interactions during fibrosis [48, 49].

The concentrations of recombinant TGF-α used in this study (0.1–100 ng/mL) were within ranges commonly used in EGFR ligand–mediated signaling studies and elicited a dose-dependent profibrotic response in macrophages, although differences in response sensitivity were observed between macrophage models. Importantly, IEC-specific deletion of PIEZO1 attenuated intestinal fibrosis in vivo, highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting the epithelial PIEZO1-TGF-α axis.

In addition to reduced intestinal fibrosis, CKO mice also exhibited attenuated intestinal inflammation, suggesting a broader role of epithelial PIEZO1 in coordinating inflammatory and fibrotic responses.

Given that macrophages serve as central regulators of both inflammation and tissue remodeling, reduced epithelial PIEZO1 signaling may limit macrophage activation, thereby concurrently suppressing inflammatory responses and MMT-associated fibrogenesis. Supporting this notion, transcriptomic profiling of PIEZO1-deficient IECs revealed alterations in genes associated with epithelial signaling and homeostasis, including pathways related to cAMP signaling, calcium signaling, focal adhesion, and PI3K–Akt pathways. These signaling pathways are widely implicated in cellular mechanotransduction and may reflect downstream effects of PIEZO1-dependent signaling [49–52], reinforcing the role of PIEZO1 as a key upstream sensor that integrates biomechanical cues into fibrogenic responses.

This work also complements and extends prior studies that have identified MMT as a source of fibroblasts in organs such as the heart and kidney [4, 5]. By focusing on the intestinal epithelium, this study provides new insights into how epithelial mechanosensation contributes to fibrosis, highlighting a cross-compartment signaling axis in which mechanically activated IECs regulate macrophage reprogramming via the PKA/NF-κB P65 pathway.

However, several limitations should be noted. First, while TGF-α was identified as a critical mediator, the full secretome of PIEZO1-activated IECs remains incompletely characterized. Other cytokines might also contribute to MMT. Second, direct in vivo validation is needed to strengthen the causal link, such as macrophage-specific targeting of TGF-α signaling or exogenous TGF-α supplementation. Finally, the small sample size in the single-cell analysis (n = 6 per group) limits statistical power, so further studies with larger cohorts are required. Despite these limitations, the consistency of in vitro and in vivo findings supports the robustness of the proposed mechanism.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that intestinal epithelial PIEZO1 functions as a key mechanosensor that regulates MMT through induction of TGF-α secretion and subsequent activation of the PKA/NF-κB P65 signaling pathway. By elucidating this epithelial–immune signaling axis, these findings suggest that the PIEZO1–TGF-α pathway may represent a potential therapeutic target for intestinal fibrosis in Crohn’s disease.
