VID: A comprehensive dataset for violence detection in various contexts
Authors: Abu Bakar Siddique Mahi, Farhana Sultana Eshita, Tabassum Chowdhury, Rashik Rahman, Tanjina Helaly
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Security professionals and law enforcement, who presently have to manually go through hours of film to find minor incidents, are becoming more interested in automated crime and violence detection. Developments in computer vision and machine learning are enabling automated systems to quickly scan and mark relevant incidents, reducing the workload of human investigators. Most existing datasets tend to focus on too specific situations (e.g., vehicle violence). Typically, these datasets consist of a limited number of video clips gathered from various sources, such as YouTube. Furthermore, the lack of distinct action-based groups or precise diversity in these datasets may limit their broader usefulness. To overcome the constraints of existing datasets, we have developed a balanced dataset that includes 3020 video clips. This dataset is evenly divided between 1510 non-violent clips and 1510 violent clips, capturing a diverse range of real-world situations. The duration of the clips ranged from 3 to 12 seconds, and they were recorded by non-professional actors. The aim of this comprehensive and balanced approach is to offer an extended resource for the training and evaluation of automated video analysis systems.

Background

Violence is a widespread issue impacting societies world-wide. A report highlights that countries like Venezuela have crime index of 83.76; Papua New Guinea follows closely with 80.79 crime index; and South Africa has high crime rates [1]. With growing attention towards the automation of crime detection systems in videos, satisfying the demands for an extensive quantity of data is also increasing, which can be resolved through the proposed dataset. The previously available ARTLAB dataset [2] has only 350 videos, a limited variety of scenarios, and effects of lighting. Another existing dataset, MoLa InCar AR [3] consists of 6400 videos. Although it contains more data, they are created for a particular situation as the footages were recorded only in the car. The weapon dataset [4] encompasses 334 videos. Since the data are collected from the game, they are programmatic, less reliable and lack randomization than the real world. The NTU_CCTV [5] and Hockey fight dataset [6] both contain 1000 videos which were collected from YouTube. These types of data increase the false negative rate of the model. To confront these challenges, the proposed dataset entails substantial amount of data in real-time, classifying multi context scenarios, pose-estimated videos to recognize human action.

Data Description

With the potential to benefit professionals in security and law enforcement authorities, automated identification of illegal activities and violent incidents in video footage has emerged as an important aspect of research. Research on automated violence detection can be considerably benefited by the availability of a large, high-quality, generalized dataset based on violent occurrences. With the wide range of real-world violent incidents, these types of datasets make it easier to construct more reliable machine learning models with higher accuracy.

Violent acts are immoral and damaging to society, underlining the vital need for innovative technical solutions that can accurately recognize such instances. In a previous study [7], researchers enhanced the reliability of training their model for violence detection by including a large dataset consisting of over three thousand videos gathered from YouTube and CCTV. Another research proposal in [8], a flow-gated network architecture was created by combining the advantages of optical flow analysis with 3D convolutional neural networks (3D-CNNs). Utilizing the combined benefits of both methods to improve violence detection, this approach was created and assessed using a dataset of 2,000 security camera recordings. Another research work in [9], researchers developed a balanced 2,000-video dataset that included 1,000 violent and 1,000 non-violent segments in order to develop and evaluate automated violence detection systems.

The proposed video dataset is composed of a large number of videos that show both violent and non-violent activities that were taken from various places, backgrounds, and angles, as depicted in Fig. 1. The primary goal of this dataset is to aid in accurately identifying the particular human body motions associated with the observed behaviors. Pose-estimated data minimizes noise or irrelevant data from the films. Hence, we generate pose-estimated masked data to enhance the dataset's effectiveness in detecting movements or actions. Using precisely selected body movement variables, a system can differentiate between violent and non-violent activities.Fig. 1Some sample frames of our video dataset.Fig 1:

Some sample frames of our video dataset.

This dataset comprises 6040 videos of violence and non-violence that were collected from 72 voluntary participants. 3020 raw videos and 3020 masked videos are the categories into which these videos are divided. The raw videos are once again divided into two categories: violence (1510) and non-violence (1510). The non-violent daily activity clip depicts a wide range of everyday activities, including walking, working out etc. 10 of these non-violent clips were captured using CCTV cameras, and the remaining 1500 videos were recorded in different background settings. There are 1510 videos of violence, including 320 videos of domestic violence, 434 videos of street violence, 616 videos of classroom violence. There are 140 films featuring various forms of violence in various settings, with ten CCTV clips of violent acts included. Again, the masked videos are categorized as masked violence (1510) and masked non-violence (1510). There are 320 masked videos of domestic violence, 434 masked videos of street violence, 616 masked videos of classroom violence, and ten CCTV footage of violent actions are presented, along with 140 masked videos depicting different types of violence in different contexts as shown in Fig. 2.Fig. 2Description of the data separation.Fig 2:

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

The dataset was created by capturing most of the videos with smart phones and a few from CCTV cameras. The preponderance of the videos was captured in daylight. Nevertheless, we also incorporated videos captured in low-light conditions, particularly during the night, to improve the efficacy of computer vision model. To ensure the privacy of all individuals in our dataset, we implemented a face-blurring process for all video data. This process involved several steps to accurately detect and obscure facial features. Initially, each video frame was processed using a pre-trained Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG)-based face detector from the dlib library. The face detector was applied to the RGB frames to enhance detection accuracy and computational efficiency. Once faces were detected, bounding boxes were defined around each face region. These regions were then subjected to a Gaussian blur using OpenCV, which effectively anonymized the individuals by obscuring facial details while preserving the overall structure of the video. This approach ensured that the dataset complies with privacy standards, allowing for ethical use in subsequent analysis and model training. The processed frames were then compiled into a new video file, maintaining the original video's resolution and frame rate. The blurring procedure is shown in Algorithm 1. The original videos were masked with YOLOv7 Pose, a two-stage pose estimation algorithm that works in two steps: person identification and key point localization. The YOLOv7 Pose [10], which is an expansion of the one-shot posture detector, is also a multi-person keypoint detector that operates in a single stage. For pose estimation algorithm applied to video data, the process began with preprocessing the input frame, including letterbox resizing for compatibility with the model's input size. Subsequently, the frame underwent tensor conversion and was loaded onto the designated computation device. During inference, non-maximum suppression was employed to refine keypoint detections, followed by conversion of the output to a format containing keypoints. The annotated image was then prepared for display, with skeletons and keypoints plotted, facilitating visual interpretation. Finally, the algorithm returned the annotated image alongside the frames per second (fps) achieved during inference, providing insight into computational efficiency. This comprehensive approach ensured accurate pose estimation across video frames, crucial for various applications, from human behavior analysis to fitness tracking. The pose estimation procedure followed Algorithm 2. To evaluate the performance of the dataset and assess the potential of the dataset, we developed a custom model and analyzed the result. The details of the model and results are available in the supplementary document “Supplementary Document_Data analysis of VID dataset”.
