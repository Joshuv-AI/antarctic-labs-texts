Association of sella turcica bridging with class II malocclusion: a cephalometric study
Authors: Vidya Lohe, Simran Badki, Ravindra Kadu, Ravikat Sune, Mrunal Meshram, Shraddha Patel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aim of this prospective study was to investigate the prevalence of sella turcica bridging (STB) and to check whether sella can be considered as diagnostic marker for class II malocclusion. Secondarily, any possible association between STB and dental anomalies was evaluated.MethodsPanoramic and cephalometric radiographs taken for pre-orthodontic evaluation of 150 subjects (75 female and 75 males) in the age from 13 to 25 years were evaluated. The malocclusion and other dental anomalies were identified through clinical examination and radiographic confirmation of the diagnosis. The presence, extent of the bridging and dimensions of the sella turcica were evaluated on cephalometric radiographs. The link between STB and malocclusion was explored by chi square test and the student’s unpaired t test.ResultsOf one hundred and fifty subjects, 47 displayed Sella turcica bridging (STB); the majority had partial bridging (Type II, n = 34). The sellar anatomical types with regard to bridging were not linked to gender, but significant differences were observed among different category of malocclusion. Between subjects with STB and existence of class II malocclusion, highly significant (P = 0.0001) association was recorded. A statistically significant correlation (P = 0.0001) was discovered between the prevalence of STB (both partial and total) and the existence of teeth crowding. STB did not vary regarding the impaction of maxillary canines.ConclusionsThe findings of the current study indicate a definite link between Sella turcica bridging and class II malocclusion and teeth crowding. Sella turcica bridging STB did not vary in subjects with impaction of the maxillary canine, and gender does not influence it.

Background

The intracranial surface of the sphenoid bone contains an anatomical structure called the sella turcica (ST), which has three dimensions and resembles a saddle [1]. The sella point, located in the middle of the ST, is crucial for determining orthodontic cephalometric landmarks and is used in image analysis [2] to compare the therapeutic effects of skeletal malocclusions corrected by dentofacial orthopaedic appliances and assess changes in craniofacial growth [3, 4].

The pituitary gland’s surrounding posterior margin is referred to as the dorsum sellae while the term tuberculum sellae refers to anterior margin [5]. Neural crest cells and para-axial mesoderm are used to create the anterior and posterior walls of the ST independently [6]. Before the age of seven, ST has reached its full size, and numerous studies have demonstrated a connection between ST and the pituitary gland. Since the pituitary gland must complete its developmental stage before the ST may form [7], a growth issue in the pituitary gland will disrupt the ST’s morphology [8]. Growth hormone secretion is impacted by a malfunctioning pituitary gland, which is detrimental to the growth of bones and the body. Additionally, signalling conduction suggests that mutations in the homeobox genes in the cranial neural crest cells may affect how the dentition and midface develop [9]. Additionally, prior research has demonstrated that ST forms were important for the formation of neural crest cells and the pituitary gland. The fronto-nasal, maxillary, palatal, and mandibular developmental fields, which make up the craniofacial regions, can exhibit anomalies that are related to abnormalities in the ST/pituitary gland [6].

The ST bridge (STB) is an uncommon occurrence of interclinoid ligament (ICL) ossification and is a structural variant of the skeletal link between the anterior and posterior clinoid processes. Different types of ST were categorised by Axellson et al. based on their degree of bridging into three categories: total bridging, partial bridging, and no bridging at all. Additionally, nevoid basal cell carcinoma syndrome and Williams’s syndrome, genetic developmental disorders that can have an impact on the craniofacial region, are associated with STB [10]. Skeletal class III and class I malocclusions are also associated with STB [11, 12]. Further investigations are required to get additional data and information because considerable variations in the morphology of ST were discovered in people of various races in earlier studies. Dental specialists who regularly assess lateral cephalometric radiographs should be able to spot problems even before they can notice the patient’s malocclusion in its clinical manifestation. The present study sought to examine whether STB can be used as a diagnostic marker for dental malocclusion in light of the genetic evidence that both skeletal malocclusion and STB have, as well as the shared embryonic origins linked to both tissues.

Methods

This cross-sectional observational radiographic study was conducted over a 7-months period, from April 2021 to October 2021, in the Department of Oral Medicine and Radiology at the department of Oral Medicine and Radiology at Sharad Pawar Dental College, Datta Meghe Institute of Medical Sciences (Deemed to be university), Sawangi (M), Wardha, India after receiving consent from the Institutional Ethics committee (Datta Meghe Institute of Medical Sciences (Deemed to be University) / Institutional Ethics committee /2020-21/272, dated 15/04/2021). [DMIMS (DU)/IEC/2020-21/272, dated 15/04/2021]

The present study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study population

The standardized digital lateral cephalometric radiographs and panoramic radiographs of Seventy five males and seventy five females were taken for the pre-orthodontic evaluation during the period of April 2021 to October 2021were included and screened for the presence of STB and were taken for pre-orthodontic evaluation of the subjects. The radiographs were not recommended as a screening method in any scenario. Dental malocclusion was identified through clinical examination and radiographic confirmation of the diagnosis. 150 individuals (75 men and 75 women) made up the study sample. Cleft palate and lip, other craniofacial anomalies, syndromic conditions, trauma, chemotherapy, those with severe craniofacial abnormalities who had undergone or were currently undergoing orthodontic treatment, prior craniofacial surgeries, poor image quality, and missing either lateral or panoramic radiographs were all excluded.

Cephalometric analysis of sella turcica

All patients had their pre-treatment lateral cephalometric radiographs taken using a standardised method for assessing the cranial structures. The Planmeca Proline-EC/XC panoramic machine with cephalostat, standard source to subject distance, and standard exposure settings (72 kVp, 12 mA, and 1.2 s) was used to take the cephalograms. Using Trophy DICOM software, on the computer screen, the digital lateral cephalograms were traced and examined for the presence or lack of bridging. Only radiographs that accurately reproduced the ST region were selected. The principal author reviewed and measured all cephalometric radiographs. The mid-sagittal enlargement was adjusted to 100% uniformly for all cephalograms. All linear measurements were made with the help of Image works CR13.01 software.

Following the methodology of Silverman and Kisling, three linear dimensions of the ST were measured in the mid-sagittal plane: length, diameter, and depth [11]. Length: the length from the tuberculum sella to the dorsum sella tip, Depth: A line drawn from the deepest point on the floor to a line perpendicular to the line drawn above, antero-posterior (A-P) diameter: Line drawn from the posterior point on the posterior inner wall of the fossa to the tuberculum sella (Fig. 1).

The ST length (the distance from the tuberculum sella to the tip of the dorsum sella) and antero-posterior greatest diameter (the distance from the tuberculum sella to the furthest point on the inner wall of the pituitary fossa) were measured to determine the extent of an STB from each profile radiograph. It was made clear that the length measurement is an indicator of ICL (Interclinoid ligament) calcification rather than a measurement of actual bone.

Only radiographs that accurately reproduced the ST region were selected. The principal author reviewed and measured all cephalometric radiographs. The mid-sagittal enlargement was adjusted to 100% uniformly for all cephalograms. All linear measurements were made with the help of Image works CR13.01 software.

Using a calliper, all measurements were taken to the nearest 0.1 mm. Using a standardised scoring system, ST length and diameter were compared [13]. In brief, sella were classified as type I (no calcification) if their length was greater than or equal to three quarters of the largest antero-posterior diameter, type II (ICL partially calcified) if their length was less than or equal to three quarters, and type III (ICL completely calcified) if the diaphragm sella was radiographically visible. The largest posteroanterior ST diameter measurement was divided by four, and the result was compared to ST length.

Reliability of measurements

Using duplicate tracings of 10 radiographs taken on two separate occasions by the same author (VL), with a 2-week break between tracings, the random error was estimated [14]. In the present study, reliability was assessed by duplicate tracing of 10 randomly selected cephalograms performed by the same investigator at a two-week interval. The mean differences between the first and second measurements were statistically non-significant, indicating acceptable intra-examiner reproducibility. Measurement errors for the linear dimensions of the sella turcica ranged from 0.15 to 0.25 mm, which were considered clinically negligible and within acceptable limits for cephalometric studies.

Between the first and second measurements, the mean difference was not statistically significant. Sella measurement errors for length and diameter were 0.15 to 0.25 mm, which was regarded as a modest inaccuracy. (Figures 1 and 2).

Statistical analysis

Traditional descriptive statistics were used for the statistical analysis. The next set of inferential statistics used the chi square test and the student’s unpaired t test. Both Graph Pad Prism (7.0 version) and the Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS 27.0 version) were used to conduct the statistical analyses. P 0.05 served as the significance cut-off.

Results

In the current study, radiographs acquired for pre-orthodontic examination were used to analyze a total of 150 lateral cephalograms for evaluating the ST and 150 panoramic radiographs for evaluating the dental malocclusion. There were 150 individuals, 75 males and 75 females, ranging in age from 13 to 25. The class I, II, and III malocclusions were present in 92 (61.33%), 45 (30%), and 13 (8.66%) of the patient population, respectively.

Out of 150 patients, the cephalometric investigation showed that 103 did not have STB (Type I). Of all the participants, 47 displayed STB; the majority of these reported partial STB (Type II, n = 34), while 13 subjects displayed full STB (Type III). There were substantial differences in the overall proportions of Types I, II, and III STB between the participants with various dental malocclusions (Table 1). Among 103 Type I STB, 78 had Class I dental malocclusion, 18 had Class II, and 7 had Class III dental malocclusion. More than half (n = 20) of the 34 patients with partial bridging fell into the class II malocclusion category, whereas the remaining subjects (n = 8) and (n = 6) belonged to the class I and class III malocclusion categories. Of the 13 patients that had complete bridging, six subjects had class I malocclusion and seven had class II malocclusion. Between the prevalence of STB (both partial and total) and the existence of dental class II malocclusion among the study individuals, a statistically highly significant (P = 0.0001) association was recorded (Table 1).

Of these forty one subjects, 28(68.29%) and 13 (31.70%) subjects belonged to Type II and Type III STB respectively. Discussion

The current prospective study examined the prevalence of STB and its relationship to dental malocclusion and other dental defects, and it found a definite link between STB and crowding, impaction of the maxillary canine, and Angle’s class II malocclusion. The basis for this was prior research showing that individuals with this condition were more likely to have extremes in craniofacial skeletal variation and localised dental malformations [15, 16]. Furthermore, abnormalities linked to ST have been shown to be a component of human craniofacial disorders [17].

Over the years, numerous researches has been conducted to determine the incidence of STB among various population groups and to perhaps link bridging to dentofacial anomalies. According to the cephalometric analysis of the current study’s 150 participants, 47 (31.33%) of them had STB. Previous studies also noticed STB prevalence of 18.6% and 16.7% in patients receiving surgical orthodontic treatment [18, 19]. The highest distribution of partial STB was found among the forty-seven individuals, with 34 (68.08%) having partial and the remaining 13 (27.657%) having complete bridge. Past investigations also showed that incomplete STB was more prevalent [15, 16].

There were substantial differences in the proportions of Types I, II, and III STB between the participants with various dental malocclusions. While Angle’s class I malocclusion was common in type I STB participants, Angle’s class II malocclusion was much more frequent in partial STB subjects. Children with ST anomalies (mostly bridge) possess more protruded incisors and a more distal position of the maxilla and mandible than children with normal ST morphology [20]. The connection between sellar morphology and the kind of malocclusion was also described by Motwani et al. [21] Additionally, prior research suggested that STB is more frequently linked to skeletal class II and class III malocclusion [15, 16].

In the analysis of 40 lateral cephalometric pictures, Najim et al. found no statistically significant changes in the linear dimensions of ST across sexes [22]. The current investigation also revealed non-significant differences between men and women subjects’ sellar anatomical types in terms of bridging. Additionally, Arcos-Palomino and Ustrell-Torrent claimed that STB is highly influenced by age but unrelated to sex [23]. Evaluation of the incidence of dental malocclusion revealed a statistically significant difference between the type of ST and crowding (P = 0.0001). This can be attributed to the fact that the anterior part of ST is thought to form and develop primarily from neural crest cells and that dental epithelial progenitor cells differentiate through a series of sequential interactions with neural crest-derived mesenchyme [24, 25]. Therefore, in children with ST calcification, careful monitoring of the timing of tooth emergence is required. Additionally, while the majority of ST bridges can be found early in childhood, in a small number of instances, calcification forms over time and is only visible on lateral cephalometric radiographs acquired at a later stage [16].

In the current investigation, impacted maxillary canines were found in more than half of the STB patients (n = 27), with partial STB patients having a higher incidence. This is consistent with the findings of earlier research [26, 27]. In previous research, people with mal-positioned canine had a greater incidence of STB [13, 22, 28]. A startling correlation between STB and affected dogs raises the possibility that the same elements that affect ST development may also have an impact on canine development [29]. Without a doubt, as calcification in this area may or may not become visible during early development, it may help with the early detection of various disorders or serve as a diagnostic predictor of susceptibility for localised dental problems [16].

In the present investigation, cephalograms were used to examine the sellar anatomy. Cephalometry is a significant diagnostic tool, particularly in orthodontics, however due to its 2D nature, it only offers a limited amount of data. Studies using cephalometric radiographs have indicated higher rates of ST bridging than anatomical studies. [10, 1319] These variations in size and shape have been attributed to the clinoid processes, particularly the anterior ones, which can appear on lateral cephalograms as short, blunt structures or as elongated processes extending towards the posterior clinoid processes, with which they occasionally unite [10]. A bridge-like union between the bony processes may also appear on cephalometric radiographs due to the superimposition of the overlapping clinoid processes [19]. The results validate earlier information signifying a link between STB and Class II malocclusion and providing added information concerning its association with crowding and impacted canines.

From a clinical viewpoint, sella turcica bridging is often recognized on routine lateral cephalograms acquired for pre-orthodontic assessment. Though it should not be viewed as an independent diagnostic marker, recognition of STB can alert clinicians to the possibility of presence of allied craniofacial and dental anomalies. Identification of STB as early as possible may enable prompt evaluation and correlation with impacted canines, crowding, and skeletal abnormalities, thus providing holistic diagnosis and customized treatment planning for an individual. Accordingly, STB can be considered a valuable cephalometric finding that complements clinical and radiographic assessment during pre-orthodontic evaluation of the patient.

Two-dimensional cephalometric radiographs possess inherent limitations when evaluating sella turcica morphology. Superimposition of anatomical structures, projection errors, and inability to assess the structure in three dimensions may result in underestimation or overestimation of bridging compared with CBCT-based assessment. At the moment, 3D radiographs appear to be the most trustworthy research material. The ALARA rule, however, prohibits the use of CBCT as a routine diagnostic method in dentistry.

As the primary objective of this study was to evaluate the association between sella turcica bridging and malocclusion characteristics rather than age-related variations. Consequently, age was not incorporated as a covariate in the statistical analysis. However, the age may influence craniofacial growth and development and therefore could potentially act as a confounding factor.

Future studies with larger sample size and comprehensive clinical data are recommended to perform multivariate regression analyses and evaluate the independent contribution of STB to malocclusion and other dental anomalies.

Conclusions

The findings of this study demonstrate that patients with a Class II malocclusion much more frequently have a ST bridge. The ST’s dimensions showed no gender-specific differences between males and females. Dental anomalies, such as crowding, and STB are clearly related. The typical anatomy of the ST must be understood by orthodontists, and they must be able to identify any morphological variations that might be caused by pathologic disorders. In light of this, it is advised to include the ST in the typical analysis of cephalometric radiographs.
