Diagnosis of Functional Dyspepsia and Novel Gastrointestinal Functional Assessments
Authors: Tsutomu Takeda, Mariko Hojo, Akihito Nagahara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFunctional dyspepsia (FD) is a common disorder of gut-brain interaction characterized by postprandial distress and epigastric pain, which significantly impairs quality of life and increases healthcare burden. Although the Rome IV criteria and Japanese guidelines have refined its definition, the pathophysiology remains multifactorial, involving gastric motility abnormalities, visceral hypersensitivity, and autonomic dysfunction. Traditional diagnostic approaches, mainly based on clinical history and exclusion of organic diseases, are limited in elucidating the underlying mechanisms.SummaryRecent advances in gastrointestinal functional assessments have enabled more detailed evaluation of gastric accommodation, emptying, motility, and sensory function. Techniques such as barostat, antroduodenal manometry, gastric scintigraphy, and gastric emptying breath tests have been established in research settings, while newer modalities – including cine magnetic resonance imaging, body surface gastric mapping (BSGM), and wireless motility capsule (WMC) – offer noninvasive and comprehensive insights into gastric motor and sensory function. Furthermore, the use of endoscopy-based functional testing has expanded diagnostic capabilities. Autonomic nervous system testing, including heart rate variability analysis and exploratory wearable device approaches, provides additional perspectives on the gut-brain axis. Collectively, these tools have advanced the understanding of FD pathophysiology; nonetheless, their availability and standardization remain limited.Key MessagesFD is highly prevalent and burdensome, with a complex and multifactorial pathophysiology. A wide range of gastrointestinal functional tests has been developed from established barostat and scintigraphy to emerging noninvasive methods such as cine magnetic resonance imaging, endoscopy-based functional testing, WMC, and BSGM. These novel approaches can capture both motor and sensory abnormalities, offering new opportunities for personalized management of FD. Standardization, validation, and wider clinical implementation of these functional assessments are needed to translate research advances into routine practice.

Introduction

Functional dyspepsia (FD), characterized by symptoms such as epigastric pain and postprandial fullness, has a substantial impact on daily quality of life (QOL) of patients. According to a large multinational internet survey involving 24 countries, 40.3% of participants reported at least one disorder of gut-brain interaction (DGBI; formerly termed functional gastrointestinal disorder), and 7.2% satisfied the diagnostic criteria for FD [1]. In addition to adversely affecting healthcare expenditures, the economic losses attributable to impaired QOL are of considerable concern. Nevertheless, the underlying pathophysiology remains insufficiently elucidated, and a substantial proportion of cases are refractory to treatment.

In Japan, the FD clinical practice guidelines have recently been revised [2], contributing to a more structured understanding of the disorder. In daily clinical practice, the diagnosis of FD relies fundamentally on history taking to capture symptom profiles. Given the heterogeneity of symptoms, differentiation from organic diseases is critical. Recently, several novel gastrointestinal function tests have been introduced, and their potential value in pathophysiological assessment has been recognized. In this narrative review, we summarize the diagnostic approach to FD, with particular emphasis on new gastrointestinal functional assessments.

Diagnosis of FD

Definition of FD

FD is a DGBI characterized by epigastric-centered abdominal symptoms in the absence of any detectable organic disease. According to the Rome IV criteria, FD is defined as the presence of one or more of the following symptoms – postprandial fullness, early satiation, epigastric pain, or epigastric burning – in the absence of any organic, systemic, or metabolic disease that could explain the symptoms [3]. Criteria must be fulfilled for the last 3 months, with symptom onset at least 6 months before diagnosis. Among these four symptoms, patients with postprandial fullness and early satiation are classified as having postprandial distress syndrome, while those with epigastric pain and epigastric burning are classified as having epigastric pain syndrome. In contrast, the Japanese clinical practice guidelines define FD as a chronic disorder characterized by epigastric-centered abdominal symptoms, such as epigastric pain or postprandial fullness, in the absence of organic, systemic, or metabolic diseases that could account for the symptoms. This definition is considered more closely aligned with actual clinical practice [2]. The pathophysiology of FD is multifactorial, involving altered gastric acid secretion, visceral hypersensitivity, abnormalities of gastric and duodenal motility, and psychological factors. Consequently, the clinical manifestations are diverse, and careful differentiation from organic diseases is essential in the diagnostic process.

Differential Diagnosis and Clinical Evaluation of FD

Among the clinical evaluations and tests recommended by the guidelines for the diagnosis of FD, history taking remains the cornerstone for symptom assessment and physical examination [2, 4]. In history taking, the identification of alarm signs is crucial, as these serve as triggers for suspecting organic disease. Findings such as weight loss, recurrent vomiting, gastrointestinal bleeding, dysphagia, abdominal mass, fever, a family history of esophageal or gastric cancer, and the onset of new symptoms in older age should prompt consideration of organic disease and guide the differential diagnosis [2, 4]. FD frequently coexists with other DGBIs, such as gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) and irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) [5, 6]. Therefore, it is important to assess reflux symptoms, such as heartburn and bowel habits, as well as evaluate psychosocial factors and pay attention to symptom variations related to meals [2, 3, 7]. It has long been recognized that, similar to IBS, FD can develop following infectious gastroenteritis; therefore, a history of diarrhea or fever suggestive of prior enteritis should be taken into consideration [8]. In addition, medications such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and low-dose aspirin can cause dyspeptic symptoms, underscoring the importance of obtaining a detailed medication history [2]. A history of allergies may indicate eosinophilic gastrointestinal disorders [9], while a history of alcohol consumption may be related to liver disease or chronic pancreatitis. Gastroparesis can occur idiopathically and in association with conditions such as diabetes, Parkinson’s disease, and renal failure [10–12]; therefore, reviewing past medical history is essential. In superior mesenteric artery (SMA) syndrome, the characteristic presentation is postprandial exacerbation of epigastric pain accompanied by nausea and vomiting, which tends to worsen in the supine position, but may improve in the prone or left lateral decubitus position [13, 14]. In median arcuate ligament syndrome, epigastric pain during meals is a typical feature, and symptoms may be alleviated in the knee-chest position or during inspiration [15, 16]. On physical examination, attention should be paid to findings such as anemia, jaundice, abdominal masses, lymphadenopathy, and ascites. Laboratory evaluation may include complete blood counts, hepatobiliary enzymes, pancreatic enzymes, renal function tests, and inflammatory markers as clinically indicated [2, 17]. In addition, systemic conditions such as diabetes mellitus, thyroid disorders, and electrolyte abnormalities can affect gastrointestinal function and are useful considerations in the differential diagnosis [2, 17].

Self-Administered Symptom Questionnaire

FD symptoms are diverse, and it is often difficult to capture all of them within the limited time of a clinical consultation. Moreover, symptom patterns may fluctuate over time. Therefore, self-administered questionnaires provide an objective means of assessment. In Japan, tools such as the Gastrointestinal Symptom Rating Scale, Global Overall Symptom scale, Izumo scale [18], and Frequency Scale for the Symptoms of GERD [19] are commonly used. The Izumo scale is a questionnaire assessing abdominal symptom-related QOL, consisting of items related to GERD, FD, IBS, and constipation. This tool is useful for identifying comorbidity and distinguishing FD from GERD, IBS, or functional constipation. The Frequency Scale for the Symptoms of GERD enables a detailed evaluation of the frequency of GERD and FD symptoms. These questionnaires are also valuable for monitoring the disease course and assessing therapeutic efficacy and are recommended for the diagnosis of FD.

Diagnostic Testing for Helicobacter pylori Infection

Although the prevalence of H. pylori infection has been gradually declining in Japan, it remains a common condition encountered in daily clinical practice. Infected individuals may present with dyspeptic symptoms, such as epigastric pain or postprandial fullness, in a certain proportion of cases. Diagnostic tests for H. pylori infection include measurement of serum anti-H. pylori immunoglobulin G antibodies, urea breath test, stool antigen test, and endoscopy-based methods such as rapid urease test and histological examination. In recent years, fully automated genetic analysis systems using intragastric fluid have also become available [20]. In clinical practice in Japan, diagnostic methods include nucleic acid testing for H. pylori and the detection of clarithromycin resistance genes. Patients testing positive for H. pylori are eligible for eradication therapy; if symptoms resolve within 6–12 months following eradication, the diagnosis should be revised from FD to H. pylori-associated dyspepsia [21]. In clinical practice, symptoms commonly persist despite successful eradication of H. pylori, necessitating continued treatment directed at FD. Moreover, as the risk of gastric cancer remains even after eradication, independent of the presence or absence of symptoms, regular endoscopic surveillance is recommended.

Upper Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (Esophagogastroduodenoscopy)

For the diagnosis of FD, the exclusion of organic pathology is indispensable, highlighting the importance of upper gastrointestinal endoscopy. According to the revised Japanese guidelines [2], endoscopy is no longer mandatory for establishing the diagnosis of FD; nevertheless, it should be proactively performed in patients with suspected organic disease. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy should be considered particularly in patients who present with alarm features, have a family history of gastric cancer, or develop symptoms for the first time at an advanced age [2]. Furthermore, given that FD often overlaps with GERD, patients exhibiting reflux-related complaints (e.g., heartburn) are recommended to undergo endoscopic examination to rule out reflux esophagitis [2].

In recent years, the potential role of chronic duodenal mucosal inflammation in the pathogenesis of FD has attracted increasing interest. Image-enhanced endoscopy, by enabling superior visualization of mucosal architecture, is regarded as a promising tool for the accurate evaluation of duodenal villi. To date, endoscopic assessment of the duodenal mucosa has predominantly been described in the context of disorders such as celiac disease [22]. We focused on the duodenal bulb and examined its association with abdominal symptoms using image-enhanced endoscopy. Through linked color imaging, duodenal erythema can be more clearly visualized. Therefore, we investigated the relationship between the redness parameter (a*) of the duodenal bulb and symptoms in an elderly cohort with a mean age of 70.9 years using linked color imaging [23]. Correlation analysis demonstrated that the redness parameter (a*) of the duodenal bulb was significantly associated with epigastric pain-related QOL scores (Rho = 0.118, p = 0.041). However, in multiple regression analysis, no association was observed with epigastric pain-related QOL scores; instead, a negative association was identified with constipation-related QOL scores (β = −0.122, p = 0.027). Recent advances in image-enhanced and magnifying endoscopy have improved the ability to detect subtle mucosal changes, creating opportunities for the development of endoscopy-based diagnostic approaches in DGBIs. In parallel, the use of artificial intelligence (AI) has led to remarkable progress in endoscopy, with reports highlighting the potential of AI-based image models to detect subtle duodenal alterations in FD [24]. A diagnostic model to determine the presence of FD in patients with or without H. pylori infection has been developed. In H. pylori-negative patients, the model demonstrated a sensitivity of 58.3%, specificity of 100%, positive predictive value of 100%, negative predictive value of 77.3%, and an area under the curve of 0.85. These findings suggest the potential for AI-assisted endoscopic diagnosis of FD and highlight the prospect of further advances in novel AI-based diagnostic methods for this disorder.

Abdominal Imaging

Abdominal imaging techniques, including ultrasonography, computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), play an important role in ruling out organic pathology, particularly malignant tumors. Ultrasonography and CT are particularly useful for the differential diagnosis of hepatobiliary and pancreatic disorders [25–27]. In cases of eosinophilic gastrointestinal disease, CT may demonstrate thickening of the esophageal, gastric, or intestinal walls [28]. In chronic pancreatitis, ultrasonography and CT are useful for detecting intraductal calculi and diffuse calcification throughout the pancreas, while magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography may reveal irregular dilatation of the main pancreatic duct [26, 29]. Further evaluation can be performed with endoscopic ultrasonography or endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography. However, the symptoms of chronic pancreatitis often resemble those of FD, and differential diagnosis from early-stage chronic pancreatitis may be required in some cases [2, 29]. Exocrine pancreatic insufficiency or abnormal pancreatic enzyme levels may also be observed, although detailed evidence remains limited and further accumulation of clinical data is warranted [30–32]. The SMA syndrome arises from compression of the horizontal duodenum between the SMA and the aorta. On CT imaging, narrowing of the aortomesenteric angle is a characteristic finding in SMA syndrome [13]. Median arcuate ligament syndrome is characterized by extrinsic compression of the celiac artery by the median arcuate ligament. Diagnostic evaluation can be facilitated by Doppler ultrasonography or contrast-enhanced CT [16].

Gastrointestinal Function Tests for FD

A wide range of gastrointestinal function tests have been developed over the years [33]. However, at present, these tests are performed only in a limited number of research institutions, and their methodologies and clinical utility have not yet been fully established. In the Japanese guidelines (2021), the usefulness of gastrointestinal function tests in routine clinical practice is considered unclear, and routine testing is not recommended; such tests may be helpful for identifying pathophysiologic mechanisms (e.g., delayed gastric emptying or impaired accommodation) in selected cases but are generally limited to specialized/research settings [2]. This view is consistent with international guidance; both the ACG/CAG guideline and the Asian consensus do not recommend gastrointestinal function testing as a routine clinical procedure [4, 34]. A summary of these tests is provided in Table 1. Assessments of gastric emptying or motor function have employed fluoroscopy [35], ultrasonography [36, 37], intraluminal manometry [38, 39], scintigraphy [40, 41], and breath testing [42, 43]. In addition, gastric accommodation and visceral perception/sensitivity have also been evaluated using barostat-based methods and nutrient drinking tests [44, 45]. The selection and implementation of these examinations differ among research centers, and no standardized approach has yet been established. In Japan, imaging-based assessments of gastrointestinal function are not covered by national health insurance, which further limits their use in routine practice [46, 47].

Gastric Emptying Scintigraphy

Several methods have been developed for assessing gastric emptying, including scintigraphy, radiopaque marker testing, the acetaminophen absorption method, and the 13C-labeled breath test. Of these, gastric emptying scintigraphy (GES) is recognized as the gold standard technique, with standardized reference values established through international multicenter collaborations [48]. Standardization based on clinical guidelines has progressed, and gastric emptying is considered delayed when > 10% of a radiolabeled solid test meal remains in the stomach 4 h after ingestion [40]. Furthermore, in a study of healthy subjects, Tomita et al. [41] reported that scintigraphy correlated with the barostat, provided excellent assessment of gastric motility including accommodation, and demonstrated good reproducibility and interobserver agreement.

Contemporary guidance endorses 4-h solid meal GES with specified pretest conditions (withholding prokinetics, anticholinergics, opioids; maintaining euglycemia) to optimize interpretability [10, 40]. In healthy cohorts, performance characteristics show that the 4-h retention endpoint has the most favorable variability – inter-subject coefficient of variation about 9.6% and intra-subject coefficient of variation about 12.6% – supporting standardized 4-h protocols [49]. Severity grading at 4 h (11–20% mild, 21–35% moderate, 36–50% severe, >50% very severe retention) facilitates consistent reporting [40]. A systematic review/meta-analysis supports an association between optimally measured delayed gastric emptying and upper GI symptoms, underscoring clinical relevance [50]. As a noninvasive test, GES offers the advantages of directly quantifying solid meal emptying with consensus-based standardized protocols and established cutoff values; however, it is relatively time-consuming, requires nuclear medicine facilities, and is not universally available in routine practice [33, 40, 48]. Contemporary dosimetry data indicate that the effective dose from standard solid meal GES is low, roughly 0.2–1.2 mSv, which is generally lower than a routine upper GI fluoroscopic series, and can be further reduced with optimized protocols [51, 52].

In Asian countries, including Japan, gastric emptying tests such as scintigraphy are often not reimbursed and are unavailable in many institutions, which substantially limits their use in day-to-day clinical practice [53, 54]. Taken together, 4-h solid meal GES remains the standardized gold standard for quantifying gastric emptying – anchored by international normal values and a > 10% 4-h cutoff – and shows favorable reproducibility and clinical relevance, supporting its use when delayed gastric emptying is suspected [40, 48, 49].

Gastric Emptying Breath Test

The gastric emptying breath test is a noninvasive method for indirectly assessing gastric emptying. After ingestion of a test meal containing a 13C-labeled substrate (e.g., 13C-octanoic acid, 13C-acetate, or 13C-spirulina), the metabolized product 13CO2 is measured in exhaled breath [55, 56]. It has been shown that this test correlates well with GES [57] and has been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration [58]. According to the American College of Gastroenterology clinical guidelines, the gastric emptying breath test (13C-spirulina) is considered useful for evaluating gastric emptying in patients with suspected gastroparesis [10]. In particular, 13C-gastric emptying breath tests can serve as a practical alternative to scintigraphy and that the 13C-spirulina breath test reliably detects delayed gastric emptying in patients with upper gastrointestinal symptoms suggestive of gastroparesis [10, 58]. In a study from Japan, Urita et al. [59] showed that the combination of the lactulose hydrogen breath test and the 13C-acetate breath test provides a simple noninvasive method for evaluating both gastric emptying and orocecal transit time. Aoki et al. [60] reported comparative findings between the gastric emptying breath test and ultrasonography. Shindo et al. [61] examined the relationship between acylated ghrelin levels and gastric emptying using the 13C-acetate breath test in patients with FD and healthy subjects. The results showed delayed time to maximal concentration and reduced ghrelin levels in those with postprandial distress syndrome. Yamawaki et al. [62] evaluated the effects of acotiamide on gastric emptying using the breath test and reported that gastric emptying was improved in the acotiamide group compared with the group receiving proton pump inhibitor monotherapy. Using the 13C-breath test, Mori et al. [63] reported that gastric emptying was delayed in healthy women compared with healthy men (time to maximal concentration: 53.6 ± 19.3 vs. 42.7 ± 16.9 min, respectively, p = 0.04). This finding suggests that sex differences should also be taken into account in the management of FD.

International standardization efforts have demonstrated that 13C-gastric emptying breath testing correlates closely with scintigraphy, forming the methodological basis for its clinical use in motility disorders [64]. In a comprehensive review from Japan, Sanaka summarized analytic models, test meals, and methodological considerations that have guided the practical implementation of stable isotope breath tests in Japanese institutions [65]. A workshop organized by the Japanese Society of Smooth Muscle Research subsequently proposed unified protocols for test meals, sampling schedules, and analytic parameters, providing a reference framework for the use of 13C-breath testing in Japan [66].

The major advantages of the 13C-gastric emptying breath test are its noninvasive nature, freedom from radiation exposure, and the ability to be performed easily and repeatedly in clinical practice [65]. Because 13CO2 recovery reflects not only gastric emptying but also intestinal absorption, hepatic metabolism, and pulmonary excretion of CO2, abnormalities in these physiological processes have been considered potential confounders of 13C-gastric emptying breath tests [65]. However, in a large cohort study using the 13C-octanoic acid breath test, digestive and metabolic disorders – including pancreatic, hepatic, pulmonary disease, diabetes, and inflammatory bowel disease – had only minor effects on 13CO2 excretion parameters, suggesting that such confounding is usually limited in routine practice [67].

The 13C-gastric emptying breath test has potential for wider adoption, as many institutions are already equipped with analyzers for 13CO2 measurement due to the use of the 13C-urea breath test for H. pylori diagnosis. Although this test has not been approved for national health insurance coverage in Japan and is not yet widely used, its noninvasive nature and lack of radiation exposure make it a promising tool for future clinical application.

Wireless Motility Capsule

The wireless motility capsule (WMC) is a noninvasive test that measures pH, pressure, and temperature throughout the gastrointestinal tract, allowing assessment of gastric emptying as well as small and large bowel transit times [58]. Studies comparing antroduodenal manometry (ADM) with the WMC have reported that the capsule is primarily emptied from the stomach in association with the occurrence of the phase III migrating motor complex, rather than in relation to meal ingestion [68]. Kuo et al. [69] demonstrated that WMC provides good diagnostic accuracy for gastroparesis, showing a strong correlation with the 4-h value of GES (r = 0.73), and emphasized its advantages as a noninvasive method without radiation exposure. In studies evaluating WMC findings in patients with gastroparesis, no association was observed between upper gastrointestinal symptoms and WMC transit parameters, whereas lower gastrointestinal symptoms were associated with transit and contractility abnormalities [70]. In a multicenter study using WMC, Sarosiek et al. [71] reported that patients with gastroparesis had significantly prolonged gastric emptying time and colonic transit time compared with healthy controls. The WMC enables the measurement of regional transit times and has demonstrated that patients with gastroparesis may also have colonic motility disorders, which could contribute to their symptom presentation [71]. Recent machine learning work demonstrated that gastric emptying time obtained by the WMC can replace scintigraphy without loss of predictive accuracy in treatment response models of suspected gastroparesis [72].

In contemporary clinical pathways, WMC is positioned as a complementary tool rather than a first-line investigation, and current guidelines for gastroparesis regard it as an alternative to scintigraphic gastric emptying, particularly when multiregional dysmotility is suspected or standardized scintigraphy is unavailable or inconclusive [10, 73]. The principal strengths of WMC are the acquisition of whole-gut regional transit and pressure profiles in a single, radiation-free study and its generally good tolerability, which reduces procedural burden compared with repeated imaging sessions [58, 74, 75]. On the other hand, WMC requires dedicated hardware and software, as well as specific expertise in data interpretation, and is associated with higher per-test costs than conventional scintigraphy or breath tests [76]. In Japan, WMC is currently not covered by the national health insurance, and its use in day-to-day clinical practice is therefore confined to a few specialized or research centers. WMC has been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for the measurement of gastric emptying and represents a promising tool for advancing the understanding of gastrointestinal motor disorders [74]. However, clinical implementation remains limited due to issues related to reimbursement and standardization. Thus, further validation in larger, multicenter, studies is warranted.

Upper Gastrointestinal Barium Meal Study

The barium meal study involves the ingestion of a barium suspension, with evaluation of the morphology and transit of the esophagus, stomach, and duodenum under fluoroscopy. This method is widely used for excluding organic disease. Historically, its primary purpose has been the assessment of organic pathology or gastroduodenal obstruction; therefore, relatively few studies have focused on its usefulness in evaluating motor function. Previous studies using electrogastrography (EGG) have reported a stimulatory effect of barium on gastric peristaltic activity [77]. Pharmacological interventions on gastric emptying have been examined in previous studies [78, 79]; however, data specifically addressing FD remain scarce [80, 81]. In an 8-year retrospective study of gastroparesis, Levin et al. [35] reported reduced gastric peristaltic activity and even absence of peristalsis in some cases. However, limitations such as insufficient quantification and standardization, as well as concerns regarding radiation exposure, restrict its role to that of a supplementary tool for functional assessment in FD. In line with major guidance, 4-h GES is the standard when quantitative measurement of gastric emptying is required; by contrast, upper gastrointestinal barium studies are not validated for quantifying gastric emptying and are used primarily to exclude structural disease [10, 82].

Barostat

The technique involves the introduction of a thin polyethylene bag into the stomach, which is subsequently inflated to allow measurement of intragastric pressure as well as evaluation of gastric compliance [83]. Tack et al. [84] reported impaired gastric accommodation in 40% of patients with FD, which was associated with symptoms of early satiety. In studies evaluating the therapeutic effect of buspirone in FD, significant improvement in gastric accommodation was observed [85]. The barostat has greatly contributed to understanding the pathophysiology of FD by enabling the assessment of gastric accommodation, sensory thresholds, and related parameters. In combination with patient-reported questionnaires, this approach permits quantitative assessment of visceral sensitivity.

Methodologically, the gastric barostat is regarded as the research gold standard for direct measurement of meal-induced accommodation and proximal gastric tone [86, 87]. Within-center reproducibility has been demonstrated in both healthy volunteers and dyspeptic patients, whereas methodological heterogeneity and the lack of multicenter reference standards limit broader implementation [87, 88]. In Japan, barostat testing is not routinely recommended in typical FD and is largely confined to tertiary/research centers in line with national guidance [2]. Practical pitfalls include potential balloon-induced modulation of fundic tone, possible discomfort-related autonomic effects, and protocol heterogeneity (bag material, inflation steps/pressure targets), all of which necessitate careful control of confounders [87–90]. Consequently, contemporary consensus documents position the barostat as a research tool to be reserved for selected indications in specialized units rather than a routine test for FD [91].

Satiety Drinking Test

The test involves ingestion of either water or a caloric liquid meal at a standardized rate, with assessment of the maximum tolerated volume and the severity of symptom induction, thereby providing an evaluation of postprandial gastric sensorimotor function [84]. Studies have demonstrated that, relative to healthy individuals, patients with FD exhibit a significantly lower maximum tolerated volume and more intense symptom responses to liquid meal ingestion [92]. Accordingly, this test represents a convenient and clinically meaningful method for the evaluation of visceral hypersensitivity. Considerable heterogeneity exists among researchers in terms of the liquid used, the volume administered, and the rate of ingestion, reflecting a lack of standardized methodology [93].

In Japan, GI function tests are not used routinely in typical FD and are generally confined to specialized/research settings [2]. Lower maximum tolerated intake (volume/calories) in FD and strong correlation with barostat-measured accommodation, supporting mechanistic validity [92]. The slow nutrient drinking test demonstrates short-term test-retest reproducibility and symptom endpoint coupling in both healthy controls and FD cohorts [94]. Recent Japanese data indicate the test is feasible and minimally invasive in clinical FD cohorts, reinforcing outpatient practicality [46]. Overall, drink tests provide a simple, minimally invasive probe of postprandial gastric sensorimotor function and symptom susceptibility, but owing to protocol heterogeneity and limited normative/FD-specific thresholds, they are best used as adjunctive tools rather than stand-alone diagnostics, with further standardization needed for broader clinical adoption [95–97].

Single-Photon Emission Computed Tomography

Single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) is a nuclear medicine technique in which intravenously administered 99mTc-pertechnetate accumulates in the gastric mucosa, allowing three-dimensional evaluation of gastric morphology and volume using a gamma camera [98]. In contrast to the traditional barostat, this technique offers the advantage of noninvasive assessment of postprandial fundic accommodation. Nevertheless, as no external distending pressure is applied to the gastric wall, it has been reported to be inadequate for evaluating alterations in gastric tone and relaxation [99]. While the procedure is relatively low in invasiveness, the drawbacks of radiation exposure and high cost have restricted its application to a limited number of specialized institutions.

In Japan, functional testing is not routinely recommended for typical FD; accordingly, SPECT gastric volumetry is generally limited to tertiary nuclear medicine centers [2]. Standardized 99mTc-pertechnetate SPECT protocols enable protocolized quantification of postprandial total gastric volume/accommodation in clinical and research workflows [100, 101]. Reproducibility of SPECT-derived gastric accommodation has been demonstrated – same day and different time repeats in healthy volunteers [102], and intra-/inter-individual reproducibility across protocols [103]. A large performance study characterized inter- and intra-individual variability, supporting reliability for intervention designs [104]. Low-dose 99mTc-pertechnetate SPECT protocols maintain image quality for gastric accommodation measurements, reducing radiation burden and improving feasibility [101]. Despite these strengths, inter-laboratory standardization and widely accepted reference ranges for FD remain limited; thus, current use is typically adjunctive in tertiary settings [33, 86, 96].

Endoscopy-Based Functional Testing

Endoscopy-based functional testing has long been explored as a means to quantify gastric distension-related perception and tone during routine upper endoscopy. An early study measured intragastric pressure during air insufflation to estimate visceral perception thresholds in FD [105]. More recently, simplified and standardized approaches have been proposed, including methods for objective intragastric pressure measurement during endoscopy and endoscopy-assisted protocols aimed at probing gastric hypersensitivity [106, 107]. In parallel, a pressure-regulated endoscopic insufflation concept (“endoscopic barostat”) has been investigated as a potential platform to maintain stable preset intragastric pressure and probe distension-related sensory responses relevant to FD [108].

In recent years, the Endoscopic Pressure Study Integrated System (EPSIS) has attracted attention as a novel endoscopy-based method for evaluating gastrointestinal function [107]. The EPSIS technique consists of insufflating CO2 into the stomach under endoscopic visualization, thereby enabling the measurement of intragastric pressure in parallel with real-time monitoring of symptom occurrence. In a comparative study, Momma et al. [109] demonstrated that patients with treatment-resistant FD developed symptoms at lower levels of CO2 insufflation and intragastric pressure than non-FD controls and that the frequency of belching was also significantly reduced. These findings indicate that patients with drug-resistant FD perceive symptoms at relatively low intragastric pressures and insufflation volumes, whereas those without FD are less likely to experience symptoms even at higher pressures. This suggests that endoscopy-based functional testing may be useful for elucidating the pathophysiology of FD, and further evidence may establish its contribution to the diagnostic process. Importantly, safety data suggest low complication rates comparable to routine upper endoscopy [110]; current use is best considered adjunctive/investigational in specialized settings. Taken together, multicenter validation with standardized protocols is still needed to establish reproducibility, reference ranges, and clinical utility for FD.

Antroduodenal Manometry

Gastroduodenal manometry is a test in which pressure sensors on an intraluminal catheter record contraction waves of the stomach and duodenum, allowing analysis of motor patterns during both fasting and postprandial states. In patients presenting with persistent upper abdominal symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, or early satiety in the absence of structural abnormalities, gastroduodenal manometry is useful for diagnosing primary or secondary gastrointestinal motor disorders. It has contributed to elucidating the pathophysiology of conditions associated with nausea and vomiting, generalized motor disorders, FD, and gastroparesis [38, 39, 111]. Patcharatrakul et al. [38] reported that gastrointestinal motility testing provides clinical benefit in a subset of patients, including those with suspected small intestinal pseudo-obstruction, unexplained abdominal pain accompanied by nausea or vomiting, and cases requiring exclusion of diffuse gastrointestinal motor disorders. In recent years, the introduction of high-resolution manometry has enabled more detailed assessment of gastrointestinal motility than conventional methods [112]. Soliman et al. [112] investigated healthy subjects and patients with DGBI, including FD. Compared with healthy individuals, patients with DGBI exhibited reduced duodenal contractions during phase II with respect to amplitude, frequency, and contraction integral per sensor. In addition, antral contraction integral per sensor was lower in patients with FD during phase III [112]. These findings may contribute to a better understanding of antral and duodenal motility and the pathophysiology of FD and DGBI.

Expert guidance does not recommend routine ADM for typical FD [58, 91]. ADM can help differentiate neuropathic from myopathic gastric or proximal small bowel dysfunction and reveal pseudo-obstruction-like patterns and is therefore considered selectively in severe or refractory cases after structural disease is excluded, typically in tertiary centers [58, 91]. While within-center protocols can yield consistent MMC and fed-response metrics, day-to-day variability, sedation effects, and interobserver differences limit generalizability, and multicenter standardization with shared reference ranges remains limited [91]. Interpretation is also vulnerable to artifacts – typically simultaneous pressure rises due to cough, movement, or straining – so careful annotation and exclusion are required [75, 91]. ADM requires specialized catheters/equipment and prolonged recordings and can be uncomfortable, contributing to limited availability and patient burden. Given these constraints, in FD it is positioned as an adjunct for phenotyping severe or refractory cases rather than a first-line diagnostic test [33, 58, 91].

Ultrasonography for Functional Assessment

Noninvasive abdominal ultrasonography has been applied to the evaluation of gastric motility. Gastric function can be assessed before and after ingestion of liquid or test meals, and this approach has been used to evaluate antral contractility, gastric emptying, duodenogastric reflux, and fundic relaxation [36, 113–115] (Fig. 1). In practice, the antral wall contour can be traced on transverse images to quantify the antral cross-sectional area and contractile change, and serial measurements may be used to estimate meal retention/emptying (Fig. 1c, d). Beyond diagnostic applications, ultrasonography has been employed to evaluate the effects of pharmacological therapy in FD. Kusunoki et al. [37] investigated the efficacy of acotiamide using real-time ultrasonography and demonstrated significant enhancement of gastric emptying rates, as well as the gastric accommodation reflex in patients with FD. In addition, a three-dimensional sonographic method has been developed to improve visualization of gastric structures. This technique has been reported as a novel approach for assessing parameters such as gastric volume [116, 117].

In outpatient settings, ultrasonography shows good correlation with scintigraphic gastric emptying [118]. Standardized real-time ultrasonography protocols demonstrate acceptable reproducibility and clinical applicability for measuring gastric emptying [119]. FD-specific multicenter reference ranges remain limited; accordingly, ultrasonography is positioned as an adjunct for phenotyping and therapy monitoring in FD rather than as a stand-alone diagnostic test [120].

On balance, abdominal ultrasonography may be influenced by operator-dependent imaging conditions and factors such as body habitus and intestinal gas. Nevertheless, it offers significant advantages, including the absence of radiation exposure, the ability to perform real-time and repeated assessments, relatively low cost, and high safety, making it applicable to a wide range of populations [75, 120]. Thus, ultrasonography is considered a valuable tool for gastrointestinal functional testing. Future challenges include the standardization of examination protocols and measurement criteria across institutions [120].

Cine MRI

In recent years, cine MRI has been applied to the evaluation of gastric function, and its utility has also been reported in academic meetings [121]. The use of MRI enables the assessment of gastrointestinal motility [122–125], allowing disease evaluation without exposure to radiation or the need for invasive procedures. Cine MRI is a technique that acquires rapid, sequential MR images of the same region. This approach shows considerable promise for evaluating dynamic gastric function and may provide novel insights into the pathophysiology of FD. Cine MRI has been applied to the evaluation of gastric motility in healthy individuals [126–129], in patients after pylorus-preserving gastrectomy [130], and in those with Parkinson’s disease [131]. There are currently few studies on its usefulness in FD [132, 133].

At our institution, cine MRI has recently been utilized as a modality for the evaluation of gastrointestinal function [133]. It permits the observation of sequential arbitrary planes and offers acceptable reproducibility (Fig. 2). In our study, cine MRI was performed before and after ingestion of a standardized test meal in both healthy individuals and patients diagnosed with FD. Gastric motility was assessed by measuring the short-axis diameter, contraction amplitude, contraction frequency, peristaltic wave height, peristaltic wave velocity, and the gastric motility index. The study demonstrated that contraction amplitude, peristaltic wave height, and overall gastric motility in the gastric body were significantly greater in healthy subjects than in patients with FD. Importantly, cine MRI was shown to permit the evaluation of gastric motor function, including postprandial adaptive relaxation. In addition, its clinical utility as a novel gastrointestinal functional test continues to be explored.

In Japan, cine MRI for gastric motility is not part of routine insured FD care and is used selectively in tertiary centers. Cine MRI protocols are feasible in outpatient research workflows and show acceptable within-center reproducibility for gastric volume; MRI meal-only volumes align with scintigraphy, supporting emptying assessment [134]. Nonetheless, inter-site variability in meal protocols, subject positioning, MRI sequences, segmentation methods, and outcome modeling persists; a systematic review called for standardized protocols and the establishment of reference values for MRI gastric emptying [135]. Given that FD-specific cine MRI evidence remains limited and largely single-center, and that multicenter standardization of MRI protocols is still lacking, cine MRI is best considered adjunctive in specialized settings pending broader validation [135, 136].

Electrogastrography

Percutaneous EGG has been reported as a method for assessing gastric function [137]. EGG is a noninvasive technique that records gastric myoelectrical slow-wave activity from cutaneous electrodes over the stomach, with consensus and validation studies showing that surface EGG signals correlate with serosal recordings, distinguish normal 3-cpm rhythms from dysrhythmias, and detect abnormal postprandial responses in patients with nausea, vomiting, and dyspepsia [75, 137, 138]. Despite these strengths, both the American Motility Society task force document and subsequent methodological reviews emphasize important limitations – including susceptibility to artifacts, lack of fully standardized protocols and reference values, and modest diagnostic specificity – which have restricted EGG to a mainly adjunctive or research tool rather than a routine clinical test [137, 138]. Japanese experience with multichannel EGG has been summarized in a review describing methodological advances, quantitative indices such as the percentage of time in normogastria and slow wave coupling, and clinical applications in gastric motility disorders [139]. Recent systematic review and meta-analytic data in FD indicate that conventional cutaneous EGG can consistently detect abnormalities in gastric slow wave rhythms, with reduced time in normogastria and increased bradygastria and tachygastria compared with healthy controls [140]. However, clinical correlations with symptom patterns are inconsistent and EGG methodologies are heterogeneous, underscoring the need for more robust and spatially resolved techniques to characterize gastric dysrhythmias [140].

In recent years, the quality of recordings has gradually improved, and body surface gastric mapping (BSGM) has been reported as a novel method for evaluating gastric function. BSGM records gastric electrical activity from the body surface using a high-density electrode array, enabling the visualization of conduction abnormalities and spatial patterns that are difficult to capture with conventional EGG. Compared with frequency-based analysis alone, this approach allows the noninvasive detection of spatial abnormalities, such as disordered conduction and retrograde propagation [141]. In a study using BSGM in adolescents and young adults aged 12–21 years, including healthy controls, patients with FD, and patients with gastroparesis, three distinct BSGM phenotypes were identified: normal, delayed, and low-stability/low-amplitude patterns [142]. BSGM phenotypes were associated with differences in symptoms (e.g., nausea), physical health, and psychometric measures (e.g., anxiety), suggesting potential utility for personalized management tailored to underlying pathophysiology. Furthermore, BSGM has been used to evaluate the impact of gastric distension induced by the liquid nutrient drink test (LNDT) on myoelectrical activity in healthy subjects [143]. The LNDT revealed that the principal gastric frequency was significantly decreased, the Gastric Alimetry Rhythm Index was reduced, and nausea was increased. BSGM assessment during the LNDT further demonstrated that excessive gastric distension induces abnormal gastric myoelectrical activity, which is closely associated with the occurrence of upper gastrointestinal symptoms.

Current evidence suggests that these technologies are emerging adjunctive tools that can provide additional phenotypic information beyond conventional investigations such as endoscopy and gastric emptying tests, particularly in patients with chronic or unexplained gastroduodenal symptoms [144, 145]. BSGM provides standardized spectral and spatial metrics with improved signal quality and more robust artifact handling than conventional EGG, and its noninvasive, wearable format results in a relatively modest patient burden despite the need for several hours of recording [145, 146]. However, the requirement for proprietary hardware, dedicated analysis software, and trained readers currently restricts access to a relatively small number of expert centers, even though multicenter and international cohorts have now established normative ranges and demonstrated good short- and long-term reproducibility of BSGM metrics [145–147]. Although these novel devices have not yet been introduced in Japan, they are expected to contribute to elucidating the role of gastric myoelectrical function in the pathophysiology of neurogastro-duodenal disorders.

Evaluation of the Autonomic Nervous System

The autonomic nervous system (ANS) plays a crucial role in normal gastrointestinal function, and impaired vagal signaling in patients with FD may contribute to pathophysiological alterations [148, 149]. Several methods for the evaluation of the ANS have been reported, including baroreflex sensitivity, plasma catecholamine levels, cardiac sympathetic neuroimaging, electrodermal activity, and heart rate variability (HRV) [149]. Among these, HRV has been used to evaluate ANS function in the gastrointestinal field [150] and has been used in assessing the efficacy of vagal nerve stimulation for FD [151]. A systematic review of HRV studies investigating the relationship between the ANS and both FD and gastroparesis demonstrated that 12 out of 13 studies reported associations of these conditions with reduced vagal tone and/or increased sympathetic activity [149].

Regarding the relationship between autonomic dysfunction and FD, earlier FD-specific studies using 24-h Holter HRV combined with cardiovascular reflex tests showed significantly reduced vagally mediated indices such as high-frequency power and root mean square of successive differences in patients with dysmotility-like FD compared with healthy volunteers [152]. Another 24-h HRV study that assessed basal autonomic activity and autonomic responses to a meal and to cold pressor and mental arithmetic stressors, and examined the effects of the autonomic agent tofisopam, reported low HF, elevated LF/HF ratios, delayed recovery of autonomic indices after stress, and partial improvement of both HRV parameters and dyspeptic symptoms with tofisopam, and concluded that imbalanced autonomic function and vulnerability to recovery from external stimuli are present in patients with FD [153]. More recently, wearable devices have been reported as a novel method of assessment [154]. In a study including patients with FD and healthy controls, autonomic nervous activity – specifically low-frequency/high-frequency ratio and high-frequency power – was continuously recorded in real time over 24 h using a T-shirt-type wearable device, while daily life events and abdominal symptoms were simultaneously documented via a smartphone application. The main findings demonstrated that patients with FD exhibited significantly more abnormal autonomic activity compared with healthy controls, which correlated with dyspeptic symptoms and was negatively associated with QOL [154]. This approach allows simple assessment of sympathetic and parasympathetic activity indices and is expected to contribute to future clinical management.

In a broader context, a systematic review of HRV in GERD and functional gastrointestinal disorders, including FD and gastroparesis, concluded that HRV is a noninvasive and relatively simple method for assessing autonomic function and can be used to quantify autonomic responses to various interventions but emphasized substantial heterogeneity in recording and analysis protocols and noted that HRV primarily reflects cardiac autonomic output [149]. Overall, these findings are consistent with a contributory role of autonomic imbalance, particularly reduced vagal activity, in at least a subset of patients with FD and suggest that HRV-based assessments may be useful noninvasive tools for pathophysiological and interventional research, although existing FD studies are small and heterogeneous and, to our knowledge, FD-specific test-retest reproducibility and clinically useful cutoff values have not yet been established [149, 152, 153].

Clinical Positioning and Comparative Considerations

In typical FD, current guidelines prioritize excluding structural disease (selective endoscopy based on alarm features/risk) and a H. pylori test-and-treat strategy. Routine physiologic testing is not recommended; rather, functional assessments should be used selectively to address a specific clinical question, usually in specialist/tertiary settings [2, 4, 7]. Gastric Emptying

When delayed gastric emptying is clinically suspected, 4-h solid meal GES is the gold standard, anchored by international multicenter normal values and consensus thresholds, with specified pretest conditions to optimize interpretability [10, 40, 48]. GES has favorable test characteristics – most reproducible at 4 hours – and its results correlate in clinically meaningful ways with upper GI symptoms, supporting selective use to phenotype suspected gastroparesis overlap rather than routine testing for all FD [49, 50]. As non-radiative alternatives, gastric emptying breath tests show reasonable agreement with scintigraphy under standardized conditions, but availability and protocol variability generally position them as adjunctive tools [56, 57, 155, 156]. WMC can provide a pan-gut transit profile and may complement emptying assessment in selected patients with suspected generalized dysmotility; however, access, cost, and interpretive variability typically reserve it for tertiary settings and specific questions rather than routine FD workup [10, 58, 157, 158]. By contrast, barium studies are useful for structural evaluation but are not validated for quantitative assessment of gastric emptying and should not be used as a substitute for 4-h GES when objective measurement is required [82].

Gastric Accommodation and Visceral Perception/Sensitivity

The gastric barostat remains the research gold standard for meal-induced accommodation and tone but is invasive and methodologically heterogeneous; contemporary guidance reserves it for selected indications in expert centers [86, 90]. SPECT gastric volumetry provides a noninvasive quantification of meal-induced gastric volume (a surrogate of accommodation) using standardized, repeatable imaging protocols; however, it primarily measures volume rather than directly assessing tone/compliance and is typically used as an adjunct in tertiary/expert settings [100, 101]. The satiety/slow nutrient drinking test provides a simple bedside assessment of gastric sensorimotor sensitivity and correlates with impaired accommodation on barostat testing, but protocol heterogeneity and limited standardization restrict generalization; thus, it is primarily adjunctive [92, 94, 159]. Endoscopy-based functional testing can also contribute to sensorimotor phenotyping in selected patients. Among these, EPSIS – originally developed and most commonly applied in the GERD field – can capture symptom-pressure relationships during routine endoscopy and appears feasible with low complication rates; however, FD-specific multicenter validation is still lacking, and its current role in FD should be regarded as investigational/adjunctive [107, 160].

Gastric Motor Function

ADM is not recommended routinely in typical FD; it may be considered selectively when severe or refractory symptoms raise concern for (proximal) small bowel dysmotility after structural disease is excluded, and it is generally performed in tertiary expert centers [58, 91]. Abdominal ultrasonography provides radiation-free, repeatable, real-time assessments and shows reasonable correlation with scintigraphic gastric emptying; however, its operator-dependent nature limits standardization. Accordingly, it is best positioned as an adjunctive tool for phenotyping and therapy monitoring rather than as a stand-alone diagnostic test in FD [118–120]. Cine MRI enables radiation-free, dynamic assessment of gastric volume and motility with acceptable within-center reproducibility; however, multicenter standardization and FD-specific reference ranges remain limited. Accordingly, its clinical role is best considered emerging and selective [134, 135].

Neurogastroenterological Regulation

Electrophysiologic approaches (EGG/BSGM) and ANS assessments (e.g., HRV) can support mechanistic phenotyping in research. BSGM in particular is advancing toward higher resolution, spatially informative biomarkers compared with traditional EGG, but heterogeneous protocols, limited FD-specific cutoffs, and incomplete reproducibility/standardization generally position these modalities as non-routine tools outside specialized settings [145, 149, 152, 153].

Conclusion

This review summarized the diagnostic methods for FD and the examinations required for accurate assessment. Although upper gastrointestinal endoscopy is no longer mandatory for the diagnosis of FD, it should be actively performed when organic disease is suspected based on history or physical examination. Self-administered questionnaires can aid symptom characterization and follow-up in FD, and an H. pylori test-and-treat strategy helps identify H. pylori-associated dyspepsia. In recent years, new insights have been reported from gastrointestinal function tests. Based on the assessment of both motor and sensory impairments, these methods may support the development of personalized therapeutic strategies for FD. Advancing these functional assessments into routine clinical practice necessitates further work on standardization, validation, and broader dissemination. Priority next steps for clinical implementation include (i) insurance coverage and reimbursement to enable broader access, (ii) standardized protocols for test performance and interpretation, and (iii) multicenter validation with normative ranges; broader dissemination of these functional assessments will support mechanistic phenotyping in FD and, ultimately, the integration of targeted functional testing into diagnostic algorithms. As the pathophysiology of FD continues to be elucidated, further advances in its diagnosis can be anticipated.
