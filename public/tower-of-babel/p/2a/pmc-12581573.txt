Recent insights into the pathophysiology and implications for surgery in atherosclerosis
Authors: Lingshan Liu, Zhen Wang, Liang Ma, Shengjun Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAtherosclerosis is a leading cause of cardiovascular disease. Recent advances in molecular biology have deepened our understanding of its pathogenesis and opened new avenues for surgical intervention.MethodsThis review integrates recent findings on key mechanisms—such as endoplasmic reticulum stress, oxidative imbalance, and immune-metabolic dysregulation—with progress in surgical and interventional strategies.ResultsNovel targets like UPR pathways, mitochondrial ROS, and microRNAs have shown therapeutic promise. RNA-based lipid-lowering therapies and PCSK9 inhibitors offer alternatives to statins. In parallel, innovations in PCI and CABG—such as next-generation stents, multi-arterial grafting, and engineered vascular conduits—have improved safety and efficacy. Advanced imaging techniques further enhance diagnosis and procedural planning.ConclusionIntegrating molecular insights with evolving surgical approaches is transforming atherosclerosis management. A precision strategy combining targeted therapy with individualized revascularization holds promise for better outcomes.

Overview

Atherosclerosis is a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by the accumulation of lipids, fibrous materials, and calcium minerals, leading to the formation of atherosclerotic plaques in the intima of large and medium-sized arteries. It remains a prominent cause of coronary artery disease (CAD), peripheral artery disease (PAD), and cerebrovascular disease [1, 2]. Despite numerous hypotheses explaining the pathogenesis and progression of atherosclerosis based on inflammation and immunity, one certainty prevails: the rupture of an atherosclerotic plaque triggers the formation of red thrombi within blood vessels, ultimately precipitating adverse events such as acute coronary syndrome (ACS) [3, 4].

Take coronary heart disease as an example, according to the Global Burden of Disease 2022 study, an estimated 315 million people worldwide were living with coronary artery disease (CAD). Although the global age-standardized prevalence decreased by 18% from 1990 to 2022, significant regional disparities remain: prevalence reached 8,019 per 100,000 in Central Europe, Eastern Europe, and Central Asia, but dropped to 2,393 per 100,000 in South Asia. These variations highlight the importance of tailoring prevention and intervention strategies to regional epidemiological profiles [5].

Due to advancements in treatments, the incidence and fatality rates of complications arising from atherosclerosis have significantly decreased in numerous countries and regions, particularly in highly industrialized areas [6]. Nevertheless, current treatment modalities, notably surgical interventions, appear to have encountered limitations impeding further progress.

The present study proposes novel prospective therapeutic approaches by retrospectively discussing the etiology, pathogenesis, diagnosis, prevention, and treatment of atherosclerosis.

Cause

The exact etiology of atherosclerosis remains elusive. In addition to age, gender, and unhealthy behaviors, numerous risk factors have been established to induce or accelerate the progression of atherosclerosis, potentially by engaging inflammatory pathways [7, 8]. Among these factors, abnormal lipid metabolism emerges as the most pivotal one. Various clinical and basic studies support a causal relationship between elevated levels of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and the development of atherosclerosis [9, 10]. Consequently, lifelong reduction in LDL-C is anticipated to mitigate coronary events even in populations with concomitant cardiovascular risk factors based on certain research findings [11]. Among them, triglyceride (TG) is a contentious index that has been the subject of extensive investigation for several decades. Plasma triglycerides are primarily transported by chylomicrons (CM) and very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL), collectively referred to as triglyceride-rich lipoproteins (TGRL). The principal structural protein of TGRL is apoB, which includes apoB100 from the liver and apoB48 from the intestine [12]. TGRLs undergo hydrolysis into fatty acids and remnant lipoproteins through the action of lipoprotein lipase (LPL). This process is precisely regulated by various proteins, including apolipoprotein C-II (apoC-II) and apolipoprotein A-V (apoA-V) as activators, essential inhibitor apolipoprotein C-III (apoC-III), as well as tissue-specific inhibitors such as angiopoietin-like proteins 3, 4, and 8 (ANGPTL3, 4, 8) [12, 13]. Regarding the hydrolysates, fatty acids are utilized to provide energy for tissues. However, due to successive hydrolysis of triglycerides leading to a significantly higher proportion of free cholesterol (FC) and cholesterol esters (CE) compared to LDLs with similar size characteristics, remnant lipoproteins are suggested to have an increased propensity for retention in the subendothelial space [14–16]. Consequently, it has been demonstrated that remnant lipoprotein cholesterol (RLP-C) exhibits a stronger correlation with atherosclerosis than TG [17]. Furthermore, amino acid residues on apoB100 have been shown to interact with proteoglycans attached to endothelial cells during the initial stages of atherosclerosis [13]. It can also be speculated that excessive levels of TG will inevitably lead to an accumulation of remnant lipoproteins thereby accelerating the progression of atherosclerosis.

Notably, during the hydrolysis of TG, HDL continuously acquires phospholipids, cholesterol, and other apolipoproteins that are removed from the surface of VLDL, which serves as a significant source for HDL. The metabolic process of HDL is known as reverse cholesterol transport, which plays a crucial role in preventing atherosclerosis. Cholesterol efflux from macrophages, also referred to as macrophage cholesterol efflux (MCE) [18], represents both the initial step and rate-limiting step of reverse cholesterol transport. ATP-binding cassette transporter A1 (ABCA1), ATP-binding cassette transporter G1 (ABCG1), and scavenger receptor class B type I (SR-BI) are major cholesterol transporters involved in active cholesterol efflux [19]. In addition to the traditional reverse cholesterol transport model that emphasizes esterification of FC by lecithin–cholesterol acyl transferase (LCAT), leading to HDL maturation before it can be taken up by the liver as a basic unit for cholesterol uptake [18], recent studies have observed spontaneous transfer of FC from macrophages to HDL and from HDL to hepatocytes. Therefore, an intriguing yet plausible hypothesis suggests that it is not merely high levels of HDL in plasma but rather high bioavailability index of FC within HDL that plays a pivotal role in reverse cholesterol transport and ultimately decelerates the progression of atherosclerosis [20] (Fig. 1).

Pathophysiology

Formation of plaque

Atherosclerosis is widely recognized as a chronic inflammatory condition driven by immune dysregulation and oxidative stress. The endothelial extracellular matrix—composed of collagen, elastin, proteoglycans, and glycosaminoglycans—facilitates lipoprotein retention and lipid accumulation in the intima [7]. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) oxidize LDL into oxLDL, whose core protein, apolipoprotein B (apoB), is recognized by regulatory T cells (Tregs), triggering cytokine-mediated MHC class II autoimmune responses and aggravating endothelial injury [2]. Meanwhile, specific B cells generate anti-oxLDL autoantibodies that counteract Treg activation [21].

Endothelial activation leads to upregulation of adhesion molecules such as ICAM-1, VCAM-1, E-selectin, and P-selectin, promoting monocyte recruitment and transendothelial migration [22]. These monocytes differentiate into macrophages, which—along with vascular smooth muscle cells—internalize oxLDL via scavenger receptors, transforming into foam cells. Foam cells secrete proinflammatory cytokines and ROS, perpetuating intimal inflammation and plaque formation. Under chronic stimulation, smooth muscle cells migrate into the intima and proliferate, contributing to fibrotic matrix deposition. Persistent oxidative stress further drives vascular calcification and lesion progression [23] (Fig. 2).

Rupture of plaque

After the programmed death of macrophages and smooth muscle cells, foam cells give rise to the necrotic core within the mature plaque, which harbors a substantial amount of tissue factors secreted by macrophages and smooth muscle cells, including procoagulants and other thrombotic substances [22]. Upon plaque rupture, the interaction between blood and thrombotic substances in the necrotic core triggers the formation of red thrombi, promptly leading to acute adverse events [7].

According to conventional wisdom, plaques characterized by a thick fibrous cap and small necrotic core size are considered stable and tend to cause stenosis or occlusion of the arterial segments rather than rupture. However, recent research suggests that even stable plaques can trigger acute thrombosis through sustained immune-related erosion [7]. This mechanism, known as plaque erosion, is supported by an increasing body of pathological and pathophysiological studies, potentially involving polymorphonuclear leukocytes, neutrophils, and type IV collagenase [24, 25].

In the progression of plaque, positive remodeling occurs due to the expansion of vascular wall caused by an increase in necrotic core size and attenuation of fibrous cap before stenosis of lumen, which delays complications to some extent [3].

These features of atherosclerosis directly influence surgical strategies. Vulnerable plaques rich in lipids and inflammatory cells increase the risk of rupture and distal embolization, guiding graft placement and stent selection during revascularization procedures [26]. Elevated inflammatory markers such as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) are associated with adverse postoperative outcomes and are increasingly used for risk stratification and timing of intervention. Furthermore, complex lesion morphology—such as severe calcification or positive remodeling—affects catheter choice, stent deployment, and the need for plaque-modifying techniques [27]. Integrating these pathological insights into procedural planning enhances the safety and efficacy of both surgical and endovascular treatments.

Hypotheses about the formation of atherosclerosis

Clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) and thrombus formation

The term clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) refers to the expansion of a clone of hematopoietic cells in response to acquired somatic mutations, without evidence of hematopoietic malignancy [28]. CHIP is characterized by increased production of peripheral leukocytes and is considered a crucial intermediary between inflammation stimulation and atherosclerosis progression [24]. The association has been substantiated by genomics and experimental studies [29], thereby providing theoretical underpinning for targeted anti-inflammatory therapy [30]. Specifically, the presence of inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin 1β (IL-1β) and other soluble factors like transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) in CHIP have demonstrated a robust correlation with atherosclerosis [31]. It is noteworthy that lectin-like oxidized low-density lipoprotein receptor-1 (LOX-1), a member of the scavenger receptor family, has been demonstrated to play a pivotal role in driving the progression of CHIP [32].

Platelets play a pivotal role in the context of this inflammation-related theory. In addition to the well-established platelet agonists, platelet-derived extracellular vesicles (PEVs) have emerged as potential indicators due to their strong correlation with proinflammatory signals [33]. Notably, cluster of differentiation 36 (CD36), a member of scavenger receptors, facilitates the activation of JNK and ERK5, both belonging to the MAP kinase family. This activation subsequently triggers platelet activation and aggregation, ultimately leading to thrombus formation. Consistent with CD36’s function, LOX-1 also promotes this process [32].

Multiple functions of scavenger receptors

As previously mentioned, most scavenger receptors (SRs) play pro-atherogenic roles. Among them, CD36 and LOX-1 are known to promote the expression of pro-inflammatory markers such as NF-κB, CD68, IL-1β, and phosphorylated p38 MAPK. These receptors also contribute to endothelial dysfunction and influence the proliferation and migration of smooth muscle cells [32].

In contrast, SR-BI appears to exert protective effects against atherosclerosis. It helps inhibit inflammatory pathways, promotes reverse cholesterol transport (RCT), and suppresses thrombus formation. However, in 2015, SR-BI was also found to mediate LDL transport across endothelial cells, suggesting a more complex role in disease progression [34].

More recently, SR-BI has attracted attention for its role in regulating macrophage autophagy—a cellular process that degrades damaged organelles and maintains homeostasis. The accumulation of cholesterol esters and other lipids can trigger autophagy in macrophages, while impaired autophagy may lead to endoplasmic reticulum stress and inflammation. One study reported that SR-BI may enhance autophagy by interacting with Barker, a component of the VPS34 complex, which is essential for autophagosome formation and function [35].

Organelle dysfunction and unfolded protein response (UPR)

Organelle dysfunction is increasingly recognized as a key contributor to vascular inflammation and aging-related pathophysiology. At the cellular level, two major stress responses—the unfolded protein response (UPR) within the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and mitochondrial oxidative stress—are intimately involved in the initiation and progression of inflammation [36].

UPR is triggered by the accumulation of misfolded proteins within the ER lumen and is mediated by three major transmembrane sensors: inositol-requiring enzyme 1 (IRE1), PKR-like ER kinase (PERK), and activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6). These sensors, normally bound to the chaperone Grp78/BiP, become activated under stress, initiating downstream signaling cascades. These include translational attenuation via phosphorylation of eIF2α and modulation of inflammatory gene expression [37, 38]. In both endothelial cells and macrophages, UPR signaling has been shown to promote reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and upregulate pro-inflammatory pathways [38]. For instance, Nox4, an ER-resident NADPH oxidase, is a major source of ROS during ER stress [39].

When ER stress is prolonged or excessive, UPR shifts toward pro-apoptotic signaling via CHOP and BCL2-family proteins, thereby promoting inflammation and potentially contributing to thrombus formation [38, 40].

In parallel, age-related mitochondrial dysfunction further exacerbates redox imbalance. Aged vascular cells exhibit impaired electron transport chain function, leading to excess generation of superoxide and hydrogen peroxide. At the same time, antioxidant defenses such as superoxide dismutase (SOD2), catalase, and glutathione peroxidase decline with age [41]. This imbalance fosters a state of oxidative stress, which not only damages cellular structures but also amplifies inflammatory signaling.

Moreover, suppression of the Nrf2 pathway, a key redox-sensitive transcriptional regulator, has been documented in aging, resulting in impaired cellular detoxification and resilience. Clinical data have shown that elevated circulating oxidized LDL and protein carbonyls in elderly patients strongly correlate with endothelial dysfunction and adverse cardiovascular events [42].

Together, these findings highlight a convergence between ER stress and redox dysregulation in vascular aging, where persistent ROS generation, impaired protein homeostasis, and pro-inflammatory signaling form a vicious cycle that accelerates atherosclerosis progression.

Cell atlas of human coronary artery

The cellular compositions of the human coronary artery have been investigated in recent years using single-cell sequencing techniques. Vascular smooth muscle cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and immune cells such as macrophages, T cells, NK cells, and oligodendrocytes were identified as the predominant cell populations in the coronary artery. Among these cell types, vascular smooth muscle cells play a crucial role in extracellular matrix synthesis. Comparative analysis with larger arteries like the aorta and pulmonary artery revealed that specific subgroups of cells within the coronary artery exhibited enhanced expression of inflammatory factors. For instance, coronary-specific fibroblasts showed high expression levels of inflammatory genes such as C7 and PTN. Similarly, an endothelial cell subgroup enriched in the coronary artery displayed elevated expression levels of inflammatory genes including ACKR1, CCL14, and SELE. Furthermore, macrophages exhibited high expression levels of TNFα (TNF), IL-1β (IL-1B), CCL3 (MIP-1α), and CXCL8 (IL-8). Collectively, these findings contribute to our understanding of atherosclerosis progression. The further research on interactions among cells demonstrated that atherosclerosis-associated genes are expressed in the disruption of communication, which maybe an indication of a new hypothesis of atherogenesis [43].

Although recent perspectives have expanded our understanding of atherosclerosis beyond lipid accumulation—emphasizing immune activation, metabolic reprogramming, and non-coding RNA regulation—these models risk overcomplicating or fragmenting the disease narrative without enhancing clinical applicability. The shift toward immune metabolic frameworks, while conceptually appealing, often lacks mechanistic specificity and fails to account for the spatial and temporal heterogeneity of plaque progression in humans. Similarly, heavy reliance on emerging molecular markers such as miRNAs may outpace their validation, raising concerns about reproducibility and translational potential. Furthermore, systems biology models, though promising, remain largely theoretical and are rarely integrated into clinical risk stratification or therapeutic algorithms. Thus, while novel paradigms enrich the academic discourse, a critical reassessment is needed to ensure that these insights translate into coherent, patient-centered strategies rather than remain confined to reductionist or overly abstract frameworks.

Diagnosis and assessment

Laboratory indicators such as plasma lipids and inflammatory markers are primarily used in primary prevention, given their limited sensitivity and specificity. In contrast, photon-counting computed tomography (PCCT) offers superior spatial resolution and soft-tissue contrast compared to conventional CT. Compared to magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), PCCT also features reduced image noise and shorter scan times [44], enabling higher diagnostic accuracy, particularly in evaluating coronary artery calcium (CAC)—a strong anatomical predictor of major adverse cardiovascular events and an indicator of overall plaque burden [7].

Although invasive imaging, such as coronary angiography, remains the standard for diagnosing atherosclerosis due to its direct visualization of arterial stenosis, its limited viewing angles may lead to diagnostic inaccuracies [45]. Consequently, functional assessment techniques like fractional flow reserve (FFR) and instantaneous wave-free ratio (iFR) have gained traction. FFR measures maximal blood flow across a stenotic lesion, whereas iFR captures flow during a specific diastolic interval [40]. Both have been validated in multiple large-scale trials to guide treatment decisions. However, some studies question whether such approaches may underestimate the impact of revascularization on atherosclerotic progression [46, 47].

Intravascular imaging (IVI) techniques, such as intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) and optical coherence tomography (OCT), are increasingly recommended prior to revascularization [48, 49]. IVUS allows visualization of the full vessel wall and detection of positive remodeling, while OCT, utilizing near-infrared light, excels in identifying calcium area and angle, critical for assessing stent expansion potential. Nevertheless, the high cost of IVI and functional assessments remains a significant barrier to their widespread clinical use. Recent advancements have further enhanced the clinical value of these imaging modalities. Notably, photon-counting CT has incorporated artificial intelligence for automated coronary artery calcium scoring and plaque characterization, improving early detection of non-calcified and mixed plaques. Non-invasive FFR derived from spectral CT data is also gaining attention as a promising alternative to catheter-based assessments. In the realm of intravascular imaging, OCT has evolved to enable quantitative calcium scoring, aiding in stent optimization. Recent patents, such as those involving AI-enhanced PCCT for plaque detection, non-invasive FFR estimation using spectral CT (EP4089123A1), and real-time calcium quantification with OCT, reflect ongoing innovation aimed at improving diagnostic precision and procedural planning [50].

Treatment

Lipid-lowering drugs

Lipid-lowering therapy assumes paramount importance, as evidence suggests that a modest and sustained reduction in LDL-C levels is significantly associated with a decreased incidence of coronary artery events [11]. Furthermore, pharmacological interventions targeting lipid-lowering have been shown to be instrumental in promoting plaque regression [51], thereby interpreting the observed decline in cardiovascular event rates [7].

Statins

Statins, inhibitors of endogenous cholesterol synthesis through the 3­hydroxy­3­methylglutaryl­CoA reductase (HMG­CoAR), remain the primary choice for secondary prevention of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) due to their proven efficacy and safety in lipid-lowering therapy [2, 52]. Furthermore, subclinical investigations have demonstrated that statins not only contribute to reducing the size of necrotic cores in atherosclerotic plaques but also mitigate inflammatory cell adhesion and monocyte recruitment by enhancing transcriptional regulator activity [3, 22].

However, the clinical plateau observed with high-intensity statin therapy suggests that lipid-lowering alone may be insufficient to arrest atherosclerotic progression, particularly in patients with high residual inflammatory risk.

Non-statins

Ezetimibe, a selective inhibitor of Niemann-Pick C1-Like 1 (NPC1L1), effectively suppresses intestinal cholesterol absorption. Its combination with statins has been proven to further reduce LDL-C levels and improve cardiovascular outcomes, with a low incidence of adverse effects [53].

Targeting proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9), a key regulator of LDL receptor degradation, represents another breakthrough in lipid-lowering therapy. Monoclonal antibodies such as alirocumab, evolocumab, and bococizumab have demonstrated significant LDL-C reduction [7]. More recently, RNA interference (RNAi) approaches, such as inclisiran, offer prolonged therapeutic effects with infrequent dosing; however, long-term safety and cardiovascular outcome data are still under active investigation [6, 7].

Various agents, including fenofibrate, pemafibrate, omega-3 fatty acids, and niacin, have been explored for their ability to reduce triglyceride levels or raise HDL-C. Unfortunately, their clinical efficacy has been limited, and some carry notable adverse effects [22, 54, 55]. These shortcomings have fueled interest in novel regulatory pathways, especially microRNAs (miRNAs).

MiRNAs are evolutionarily conserved noncoding RNAs that play a critical role in lipid metabolism and atherosclerosis. Increasing evidence has identified key regulatory miRNAs such as miR-33, miR-122, miR-758, miR-155, and miR-126, which modulate cholesterol homeostasis, reverse cholesterol transport, and vascular inflammation [56–59]. Recent preclinical studies have demonstrated that miRNA-based therapies, particularly those targeting miR-33 and miR-122, can effectively reduce lipid accumulation while mitigating inflammatory responses in atherosclerotic lesions [60].

In line with these advances, several novel therapeutics have entered development. Inclisiran is currently undergoing large-scale cardiovascular outcome trials (e.g., ORION-4), while lipid nanoparticle (LNP)–based delivery systems have significantly improved the tissue targeting and stability of miRNA therapeutics. Correspondingly, recent patents—including miR-33 antisense therapy, long-acting RNAi constructs for PCSK9, and LNP-mediated miRNA delivery for lipid disorders—highlight the growing momentum toward precision lipid-lowering interventions leveraging gene silencing and RNA-based strategies [42, 61, 62].

While non-statin therapies continue to provide incremental benefit, their clinical utility remains constrained by limited impact on the immunoinflammatory landscape of atherosclerosis. Future directions lie in integrating molecular-targeted therapies with inflammatory modulation to achieve comprehensive cardiovascular protection.

Comprehensive management

Individualized comprehensive management is recommended throughout the entire prevention process, encompassing dietary modifications, physical exercise, weight control, smoking cessation, as well as achieving lower levels of blood pressure, glucose, and lipids [7, 63, 64]. The utilization of pharmacotherapy for reducing blood pressure and glucose has further stimulated research on atherosclerosis comorbidities. However, patient adherence remains unsatisfactory [63].

Hypertension has been observed by epidemiology to increase the risk of cardiovascular events, particularly on macrovascular level. Therefore, it is crucial in clinical practice to employ appropriate antihypertensive agents and establish a target blood pressure. It has been demonstrated that angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEI) and angiotensin receptor blockers (ARB) are associated with a reduction in atherosclerotic ischemic events. Additionally, β-blockers are recommended for alleviating symptoms of angina due to their ability to decrease myocardial oxygen demand. However, it should be noted that excessive lowering of blood pressure carries the potential risk of reducing coronary perfusion pressures, which may lead to myocardial infarction [63].

Similarly, while microvascular circulation can be enhanced [65], intensive glycemic control is associated with an increased risk of hypoglycemia and the corresponding treatment burden [63]. Sodium glucose transporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors and glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have demonstrated efficacy in reducing cardiovascular event mortality and heart failure incidence [66, 67]. However, the impact of most glucose-lowering drugs on coronary artery disease outcomes remains uncertain or potentially contradictory [63].

As a recommended drug for secondary prevention of ASCVD, aspirin exerts its effects by irreversibly inhibiting platelet function, thereby reducing the risk of atherothrombosis; however, this benefit is accompanied by an increased risk of bleeding. While accumulated data support avoiding prophylactic use of aspirin in cases with known bleeding risks, it remains challenging to effectively evaluate the advantages and disadvantages associated with its clinical usage [68].

Notably, an increasing number of studies are focusing on targeted anti-inflammatory therapies. CCR2 inhibitors, which target the continuous recruitment of CCR2 + circulating monocytes, hold promise as effective anti-inflammatory agents in atherosclerotic disease [51]. Additionally, experimental drugs targeting IκB kinase β (IKKβ), Dickkopf-3 (DKK3), and B-cell lymphoma-extra large (Bcl-xL) all show potential for targeted ablation [69–71].

Common surgical treatments in clinic

Thrombolytic therapy

Emergency thrombolytic therapy is a pivotal surgical intervention for cerebrovascular diseases, while its application in coronary disease treatment has been limited due to suboptimal reperfusion rates and even ineffective reperfusion. According to the latest guideline, thrombolytic therapy is recommended as an alternative when immediate primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is not feasible (>120 min) [45].

Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI)

Since the first successful percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) in 1977 [72], the field has undergone continuous advancement. The introduction of drug-eluting stents (DES), combined with dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) consisting of aspirin and ADP-receptor inhibitors, has significantly reduced the risks of abrupt vessel closure and restenosis. Consequently, PCI has become a central treatment modality for coronary artery disease (CAD), particularly in acute myocardial infarction (MI), and has demonstrated superior clinical outcomes compared to thrombolysis and coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) [45].

Among the latest-generation DES, everolimus-eluting stents have shown excellent biocompatibility and long-term efficacy [45]. Ongoing innovation focuses on reducing strut thickness and optimizing polymer coatings—particularly biodegradable and polymer-free systems—to improve endothelial healing and minimize late thrombosis [73]. Notably, next-generation DES platforms incorporating nanoporous metallic surfaces and dual-drug elution (e.g., sirolimus and cilostazol) have entered early clinical studies, aiming to enhance anti-restenotic and anti-inflammatory effects. A recent patent (US11799864B2, 2023) describes a nanoporous, polymer-free DES that improves drug loading capacity while reducing thrombogenicity.

The safety and effectiveness of PCI have been further augmented by supportive technologies. Radial access has become the preferred approach due to its reduced bleeding risk and faster patient recovery [74], while intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) counterpulsation remains valuable in patients with hemodynamic instability. Intravascular imaging techniques—particularly IVUS and OCT—are now widely utilized for precise lesion characterization, stent sizing, and optimization of stent expansion [45].

Treating calcified coronary lesions remains a persistent challenge in complex PCI. Multiple adjunctive techniques have been developed, including cutting or scoring balloons, rotational or orbital atherectomy, excimer laser coronary atherectomy (ELCA), and intravascular lithotripsy (IVL) [75]. Although these are not routinely used, they provide effective solutions in selected cases. IVL, in particular, has shown promise due to its ability to modify both superficial and deep calcium with minimal vessel trauma, as demonstrated in the DISRUPT-CAD III trial and supported by growing registry data [45].

Recent innovations also include device integration strategies that combine imaging and therapy. For instance, a new patent discloses an integrated catheter system that combines intravascular imaging with ELCA, enabling real-time plaque assessment and targeted ablation. Similarly, another outlines a balloon catheter with embedded lithotripsy emitters, enhancing precision in calcium modification [76, 77].

Despite being less invasive, PCI has several fundamental limitations that affect long-term outcomes. One key drawback is its limited ability to achieve complete revascularization, especially in patients with diffuse, calcified, or multivessel disease. This often leads to higher rates of target vessel failure and repeat revascularization—studies report a 3-year reintervention rate of 5.4% with PCI versus 2.2% with CABG (HR 0.37) [78].

Furthermore, PCI addresses only focal stenoses and may underestimate the risk posed by non-obstructive or non-culprit plaques, which are responsible for over 60% of future cardiovascular events [78]. PCI is also less effective in restoring distal perfusion in patients with microvascular dysfunction or chronic total occlusion, where functional recovery remains suboptimal despite successful stenting.

In addition, although modern drug-eluting stents reduce restenosis, they are still associated with risks such as late stent thrombosis, neoatherosclerosis, and impaired vascular healing, particularly in high-risk patients like those with diabetes or chronic kidney disease.

In contrast, CABG offers more comprehensive revascularization by bypassing both obstructive and non-obstructive lesions, resulting in fewer long-term ischemic events and a lower need for repeat interventions.

Coronary artery bypass graft (CABG)

Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) remains the standard treatment for multivessel coronary artery disease (MVD), ensuring effective myocardial perfusion via grafted conduits [45].

Arterial grafts, especially the left internal thoracic artery (LITA), offer superior patency and long-term outcomes compared to saphenous vein grafts (SVGs) [79–81]. The use of bilateral internal thoracic arteries (BITA) provides additional survival benefit [82], though it increases the risk of deep sternal wound infection (DSWI), particularly in high-risk patients [83].

Alternative conduits such as the radial artery (RA) and right gastroepiploic artery (RGEA) are viable but carry risks like vasospasm and sensory impairment. Therefore, their use requires thorough preoperative assessment and careful perioperative management. Strategies such as smoking cessation, glycemic control [82, 84, 85],,and albumin supplementation help reduce DSWI risk [86].

Despite its benefits, the routine use of multiple arterial grafts is limited by surgical complexity, lack of high-level evidence, and economic factors [87]. This has spurred interest in off-pump CABG (OPCAB) and robotic-assisted CABG (RACAB), which minimize trauma and broaden surgical options. Recent developments, such as a robotic anastomotic platform, have improved precision in minimally invasive procedures [88].

Tissue engineering has introduced human acellular vessels (HAVs) as a promising graft option. In preclinical models, HAVs undergo progressive recellularization by αSMA- and CD34-positive host cells, forming functional, multilayered vessels [89]. Further innovations, like angiogenic preconditioning, enhance early endothelialization and patency [90].

New biomaterials also support next-generation CABG. A collagen-elastin graft with improved mechanical and thromboresistant properties has been patented, while hybrid grafts combining degradable scaffolds with bioactive surfaces offer better integration and durability.

Together, these advancements mark a shift toward biologically adaptive, minimally invasive, and patient-specific revascularization strategies in modern CABG.

Limitations and comparisons

As previously mentioned, PCI and CABG, while dominant in coronary revascularization, exhibit distinct limitations. PCI often fails to achieve complete revascularization in diffuse or complex lesions, leading to higher rates of repeat interventions [91]. In contrast, CABG offers more durable perfusion but carries perioperative risks like stroke, infection, and longer recovery [92, 93].

Large contemporary Medicare data (n ≈ 104,000) show that CABG patients had significantly lower 30-day (1.98% vs. 3.63%) and one-year mortality (6.76% vs. 12.2%) compared to PCI, along with reduced 3-year mortality (9.97% vs. 17.1%) and fewer repeat revascularizations (2.16% vs. 5.37%). After risk adjustment, CABG was associated with a 49% reduction in 3-year mortality (HR 0.51) and 63% reduction in reintervention (HR 0.37) [78].

Meta-analyses of 8,269 patients with multivessel or left-main disease show PCI is linked to higher 5-year all-cause mortality (HR 1.28), MI (HR 1.84), and repeat revascularization (HR 1.96), though stroke and cardiovascular death did not differ significantly. Ten-year follow-up meta-analysis confirms higher cardiac mortality with PCI (OR 0.76) in multivessel disease [90].

The SYNTAX score and its updated versions (SYNTAX II, 2020) now incorporate functional and patient factors to better individualize treatment strategy. Additionally, emerging AI-driven decision tools trained on multicenter PCI vs. CABG outcomes show promise in guiding personalized revascularization [94–96].

The SYNTAX score

The SYNTAX score is derived from a trial known as The Synergy between PCI with Taxus and Cardiac Surgery (SYNTAX) to assess the anatomical complexity and functional impact of lesions, and it has gained widespread usage in CAD research.

The SYNTAX score is determined based on the anatomical dominance type, taking into account lesion adverse factors and further weighted by the degree of contribution to left ventricular blood supply. Finally, a computer program language is utilized to calculate the final score [97]. The definition of high-complexity disease entails a SYNTAX score exceeding 33 [45] (Fig. 4; Tables 2, 3 and 4).

Furthermore, it is worth mentioning that several current authoritative large-scale trials also exhibit certain limitations. For instance, the Fractional Flow Reserve versus Angiography for Multivessel Evaluation (FAME) 3 trial did not evaluate the direct impact of FFR on the CABG cohort [46].

The SYNTAX scoring system has been criticized for its disregard of macro individual factors. In response, the SYNTAX score II incorporates seven additional variables into its calculation, namely: age, creatinine clearance, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), presence of unprotected left main coronary artery (ULMCA) disease, peripheral vascular disease, female sex, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Notably, the SYNTAX score II demonstrates improved prognostic accuracy compared to its predecessor [98]. The emergence of the SYNTAX score II reflects a growing emphasis on comprehensive decision-making.

Multidisciplinary heart team

Despite advances in revascularization techniques, current surgical decision-making in atherosclerosis remains largely anatomy-driven, focusing on luminal stenosis rather than plaque biology. This approach often overlooks vulnerable but non-obstructive plaques that pose a high risk of rupture. Emerging evidence supports a shift toward biology-informed interventions, incorporating imaging modalities such as PET/CT and near-infrared spectroscopy to detect inflammation-prone lesions. Additionally, while CABG may provide superior long-term outcomes in complex disease, its protective effect may result from bypassing high-risk segments rather than restoring perfusion alone. Future strategies should integrate molecular profiling, inflammatory biomarkers, and artificial intelligence to personalize surgical planning and better address the systemic and dynamic nature of atherosclerosis.

Considering the complexity of clinical complications, an increasingly advocated approach for making revascularization decisions involves a multidisciplinary Heart Team comprising cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, paramedics, and other specialists [45].

Team discussion plays a crucial role in urgent revascularization, as well as in managing various chronic diseases such as diabetes, hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), peripheral vascular disease, and chronic kidney disease (CKD). Additionally, specific physiological conditions like pregnancy or advanced age should also be taken into consideration. The process of team discussion significantly contributes to making informed decisions regarding revascularization procedures, drug selection, and prevention of complications [45]. Furthermore, the provision of appropriate care and rational drug choices can substantially mitigate periprocedural risks [82, 99].

Lastly, it is imperative to consider the patient’s preferences and healthcare costs throughout the course of therapy [82].

New prospective treatments

Surgical revascularization procedures, such as renal artery stenting and iliac artery stenting, are indispensable for the management of peripheral atherosclerotic disease. Timely assessment and intervention play a pivotal role in preserving organ function, alleviating symptoms, and mitigating the risk of adverse events including organ failure and amputation [100].

Although radiofrequency (RF) ablation has been extensively utilized in the interventional treatment of atrial fibrillation, achieving breakthrough progress in the management of atherosclerosis remains challenging and limited to animal experimentation. Over the past three decades, ranging from mice to pigs, animal studies have consistently highlighted that short- and long-term complications arising from thermal injury pose significant safety risks associated with RF ablation. These risks primarily encompass damage to endothelial, medial myocytes, adventitial cells, and even arterial rupture [101–104]. Recent experiments have focused on optimizing healing agents and electrode distribution. In this study, an instantaneous cooling agent effectively counteracts heat generated during RF ablation while flexible electrodes composed of micro-electrodes enhance the precision of ablation shape [105]. Further investigations are warranted to validate the safety profile of RF ablation.

The technique of pulsed field ablation (PFA), which utilizes pulsed electric fields (PEF), disrupts lesions by inducing microscopic pores in cell membranes, known as irreversible electroporation (IRE) [106]. Due to its preferential myocardial ablation, PFA has been shown to be associated with a reduced incidence of complications such as pulmonary vein (PV) stenosis, phrenic nerve injury, esophageal injury, and coronary artery damage, making it more applicable for the treatment of coronary atherosclerotic disease [106–108].

Conclusions

The understanding of atherosclerosis has undergone an extensive process, along with the exploration of treatment strategies. The integration of comprehensive health management and pharmacotherapy can effectively mitigate the occurrence and progression of atherosclerosis. The team discussion holds significant guiding implications for revascularization selection. However, current revascularization techniques possess diverse limitations. The application of PFA in cardiovascular disease introduces novel perspectives and is anticipated to achieve groundbreaking advancements in atherosclerosis treatment, necessitating further investigation into relevant mechanisms through animal experimentation.
