Clinical features and reproductive outcomes after first frozen embryo transfer in patients with different ultrasound-diagnosed adenomyosis subtypes following ultralong gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist pretreatment: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jingwen Jiang, Lijuan Li, Xiaoni Hou, Yingchun Zhu, Haixiang Sun, Chenyang Huang, Jie Mei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe differences between adenomyosis subtypes in clinical features and reproductive outcomes remain largely unexplored. This study aimed to compare clinical features and reproductive outcomes after the first frozen embryo transfer (FET) among patients with different ultrasound-diagnosed adenomyosis subtypes following ultralong gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist (GnRHa) pretreatment.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted and included infertility patients with (n = 179) and without (n = 179) adenomyosis undergoing first FET. Patients with adenomyosis underwent the ultralong GnRHa pretreatment before their first FET cycle. Adenomyosis was diagnosed by two-dimensional (2D) and three-dimensional (3D) transvaginal sonography (TVS) and classified into subtypes according to types (diffuse/focal/mixed) and locations (inner/outer myometrium) of lesions. Baseline characteristics, sonographic features and pregnancy outcomes after the first FET cycle were collected. The primary outcome was live birth rate after the first FET cycle. Secondary outcomes included implantation rate, clinical pregnancy rate, and miscarriage rate.ResultsFor different subtypes of adenomyosis, patients with internal adenomyosis had a significantly higher incidence of secondary infertility than those with external adenomyosis (70.0% vs. 47.0%, P = 0.006). Patients with external adenomyosis had higher incidence of coexisting ovarian endometriosis than internal adenomyosis (27.3% vs. 13.6%, P = 0.029). Patients with diffuse and internal adenomyosis exhibited significantly larger uterine sizes and thicker anterior walls compared to those with focal (180.57 ± 33.90 mm vs. 164.32 ± 25.12 mm, P = 0.026; 21.86 ± 8.35 mm vs. 18.54 ± 7.25 mm, P = 0.042) and external adenomyosis (183.73 ± 33.56 mm vs. 165.95 ± 28.44 mm, P < 0.001; 22.92 ± 8.47 mm vs. 18.13 ± 6.81 mm, P < 0.001), respectively. No statistically significant differences were found between infertility patients with adenomyosis and without adenomyosis in baseline characteristics. Patients with adenomyosis had a lower implantation rate than those without adenomyosis (50.7% vs. 62.8%, P = 0.010), whereas live birth rate after the first FET cycle did not differ significantly between groups (49.2% vs. 47.5%, P = 0.833). In adenomyosis patients, multivariable logistic regression showed that greater reduction in junctional zone thickness after treatment was associated with a lower risk of failure to achieve live birth after the first FET cycle (OR = 0.694; 95% CI: 0.502–0.961; P = 0.028). Endometrial thickness was independently associated with failure to achieve implantation after the first FET cycle (OR = 1.257; 95% CI: 1.039–1.521; P = 0.018).ConclusionsUltrasound-based adenomyosis subtyping revealed distinct clinical profiles. Although live birth rate after the first FET cycle did not differ significantly between groups, adenomyosis was associated with reduced implantation rate. Exploratory regression analyses suggested that greater JZ thickness reduction indicated a favorable effect on live birth, and increased endometrial thickness may be associated with implantation failure. These findings should be interpreted cautiously because of the retrospective design and limited power of subgroup analyses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-026-09624-x.

Background

Adenomyosis is a benign gynecological disease that has recently attracted increasing attention because of its potential impact on infertility [1]. Several meta-analyses have shown that adenomyosis has potential detrimental effects on pregnancy outcomes in assisted reproductive technology (ART), particularly resulting in high miscarriage rate [2, 3]. Adenomyosis is characterized by the presence of endometrial glands and stroma in myometrium and its exact pathogenesis remains unknown [4]. Transvaginal sonography is widely used in adenomyosis diagnosis because it is more accessible and cost-effective than magnetic resonance imaging [5]. Although histological examination remains the gold standard for diagnosis, its clinical application is limited by its invasiveness and the need for extensive tissue sampling to ensure diagnostic accuracy [6]. In 2015, the international Morphological Uterus Sonographic Assessment (MUSA) group published a consensus on adenomyosis ultrasonography terminology [7]. Later, a sonographic classification and reporting system for adenomyosis diagnosis was published, which classified adenomyosis by location, differentiation and extent of adenomyotic lesions [8].

The clinical significance of adenomyosis subtyping is becoming increasingly recognized due to potential differences among these subtypes regarding clinical presentation, pregnancy outcomes, and pathogenesis. A previous study has shown that focal adenomyosis of the outer myometrium (FAOM) was an independent factor of primary infertility, while diffuse adenomyosis was not associated with infertility [9]. It was also reported that ultralong gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist (GnRHa) protocol was associated with improved clinical pregnancy and live birth rates in patients with diffuse adenomyosis, while this kind of effect was not found in focal adenomyosis [10]. Another description of adenomyotic lesions, considering the location of infiltrated uterine layer, classifies adenomyosis into the inner type and the external type. The major difference between these two types is the impairment of junctional zone (JZ). The JZ, which appears as a hypoechoic subendometrial halo on sonography, is more easily identified on MRI [11]. JZ may function as the origin of myometrial contractions in the non-pregnant uterus and may response to ovarian sex steroids [12]. Inner adenomyosis invades the JZ, which is also known as inner myometrium [13], and external adenomyosis involves the middle and/or outer myometrium. Some speculated that the lesion of internal adenomyosis might be activated by the damage of JZ and therefore allow stem cells to grow beyond the endometrial–myometrial interface [14], while the external adenomyosis, like the FAOM, might develop through a “outside-in” invading process [15]. And this theory is supported by the high association between anterior FAOM and endometriosis bladder nodules [15, 16].

These scattered studies revealed the possible different features between adenomyosis subtypes. However, no consensus has been reached and more evidence is needed to verify whether adenomyosis subtypes have different effects on pregnancy outcomes. Ultralong GnRHa protocol followed by frozen embryo transfer (FET) cycle is widely used in adenomyosis patients and was suggested to be beneficial to patients with adenomyosis [3, 10, 17–20]. Therefore, we enrolled patients with adenomyosis undergoing the ultralong protocol followed by FET cycles, aiming to compare clinical features and pregnancy outcomes between different subtypes of adenomyosis.

Methods

Study design and population

With approval from the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital (number AF/SC-08/03.0), we performed a retrospective cohort study. During the study period from October 2022 to October 2023, a total of 179 consecutive infertility patients with adenomyosis who underwent the ultralong GnRHa protocol and FET cycle at our reproductive medicine center were included as the study group. To minimize confounding bias, a control group of 179 infertility patients without adenomyosis undergoing FET during the same period was selected using a 1:1 matching ratio. The matching was performed based on female age and body mass index (BMI).

Prior to IVF treatment, all patients underwent a standardized clinical work-up at our center. This routine evaluation mainly included basal serum hormone profiling, male partner semen analysis, and an expert transvaginal two-dimensional (2D)/ three-dimensional (3D) sonography performed by specialized sonographers to systematically assess the uterine and ovarian conditions. Patients in the control group underwent the same standardized transvaginal sonography evaluations by experienced sonographers to rule out adenomyosis (Fig. 1). To establish a stringent baseline, patients were excluded from the control group if they (1) had a diagnosis of ovarian endometriosis, unexplained infertility, or other uterine abnormalities, or (2) underwent preimplantation genetic testing (PGT).

Adenomyosis classification and ultrasound examination

Adenomyosis was diagnosed by 2D and 3D transvaginal sonography and recorded according to the adenomyosis sonographic reporting system [7, 8]. Adenomyosis was classified by the types and locations of adenomyotic lesions.

The types of adenomyotic lesions included focal, diffuse and mixed by evaluating the proportions of the lesion and the surrounding normal myometrium. Focal adenomyosis was defined as > 25% of the circumference of the lesion surrounded by normal myometrium. Diffuse adenomyosis was defined as < 25% of the lesion surrounded by normal myometrium. If both diffuse and focal were found in the uterus, it would be classified as mixed type. The location of adenomyotic lesions depended on the uterine layer infiltration: internal adenomyosis involved the junctional zone (inner myometrium), while external adenomyosis involved middle or outer myometrium (subserosa). We also classified adenomyosis into focal internal, diffuse internal, focal external and diffuse external by combining the types and locations of lesions.

Our ultrasound reporting system also included the extent of lesions, which was recorded based on the proportion of affected uterus: < 25%, 25%-50%, 50%-75%, or > 75%. For lesions infiltrating the junctional zone, we also recorded the thickness and proportion of the affected junctional zone: < 25%, 25%-50%, or > 50%. To minimize the interference of transient myometrial contractions, JZ thickness was preferentially evaluated using 3D sonography. Measurements were taken at the maximal thickness of the JZ during a relaxed uterine state.

Ultralong GnRHa treatment and FET protocol

Patients with adenomyosis received the injection of long-acting GnRHa (Decapeptyl, triptorelin acetate, 3.75 mg) on day 2 or 3 of the menstrual cycle, and repeated once every 28 days. Patients received treatment for at least 3 months, with the duration individualized according to clinical response. Ultrasound examination was performed before the treatment and after every cycle of GnRHa treatment.

To prepare for the embryo transfer, serum sex hormone levels and endometrial thickness were monitored since the second day of the menstrual cycle. Patients took oral oestradiol for 12–14 days (Femoston, 6 mg oestradiol, 2 mg t.i.d.). When the endometrial thickness reached the expected thickness, oral oestradiol and dydrogesterone compound tablets (Femoston, 2 mg oestradiol and 10 mg dydrogesterone, t.i.d.) and intramuscular injection of progesterone (60 mg q.d.) were administered to induce endometrial transformation. The timing of embryo transfer was determined by the frozen embryo stage: cleavage-stage embryos were transferred on the fifth day of transformation and blastocysts were transferred on the sixth day. Luteal phase support was provided with Femoston (2 mg oestradiol and 10 mg dydrogesterone, t.i.d.) and progesterone sustained-release vaginal gel (90 mg, q.d.).

Data collection and outcomes

The baseline characteristics mainly included age, BMI, type of infertility, duration of infertility and basal hormone levels. The extent of dysmenorrhea and history of ovarian or deep infiltrating endometriosis were also collected for adenomyosis patients.

Ultrasound examinations were conducted before patients receiving any treatment and before embryo transfer. Sonographic features included uterus size (defined as the sum of uterus radial lines), thickness of uterus walls, proportion of affected uterus, thickness of the JZ and affected proportion of the JZ. Variation of JZ thickness was defined as JZ thickness before treatment minus JZ thickness after treatment.

The primary outcome was live birth rate after the first FET cycle. Secondary outcomes included implantation rate, clinical pregnancy rate, and miscarriage rate. Live birth was defined as delivery of at least one living newborn after 28 weeks of gestation. Failure to achieve live birth was defined as the absence of live birth after the first FET cycle. Implantation rate was calculated as the number of gestational sacs divided by the number of embryos transferred. Failure to achieve implantation was defined as the absence of any gestational sac after the first FET cycle. Clinical pregnancy was defined as the presence of at least one gestational sac on ultrasound 28–30 days after embryo transfer. Miscarriage was defined as pregnancy loss after confirmation of clinical pregnancy. Because this study focused on cycle-specific outcomes after the first FET cycle immediately following ultralong GnRHa pretreatment, and considering our retrospective clinical system could not reliably capture complete subsequent embryo-transfer trajectories, embryo exhaustion, treatment discontinuation, cumulative live birth rate was not assessed.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed by IBM SPSS Statistics program (version 26). Continuous variables were evaluated for normal distribution using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov/Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data (e.g., age, BMI, and ultrasound parameters) are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were analyzed using the Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA. Skewed data (e.g., basal hormone levels, antral follicle count, duration of infertility, and hormone levels) are presented as median with interquartile range (IQR) and were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test as appropriate. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with failure to achieve live birth after the first FET cycle and failure to achieve implantation after the first FET cycle among patients with adenomyosis. Variables with P < 0.20 in the univariate analysis, together with clinically relevant variables, were considered for inclusion in the multivariable models. Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Baseline JZ thickness and variation of JZ thickness were not entered simultaneously into the same multivariable model because they were correlated and reflected overlapping information. Variation of JZ thickness was selected for the main model because it better reflected the response of the JZ to ultralong GnRHa pretreatment. For all comparisons, P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Comparison between patients with adenomyosis and without adenomyosis

There were no statistically significant differences between 179 adenomyosis patients and 179 non-adenomyosis patients in age, BMI, type of infertility and duration of infertility, as shown in Table 1. Basal hormone levels (basal follicle-stimulating hormone, luteinizing hormone and anti-Mullerian hormone) were also comparable between the groups. There were no differences in antral follicle count (AFC), endometrial thickness and number of transferred embryos either. Additionally, there was no significant difference in the proportion of high-quality embryo between the two groups.

For reproductive outcomes, infertility patients with adenomyosis had significantly lower embryo implantation rate as compared to infertility patients without adenomyosis (50.7% vs. 62.8%, P = 0.010), while no statistically significant differences were found in miscarriage rate, clinical pregnancy rate and live birth rate (Table 1).

Comparison of adenomyosis with different types of lesions (diffuse/focal/mixed)

We first classified patients with adenomyosis by different types of lesions: diffuse, focal, or mixed adenomyosis (Table 2). Among the 179 adenomyosis patients, 74.86% (134/179) were diagnosed with diffuse adenomyosis, 23.46% (42/179) as focal adenomyosis, and 3 of them were classified as mixed adenomyosis. There were no statistical differences between these three subtypes of adenomyosis in terms of age, BMI, type and duration of infertility and basal hormone levels. However, focal adenomyosis patients had thicker endometrium than patients without adenomyosis (10.87 ± 2.15 mm vs. 9.96 ± 1.81 mm, P = 0.023).

Implantation rates were both reduced in patients with diffuse and focal adenomyosis as compared to patients without adenomyosis (49.4% and 48.1% vs. 62.8%, P = 0.024), while no statistically significant difference was found in miscarriage, clinical pregnancy and live birth rates between groups (Table 2).

We further analyzed sonographic features of uterus in different types of adenomyosis (Supplemental Table 1). Compared with focal adenomyosis, patients with diffuse adenomyosis had significantly larger size of uterus (180.57 ± 33.9 mm vs. 164.32 ± 25.12 mm, P = 0.026) and thicker anterior wall (21.86 ± 8.35 mm vs. 18.54 ± 7.25 mm, P = 0.042) even after proper treatment. Moreover, there were significantly more severe cases (> 75% of uterus affected by the lesions) in diffuse adenomyosis than focal adenomyosis (39.8% vs. 3.3%, P < 0.001).

Comparison of adenomyosis with different locations of lesions (internal/external)

We also classified adenomyotic lesions by different locations (Table 3) excluding 3 patients with mixed adenomyosis who could not be simply classified into internal or external type. Overall, 62.5% of 176 adenomyosis patients had internal lesions and patients with internal adenomyosis had a significantly higher incidence of secondary infertility (70.0%) compared to both patients with external adenomyosis (47.0%, P < 0.05) and those without adenomyosis (55.9%, P < 0.05). Patients with external adenomyosis were significantly more likely to have coexisting ovarian endometriosis than internal adenomyosis (27.3% vs. 13.6%, P = 0.029). There were no statistical differences between these two subtypes of adenomyosis in age, BMI and basal hormone levels. However, patients with external adenomyosis had a thicker endometrium than those without adenomyosis (10.55 ± 2.05 mm vs. 9.96 ± 1.81 mm, P = 0.018). Furthermore, ultrasound evaluations revealed that the disease extent was generally more severe in patients with the diffuse or internal subtypes compared to those with the focal or external subtypes (3.32 ± 1.47 vs. 2.83 ± 1.47, P = 0.022).

Discussion

Our study showed that live birth rate after the first FET cycle did not differ significantly between patients with and without adenomyosis, whereas implantation rate was significantly reduced in patients with adenomyosis. In exploratory regression analyses among adenomyosis patients, greater reduction in JZ thickness after ultralong GnRHa pretreatment was associated with a lower risk of failure to achieve live birth, while increased endometrial thickness was associated with a higher risk of implantation failure.

Ultralong GnRHa pretreatment is a recommended strategy for women with adenomyosis. Consistent with previous literature, adenomyosis patients undergoing the ultralong GnRHa protocol had significantly lower miscarriage rate than those undergoing the long GnRHa protocol [10, 20]. This effect was further supported by a recent meta-analysis [18], which showed that clinical pregnancy rate, miscarriage rate and live birth rate were not statistically different between adenomyosis patients who underwent ultralong protocol FET cycles and patients without adenomyosis [18]. The ultralong protocol may have effectively suppressed the adenomyotic lesions and mitigated the hostile, inflammatory uterine environment prior to FET. Ultralong GnRHa pretreatment may have partly attenuated adverse post-implantation effects, which could help explain the comparable miscarriage and live birth rates observed between groups.

Our findings support the potential clinical value of ultrasound-based adenomyosis subtyping. Notably, patients with internal adenomyosis had a significantly higher incidence of secondary infertility than those with external adenomyosis. This finding may be explained by the distinct underlying pathogenesis of these subtypes. Internal adenomyosis is widely hypothesized to originate from the invagination of the endometrial basalis into the myometrium due to tissue injury and repair (TIAR) at the junctional zone [14]. Such mechanical micro-trauma is frequently induced by prior pregnancies, deliveries, or intrauterine surgical procedures (e.g., curettage), which inherently constitute the typical obstetric history of patients with secondary infertility. Therefore, our clinical observation aligns with this “inside-out” pathophysiological theory. Conversely, external adenomyosis, which we found to be highly associated with coexisting ovarian endometriosis, is thought to originate from an “outside-in” invasion of deep infiltrating endometriotic lesions into the outer myometrium [15, 21]. We also found that internal adenomyosis generally presented with more severe sonographic features than external adenomyosis and they tended to receive more GnRHa injections during reproductive treatment. Previous studies proposed that different characteristics might also exist between diffuse and focal adenomyosis. A retrospective cohort study including 428 diffuse adenomyosis and 718 focal adenomyosis patients showed that diffuse adenomyosis had a negative effect on clinical pregnancy and live birth as compared with focal adenomyosis and tubal infertility patients, and both diffuse and focal adenomyosis acted as risk factors for miscarriage [22]. However, our study did not demonstrate statistical difference between diffuse and focal adenomyosis in pregnancy outcomes, which is likely due to our restriction on FET cycles and limitation of the sample size.

The ability to characterize clinical features and outcomes based on adenomyosis subtypes may have implications for personalized ART treatment. Recognizing these distinct phenotypes allows clinicians to tailor pre-treatment strategies. For example, since patients with diffuse or internal adenomyosis tend to exhibit more severe uterine enlargement and deeper junctional zone disruption, they might benefit from a more prolonged or aggressive GnRHa downregulation protocol prior to FET to adequately suppress the lesions. On the other hand, the high coexistence of external adenomyosis with pelvic endometriosis suggests that these patients require rigorous screening for deep infiltrating endometriosis, and potentially surgical evaluation, before proceeding with embryo transfer. Incorporating subtype classification into routine ultrasound reporting may help guide individualized assessment and pretreatment planning before FET.

The association between variation of JZ thickness and failure to achieve live birth may reflect the importance of treatment response in the junctional zone. The JZ is involved in uterine peristalsis and embryo implantation, and abnormal thickening or disruption of the JZ has been linked to impaired reproductive outcomes. In the present study, a greater reduction in JZ thickness after GnRHa pretreatment was associated with a lower risk of failure to achieve live birth after the first FET cycle, suggesting that sonographic improvement of the JZ may be a potential marker of treatment response. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because baseline JZ thickness and its variation were correlated and the analysis was exploratory.

The role of endometrial thickness on pregnancy outcomes in adenomyosis patients has not been widely discussed. Our study showed that endometrial thickness was independently associated with failure to achieve implantation after the first FET cycle. This finding suggests that excessive endometrial thickening in adenomyosis patients may not necessarily indicate better receptivity and may instead reflect altered endometrial function or disease-related hormonal dysregulation. This trend was also observed in another study involving 3104 patients undergoing the long GnRHa protocol and 1527 patients undergoing the GnRH antagonist protocol. The study demonstrated that clinical pregnancy rate and live birth rate were significantly decreased when the endometrial thickness exceeded 12 mm [23]. Endometrial thickness has been widely used as a potential marker of endometrial receptivity. A meta-analysis suggested that both insufficient and excessive endometrial thickness may be associated with impaired endometrial receptivity [24]. Higher serum oestradiol is associated with higher endometrial thickness [25], and one of the typical changes of eutopic endometrium in adenomyosis was the local production of oestradiol activated by CYP-19 (Cytochrome P450 Family 19) and progesterone resistance [26]. Also, the administration of long-acting GnRHa might contribute to increased endometrial thickness [27]. It is important to acknowledge that the severe anatomical distortion and indistinct junctional zone inherent to adenomyosis can make the precise ultrasound measurement of endometrial thickness technically challenging, which might introduce measurement bias despite standardized protocols (mid-sagittal plane, maximal double-layer thickness). Abnormal thickening of the endometrium in these patients may indicate impaired endometrial receptivity or point to an exacerbated underlying disease state.

Determining clinically meaningful cut-off values for the variation in JZ thickness and endometrial thickness may be valuable for risk stratification. However, the current cohort lacked sufficient statistical power to establish reliable thresholds. Large-scale prospective studies are needed to define and validate such thresholds.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study focused on cycle-specific outcomes after the first FET cycle immediately following ultralong GnRHa pretreatment. Therefore, cumulative live birth rate (CLBR) was not assessed. Although live birth rate after the first FET cycle is an important clinical endpoint, CLBR is more patient-centered. However, accurate CLBR calculation requires complete longitudinal follow-up of all subsequent embryo transfers, embryo exhaustion, treatment discontinuation, and live birth outcomes. Because our retrospective clinical system could not reliably capture all these data, calculating CLBR in the present cohort might have introduced misclassification and inaccurate estimates. Future prospective studies with complete follow-up should evaluate CLBR as a key endpoint. Second, the regression analyses were exploratory and may have been limited by the sample size and missing ultrasound data. Third, some ultrasound parameters, particularly baseline JZ thickness and variation of JZ thickness, were correlated, and therefore their independent effects could not be fully separated. Finally, subgroup analyses according to adenomyosis subtype were underpowered, especially for small subgroups such as the mixed type.

Conclusion

In conclusion, live birth rate after the first FET cycle did not differ significantly between patients with and without adenomyosis, although adenomyosis was associated with reduced implantation rate. Ultrasound-based subtyping identified distinct clinical profiles, with internal adenomyosis associated with secondary infertility and more severe uterine enlargement, and external adenomyosis more frequently coexisting with ovarian endometriosis. Exploratory regression analyses suggested that greater reduction in JZ thickness after GnRHa treatment indicated a favorable effect on live birth, whereas increased endometrial thickness was associated with implantation failure. These findings support the potential value of ultrasound-based assessment before FET, but larger prospective studies with complete follow-up and cumulative live birth rate as a key endpoint are needed for validation.
