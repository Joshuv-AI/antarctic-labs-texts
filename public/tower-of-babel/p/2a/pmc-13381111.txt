Obinutuzumab as a Rescue and Maintenance Strategy in Children with Difficult-to-Treat Nephrotic Syndrome After Rituximab-Based Treatment Failure or Intolerance: A Preliminary Single-Center Real-World Retrospective Study
Authors: Haoqiong Sun, Ling Guo, Panpan Shao, Wenpei Liang, Xueqing Ma, Yonghua He, Liru Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis preliminary study aimed to describe the effectiveness and safety of obinutuzumab as a rescue and maintenance anti-CD20 strategy in children with difficult-to-treat nephrotic syndrome after failure or intolerance of rituximab-based multi-target immunosuppression.Patients and MethodsThis exploratory single-center retrospective real-world study included consecutive children who received obinutuzumab between April 2024 and January 2025. Nine patients were included in the safety set, and eight who completed treatment and had follow-up data comprised the efficacy set. The primary outcome was 12-month relapse-free survival. At last follow-up, seven had discontinued both glucocorticoids and all non-obinutuzumab immunosuppressants, while one had discontinued glucocorticoids with a reduced dose of mycophenolate mofetil. Introduction

Primary nephrotic syndrome is among the most common glomerular disorders in childhood. Although most affected children initially respond to glucocorticoids, many subsequently develop frequently relapsing nephrotic syndrome (FRNS) or steroid-dependent nephrotic syndrome (SDNS), and a smaller subset has steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRNS).1 Some patients initially classified as SRNS subsequently achieved complete remission after calcineurin inhibitor-based multi-target immunosuppression; after remission was induced, they could still experience recurrent relapses or treatment dependence during tapering of glucocorticoids or other immunosuppressive agents.2–4 These children may experience a prolonged relapsing course and often require repeated exposure to glucocorticoids and other immunosuppressive agents, thereby increasing the risks of infection, growth impairment, metabolic complications, and cumulative treatment-related toxicity.5

B-cell-targeted therapy has expanded treatment options for children with difficult-to-treat nephrotic syndrome. Rituximab (RTX) is now widely used in FRNS/SDNS, and randomized studies have shown that it can prolong relapse-free survival and reduce glucocorticoid exposure.6,7 However, durable disease control is not achieved in all of those patients. Many children relapse after peripheral B-cell reconstitution, and some relapse despite apparently adequate B-cell depletion.2,8–10 Repeated rituximab exposure may also lead to the development of anti-rituximab antibodies, which have been reported in approximately 29% of patients and may be associated with incomplete B-cell depletion, reduced drug exposure, infusion reactions, and treatment resistance.11,12 As a result, a small but clinically challenging subgroup remains difficult to manage despite rituximab-containing multi-target immunosuppression.

Obinutuzumab is a glycoengineered type II anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody that may overcome some mechanisms of RTX resistance.13,14 Compared with RTX, obinutuzumab may induce deeper and more sustained B-cell depletion and has shown therapeutic potential in immune-mediated kidney diseases, including small pediatric nephrotic syndrome series.15–18 However, the current pediatric evidence base remains limited and derives largely from small observational studies and case reports, with limited data in children who have already failed or could not tolerate rituximab-containing multi-target immunosuppression.14,19 From a drug development and therapeutic perspective, obinutuzumab represents a next-generation anti-CD20 strategy with potential advantages in patients with inadequate response or intolerance to rituximab. We therefore conducted a preliminary single-center retrospective real-world study to provide clinical observations on the effectiveness and safety of obinutuzumab in this highly selected pediatric population.

Patients and Methods

Study Design, Setting, and Ethics

This exploratory, single-center, retrospective real-world study was conducted in the pediatric nephrology service of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, and is reported in accordance with the STROBE statement for observational studies. No formal sample-size calculation was performed. All consecutive children who met the eligibility criteria during the study period were included. Clinical data were retrieved from the electronic medical record system and the dedicated follow-up database.

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Approval No. TJ-IRB202603088). Given the retrospective observational design, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the ethics committee. Patient data were de-identified before analysis, confidentiality was strictly protected, and the study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patients and Analysis Sets

Children were eligible if they were aged younger than 18 years, had SDNS or SRNS, and received obinutuzumab between April 2024 and January 2025 after failure or intolerance of rituximab-containing multi-target immunosuppression. At our center, multi-target immunosuppression referred to glucocorticoids combined with at least two mechanistically distinct immunosuppressive agents, including rituximab.

FRNS was defined according to KDIGO criteria as two or more relapses within 6 months after initial remission or three or more relapses in any subsequent 12-month period. FRNS was described in the Introduction as part of the broader relapsing nephrotic syndrome spectrum, however, eligibility for this study was based on SDNS or SRNS.

Prior rituximab-containing therapy was considered unsuccessful when disease control remained inadequate despite appropriate treatment exposure, including relapse during rituximab-induced B-cell depletion, relapse after incomplete B-cell depletion, two or more relapses within 6 months, persistent steroid resistance, or inability to continue rituximab because of severe treatment-related adverse events. Children with severe active infection or other investigator-assessed contraindications to anti-CD20 therapy were not scheduled to receive obinutuzumab.

All nine patients who initiated obinutuzumab infusion were included in the safety set. The efficacy set comprised eight patients who completed treatment and had post-treatment follow-up data. One child developed severe anaphylactic shock during the first infusion, discontinued treatment, and was included in the safety set only.

Treatment Protocol and Follow-Up

Obinutuzumab was administered during clinical remission as a scheduled rescue/maintenance strategy to reduce the risk of further relapse. The dose was 20 mg/kg per infusion, up to a maximum of 1000 mg. The calculated dose was diluted in 0.9% sodium chloride to a final concentration of approximately 4 mg/mL and infused intravenously using a stepwise rate-escalation protocol. The infusion was started at a low rate and increased every 30 minutes if tolerated; the maximum infusion rate did not exceed 100 mL/h. Repeat dosing was planned at 6-month intervals according to the treating-team protocol.

The 6-month scheduled re-evaluation and repeat-dosing strategy was based on the treating team’s protocol and the clinical aim of maintaining B-cell depletion before relapse or early B-cell reconstitution. Repeat obinutuzumab was considered when remission was maintained, no severe active infection or other contraindication was present, and the treating team judged that continued anti-CD20 maintenance was clinically appropriate.

To reduce infusion-related reactions, all patients received standard premedication with oral ibuprofen suspension, oral promethazine hydrochloride, and intravenous dexamethasone before infusion. Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole prophylaxis was routinely prescribed for 6 weeks after treatment. Intravenous immunoglobulin replacement was administered when serum IgG fell below 4.0g/L.

Patients were assessed in the outpatient clinic approximately every 2 months and were re-admitted every 6 months for scheduled re-evaluation and repeat infusion when indicated. Follow-up assessments included urine protein status, peripheral CD19+ B-cell count, serum immunoglobulins (IgG and IgM), serum creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR; calculated with the Schwartz formula), and adverse events. When sustained clinical remission was maintained, background glucocorticoids and non-obinutuzumab immunosuppressive agents were tapered in an individualized stepwise manner.

Background glucocorticoids and non-obinutuzumab immunosuppressive agents were continued at obinutuzumab initiation when clinically required and were tapered only after sustained remission. No uniform tapering schedule was mandated in this retrospective study; tapering decisions were individualized according to urine protein status, remission duration, kidney function, immunoglobulin levels, adverse events, and physician judgement.

Outcomes and Definitions

The primary outcome was 12-month relapse-free survival, estimated as the proportion of patients remaining relapse-free 12 months after the first obinutuzumab infusion. Time to first relapse was calculated from the first obinutuzumab infusion to the first documented relapse or censoring at the last follow-up visit.

Definitions of SDNS, SRNS, complete remission, and relapse were based on the KDIGO 2025 Clinical Practice Guideline for the Management of Nephrotic Syndrome in Children. In brief, complete remission was defined as urine protein negativity/trace or a urine protein-to-creatinine ratio of 0.2 g/g or less on at least 3 consecutive days, and relapse as recurrence of nephrotic-range proteinuria after complete remission.

Secondary outcomes included annualized relapse rate, glucocorticoid-free status, non-obinutuzumab immunosuppressant-free status, peripheral B-cell depletion, longitudinal changes in eGFR and immunoglobulin levels, and safety. All efficacy outcomes were analyzed in the efficacy set, whereas safety was evaluated in the safety set. Complete B-cell depletion was defined as a peripheral blood CD19+ B-cell count below 5 cells/μL. Hypogammaglobulinemia was defined according to age-adjusted pediatric reference ranges. Adverse events were summarized descriptively according to clinical severity; neutropenia and infections were graded using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) terminology when applicable.

Statistical Analysis

Given the exploratory nature and very small sample size of this preliminary study, analyses were primarily descriptive. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median [interquartile range (IQR)], as appropriate, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Kaplan-Meier methods were used descriptively to estimate relapse-free survival and time to first relapse. Annualized relapse rate was calculated as episodes per person-year before and after obinutuzumab treatment. The absolute reduction in annualized relapse rate was calculated as the pre-obinutuzumab rate minus the post-obinutuzumab rate, with 95% confidence intervals estimated using an exact Poisson method. Where paired comparisons of longitudinal laboratory variables were performed, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used only as an exploratory analysis. Two-sided P values less than 0.05 were considered descriptive and hypothesis-generating, not confirmatory. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0.

During the study period, nine children initiated obinutuzumab treatment. All nine were included in the safety set, and eight who completed treatment and had post-treatment follow-up data comprised the efficacy set. One child developed severe anaphylactic shock during the first infusion, discontinued obinutuzumab, and was therefore included in the safety set only. The study flow is shown in Figure 1.

Treatment-Sparing Outcomes

At obinutuzumab initiation, all eight patients in the efficacy set were receiving background therapy; six were receiving glucocorticoids, five calcineurin inhibitors, and three mycophenolate mofetil (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). Following sustained remission after obinutuzumab, background therapy was progressively tapered (Figure 3).

At last follow-up, all eight patients in the efficacy set had discontinued glucocorticoids, and seven of eight (87.5%) had also discontinued all non-obinutuzumab immunosuppressive agents (Table 2 and Figure 3). The remaining patient had discontinued glucocorticoids and reduced mycophenolate mofetil from 23.5 mg/kg/day to 3.9 mg/kg/day while maintaining remission.

Four infectious episodes were recorded, including two episodes of pneumonia requiring hospitalization for intravenous antibiotics; both were CTCAE grade 2 events, and none progressed to grade 3 or higher infection, required intensive care, or was associated with nephrotic syndrome relapse (Figure 3 and Table 2).

Safety

Among all nine children in the safety set, eight (88.9%) completed obinutuzumab infusion without clinically significant infusion reactions. One child (11.1%) developed severe anaphylactic shock during the first infusion, presenting with dyspnea, hypotension, and altered consciousness (Table 2). The infusion was stopped immediately, and the patient recovered after emergency treatment with epinephrine, dexamethasone, and rapid fluid resuscitation. Obinutuzumab was discontinued thereafter.

No treatment-related deaths were observed. One patient in the efficacy set developed grade 1 neutropenia (Table 2).

Discussion

This preliminary exploratory single-center retrospective study provides hypothesis-generating real-world observations on obinutuzumab as a rescue and maintenance anti-CD20 strategy in children with difficult-to-treat nephrotic syndrome after rituximab failure or intolerance. In the efficacy set, no relapses were observed over a median follow-up of 1.24 years, peripheral B-cell depletion was sustained at 6 months, and most patients were able to discontinue glucocorticoids and background immunosuppressive therapy. These findings are clinically encouraging but should not be interpreted as proof of efficacy because the study included only eight evaluable patients and had no comparator group.

Compared with previously reported pediatric obinutuzumab studies, the present cohort represents a particularly challenging subgroup because all patients had already failed or could not tolerate multiple prior immunosuppressive strategies, including rituximab-containing regimens.12,14,17–21 The heterogeneity of the cohort, including both SDNS and SRNS and different biopsy findings, is clinically important and limits generalizability. In this setting, the clinical problem is not simply recurrent relapse after standard therapy, but persistent treatment dependence or inadequate disease control despite repeated exposure to mechanistically distinct immunosuppressive agents.

RTX has an established role in FRNS/SDNS and can prolong relapse-free survival while reducing glucocorticoid exposure.6,7 However, its long-term effect is often incomplete. Many children relapse after B-cell reconstitution, and some relapse despite apparently adequate depletion, requiring repeated anti-CD20 treatment or reintroduction of immunosuppressive therapy.2,8–10 In addition, anti-rituximab antibodies may emerge after repeated exposure and have been associated with incomplete B-cell depletion, lower drug exposure, infusion reactions, and treatment resistance.11,12,22

From a therapeutic perspective, obinutuzumab has several potential pharmacologic advantages in this setting. As a glycoengineered type II anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody, it can induce stronger direct B-cell death and enhanced effector-cell-mediated cytotoxicity than RTX and may achieve deeper B-cell depletion in blood and tissue.13,15,23,24 Preliminary activity has also been reported in pediatric nephrotic syndrome and in other rituximab-resistant kidney diseases.14,16–21 Consistent with this biologic rationale, all patients in the efficacy set achieved complete peripheral CD19+ B-cell depletion and remained depleted at 6 months. However, because anti-RTX antibodies were not systematically measured in our cohort, any suggestion that obinutuzumab overcame rituximab resistance remains speculative and should be tested in future biomarker-guided studies.

An additional clinically relevant observation was the treatment-sparing effect. Long-term exposure to glucocorticoids, calcineurin inhibitors, and mycophenolate mofetil contributes substantially to cumulative toxicity in growing children. By last follow-up, all patients in the efficacy set had discontinued glucocorticoids, and seven of eight had also discontinued all background non-obinutuzumab immunosuppressive agents. Because tapering was individualized and no standardized tapering protocol was used, these data should be interpreted as preliminary observations suggesting that obinutuzumab may facilitate de-escalation of background immunosuppression in selected patients who maintain remission.

Safety remains a key consideration for any next-generation anti-CD20 strategy. Hypogammaglobulinemia, particularly low IgM, was common during follow-up, and three patients required intravenous immunoglobulin replacement because serum IgG fell below 4 g/L. Decline in IgG or IgM levels can be caused by various factors. Autoimmune diseases themselves may also be an important factor. Factors such as treatment dose, frequency, and frequency of infusions appear to have significant influences.25 All patients had previously received one or multiple rituximab infusions, and prior exposure to rituximab and other immunosuppressants may also reduce immunoglobulin reserves before obinutuzumab treatment. After CD20 monoclonal antibody treatment, as B cells gradually recover, IgG gradually recovered to. However, compared to IgG or other immunoglobulins, it takes longer for IgM levels to return to normal. Even after B cell counts return to normal levels, IgM deficiency may persist, and the recovery process is slow and incomplete.25,26 The relatively stable reduction in IgM levels during follow-up in this study is noteworthy. Most serum IgM antibodies are produced by short-lived plasma blasts and plasma cells, whose precursors express CD20, allowing direct targeted treatment with rituximab and obinutuzumab antibodies.14,24,26,27

Although infections were limited to CTCAE grade 1–2 events during the current follow-up, the long-term implications of hypogammaglobulinemia in heavily pretreated children remain uncertain. Long-term surveillance of IgG, IgM, vaccine protection, infection frequency, and the need for IVIG replacement is therefore essential in future studies.

The severe anaphylactic shock that occurred during the first infusion in one child is a clinically meaningful safety signal. Although the patient recovered promptly after emergency treatment and no treatment-related death occurred, this event highlights the need for careful risk assessment, standardized premedication, slow rate escalation, and close monitoring during infusion. The event also raises the possibility of shared anti-CD20 hypersensitivity in susceptible patients, especially those with prior hypersensitivity to rituximab or other anti-CD20 antibodies.28

This study has several strengths. It reflects routine clinical practice, included all consecutively eligible patients treated during the study period, and explicitly distinguished between safety and efficacy analysis sets. At the same time, the limitations are substantial. The study was retrospective, single-center, and very small; the cohort was clinically and histopathologically heterogeneous; there was no contemporaneous control group; patient selection and individualized tapering may have introduced bias; and the statistical analyses were underpowered and should be interpreted descriptively. Anti-rituximab antibodies and other mechanistic biomarkers were not systematically assessed, and follow-up remains insufficient to define the durability of remission, immune reconstitution patterns, and late safety.

Taken together, these preliminary observations support further investigation of obinutuzumab as a rescue and maintenance therapeutic option in carefully selected children with difficult-to-treat nephrotic syndrome after rituximab failure or intolerance. Larger prospective multicenter studies with standardized biomarker assessment, comparator groups, patient-level longitudinal reporting, and longer follow-up are needed to clarify the optimal dosing interval, duration of therapy, and long-term safety profile of this strategy.

Conclusion

In this preliminary exploratory single-center real-world retrospective study, obinutuzumab was associated with a favorable relapse profile, sustained peripheral B-cell depletion, and reduction of background immunosuppression in a highly selected group of children with difficult-to-treat nephrotic syndrome after rituximab-based treatment failure or intolerance. These findings are hypothesis-generating and support larger prospective studies to evaluate long-term efficacy, safety, and optimal maintenance strategies.
