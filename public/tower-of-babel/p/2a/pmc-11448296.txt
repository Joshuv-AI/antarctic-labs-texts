A comparative effectiveness study of usual care, simple Chuna manual therapy, and complex Chuna manual therapy for the treatment of pelvic biomechanical lesions in patients with non-acute low back pain: a protocol for multicenter, randomized controlled trial
Authors: Sun-Young Park, Yeong-Jae Shin, In-Hyuk Ha, Jung Min Yun, Jun-Su Jang, Sanghun Lee, Mi Hong Yim, Woosu Choi, Hae Sun Suh, Hyuna Yoon, Dahye Ryu, Yeon-Woo Lee, In Heo, Man-Suk Hwang, Eui-Hyoung Hwang, Byung-Cheul Shin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChuna manual therapy (CMT), a Korean manual therapy technique predominantly used for treating low back pain (LBP) and related disorders, lacks well-conceived research focusing on its comparative effectiveness, safety, and economic evaluation, particularly with respect to complex CMT with established CMT diagnostic algorithms. This study aims to illustrate a protocol for a randomized clinical study for comparative effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of complex CMT with simple CMT and usual care.MethodsThis is a protocol for a three-armed, multicenter, assessor-blinded, pragmatic, randomized controlled trial study. A total of 81 patients suffering from non-acute LBP with pelvic biomechanical lesions (PBL), characterized by a pain duration of at least two weeks and a Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) score of 5 or higher, will be recruited from two Korean medicine hospitals. These participants will be randomly assigned to one of three groups: complex CMT plus usual care (UC; n = 27), simple CMT plus UC (n = 27), or UC groups (n = 27). They will undergo treatment for 4 weeks, and follow-up assessments will be performed 8 weeks after treatment completion. The primary outcome will be the NRS score of LBP, and secondary outcomes will include the Oswestry Disability Index, Patient Global Impression of Change, credibility and expectancy questionnaire, three-dimensional posture analysis indicators, quality of life assessment, economic evaluation, and safety assessments.DiscussionThis will be the first study to assess the comparative effectiveness, safety, and cost-effectiveness of complex CMT compared to UC and simple/complex CMT in patients with LBP and PBL. We will also analyze useful diagnostic methods to help in clinical practice for CMT diagnosis.Trial registrationClinical Research Information Service (CRIS), KCT0009210. Background

Chuna manual therapy (CMT), a distinctive Korean form of manual treatment administered by Korean medicine doctors (KMD), involves the use of hands, other body parts, and tools to address musculoskeletal system conditions [1, 2] and integrates other global manual therapy practices with the traditional Korean meridian theory [3]. The three levels of CMT techniques included in the national health insurance system in Korea are simple (gentle manual techniques on soft tissues and joints, such as distraction, joint mobilization, and myofascial techniques), complex (incorporating simple CMT with joint thrust), and special (reduction techniques for joint dislocations) [4].

KMD generally treat musculoskeletal conditions by using forces with high velocity with low amplitude on joints, and various examination methods are performed manually by KMD, especially to identify pelvic biomechanical lesions (PBL), such as the “functional leg length analysis test,” and “sitting posture flexion test [5, 6].” Radiographic imaging examinations are realistically limited in local clinics and require considerable time and effort with the possibility of varying diagnoses depending on the KMD skill level [7]. Thus, the need for more precise diagnostic methods is evident, thereby inspiring our research which uses manual examinations with three-dimensional (3D) posture analysis, and could culminate in the creation of universal diagnostic algorithms for KMD [8].

Since the implementation of health insurance coverage for CMT in South Korea on April 8, 2019, it has been administered in over 4.79 million cases to more than 1,17 million patients according to the Korean Health Insurance Review and Assessment (HIRA) statistics for 2023 [9]. The introduction of insurance coverage has increased the demand for CMT [5]. However, the limitation of complex CMT insurance for specific diagnoses, such as herniated discs and spinal stenosis caused by the discrepancies between KMD daily clinical practice definitions of medical acts and the current insurance system, presents practical challenges for its broader development [10]. There are studies on the comparative effectiveness of manipulation and mobilization [11, 12]; however, the difference in their effects is controversial. In Korea, especially, the question of the effectiveness of simple versus complex CMT techniques remains uncertain. Although it is difficult to survey the prevalence and treatment costs of low back pain (LBP) in patients with PBL, previous studies have investigated the correlation between PBL and age and sex, and have reported that older people, especially women, are more likely to have lesions [13, 14]. These findings indicate a relationship between lifestyle and age. Furthermore, previous studies examining the relationship between pelvic insufficiency and the location of LBP onset [15] and pragmatic randomized controlled trials (RCT) assessing the effectiveness of CMT in patients with non-acute LBP [16] have indicated that manual therapy is the most utilized treatment for pelvic area conditions. However, no RCT have been conducted to verify whether intensive complex CMT is more effective or cost-effective than simple CMT or usual care (UC) following the confirmation of PBL.

Therefore, a rigorous, high-quality RCT to assess the comparative effectiveness, safety, and economic benefits of complex CMT with diagnostic algorithms, fulfilling the societal need for evidence-based treatments, is required. This study aims to illustrate a protocol for a randomized clinical study for comparing the effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of complex CMT with simple CMT and UC.

Study design

A three-armed, multicenter, assessor-blinded, pragmatic RCT will be conducted at two Korean medicine hospitals (Pusan National University Korean medicine hospital [Yangsan, Korea] and Jaseng Hospital of Korean Medicine [Seoul, Korea]). Before the initiation of the research, training will be conducted led by the principal investigator to ensure that clinical study procedures are standardized through standard operating procedures (SOP). After obtaining voluntary written consent from the participants, those meeting the inclusion criteria during the screening visit will be enrolled with assessments, including radiographic and manual examination, by specialists in Korean medicine rehabilitation to determine the presence of PBL. To verify the characteristics and safety of the pelvis before and after the procedure, a radiographic examination of the pelvis in the anteroposterior standing position will be conducted, and the results will be reviewed by a blinded third assessor. The manual examination will involve two evaluators independently conducting standing posture flexion tests to screen for participants with observed changes in the positions of the left and right posterior superior iliac spines (pelvic motion). After the examination, the results will be recorded and compared between evaluators to confirm inter- and intra-examiner concordance. For participants identified with PBL through manual examination, markings will be made on the participant’s body according to the SOP, and 3D posture analysis will be performed in two positions (standing posture and standing with the lumbar region flexed) using ibalance (Teamelysium, Seoul, Korea). Participants identified with pelvic deviation will undergo 3D posture analysis [18], and those falling within the abnormal range of will be included. The enrolled participants will be randomly assigned to one of the following three groups in a 1:1:1 ratio: complex CMT plus UC, simple CMT plus UC, and UC alone, and undergo a 4-week treatment period followed by a follow-up 8 weeks after treatment completion at week 12. Randomization, allocation, and blinding

At each institution, participant recruitment will cease when complete, and participants will be randomly assigned to one of three groups in a 1:1:1 ratio using block randomization via the R version 4.2.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The random assignment codes will be sealed in opaque envelopes and stored in locked cabinets. After completing the baseline assessment and ensuring that the participants meet the inclusion criteria and do not fall under the exclusion criteria, the random allocation envelope will be opened in the order of participant registration to assign the participant identification code number. Participants will receive a unique code based on the order of recruitment, and these details will be centrally communicated to each institution for treatment implementation within the assigned groups. Participants will be assigned random allocation numbers after completing the baseline assessment only if they met the selection criteria and are not excluded. Allocation will be performed by opening the random allocation envelope in order of participant registration to assign the participant identification code number. Since it is impossible to blind practitioners and participants in this study, only the assessors will be blinded. Outcome assessments will be conducted by either a research nurse or a KMD, both of whom will be blinded to the allocation, and will carry out evaluations in a room separate from the treatment area. They will not be involved in the treatment process and will make no effort to learn about the allocation.

Participants

Inclusion criteria

(1) Participants with non-acute LBP (lasting > 2 weeks after onset) accompanied by PBL and with a Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) score of 5 or higher.

(2) Participants diagnosed with PBL through manual examination by a Korean medicine rehabilitation specialist or resident trainee with > 2 years of clinical experience, who fall within the abnormal range for the pelvic area as determined by 3D posture measurement in a preliminary study.

(3) Participants aged > 19 but <–65 years.

(4) Participants who are capable of everyday communication.

(5) Participants who voluntarily sign the clinical trial consent form.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Participants diagnosed with specific severe diseases that could cause LBP, or whose cause of LBP is not PBL (i.e., vertebral metastasis of tumor, acute fracture and dislocation, polio, etc.)

(2) Participants with absolute contraindications for CMT (acute cauda equina syndrome, vertebral dislocation, cerebral aneurysm, etc.)

(3) Participants considered inappropriate for the clinical trial by the staff (e.g., lumbosacropelvic operation history, knee operation history, innate vertebral disease, nerve root block operation history, etc.)

(4) Participants who received CMT within a week, or were taking medicines such as steroids, immune depressants, mental disorder modifications, or other drugs that could affect the study results (e.g., non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs).

(5) Participants who had a scheduled operation or medical procedure, which could affect the clinical trial results during the treatment period.

(6) Participants with chronic diseases that could affect the outcome of the clinical study, such as cardiovascular disease, kidney disease, diabetic neuropathy, dementia, and epilepsy.

(7) Participants who participated in other clinical trials within 1 month of the study.

(8) Participants incapable of ordinary communication due to dementia or mild cognitive disorders.

(9) Pregnant women.

(10) Participants who had difficulty signing the clinical trial consent form or were considered incapable of participation.

Intervention

Participants will receive 8–12 sessions of intervention over a period of 4 weeks, with a minimum attendance requirement of at least eight sessions. These sessions will be scheduled two to three times per week. In this trial, CMT will be provided by a KMD with over three years of clinical experience in CMT. They will undergo training with the SOP to ensure a standardized treatment approach. The descriptions of each treatment are provided below:

Complex CMT involves the application of simple CMT, such as joint mobilization manipulation, joint distraction manipulation, and fascial manipulation, to induce appropriate relaxation of the target joint or muscle (myofascial tissue). Subsequently, the treatment aims to exceed the physiological range of motion of the joint using high-velocity, low-amplitude techniques (momentary correction techniques). This approach is specifically intended to widen the joint space and facilitate the recovery of the joint’s physiological mobility. This therapeutic intervention corresponds to joint correction manipulation [4].

In simple CMT, the practitioner actively moves and neuromuscularly stimulates the target joint within its normal physiological range of motion. This manipulation can also involve inducing relaxation or enhancing strength in associated structures, such as the muscles, ligaments, fascia, and tendons. The techniques employed include joint mobilization, manipulation, and distraction and myofascial (muscle and fascia) release manipulation [8].

In this study, UC consists of physical therapy and education on back pain. LBP educational materials, including videos and informational handouts, will be provided by the research institution. UC physical therapy is based on the ranking of the top 10 most commonly utilized treatments for patients with LBP, as determined by 2011 statistics from HIRA [19]. Practitioners will choose two of the top 10 treatments to administer, dedicating 15 min to each treatment. Because all three groups will undergo UC interventions, the same methodology, frequency, session duration, total treatment period, and number of sessions will be followed.

During the 4-week intervention period, any treatment beyond that outlined in the protocol, such as other pain-related medications, acupuncture, procedures, or surgery, will be prohibited.

Outcome

Primary outcome measurement

NRS: For NRS pain score, participants will be asked to report pain intensity on a single 11-point numeric scale, with 0 indicating “no pain” and 10 representing “worst imaginable pain” [20].

Secondary outcome measurement

Oswestry disability index (ODI)

In this index, each item offers six answer options, scored from 0 to 5, leading to a maximum total score of 50, with a higher score indicating a higher level of disability [21]. An accredited Korean version of the ODI [22] will be used in this study.

The patient global impression of change (PGIC)

The PGIC was originally developed for use in psychological contexts, and was used to evaluate overall changes. The PGIC scale ranges from 1 to 7, with lower scores indicating greater improvement after treatment [23].

Credibility and expectancy

Participants’ expectations regarding treatment will be evaluated using a 9-point Likert scale [24].

3D posture analysis indicators

Analysis includes rotation angles of the pelvic area, differences in volume between the left and right units, and values of the lateral shift.

Quality of life assessment

EuroQol-5 Dimension (EQ-5D)-5 Levels (5 L): The EQ-5D-5 L is a preference-based tool developed to measure health-related quality of life and is widely used in the healthcare sector. It consists of five questions about current health status across five domains: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression, with each domain evaluated on five points [25, 26]. The utility weights will be derived using the EQ-5D index tariff for the Korean population [26, 27].

It consists of five questions about current health status across five domains: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression, with each domain evaluated on five points [25, 26]. The utility weights will be derived using the EQ-5D index tariff for the Korean population [26, 27].

EuroQol Visual Analog Scale (EQ-VAS): The EQ-VAS is a tool designed to help express how good or bad a health state is, with one end representing the worst imaginable health state and the other the best imaginable health state on a vertical line of 100 mm length, where participants mark their perceived health state [28].

The 12-Item Short-Form Health Survey version 2 (SF-12v2) The SF-12v2 is a shortened assessment tool derived from The Short Form-36 Health Survey and is widely used to evaluate health-related quality of life. Cost data investigation

Costs are evaluated from various perspectives, including societal, limited societal, and healthcare systems. These perspectives encompass direct medical costs, direct non-medical costs, and indirect costs, such as the loss of productivity for patients. Direct medical costs include intervention costs, treatment expenses, healthcare service utilization costs, and expenses related to health supplements and medical devices for managing non-acute LBP. Direct nonmedical costs include transportation expenses, patient time, and nursing care. The indirect costs include the economic impact of reduced work capacity. Loss of productivity costs will be assessed using the iMTA Productivity Cost Questionnaire (iPCQ). Cost data will be sourced from organizational records, patient-reported information, and national data.

Economic evaluation

An economic evaluation will be performed using a within-trial analysis. The primary economic endpoint will be the incremental cost-utility ratio (ICUR). ICUR is calculated as the difference in costs and quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) between the trial and control groups. QALYs are based on the EQ-5D scores measured during the visits.

The secondary economic endpoint is the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) for cost-effectiveness analysis. The ICER will be determined using clinical efficacy evaluation variables, representing the incremental cost per unit of effect between the trial and control groups.

Basic analyses will be conducted at 4 and 12 weeks; if long-term follow-up beyond 12 weeks is required, modeling will be based on the Markov model, with the necessary transition probabilities estimated from clinical data collected over 12 weeks. Furthermore, if the analysis extends beyond 12 months, the cost unit is standardized to the currency unit of South Korea (Won) for a specific year, and a discount rate of 4.5% will be applied in accordance with the guidelines for the economic evaluation of HIRA. The economic perspectives applied will include a societal perspective, limited societal perspective, and healthcare system perspective.

Adverse events (AEs)

Details of events expected to be AEs of the procedure (symptoms and signs, start date, duration, etc.) will be fully recorded in the AE investigation form, and any unrecorded event will be classified as subjective symptoms. The presence of AEs will be assessed at each visit. The evaluation of the severity of AEs is based on the principle that the researcher assesses them in stages according to the severity of symptoms with reference to the evaluation criteria. The causality of the procedure will be assessed by the responsible physician in six stages, according to the evaluation criteria. The severity of objective and subjective symptoms is assessed in stages according to existing criteria for abnormalities in test items, such as the World Health Organization adverse event evaluation criteria. If these criteria are not appropriate, the three-level classification method by Spilker is used [31]. A serious AE refers to an AE during the clinical study process that meets any of the following criteria: results in death or is life-threatening; requires hospitalization or prolongs existing hospitalization; causes persistent or significant disability or impairment in function; or involves other medically significant conditions. Investigators must report serious AEs promptly. During the research period, in the event of a serious AE, the Institutional Review Board of the respective institution and the sponsoring clinical research organization (Pusan National University Korean Medicine Hospital) will decide whether to continue or discontinue the study. A participant experiencing an AE will receive ongoing follow-up care until the event is resolved or until their condition reaches a stable, chronic state.

Stopping rules

If a serious AEs or any AE deemed to harm the participant is observed at any point during the clinical study, the researcher may temporarily suspend the participant’s involvement in the clinical study.

Sample size

The sample size for this study was calculated based on a previous study [16] using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7; University of Düsseldorf, Germany). Considering a significance level of 5% and power of 80%, and after applying a group allocation ratio of 1:1:1 to the sample size calculation formula, an anticipated dropout rate of 20% was assumed. Using the difference in the average change in NRS of 1.66 before and after treatment between the CMT and UC groups obtained from a previous study, and a common standard deviation of 1.735, a sample size of 21 participants per group, totaling 63 participants, was selected. Considering an anticipated dropout rate of 20%, the total target number of participants for the study was 81, who were evenly distributed across the three groups, with a minimum of 27 participants per group.

Statistical methods

General principles of analysis

The analysis groups derived from the participants in this clinical study will be classified into full analysis set (FAS) and per protocol set (PPS). All statistical analyses will be conducted using statistical analysis software R version 4.2.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with significance determined at p < 0.05, with a power of 0.8.

FAS analysis group: Closely adhering to the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, encompassing all participants who will be screened and will meet the inclusion and exclusion criteria, will be enrolled in the study, and from whom at least one primary efficacy variable will be obtained.

FAS analysis group: Closely adhering to the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, encompassing all participants who will be screened and will meet the inclusion and exclusion criteria, will be enrolled in the study, and from whom at least one primary efficacy variable will be obtained.

PPS analysis group: The FAS group will consist of participants who complete the clinical trial as per the protocol without any major violations and undergo the minimum treatment criteria of attending sessions twice a week for a total of at least 8 sessions.

PPS analysis group: The FAS group will consist of participants who complete the clinical trial as per the protocol without any major violations and undergo the minimum treatment criteria of attending sessions twice a week for a total of at least 8 sessions.

Missing values for all assessment indicators will be substituted with the last observation carried forward method. Summary statistics for the demographic characteristics will be presented for each group. Continuous data will be described using mean, standard deviation, two-sided 95% confidence intervals, and minimum and maximum values, with group comparisons analyzed via analysis of variance (or Kruskal–Wallis test). Categorical data will be analyzed using frequency and percentage using the chi-square test (or Fisher’s exact test) used for analysis. In the manual examination, inter-assessor and intra-assessor reliabilities will be analyzed using the chi-square test. The algorithm for comparing the 3D posture measurement values utilizes a paired t-test for bilateral comparisons and an independent t-test for individual data comparisons. The null hypothesis for NRS values as primary efficacy variables tests “no difference between the two groups [(complex CMT + UC) group vs UC group, (complex CMT + UC) group vs (simple CMT + UC) group],” while the alternative hypothesis tests that “a difference exists.” Analysis of Covariance will be used, taking baseline NRS values as covariates and each treatment group as fixed factors and adjusting for covariates if any clinical differences among the groups will be observed at baseline. The difference in NRS values before and after treatment within each group will be analyzed using the paired t-test (or Wilcoxon signed-rank test if normality is not satisfied). To analyze the differences in NRS values before and after treatment within each group, the change in NRS values from baseline to 12 weeks after treatment and following 4 weeks of treatment will be calculated for each study participant. The differences in these changes within each group will then be tested using a paired t-test (or Wilcoxon signed-rank test if normality is not met). The analysis methods for differences between and within each group for the secondary efficacy variables are the same as those for the primary evaluation, using the chi-square test (or Fisher’s exact test) for categorical data. All AEs reported during the study period will be recorded, and the incidence rate of AEs will be calculated. The proportion of participants who experience AEs in each group will be computed, and the differences between the groups will be compared using either the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test.

Data collecting and monitoring

Data management will be handled by independent third parties, with critical documents and the source document securely stored in locked spaces or databases with restricted access, accessible only to the principal investigator and designated sub-investigators. All original documents, including informed consent forms, questionnaires, and worksheets, will be gathered according to the SOP. Data collection will be conducted via an electronic data capture system using electronic case report forms. The Korea Institute of Oriental Medicine will oversee the quality control throughout the study. Clinical research monitors may verify the source documents and monitor the conduct of the trial in accordance with the study protocol and relevant guidelines by accessing data management systems or through methods, such as phone calls, email, or direct visits, as necessary.

Discussion

Numerous studies have been published on the empirical effectiveness of CMT for musculoskeletal disorders [32, 33] as well as its effects on other diseases [34, 35]. This study focuses on assessing the comparative effectiveness, safety, and cost-effectiveness of complex CMT in patients with non-acute LBP with PBL in both the field of Korean medicine and enhancement of national health services by examining pain relief, functionality, quality of life, and AEs in real-world clinical settings, and necessitates an evaluation of the accuracy among examiners and within examiners for manual examination of PBL diagnosis.

CMT manual examination can be used instead of or prior to radiographic measurement, and posture analysis in clinical practice [36]. The treatment location, method, and approach are established post-diagnosis through manual examination, which incorporates both stationary aspects of positional irregularities and moving aspects of joint movement limitations [4]. Despite the considerable time spent on CMT diagnosis with manual examination before administration, the validity and reliability of manual examination and diagnostic imaging is still controversial [37–40]. KMD primarily diagnoses PBL based on manual examination methods and has recently attempted to use radiographic imaging to overcome these limitations and improve objectivity [41, 42]. Therefore, numerous studies have been conducted, particularly those that present diagnostic criteria for radiographic diagnoses [43]. Owing to limited access to diagnostic approaches in local clinics [37], there is a need for research on new diagnostic approaches to overcome the limitations of KMD manual examinations and to explore a rational and standardized CMT diagnostic method.

Insurance coverage for CMT after 2019 has significantly improved access to Korean medical treatment for patients with musculoskeletal disorders [44]. The number of complex CMT sessions per patient has increased from an average of 7.9 to 9.0 sessions per patient after insurance coverage [45]. The differentiation between complex and simple CMT in insurance centers is based on the concept of joint misalignment, specifically PBL, rather than on distinct medical conditions, such as herniated discs or spinal stenosis. The lack of full national health insurance coverage for CMT in Korea, despite its high level of satisfaction, especially for patients with LBP [46], limits accessibility due to financial constraints and underscores the challenges in variance between the categorization of treatments by KMD and current health insurance policies, hindering the advancement of CMT practices.

This study is significant in that it investigates the validity and reliability of manual diagnostic examinations used by KMD as well as the upgraded existing 3D posture analysis devices [47, 48], thereby reflecting the clinical demands for complex CMT in current health insurance policies. Furthermore, high-quality evidence is needed for diagnosis to treatment of CMT.
