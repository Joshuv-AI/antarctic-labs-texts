Synonymous codon substitutions modulate transcription and translation of a divergent upstream gene by modulating antisense RNA production
Authors: Anabel Rodriguez, Jacob D. Diehl, Gabriel S. Wright, Christopher D. Bonar, Taylor J. Lundgren, McKenze J. Moss, Jun Li, Tijana Milenkovic, Paul W. Huber, Matthew M. Champion, Scott J. Emrich, Patricia L. Clark
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceA central goal of synthetic biology is to express protein-encoding genes at desired levels, but the full repertoire of DNA sequence elements that determine the transcription and translation level remains poorly understood, particularly for sequence elements that lie far from the canonical transcription start site. Here, we show that synonymous codon changes far within a coding sequence can alter both transcription and translation of a neighboring gene. These results highlight a previously under-appreciated selective pressure on codon usage in bacteria, where protein-coding sequences are often packed close together.

Synonymous mutations in coding sequences are often regarded as “silent” (e.g., genomic background noise), as these substitutions preserve the amino acid sequence of the encoded protein. Yet synonymous substitutions can perturb diverse mechanisms related to gene expression and protein homeostasis (1–6). While much attention has been paid to the effects of synonymous substitutions on mRNA lifetime (7), translation initiation efficiency (8, 9), translation elongation rate (10), and downstream effects on protein folding (11), which can lead to misfolding (12, 13) and degradation (14), synonymous substitutions can also affect transcriptional regulation (15).

Transcription is regulated by a diverse array of mechanisms that can act additively, synergistically or in opposition to one another. For example, while the σ70 DNA-binding sites located roughly −35 and −10 nt upstream from the transcription start site (TSS) are key for RNA polymerase (RNAP) engagement in bacteria (16), alternative σ factors and neighboring DNA elements can affect transcription level through a complex array of activation, repression, and derepression mechanisms (17), which has complicated the development of a predictive model for gene expression level. Recent developments in DNA library generation, screening, and machine learning have begun to reveal how sequence context at canonical TSSs affects gene expression (18). Yet, many additional RNAP binding sites have been found within coding sequences (19). The impact of transcription originating from these noncanonical, intragenic TSSs on overall gene expression is less clear, as intragenic TSSs can have either a positive or negative impact on gene expression, including the expression of neighboring genes. An example of a positive impact is an intragenic TSS that enhances transcription of the coding strand, which can enhance transcription of a downstream, tandem gene in an operon (20). Alternatively, an intragenic TSS that leads to antisense RNA (asRNA) transcription can have a negative impact on transcription of the sense strand via a variety of mechanisms (21), including transcriptional interference (22–24).

While studying the effects of synonymous mutations on cotranslational folding, we found that synonymous mutations deep within the coding sequence of chloramphenicol acetyltransferase (cat) can affect expression of the upstream divergent gene, tetR. We designed nine synonymous versions of cat to broadly sample the vastness of synonymous sequence space and expressed these sequences in Escherichia coli using a well-characterized Tet ON/Tet OFF plasmid-based gene expression system. We found that synonymous substitutions can significantly enhance transcription of an asRNA originating from an intragenic TSS within cat, leading to enhanced transcription and translation of the divergent upstream tetR gene. Surprisingly, transcription originating from the intragenic TSS bypasses the canonical transcriptional repression mechanism. Moreover, we found that the asRNA arising from the intragenic TSS sets the expression level for the upstream gene. Given the vastness of synonymous sequence space, it is noteworthy that four out of our nine synonymous cat sequences significantly enhanced expression of the upstream gene, relative to the wild type (WT). Given the dense packing of genes in prokaryotic chromosomes, these results suggest that synonymous codons may be broadly under selection to prevent high-level aberrant expression of neighboring genes.

Results

Synonymous cat Coding Sequences Alter CAT Protein Accumulation.

We previously identified Shuf1, a synonymous recoding of the E. coli chloramphenicol acetyltransferase (cat) coding sequence that subtly alters the CAT protein native structure, despite preserving the WT CAT amino acid sequence (14). Shuf1 maintains the overall global codon usage frequency features of WT cat but rearranges local patterns of codon usage; this recoding strategy was designed to preserve overall protein synthesis levels but modulate local translation elongation rates. As a next step toward developing a broader understanding of how synonymous substitutions affect CAT protein homeostasis, we constructed eight additional sequences as representatives of the astronomical vastness of synonymous sequence space. As with Shuf1, each sequence retains the first 46 codons from WT-cat, to avoid effects of 5’ codon usage on translation initiation. Five of the eight new sequences were created using the CHARMING codon harmonizing algorithm (25) and designed to correlate closely with [e.g., “harmonize” to] the WT cat profile for a published codon usage measure (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 and Table S5). The five codon usage measures we selected (CAI, tAI, nTE, %MinMax, and High-phi %MinMax) are distinct from one another (25) (SI Appendix, Supplementary Methods and Table S6), but each is commonly used as proxy for translation elongation rate (1). We also used CHARMING (25) to create a sixth harmonized sequence designed to correlate as closely as possible to all five codon usage measures (AllModel; AllM). In addition to the six harmonized sequences, we also created a synonymous sequence using a commercialized algorithm (26) that uses a more traditional codon optimization strategy, rather than harmonization (GeneArt; GA). Finally, in contrast to all other recoded cat sequences tested here, the codon composition of the final sequence, WTscramble (WTscr), is identical to WT CAT; it was designed by randomly swapping synonymous codon positions in the WT coding sequence (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). Of note, each recoded sequence is only ~50% identical to the other sequences at the codon level (SI Appendix, Tables S1 and S2), but all have very similar %GC content. Additional details on the synonymous sequence design strategy are included in SI Appendix, Supplementary Methods and Fig. S1.

To determine whether the synonymous recoded cat sequences alter CAT protein accumulation, we expressed the ten synonymous cat sequences (WT, Shuf1, and the eight new sequences) in E. coli and quantified intracellular accumulation of CAT protein. Expression of many of the synonymous sequences led to CAT levels similar to WT, independent of the codon substitution design strategy used. However, two sequences (GA and CAI; see Materials and Methods for detailed descriptions) led to a severe defect in CAT protein accumulation (Fig. 1 A and B; *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01, Kruskal–Wallis, uncorrected Dunn’s test). Expression of either GA-cat or CAI-cat also led to dramatically slower E. coli growth rates in the presence of chloramphenicol (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). We previously showed that when tagged with the C-terminal ssrA degradation sequence, Shuf1-CAT is more susceptible than WT CAT to degradation by the E. coli protease ClpXP (8). To test whether lower CAT protein levels were due to synonymous codon-induced altered protein folding, leading to degradation, we measured in vivo protein turnover (SI Appendix, Fig. S3) and solubility (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). We did not observe significant differences for GA and CAI relative to WT, suggesting protein misfolding is not the primary source of the severe defect in protein accumulation. Similarly, CAT-specific activity in cell lysates was also indistinguishable (SI Appendix, Fig. S5).

The results above suggested the effects of synonymous cat substitutions might occur at the level of transcription. Consistent with this model, RT-qPCR measurements of relative cat mRNA abundance revealed a significant correlation between cat mRNA levels and CAT protein abundance (Fig. 1C). To determine whether these different mRNA levels were due to variations in the plasmid copy number between cat constructs, we quantified relative plasmid abundance but found no significant difference (Fig. 1D). These data indicate the differences in CAT protein accumulation are primarily due to different levels of cat mRNA for our synonymous sequences, most strikingly for GA and CAI.

Synonymous Substitutions in cat Increase Protein Accumulation from an Upstream Coding Sequence.

In our plasmid, cat expression is under the control of the tet promoter system, regulated by a negative feedback loop through the tet repressor, TetR (Fig. 2A) (27). We quantified TetR protein levels to determine whether differences in cat mRNA levels were due to differential expression of tetR. We found that TetR protein levels were significantly higher for several recoded cat sequences (Fig. 2 B and C), leading to an overall inverse relationship between TetR protein accumulation and cat mRNA abundance (Fig. 2D). This inverse relationship is consistent with the established model for tetR regulation, where higher levels of TetR protein are expected to suppress cat transcription (Fig. 2A) (27). However, it was surprising that TetR was unable to repress its own transcription, even though it was able to repress cat.

To determine the mechanism that causes TetR protein production to be sensitive to cat synonymous recoding, bypassing TetR repression, we first considered a potential influence from coupling between transcription and translation in bacteria (28). To test whether cat translation affects tetR regulation, we mutated the cat ribosome binding site (RBS) to prevent its translation. The elevated accumulation of TetR for GA and CAI persisted (Fig. 3 A and B), indicating that the connection between cat synonymous codon usage and TetR accumulation is independent of cat translation.

We next considered whether the underlying mechanism leading to increased TetR accumulation is dependent on induction but found that TetR protein accumulation is elevated even in the absence of inducer (Fig. 3 C and D). We also considered that cat codon substitutions may have introduced one or more additional TetR binding sites. Conceivably, these sites could sequester TetR, preventing it from repressing tetR transcription. Though it was unclear how efficient repression of cat transcription would be maintained in this scenario, we nevertheless tested this potential mechanism by inserting an additional copy of the recoded cat region (codons 40 to 220) 287 nucleotides downstream of the cat stop codon. Our expectation was that if the GA and CAI cat sequences harbored additional TetR binding sites that lead to TetR sequestration, this insertion would lead to increased TetR protein accumulation, even in the WT cat background. However, we instead observed TetR protein accumulation similar to WT across all mutant insertions (Fig. 3 E and F), indicating the GA and CAI cat sequences do not introduce additional TetR binding sites. Taken together, these results indicate that the underlying mechanism leading to increased TetR protein production is independent of regulatory action at the canonical tet promoter site.

TetR Is Up-Regulated Via Transcription Originating within the cat Coding Sequence.

An alternative mechanism for increased TetR protein accumulation is transcription originating from an intragenic TSS within the anti-sense strand of cat. asRNAs arising from intragenic TSSs are pervasive across prokaryotic genomes (19, 29), including E. coli (30, 31). Although asRNA levels are typically quite low and a functional role for the vast majority of these RNA products remains unclear (32, 33), a few well-characterized asRNAs have been shown to alter gene expression via diverse mechanisms (19, 21). Given the low information content for bacterial promoters (17, 31) and the frequency with which functional promoter can be introduced by random chance (34), synonymous codon substitutions within cat could either introduce a new internal promoter or modulate transcription from an existing intragenic TSS, by enhancing transcriptional activation and/or reducing repression (35, 36). Conceivably, transcription originating from this intragenic TSS could bypass the native TetR negative feedback loop and increase the total amount of tetR RNA, leading to increased TetR protein production while preserving cat repression.

To experimentally determine whether tetR transcription can originate from a TSS within the noncoding strand of cat, we designed RT-qPCR primers to amplify only RNA that includes both a portion of tetR and the intergenic region upstream of the canonical tetR TSSs (Fig. 4A, schematic). Amplification of this region should occur only when tetR transcription is initiated from a TSS within the cat coding sequence. Surprisingly, we detected transcription originating from within all cat coding sequences using this approach, including WT cat (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Moreover, four of the nine recoded cat sequences (CAI, GA, Shuf1, and AllM) led to a significant increase in tetR RNA originating from the intragenic TSS relative to WT cat, with the largest increases for the CAI and GA coding sequences (91-fold and 29-fold more tetR RNA from the intragenic TSS than WT, respectively) (Fig. 4A).

We reasoned that if transcription originating from an intragenic TSS within cat is the origin of up-regulated TetR, it should be blocked by introduction of a transcription terminator sequence. To test this hypothesis, we replaced a 54 bp segment in the invariant 5′ portion of the cat coding sequence with an antisense transcription terminator sequence (37) (Fig. 5A) (SI Appendix, Supplemental Methods). Consistent with the proposed model of increased TetR accumulation due to transcription of an asRNA from an intragenic TSS, the presence of the terminator led to significantly less tetR RNA originating from the intragenic TSS (Fig. 5C), and lower TetR protein levels (Fig. 5 B and D). Thus, blocking the progression of transcription originating from the intragenic TSS results in lower expression of the upstream tetR gene.

To precisely identify the intragenic TSS, we used 5′ rapid amplification of complementary DNA ends (RACE), which identified C271 as the intragenic TSS for both GA and CAI (SI Appendix, Fig. S7), where position 271 represents the 271st base pair of the cat coding sequence sense strand. Although RT-qPCR analysis also detected intragenic transcription for WT cat (SI Appendix, Fig. S6), its abundance was below the limit of detection in our 5′ RACE analysis. Of note, for each cat construct, the sequence motifs upstream from the identified intragenic TSS bear some similarity to the consensus E. coli −10 and −35 promoter elements (SI Appendix, Table S3), though on average less than several well-characterized σ70-dependent promoter sequences (SI Appendix, Table S4). We tested whether a transcription initiation algorithm can predict the effects of cat synonymous codon substitutions on the level of RNA transcribed from the intragenic TSS. We used Promoter Calculator (18) as an example of a prediction algorithm that employs machine learning and biophysical modeling to predict the rate of transcription initiation, for both the canonical tetR TSSs and in the upstream noncoding sequences for our cat constructs. This algorithm accurately predicted the locations of the canonical tetR TSSs (SI Appendix, Fig. S8), and correctly identified the intragenic TSSs of GA and CAI as stronger than the analogous sequences from other cat constructs (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). However, the algorithm predicted other positions in the GA and CAI sequences, as well as in other cat constructs, including WT cat, as stronger promoters than the identified intragenic TSS of GA and CAI (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). These results indicate that more remains to be learned about the sequence features that determine promoter strength, particular for promoters within coding sequences.

To determine whether the identified promoter region is sufficient to regulate tetR expression, we first replaced the −1 to −50 nt region of the identified intragenic promoter sequence from GA and CAI with the corresponding sequence from WT cat (Fig. 6A). These chimeras led to less tetR expression than the parent GA and CAI constructs, consistent with the model that the identified intragenic promoter sequence is important for expression of tetR from the intragenic TSS (Fig. 6B). We also replaced the 50 nt promoter region from WT with the corresponding GA and CAI sequences but did not observe a significant increase in tetR expression: tetR expression for these chimeric constructs was instead lower than for WT cat (Fig. 6B). We suspect this result reflects observations by others that more distant flanking sequences can also contribute significantly, both positively and negatively, to transcriptional regulation (38–45). Clearly, many more chimeric sequences will need to be tested to sort out the full set of sequence elements responsible for the regulation of tetR transcription from the intragenic TSS.

TetR Abundance Correlates with Intragenic RNA, Not Total tetR RNA.

In addition to the synonymous codon-derived differences in asRNA level from the intragenic TSS (Fig. 4A), we also detected a nearly 10-fold variation in total tetR RNA across the synonymous cat constructs (Fig. 4B). There was no correlation between intragenic and total tetR mRNA levels (Fig. 7A). Yet surprisingly, TetR protein abundance correlated more closely with intragenic tetR transcript abundance (Fig. 7B), rather than with total tetR mRNA level (Fig. 7C). These results indicate that the observed increase in TetR protein accumulation is mainly driven by the presence of tetR transcripts originating from the intragenic TSS.

Discussion

A growing body of evidence has established that synonymous codon substitutions can modulate many gene expression mechanisms, including transcription level (15, 31, 46), translational efficiency (8, 9, 47), and cotranslational folding (11, 14, 48). Here, we show that synonymous codon substitutions can also activate an intragenic TSS, significantly enhancing the transcription and translation of a divergent gene located upstream of the recoded coding sequence. Perhaps most surprisingly, accumulation of the upstream protein product was well correlated with the subset of the longer tetR RNA arising from the intragenic TSS, rather than total RNA, implying that RNA from the intragenic TSS leads to more efficient TetR translation than the canonical mRNA. More investigation will be needed to determine precisely how the intragenic TSS RNA affects upstream protein accumulation. For example, the longer 5′ UTR of the asRNA may modulate mRNA structure, improving access to the RBS (Fig. 8). Alternatively, the asRNA may itself not be translated, but instead regulate as a noncoding RNA the translation of the canonical tetR mRNA (not shown). However, regardless of the precise mechanism, intragenic transcription leads to a subset of tetR RNA with higher translational efficiency, which could explain the overall low correlations observed between mRNA and protein abundance in E. coli and other organisms (3, 49, 50).

Model for synonymous substitutions up-regulating transcription and translation of an upstream gene. Synonymous substitutions in the cat coding sequence (orange) can enhance transcription from an internal promoter on the antisense strand. Although this asRNA transcript represents a subset of total tetR mRNA, its level correlates closely with the resulting level of TetR protein. The correlation between asRNA abundance and TetR protein level may occur because (as shown here) the asRNA is translated with higher efficiency than mRNA from the canonical tetR TSS. Alternatively (not shown), the asRNA may act as a noncoding RNA to up-regulate translation of the canonical tetR RNA. Regardless of the precise mechanism, the tetR asRNA provides a mechanism to maintain TetR-mediated transcriptional repression of cat while bypassing repression of tetR.

In our system, synonymous codon substitution-derived changes to asRNA level led to marked phenotypic consequences because the affected upstream gene is the transcription factor TetR, which regulates expression of a selectable marker (resistance to chloramphenicol). Both cat and tet are endogenously plasmid-encoded, and our expression system is similarly plasmid-based. Because genes in prokaryotic genomes are closely packed together, such effects could also occur for chromosomally encoded genes. In this way, asRNA transcription may provide an explanation for currently poorly understood global fitness effects observed upon synonymous codon substitutions, especially for substitutions located in genomic regions rich in directional and/or divergent promoters (20). Of note, within the E. coli chromosome the histone-like H-NS protein can act to suppress asRNA transcripts, both by occluding access to promoter sites and in some cases directly inhibiting RNAP (51). In addition, complexes of asRNA with a complementary RNA are generally targets of RNase III degradation. In our plasmid-based system, the absence of H-NS repression and no likely association with a complementary RNA could further enhance the level of asRNA originating from the intragenic TSS.

The observed effects of cat codon substitutions on tetR transcription and translation were significantly larger than previously reported effects on CAT protein folding efficiency (14), demonstrating that transcriptional regulation can be more sensitive than cotranslational folding to synonymous codon substitutions, even in a system with demonstrated effects of synonymous codon usage on folding efficiency. Similarly, others have shown that intragenic 5′ codon substitutions typically result in larger effects on translation initiation than cotranslational folding (8, 9, 52).

There are >1080 synonymous sequences that can encode the WT CAT protein sequence. Of the nine synonymous cat sequences studied here, four showed significantly increased levels of asRNA transcription relative to WT. The frequency of these observed changes in transcription demonstrates that sequences that lead to significant levels of asRNA transcription are readily accessible in synonymous sequence space. These results have two important implications: First, synonymous codon usage may be subject to selective pressures to avoid enhancing TSSs within protein-coding sequences. In addition to the triggering of regulatory mechanisms as described here, other reasons to avoid enhancing internal promoters include avoiding transcriptional interference and the metabolic costs and resulting fitness costs of uncontrolled transcription (19, 34). Similarly, in mycobacteria, synonymous codon substitutions introduced intragenic TSSs on the sense strand, some of which led to translation of new, shorter protein isoforms (53). Second, these selection pressures may be gene context dependent, as in some contexts, selection for sequences that up-regulate neighboring genes might provide an advantage (20, 54, 55). More investigation is needed to determine which, if any, genes have evolved to exploit internal promoter sites to regulate neighboring genes.

Of note, while promoter prediction algorithms can now accurately predict the expression level for naturally occurring promoters (34), prediction results for the intragenic TSS identified here were mixed, which may complicate computational identification of the impact of synonymous mutations on transcription levels using existing algorithms. Nevertheless, if selection for or against internal promoter-guided transcription is widespread, it may partially explain why synonymous codon usage is under selection in bacterial genomes (56–58). A major implication of these findings is that the transcriptional regulatory network and the selective pressures it places on synonymous codon usage may be significantly larger than our current understanding. Such selective pressures could provide an explanation for the overall low levels of pervasive transcription, a term used to describe the widespread detection in bacteria of RNAs derived from unexpected TSSs, often on the noncoding strand (19, 29). Our results support the model (19, 21) that synonymous mutations that enhance transcription of these RNAs can result in important functional consequences.

Materials and Methods

Construction of Synonymous mRNA Sequences and Terminator Addition.

The designed cat synonymous sequences were cloned into plasmid pKT under the control of a titratable tet promoter (59). All oligonucleotides (SI Appendix, Tables S7 and S8) were ordered from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT). MEGAWHOP (60) and FastCloning (61) were used to clone synonymous cat sequences into the pKT plasmid. Initially, these sequences each included an N-terminal 7X His tag and a C-terminal ssrA degradation tag; the ssrA tag was subsequently removed using FastCloning to generate the final constructs used here. To prevent CAT translation, the cat ribosome-binding site (RBS) was mutated from GAAGGA to TTCTCT. To test whether some cat coding sequences introduce new TetR binding sites, the cat expression plasmid was modified using FastCloning to insert codons 40 to 220 from four cat mutants (WT, GA, CAI, and WTscr) 287 nucleotides downstream of the cat stop codon. To decrease intragenic transcription originating from within the cat coding sequence, the 57 nt bacterial terminator sequence ECK120033737 (37) was used to replace a sequence of the same length within the 5′ invariant region of three cat coding sequences (WT, GA, and CAI). Specifically, nucleotides 8 to 64 of each RBS-mutated cat coding sequence were replaced with ECK120033737, where nucleotide 1 denotes the first nucleotide of the start codon. The sequence of each construct used here was confirmed by Sanger and nanopore sequencing.

Protein Quantification.

Saturated overnight cultures of E. coli KA12 were prepared by inoculating 5 mL of Luria Broth (LB) containing 100 μg/mL ampicillin with a single colony followed by growth overnight with shaking at 37 °C. The resulting saturated culture was used to inoculate to 3 mL of LB containing 100 μg/mL ampicillin and 600 ng/mL tetracycline (tc) to an OD600 of 0.05. Cultures were grown with shaking at 37 °C for 3 h, then, cells were pelleted, resuspended to OD600 10 in SDS loading dye, boiled for 20 min, and centrifuged for 1 min at 15,000 rpm, and 15 µL of cleared cell lysate (or resuspended insoluble material) was separated by electrophoresis on a 12% polyacrylamide gel. Protein bands were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane and probed with primary antibodies [mouse anti-His (Qiagen) to detect CAT; mouse anti-TetR (MoBiTec); mouse anti-GAPDH (Invitrogen)]. The membrane was washed three times and then incubated with goat anti-mouse Alexa647-labeled secondary antibody (Invitrogen) followed by measurement of fluorescence emission (excitation: 628 nm; emission: 676 nm) across the blot. CAT and TetR protein bands were quantified using ImageJ, corrected to the loading control GAPDH, and normalized to WT to compare bands across multiple blots. For each experiment, the linear response range was determined by quantifying bands from a serial dilution and analyzing by linear regression intensity as a function of dilution.

mRNA Quantification.

mRNA abundance was determined using RT-qPCR. Saturated overnight cultures of E. coli KA12 containing 100 μg/mL ampicillin were used to inoculate 12-well plates containing 3 mL of LB, 100 μg/mL ampicillin, and 600 ng/mL tetracycline (tc). Wells were inoculated to OD600 0.05 and grown using a Synergy H1 microplate reader (BioTek) with continuous double-orbital shaking at 37 °C, for 3 h. An aliquot of cells was diluted to OD600 0.3, and 500 µL of diluted cells was added to 1 mL of RNAprotect Bacteria Reagent provided in the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen; catalog #74104). Protocols provided in the “RNAprotect Bacteria Reagent Handbook 2015” were used for RNA extraction (“Protocol 1”), on-column DNase digestion (“Appendix B”), and RNA purification (“Protocol 7”). RNA concentration was determined by absorbance at 260 nm. To remove any contaminating genomic or plasmid DNA, 10 µL of RNA (1.0 to 1.9 µg) was digested with 2 units of RNase-free DNase (Ambion/Invitrogen) in 10 µL of RNase-free DNase buffer at 37 °C for 30 min. Samples were spiked with 1 µL of DNase and incubated at 37 °C for another 30 min. To denature the DNase, 1 µL of 100 mM EDTA was added, and samples were incubated at 65 °C for 10 min.

Reverse transcription (RT) was performed with the iScript RT kit (BioRad) using 10 µL (0.50 to 0.95 µg) of RNA digested with DNase. Total RT reaction volume was 20 µL (10 µL RNA, 4 µL 5 × buffer, 2 µL random hexamer primers, and 1 µL reverse transcriptase). A control lacking reverse transcriptase was included for all mutants. The RT reaction was performed on a MyCycler (BioRad) using the following parameters: 5 min at 25 °C, 30 min at 42 °C, and 5 min at 85 °C.

qPCR volume per sample was 5 µL total [1 µL of fivefold diluted cDNA, 60 nM primers (IDT), and 2.5 μL SsoAdvanced SYBR Green Supermix (BioRad)]. Using a liquid handling robot, 5 µL was dispensed in technical triplicate for each CAT mutant into a hard-shell, 96-well, thin-wall PCR plate (BioRad). Amplification was performed using a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (BioRad). Primers used for amplification were cat (forward primer: GCATCACCATCACCATCACCATAAC; reverse primer: GATAAAACTCAAAATGCTCACGGCG), total tetR RNA (forward primer: CGTAAACTCGCCCAGAAGCTA; reverse primer: ATCTTGCCAGCTTTCCCCTTC), tetR asRNA (forward: TAAGCAGCTCTAATGCGCTGT; reverse: TTAATTTCGCGGGATCGGCTA), and GAPDH reference gene (forward primer: CGGTACCGTTGAAGTGAAAGAC; reverse primer: ACCAGTTGCTTCAGCGAC). PCR efficiency for each primer set was as follows: cat (103.98%; R2 = 0.996), total tetR RNA (103.58%; R2 = 0.987), tetR asRNA (98.88%; R2 = 0.989), and GAPDH (95.60%, R2 = 0.998).

To calculate fold change, ΔCt values were calculated for each biological replicate per mutant (ΔCt = Ct of target − Ct of GAPDH). To calculate ΔΔCt for all constructs, the ΔCt of WT was subtracted from the ΔCt for each biological replicate (ΔΔCt = ΔCt target − ΔCt WT). This approach was used to minimize variation in the data due to plate-to-plate variation between biological replicates. This results in a ΔΔCt value for each biological replicate per construct. The fold change is calculated as 2−ΔΔCt per biological replicate per construct. Fold change was averaged across three biological replicates; mean fold change and SD are plotted relative to WT.

Plasmid DNA Quantification.

E. coli KA12 transformed with a pKT-cat expression plasmid was grown as described above for 3 h without inducer. A 1 mL culture aliquot was pelleted at 4,600×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and cell pellets were resuspended in PBS pH 7.4 (1.47 mM KH2PO4, 7.81 mM Na2HPO4, 137 mM NaCl, and 2.68 mM KCl). Cells were diluted 1:100 in PBS, and 100 µL was lysed as described (62). qPCRs were carried out as described above for mRNA quantification using the same CAT and GAPDH primers and 1 μL of lysate in place of cDNA. Mean fold change was calculated as described above, except that ΔΔCt was calculated by subtracting the average ΔCt WT (ΔΔCt = ΔCt target – average ΔCt WT) instead of using ΔCt WT per biological replicate, as all DNA qPCRs were performed on a single 96-well plate.

Rapid Amplification of cDNA Ends (5’-RACE) Analysis.

RNA extraction was performed as for RT-qPCR experiments. The 5′-RACE kit (Invitrogen) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Specifically, cDNA synthesis was performed using the tetR-specific primer (GSP1), TACTTTTATCTAATCTCGAC, and purified. Next, a homopolymeric tail was added to each cDNA 3′ end using dCTP and terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase. dC-tailed cDNA was amplified using a primer specific for our system that binds to the intergenic region (ACCACTCCCTATCAGTGATAGAGAAAAGTCTAGCCGATCCCGC) and the abridged anchor primer provided by Invitrogen. For further amplification, this PCR product was diluted (0.01%) in a nested PCR using cat-specific primer GCATCACCATCACCATCACCATA and the abridged universal anchor primer provided by Invitrogen. The resulting 5′ RACE PCR products were purified and subjected to Sanger sequencing using the cat-specific primer listed above.
