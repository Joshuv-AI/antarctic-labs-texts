Long‐Term Outcome and Mortality in Adolescent Girls 8 Years After Treatment for Anorexia Nervosa
Authors: Norbert Quadflieg, Silke Naab, Manfred Fichter, Ulrich Voderholzer
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveAssessment of the longer‐term outcome of anorexia nervosa (AN) in female adolescent inpatients (N = 132).MethodA follow‐up (mean 8.2 years) after treatment was conducted. A subsample of 39 patients with at least 10 years of follow‐up (mean 14 years) was defined.ResultsOver the 8‐year follow‐up period, the body mass index (BMI) increased from 14.33 (1.65) to 19.04 kg/m2 (2.97; t[112] = 17.33, p < 0.001, d = 1.63), and BMI percentiles increased from 0.50 (1.14) to 24.96 (26.81; t[112] = 9.83, p < 0.001, d = 0.92). Remission was found in 32.5% (8‐year total sample) and 48.6% (14‐year subsample). In the 8‐year total sample, 15.1% still had AN or had relapsed (8.1% in the 14‐year subsample). A cross‐over from AN to binge‐eating disorder was rare. The main cross‐over occurred from AN to an eating disorder not otherwise specified (37.5% and 27.0%, respectively). The standardized mortality ratio was 21.7.DiscussionIn the long run, eating disorder diagnoses decreased significantly. Although a considerable proportion of patients recovered from their eating disorder, the number of recovered patients remained limited, with long‐term negative consequences in a large proportion of patients. Standardized mortality was excessive, calling for ever‐better therapies. Additional studies are needed to show if improved therapies lead to a better long‐term outcome.

Summary The study contributes data on the long‐term course and outcome of AN in a very large sample of inpatients.In the long run, only one‐third to one‐half of the patients achieve freedom from eating disorder symptoms, with 8% to 15% showing a chronic course of AN.Although this study is not a dedicated therapy evaluation study, it emphasizes the need for improved psychotherapy.

Introduction

Anorexia nervosa (AN) is a severe mental disorder with a frequent beginning in late childhood or adolescence. Patients and their caregivers are deeply interested in how this illness will turn out in the long run. Interest in the future course of this illness is further increased by the young age of many who are affected by this potentially life‐threatening condition with its severe emaciation and cognitive focus on avoidance of weight gain, food, and body shape, as well as the extensive dependence of self‐esteem on body weight and shape.

Evidence on the longer‐term outcome of AN is limited. One important criticism is that psychological symptoms are not included in many categorizations of outcomes (Bardone‐Cone et al. 2010). Eating disorder (ED) diagnoses of AN include both physical (body weight) and cognitive (psychological) symptoms, and using ED diagnoses as an outcome measure seems to be a suitable approach to consider both points. We used this approach to outcome categorization in the present study.

In studies on adolescent patients with AN, outcome diagnoses were 2.9% AN, 11.8% bulimia nervosa (BN), 29.4% ED not otherwise specified (EDNOS), and 55.9% no ED at 7‐year follow‐up (Herpertz‐Dahlmann et al. 2001). Across three studies, about 8 years after treatment, 7.7%, 2.9%, and 2.0% were diagnosed with AN, 9.6%, 5.9%, and 2.0% with BN, and 19.2%, 23.5%, and 14.3% were diagnosed with EDNOS. The proportions without an ED diagnosis were 63.5%, 67.6%, and 81.6% (Herpertz‐Dahlmann et al. 2018; Nilsson and Häglöff, 2005; Halvorsen, Andersen, and Heyerdahl 2004, respectively). 10 years after inpatient treatment, Herpertz‐Dahlmann et al. (2001) reported 2.6% AN, 5.1% BN, 23.1% EDNOS, and 69.2% without an ED diagnosis. A recent study found 5.8% AN and 9.3% BN after 9 years (Chapelon et al. 2021).

Studies with longer follow‐ups (11 to 22 years after treatment) found a wide range of diagnostic outcome rates, percentages ranging from 2.7% to 15.8% for AN, 0% to 9.5% for BN, 10.3% to 19.2% for EDNOS, and 57.7% to 85.3% for no ED (Amianto et al. 2023; Andrés‐Pepiñá et al. 2020; Castro‐Fornieles et al. 2021; Lange et al. 2019; Nilsson and Hägglöf 2005; Strober, Freeman, and Morrel 1997). Two studies reported one death each (Herpertz‐Dahlmann et al. 2018; Andrés‐Pepiñá et al. 2020).

To add to this evidence, we capitalized upon the availability of data from a well‐characterized, large clinical sample of patients with AN described in detail elsewhere (Fichter and Quadflieg 2016; Fichter et al. 2017). This large sample of 1693 patients included 179 adolescent girls aged below 18 years at the beginning of treatment, who formed the basis for our present study sample. The aim of our study was to describe the outcome of ED diagnosis about 8 years after inpatient treatment. This knowledge is important for informing patients and their caregivers, clinicians, and clinical researchers on the long‐term perspective of the disorder's course and outcome, and for improving therapy.

Method

Participants

Between September 1985 and March 2006, 179 adolescent girls (mean age 17.12 [0.75] years) were treated for AN at the Schoen Clinic Roseneck. Treatment followed the guidelines for cognitive‐behavioral psychotherapy (CBT) for EDs and took place in open units. Starting in June 2007, a questionnaire was sent (with additional mailings to nonresponders) in several batches to the former patients. A total of 125 patients returned the follow‐up questionnaire. Seven patients were deceased at the time of follow‐up. Forty‐seven patients could not be assessed. Of these, 28 (59.6%) did not respond to any messages from our work group, 8 (17.0%) refused participation explicitly, and for 11 patients no valid address was available. Besides age below 18 years, inclusion criteria were a DSM‐IV (American Psychiatric Association 1994) diagnosis of AN, inpatient treatment of at least 21 days to ensure a minimum of ED‐specific treatment, and a completed set of questionnaires at admission. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Bavarian Medical Association. All participants provided informed consent. Thirty‐nine patients had a follow‐up of at least 10 years (mean 14.08 years). To provide insight into the very long‐term outcome, we defined these patients into an additional subsample. All 39 patients of this subsample were also included in the 8‐year follow‐up total sample. We will use the terms 8‐year total sample and 14‐year subsample to differentiate between these two groups. Patient flow is shown in Supporting information.

Assessment Instruments

At admission and follow‐up, patients completed a set of questionnaires including the following:

Structured Inventory for Anorexic and Bulimic Eating Disorders Self‐Rating Form (SIAB‐S)

This questionnaire (Fichter and Quadflieg 2000) provided DSM‐IV ED diagnoses and additional clinical information at the beginning of inpatient treatment and follow‐up. Diagnoses were defined according to a standardized algorithm (see online source shown below in the reference of Fichter and Quadflieg 2000) and included AN, BN, binge‐eating disorder (BED), and the five types of EDNOS described in DSM‐IV. Four additional ED syndromes consisting of constellations of ED symptoms that did not meet the threshold of EDNOS as described in DSM‐IV included less severe syndromes like (1) low weight and one of either criterion B or C of AN, (2) binge eating and one of either criterion B or C of BN, (3) atypical binges, and one of either criterion B or C of BN, and (4) swallowing and regurgitation of food. More recent studies do not use DSM‐IV EDNOS diagnoses but the DSM‐5 (American Psychiatric Association 2013) diagnosis of other specified feeding and eating disorders (OSFED). ED syndromes in our study would be categorized as unspecified feeding and eating disorders (UFED) in DSM‐5.

Eating Disorder Inventory and Brief Symptom Inventory

The early 64‐item version was used and the 6‐point rating scale from “never” to “always” was transformed into a 0–3 format as recommended by the original authors for clinical samples (Garner, Olmstead, and Polivy 1983; Paul and Thiel 2004).

The Brief Symptom Inventory (BSI; Derogatis and Melisaratos 1983; Franke 2000) measured general psychopathology in nine subscales and three general indices. See the Supporting Information for a list of Eating Disorder Inventory (EDI) and BSI subscales with Cronbach's alpha.

Information on body weight and height and sociodemographic characteristics was obtained from hospital records and the participants at follow‐up. Hospital records also provided discharge body weight for the majority of patients.

Statistical Analyses

Means and standard deviations are presented for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. BMIs were converted to gender and age‐standardized percentiles derived from the German Nutrition Survey of a nationally representative sample of 24,632 individuals (Hebebrand et al. 1996). Paired t‐tests compared admission and follow‐up values. Effect sizes are reported as Cohen's d (Cohen 1988). Point biserial correlation coefficients were computed for the association of admission and discharge BMIs with remission status. For each analysis, the number of valid cases or degrees of freedom is included. The standardized mortality ratio (SMR) was computed as described elsewhere (Fichter and Quadflieg 2016). A total of 1071 person‐years were included in this analysis of adolescent girls.

Diagnostic ED outcome at follow‐up was categorized into remission [no 3‐month diagnosis of AN, BN, BED, EDNOS (OSFED), or ED syndrome (UFED)], partial remission [diagnosis of an ED syndrome (UFED)], and poor diagnostic outcome (diagnosis of AN, BN, BED, or EDNOS).

Results

The mean follow‐up period was 8.18 (4.86) years after the end of the index treatment and the age at follow‐up was 25.52 (4.74) years. In the 14‐year subsample, patients were assessed 14.08 (3.80) years after the treatment, and the age at follow‐up was 31.19 (3.91) years. At admission, patients without follow‐up information scored lower than participants on the EDI Drive for thinness scale (8.09 [6.05] vs. 10.55 [5.33]; t[170] = 2.61; p = 0.010; d = 0.45). Groups did not differ (data not shown) concerning AN subtype, age at admission, duration of treatment, duration of ED, comorbid Axis‐I disorder, comorbid personality disorder, and the subscales of the BSI and EDI (except EDI Drive for thinness).

The mean age at admission was 17.12 (0.75) years in the 8‐year total sample including deceased patients (N = 132). The duration of ED was 2.65 (1.33) years, and a mean of 0.90 (1.23) inpatient and 1.57 (1.82) outpatient treatments before the index admission was reported. One‐hundred‐ and ‐fourteen patients (86.4%) reported medical treatment for AN before the index admission [64 (48.5%) inpatient and 103 (78.0%) outpatient treatment]. Sixty‐five (49.2%) patients were diagnosed with at least one comorbid psychiatric disorder at treatment. Fifty‐seven (43.2%) patients were diagnosed with AN restricting type and 75 (56.8%) patients with AN binge‐eating/purging type. The mean index inpatient treatment was 83.41 (31.38) days. The BMI (N = 123 with discharge information available) increased from 14.42 (1.69) kg/m2 at admission to 16.42 (2.01) kg/m2 at discharge (t[122] = 14.46, p < 0.001, d = 1.30), and BMI percentiles increased from 0.58 (1.19) at admission to 7.02 (12.86) at follow‐up (t[122] = 5.70, p < 0.001, d = 0.51).

Diagnostic ED outcome at follow‐up is shown in Table 1 and Figures S1 and S2. About one‐third (32.5%) of the patients were remitted and 6.7% reported partial remission. AN was still present in 18 (15.0%) patients, 6 patients with restricting type, and 12 patients with binge‐eating/purging type. An additional nine patients had EDNOS type 1, which in the newer DSM‐5 would also qualify for a full AN diagnosis. The transition from a full AN diagnosis to EDNOS was reported by 45 (37.5%) patients. Cross‐over to BED was rare. The outcome status of remission from an ED did not correlate with the BMIs at admission (r = −0.012; p = 0.892; N = 124) or at discharge (r = 0.039; p = 0.667; N = 116). Remitted patients did not differ from nonremitted patients in BMI at admission or discharge. In the 14‐year subsample, remission was higher (48.6% vs. 32.5%) and the percentage of EDNOS was lower (27.0% vs. 37.5%) than in the 8‐year total sample. In this sample, the BMI increased from 14.33 (1.65) kg/m2 at admission to 19.04 kg/m2 at follow‐up (t[112] = 17.33, p < 0.001, d = 1.56), and BMI percentiles increased from 0.50 (1.14) at admission to 24.96 (26.81) at follow‐up (t[112] = 9.83, p < 0.001, d = 0.92). Of the 112 patients with data on diagnostic ED outcome and discharge BMI, 75 (67.0%) had been discharged with a BMI below 17.50 kg/m2 and 37 (33.0%) with a BMI of at least 17.5 kg/m2. BMI at follow‐up did not differ between these groups with a mean BMI of 18.70 (3.02; N = 70) in patients with discharge BMI below 17.50 kg/m2, and a mean BMI of 19.63 (2.96; N = 34) in patients with a discharge BMI of at least 17.50 kg/m2 (t[102] = 1.49; p = 0.140; d = 0.31). Diagnostic ED outcomes (AN, BN, BED, EDNOS, partial or full remission) also did not differ between these groups. Results for all subscales of the EDI and BSI are provided in Tables S1–S4 (see also Figures S3 and S4) in the Supporting Information.

Discussion

Our study adds a large sample of female adolescents to the literature on the outcome of AN. At an adult age, 8 years after intensive treatment 15% still met the criteria for AN and 7.5% met the criteria for BN. Many former patients crossed over to EDNOS (37.5%). Remission from ED was found in 32.5%. In the 14‐year subsample 8.1% each were diagnosed with AN and BN and 27.0% with EDNOS. The remission rate was higher in this subsample (48.6%).

Several studies with follow‐up periods of 7 to 8.5 years reported higher rates of remitted former inpatients (including a total of 10 males) without an ED diagnosis at follow‐up than in our 8‐year follow‐up. There were several important differences from the current study. The patients in these studies were younger with a mean age of 14.9, 16.2, and 12.5 and a median of 15.0 years compared to 17.12 years in our sample (Halvorsen, Andersen, and Heyerdahl 2004; Herpertz‐Dahlmann et al. 2001, 2018; Nilsson and Hägglöf 2005; respectively). Treatment duration was much longer with 19.9, 5.7, and a median of 12 months compared to 2.8 months in our sample (Halvorsen, Andersen, and Heyerdahl 2004; Herpertz‐Dahlmann et al. 2001; Nilsson and Hägglöf 2005; respectively). Our sample also included a larger proportion (56.8%) of binge‐eating/purging type AN compared to 8.2% in the study of Herpertz‐Dahlmann et al. (2018), and our sample had a longer duration of AN before index treatment (2.65 years) than the 11 months in the sample of Halvorsen, Andersen, and Heyerdahl (2004). Additionally, our sample had more medical treatment for AN before index admission (86.4%) than the sample of Herpertz‐Dahlmann et al. (2001; 51%), and also more inpatient treatment before index admission (48.5%) than the patients of Herpertz‐Dahlmann et al. (2018) who were selected for their first inpatient treatment. An older review showed chronicity to be a predictor of poor outcomes (Steinhausen 2002). Our sample showed a more chronic course of AN before index treatment and a high proportion of binge‐eating and purging behavior, which was shown to be a risk factor for poor outcomes (Steinhausen 2002). Compared to our 14‐year subsample, remission rates were higher in the 15‐year follow‐up of Amianto et al. (2023) and in the 16‐year follow‐up of Nilsson and Hägglöf (2005). Differences between our and Nilsson and Hägglöff's samples are described above. The patients of Amianto et al. (2023) were treated as outpatients and the treatment duration was again much longer (12 months). Additionally, 26% of the patients of Amianto et al. were not treated for full AN but were diagnosed as EDNOS. Cross‐over to BN in our sample was comparable to other studies (Halvorsen, Andersen, and Heyerdahl 2004; Herpertz‐Dahlmann et al. 2001, 2018; Nilsson and Hägglöf 2005) and diagnostic change to BED was rare. As in these other studies, the most frequent cross‐over was to EDNOS. This may indicate an improvement of symptoms, but, on the other hand, this and the prevalence of cognitive symptoms at follow‐up reflect a persistence of a serious ED into adulthood calling for more support over time. This is also reflected in the improved EDI and BSI scores, which were still elevated at follow‐up (Franke 2000; Garner, Olmstead, and Polivy 1983). Although the BMI increased over time to a mean normal level, our data show that focusing on body weight is not enough to achieve remission from AN. On the other hand, we cannot exclude the possibility that the low discharge weight in our sample contributed to the limited remission from ED. A relatively large proportion of our sample was discharged with a BMI below 17.50 kg/m2, probably reflecting a common discharge practice at that time. To shed additional light on this issue, we compared the BMI with a sample treated in the same clinic about 15 years later (Quadflieg et al. 2023). The discharge BMI was more than one BMI point higher (mean 17.53 [1.58] kg/m2) and the percentage of patients discharged with a BMI below 17.50 kg/m2 was considerably lower (43%) than in our present study sample. One probable reason is a change in the approach to the treatment of AN. In the earlier years of treatment, the focus was on inpatient treatment with subsequent outpatient treatment being scarcely available. With more outpatient opportunities becoming available over time, efforts were made to limit inpatient treatment, resulting in an increased focus on weight gain during inpatient treatment by supervised meals, exposure in teaching kitchens, and close weight monitoring.

In our study, neither admission nor discharge BMI showed an association with ED outcome. Diagnostic outcomes also did not differ between patients discharged with a lower and higher weight. Several studies emphasized the relevance of a higher discharge BMI for a better outcome after treatment (Amemiya et al. 2012; Fichter and Quadflieg 1999; Föcker et al. 2015; Redgrave et al. 2021; Steinhausen et al. 2008). However, only Steinhausen et al. (2008; 8‐year follow‐up) and Föcker et al. (2015; 1‐year follow‐up) reported on adolescent samples, the other studies included adults or a mixed sample of adolescents and adults. In their meta‐analysis, Vall and Wade (2015) concluded that higher weight at discharge was a mediator of better outcomes. The scoping review of Frostad et al. (2022) concluded that a lower body weight in the normal range at discharge predicted relapse. However, the longest follow‐up interval was 4.6 years in the meta‐analysis, and 2.2 years in the scoping review, both considerably shorter than our 8 years of follow‐up. Thus, the empirical evidence on the role of body weight at discharge for long‐term outcomes in adolescents remains inconclusive, calling for more long‐term studies on the outcome of adolescent AN.

Adolescent girls with AN carried a 21‐fold increased risk of death compared to girls of the same age in the German population. In a mixed adolescent and adult sample, Zipfel et al. (2000) reported an SMR of 9.8 12 years after inpatient treatment. A meta‐analysis reported an overall SMR of 5.86 after 14.2 years (Arcelus et al. 2011). Thus, mortality was very high in our sample. However, our sample was small and the confidence interval was large. Additionally, mortality in this age group is generally low, so each death in this young group contributes heavily to the SMR.

The study has several important strengths: First, our data were assessed prospectively in a large clinical sample and covered a very long time period. Second, for a clinical sample of adolescents with one diagnosis, the sample size was very large with a very good participation rate. Third, all patients received the same multimodal treatment based on cognitive‐behavioral principles in special units for EDs.

On the other hand, our study has several limitations: First, hospitalized patients constitute a sample on the more severe end of the spectrum. We do not know if our results extend to less severe cases or to patients in outpatient or day treatment. Similarly, we included only female adolescents and it is unclear if our results extend to male adolescents. However, a previous comparison of male and female adolescents from the original sample of 1693 patients with AN showed male and female adolescent patients to be generally similar (Quadflieg et al. 2022). We had information only on the sex assigned at birth and could not analyze gender groups. In addition, race and ethnicity are not included in the hospital documentation. Second, treatment in our sample occurred many years ago and does not reflect more recent developments in psychotherapy. This could limit the relevance of the findings to current treatment practices. However, in long‐term outcome studies, such delay is unavoidable. We strongly emphasize the need for additional long‐term outcome studies for evaluating more recent psychotherapies. Third, although a prospective study, we have only data from cross‐sectional assessments of outcomes, and the evidence on the full course of AN remains limited. On the other hand, frequent assessments may constitute an additional intervention that may influence the course and outcome of EDs by itself. Fourth, ED diagnoses were based on patients' self‐ratings and not on expert interviews. To account for this, we applied the standardized diagnostic algorithm of the SIAB‐S at admission and follow‐up. Fifth, due to ethical, budgetary, and staff restrictions, we could not include an untreated control group and thus could not determine whether the observed outcome was attributable to the treatment received. Sixth, understanding the factors contributing to the death of patients with AN could provide valuable insights into improving treatment. We were not able to ascertain the causes of death and therefore we do not know if the death was attributable to complications of AN.

Conclusion

On a positive note, many patients with very severe AN in adolescence no longer have an ED diagnosis in adulthood. A large proportion of patients, however, retain symptoms or even diagnoses of ED. As is known from other studies, outcomes improved with the time of follow‐up. There is a need for after‐treatment programs to maintain improvements achieved during treatment.
