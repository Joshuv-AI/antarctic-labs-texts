Encapsulation of probiotics to improve their survival: A focus on Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis BB-12
Authors: Defeng Shu, Tianning Zhang, Yajie Dong, Jinlong Xu, Yongkai Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProbiotic viability during processing, storage, and gastrointestinal transit remains a key limitation to probiotic functionality. Although encapsulation is widely used to enhance probiotic survival, most reviews focus on materials and techniques while neglecting strain-specific differences. This review adopts a strain-centered perspective by using Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis BB-12 as a model to systematically reassess encapsulation strategies. By integrating single-strain and co-encapsulation systems, this review elucidates how carrier structure, protection mechanisms, and environmental stresses collectively govern BB-12 stability. In addition, food applications are reinterpreted based on matrix-dependent stress conditions, including acid, osmotic, and oxidative factors. A conceptual “matrix–mechanism–carrier matching” framework is proposed to guide the rational design and selection of encapsulation systems. Overall, this review provides a mechanism-oriented and transferable understanding of probiotic encapsulation, offering practical insights for the design and industrial application of functional foods.Keywords: Encapsulation, Probiotics, Survival, BB-12, Food application

Introduction

Probiotics have attracted significant attention as functional food ingredients and are widely applied in fermented dairy products, functional beverages, dietary supplements, and other probiotic-enriched foods (Anumudu, Miri, & Onyeaka, 2024; Barajas-Álvarez, González-Ávila, & Espinosa-Andrews, 2023) due to their diverse health benefits, including alleviating colitis and constipation (Chen et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025; Ji et al., 2025; Virk et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025; the enhancement of immune responses (Sheng et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024); the regulation of gut microbiota (Chandrasekaran, Weiskirchen, & Weiskirchen, 2024; Zhang et al., 2024), reducing cancer-related risk (Chen et al., 2024); the regulation of nerves (Lan et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2025), and the reduction of blood sugar and lipids (Jing et al., 2025; Wang, Wu, Dhital, Yu, & Chen, 2025; Zhu et al., 2025). According to the accepted definition, these health-promoting effects require that probiotics reach the colon in a viable state (Aykut, Erdoğan, Çelik, & Gürbüz, 2024; Yu et al., 2025). However, maintaining probiotic viability is highly challenging, as cells are subjected to multiple stresses during industrial processing, storage, and gastrointestinal transit (Virk et al., 2025). For instance; freeze-drying and spray-drying can induce physical damage; desiccation stress; and thermal shock (Ge et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024), while environmental factors such as temperature, humidity, and oxygen can affect their metabolic stability during shelf life (Han, McClements, Liu, & Liu, 2024). Following oral administration; gastric acid and bile salts further compromise membrane integrity and cellular activity (Ma, Liu, Chen, Zhang, & Yuan, 2025). These limitations collectively represent a major bottleneck in the development of stable and effective probiotic food products.

Encapsulation has been recognized as an effective strategy to mitigate these adverse conditions by forming a protective barrier around probiotic cells (Shu, Liu, Xu, & Yuan, 2025; Yang et al., 2023; Zhong et al., 2025). Over the past decade, substantial progress has been made in the development of encapsulation technologies, and a variety of encapsulation systems—such as microcapsules, emulsions, hydrogels, films, and nano-coatings—have been developed and demonstrated to improve probiotic survival to varying degrees (Nawaz et al., 2023; Virk et al., 2024; Virk et al., 2025; Yuan, Yin, Zhai, & Chen, 2024). The efficiency of these systems is largely determined by the composition of wall material, formulation ratios, encapsulation techniques, and application environments, and these aspects have been extensively reviewed (Barajas-Álvarez et al., 2023; Bu et al., 2025; Muhammad et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024). Despite these advances, most existing reviews primarily compare encapsulation materials and technologies across multiple probiotic species while implicitly assuming that encapsulation performance is broadly transferable among strains. However, probiotic strains often exhibit substantial physiological and stress-response differences that can influence their interaction with encapsulation matrices and their survival during processing and gastrointestinal transit. Consequently, even within the same encapsulation system, different strains may display distinct tolerance profiles (Hao et al., 2025; Rahali, Ariech, Moussaoui, & Riazi, 2022), particularly when applied to diverse carrier matrices. The role of probiotic strain differences has therefore received comparatively limited attention in current encapsulation studies. Under these circumstances, comparisons across multiple strains may obscure the intrinsic performance of encapsulation systems because strain-dependent variability can confound the interpretation of encapsulation efficiency and stability outcomes. This lack of strain-specific evaluation may represent a key limitation in the current state of encapsulation research. Therefore, focusing on a single, well-characterized probiotic strain provides a more controlled framework for systematically evaluating encapsulation effectiveness.

Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis BB-12 (BB-12) is among the most well-established and widely commercialized probiotics (Collins, Jiménez, & Wellejus, 2025). It has been extensively incorporated into dairy and non-dairy food products and serves as a benchmark strain in both academic research and industrial applications. Moreover; BB-12 exhibits many of the common challenges associated with probiotic survival and functionality; while simultaneously serving as a robust model for encapsulation studies (Lejaniya et al., 2023). Importantly, BB-12 displays strain-specific physiological traits, including a relatively robust cell wall structure, moderate tolerance to acid and bile stress, and stable metabolic activity, which collectively influence its response to encapsulation processes. Compared with more stress-sensitive probiotic strains, such as highly acid- or oxygen-sensitive species, these characteristics enable BB-12 to better withstand certain processing and storage stresses while still benefiting substantially from encapsulation-mediated protection. Because BB-12 has been widely used in both experimental encapsulation studies and commercial food systems, it provides a particularly suitable model strain for systematically examining how encapsulation materials, carrier structures, and processing strategies influence probiotic stability and delivery performance. By minimizing confounding effects arising from strain heterogeneity, a BB-12–centered analysis allows clearer comparison of encapsulation strategies in terms of protection efficiency, gastrointestinal stability, scalability, and cost-effectiveness. However, despite the extensive use of BB-12, a comprehensive strain-focused synthesis integrating encapsulation mechanisms, carrier design, co-encapsulation strategies, and food application contexts remains limited in the current literature.

More importantly, focusing on a single well-characterized strain allows the identification of mechanistic relationships between probiotic physiology, encapsulation material properties, and delivery performance. In this review, BB-12 is used not merely as a representative strain but as a model system to examine how carrier structure (e.g., glassy matrices, hydrogel networks, multilayer barriers), encapsulation mechanisms (e.g., vitrification, diffusion limitation, spatial confinement), and food matrix conditions interact to influence probiotic survival. By organizing the literature around these mechanism–structure–performance relationships, this review aims to provide a conceptual framework for interpreting probiotic encapsulation systems beyond simple material comparison, enabling clearer identification of how carrier architecture and physicochemical properties govern probiotic survival.

Therefore, the purpose of this review is to systematically re-examine probiotic encapsulation strategies using BB-12 as a model strain. Unlike previous reviews that broadly compare encapsulation strategies across multiple probiotic species, this review adopts a strain-centered perspective to explicitly evaluate how encapsulation materials, structures, and mechanisms influence BB-12 stability and functionality. Specifically, this review focuses on encapsulation systems developed for BB-12, evaluates the co-encapsulation of BB-12 with other strains, and summarizes recent food applications. By integrating encapsulation mechanisms, carrier-level advantages and limitations, and application-specific considerations, this review aims to provide a more mechanism-oriented synthesis that may help guide the rational design and selection of encapsulation systems for probiotics with similar physiological characteristics. In addition, key challenges related to scalability, cost, long-term stability, and product quality are critically discussed to derive mechanism-driven and generalizable insights that can inform the rational design of encapsulation systems for other probiotic strains with similar physiological characteristics.

Regarding the methodology of this review, relevant literature was retrieved from the Web of Science, Scopus, and PubMed databases using combinations of keywords including “Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis BB-12”, “probiotic encapsulation”, “microencapsulation”, “co-encapsulation”, and “food application”. Studies were included based on their relevance to encapsulation or co-encapsulation strategies involving BB-12, particularly in the context of food systems, storage stability, and gastrointestinal survival, while non-peer-reviewed publications and studies not related to encapsulation were excluded.

Encapsulation of single BB-12 strain

Encapsulation of BB-12 is widely considered important for maintaining its viability during food processing, storage, and gastrointestinal transit, and a range of encapsulation methods has been developed to address this challenge (Hao et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2024). These methods differ substantially in terms of protection efficiency, processing complexity, scalability, and economic feasibility, which is important for practical application in the food industry (Bu et al., 2025). Before discussing specific encapsulation technologies, it is important to consider the physiological characteristics of BB-12 that influence its response to encapsulation strategies. In this section, BB-12 is intentionally used as a model probiotic strain to evaluate how different encapsulation strategies specifically affect BB-12 survival, rather than drawing generalized conclusions for probiotics as a whole. This strain-centered approach enables clearer comparison of encapsulation performance by minimizing confounding effects arising from strain-to-strain variability. Accordingly, this section first discusses the physiological characteristics of BB-12 and their interaction with encapsulation materials, followed by a review of major encapsulation methods applied to this strain.

Physiological characteristics of BB-12 and its encapsulation response mechanism

BB-12 is one of the most widely used probiotic strains in functional foods due to its well-documented safety, gastrointestinal resilience, and functional properties. Nevertheless, similar to many probiotic microorganisms, BB-12 remains sensitive to environmental stresses encountered during food processing, storage, and gastrointestinal transit, including heat exposure, oxygen stress, osmotic pressure, and acidic environments (Xu et al., 2024). These stresses can disrupt membrane integrity; impair cellular metabolism; and ultimately reduce probiotic viability (Ge et al., 2024; Ma et al., 2025).

The physiological characteristics of BB-12 play an important role in determining its response to encapsulation strategies. Structurally, BB-12 possesses a Gram-positive cell envelope composed of a thick peptidoglycan layer and associated surface proteins that contribute to mechanical stability and environmental tolerance (Collins et al., 2025; Lejaniya et al., 2023). However, dehydration, oxidative stress, and high processing temperatures may still compromise membrane stability and affect metabolic activity. In addition, BB-12 is an obligate anaerobe with oxygen-sensitive metabolic pathways, which makes it particularly vulnerable to oxidative damage during processing and storage (Collins et al., 2025; Lejaniya et al., 2023). These strain-specific physiological characteristics indicate that BB-12 is particularly susceptible to oxidative stress and dehydration-induced membrane damage, while exhibiting moderate tolerance to acid and bile stress, which together define its encapsulation requirements. These physiological features influence how encapsulation materials interact with the bacterial cell surface and surrounding microenvironment.

From a mechanistic perspective, several protection mechanisms have been proposed to explain the improved survival of encapsulated BB-12. Glass-forming matrices such as milk solids, polysaccharides, and certain proteins may immobilize bacterial cells during dehydration through vitrification and glass transition phenomena (Dantas, Verruck, de Liz, Hernandez, & Prudencio, 2021; Yang et al., 2024). This process reduces molecular mobility within the matrix and limits oxidative and thermal damage to cellular components. For BB-12, this mechanism is particularly relevant because vitrification can stabilize membrane structure and intracellular components under dehydration and thermal stress conditions, which are major causes of viability loss for this strain. Ionically cross-linked hydrogel systems, particularly alginate-based matrices, form three-dimensional networks through interactions with divalent ions (e.g., Ca2+), creating diffusion barriers that slow the penetration of acids, bile salts, and digestive enzymes (Frakolaki, Katsouli, Giannou, & Tzia, 2020; Vinceković et al., 2024). Given the moderate acid and bile tolerance of BB-12, such diffusion-limiting systems are generally sufficient to enhance gastrointestinal survival without requiring excessively dense or complex carrier structures. In addition, covalent protein-polysaccharide networks formed through reactions such as the Maillard reaction can generate dense matrices with improved structural stability, thereby enhancing resistance to thermal stress and gastrointestinal conditions (El Hosry et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2024). These dense networks are particularly advantageous for BB-12 under heat-processing conditions, where enhanced thermal shielding is required to prevent rapid viability loss.

These interactions between the intrinsic physiological characteristics of BB-12 and the structural properties of encapsulation materials highlight the importance of strain-specific considerations when designing probiotic delivery systems. Importantly, the effectiveness of encapsulation strategies for BB-12 is not solely determined by the material type, but by how well the carrier properties match the strain-specific stress-response profile of BB-12. Understanding these mechanisms helps explain why different encapsulation strategies exhibit varying levels of protection efficiency for BB-12 and provides a mechanistic basis for selecting suitable carrier materials for probiotic stabilization. Therefore, encapsulation design for BB-12 should follow a mechanism–physiology matching principle, in which carrier selection and structural design are tailored to address specific vulnerabilities such as oxygen sensitivity, dehydration stress, and thermal instability. These observations highlight that encapsulation design for BB-12 should consider strain-specific traits such as oxygen sensitivity, membrane robustness, and metabolic activity, rather than relying solely on generic carrier selection strategies. It provides a more rational and design-oriented framework that goes beyond conventional material comparison and supports the development of more effective encapsulation systems for BB-12 and similar probiotic strains.

Spray-drying encapsulation

Among the encapsulation methods, spray-drying remains one of the most widely used techniques due to its efficiency and suitability for large-scale production. From a mechanistic perspective, the rapid dehydration and formation of a solid matrix during spray-drying can physically immobilize BB-12 cells, thereby reducing metabolic activity and oxidative damage during storage. For BB-12, this immobilization effect is particularly important because dehydration-induced membrane damage and oxidative stress are major factors contributing to viability loss during processing and storage. Dantas, Verruck, de Liz, et al. (2021) explored the use of lactose-free milk; supplemented with prebiotics such as inulin and oligofructose; for microencapsulation of BB-12; and the results showed that the lactose-free milk-inulin matrix achieved the highest encapsulation efficiency (88.01%) and storage stability; maintaining probiotic viability for up to 120 days. The inclusion of prebiotics not only improved encapsulation efficiency but also enhanced resilience of BB-12 under simulated gastrointestinal conditions (Dantas, Verruck, de Liz, et al., 2021). The improved stability observed in this system can be attributed to the glass-forming properties of milk solids and the water-binding capacity of prebiotics, which together mitigate thermal and osmotic stresses experienced by BB-12 cells. This is consistent with the mechanism–physiology relationship discussed in Section 2.1, where glass transition-based matrices are particularly effective for stabilizing BB-12 under dehydration and thermal stress conditions. This further demonstrates that the performance of spray-drying systems for BB-12 is governed by the alignment between carrier-induced vitrification effects and the strain-specific vulnerabilities of BB-12, particularly dehydration-induced membrane damage and oxidative stress.

From an industrial perspective, spray-drying offers clear advantages in terms of high throughput, low unit cost, and compatibility with existing food-processing infrastructure, making it one of the most practical encapsulation approaches for large-scale BB-12 applications despite its sensitivity to thermal shock. However, spray-drying has a major drawback: the high temperatures involved in the process may compromise the survival of the probiotics, highlighting a trade-off between process scalability and biological protection, despite the positive outcomes reported when prebiotics are used to protect the cells (Wang et al., 2024; Yin et al., 2024). In the case of BB-12, this trade-off is particularly evident, as its moderate thermal tolerance means that while spray-drying is feasible, survival rates remain highly dependent on carrier composition and process optimization. Therefore, spray-drying systems for BB-12 often require the incorporation of protective agents (e.g., sugars, proteins, or prebiotics) to compensate for heat-induced damage.

Overall, spray-drying represents a cost-effective and scalable encapsulation strategy for BB-12; however, its effectiveness is strongly dependent on the alignment between carrier properties and the strain-specific sensitivity of BB-12 to thermal and oxidative stress. This highlights the need to optimize formulation design rather than relying solely on process efficiency when applying spray-drying to BB-12 encapsulation.

Maillard reaction-based encapsulation systems

In contrast to spray-drying, Maillard reaction-based encapsulation methods, such as protein–polysaccharide conjugates, may offer additional advantages, particularly in terms of stability and heat resistance. Yan et al. (2024) investigated the use of soy protein isolate conjugated with sodium carboxymethyl cellulose through the Maillard reaction, and this conjugation significantly enhanced the heat tolerance and gastrointestinal stability of the encapsulated BB-12, improving its overall viability. This enhanced protection is primarily associated with the formation of covalent bonds between proteins and polysaccharides, which results in a denser and less permeable matrix that restricts heat transfer and digestive fluid penetration.

For BB-12, such dense and covalently cross-linked structures are particularly beneficial because they provide enhanced thermal shielding and structural stability, which are critical for mitigating heat-induced membrane damage and metabolic inactivation during processing. Compared with spray-drying, Maillard reaction-based systems provide superior thermal protection and gastrointestinal resilience. This is especially relevant for BB-12, whose viability is strongly affected by thermal stress despite its moderate tolerance to acidic and bile environments, making thermal protection a key design requirement. In the case of BB-12, these systems are particularly effective in mitigating heat-induced viability loss during processing.

However, while effective, the Maillard reaction requires complex chemical modifications (El Hosry et al., 2025; Han, McClements, et al., 2024), which increase processing complexity and production costs, and may pose challenges for large-scale manufacturing and regulatory standardization, potentially limiting their application in mass-market probiotic foods. In addition, the formation of covalent conjugates may introduce variability in reaction conditions and product consistency, which can further complicate industrial implementation.

From a design perspective, Maillard reaction-based encapsulation systems represent a high-performance but high-complexity strategy for BB-12, where enhanced protection is achieved at the expense of scalability and cost. Therefore, their application may be more suitable for high-value functional foods or specialized delivery systems, rather than large-scale commodity products. Overall, the effectiveness of these systems depends on balancing structural density and process feasibility, while ensuring that the carrier properties align with the strain-specific sensitivity of BB-12 to thermal stress.

Alginate-based gel encapsulation

Extrusion- and emulsification-based gelation methods, typically employing calcium alginate matrices, have emerged as effective approaches for encapsulating probiotics such as BB-12, offering both protection during storage and resilience under gastrointestinal conditions. Frakolaki et al. (2020) combined extrusion with double emulsification (water-in-oil-in-water; W1/O/W2) to create calcium alginate that effectively preserved BB-12 viability for up to four weeks under refrigerated and frozen conditions. This dual-method encapsulation strategy also exhibited a high survival rate (86.1%) under simulated gastrointestinal conditions; demonstrating its robustness (Frakolaki et al., 2020).

The effectiveness of alginate-based systems can be attributed to ionically cross-linked gel networks that act as physical barriers, slowing acid diffusion and enzymatic degradation of BB-12. For BB-12, this diffusion-limiting mechanism is particularly relevant because its moderate tolerance to acidic and bile environments means that partial barrier protection is often sufficient to significantly enhance survival during gastrointestinal transit. Additionally, the inclusion of additives like nanocrystalline cellulose, agar, whey, and casein has been shown to further improve the survival of BB-12 (Frakolaki, Giannou, Topakas, & Tzia, 2021; Vinceković et al., 2024). These additives can enhance network density, reduce porosity, and improve water retention, thereby strengthening the protective effect of alginate matrices for BB-12. These findings suggest that alginate-based gels can be tailored to optimize probiotic protection. Similarly, Georgia, Virginia, and Constantina (2022) explored more complex alginate-based gel systems incorporating carrageenan; glucose; and inulin; which also demonstrated superior protection for BB-12. Microstructural analysis confirmed effective entrapment of the probiotics; thereby supporting long-term viability (Georgia et al., 2022). Such composite systems highlight that alginate matrices can be functionally enhanced to compensate for their relatively loose network structure and limited resistance to environmental stresses.

Collectively, these findings indicate that alginate-based systems are highly suitable for enhancing BB-12 storage stability and gastrointestinal survival, although multistep fabrication processes and multiple additives may limit scalability and increase production costs. However, compared with more complex systems such as Maillard conjugates or multilayer carriers, alginate-based encapsulation represents a balanced strategy for BB-12, providing sufficient protection with relatively lower processing complexity. This makes alginate systems particularly suitable for applications where moderate protection is required, such as refrigerated foods or products with less extreme processing conditions, rather than high-temperature or highly oxidative environments.

Overall, alginate-based encapsulation systems for BB-12 can be considered a “moderate-protection, moderate-complexity” approach, where the effectiveness depends on optimizing network structure and additive composition to match the specific stress conditions encountered during processing and gastrointestinal transit.

Vesicle-inspired encapsulation systems

Emerging vesicle-inspired encapsulation methods, such as milk exosome-based systems, have also been explored for BB-12 delivery. Hao et al. (2025) developed a system using milk exosomes combined with distearoyl phosphatidyl ethanolamine-polyethylene glycol-phenylboric acid to encapsulate BB-12; achieving an encapsulation efficiency of 84.47% and enhanced resistance to gastrointestinal conditions; with a survival rate of 85.28%. Moreover; the encapsulated BB-12 exhibited improved hydrophobicity; auto-aggregation; and mucoadhesion; properties that may enhance its ability to colonize the gastrointestinal tract (Hao et al., 2025).

For BB-12, these surface-related functional enhancements are particularly important because effective colonization and interaction with the intestinal mucosa are closely linked to its probiotic functionality. Unlike conventional encapsulation systems that primarily focus on improving survival, vesicle-inspired systems also influence post-delivery behavior, including adhesion and colonization efficiency. These functionalities suggest potential advantages for targeted intestinal delivery.

From a mechanistic perspective, vesicle-inspired carriers can provide a biomimetic microenvironment that facilitates interactions between BB-12 and host tissues, while also offering physical protection against gastrointestinal stress. This dual functionality distinguishes them from traditional barrier-based systems (e.g., alginate gels), which mainly rely on passive protection mechanisms.

Although this technique offers several advantages, its complexity and the need for specialized materials significantly increase production costs and currently restrict its application to high-value or niche products, thereby limiting scalability and cost-effectiveness for large-scale production. In addition, the use of biologically derived carriers such as exosomes may introduce challenges related to standardization, reproducibility, and regulatory approval.

Overall, vesicle-inspired encapsulation systems represent an advanced, functionality-oriented strategy for BB-12 delivery, where the focus extends beyond survival enhancement to include targeted delivery and improved host interaction. However, their practical application is currently constrained by high cost and technical complexity, making them more suitable for specialized or high-value applications rather than large-scale food systems.

Electrospinning-based encapsulation

Electrospinning-based encapsulation methods have also attracted increasing attention for BB-12 delivery. Mojaveri, Hosseini, and Gharsallaoui (2020) utilized chitosan/polyvinyl alcohol nanofiber mats loaded with BB-12 and inulin; thereby significantly enhancing the viability of the probiotic under simulated gastrointestinal conditions and heat treatment. For BB-12; this nanofiber-based architecture provides a unique balance between physical protection and mass transfer; allowing effective shielding against gastrointestinal stress while maintaining sufficient permeability for nutrient exchange and metabolic activity. Compared with conventional microencapsulation techniques; electrospinning enables the formation of highly porous structures with large surface areas; which contribute to improved protective performance. This structural feature may be particularly advantageous for BB-12 in applications where both survival and post-delivery activity are required. For BB-12; this structural advantage is associated with enhanced resistance to gastrointestinal stress and higher post-digestion viability; however; scalability and cost-effectiveness remain major challenges due to the need for specialized equipment (Chung et al., 2025). From a design perspective, electrospinning represents a high-precision but currently low-scalability strategy, offering tunable microstructures that can be tailored to specific delivery requirements, but requiring further technological development for industrial translation.

Overall, the encapsulation strategies developed for BB-12 reflect different trade-offs between protection efficiency, processing complexity, scalability, and cost. Importantly, these strategies can be categorized into distinct functional groups based on their dominant protection mechanisms, including dehydration-based stabilization (spray-drying), covalent network reinforcement (Maillard systems), diffusion-barrier protection (alginate gels), biomimetic delivery (vesicle-inspired systems), and nanostructure-enabled protection (electrospinning). Spray-drying remains an efficient and cost-effective option for large-scale applications, although the high temperatures involved may compromise probiotic viability. Compared with conventional encapsulation techniques such as spray-drying, more complex methods—Maillard reaction-modified conjugates, extrusion combined with calcium alginate gel, and innovative systems such as milk exosomes—provide superior stability and gastrointestinal resilience but generally involve higher production costs and greater technological complexity. Compared with conventional microencapsulation techniques, electrospinning is particularly promising for protecting probiotics under gastrointestinal conditions, but scalability remains a concern due to specialized equipment and the potential for high production costs.

Within this framework, carrier design (e.g., matrix density, multilayer architecture) governs diffusion resistance and cellular stabilization, ultimately determining probiotic survival under processing and gastrointestinal stresses. Looking ahead, the future of probiotic encapsulation will likely focus on optimizing current methods to achieve a better balance between efficacy, cost, and scalability. Efforts will be directed towards developing more efficient and scalable processes that can maintain probiotic viability while remaining economically feasible. Enhanced formulations and novel encapsulation systems may further improve the ability to protect probiotics during storage and transit through the gastrointestinal tract. Moreover, with growing consumer demand for functional foods, it will be essential to refine these technologies to meet the needs of the food industry, ensuring that probiotics remain viable and beneficial ingredients in the market.

Co-encapsulation of multiple strains containing BB-12

The co-encapsulation of multiple probiotic strains has attracted increasing attention as a strategy to improve probiotic survival, functionality, and synergistic effects within food systems (Agriopoulou, Tarapoulouzi, Varzakas, & Jafari, 2023). Single-strain encapsulation often offers limited benefits, as probiotics differ in their stress tolerance, metabolic profiles, and functional properties. Compared with single-strain encapsulation, multi-strain systems introduce additional functional dimensions by combining strains with complementary physiological traits and metabolic activities. Importantly, in these systems, BB-12 serves as a model strain to evaluate how spatial organization and inter-strain interactions influence survival outcomes, thereby providing insights that extend beyond general co-encapsulation effects.

For BB-12, co-encapsulation is not only a strategy for improving survival, but also a means of compensating for its functional and physiological limitations. Although BB-12 exhibits relatively stable metabolic activity and moderate gastrointestinal tolerance, its performance may be constrained in complex environments due to its sensitivity to oxygen and limited competitiveness under nutrient-restricted conditions. Therefore, combining BB-12 with complementary strains can provide functional support, such as enhanced stress tolerance, metabolic cooperation, and ecological adaptability. Accordingly, combining BB-12 with complementary species such as L. plantarum, L. acidophilus, S. thermophilus, and L. rhamnosus GG can broaden the metabolic activities, enhance resilience against gastrointestinal stress, and promote more diverse health outcomes. In this context, BB-12 can be considered a functionally stable but environmentally sensitive strain that benefits from cooperative interactions within multi-strain systems. Importantly, in these systems, BB-12 serves as a model strain to evaluate how spatial organization and inter-strain interactions influence survival outcomes, thereby providing insights that extend beyond general co-encapsulation effects.

Recent studies have demonstrated notable progress in this field. Probiotic-enriched chocolates containing mixtures of BB-12, L. plantarum, La5, L. sanfranciscensis, and S. thermophilus significantly increased short-chain fatty acid production during in vitro colonic fermentation, with higher cocoa content favoring fatty acid formation and lower cocoa favoring vitamin B12 synthesis (Hossain, Ranadheera, Fang, & Ajlouni, 2022). These results suggest that BB-12 may contribute to metabolic outputs more effectively when supported by complementary strains, highlighting the role of co-encapsulation in enabling functional cooperation rather than merely enhancing survival.

Beyond confectionery applications, BB-12 has been co-encapsulated with L. plantarum using coaxial electrospraying and freeze-drying, with electrospraying yielding superior gastrointestinal stability and long-term storage survival compared with freeze-drying (Farahmand et al., 2024). As illustrated in Fig. 1, the coaxial configuration enables spatial separation of probiotic strains within a core–shell structure, which reduces direct exposure to environmental stresses and explains the improved stability observed in co-encapsulated BB-12 systems. Such spatial organization is particularly important for BB-12, as it allows protection from oxygen exposure while maintaining proximity to metabolically active companion strains.

Likewise, as shown in Fig. 2, a double-layered millifluidic vehicle achieved an encapsulation efficiency exceeding 98% and enhanced protection under gastrointestinal conditions, with viable counts sustained above the probiotic threshold after five months of frozen storage (Farahmand et al., 2022). Fig. 2 highlights the role of multilayer compartmentalization in limiting acid and bile diffusion, providing a mechanistic explanation for the enhanced gastrointestinal stability of BB-12 reported in such systems. These compartmentalized designs also enable functional differentiation, where different strains can occupy distinct microenvironments optimized for their survival and activity.

In meat systems, the incorporation of BB-12 with L. plantarum and L. acidophilus preserved sensory quality and reduced lipid oxidation, while ensuring probiotic viability after cooking and digestion (de Marins et al., 2022). This indicates that co-encapsulation not only protects BB-12 biologically but can also may indirectly enhance food quality through metabolic interactions that suppress oxidative deterioration. Such interactions further demonstrate that the role of BB-12 in co-encapsulated systems extends beyond survival to participation in broader functional networks.

Moreover, targeted nutritional strategies, such as “glucose parcels”, have been proposed to promote proliferation of BB-12 and L. rhamnosus GG in colon environments where survival is typically limited (Wang et al., 2023). As shown in Fig. 3, the “food parcel” concept emphasizes targeted nutrient delivery to the colon, illustrating a design principle that complements encapsulation strategies by indirectly supporting BB-12 survival and functionality in complex food systems. Notably, BB-12 benefited from such targeted nutrient delivery by maintaining viable populations under otherwise restrictive colonic conditions. This highlights that co-encapsulation strategies can be integrated with nutrient-delivery approaches to address strain-specific ecological limitations.

Schematic of the proposed “food parcels” (glucose parcels) for colon microbes, aimed at delivering nutrients directly to the human colon to promote the growth of endogenous probiotics and gut bacteria (Wang et al., 2023). The parcel structure protects encapsulated nutrients during upper gastrointestinal transit and enables their release in the colon, thereby supporting probiotic survival and metabolic activity in nutrient-limited colonic environments.

The main advantages of co-encapsulation include the enhanced tolerance of probiotics to processing, storage, and gastrointestinal stresses, as well as synergistic metabolic functions that may translate into broader health benefits. When BB-12 is included in multi-strain systems, co-encapsulation has been shown to improve its survival, functional stability, and persistence through synergistic interactions with complementary strains. However, these benefits are highly dependent on the compatibility and functional roles of the co-encapsulated strains. Limitations still exist, as strains within the same carrier may compete for nutrients or space, and encapsulation conditions optimized for one species may not equally protect others (Gu et al., 2022). For BB-12, such competition may reduce its relative abundance or functional contribution if system design does not account for strain-specific requirements.

Advanced technologies such as electrospraying and millifluidics offer precise control of capsule characteristics, but their industrial scalability and cost-efficiency remain challenges. Therefore, the design of co-encapsulation systems should shift from simple co-loading of strains to rational structuring based on functional differentiation and spatial organization. Future work may benefit from rational design of co-encapsulation systems may help address these limitations. For example, positioning different bacterial strains in distinct layers or compartments within the same matrix may minimize competition and ensure balanced viability. Smart carriers with pH- or enzyme-responsive properties could be engineered to allow sequential release, thereby creating ecological niches tailored for each strain, while incorporating prebiotic “parcels” such as glucose or dietary fibers could selectively stimulate probiotic growth in the colon.

To validate these strategies, long-term in vivo studies will be essential to confirm the synergistic effects of multi-strain encapsulation on gut microbiota modulation and host health. In summary, the co-encapsulation of BB-12 with complementary probiotics represents a promising approach for next-generation functional foods, particularly when guided by mechanism-driven design principles that consider strain-specific physiology, functional roles, and inter-strain interactions, but further research is needed to refine delivery structures, ensure strain compatibility, and enable large-scale, cost-effective production.

Yogurt

The application of BB-12 in yogurt has attracted interest due to its potential health benefits, particularly in modulating gut microbiota balance. Compared with other food matrices, yogurt provides a relatively mild and protective environment for probiotics, as dairy matrices generally exhibit higher buffering capacity, moderate acidity, and the presence of milk proteins and fat globules. These factors can partially mitigate acid-induced damage and stabilize bacterial membranes, thereby reducing the encapsulation burden required for BB-12 survival.

Studies have shown that encapsulated BB-12 in yogurt formulations not only retains high viability over extended storage periods but also improves the texture and sensory attributes of the final product (Zhang et al., 2025). For example; in lactose-free yogurt; encapsulated BB-12 has demonstrated consistent viability (>7 log CFU/g) for up to 120 days at refrigeration temperatures; with physicochemical properties like pH and acidity remaining stable (Dantas, Verruck, Canella, Hernandez, & Prudencio, 2021).

Encapsulation also plays a role in enhancing the bioavailability and functional efficacy of probiotics in the gut (Shishir et al., 2024). When BB-12 was encapsulated in goat milk and inulin; a significant reduction in E. coli counts was observed in the colon; particularly in the formulation containing inulin. This effect was attributed to the production of organic acids by the Bifidobacteria; which not only contributed to pathogen suppression but also revealed the presence of viable but non-culturable E. coli cells; suggesting a potential health benefit of encapsulated probiotics in fermented dairy products (Verruck et al., 2020).

However, despite these promising outcomes, challenges remain in the encapsulation of BB-12 for yogurt applications. One of the primary concerns is ensuring the long-term viability of encapsulated probiotics during storage, particularly under refrigerated conditions. Furthermore, the efficiency of different encapsulation methods—such as emulsification and extrusion—varies, with emulsification often yielding higher encapsulation efficiency at the expense of increased process complexity and cost (Frakolaki, Kekes, Lympaki, Giannou, & Tzia, 2022).

From a design perspective, yogurt systems generally require moderate-protection encapsulation strategies that prioritize stability during cold storage and controlled release in the gastrointestinal tract, rather than extreme barrier resistance. Overall, yogurt provides a favorable matrix for delivering BB-12; however, long-term viability and controlled intestinal release remain important optimization targets. In this context, relatively simple carrier systems (e.g., alginate gels or protein-based matrices) are often sufficient, while highly complex encapsulation strategies may offer limited additional benefit in such buffered environments.

Future research on BB-12 encapsulation in yogurt should therefore focus on addressing these challenges through the development of more advanced encapsulation technologies. In addition, long-term in vivo studies are essential to validate the health benefits of encapsulated BB-12 in yogurt and to ensure probiotic viability under realistic gastrointestinal conditions.

Cheese

The encapsulation of BB-12 in cheese presents considerable potential for enhancing the probiotic content and nutritional value of dairy products. Compared with liquid dairy systems, cheese provides a more structured matrix with higher fat and protein content as well as reduced oxygen diffusion, which can create a protective microenvironment that enhances probiotic stability during storage and gastrointestinal transit. In contrast to yogurt, the lower water activity and denser protein–fat network in cheese reduce molecular mobility and limit oxygen exposure, thereby partially alleviating environmental stresses on BB-12.

Under these conditions, the role of encapsulation shifts from primary protection to stability enhancement and functional integration within the food matrix. Co-encapsulation technologies, such as incorporating BB-12 with antioxidants like pomegranate peel extract and sensitive ingredients like fish oil, have shown considerable improvements in the encapsulation efficiency of probiotics while also protecting them from oxidation (Al-Moghazy, El-Sayed, & Abo-Elwafa, 2022). The inclusion of pomegranate peel extract in the microcapsules acts as a potent antioxidant; preserving the integrity of fish oil and enhancing the viability of probiotics throughout storage (Al-Moghazy et al., 2022).

In addition to antioxidant protection, another key advantage of encapsulating BB-12 in dairy products like cheese is the ability to enhance probiotic stability during processing and storage. Sodium caseinate-based edible films containing BB-12, along with prebiotics such as inulin and fructooligosaccharide, have been used for coating processed cheese (Ceylan & Atasoy, 2022). These prebiotics help to preserve BB-12 stability during the drying process; which is essential for maintaining the desired probiotic levels in the final product (Ceylan & Atasoy, 2022). Additionally; the coating films help maintain the texture; color; and overall quality of the cheese (Ceylan & Atasoy, 2022); which is important for consumer acceptance. However; despite these advantages; a decrease in probiotic viability was observed over time; with counts falling below the therapeutic threshold after 30 days of storage (Ceylan & Atasoy, 2022), indicating that further research is needed to improve the long-term survival of BB-12 in cheese. This suggests that, although the cheese matrix provides intrinsic protection, it is insufficient to fully prevent gradual viability loss, highlighting the need for optimized carrier–matrix interactions.

The main challenge in incorporating encapsulated BB-12 into cheese products lies in ensuring the long-term viability of BB-12. Factors such as temperature fluctuations, pH levels, and storage conditions can still negatively impact the viability of the encapsulated probiotics (Liu et al., 2025). From a design perspective, encapsulation strategies for cheese systems should prioritize compatibility with the dense matrix structure, controlled moisture retention, and protection against oxidative degradation, rather than solely focusing on barrier strength.

While encapsulating BB-12 in cheese offers significant potential to improve probiotic stability and enhance the nutritional profile of dairy products, further research is needed to address these challenges related to long-term probiotic viability and shelf-life. In this context, relatively simple carrier systems (e.g., protein-based coatings or moderate-density hydrogels) are often sufficient, whereas highly complex encapsulation architectures may not provide proportional benefits in such structurally protective environments.

Butter

The incorporation of BB-12 into butter has shown promise for developing value-added functional food products. Encapsulation techniques, particularly using whey protein hydrolysate-maltodextrin, have been successfully employed to enhance the stability and viability of BB-12 during butter storage. Studies have demonstrated that encapsulated probiotics in butter maintain higher levels of viability compared to unencapsulated cells, with the encapsulated variant exhibiting a protective effect that prolongs probiotic survival during refrigerated storage (Gaba, Anand, & Syamala, 2023; Kaushik, Gaba, Anand, & Djira, 2024). Compared with yogurt and cheese, butter represents a low-moisture, high-fat matrix with limited aqueous phase and reduced oxygen diffusion, which inherently restricts microbial metabolic activity and environmental stress exposure. In such systems, the physiological state of BB-12 is largely stabilized due to reduced water activity, thereby lowering the overall stress burden during storage.

One of the main advantages of encapsulating probiotics in butter is the improved stability of probiotics such as BB-12, which can be preserved for up to 63 days at refrigeration temperatures, whereas free cells drop sharply in viability within a shorter period (Kaushik et al., 2024). This extended viability makes encapsulated probiotics a viable option for long shelf-life functional dairy products. The ability to incorporate encapsulated probiotics at concentrations as low as 1% without significantly affecting the sensory properties of the butter; such as taste or texture; is another key advantage (Gaba et al., 2023). However, in butter systems, the primary role of encapsulation is not to provide strong environmental shielding, but rather to ensure uniform dispersion of probiotic cells and to prevent localized stress or phase separation within the lipid matrix. This distinguishes butter from more stress-intensive matrices such as beverages, where encapsulation is essential for survival.

However, challenges remain in fully optimizing the encapsulation process to balance probiotic viability with the desired sensory and physicochemical properties of butter. For instance, slight changes in acid values and hardness were observed during storage, though these did not significantly affect product quality (Gaba et al., 2023). From a design perspective, encapsulation systems for butter should prioritize compatibility with lipid phases, minimal impact on texture, and stable dispersion, rather than high-density barrier formation.

Overall, butter represents a matrix in which encapsulation serves a supportive rather than essential role for BB-12 stability, and relatively simple carrier systems are generally sufficient to achieve the desired level of probiotic protection and product performance.

Ice cream

The application of BB-12 probiotics in ice cream has gained significant attention as a means to enhance the functional properties of this popular dessert. Unlike other dairy matrices, ice cream presents a unique low-temperature and freeze–thaw environment, where probiotic viability is primarily affected by ice crystal formation, osmotic stress, and mechanical damage during freezing and storage. Studies have demonstrated that microencapsulation, when combined with natural ingredients like Stevia, can significantly enhance the viability of BB-12 during storage at −20 °C for up to 90 days (Banuree et al., 2022). This method offers a considerable advantage by reducing the loss of probiotics and maintaining sensory and physicochemical stability in the product, which is crucial for consumer acceptance.

For BB-12, freezing-induced damage is particularly critical, as ice crystal formation can disrupt cell membranes and reduce metabolic activity, leading to irreversible viability loss. Therefore, encapsulation in ice cream systems must address not only environmental exposure but also mechanical and osmotic stresses associated with freezing.

A major benefit of incorporating BB-12 into ice cream is the ability to deliver functional probiotics in a palatable form, contributing to gut health without compromising taste. Additionally, the development of functional ice creams using sheep milk enriched with whey proteins, omega-3 fatty acids, and prebiotics like inulin has been successful in maintaining both the nutritional and probiotic integrity of the product during storage (Lai et al., 2024).

Despite these advancements, several challenges remain in optimizing probiotic delivery in ice cream. The freezing process can negatively impact probiotic viability (Wang et al., 2025); making it essential to refine encapsulation methods to enhance stability and ensure adequate probiotic doses upon consumption. Moreover; balancing the texture; taste; and mouthfeel of ice cream with the presence of functional ingredients; including probiotics and prebiotics; is an ongoing challenge for product developers (Gaba & Anand, 2023; Sarkar, 2024).

From a design perspective, encapsulation systems for ice cream should prioritize resistance to freeze–thaw stress, stabilization of cell membranes, and reduction of ice crystal-induced damage, rather than solely focusing on acid or oxygen barriers. Cryoprotective materials (e.g., sugars, proteins, and polyols) and flexible hydrogel or glass-forming matrices are particularly relevant for preserving BB-12 viability under frozen conditions.

Overall, ice cream represents a matrix where encapsulation plays a critical protective role against low-temperature stress, and effective carrier design must specifically address freezing-related damage mechanisms to ensure long-term probiotic stability.

Beverage

The increasing attention in non-dairy probiotic beverages stems from their ability to offer health benefits without common allergens such as lactose and milk proteins (Aregbe, Boasiako, Xiong, Rahman, & Ma, 2024; Vajdovich, Csajbókné Csobod, & Benedek, 2025). However, the incorporation of probiotics, such as BB-12, into non-dairy beverages presents challenges due to their low pH and the need for probiotics to survive both food processing and the acidic conditions in the gastrointestinal tract.

Compared with dairy matrices, many non-dairy beverages such as fruit juices and plant-based drinks typically exhibit lower pH values and weaker buffering capacity. These characteristics expose probiotics to stronger acid stress, which can accelerate proton diffusion across the cell membrane and disrupt cellular metabolism, ultimately reducing probiotic viability. For BB-12, this challenge is particularly significant because, although it exhibits moderate tolerance to acidic and bile conditions, prolonged exposure to low pH without buffering can still lead to rapid viability loss. Therefore, unlike dairy systems where the matrix itself provides partial protection, encapsulation in beverage systems becomes a primary requirement rather than a supplementary strategy. Consequently, encapsulation systems with stronger barrier properties are often required to maintain probiotic stability in these acidic beverage environments.

Accordingly, barrier-resistant encapsulation systems have been widely explored to improve probiotic stability and viability in non-dairy beverage systems. For instance, the use of sodium alginate and xanthan gum for encapsulating BB-12 in soy-based beverages has been shown to maintain high BB-12 viability (8 log CFU/g) over 30 days under various pH conditions (2.0–5.0) (de Barros Alexandre et al., 2025). Furthermore; the co-encapsulation of BB-12 with bioactive ingredients has also been explored to enhance the benefits of non-dairy probiotic beverages. Madybekova et al. (2024) developed a microencapsulation system that co-loaded BB-12 and Stevia leaf extract; achieving high encapsulation efficiencies for both components. These microcapsules exhibited controlled release; which allowed for better probiotic survival and a more stable product (Madybekova et al., 2024). Similarly; the encapsulation of probiotics in hydrogel matrices has shown promise in improving their survival in beverages with low pH. Yousefi, Khanniri, Khorshidian, Sohrabvandi, and Mortazavian (2023) demonstrated that encapsulation of both L. plantarum and BB-12 in xanthan-chitosan hydrogels improved probiotic survival during storage in apple juice. This approach also resulted in higher sensory scores compared with juices containing free probiotics; showing that encapsulated probiotics can maintain overall beverage quality and consumer acceptability without negatively impacting their taste; texture; or appearance (Yousefi et al., 2023).

From a design perspective, encapsulation systems for beverage applications must prioritize strong acid resistance, low permeability, and sustained protection during prolonged storage. Multilayer structures, composite hydrogels, and dense polymer networks are particularly suitable, as they can effectively limit proton diffusion and protect BB-12 from continuous acid exposure.

While the encapsulation of BB-12 has proven beneficial for enhancing probiotic survival and stability in non-dairy beverages, challenges remain in optimizing encapsulation methods for different beverage types. For example, in soy drinks fermented with sea buckthorn syrup, while BB-12 showed enhanced antioxidant capacity and viability, the survival of other probiotics, such as L. paracasei, was lower during digestion (Maftei et al., 2023). It further highlights that co-encapsulation performance is highly strain-dependent, and carrier systems must be tailored to accommodate differences in strain tolerance and interaction.

The continued development of non-dairy probiotic beverages with encapsulated BB-12 is promising for creating functional drinks that can deliver digestive health benefits while offering a wide range of flavors and bioactive compounds (Anumudu et al., 2024). Overall, beverage systems represent the most demanding application scenario for BB-12 encapsulation, where effective carrier design is essential to ensure both survival and functional delivery.

Jam

The growing interest in functional foods has led to a surge in the development of probiotic-enriched products, especially those that cater to consumers avoiding dairy. However, the challenge lies in ensuring the stability and viability of probiotics, such as BB-12, in these matrices throughout processing, storage, and digestion. Compared with other food systems, jam represents a highly challenging matrix characterized by low pH and high sugar content, which imposes combined acid and osmotic stresses on probiotic cells. The high sugar concentration reduces water activity and creates osmotic pressure, while the acidic environment accelerates proton diffusion, together leading to membrane damage and metabolic inhibition.

Encapsulation technologies have emerged as a key strategy to protect probiotics in jams, and several advancements have been made in this area. For instance, using an alginate-jaboticaba peel blend for encapsulating BB-12 has been shown to improve encapsulation efficiency (>90%) and enhance probiotic survival during simulated gastrointestinal digestion (Cedran, Rodrigues, & Bicas, 2021). When incorporated into jaboticaba jam; the encapsulated probiotics exhibited a slower viability loss (0.5 log CFU/g/day); compared with the more rapid decline observed in free probiotics (1.0 log CFU/g/day); indicating a better protective effect offered by the encapsulation (Cedran et al., 2021). For BB-12, such osmotic and acid stresses are particularly detrimental, as they can disrupt membrane integrity and reduce metabolic activity during storage. Therefore, encapsulation systems in jam applications must provide both diffusion resistance to acidic conditions and structural protection against osmotic stress.

Despite these promising results, challenges remain in scaling up the encapsulation process, ensuring consistency in large-scale production, and maintaining the sensory qualities of the final product. From a design perspective, encapsulation strategies for jam systems should emphasize combined acid–osmotic resistance, stable matrix integration, and minimal impact on texture and flavor. Composite hydrogels and polysaccharide-based systems with strong water-binding capacity are particularly suitable for mitigating osmotic stress and maintaining BB-12 viability.

Overall, jam represents a dual-stress environment for BB-12, where effective encapsulation must simultaneously address acid and osmotic challenges, making it more demanding than many dairy-based systems but less extreme than continuously acidic beverage environments.

Fermented meat products

The incorporation of microencapsulated BB-12 into fermented meat products has gained attention for its potential to enhance both product quality and health benefits (Munekata et al., 2022). Microencapsulation technology effectively protects BB-12 during the fermentation process, ensuring its viability even under challenging conditions such as low pH and high salt content. Compared with plant-based or dairy systems, fermented meat products present a complex stress environment characterized by combined acidification, high ionic strength, and oxidative reactions during fermentation and storage. High salt concentrations can induce osmotic stress and disrupt cellular homeostasis, while lipid oxidation products may further impair membrane integrity.

Studies have demonstrated that microencapsulated BB-12 maintains its viability in various fermented meat products, including salami and coppa, while also stabilizing lipid and protein oxidation, which are key factors in improving shelf life and overall product quality (Camargo et al., 2021; de Oliveira Gomes, de Mesquita Oliveira, de Marins, Gomes, & Feihrmann, 2021). For BB-12, encapsulation in such systems not only provides physical protection against environmental stress but may also indirectly contribute to product stability by modulating oxidative processes and microbial interactions.

Despite these advantages, several challenges remain in incorporating BB-12 into fermented meat products. The encapsulation process, while effective, can be costly and complex, with scalability issues that need to be addressed for large-scale commercial production. Furthermore, ensuring the survival of BB-12 in the gastrointestinal tract, where BB-12 must endure low pH and digestive enzymes, requires further optimization of encapsulation materials and methods. From a design perspective, encapsulation systems for fermented meat applications should prioritize resistance to salt-induced osmotic stress, oxidative stability, and compatibility with fermentation-driven biochemical changes, while maintaining minimal impact on texture and flavor development. Future progress in synbiotic fermented meats will depend on optimizing encapsulation techniques to improve both the efficiency of BB-12 protection and the cost-effectiveness of production.

Overall, food application studies indicate that the effectiveness of BB-12 encapsulation is strongly matrix-dependent, reflecting distinct physicochemical stress environments across food systems rather than uniform performance trends. Rather than representing a uniform performance trend, encapsulation efficiency varies systematically with the dominant stress factors of each food matrix, including acid stress (beverages), combined acid–osmotic stress (jam), low-temperature stress (ice cream), and salt–oxidative stress (fermented meat products). Dairy-based systems such as yogurt and cheese generally provide a more supportive environment for BB-12 survival due to buffering capacity and favorable water activity, whereas non-dairy matrices, including beverages and fruit-based products, require more robust encapsulation strategies to counteract low pH and oxidative stress. These observations suggest that effective probiotic delivery is governed by a “matrix–mechanism–carrier matching” principle, in which encapsulation materials and structures must be selected according to the dominant physicochemical stresses of the target food system. These comparisons highlight that the successful application of encapsulated BB-12 relies not only on encapsulation technique selection but also on rational matching between carrier systems and food matrices. Importantly, by using BB-12 as a model strain, this review identifies generalizable relationships between carrier architecture (e.g., multilayer structures, hydrogel networks), protection mechanisms (e.g., diffusion limitation, vitrification, spatial confinement), and matrix-specific stress conditions. From a design perspective, this matrix-specific response underscores the value of using BB-12 as a model strain to derive transferable formulation principles for other probiotics with similar physiological traits. Future work should therefore focus on matrix-specific optimization to balance probiotic viability, sensory quality, and industrial feasibility.

Conclusion

This review provides a strain-centered analysis of probiotic encapsulation by focusing on BB-12, thereby minimizing confounding effects arising from strain-specific variability. Rather than presenting a conventional summary of encapsulation technologies, this review aims to reinterpret existing strategies through a mechanism-oriented and structure–function perspective. As one of the most extensively studied probiotics, BB-12 offers a representative model for systematically evaluating encapsulation strategies, ranging from single-strain protection to more complex co-encapsulation architectures. Collectively, the literature demonstrates that diverse encapsulation methods can significantly enhance the stability of BB-12; however, no single approach simultaneously achieves optimal biological protection, cost efficiency, and industrial scalability. While conventional techniques such as spray drying remain attractive for large-scale food applications due to their economic feasibility, more advanced systems—including multilayer gels, protein–polysaccharide conjugates, electrospun fibers, and vesicle-inspired carriers—provide superior protection at the expense of increased processing complexity and cost. Co-encapsulation with complementary strains further improves probiotic performance but introduces additional challenges related to strain compatibility and formulation design. Importantly, the analysis presented in this review indicates that the performance of encapsulation systems is governed not only by material selection but also by the interplay between carrier structure, protection mechanism, and environmental stress conditions. By integrating carrier-level advantages, key challenges, and underlying protection mechanisms (Table 2), this review highlights the importance of mechanism-driven encapsulation design for balancing efficacy, cost, and manufacturability. Furthermore, by synthesizing evidence across encapsulation systems and food matrices, this review proposes a generalized “matrix–mechanism–carrier matching” framework, which provides a more systematic basis for selecting encapsulation strategies under different application scenarios.

Future research should prioritize the optimization and hybridization of existing technologies, the incorporation of prebiotics or other bioactive compounds, and the development of scalable manufacturing processes that preserve probiotic functionality. In addition to technical optimization, future research should more explicitly address regulatory, industrial, and consumer-related challenges. From a regulatory perspective, the safety, standardization, and approval pathways of novel encapsulation materials and complex carrier systems require systematic evaluation, particularly for emerging composite carriers such as protein–polysaccharide conjugates or multilayer biopolymer systems, where regulatory classification and safety assessment frameworks are still evolving in different jurisdictions. At the industrial level, process scalability, batch-to-batch consistency, and cost-effectiveness remain critical barriers to commercialization, especially when translating laboratory-scale encapsulation techniques (e.g., electrospinning or multilayer assembly) into continuous food manufacturing processes while maintaining probiotic viability. Moreover, consumer acceptance—including sensory quality, transparency of ingredient labeling, and perceived health benefits—will play a decisive role in the successful translation of encapsulated probiotics into market-ready functional foods, highlighting the need for more studies that simultaneously evaluate probiotic stability, sensory attributes, and consumer perception in real food matrices.

Overall, by using BB-12 as a model system, this review not only summarizes existing encapsulation strategies but also establishes a mechanism-based conceptual framework that links probiotic physiology, carrier architecture, and food matrix conditions. These insights may contribute to a more rational and transferable design of encapsulation systems for other probiotic strains, thereby enhancing both the scientific understanding and practical implementation of probiotic delivery technologies.
