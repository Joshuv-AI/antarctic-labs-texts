Repeated tDCS of the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex for acute DoC: a pilot randomized controlled trial
Authors: Hui Ma, Kehong Zhao, Chenggong Hu, Cheng Huang, Xianrui Hu, Ziqi Zhang, Xiying Chen, Chengsen Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to investigate the safety and feasibility of transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) in patients with acute disorders of consciousness (DoC), and to preliminarily explore its potential intervention effects. In this sham-controlled randomized trial, participants with acute DoC within 7–28 days post-onset were randomly assigned (1:1) to either the active or sham stimulation group. The active-stimulation group received 2-mA direct current stimulation at the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) for 20 min per session, twice daily, over 10 consecutive working days. Primary outcomes were safety and feasibility. Exploratory efficacy outcomes included the Coma Recovery Scale-Revised (CRS-R), Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), and Full Outline of UnResponsiveness (FOUR) scale, assessed at baseline, after 5 days, and upon completion of the intervention. Between May 2022 and May 2023, 22 eligible patients were enrolled and randomly assigned to the active (n = 11) or sham (n = 11) group. All participants completed the full protocol without dropouts. No severe adverse events were reported; transient skin redness occurred in six patients (54.55%) in the active-stimulation group. After 10 days, the active-stimulation group showed significantly higher scores on exploratory neurobehavioral measures (CRS-R: 15.91 ± 6.56 vs. 7.64 ± 5.22, t = 3.27, p < 0.01; FOUR: 13.73 ± 2.97 vs. 9.36 ± 4.65, t = 2.62, p = 0.01). Compared with sham stimulation, the tDCS protocol demonstrated favorable safety and feasibility in patients with acute DoC, with preliminary signals of neurobehavioral improvement. Introduction

Consciousness is a state in which individuals can be aroused and interact with the environment, composed of two main components: arousal and awareness. Disruption of one or both of these components can result in disorders of consciousness (DoC)1. According to the American Academy of Neurology (AAN) practice guideline on DoC, patients with DoC lasting less than 28 days are defined as being at an acute DoC stage2. Coma is a transient state, rarely persisting more than three weeks; it can evolve into emergence from coma, prolonged DoC or death3. At a conservative estimate, about 5 per 100,000 patients transition to prolonged DoC from acute DoC, and the incidence and prevalence of DoC are progressively increasing, driven by advances in neurocritical care4,5. Due to disturbances of consciousness and prolonged bed rest, patients with DoC cannot participate in active physical therapy, resulting in severe medical complications, including respiratory system disorders, skeletal muscle system disorders, endocrine and metabolic abnormalities, urinary tract infections, autonomic nerve dysfunction, deep vein thrombosis and other conditions, which impede recovery and may lead to deterioration or death6,7. DoC and their complications have become a heavy burden for families and society.

Substantial work has been done on the mechanisms, diagnosis, and emerging therapies for DoC8. DoC is caused by structural brain injury or generalized brain dysfunction. Major etiological categories include global anoxia-ischemia, cerebrovascular disease, traumatic brain injury, seizures, infection, noninfectious inflammation, hydrocephalus, neoplastic disorders, metabolic and endocrine disorders, hypothermia, and various toxins9. Although the neural mechanisms of DoC remain unclear, the frontal cortex, central thalamus, brain stem, striatum and globus pallidus interna are believed to play important roles in consciousness processing, according to the fronto-parietal mesocircuit model8. Therefore, these areas are also targets for DoC interventions10.

Accurate assessment and diagnosis of DoC can influence clinical decision-making, such as initiating palliative care or active treatment11,12. Neuroimaging and electrophysiological procedures, including EMG, EEG, fMRI, and PET, are evolving as potential assessment methods to monitor the brain function of DoC patients from different perspectives; however, there is insufficient evidentiary support to include them in formal diagnostic criteria or routine clinical care13,14. Neurological and behavioral assessments remain the primary approaches to determining DoC progression. Scales commonly used clinically include: the Coma Recovery Scale-Revised (CRS-R), which has high sensitivity and is considered the gold standard for DoC assessment15; the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), which is widely and reliably used by neurosurgeons in more than 80 countries16; and the Full Outline of UnResponsive (FOUR), which serves as a predictive tool of mortality or functional outcome17.

Promoting consciousness recovery in patients with DoC is challenging, and therapeutic options are also limited. The AAN-published practice guideline recommends both pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions for DoC, though limited evidence supports their therapeutic effects2. For pharmacological intervention, amantadine is the only drug with Class II evidence for patients with traumatic brain injury in prolonged DoC (4–16 weeks post-injury)18. Non-pharmacological interventions include neuromodulation techniques, such as invasive and non-invasive brain stimulation (NIBS). Deep brain stimulation (DBS) of thalamic intralaminar nuclei carries potential side effects, but studies suggest its therapeutic effects are inconsistent19–21. A case study reported a patient with unresponsive wakefulness syndrome (UWS) for 15 years benefited from vagal nerve stimulation, though results require cautious interpretation22. NIBS primarily involves repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) and transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS). Most available studies suggested no significant behavioral improvements in DoC patients receiving rTMS, regardless of the stimulation target (primary motor cortex [M1] or dorsolateral prefrontal cortex [DLPFC])23–25. As a simple, inexpensive, and non-invasive technique, tDCS has been widely used in psychiatric disorders and post-stroke rehabilitation26,27. Our systematic review of tDCS for DoC concluded that anodal left DLPFC stimulation with more than 20 sessions significantly improves CRS-R scores in patients with minimally conscious state (MCS)28. However, existing tDCS research predominantly focuses on prolonged DoC (> 28 days), with limited evidence available for acute-stage applications. Meanwhile, Wu et al.’s recent trial demonstrated that right median nerve stimulation (RMNS) significantly facilitated a greater and more rapid emergence from coma in patients with acute post-traumatic coma at 6 months29. This finding inspired us to investigate the use of tDCS in patients with acute DoC. Thus, this study aimed to primarily investigate the safety and feasibility of tDCS in patients with acute DoC, and to preliminarily explore its potential intervention effects.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was designed as a single-center, parallel-group, sham-controlled, double-blind randomized controlled trial. The study design adhered to CONSORT 2010 guidelines30. Eligible participants were randomly assigned to the active-stimulation group or sham-stimulation group at a 1:1 ratio using a computer-generated random number sequence. Assignment codes were securely stored in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. These envelopes were opened exclusively by study therapists after participant enrollment to prevent selection bias. As a double-blind study, both participants and outcome assessors were blinded to group allocation. Blinding of the intervention providers was not feasible due to the technical requirements of device operation; however, they were excluded from all follow-up evaluations to prevent assessment bias. This trial was prospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (identifier: ChiCTR2200060209) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Biomedical Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (May 6, 2022; Approval No. 2022-284). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients’ legal guardians.

All participants were recruited from the Neurological Intensive Care Unit (NICU) of West China Hospital, Sichuan University. Patients who met the following criteria were deemed eligible for the trial: (1) aged 18–70 years; (2) severe brain injury (traumatic or non-traumatic) incurred 7–28 days prior to enrollment; (3) a GCS total score of 3–8 with a GCS motor subscale score of less than 5 at enrollment; (4) no administration of neuromuscular blockers or sedatives within 24 h prior to study initiation. Those who met the following criteria were excluded from the trial: (1) unstable vital signs; (2) history of epilepsy pre-enrollment or during hospitalization; (3) contraindications to tDCS (implanted cardiac pacemaker, metallic implants in the stimulation area, pregnancy); (4) failure to provide informed consent.

Treatment protocol

After hospital admission, all patients were assessed and received standard treatment according to the guidelines, targeting intracranial hypertension and a variety of supportive treatments (surgical intervention when indicated). All sedative medications were administered in strict accordance with clinical guidelines.

The tDCS intervention was administered using a battery-driven direct current stimulator (VC-8000 C, Wogao Medical Co., Ltd, Nanjing, China). Direct current was delivered via saline-soaked sponge electrodes (7 cm × 5 cm), with the anode positioned over the left DLPFC (position F3 of the 10–20 international EEG system) and the cathode placed over the right supraorbital region (Fp2). Active stimulation commenced at 2 mA immediately after initiation, though reduced to 1 mA for patients with craniectomies at the stimulation site31. Sham stimulation employed identical electrode placement with automated current termination after 30 s, while maintaining 30-second ramp-up/ramp-down periods to mimic active stimulation sensations. Both groups received 20-minute sessions twice daily over 10 consecutive workdays.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes were safety and feasibility of the tDCS protocol in patients with acute DoC. Safety was assessed by monitoring the incidence, type, and severity of adverse events throughout the intervention period, including but not limited to skin erythema, electrical burns, excessive sweating, and seizures. Feasibility was evaluated based on recruitment rate, protocol adherence, and study completion rate.

Exploratory efficacy outcomes included changes in neurobehavioral measures: the CRS-R, GCS, and FOUR scale. All scales are validated and widely used tools for assessing consciousness in critically ill patients.

All patients were assessed by trained and blinded certified examiners in accordance with standardized protocols. Safety monitoring was performed after each stimulation session and throughout the hospitalization period. The CRS-R and FOUR scales were administered at baseline, after 5 days of stimulation, and upon completion of the 10-day intervention. The GCS was assessed at baseline and after each stimulation session. To ensure that all consciousness evaluations were performed in an optimal and stable arousal state, our protocol employed stringent operational criteria: all sedative medications were discontinued, and formal CRS-R assessment was initiated only after a minimum 24-hour washout period, followed by an additional 24-hour observation period during which the patient’s CRS-R total score remained stable to confirm the resolution of sedative effects.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics 25.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), with visualizations created in Prism 7.0 (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA). Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies (%), while continuous variables are reported as mean ± SD (normally distributed) or median (IQR) (non-normal distribution). Normality of the data was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk normality test. Student’s t-test and one-way ANOVA were used to compare means of normally distributed continuous variables between two and three independent groups, respectively. For non-normally distributed continuous variables, the Mann-Whitney U test was used for between-group comparisons. The proportions of categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. For outcome measures (CRS-R, GCS, and FOUR), if the data were normally distributed, a two-way mixed-model ANOVA was conducted with Group as the between-subjects factor and Time as the within-subjects factor. Mauchly’s test of sphericity was performed to assess the assumption of sphericity; if the assumption of sphericity was violated, Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied. If a significant interaction effect was identified, simple effect analyses were conducted by fixing time points and performing post hoc tests between groups with Bonferroni adjustment.

The sample size for this pilot randomized controlled trial was based on feasibility considerations, specifically the maximum number of eligible participants that could be ethically recruited within the 1-year study period approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee. No formal a priori power calculation was performed. Upon study completion, a post-hoc power analysis for the exploratory efficacy outcome (CRS-R) was planned using G*Power 3.1 software. In accordance with Cohen’s conventions, a power level ≥ 80% under the current sample size constraints was to be considered as a benchmark; this analysis was intended solely to estimate the observed effect size and to inform sample size calculations for future confirmatory trials.

All tests were two-sided with α = 0.05 defining statistical significance. To account for multiple comparisons arising from testing group differences at three time points (T0, T1, T2), a Bonferroni correction was applied. The significance level (α) was adjusted to 0.0167 (0.05/3) for each comparison to maintain the overall family-wise error rate at α ≤ 0.05.

Results

Patients’ characteristics

Between May 2022 and May 2023, a total of 51 patients were screened for eligibility (Fig. 1). Twenty-nine were excluded, yielding 22 enrolled participants who were randomly assigned to the intention-to-treat population (active-stimulation group: n = 11; sham-stimulation group: n = 11). All participants completed follow-up and were included in the final analysis. The baseline demographics and clinical characteristics of enrolled patients were shown in Table 1 (see Supplementary Material for details). Time from injury to stimulation initiation was similar between the two groups (14.45 ± 7.22 vs. 14.91 ± 6.14, p = 0.875). The two groups were well balanced at baseline, and no differences were observed in any baseline parameters.

Feasibility

Of the 51 patients screened, 22 (43.1%) met the eligibility criteria and were enrolled. All 22 enrolled participants (100%) were successfully randomized and completed the full 10-day stimulation protocol without any dropouts or protocol deviations. The recruitment rate averaged approximately 1.8 patients per month over the 12-month study period.

Adverse events

Throughout the entire intervention, all participants tolerated tDCS well, and no severe tDCS-related adverse events (e.g., electrical burns, excessive sweating, and seizures) were reported. Transient skin erythema was observed at the stimulation site in 6 patients (54.55%) of the active-stimulation group after stimulation, which resolved spontaneously within a few minutes without residual scarring.

Discussion

The aim of this randomized double-blind sham-controlled trial was to investigate the safety and feasibility of tDCS in patients with acute DoC, and to preliminarily explore its potential intervention effects. To our knowledge, this is the first clinical trial investigating the use of tDCS in patients with acute DoC. Altogether, the findings of this pilot study suggest that anodal tDCS over the left DLPFC—administered for 20 min per session, twice daily, over 10 consecutive days—may be associated with improvements in conscious levels and functional outcomes (as measured by CRS-R, FOUR, and GCS) in patients with acute DoC. However, given the substantial limitations in trial design and implementation, these findings warrant caution in drawing conclusions and implementing the intervention into clinical practice.

The present study confirms that the applied tDCS protocol is both safe and feasible in patients with acute DoC. Regarding safety, no severe adverse events were observed; transient skin erythema occurred in six patients (54.55%) in the active‑stimulation group and resolved spontaneously within minutes. This safety profile is consistent with that established in chronic DoC populations. The intervention was well‑tolerated, did not disrupt routine NICU bedside care, and appeared safer than some alternative neuromodulation approaches that are logistically challenging or carry recognized risks in the acute setting. Nevertheless, given the potential for rapid neurological deterioration during the acute stage, rigorous monitoring remains essential when implementing tDCS for consciousness promotion. In terms of feasibility, the recruitment rate of 1.8 patients per month and the 100% protocol completion rate (22/22 participants, no dropouts or deviations) demonstrate that twice‑daily tDCS sessions are practical and well‑accepted in the acute NICU environment. Collectively, these findings support the viability of conducting larger, adequately powered trials using this protocol in the acute phase of DoC.

As an exploratory analysis of potential efficacy signals, outcomes were evaluated at multiple time points following stimulation. For CRS-R, FOUR, and GCS scores, significant improvements were observed after 10 days of stimulation, whereas no significant changes were detected after 5 days of stimulation. While this temporal pattern might appear consistent with a potential dose-dependent trend, our findings cannot definitively confirm a dose-response effect given the study’s pilot design and limited sample size. Notably, Feng et al. reported no significant correlation between stimulation dose and effect sizes in their meta-regression analysis of a systematic review32. These authors posited that behavioral changes induced by short-term tDCS may be too subtle to be reliably captured by the CRS-R. Thibaut et al. found that a single session of tDCS over the left DLPFC could transiently improve signs of consciousness as measured by the CRS-R33. In contrast, Martens et al. did not observe any behavioral treatment effects of tDCS in patients with DoC34. In our previously published meta-analysis, we conducted a subgroup analysis stratified by the total number of tDCS sessions and found that only more than 20 sessions could significantly improve CRS-R and GCS scores28, a finding that aligns with the temporal improvement pattern seen in the present study. However, it is critical to note that previous studies enrolled chronic-stage DoC patients, whereas our cohort comprised acute-phase patients, introducing a key methodological difference. The relationship between tDCS dose and therapeutic effect size remains incompletely understood, and further research is warranted to determine the optimal stimulation parameters, particularly in the acute phase of DoC.

In this trial, all participants had sustained various severe brain injuries (traumatic or non-traumatic) within 28 days prior to recruitment and were clinically diagnosed with DoC. Active tDCS initiated in the acute DoC stage appeared to yield greater improvements in consciousness recovery compared to the sham stimulation group, the latter of which primarily exhibited spontaneous recovery. These results offer preliminary support for the potential of early intervention. Several published studies35–38 have shown that tDCS can promote coma emergence in patients with DoC, especially those in MCS. However, the subjects in these studies were in the prolonged phase of DoC (> 28 days post-injury), which limits comparability with our study. One study by Wu et al.29 on acute post-traumatic coma demonstrated that continuous application of RMNS for 2 weeks resulted in greater and more rapid coma emergence at 6 months, compared with the control group. Although the therapeutic mechanisms of RMNS and tDCS differ, these findings indicate that the acute phase of DoC is also a critical treatment window. Due to limited evidence, it remains unclear which stage (acute or chronic) is more favorable for initiating intervention. Theoretically, rapid emergence from coma would enable patients to actively engage in physical therapy, thereby avoiding complications associated with prolonged bed rest that impede recovery. Thus, exploring earlier and more proactive awakening interventions is warranted, provided that such interventions do not result in more adverse events than interventions in the chronic phase. The favorable safety profile observed in this study supports the feasibility of such early intervention approaches.

The stimulation protocol used in this trial was based on previous published studies and expert consensus39. Although promising signals of therapeutic benefit were observed in this trial, the optimal stimulation parameters—including stimulation targets, current intensity, and duration—remain to be determined. Based on current understanding of human consciousness-related pathways, wakefulness pathways originating in the brainstem activate the awareness network, including its thalamocortical projections, conceptualized as the ascending reticular activating system. Awareness is mediated by the cerebral cortex, and the DLPFC is considered a key brain region to regulate higher cognitive functions. It has also been shown that stimulating the DLPFC can reduce thalamic inhibition, thereby facilitating wakefulness. Thus, the DLPFC is frequently selected as a stimulation target to promote consciousness recovery. However, for promoting motor recovery and behavioral improvements in patients with DoC, some researchers have chosen the primary motor cortex as the stimulation target, which has also been shown to promote consciousness recovery as measured by the CRS-R40. The current intensity employed in all published trials ranges from 1 to 2 mA, which is considered a safe intensity for tDCS; however, no studies have explored the effects of higher intensities or dose-response relationships. Stimulation parameters for tDCS in DoC vary widely, and further studies are warranted to address this gap.

This study has several limitations. First, although a post-hoc power analysis yielded an observed power exceeding 80%, the small cohort of 22 eligible participants inherently limits the strength of evidence and increases the risk of bias. This sample size limitation stemmed from the 1-year ethical approval timeframe and the scarcity of acute-phase patients meeting both clinical stability and tDCS eligibility criteria. Consequently, this pilot trial primarily assessed the feasibility and safety of tDCS and preliminarily explored its efficacy in acute DoC. Second, the present study included patients with heterogeneous etiologies (traumatic and non-traumatic brain injury), and we did not perform adjusted or stratified analyses by etiology, lesion location, or extent. This introduced unavoidable clinical heterogeneity, which represents a common methodological challenge in preliminary neuromodulation studies of DoC41–43, largely due to the small sample size, restricted 1-year ethical approval timeframe, and difficulties recruiting sufficiently large homogeneous subgroups within acute-phase DoC cohorts. Future investigations with expanded sample sizes are encouraged to implement etiology-stratified designs and lesion quantification analyses to address this limitation. Third, the absence of long-term follow-up precludes evaluation of sustained tDCS effects or prognostic impacts. In the next phase of the study, the Glasgow Outcome Scale Extended will be employed to assess participants’ consciousness levels at 1, 3, and 6 months post-intervention, aiming to further investigate the sustainability of tDCS effects. Fourth, reliance solely on neurobehavioral scales limits mechanistic insights into cerebral functional changes; incorporation of multimodal biomarkers (e.g., fMRI/EEG) remains essential. While this study establishes tDCS as a potentially safe intervention for acute DoC, subsequent phase II trials will expand recruitment, employ stratified designs, extend follow-up durations, and integrate objective neuroimaging biomarkers to strengthen causal inference and clinical generalizability.

Conclusion

We conservatively conclude that the adopted tDCS protocol exhibits a favorable safety and feasibility profile. Compared with the sham-stimulation group, active tDCS was associated with higher scores on relevant neurobehavioral scales (i.e., CRS-R, FOUR, and GCS) without any severe adverse events. These preliminary findings provide a rationale for and support the conduct of larger, well-powered trials to confirm the therapeutic efficacy of tDCS in this population.
