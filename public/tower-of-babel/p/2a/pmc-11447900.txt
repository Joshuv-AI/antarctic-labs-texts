Blocking CXCR4–CARM1–YAP axis overcomes osteosarcoma doxorubicin resistance by suppressing aerobic glycolysis
Authors: Zihua Li, Hengli Lu, Yiwei Zhang, Jiyang Lv, Yi Zhang, Tianyang Xu, Dong Yang, Zhengwei Duan, Yonghao Guan, Zongrui Jiang, Kaiyuan Liu, Yuxin Liao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteosarcoma, recognized for its aggressiveness and resistance to chemotherapy, notably doxorubicin, poses significant treatment challenges. This comprehensive study investigated the CXCR4–CARM1–YAP signaling axis and its pivotal function in controlling aerobic glycolysis, which plays a crucial role in doxorubicin resistance. Detailed analysis of Dox‐resistant 143b/MG63‐DoxR cells has uncovered the overexpression of CXCR4. Utilizing a combination of molecular biology techniques including gene silencing, aerobic glycolysis assays such as Seahorse experiments, RNA sequencing, and immunofluorescence staining. The study provides insight into the mechanistic pathways involved. Results demonstrated that disrupting CXCR4 expression sensitizes cells to doxorubicin‐induced apoptosis and alters glycolytic activity. Further RNA sequencing revealed that CARM1 modulated this effect through its influence on glycolysis, with immunofluorescence of clinical samples confirming the overexpression of CXCR4 and CARM1 in drug‐resistant tumors. Chromatin immunoprecipitation studies further highlighted the role of CARM1, showing it to be regulated by methylation at the H3R17 site, which in turn affected YAP expression. Crucially, in vivo experiments illustrated that CARM1 overexpression could counteract the tumor growth suppression that resulted from CXCR4 inhibition. These insights revealed the intricate mechanisms at play in osteosarcoma resistance to doxorubicin and pointed toward potential new therapeutic strategies that could target this metabolic and signaling network to overcome drug resistance and improve patient outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

The most typical primary malignant bone tumor affecting children and young adults is osteosarcoma (OS). 1 Currently, neoadjuvant chemotherapy, predominantly employing Dox, cisplatin, and methotrexate, is combined with surgical resection as the primary form of treatment for OS. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 The survival rate for OS patients has plateaued over the past three decades despite advances in chemotherapy, partly because of the emergence of treatment resistance. 9 , 10 Since the introduction of chemotherapy in the 1970s, the 5‐year survival rate has increased from 20% to 70%; nevertheless, over 40% of patients with OS continue to be resistant to chemotherapy, resulting in a low 5‐year survival rate of just 16%–20%. 11 , 12 While earlier studies have shed light on the intricate nature of chemoresistance in tumors, involving dysregulated genes, proteins, miRNAs, and signaling pathways, our current understanding of the specific cellular mechanisms regulating the emergence and advancement of chemoresistance in OS remains inadequate. 13 , 14 This knowledge gap is particularly crucial as chemoresistance frequently results in treatment failure and a poor prognosis, making it difficult to increase OS therapy's efficacy. Therefore, it is imperative to elucidate the underlying molecular pathways involved in OS chemoresistance in order to pave the way for the development of innovative therapeutic strategies.

The Warburg effect, a metabolic change characterized by aerobic glycolysis that predominates in drug‐resistant cancer cells and adversely affects treatment results, has also been linked to dysregulation of YAP signaling. 15 , 16 The Warburg effect and chemotherapy resistance have been linked extensively in studies, which indicates that cancer cells that are resistant to chemotherapy exhibit an intense reliance on aerobic glycolysis. 17 The Warburg effect and chemotherapy resistance are both influenced by dysregulation of glycolytic enzymes, transporters, and signaling pathways. Additionally, the Warburg effect, which is modulated by elements including hypoxia and nutritional restriction, further strengthens chemotherapy resistance through the tumor microenvironment and cellular heterogeneity. For the development of efficient therapeutic approaches to address drug resistance, it is critical to acquire an in‐depth understanding of the association between the Warburg effect and chemotherapy resistance.

The SDF‐1–CXCR4 axis plays a crucial role in various malignancies, including OS, exerting diverse effects on cell proliferation, migration, and apoptosis through downstream signaling pathways. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 Furthermore, across a range of tumor types, CXCR4‐mediated chemotherapy resistance gains increasing attention. 23 , 24 , 25 In our previous research, we demonstrated that altering CXCR4 had an effect on autophagy, which in turn influenced Dox resistance in OS. Osteosarcoma cells became more sensitive to Dox treatment when CXCR4 was inhibited, and the CXCR4 antagonist AMD3100 further enhanced the effectiveness of Dox treatment in OS. These findings highlighted the therapeutic potential of targeting the SDF1–CXCR4 axis to treat chemotherapy resistance in OS. 26

A prevalent post‐translational modification process in eukaryotes is histone arginine methylation, which is catalyzed by the protein arginine methyltransferase (PRMT) family. 27 , 28 It is pivotal for many physiological functions, including transcriptional control, signal transmission, RNA processing, and DNA repair. It entails the transfer of methyl groups from S‐adenosylmethionine to arginine residues on histone proteins. With an unambiguous preference for methylation at the H3R17 site among the PRMT family members, protein arginine methyltransferase 1 (CARM1 or PRMT4) particularly induces methylation at the H3R2, H3R17, and H3R26 sites on histones to assist activating gene transcription. 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 Methylation of specific sites on core histones plays a critical role in transcriptional control, heterochromatin formation, and the regulation of pluripotency and embryonic development. Notably, CARM1 has been demonstrated to play an essential part in the control of aerobic glycolysis enzyme transcription levels. 36

Through each of its constituent parts, the Hippo pathway—a conserved signaling mechanism that controls carcinogenesis and organ size—is directly connected to the emergence of heart failure. 37 , 38 The onset of carcinogenesis in mice has been associated with increased YAP activation, which is commonly seen in tumor cells. 39 A key transcription cofactor in the Hippo pathway known as YAP demonstrates persistent activity that promotes malignant traits such as increased cell proliferation, resistance to chemotherapy, disturbed cell polarity, and metastasis. YAP interacts with TEAD1 in OS, which highlights its potential as a prognostic marker and therapeutic target by encouraging cell survival and proliferation. 16 , 40 , 41 Our research group proposed the hypothesis that the CXCR4–CARM1–YAP axis regulates Dox resistance in OS based on the results of previous research. This theory proposed that CXCR4 facilitates the histone H3R17 methylation mediated by CARM1 and enhances the transcriptional activity of YAP, activating aerobic glycolysis, and ultimately results in Dox resistance in OS. In this paper, we will elucidate the novel regulatory mechanism of the CXCR4–CARM1–YAP axis in OS, offering new therapeutic strategies for the clinical reversal of Dox resistance in OS.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient samples

In total, 44 patients with primary OS who underwent radical resection at Shanghai Tenth People's Hospital and Shanghai First People's Hospital from 2010 to 2019 were included in our study (Table S1). The OS samples and paired nontumor tissues were applied to examine CXCR4 and CARM1 protein expression by immunofluorescence. The studies involving human participants were reviewed and approved by the Shanghai Tongji Hospital: 2021‐KYSB‐061. Written informed consent was obtained from the individual(s) for the publication of any potentially identifiable images or data included in this article.

Cell culture and transfection

MG63 and 143B cells were obtained from the ATCC in Shanghai, while the MG63‐DoxR and 143B‐DoxR cells were generously provided by Dr. Eugenie Kleinerman from the M.D. Anderson Cancer Center in Houston, TX, USA. These cell lines were cultured in high‐glucose DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), which was supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 μg/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. The cultures were incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 in air. A density of 3 × 105 cells was seeded into a six‐well plate and incubated for 12 h to allow attachment. Subsequently, the cells were transfected with the relevant reagents using Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. The specific short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting CXCR4 and CARM1, as well as the relative negative control (sh‐NC), the pcDNA3.1‐CXCR4 and CARM1, and empty vectors, were all obtained from Shanghai Asia‐Vector Biotechnology (Shanghai, China).

Immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry

Tissue sections were prepared for both immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence to assess CXCR4 and CARM1 expression levels. Initially, sections were dewaxed, hydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval via microwave treatment. For immunohistochemistry, sections were blocked and incubated with primary antibodies against CXCR4 and CARM1 (diluted 1:100 in 3% BSA; Cell Signaling Technology) at room temperature for 1 h, followed by the application of biotinylated donkey anti‐rabbit IgG (diluted 1:150 in 3% BSA; ThermoScientific) and HRP‐conjugated streptavidin (diluted 1:200 in 3% BSA; BioLegend, USA). The reaction was visualized using DAB (Dako, Denmark), and counterstaining was performed with hematoxylin. Staining intensity was evaluated on a scale from 0 (negative) to 2 (brown intensity). For immunofluorescence, after processing with a Leica TP1020 tissue processor (Leica Biosystems, Germany), sections were treated similarly for antigen retrieval, blocked, and then incubated overnight at 4°C with the same primary antibodies. This was followed by the application of the corresponding secondary antibodies. The expression of CXCR4 and CARM1 was quantified relative to the total nuclei per section, highlighting their roles in modulating Dox resistance through effects on aerobic glycolysis in OS cells.

RNA sequence and bioinformatics analysis

Transcriptome sequencing and bioinformatics analysis. Transcriptome sequencing and quality control, with or without CXCR4 knockout MG63‐DoxR cells, were conducted by Biomarker Technologies Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Differential gene expression (DEG) analysis, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), and visualization of results were performed using R software (version 4.2.1) to identify significantly altered DEGs and pathways between MG63‐DoxR cells with or without CXCR4 knockout. Osteosarcoma (GSE21257) and pan‐cancer RNA sequencing data were extracted from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) databases, respectively. In particular, TCGA cohort was divided into TCGA Train and TCGA Test cohorts based on a 7:3 ratio. Patients were classified into high and low CARM1 groups based on the median CARM1 expression in the three cohorts. Kaplan–Meier curves and COX regression analysis were performed with the survival R package (version 3.4‐0). Nomograms, calibration curves, and decision curves were plotted using the rms (version 6.7‐0) and ggDCA (version 1.2) R packages. The ssGSEA of CARM1 and tumor malignancy pathways were analyzed using the GSVA R package (version 1.44.5). The association of CARM1 with immune cell infiltration and immune‐related functional gene expression in 33 tumors was analyzed using the TIMER database. 42 Drug sensitivity analysis between high and low CARM1 groups was performed using the CellMiner database. 43

Western blotting

After cell lysis with RIPA buffer containing a complete protease and phosphatase inhibitor, total proteins were separated using SDS‐PAGE gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA). The membranes were then blocked in 5% skimmed milk for 1 h. Following the blocking step, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with the indicated primary antibodies, including anti‐CXCR4 (1:100, #ab124824; abcam), anti‐CARM1 (1:1000, #M301315; abmart), anti‐PKM2 (1:1000, # ab137852; abcam), anti‐HK2 (1:1000, # 2867; CST), anti‐LDHA (1:1000, # 3582; CST), anti‐PARP, and anti‐MDR1. anti‐H3R17me (1:1000, # ab6002; abcam), anti‐YAP (1:1000, # ab52771; abcam), anti‐actin (1:20000, #BS6007M; Bioworld), and anti‐GAPDH (1:5000, #5174; CST), Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, the proteins were visualized using the electrochemiluminescence (ECL) method with a chemiluminescence reagent (Millipore, USA).

Quantitative real‐time polymerase chain reaction (qRT‐PCR)

Quantitative real‐time PCR (qRT‐PCR) was performed to measure the expression levels of CXCR4, CARM1, and PKM mRNA. This analysis was carried out in triplicate using specific primers in three independent experiments. Glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as the internal reference gene. qRT‐PCR was conducted on the ABI 7500 real‐time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA). The relative expression levels of the mRNAs were determined using the 2–ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences are provided in the Table S2.

The chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

ChIP assay was performed to investigate the association between CARM1 overexpression and the enrichment of YAP and H3R17me in MG‐63‐DoxR cells. Briefly, the cells were cross‐linked with formaldehyde to stabilize protein–DNA interactions. The chromatin was then sonicated to obtain DNA fragments of the desired size. Immunoprecipitation was performed using antibodies specific to anti‐H3R17me (1:1000, # ab6002; abcam), anti‐ YAP (1:1000, # ab52771; abcam), anti‐mouse IgG(HRP) (1:5000, # 7076; CST), or anti‐rabbit IgG (HRP) (1:5000, # 7074; CST). The immunoprecipitated DNA–protein complexes were purified and subjected to PCR amplification using primers designed for the target regions. qPCR analysis of input DNA samples confirmed the integrity of the chromatin and the successful amplification of the desired fragments. ChIP assay results were evaluated based on the fold enrichment of YAP and H3R17me in the specific fragments compared with the input control, providing insights into the association between CARM1 overexpression and the regulation of YAP and H3R17me in MG‐63‐DoxR cells.

Cell viability

Cell viability was evaluated using the ATPlite Luminescence Assay System (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer's guidelines. The Synergy HT Multi‐Detection Microplate Reader was employed to quantify the relative luminescence units (RLUs). The untreated cell RLUs served as the baseline for 100% viability, and the outcomes were presented as a percentage of viable cells compared with the untreated controls. To determine the IC50 (half‐maximal inhibitory concentration), 2 × 103 cells, including MG63, MG63‐DoxR, MG63‐DoxRCARM1‐OE, and MG63‐DoxRsh CARM1, were incubated with increasing concentrations of Dox (ranging from 10 nM to 0.5 mM) for 72 h. The IC50 value was determined as the concentration of the drug that reduced cell viability to 50% using the CalcuSyn software (BioSoft, Cambridge, UK), which facilitates IC50 calculations. The fold change in Dox sensitivity was calculated by comparing the IC50 values of the Dox‐resistant variants.

Cell apoptosis analysis

Cells, including MG63, MG63‐DoxR, MG63‐DoxRCARM1‐OE, and MG63‐DoxRsh CARM1, were seeded in six‐well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were harvested and subjected to flow cytometry analysis to assess cell apoptosis. After the appropriate treatment, the cells were centrifuged at 400 g for 5 min and washed three times with 1× PBS. Following washing, the cells were incubated with 5 μL of FITC‐conjugated Annexin V and 5 μL of propidium iodide (PI) for 10 min at 37°C in the dark.

Flow cytometry analysis was performed using the Beckman Coulter (FC500) system to evaluate the stained cells and measure apoptosis levels. The cells were stained using the mentioned apoptosis detection kit (YEASEN, 40302ES20) and were subsequently analyzed using flow cytometry to measure the fluorescence intensity, which corresponded to the level of cellular apoptosis. The percentage of apoptotic cells was determined based on the population exhibiting increased fluorescence compared with the control group. Statistical analysis was then conducted to assess differences in apoptotic response among the experimental groups, providing insights into the impact of CXCR4 and CARM1 modulation on cell apoptosis in MG63‐DoxR cells.

Seahorse XF glycolysis stress assay

For the Seahorse XF glycolysis stress assay, the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) was measured using the Seahorse XF Glycolysis Stress Test Kit from Agilent (#103015–100; Agilent Technologies) and the XF24 Extracellular Flux Analyzer, following a previously described protocol. In brief, cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well in Seahorse XF24 V7 PS Cell Culture Microplates and cultured overnight. ECAR was measured in XF base medium supplemented with 2 mM glutamine (pH 7.4) following the sequential injection of glucose (10 mM), oligomycin (1 μM), and 2‐DG (50 mM). Assessment of in vivo tumor growth

BALB/c nude mice were sourced from the Hubei Laboratory Animal Centre of Tongji University, Shanghai, China, and were maintained under specific pathogen‐free conditions with controlled temperature (25°C) and photoperiods (12 h:12 h, light:dark cycles). For the subcutaneous tumor model, mice aged 6–8 weeks were divided into four groups (MG63‐DoxR, MG63‐DoxRCARM1‐OE, MG63‐DoxR + AMD3100, MG63‐DoxRCARM1‐OE + AMD3100) of six mice each. Next, 1 × 106 MG63‐DoxR and MG63‐DoxRCARM1‐OE cells were injected subcutaneously into the subaxillary region of the mice to form tumors with a size of 60 mm3. AMD3100 started only when the tumor sizes were 0.125 cm3. The mice in the study were administered AMD3100 at a concentration of 5 mg/kg every other day, totaling 14 doses via intraperitoneal injections. Throughout the treatment period, the weights of the mice and the surface areas of their tumors were measured and recorded at regular intervals. The tumor surface area was calculated using the formula W × L, where W represents the width and L represents the length of the tumor. After the completion of the treatment regimen, the animals were euthanized by asphyxiation with CO2. The tumors were then removed surgically, and their weights were recorded. Subsequently, the excised tumors were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in preparation for histological analysis.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as means ± standard error of the mean (SEM) unless stated otherwise. Statistical analyses were performed using the two‐tailed Student's t‐test in GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Inc., CA), except for the analysis of the association of CARM1 with pan‐cancer patient survival, which was conducted using R software (version 4.2.1). A p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Regulation of doxorubicin resistance in OS through CXCR4‐mediated aerobic glycolysis modulation

Our study underscores CXCR4's crucial role in Dox resistance modulation in OS cells through aerobic glycolysis regulation, aligned with prior findings of CXCR4 upregulation in Dox‐resistant MG63‐DoxR versus MG63 cells. 26 Silencing CXCR4 significantly enhanced LM8 cells' Dox sensitivity, triggering increased autophagic cell death via the PI3K‐Akt–mTOR pathway. We further explored CXCR4's effects on aerobic glycolysis‐associated protein mRNA levels in MG63 and MG63‐DoxR cells, post‐transfection with CXCR4 overexpressing plasmid or CXCR4 siRNA. Notable modulation of HK2, PFKFB3, PGK1, LDHA, and PKM2 mRNA levels was observed (Figure 1A–F). In the CXCR4 overexpression group (MG63 CXCR4 OE), the levels of CXCR4, CARM1, HK2, PKM2, and LDHA proteins were significantly elevated, while in the CXCR4 siRNA treatment group (MG63‐DoxR CXCR4 siRNA), their expression was markedly reduced (Figure 1G, Figure S1). These results were replicated consistently in 143B and 143B‐DoxR cells (Figure S2). The Seahorse assay measured extracellular acidification (ECAR) in MG63‐DoxR across control, siCXCR4, Dox, and Dox + siCXCR4 groups, with the Dox + siCXCR4 group showing the lowest ECAR levels. This indicated CXCR4 suppression's significant effect on mitochondrial function and energy metabolism in MG63‐DoxR cells; there was a marked and statistically significant ECAR reduction compared with other groups, highlighting the combined Dox and CXCR4 siRNA treatment's impact on cellular glycolysis (Figure 1H,I).

CARM1 as a downstream regulator of CXCR4 in OS doxorubicin resistance

Using RNA sequencing to delve into molecular changes post‐CXCR4 siRNA treatment in MG63‐DoxR cells, we pinpointed 133 differentially expressed RNAs under strict statistical thresholds (p‐value <0.05 and |fold change| ≥1.5): 60 RNAs were upregulated and 73 were downregulated (Table S3). Heatmaps and volcano plots illustrate these gene expression alterations, notably including significant changes in CARM1 expression (Figure 2A,B). KEGG analysis revealed the affected pathways, such as carbon metabolism and central carbon metabolism in cancer, linking CXCR4 knockdown to metabolic shifts (Figure 2C,D). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, performed using RNA data from 53 osteosarcoma patients in the GSE21257 dataset from the GEO database, categorized patients into high and low CARM1 expression groups based on the median CARM1 expression of 7.874, and revealed that high CARM1 expression was associated with a poor prognosis (Figure 2E). GSEA further connected CARM1 levels to crucial biological processes like glycolysis, gluconeogenesis, drug metabolism, and oxidative phosphorylation, indicating CARM1's role in metabolism and drug response modulation (Figure 2F,G).

CARM1's impact on OS prognosis and doxorubicin resistance

The impact of CARM1 on pan‐cancer patient outcomes was assessed using RNA sequencing data from TCGA database, revealing that high CARM1 expression correlated with worse overall survival (OS), disease‐specific survival (DSS), and disease‐free interval (DFI) across both TCGA Train and Test cohorts. Further analyses highlighted CARM1's association with tumor immunity and malignancy and led to the development of a nomogram predicting chemotherapy effectiveness for patients with varying CARM1 expression levels (Figures S3–S9). Additionally, immunofluorescence staining of 10 OS tissue pairs, including normal, osteosarcoma, and Dox‐resistant OS tissues, showcased distinct CXCR4 and CARM1 expression patterns. Normal tissues showed minimal cytoplasmic expression, OS tissues displayed increased levels, and Dox‐resistant OS tissues exhibited significant cytoplasmic upregulation of both markers (Figure 3A). To rule out the interfering effects of chemotherapy, we compared biopsy samples before treatment from chemotherapy‐sensitive and chemotherapy‐resistant patients. As shown in Figure S10, the expression levels of CXCR4 and CARM1 were significantly higher in the chemotherapy‐resistant group compared with the chemotherapy‐sensitive group.

CXCR4–CARM1 axis regulating aerobic glycolysis in OS doxorubicin resistance

Investigating CXCR4's impact on CARM1, immunofluorescence in MG63 and MG63‐DoxR cells highlighted CXCR4 overexpression's boost in CARM1 intensity, contrasted by a reduction via CXCR4 knockdown (Figure 3B). IC50 evaluations for Dox sensitivity in both cell lines explored groups: control (NC), CXCR4 overexpression (CXCR4 OE), CARM1 siRNA (CARM1 siRNA), and CXCR4 OE + CARM1 siRNA for MG63 and 143B; and control (NC), CXCR4 siRNA (CXCR4 siRNA), CARM1 overexpression (CARM1 OE), and CXCR4 siRNA + CARM1 OE for MG63‐DoxR and 143B‐DoxR. Findings indicated that CXCR4 and CARM1 alterations influenced the Dox response, with overexpression promoting resistance and knockdown enhancing sensitivity. Notably, combined CXCR4 overexpression and CARM1 knockdown in MG63 cells, as well as CXCR4 knockdown with CARM1 overexpression in MG63‐DoxR cells, partially reversed resistance (Figure 3C,D). These experiments were also performed in 143B and 143B‐DoxR cells, yielding consistent results (Figure S11). Apoptosis assays post‐Dox showed CXCR4 siRNA increased apoptosis, whereas CARM1 OE decreased it, with combination treatment in MG63‐DoxR adjusting the apoptotic response (Figure 3E,F). Western blot analysis on HK2, PKM2, and LDHA expression after CARM1 knockdown aligned with CXCR4 siRNA's effects, showing decreased enzyme levels, opposite to the CXCR4 overexpression increase. Concurrent CARM1 knockdown and CXCR4 overexpression balanced enzyme levels (Figure 3G,H).

CARM1 promotes YAP transcription via H3R17 methylation to regulate aerobic glycolysis in OS drug resistance

To uncover CARM1's role in drug‐resistant OS metabolism, we analyzed the expression of YAP and H3R17me, key regulators of aerobic glycolysis, in MG63 and MG63‐DoxR cells via western blot, under various conditions: CXCR4 overexpression, CARM1 knockdown, and their simultaneous modulation in MG63; and CXCR4 knockdown, CARM1 overexpression, and their simultaneous modulation in MG63‐DoxR. CXCR4 overexpression in MG63 cells increased YAP and H3R17me levels but decreased phosphorylated YAP (p‐YAP). Knockdown of CARM1 led to a decrease in YAP and H3R17me and an increase in p‐YAP. Combining CXCR4 overexpression with CARM1 knockdown partially restored YAP and H3R17me levels and reduced p‐YAP. In MG63‐DoxR cells, CXCR4 knockdown reduced YAP and H3R17me levels but increased p‐YAP, while CARM1 overexpression had the opposite effect (Figure 4A). Simultaneous CXCR4 knockdown and CARM1 overexpression partially rescued these effects compared with CXCR4 knockdown alone (Figure 4B). ChIP experiments in MG63‐DoxR cells showed a significant enrichment of H3R17me at a specific fragment and higher fold enrichment of the YAP promoter in the CARM1 overexpression group versus control, demonstrating CARM1's influence on YAP and H3R17me regulation (Figure 4C–E). Consistent results were obtained when these experiments were performed in 143B and 143B‐DoxR cells (Figure S12).

In vivo study unveils the influence of CXCR4–CARM1–YAP axis on tumor growth and drug resistance in OS

Finally, a subcutaneous tumor model was performed to assess the effects of CARM1 on OS tumor growth and drug resistance in mice model. Nude mice were divided into four groups including MG63‐DoxRVector, MG63‐DoxRCARM1‐OE, MG63‐DoxRVector + AMD3100 and MG63‐DoxRCARM1‐OE + AMD3100 subcutaneously. Tumor growth curves were constructed based on the measurements of tumor volume and weight over time. Notably, the MG63‐DoxRCARM1‐OE group exhibited a significant promotion of tumor growth compared with the MG63‐DoxRVector group. Conversely, the administration of AMD3100 in the MG63‐DoxRVector + AMD3100 group led to a noticeable suppression of tumor growth, further underlined by the substantial reduction in tumor size in the MG63‐DoxRCARM1‐OE + AMD3100 group. These observations underscored the promotive role of CARM1 in tumor growth and the inhibitory effect of AMD3100 (Figure 5A–C). Building upon the delineated groups in the subcutaneous tumor model, a focused investigation was conducted through western blot analysis on selected tumor tissues. Specifically, the expression levels of key biomolecules, including CXCR4, CARM1, H3R17me, YAP, HK2, PKM2, and LDHA, were assessed. The outcomes of this analysis aligned with our previous findings, reinforcing the intricate regulatory network underlying OS. In line with the promotive role of CARM1 in tumor growth observed in the tumor model, the MG63‐DoxRCARM1‐OE group displayed a notable increase in CARM1 expression compared with the MG63‐DoxRVector group. Furthermore, this heightened CARM1 expression in the MG63‐DoxRCARM1‐OE group corresponded with elevated levels of H3R17me. Conversely, the groups that received AMD3100 treatment, specifically the MG63‐DoxRVector + AMD3100 and MG63‐DoxRCARM1‐OE + AMD3100 groups, exhibited a reduction in both CARM1 expression and H3R17me levels. In terms of the metabolic regulators, the elevated tumor growth seen in the MG63‐DoxRCARM1‐OE group was concomitant with the increased expression of HK2, PKM2, and LDHA, indicative of enhanced aerobic glycolysis. Notably, this glycolytic shift was countered by the administration of AMD3100, as reflected in the lower expression levels of these metabolic enzymes in the AMD3100‐treated groups. Additionally, the expression levels of CXCR4 were found to correlate with the expression patterns of CARM1 and its downstream factors (Figure 5D,E). The congruity between the outcomes of immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis and the aforementioned western blot results significantly strengthened the foundation of our study by corroborating the regulatory dynamics within the OS microenvironment. The IHC analysis served to reinforce and extend the observations derived from the WB analysis, thereby substantiating the intricate interplay between CXCR4, CARM1, and their downstream effectors during the progression of OS. In specific alignment with our earlier WB findings, the MG63‐DoxRCARM1‐OE group, marked by upregulated CARM1 expression, exhibited a pronounced increase in CARM1 staining as evidenced by IHC assessment. This concordance underscores the reliability of our data and reiterates the pivotal role of CARM1 in OS progression. Similarly, the experimental groups treated with AMD3100, namely MG63‐DoxRVector + AMD3100 and MG63‐DoxRCARM1‐OE + AMD3100, displayed a consistent reduction in CARM1 staining. This convergence of results accentuated the reliability of our observations regarding the impact of AMD3100 on CARM1 levels, substantiating our prior conclusions. The investigation of Ki‐67 expression, a pivotal marker of cellular proliferation, yielded particularly noteworthy insights. The MG63‐DoxRCARM1‐OE group, which exhibited enhanced tumor growth driven by heightened CARM1 expression, concurrently demonstrated elevated Ki‐67 staining (Figure 5F). Additionally, the IHC analysis unveiled distinct YAP expression patterns: the CARM1 OE group exhibited the highest YAP expression compared with the control group. Subsequently, upon AMD3100 administration, the AMD3100 group displayed reduced YAP expression. Most notably, the combined CARM1 OE + AMD3100 group showcased a reversal in this trend. Statistical analysis further affirmed the significance of these variations, highlighting the notable influence of CARM1 and AMD3100 on YAP expression levels (Figure 5G).

DISCUSSION

Due to innate or acquired drug resistance, the limited efficacy of cancer therapeutic efforts in recent years has highlighted the need to investigate the molecular pathways and prospective therapeutic targets linked to tumor growth. 44 , 45 Building on our earlier research on how CXCR4 affected OS chemoresistance and its underlying molecular mechanisms, we found that overexpressing CXCR4 in Dunn cells increased chemoresistance by inhibiting autophagy via the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. Conversely, silencing CXCR4 sensitized LM8 cells to doxorubicin by promoting autophagic cell death. 22 , 26 These findings provided valuable insights into the role of CXCR4 in overcoming OS chemoresistance via autophagic cell death. Within this context, the CXCR4–CARM1–YAP axis has emerged as a potential regulatory pathway implicated in drug resistance and aerobic glycolysis. In OS, CXCR4 has been correlated with the development of drug resistance, while CARM1 and YAP have been related to metabolism and carcinogenesis. 37 , 46 , 47 The underlying interplay and functional implications of the CXCR4–CARM1–YAP axis in the development of OS drug resistance and metabolic reprogramming, however, remain largely unresolved. We conducted an in‐depth investigation to fill this information gap in order to clarify the role of this axis in OS and its prospective implications for treatments. Our research illuminated the complex interactions between CXCR4, CARM1, and YAP, emphasizing their importance in OS drug resistance and metabolic control.

Our findings align with previous studies showing increased CXCR4 expression in drug‐resistant OS cells. Notably, we uncovered CXCR4's influence on CARM1 through RNA sequencing, a crucial link not previously established, now identified as integral to the chemoresistance framework. KEGG pathway analysis further highlighted significant changes in carbon‐related metabolic pathways, hinting at a CXCR4–CARM1 connection that could drive metabolic reprogramming. Seahorse assay results reinforced this, revealing altered glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation patterns associated with CXCR4 and CARM1 modulation, illustrating the CXCR4–CARM1 axis's potential in mediating metabolic shifts that may underlie drug resistance.

Notably, CARM1 assumes a significant role across diverse cancer types, encompassing breast, ovarian, hematopoietic, liver, pancreatic, colorectal, prostate, bone, oral, lung, and melanoma. 27 In breast cancer, CARM1 exhibits a dualistic persona, orchestrating tumor initiation and progression, while also expediting migration and metastasis, particularly evident in triple‐negative breast cancer cells. 48 , 49 In the realm of hematopoietic cancers, such as Hodgkin's lymphoma and acute myelogenous leukemia, CARM1 emerges in a state of overexpression, while its depletion disrupts leukemogenesis. Crucially, the potential therapeutic potency of CARM1 inhibition shines through in diverse models, ranging from multiple myeloma to AML and diffuse large B cell lymphoma (DLBCL), underscoring its candidacy as a therapeutic target. 30 , 50 Conversely, in hepatic and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) contexts, CARM1 is dampened, yet it continues to influence glycolysis and glutamine metabolism, hinting at its prospective therapeutic utility for these malignancies. 51 This comprehensive synthesis, intricately woven with our experimental outcomes, serves as a foundational framework to dissect the multifaceted CXCR4–CARM1–YAP axis, a novel terrain for therapeutic exploration, adding depth and clarity to the intricate interplay of these key players in the realm of drug resistance and metabolic recalibration in OS.

Our study bridges experimental insights with clinical implications, showing that high CARM1 expression correlated with poor prognosis in OS and pan‐cancer cohorts, suggesting its use as a prognostic marker. GSEA indicated CARM1's influence on metabolic pathways such as glycolysis, gluconeogenesis, drug metabolism, and oxidative phosphorylation, potentially altered by CXCR4 knockdown. Immunofluorescence staining highlighted a marked increase in both CXCR4 and CARM1 expressions in drug‐resistant OS tissues. This pattern underscores the CXCR4–CARM1 axis's role in mediating drug resistance, reinforcing CARM1's clinical relevance as a biomarker and its role in modulating metabolic pathways affecting drug response. Exploring the CXCR4–CARM1–YAP interaction, our findings conferred added significance to this axis. The interaction between CARM1 and the transcriptional regulator YAP, a known driver of aerobic glycolysis and proliferation, may underpin carcinogenesis. In vitro studies have demonstrated that modulating CXCR4 and CARM1 affects drug sensitivity in OS cells and alters the expression of metabolic enzymes, including HK2, PKM2, and LDHA. Similarly, YAP, which becomes fully active when cells consume large amounts of glucose, enhances glycolysis, promoting the proliferation and migration of cancer cells. 52 Zhang et al. 53 reported that YAP forms a complex with HIF‐1α in the nucleus, binds to the PKM2 gene promoter, and directly activates its transcription. Western blot analyses revealed that CXCR4 knockdown diminished, while CARM1 overexpression increased, the levels of YAP and H3R17me, underscoring the complexity of their regulatory network. These results solidify the therapeutic potential of targeting the CXCR4–CARM1 axis in overcoming OS drug resistance.

Our study further explores CXCR4's role in OS chemoresistance by examining CARM1's influence on tumor progression and Dox resistance using a murine model. Consistent with in vitro findings, increased tumor size in the MG63‐DoxR CARM1‐OE group suggested that CARM1 accelerates OS progression. Conversely, tumor growth was mitigated by AMD3100, corroborating its suppressive effect on OS cells observed in cell culture. The enhanced tumor growth and elevated glycolytic enzyme expression (HK2, PKM2, LDHA) in the CARM1‐OE group signify augmented glycolysis, hinting at metabolic reprogramming that confers a survival edge, possibly contributing to Dox resistance. AMD3100's inhibition of CXCR4 seems to reverse this metabolic adaptation, suggesting its potential to interrupt tumor cell energy pathways and enhance Dox sensitivity. Increased Ki‐67 staining in the CARM1‐OE group links CARM1 to tumor growth and proliferation, while its decrease post‐AMD3100 treatment affirmed AMD3100's tumor‐suppressive properties. The involvement of YAP in governing glycolysis and proliferation, and its potential interaction with CARM1, pointed to the complexity of the CXCR4–CARM1–YAP axis in Dox resistance. Targeting this axis could provide a promising approach to overcoming Dox resistance by disrupting tumor metabolism and proliferation.

Overall, our research offers thorough evidence of the CXCR4–CARM1–YAP axis's role in modulating Dox resistance and aerobic glycolysis in OS (Figure 6). The underlying mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets for treating medication resistance in OS are now well defined as a consequence of these insights.
