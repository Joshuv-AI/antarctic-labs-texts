Mental Health and Sleep Outcomes Among Patients Diagnosed With Chronic Cough
Authors: Andrew Meci, Emily N Milarachi, John P Gniady
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveChronic cough is a complex, multi‐factorial condition. Past studies have associated chronic cough with a degraded quality of life, namely related to psychological and sleep outcomes. We investigated the association between chronic cough and later diagnosis with mental health and sleep disorders using a large database.Study DesignCross‐sectional study.SettingTriNetX research database.MethodsA retrospective cohort analysis was conducted using the TriNetX research database. Patients aged ≥18 years with chronic cough diagnosed from 2008 to 2018 and no recent history of mental health or sleep disorders were included and compared to a matched control cohort. Primary outcomes were 5‐year diagnoses with sleep disorders, depression, and anxiety. Secondary outcomes included rates of interaction with the healthcare system, other mental health diagnoses, and usage of psychological medications and sleep devices.ResultsOf 65,442 included patients with chronic cough, mean age was 54.5 ± 15.3 and patients were 60.8% female, 75.2% white, and 86.5% not Hispanic/Latino. After demographic matching, patients with chronic cough had significantly higher rates of smoking, obesity, asthma, and GERD. Patients were at significantly higher risks of developing sleep disorders (RR 1.58, P < .01), mental health diagnoses (RR 1.33, P < .01), and receiving anti‐reflux, antidepressant, and CPAP treatments (P < .01 for all).ConclusionChronic cough diagnosis is associated with increased risk of later development of anxiety, depression, and disordered sleep. This is the largest study to date examining the burden of chronic cough alongside associated outcomes. Future studies will further investigate these associations and investigate improved patient care models.Keywords: chronic cough, database, mental health, outcomes research, sleep

Chronic cough, defined as adults with 8 or more weeks of cough (4 or more weeks in children), affects approximately 5.0% of the general population. 1 The condition can be notoriously difficult to treat due to its multi‐factorial nature. The complexity of chronic cough stems from its diverse underlying etiologies, which can include gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), upper airway cough syndrome, asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and idiopathic causes, among others. 2 The persistent nature of this condition creates significant challenges for both patients and healthcare providers in achieving effective symptom management and resolution.

Past studies have consistently associated chronic cough with a degraded quality of life, particularly impacting psychological well‐being and sleep outcomes. 3 Patients frequently report feelings of embarrassment, social isolation, and frustration due to the disruptive nature of their symptoms in professional and social settings. 4 , 5 The physical toll of persistent coughing can lead to exhaustion, chest pain, and urinary incontinence, while the unpredictable nature of cough episodes contributes to anxiety and depression. 4 , 6 , 7 Sleep disturbances are especially common, as nocturnal coughing interrupts both the patient's rest and that of their household members, creating a cascade of fatigue‐related complications that further compromise daily functioning and overall health status. 8 , 9 Additionally, patients affected by chronic cough have significantly higher economic burdens and healthcare resource utilization. 10 , 11

Despite growing recognition of these quality‐of‐life impacts, the relationship between chronic cough and the subsequent development of formal mental health and sleep disorder diagnoses remains inadequately characterized in large‐scale epidemiological studies. 12 We investigated the association between chronic cough and later diagnosis with mental health and sleep disorders using a large database, aiming to quantify the risk of developing these comorbid conditions. While we expect to find that chronic cough is associated with increased incidences of sleep disturbance and mental health diagnoses, quantifying and understanding this relationship will aid in developing comprehensive treatment approaches and identifying patients who may benefit from early intervention strategies.

Materials and Methods

Data Source and Study Design

The study data were obtained from the TriNetX Research Network on January 20, 2025. TriNetX is a global federated health research network providing access to electronic medical records (diagnoses, procedures, medications, laboratory values) from large healthcare organizations (HCOs). 13 The TriNetX platform provides each patient randomized identifiers that are not available to the researchers, allowing for chronological assessment. Further, no protected health information or personal data is available to the platform users. Therefore, this study was deemed exempt by the Pennsylvania State University Institutional Review Board (IRB), and the need for informed consent was waived (STUDY18629). This study follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines for cohort studies.

Participants

The database was queried to identify patients ≥18 years of age who were diagnosed with chronic cough between January 01, 2008, and December 31, 2018, and were required to have at least 5 years of follow‐up recorded within the database. Chronic cough was defined as ICD‐10‐CM R05.3: Chronic cough. Patients who possessed prior diagnoses of cough, sleep disorders, anxiety, or depression in the 1 year prior to their chronic cough diagnosis were excluded from our analysis. A cohort of patients without chronic cough was generated and compared to the study cohort.

Propensity score matching according to the demographic characteristics of age, sex, race, and ethnicity as well as clinical characteristics including obesity, asthma, COPD, GERD, and smoking was conducted via a 1:1 nearest neighbor algorithm using logistic regression. A tolerance level of 0.01 and a standard deviation (SD) of 0.1 caliper are set by default by the platform.

Outcome Measures

Cohort demographics and past medical histories were obtained from chronic cough and control cohorts. Demographic measures included age, sex, race, and ethnicity. Clinical history characteristics included smoking, obesity, asthma, COPD, emphysema, chronic bronchitis, and GERD. Histories of neck, thyroid, and laryngeal surgeries were also noted.

Primary outcomes were 5‐year diagnoses of sleep disorders, depression, and anxiety. Coding was defined as: ICD‐10‐CM G47 sleep disorders, G74.0 insomnia, R40.0 somnolence, F32 depressive episode, F33 major depressive disorder, F34 persistent mood disorders, and F41 other anxiety disorders. Secondary outcomes included rates of interactions with the healthcare system (emergency visits, hospitalizations, critical care stays), other mental health diagnoses (F20‐29 schizophrenia, schizotypal, delusional, and other nonmood psychotic disorders), and usage of antidepressants, reflux medications, and continuous positive airway pressure device (CPAP).

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were conducted on the TriNetX platform. 14 Numerical variables were summarized as means and standard deviations, while categorical variables were expressed as n and proportion of the total. Chi‐squared, Z‐tests, and T‐tests were conducted to assess differences between our cohort categorical and numerical data and were reported as relative risk (RR), 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), and P‐values as appropriate. For all statistical tests, significance was defined as P < .05.

Results

Within the database, 65,442 patients with chronic cough met our inclusion criteria. This cohort was compared to 6,932,103 patients without chronic cough. Patients with chronic cough were significantly older (mean age 54.5 ± 15.3 vs 50.9 ± 16.8 years, P < .001). The chronic cough group showed higher representation of White patients (75.2% vs 65.7%, P < .001) and lower representation of Hispanic or Latino ethnicity (4.2% vs 5.9%, P < .001) (Table 1). There were also greater proportions of smoking (8.9% vs 6.5%, P < .001), obesity (13.2% vs 9.7%, P < .001), asthma (13.4% vs 5.8%, P < .001), COPD (4.9% vs 2.0%, P < .001), and GERD (21.7% vs 11.3%, P < .001) among chronic cough patients (Table 2). After matching, most demographic and clinical measures were balanced, with a total of 65,442 patients in each cohort (Tables 1 and 2).

Clinical Features of Included Patients With and Without Chronic Cough

Examining 5‐year outcomes following chronic cough diagnosis, sleep disorders affected 21.8% of chronic cough patients versus 13.8% of controls, representing a 1.58‐fold increased relative risk (95% CI: 1.54‐1.61, P < .001). Mental health disorders were present in 22.0% of chronic cough patients compared to 16.5% of controls (RR 1.33, 95% CI: 1.30‐1.36), with depression (16.1% vs 11.0%, P < .001) and anxiety (19.5% vs 14.7%, P < .001) being particularly prevalent. Healthcare system interactions were significantly less common among chronic cough patients (28.9% vs 31.2%, P < .001) overall, as were emergency visits and hospitalizations (P < .001 for both), but there was no significant difference in the rate of critical care admissions between the groups (P = .18) (Table 3).

Treatment patterns reflected the complex, multisystem nature of chronic cough management. Reflux medications were prescribed to 45.3% of chronic cough patients compared to 34.1% of controls (RR 1.33, 95% CI: 1.31‐1.34, P < .001), while antidepressants were used by 26.2% versus 21.8% of controls (RR 1.20, 95% CI: 1.18‐1.23, P < .001). CPAP therapy had a relative risk of 1.45 (95% CI: 1.31‐1.61), though it was used in just 1.3% of chronic cough patients versus 0.9% of controls (notably, patients with a prior history of sleep disorders were excluded from our analysis, as noted in our methods) (Table 3).

Discussion

This study investigated outcomes associated with chronic cough in the 5 years following patient diagnosis. We found that among over 65,000 patients diagnosed with chronic cough over a 10‐year timeframe, there were significantly higher rates of new diagnoses with sleep disorders (RR 1.58) and mental health disorders (RR 1.33). This study utilizes a large, national database to generate large cohort sizes, the largest to date, as well as matching to increase the power and generalizability of these results, especially since many past investigations have been conducted using single‐institution formats.

Our findings confirm the results of prior investigations as well as observed findings in clinical practice. Studies assessing the burden of chronic cough are common but have been limited in their scope and geography. These studies have been undertaken in the United States, Canada, Australia, Spain, France, Italy, China, the United Kingdom, South Korea, and Taiwan, finding broadly that chronic cough may have impacts on patient quality of life and healthcare utilization overall. 1 , 5 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 Some of these studies have also investigated potential psychiatric and personality traits as potential contributors to chronic cough, noting that a psychiatric history may predispose a patient to developing chronic cough in addition to chronic cough being a cause of further psychologic distress. 15 , 19 Additionally, survey‐based studies have found that longer duration and increased severity of symptoms are associated with diminished quality of life outcomes for patients. 22 , 23

One potential explanation for our findings is the interplay between obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) and chronic cough, as OSA has been posited as a cause of chronic cough by some investigators. 24 , 25 One potential mechanism for this is OSA's contribution to the development of GERD, which in turn may contribute to the development of chronic cough. 26 It could be supposed that many of our findings of sleep disturbance were the result of deeper investigation for the cause of a patient's GERD and that this resulted in findings of OSA, however, this would not explain the discordance between the prescription rates of reflux medication and later diagnosis with sleep disorder. Instead, we hypothesize that patients waking repeatedly to cough was a likely cause of the sleep disturbance in our study cohort, especially as we excluded patients with a history of sleep disturbance from our analysis. This mechanism has been posited by past authors in smaller studies. 27 Mechanisms for later mental health diagnoses are less clear. Proposed mechanisms include sleep disruption contributing to fatigue, social isolation and embarrassment, physical complications (like incontinence, exhaustion, pain), frustration with refractory nature of the condition and lack of symptomatic relief, and chronic stress, all of which may contribute neuropsychiatric changes. 28 , 29 Given that there is a prevalence of psychomorbidity among chronic cough patients (as high as 33% with anxiety prior to chronic cough diagnosis), chronic cough patients may be more predisposed to have neuropsychiatric effects compared to the general population as well. 30

The multifactorial nature of the causes and quality of life effects of chronic cough indicate the importance of interdisciplinary care as well as targeted, individualized treatment for affected patients. This is especially true for refractory chronic cough, which is defined as the persistence of cough despite treatment for GERD, upper airway cough syndrome, and inflammatory disorders as well as minimization of risk factors. 31 , 32 A past study assessing the efficacy of a multidisciplinary cough clinic including family medicine, internal medicine, allergy and asthma, otolaryngology, pulmonary medicine, and gastroenterology providers found that patients underwent fewer referrals, fewer repeated tests, and had lower healthcare resource utilization. 33 Another study of a multidisciplinary clinic including otolaryngologists, pulmonary medicine, and gastroenterology found that with coordinated care, patient symptomology was significantly decreased in a short period of time. 34 The findings of the present study support this approach, especially relating to early screening and timely specialist referral for treatment of resultant sleep and mental health disorders, both of which were not mentioned in literature on multidisciplinary care of chronic cough. Based on our findings and the support of previous authors, we also propose that patients diagnosed with chronic cough be screened for pre‐existing mental health conditions (given high psychomorbidity rates in this population 30 ) and that there be a low threshold for referral to sleep or psychiatric specialists, especially if the chronic cough causes psychological duress or becomes refractory to treatment.

Despite the strengths and findings of our investigation, important limitations exist within the context of our study question and design. While the TriNetX network is expansive, there is the possibility that patients receiving care at HCOs outside this network were not captured in our study. However, we attempted to overcome this barrier to data fidelity by requiring that patients have at least 5 years of follow‐up for inclusion. Additionally, as with all large electronic medical record‐sourced database studies, our analysis is limited by the accuracy of data entry. Further, patients with chronic cough were noted to have an increased number of medical diagnoses, some of which may have confounded our association between chronic cough and outcome measures. It is important to note that chronic cough, with the exception of neurogenic cough, is a symptom of underlying conditions, so it is possible that those underlying conditions contributed to our findings. However, we believe that plausible mechanisms and our findings of high relative risks indicate strong associations with chronic cough itself, despite potential confounders. Future study will aim to control for confounders and determine other or underlying predictors for mental health and sleep outcomes associated with chronic cough.

Conclusion

Chronic cough is associated with increased risk of development of anxiety, depression, and disordered sleep. This is the largest study to date examining the burden of chronic cough and its associated outcomes. Improved coordination of care is recommended to mitigate these pertinent potential patient outcomes during chronic cough work‐up and treatment. Future study will further investigate these associations and investigate improved patient care models.
