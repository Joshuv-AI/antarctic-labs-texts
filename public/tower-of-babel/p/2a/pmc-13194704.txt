Integrated bioinformatic analysis and experiments reveal EFEMP1 as a novel aging-related signature gene in calcific aortic valve disease
Authors: Daoshen Liu, Jing Wang, Yi Fang, Kai Xu, Haiwei Liu, Chenghui Yan, Dan Liu, Yaling Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The risk of developing calcific aortic valve disease (CAVD) increases two-fold with each decade of life. This study aims to identify and validate critical aging-related gene involved in the pathological process of CAVD. In this study, the gene expression profile of CAVD patients was downloaded from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Integrated bioinformatic analysis such as weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), differential analysis and single cell RNA sequence (scRNA-seq) analysis was used to screen differentially expressed aging-related genes (DEAGs). EGF-like fibulin extracellular matrix protein 1 (EFEMP1) was identified by both bulk and scRNA-seq analysis. The expression signature of the EFEMP1 was validated at the tissue and cellular levels using both public and experimental data. EFEMP1 was knocked down using small interfering RNA to investigate its role in the osteogenic differentiation of valvular interstitial cells (VICs). A total of 699 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were obtained from the combined datasets, and WGCNA identified 6 co-expression modules, of which one hub module (brown module) was most correlated with CAVD. By intersecting the DEGs, hub module genes and aging-related genes, we obtained 16 DEAGs, and a close mutual interaction between these DEAGs was detected. Among the 16 DEAGs, 12 DEAGs were up-regulated, while 4 DEAGs were down-regulated. To ascertain the expression profiles of the 16 DEAGs in VICs, we analyzed another two GEO datasets, and found that only EFEMP1 and IL6 were consistently up-regulated. The EFEMP1 was mainly expressed in VICs based on scRNA-seq analysis and up-regulated in the VICs from animal models of CAVD. The results of public data analyses were further verified by experimental data, the EFEMP1 expression was up-regulated in the calcified aortic valve samples detected by immunohistochemical and immunofluorescent staining at tissue level. Consistent with the changing trends of osteogenic markers such as ALP, RUNX2, and BMP2, the expression level of EFEMP1 was up-regulated in VICs induced by osteogenic medium detected by qPCR and western blotting and the expression levels of osteogenic markers were decreased in VICs after EFEMP1 knockdown. Public and experimental data revealed that EFEMP1 was up-regulated in CAVD at both tissue and cellular levels. Furthermore, functional experiment demonstrated that EFEMP1 knockdown alleviated the osteogenic differentiation of VICs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45986-0.

Introduction

As a chronic and progressive cardiovascular disorder, calcific aortic valve disease (CAVD) is characterized by pathological thickening and calcification of the aortic valve leaflets. Recognized as one of the most prevalent valvular pathologies globally, CAVD constitutes the leading indication for surgical valve replacement due to its association with impaired hemodynamics and disease severity1. The global prevalence of CAVD was estimated as 12.6 million, and causing 102700 deaths in 20172. CAVD is increasingly regarded as a critical public health problem due to the substantial morbidity and mortality it confers3,4. Despite the prevalence of CAVD, there currently lack of effective pharmacological approaches to prevent or merely retard the progress of the disease5. Currently, surgical valve replacement remains the sole definitive therapy upon fulfillment of validated clinical and echocardiographic severity criteria5–7. Development of effective pharmacological therapies necessitates the identification of central molecular drivers underlying CAVD pathogenesis.

Aging represents a primary etiological contributor to diverse age-associated pathologies, including diabetes mellitus, neurodegenerative diseases, and cardiovascular disorders8,9. Notably, CAVD exhibits a pronounced age-dependent trajectory, with epidemiological evidence indicating a two-fold elevation in disease risk per decade of life10. Recently, advances in genome sequencing technology make it possible to obtain abundant information about genetic changes in the process of diseases. Previous studies have identified several hub genes such as S100A8, S100A9, SCG2 and CCL19 that might be involved in CAVD by analyzing published data11–13. Despite progress in elucidating the molecular landscape of CAVD, the precise pathophysiological mechanisms driving its initiation and progression remain incompletely characterized. Given the pivotal role of aging in CAVD pathogenesis, aging-related genes likely mediate critical regulatory pathways underlying disease development. To address this hypothesis, we integrated differentially expressed genes (DEG) profiling with weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) to identify differentially expressed aging-related genes (DEAGs). Subsequent GEO dataset analysis revealed that interleukin 6 (IL6) and EGF-like fibulin extracellular matrix protein 1 (EFEMP1) were consistently up-regulated in human valve interstitial cells (VICs) exposed to osteogenic medium. In addition, EFEMP1 was mainly expressed in VICs based on single cell RNA sequence (scRNA-seq) analysis and up-regulated in the VICs from animal models of CAVD. Finally, experimental validation demonstrated increased EFEMP1 expression in human calcified aortic valve tissues and the cell model for aortic valve calcification, and EFEMP1 knockdown alleviated the osteogenic differentiation of VICs.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and preprocessing

The gene expression profile data (GSE153555, GSE199718, GSE55492, GSE252266 and GSE252267) was downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). GSE153555, GSE199718, GSE55492, GSE252266 and GSE252267 datasets were both RNA-seq data14–17. Following data retrieval, the GSE153555 and GSE199718 datasets were integrated to harmonize batch effects by the “Combat” algorithm in the “sva” package, yielding a merged discovery cohort of 32 samples (17 non-calcified aortic valves and 15 CAVD cases) for downstream analyses. As detailed in a previously published study8, aging-related genes were collected from four manually curated and publicly available resources, namely AgingAtlas18, Human Ageing Genomic Resources (HAGR)19, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Reactome20 by retrieving keyword “senescence”. These experimentally validated genes recorded in more than one database were retained.

Identification of DEGs and functional enrichment analyses

With the merged dataset, differential expression analysis was subsequently performed using the “limma” package to identify genes significantly varied between CAVD and non-calcified aortic valve samples, with thresholds set at adjusted P-value < 0.05 and |logFC|> 0.8. Visualization of DEGs was implemented using the “pheatmap” and “ggplot2” packages, respectively. To further explore the signature of DEGs, the Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses were performed. The top 10 enriched pathways were visualized by the “ggplot2” package.

WGCNA

To delineate CAVD-related transcriptional networks, WGCNA was implemented using the “WGCNA” R package21. Gene expression matrices were pre-filtered by retaining transcripts with expression variance > 0.3 to exclude low-variability genes. An unsigned scale-free topology network was constructed following soft threshold power optimization. Hierarchical clustering with dynamic tree-cutting identified co-expressed gene modules, which were subsequently correlated with CAVD status. Module-trait associations were quantified using Spearman’s rank correlation and visualized as a heatmap. The module demonstrating maximal positive correlation with CAVD was prioritized for downstream characterization. The Venn diagram was plotted by the Evenn website (http://www.ehbio.com/test/venn/)22 to identify overlapped genes as DEAGs for subsequent analysis.

Protein–protein interaction (PPI), functional enrichment analyses and gene screening

In order to clarify the interaction between the DEAGs, PPI network was constructed via the STRING online tool (http://string-db.org)23. GO and KEGG items of DEAGs were conducted using the "ClusterProfiler" R package to annotate biological pathways and molecular mechanisms24. At present, the acquisition of a pro-calcification phenotype by VICs is considered to be a key step in the initiation and progression of aortic valve calcification25. In light of this, we analyzed the GSE252266 and GSE252267 datasets with the aim to identify the genes which showed consistent changes at the tissue and cellular levels, and the results revealed that IL6 and EFEMP1 were consistently up-regulated.

ScRNA-seq analysis

Based on the above analysis, whether the IL6 and EFEMP1 expressions differ in VICs between CAVD and normal control samples. To address this question, publicly available scRNA-seq dataset (GSE180278) was downloaded from the GEO database26. The GSE180278 encompassed 6,574 cells isolated from aortic valves of C57BL/6 J wild-type (WT), Ldlr−/−, and ApoE−/− mice, with the latter two genotypes maintained on a hypercholesterolemic diet to model CAVD pathogenesis. ApoE−/− mice under dietary challenge was a well-established preclinical model of CAVD. Therefore, only samples from C57BL/6 J wild-type and ApoE−/− mice was included in our analysis as in a previous study27, resulting in the initial cell number being 4,484. The scRNA-seq analysis was performed using the “Seurat” package. Dimensionality reduction via principal component analysis (PCA) was followed by graph-based clustering (resolution = 0.4) and visualization using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). Cell type annotation was achieved by identifying cluster-specific marker genes through the “FindAllMarkers” function (logFC threshold > 0.25, adjusted P < 0.05). Comparative analysis of IL6 and EFEMP1 expression between ApoE−/− and WT valves was quantified using the “VlnPlot” function. The GSE220774 dataset contained of scRNA-seq data performed with dissected human aortic valves28. Since calcification mainly occurs on the fibrosa side, we specifically analyzed the fibrosa data to further investigate the expression pattern of EFEMP1 in CAVD. For quality control in the above scRNA-seq analysis, cells retaining greater than 500 expressed genes with mitochondrial content < 20% were selected. Genes detected in ≥ 3 cells (≥ 200 genes/cell) progressed to downstream analysis. The normalization method was “LogNormalize”. Dimensionality reduction and clustering were performed through “RunPCA”, “FindNeighbors”, “FindClusters”, and “RunTSNE” workflows.

Validation analysis in the GEO database

EFEMP1 expression levels were systematically compared between CAVD and non-calcified aortic valve samples across the datasets GSE153555, GSE199718 and GSE55492. Differential expression patterns were visualized using the “ggplot2” package to generate boxplots with significance annotations. To assess the diagnostic potential of EFEMP1, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed via the “pROC” package, with the area under the curve (AUC) quantifying discriminatory capacity of EFEMP1 between CAVD and control groups.

Immunohistochemical and immunofluorescent staining

This study received approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of the General Hospital of Northern Theater Command, Shenyang, China (Approval No. 2024–12-24), and written informed consent was obtained. CAVD samples were collected from 6 male patients (mean age 58.67 ± 4.50) undergoing surgical valve replacement, while non-calcified control samples were acquired from 6 male patients (mean age 59.00 ± 10.61) requiring aortic valve replacement due to ascending aortic pathologies (aortic ectasia or aneurysm). All specimens were fixed, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned into 5-μm-thick serial slices. Following deparaffinization and antigen retrieval, sections underwent immunohistochemical staining using a primary anti-EFEMP1 antibody (HUABIO, Cat# ER63434, 1:300 dilution). Stained sections were captured, and EFEMP1 expression was quantified via ImageJ (v1.53) by measuring integrated optical density (IOD) normalized to total tissue area. Average optical density (AOD = IOD/Area) was calculated to represent EFEMP1 expression intensity, with positive signals exhibiting characteristic brown deposition. For immunofluorescent staining, the sections were incubated with the primary anti-EFEMP1 antibody (HUABIO, Cat# ER63434, 1:300 dilution) and anti-Vimentin antibody(Abcam, Cat# ab8978, 1:300 dilution), followed by incubation with fluorescently conjugated secondary antibody and counterstaining with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI).

Cell culture and Alizarin red staining

Due to the pathological condition, the application of primary VICs was restricted. A recent study has established a human VIC cell line, which maintained in a nonsenescent state and still had the potential for calcification29, therefore, human VIC cell line was used in our study. Human VIC cell line (FH-Y035) was purchased from FUHENG BIOLOGY corporation (Shanghai, China). Cells were induced by Human Mesenchymal Stem Cell Osteogenic Differentiation & Staining Kit (FUHENG BIOLOGY, Cat#WWL-G039). For comparative analysis, VICs were maintained in standard growth medium (DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin) under identical culture conditions. For Alizarin Red staining, the cells were induced for 14 days. After treatment, cells were rinsed in 1 × PBS, then fixed in 4% PFA for 15 min at room temperature and rinsed in distilled water.

The fixed cells were stained with 0.2% Alizarin Red stain (FUHENG BIOLOGY, Cat#WWL-G039) for 5 min and then washed in distilled water.

Cell transfection

Human VICs were plated at a density of 1 × 105 cells/ml in 6-well plates. When the cells were 80% confluent, the small interfering RNAs (siRNAs: siNC and siEFEMP1) were transfected according to the instructions of the jetPRIME® reagent (Polyplus Inc.). The short interfering (si)RNA-EFEMP1 (50 nM; 5’-CCCAGUCAAUAGUCUACAATT-3’), siNC (50 nM; 5’-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3’) were synthesized by Shanghai Obio Co. Ltd. To prepare the transfection mixture, 4 µl of siRNA was added to 300 µl buffer and then 6 µl transfection reagent was added. After adding each reagent, the mixture was mixed using a 1 ml pipette, and the transfection complex was allowed to stand at room temperature for 10 min. The cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) three times, and then 1.7 ml DMEM medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum was added. At 24 h post-transfection, the cells were subjected to subsequent experiments.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction

Both groups were incubated for 5 days, with medium replacement every 48 h to ensure nutrient stability and sustained osteogenic stimulus. The total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol Reagent (TIANGEN, Cat#DP424). Reverse transcription was performed using The TaqMan™ Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher), and qRT-PCR was performed using the SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit II (ACCURATE BIOLOGY, Cat#AG11702). The reaction conditions were initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 45 s. Primers used were listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Western blotting

After osteogenic induction, cellular lysates were subsequently harvested for Western blotting analysis to assess EFEMP1 protein expression dynamics. Total proteins were harvested and subjected to western blotting with primary antibodies targeting EFEMP1 (HUABIO, #ER63434, 1:1,000 dilution), RUNX2 (Cell Signaling Technology, clone D1L7F, #12,556, 1:2,000 dilution), ALP (Abclonal, #A0514,1:1,000 dilution), BMP2(Abcam, #ab214821,1:1,000 dilution) and GAPDH (Proteintech, #60,004–1-Ig, 1:1,000 dilution). Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (Meilunbio, MA0186) and quantified using ImageJ v1.53 (NIH).

Statistical analysis

Bioinformatic analyses were conducted using R (v4.3.2) with specialized packages. Differential gene expression between groups was assessed via two-tailed Wilcoxon rank-sum test (Mann–Whitney U test). For experimental data, statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA), with normality evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Parametric comparisons (unpaired Student’s t-test) were applied to normally distributed datasets, while non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05, with asterisks denoting significance levels in graphical representations: * (P < 0.05), ** (P < 0.01), *** (P < 0.001), and **** (P < 0.0001).

WGCNA network construction and identification of hub DEAGs

To prioritize hub genes implicated in CAVD pathogenesis, the “WGCNA” package was employed to construct unsigned scale-free co-expression networks. Hierarchical clustering of the combined dataset revealed distinct transcriptional subgroups (Fig. 3A). Soft threshold power was optimized to be 4 to maximize network connectivity while preserving biological interpretability (Fig. 3B). Module identification via dynamic tree-cutting resulted in 6 co-expressed gene clusters, with module similarity quantified by topological overlap matrix-based dendrograms (Fig. 3C). Spearman correlation heatmap demonstrated significant association between module genes and CAVD (Fig. 3D). Notably, the brown module exhibited the strongest positive correlation with CAVD (Spearman’s r = 0.87, P = 6 × 10⁻11), implicating its constituent genes as potential drivers of valvular calcification. On account of this, module brown was considered as the pivotal module for subsequent analysis. Based on a combined gene significance (GS) > 0.2 and module membership (MM) > 0.7, a total of 552 genes belonging to the module brown were determined as candidate hub genes (Supplementary Table S4). As shown in Fig. 3E, a significant association was demonstrated between GS for CAVD and MM (correlation efficient = 0.88, p < 1e-200). Finally, an interaction was performed between the 552 module genes, 699 DEGs and 368 ARGs (Supplementary Table S5), resulting in identification of 16 DEAGs: CDKN2B, IRS2, SERPINE1, PYCR1, PLAU, CDKN2A, MAP2K6, MAPT, CDK6, CDKN1A, NFKBIA, VEGFA, EFEMP1, HIC1, IL6(Fig. 3F).Fig. 3WGCNA analysis and Venn diagram to obtain key aging-related genes. Interestingly, we found a significant PPI among 16 DEAGs (PPI enrichment p-value: 3.36 × 10⁻8) than what would be detected for a random set of genes with similar size, indicating that the 16 DEAGs were biologically connected. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed to evaluate the biological functions of 16 DEAGs. As shown in Fig. 4B, the DEAGs were mainly enriched in “regulation of protein serine/threonine kinase activity” and “cellular senescence” at the BP level, and located in “peptidase inhibitor” at the CC level. Concerning the MF level, DEAGs were mainly enriched in “protein kinase inhibitor activity” and “protein kinase activator activity”. KEGG analysis showed that the DEAGs were mainly enriched in “cellular senescence” (Fig. 4B). Based on the cluster tree diagram (Supplementary Fig. S3A), we selected a resolution parameter of 0.4 for subsequent clustering. As shown in Fig. 5A and 5B, 4487 cells were divided into 11 clusters and annotated as different cell types. Based on the above analysis results, EFEMP1 was mainly expressed in the VICs. We further performed immunofluorescent staining to confirm the expression location of EFEMP1. Validation at cellular level and functional experiment

To further elucidate the role EFEMP1 in valvular calcification, we established an in vitro model of CAVD using human VICs, as confirmed by Alizarin Red staining (Fig. 8A). After 5 days of osteogenic induction, we performed qPCR and western blot analysis to detect the expression change of EFEMP1. The qPCR results showed the mRNA level of EFEMP1 was increased after osteogenic induction, and the mRNA levels of osteogenic markers (ALP, RUNX2 and BMP2) were increased simultaneously (Fig. 8B). Consistent with the qPCR results, western blotting analysis showed EFEMP1 was up-regulated in VICs undergoing osteogenic induction, and the osteogenic markers were simultaneously up-regulated (Fig. 8C). In addition, we conducted functional experiments to clarify the role of EFEMP1 in CAVD. Discussion

As a slowly progressive heart valve disorder, the prevalence of CAVD is more common with age. CAVD progression can be dichotomized into two distinct pathological stages: (1) aortic valve sclerosis, featured by fibrotic thickening without hemodynamic compromise, and (2) calcific aortic valve stenosis, featured by progressive leaflet calcification, restricted mobility, and clinically significant flow obstruction32. Epidemiological studies estimated that the prevalence of aortic valve sclerosis in adults > 65 years was 25–30%, whereas calcific stenosis affected 2% of individuals > 60 years, escalating to 10% in octogenarians33,34. Symptomatic stenosis portended a dire prognosis, with untreated patients exhibiting a two-year mortality rate approaching 50%34. Despite the high prevalence of early-stage sclerosis, its subclinical nature has hindered elucidation of the inciting molecular mechanisms. Deciphering the pathophysiological continuum from sclerosis to stenosis remained imperative for developing disease-modifying therapies to attenuate or prevent CAVD progression, and aging-related genes might play important roles in CAVD, and potential interventional targets might lie in them.

With this in mind, we aimed to identify critical aging-related genes involved in the pathological process of CAVD in this study. With the increasing availability of large amounts of sequencing data, previous studies have explored other CAVD hub genes related to immune, pyroptosis, ferroptosis and autophagy pathways31,34–36. In the study by Hu et al., 10 autophagy-related hub genes were identified and potential therapetic drugs were predicted35. Focusing on ferroptosis key genes in CAVD, Li et al. Identified and validated 5 hub genes in CAVD31. Previous studies have conducted insightful explorations of the pathological mechanism of CAVD from various perspectives, but the mechanism of CAVD was still far from being fully elucidated. At present, there were relatively few studies on the role of aging-related genes in CAVD. To fill this gap, we performed comprehensive analysis of public transcriptome sequencing data, and validated the results at the tissue and cellular levels using both public and experimental data. In CAVD studies, it is generally not practical to acquire large samples due to tissue and time constraints. Therefore, we integrated two CAVD datasets from the GEO database to expand the sample size. To precisely identify critical genes in CAVD, both differential expression and WGCNA analyses were performed based on the combined dataset. Despite using different datasets, the enriched pathways in our study were similar to those in a previous study11. For GO enrichment analysis, main pathways including “extracellular structure organization”, “extracellular matrix organization” and “external encapsulating structure organization” were enriched in both studies, highlighting the importance of extracellular matrix in CAVD. For KEGG enrichment analysis, the “ECM–receptor interaction” and “cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction” pathways were identified in both studies. In addition to the above pathways, the “PI3k-Akt signaling pathway” was also highlighted in our study. Accordingly, prior studies have shown that this pathway might have a potential role in CAVD37,38. By intersecting DEGs, key module genes and aging-related genes, 16 hub DEAGs were acquired, and a close mutual interaction between these DEAGs was detected according to the STRING database.

According to the current understanding of CAVD, the osteogenic differentiation of VICs was an important pathological process in CAVD. In view of this, we expected to identify molecules that showed consistent changes at tissue and cellular levels, which were more likely to play a role in valve calcification. By analyzing another two public GEO datasets, we found that EFEMP1 and IL6 were consistently up-regulated in CAVD samples and VICs subjected to osteogenic induction. In addition to the bulk sequence analyses, scRNA-seq analysis was employed to uncover the IL6 and EFEMP1 expression profile in the VICs of CAVD. The expression of IL6 was elevated in the VICs in spite of not reaching a statistically significant difference, while the expression of EFEMP1 was significantly increased in the VICs of CAVD. Previous studies had conducted experiments to explore the role of IL6 in CAVD31. The results showed that IL6 was up-regulated in VICs undergoing osteogenic induction, and IL6 knockdown alleviated the phosphate-induced mineralization. Prior studies involving human populations have also demonstrated the role of IL6 in CAVD, the reason for negative results in the scRNA-seq analysis might be due to the low expression level of IL6, and increasing the sample size might lead to significant results. The expression profile of EFEMP1 in VICs was further validated in another scRNA-seq data performed in dissected human aortic valves. The expression profile of EFEMP1 were validated with box plots in three GEO datasets, and ROC analysis showed a good ability of EFEMP1 in discriminating CAVD from control. To validate the dysregulated expression of EFEMP1 in CAVD, we performed orthogonal experimental validation employing immunohistochemical and immunofluorescent staining at the tissue level, and found that EFEMP1 was up-regulated in CAVD samples and colocalized with VIC markers. At the cellular level, the expression of EFEMP1 was up-regulated in qPCR and western blotting analysis, and EFEMP1 knockdown reduced the expression of osteogenic markers induced by osteogenic medium.

EFEMP1, also known as fibulin-3, is a member of seven extracellular matrix proteins characterized by two tandem arrays comprised with epidermal growth factor-like domains and C-terminal fibulin-type module39. Fibulin-3 was first isolated from the differently expressed genes between senescent and quiescent human fibroblasts and reported to be universally expressed in the extracellular matrix and essential for the development and maintenance of tissues40. In spontaneously hypertensive rats, the levels of fibulin-3 were increased simultaneously with matrix metalloproteinases, indicating the potential role of fibulin-3 in vascular remodeling. In addition, previous studies reported that EFEMP1 might be involved in brain aging41, neurodegeneration42, aortic dissection43 and cerebral small vessel diseases44. However, the role of fibulin-3 in cardiovascular system calcification has rarely been reported. A previous study reported that fibulin-3 might attenuate phosphate-induced vascular smooth muscle cell calcification by inhibition of oxidative stress45. Nevertheless, the recombinant fibulin-3 protein was used in this study. Furthermore, the expression of fibulin-3 remained unchanged under pathological stimulation, which undermined the explanation of the results. In our study, the expression level of fibulin-3 was consistently increased in CAVD group, and EFEMP1 knockdown alleviated the osteogenic differentiation of VICs, indicating a role of promoting osteogenic differentiation in CAVD. Despite these advances and our findings, the functional role of EFEMP1 in CAVD remained to be further explored.

There are several limitations should be noted in our study. Firstly, the data regarding the association of EFEMP1 expression with disease severity and clinical prognosis was lacking. Further data regarding the correlation of EFEMP1 with severity and prognosis would deepen its clinical relevance as a biomarker. Secondly, in the scRNA-seq analysis, a dataset from CAVD mouse model was analyzed to ascertain the expression of EFEMP1 in VICs, which might bring certain biases due to the differences among species. However, the publicly available scRNA-seq data performed with human samples was scarce at present. Due to the fact that the number of cells among the groups varied in GSE220774, we merely applied it as verification for the expression level of EFEMP1. Further analysis with scRNA-seq data performed with intact human arotic valve samples would collaborate our findings. Thirdly, the role of EFEMP1 in cellular senescence needed further exploration. The mechanism by which EFEMP1 affected the calcification of VICs by modulating cell senescence deserved further investigation. Fourthly, the cell experiments were conducted in cell line, and the results needed to be further validated in primary cells. In addition, the control tissues were derived from patients with aortic ectasia or aneurysm. Although previous studies showed that samples from patients with aortic ectasia or aneurysm could be regarded as control46, using tissues from healthy controls would make the research more rigorous. Last but not least, the mechanistic contributions of EFEMP1 to CAVD pathogenesis remained uncharacterized at the molecular level, and the small human sample size might bring about selection bias.

In conclusion, we identified EFEMP1 as a novel aging-related signature gene in CAVD by integrated bioinformatic analysis, and validated the results with experimental data at tissue and cellular levels. Functional experiment data showed that EFEMP1 knockdown alleviated the osteogenic differentiation of VICs, indicating a potential role of promoting osteogenic differentiation in CAVD.
