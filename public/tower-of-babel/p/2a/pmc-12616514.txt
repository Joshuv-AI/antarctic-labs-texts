Integration of a WASH Component in the Standard National Protocol for Treatment of Severe Acute Malnutrition in Children Aged 6–59 Months in Northern Senegal—A Costing Study
Authors: Bibata Wassonguema, Maily Lê‐Lacanette, Laura Braun, Matar Ba, Françoise Siroma, Albert Emile Cabo, Antonio Vargas Brizuela, Moustapha Seye, Jean Lapègue, Alexandre Devort, Oliver Cumming, Dieynaba S. N'Diaye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSevere acute malnutrition (SAM) affects 12.2 million children globally. Integrating a water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) kit in outpatient SAM treatment can improve recovery rates by preventing WASH‐related diseases and complications, but its cost at scale remains unknown. This study estimates the cost of integrating a WASH kit, composed of chlorine‐based water treatment, safe water storage with a lid, soap, and a hygiene promotion component into Senegal's national protocol for treating uncomplicated SAM. This costing study was nested within the TISA randomised controlled trial, which evaluated the addition of a WASH component to standard SAM treatment for children aged 6–59 months. Cost data were collected from 660 participants enroled between December 2020 and December 2021. We took a societal perspective and used a micro‐costing approach to estimate direct medical, non‐medical and indirect costs. The WASH component led to a 2021 international $105.32 additional cost per child treated, with the WASH kit, transportation and management representing $33.03. Sensitisation to hygiene and water treatment cost $13.46 at health posts and $29.63 for two at‐home visits. No additional out‐of‐pocket expenses were incurred by households, but $1.58 in opportunity costs (income loss) was observed. Human resources were the main cost driver for the WASH component, exceeding the human resources for standard SAM treatment. The total societal cost per child treated was $338.77, ranging from $238.09 to $517.29 in sensitivity analysis, with the SAM treatment representing 69% ($233.40) of this total cost. The main expense for this component was Ready‐to‐Use‐Therapeutic Food (RUTF) ($154.39). The absence of additional costs for households induced by the WASH component is encouraging, as it suggests that it would not represent an obstacle to integration into the national protocol. We produced a robust and comprehensive cost estimate for integrating a WASH kit and hygiene promotion into Senegal's SAM treatment protocol. This increased the treatment cost by 45% which was lower than estimates from a previous study. Results inform budget planning and support future cost‐effectiveness analyses of integrating WASH interventions into SAM protocols.

Introduction

Severe acute malnutrition (SAM) currently affects an estimated 12.2 million children under five globally, with 22.1% living in Africa (Levels and Trends in Child Malnutrition, 2025). The World Health Organization (WHO) defines SAM as a condition characterised by a weight‐for‐height z‐score (WHZ) below −3, and/or a mid‐upper arm circumference (MUAC) less than 115 mm and/or the observation of bilateral oedema (World Health Organization 2013). This burden has been exacerbated by the COVID‐19 pandemic (Headey et al. 2020), armed conflicts, rising of food prices and climate change (Soaring Food Costs, Low Rainfall and Insecurity Leave Children in the Sahel at Risk of Catastrophic Levels of Severe Malnutrition ‐ UNICEF, 2022).

SAM is a multifactorial condition, driven by food insecurity, inadequate caregiving practices, and unsanitary environments (UNICEF Conceptual Framework | UNICEF, 2021). The WHO recommends the community‐based management of acute malnutrition (CMAM) for children with uncomplicated SAM cases, whereby children receive outpatient treatment programmes (OTP) including medical and ready‐to‐use therapeutic food (RUTF) administered at home by caregivers. The term “uncomplicated SAM” refers to children who do not present bilateral oedemas, medical complications (e.g., diarrhoea or fever) and who pass the appetite test at the health post; hence can be treated in an outpatient setting. This decentralised model has been widely embraced, as it reduces households' costs and healthcare system constraints, thus improving access and adherence to treatment. Its implementation has improved coverage and treatment outcomes (World Health Organization 2023), thereby enhancing the response to SAM in vulnerable communities (Puett et al. 2013; Tekeste et al. 2012; Wilford et al. 2012). It also lowers provider costs and was shown to be cost effective in various settings (Puett et al. 2013; Tekeste et al. 2012; Wilford et al. 2012).

However, children treated under CMAM remain exposed to infection risks due to inadequate water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) conditions in the household and community environments (Wolf et al. 2023). As treatment is delivered in a less controlled environment, exposure to enteric pathogens may lead to recurrent diarrhoeal episodes and diverticular diseases (Cumming et al. 2016). These conditions are known to negatively affect nutritional status, contributing to growth faltering (Checkley et al. 2008; Rogawsk et al. 2018) or SAM (Dangour et al. 2013).

As WASH interventions have proven effective in reducing respiratory infections (Ross et al. 2023), and are cost‐effective for preventing diarrhoeal disease (Wolf et al. 2022, 2023), a growing number of operational initiatives have sought to improve nutritional outcomes by integrating WASH components. For instance, the WASH‐in‐Nut strategy developed by stakeholders from both the nutrition and WASH sectors in West and Central Africa promotes multisectoral programming (WASH‐Nutrition Strategy | UNICEF West and Central Africa, 2022). In Senegal, UNICEF currently distributes a WASH kit to children undergoing treatment for SAM. These kits typically include essential water treatment, hygiene and/or sanitation supplies, such as chlorine‐based water treatment or soap. They are generally distributed in specific contexts such as humanitarian crises or as support to clinical care (cholera, malnutrition…), where evidence suggests they can improve health outcomes. Integrating a WASH component into SAM treatment protocols aligns with global health priorities by addressing both the immediate consequences of malnutrition and its underlying determinants, namely poor access to safe water and hygiene. This approach is consistent with recent WHO recommendations, which encourage multisectoral strategies for preventing malnutrition.

Action contre la Faim (ACF) and partners previously conducted two studies—the Programme for Improved Nutrition in Emergencies (PURE) trial in Pakistan (Doocy et al. 2018) and the Ouadinut study in Chad (Altmann et al. 2018)—which demonstrated the clinical benefits of integrating WASH into SAM management. The PURE study reported a positive difference of 22.1 percentage points in SAM recovery rate among children who received chlorine‐based water treatment in addition to the standard CMAM protocol. Similarly, the Ouadinut study showed a statistically significant increase in recovery rates with a 10.5% difference (Confidence Interval [IC] 95% [6.7–19.8]). However, the economic and financial dimensions of these interventions were insufficiently addressed, highlighting a gap in the evidence base to inform policy, particularly in West Africa.

Given the significant burden of SAM and the constraints on health system resources, assessing the financial and economic feasibility of effective interventions is essential. Such evaluation supports policymakers and programme implementers in maximising health impact while ensuring efficient resources allocation.

To contribute to the limited evidence on the cost of operational feasibility of integrated approaches, the Traitement Intégré de la Sous Nutrition Aiguë (TISA, “Integrated Treatment of Acute Malnutrition”) trial was launched in December 2020 in northern Senegal. It was conducted in the context of ongoing discussion within the Senegalese Ministry of Health regarding updates to the national SAM treatment protocol. The TISA trial was designed to assess the effectiveness and cost‐effectiveness of adding a scalable WASH kit and sensitisation to the standard protocol for outpatient treatment of uncomplicated SAM.

This study, embedded in the TISA trial, aims to estimate the economic and financial cost of integrating a WASH component into the SAM treatment protocol during the first year of implementation, using a disaggregated societal perspective.

Methods

Study Context

The TISA trial was implemented in northern Senegal, in the districts of Podor and Pété located in the Saint‐Louis region, and Dahra and Linguère located in the Louga region. This Sahelian zone is predominantly inhabited by Fula nomadic communities, whose livelihoods primarily rely on agriculture and livestock. In 2022, the prevalence of SAM in children aged 6–59 months was 4.3% in the Louga region and 1.8% in the Saint‐Louis region (Conseil National de Sécurité Alimentaire ‐ Sénégal 2022). In 2023, the prevalence of diarrhoea among children aged 6–59 months was estimated at 23.5% (Sénégal: Enquête Démographique et de Santé Continue, 2023). According to the United Nations, in 2022, only 14% of the population had access to safely managed sanitation services (SDG indicator 6.2.1a, 2022) and 27% used a safely managed drinking water service (SDG indicator 6.1.1, 2022) (Senegal | SDG 6 Data, n. d.).

Study Design

The costing study was embedded within a cluster‐randomised trial assessing the effects of adding a WASH component into the existing OTP for SAM. During this trial, 86 health posts delivering OTP treatment were randomly allocated in two arms. The control arm, which followed the standard SAM protocol as outlined in the Senegalese national guidelines (Ministère de la Santé et de la Prévention‐Sénégal 2013), and an intervention arm, which combined the standard protocol with an additional WASH component. It included the distribution of a simple WASH kit and hygiene‐related behaviour change communication activities, and will be referred in the rest of the article by “the WASH component.”

Interventions in both arms were delivered by head nurses and community health workers working within the existing public health system. Community volunteers also took part in the intervention, carrying at‐home visits. They are members of the community, usually women, who are not part of the formal healthcare workforce but are compensated on a per‐activity basis through project funding. They are responsible for sensitisation and communication on health issues at the community level. In the context of acute undernutrition, they play a key role in community mobilisation, case detection, and linking households to health posts (Politique nationale de santé communautaire 2014). Study Population, Perspective and Time Horizon

This cost analysis focused solely on the intervention arm of the TISA trial. The study population included all children aged 6–59 months with uncomplicated SAM who received treatment during the first year of enrolment, from December 22, 2020 to December 21, 2021. A total of 660 SAM children were enroled in the intervention arm and received treatment up to 8 weeks, in accordance with the duration recommended in the national protocol.

In line with national guidelines, SAM was defined by weight‐for‐height z‐score below −3 (WHO Child Growth Standards) and/or a mid‐upper arm circumference below 115 millimetres and/or the presence of nutritional oedema (World Health Organization, & United Nations Children's Fund UNICEF 2009).

Both financial and economic costs were estimated from a societal perspective. Financial costs refer to actual expenditures by implementing actors (national health system, UNICEF, ACF) and household out‐of‐pocket expenses, while economic costs include the opportunity cost of caregivers' time.

The costing time horizon was 1 year, allowing the inclusion of annual expenses such as training sessions. The time frame of the intervention was 8 weeks, corresponding to the recommended treatment duration per child. Activity costs were calculated by dividing total annual costs by the number of children treated during the year (660), resulting in estimates expressed per child treated over an 8‐week period.

Data Collection

Costing data were collected between December 2020 and December 2021. We only considered costs incurred specially for the implementation of this study, excluding capital costs for existing infrastructure and research and trial monitoring costs. Building and nonspecific furniture costs were not taken into account, as our intervention was embedded within existing health system structures. Costs for each step of the intervention were proportionally allocated based on the actual resource use during the first year of implementation.

Sociodemographic and expenditure data were collected from caregivers at admission using the trial's enrolment questionnaire. Head nurses recorded clinical and medical data at admission and during weekly follow‐up visits, in line with the standard SAM treatment protocol. They were later extracted from the health post registries and transferred to the trial database for costing purposes. To gain a comprehensive understanding of implementation processes and identify the personnel involved in each intervention activity, in‐depth semi‐structured interviews were conducted with key informants.

We used a purposive sampling approach. Standardised questionnaires, coded using Open Data Kit software (ODK) and including both open‐ and closed‐ended questions, were used to assess the care procedures, resources mobilised and time required to perform each task of the WASH component. All 86 head nurses delivering SAM treatment at health posts and the 43 community volunteers responsible for WASH kit distribution were targeted.

Personnel costs were calculated by multiplying the time spent on each activity (obtained via time allocation questionnaires) by the hourly corresponding wage. Wages were drawn from the Wage Indicator for health system personnel in Senegal (Emplois et Revenus, n. d.) and from the study by Granström et al for community volunteers (Colnerud Granström 2009).

Material costs included anthropometric equipment (e.g., height boards) and medical examination tools (e.g., thermometer), WASH kit components (e.g., buckets) and communication materials (e.g., image box). Estimates were based on ACF and trial financial records and accounted for both quantities and frequency of use. For durable equipment with a lifespan exceeding 1 year (e.g., height boards), straight‐line annual depreciation was used, as we have no reason to assume that the depreciation rate of the material would vary overtime. Depreciation was calculated by dividing the purchase cost by its expected lifespan (Drummond et al. 2015; Johns et al. 2003). For each health post, this value was then divided by the estimated number of uses per year (approximated by the total number of consultations conducted at the health post), to obtain the cost attributable to each child and each consultation. For items with a shorter lifespan (e.g., thermometers, middle‐upper arm circumference tapes), costs were prorated based on their usage, then divided by the average number of monthly consultations to estimate the cost per consultation and per child.

Drug costs related to SAM treatment comprised antibiotics, anthelmintic, vitamin A and the measles vaccine. Following the national outpatient protocol (Ministère de la Santé et de la Prévention‐Sénégal 2013), the quantity of medication administered per child was estimated based on age and body weight at admission. Drug costs were sourced from the National Supply Pharmacy website (Pharmacie Nationale d'Approvisionnement ‐ Sénégal, n. d.) and were multiplied by the estimated quantity used to calculate total drug costs per patient.

RUTF consumption was estimated using weight‐based dosage guidelines from the Senegalese CMAM protocol (Ministère de la Santé et de la Prévention‐Sénégal 2013) based on the child's weight at admission and during weekly follow‐ups. The unit cost per RUTF sachet included the UNICEF reference price for a box (150 sachets) (UNICEF 2022) plus an additional 50% to account for supply chain and delivery cost to delivery points, as per expert consultation. Total RUTF cost per patient was calculated by multiplying the number of sachets consumed by the estimated unit cost per sachets.

Although outpatient SAM care is provided free of charge in Senegal, household incurred nonmedical costs, such as transportation and meal expenses on consultation days. In addition, indirect costs were captured by valuing the opportunity cost of caregivers' time using the human capital approach. The time spent accompanying the child to consultations and participating in hygiene promotion activities (including travel and waiting time) was given a shadow cost based on the caregiver's self‐reported occupation (mainly domestic work). We then applied the monthly income for a domestic work reported in the Harmonised Household Living Conditions Survey in Senegal (Sénégal ‐ Enquête Harmonisée sur les Conditions de Vie des Ménages (EHCVM‐2018‐2019), 2021).

Data Analysis

All individual‐level clinical, economic, financial and sociodemographic data were analysed using STATA (StataCorp, version 16, 2019). Data from structured questionnaires collected through ODK were cleaned and analysed in Microsoft Excel 2016.

Outcome

We aggregated all cost component to estimate activity costs, for each step of the intervention. The total intervention cost was then obtained by summing all activity costs and dividing the result by the number of children with SAM treated during the first year of the trial. This resulted in the primary outcome: the cost per SAM child treated.

Costs were disaggregated by funding stakeholder, and the relative share of the WASH component and standard SAM treatment costs was estimated. All costs were expressed in 2021 international dollars, using purchasing power parity (PPP) exchange rates (Conversion Rates ‐ Exchange Rates ‐ OECD Data, n. d.). No discounting rate was applied given the short time horizon of the study. Further details on data sources and calculation methods are presented in Appendix Table 2 to 7.

Sensitivity Analysis

First, we conducted a univariate deterministic sensitivity analysis on the costs induced by the WASH component, presented in the form of a tornado diagram, to identify the variables whose uncertainty has the greatest impact on the variability of our results. To construct this analysis, base case, minimum and maximum values were determined. Base case values were drawn from empirical trial data, while the minimum and maximum values were determined using interviews with field staff and literature. For parameters based on self‐reported data, the 10th and 90th percentiles were used to define plausible bounds. Other parameters were varied by ±10%, except for those with higher expected variability (such as health professional salaries and opportunity cost for community volunteers), which were varied by ±25%.

We also conducted a multivariate deterministic sensitivity analysis, to assess bounds of uncertainty variation in our total cost. We constructed two deterministic scenarios: a best‐case scenario, where all costs and parameters take their minimum value, and a worst‐case scenario, with all parameters set to their maximum value. These hypothetical scenarios provided estimate of the cost variability range.

Finally, a programmatic scale‐up scenario was developed to simulate the conditions in which the intervention would be delivered if exclusively carried out by the national health system, without ACF support. This scenario offers preliminary insight into the intervention's financial sustainability in routine conditions.

Ethical Considerations

Ethics approval on this economic evaluation was granted by the National Health Research Ethics Committee of Senegal (CNERS) (Ref. 000138/MSAS/DPRS/CNERS and 000969‐MSAS/DPRS/DR) and the Research Ethics Committee (REC) (Ref. 21278) of the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine. The trial in which this study is nested was registered at ClinicalTrials. gov (ID: NCT04667767). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection.

Discussion

Main Results

The societal and provider costs of the adapted protocol including the WASH component was $338.77 and $280.74 per child treated, respectively. Adding the WASH component increased the cost by $105.32, a 45% increase, mainly driven by HR ($61.41, including training sessions and opportunity costs for trainees) and kit constituents ($25.99). No additional out‐of‐pocket costs were incurred by the households.

These results align with the PURE study, where the combination of SAM treatment with Aquatabs cost $270 (2021 int dollar) from the provider perspective. Although the absolute increase in cost was very similar to ours ($105.00), the relative increase was higher in PURE (64% vs. 45%), likely due to contextual and design differences. The PURE study hired additional WASH personnel, whereas TISA leveraged the existing health system to deliver WASH kits. This highlights the potential for integrated care to generate cost savings from the provider perspective. Morever, Aquatabs distribution was tied to treatment duration, but TISA limited this duration to 8 weeks, capping the number of tablets provided. Lastly, PURE excluded households costs and included home water testing, which was considered a research cost in our study.

Materials, Kit Constituents and Cost Implications

WASH kit materials cost $25.99, accounting for 25% of the additional WASH‐related costs. To limit expenses and enhance sustainability, the kit was designed using locally available items (e.g., soaps, Aquatabs and buckets) thereby reducing supply chain risks.

In comparison, the PURE study provided only a safe water storage container with Aquatabs, while the Ouadinut and TISA kits were similar, with the Ouadinut kit also including a drinking cup and additional Aquatabs for a 1‐month posttreatment period. The UNICEF kit currently distributed in Senegal which includes a handwashing station, soap, bleach, a towel and Aquatabs is more complex and faces logistical challenges such as stock‐outs and low coverage. The simpler TISA kit, covered by ACF in this study, could be more cost‐effective if deployed by UNICEF, without shifting costs to households, given its ability to efficiently address logistical constrains. TISA WASH kits management and transportation costs were low: $4.57 per kit for transportation and $2.35 for management at distribution points. Kits were prepositioned and largely composed of locally sourced materials (with Aquatabs available as a mass‐market option in Senegal), helping to minimise stock‐outs and reduce transportation costs compared to the UNICEF kit. Scaling‐up the TISA intervention could reduce costs further through economies of scale, and integrating kit distribution into the Ministry of Health supply chain could yield additional savings. Other materials (e.g., the picture box and the training materials) represented 11% of the additional cost ($11.77) and could be reused in case of scale‐up and lead to subsequent savings per child treated.

Human Resources

Human resources costs for the WASH component were $61.41 per child, representing 58% of WASH‐related costs, significantly higher than HR for SAM treatment alone ($12.71). This reflects the increased workload for head nurses and community volunteers as well as their opportunity costs, under the societal perspective. Health workers' training and refresher sessions cost $26.03 per child treated, representing the largest human resources expense. While this constitutes a recurrent cost (required annually or for newly recruited staff) training efficiency may improve over time with experience, potentially reducing preparation time. However, organisational constraints could offset these gains by increasing the duration of sessions. In a scale‐up scenario, a cascade training model could reduce overall costs. Home visits by community volunteers, also contributed $25.38.

In this study, ACF and TISA staff only supported kit procurement, transport and staff training. But a national implementation could shift these tasks to UNICEF and the health system staff, entailing their own opportunity costs. A more sustainable approach would integrate the WASH kit delivery into the Ministry of Health's system, with support from local actors and community‐based organisations. This would require targeted investment in capacity‐building and training but may reduce dependency on higher‐cost agencies. Projected variations in a programmatic scale‐up scenario are presented in Appendix, Table 8. These remain preliminary estimates, requiring refinement with real‐world conditions in case of a future implementation.

Cost of the Sensitisation Component

The sensitisation activities on water treatment, hand hygiene and adherence to SAM treatment cost $43.16 per SAM case treated. Sensitisation at the health posts, led by the head nurses, were less costly ($13.46) than at‐home visits conducted by conducted volunteers. The opportunity costs for community volunteers are high, as they can face extensive sensitisation and travel times, which reduces involvement in other community tasks.

Comparison with existing literature remains limited, as to our knowledge, no study have estimated hygiene promotion costs specifically in SAM children. One study in Bangladesh evaluated a 7‐day, hospital‐based hygiene intervention for cholera contact persons at 2021 int $110 per household (George et al. 2016). (Ross et al. 2021). However, such figures should be interpreted with caution due to contextual and methodological differences.

In the event of a potential scale‐up, a value‐for‐money analysis would help determine whether maintaining two at‐home visits is justifiable to strengthen hygiene promotion during treatment.

SAM Treatment Cost

The societal cost of standard SAM treatment in the study was $233.40 per child treated for an average of 6.4 weeks. From the provider perspective, the SAM treatment cost was $177.00, mainly attributable to RUTF (88%).

A 2023 systematic review on the costs and cost‐effectiveness of treatment for children with wasting (Mdege et al. 2023), reported variability across contexts due to differences in SAM definitions, protocols and health systems. While direct comparison is limited, our societal cost estimate ($233.40) is below the review's estimated range of 2021 int $323 to $1750, while the provider cost ($177.00), is within the reported range (from 2021 int $113 to $1,389).

Several factors could explain these lower costs. First, inpatient treatment cost was excluded as referred cases exited the trial. Second, the shorter treatment duration (6.4 consultations) reflects the TISA design with data censored at 8 weeks. Finally, the CMAM programme was delivered by local staff only, with no direct NGO support, reducing wage costs.

Households' Cost

The integration of the WASH component imposed a minimal cost on households, (less than $2.00 per child treated), and primarily reflects opportunity costs linked to participation in WASH promotion activities. In comparison, SAM treatment generated higher household costs, with $43.01 in out‐of‐pockets expenses and $13.44 in opportunity cost, which is consistent with estimates from the literature (2021 int $58 per child treated, [Mdege et al. 2023]). The WASH component imposed negligible household costs compared to the standard SAM treatment, highlighting its potential affordability for broader implementation.

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analyses highlighted key cost drivers and sources of uncertainty. Within the WASH component, HR‐related activities (training and sensitisation) and kit procurement showed the greatest influence on cost variation. For the overall intervention, caregivers' opportunity cost and HR during SAM treatment were the main contributors to uncertainty. These variations reflect contextual challenges in valuing time and salaries, particularly given limited access to precise wage data. Worst‐ and best‐case scenario analyses indicated that the total cost per child treated could range from $238.09 to $517.29, highlighting the need for locally grounded data in planning and budgeting. The programmatic scale‐up scenario suggests potential cost reductions, notably through bulk purchasing and centralised procurement, though additional data are needed to confirm these projections.

Strength and Limitations

This study presents several strengths. A micro‐costing approach was adopted with costs disaggregated by stakeholders (including households) and intervention components. The pragmatic design of the trial allowed estimates to reflect routine conditions, enhancing relevance for budget planning. The use of a societal perspective provided a comprehensive view of both provider and household costs. To our knowledge, this is the first cost analysis of SAM outpatient treatment in Senegal, supported by a sample size allowing robust estimates.

However, this study also has several limitations. Expert assumptions were used to estimate the life span of materials, staff travel distance and RUTF operational costs, due to data gaps. Opportunity costs for households and community volunteers were derived using the proxy good methods, based on income data from the harmonised survey of household living conditions in Senegal (Sénégal ‐ Enquête Harmonisée sur les Conditions de Vie des Ménages, 2021). This may have led to overestimated income losses, particularly in a context where informal and pastoralist livelihoods are common. Further evidence is needed to better understand household‐level costs, especially opportunity costs, which are critical to assess the longer‐term economic burden of SAM.

Another limitation is the fact that the cost that existing furniture and buildings costs were not included in the analysis, which may have led to underestimated costs. However, as both components (standard SAM treatment and WASH component) relied on the same existing structure, this have not influenced the ratio between them.

Lastly, economies of scale could not be rigorously projected, as future price and quantity dynamics in real‐world implementation remain uncertain.

Further Research

The TISA data collection ended in July 2023. A cost‐effectiveness analysis will be possible once trial results on health outcomes become available, and if they demonstrate statistically significant difference between arms. This analysis would be particularly useful to inform policy decisions. Further studies should also address key limitations identified in this study, notably the estimation of important nonmedical costs for households and important opportunity costs for community volunteers. In particular, collecting more granular data on households' incomes levels and sources, especially in settings with a high prevalence of informal labour, would enhance the accuracy of economic evaluations and inform the long‐term financial impact on vulnerable populations.

Future research should also build on existing literature on the economics of WASH interventions in low‐resource settings and explore how WASH integrating into SAM treatment may help reduce healthcare burden. For instance, a hygiene promotion intervention for cholera prevention in Bangladesh, showed potential for reducing long‐term cost and improving outcomes (George et al. 2020) highlighting the relevance of similar approaches in other contexts.

Potential Scale Up of the TISA Intervention and Future Planning

In the event of a national scale‐up, the financial and operational sustainability of the WASH component must be carefully considered. For example, integrating at‐home hygiene sensitisation visits by community volunteers would likely require a shift in the type of compensation they perceive; from ad‐hoc project‐based compensation (with expense reimbursements) to a more formalised employment model with appropriate remuneration, as recommended by the WHO (World Health Organization 2018). While this evolution may increase provider costs, it is critical for ensuring long‐term implementation.

At scale, UNICEF would likely support the most cost‐intensive components (namely the RUTF and the WASH kit) raising important questions regarding the feasibility and budget resilience in a context of fluctuating donor support.

According to Senegal's national health information system, 31,206 cases of uncomplicated SAM were admitted to health posts in 2021 (Systeme d'information sanitaire, n. d.). Applying the TISA's cost estimates, scaling‐up the intervention would imply an additional cost of $2.5 to $5 million per year for institutional stakeholders (under the best and worst‐case scenarios). These costs must be weighed against the potential cost savings induced by reduced complications and improved health outcomes. For instance, an evaluation conducted in 2020 in the same area (Wassonguema et al. 2024) found that hospital referrals for SAM complications such as diarrhoea (targeted by the WASH kit) cost an average of 2021 int $452 per hospitalisation, including $205 in out‐of‐pocket expenses for households. Thus, by reducing the incidence of diarrhoea during SAM treatment, the WASH component may offer economic and health returns for both the health system and vulnerable households.

With appropriate contextualisation, these findings could inform WASH‐in‐NUT strategies in other Sahelian countries with similar treatment protocols. UNICEF and other regional stakeholders could leverage this cost projections to better anticipate the implications of integrated approaches such as the TISA.

Conclusion

Integrating a WASH component into the standard treatment for uncomplicated SAM in Senegal would result in a 45% increase in costs, or an additional $105.32 per child treated, with minimal additional financial burden on households. This study provides a comprehensive and context‐specific estimate of the impact of incorporating a scalable WASH component into the SAM treatment protocol. These findings are valuable for budget planning and health policy decisions, particularly in cost‐effectiveness assessments and in guiding the scale‐up of similar interventions in Senegal and other Sahelian contexts. Policymakers, donors and implementing actors can use these results to optimise resource allocation and maximise the impact of SAM treatment.
