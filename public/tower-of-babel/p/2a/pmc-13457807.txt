Comparative Evaluation of Tensile Bond Strength of Three Different Glass Ionomer Cements in Silver Diamine Fluoride Treated Carious Dentin: An In Vitro Study
Authors: Anagha Sujith, Parvathy Girija, Jyoti S Issac, Dilip Arjun Shirli, Krishna Balraj, Indulekshmi Mahesh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAim and backgroundEarly childhood caries (ECC) is one of the most prevalent and significant public health problems impacting pediatric populations across both developed and developing countries. Given its profound effects on both oral and general health, contemporary strategies emphasize prevention as the primary approach rather than focusing solely on treatment. Silver diamine fluoride (SDF) has emerged as an effective preventive agent due to its caries-arresting property, thus inhibiting further decay. Building on this, the silver-modified atraumatic restorative technique (SMART) incorporates the use of glass ionomer cement (GIC) following SDF application. This technique not only helps to mask the black staining associated with SDF but also promotes sustained fluoride release, thereby enhancing its therapeutic benefits. Although some studies have examined the bonding of resin cements and GICs to carious dentin treated with SDF, comparative analyses of different GICs in this context are still lacking. To address this, the study was done to evaluate and compare the tensile bond strength of three GICs—a Type IX, a resin-modified, and a bulk-fill glass hybrid variety, EQUIA Forte, with SDF-treated carious dentin.Materials and methodsA total of 54 freshly extracted, defect-free premolar teeth were collected for the study and stored in 10% formalin. The occlusal surfaces were flattened, and each tooth was embedded in an acrylic resin block. To simulate dentinal caries, an artificial lesion was induced using a 14-day pH-cycling protocol. The specimens were divided randomly into 3 groups (n = 18). The control group (group I) had tooth mounted with Type IX GIC following SDF application. Similarly, group II and group III were mounted with resin-modified glass ionomer cement (RMGIC) and EQUIA Forte, respectively, following SDF application. The completed specimens were stored in distilled water for 7 days and were tested for tensile bond strength using a universal testing machine. The bond failure at the restoration-tooth interface was recorded in MPa (megapascal).ResultsGroup III showed significantly higher mean tensile bond strength (2.61690 ± 0.31105 MPa), followed by group II (2.397 ± 0.117 MPa), and the least for group I (1.57843 ± 0.09221 MPa).ConclusionAmong the three tested groups, EQUIA Forte showed greater bond strength and retention to SDF-treated carious tooth than RMGIC and Type IX GIC. These findings suggest that EQUIA Forte can be effectively employed in carious primary teeth as part of the SMART, offering a reliable restorative option with improved clinical performance.Keywords: Bond strength, EQUIA Forte, Glass ionomer cement, Resin-modified glass ionomer cement, Silver diamine fluoride

Introduction

Dental caries continues to pose a major public health problem worldwide, impacting individuals young and old and often resulting in pain, infection, and poorer quality of life. Among children, ECC ranks as one of the most widespread chronic diseases.1 Management of dental caries in young, uncooperative patients and those with special health care needs (SHCNs) is particularly complex in pediatric dentistry. These patients often struggle with conventional dental procedures, making thorough caries removal and effective restoration more challenging. As a result, dental professionals must rely on tailored strategies and techniques to address their unique needs.2

Over the years, advancements in the understanding of caries pathology, along with the introduction of improved dental materials, have led to a shift in treatment philosophy—from a restorative approach to one focused on prevention and minimal intervention.3 This paradigm shift emphasizes caries arrest as an effective strategy for managing untreated dental caries, particularly in children and individuals with SHCNs who have limited tolerance for conventional restorative care.

Fluoride remains a cornerstone of preventive dentistry, with substantial evidence supporting its efficacy. When used at appropriate concentrations, the benefits of topical fluoride applications significantly outweigh potential risks. Among the various fluoride-based interventions, silver diamine fluoride (SDF) has emerged as an effective agent for caries management.4 SDF is a topical agent that integrates fluoride's remineralizing benefits with the antimicrobial action of silver. The American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (AAPD) advises applying 38% SDF twice annually for caries arrest on primary teeth.4 In addition to its ease of use and noninvasive nature, SDF has been shown to significantly reduce cariogenic biofilm formation and inhibit bacterial activity, making it effective in minimally invasive methods of caries management.3

Despite the clinical benefits of SDF, a major drawback is its tendency to cause permanent black staining of carious tooth.5 This staining effect may be esthetically unacceptable to some children and their caregivers, potentially limiting its acceptance. Potassium iodide (KI) application after SDF treatment has been shown to reduce the discoloration, thereby mitigating this concern.6 However, this additional step may adversely affect the retention of restorative materials.6,7

An alternative method to conceal SDF-induced staining is to apply an esthetic restorative material, such as GIC, over the treated site.3 This method has evolved to be known as the silver-modified atraumatic restorative technique (SMART), introduced by Alvear et al. (2016).8 The SMART technique incorporates the caries-arresting properties of SDF with the minimally invasive nature of the atraumatic restorative treatment (ART), offering an interim, aerosol-free restorative option that is especially advantageous in pediatric and special needs populations.9,10

However, prior application of SDF may influence the adhesive properties of restorative materials. The interaction of SDF with dentin facilitates the deposition of a silver phosphate layer, with silver ions penetrating and occluding the dentinal tubules. This can lead to partial or complete occlusion of the tubules, decreased permeability, and reduced bond strength of materials like GIC.11 Given that adequate adhesion is fundamental to the success of modern restorative procedures, evaluating the bond strength of GIC to SDF-treated dentin is of significant clinical relevance.12

This study aims to identify an optimal restorative material to be used in conjunction with the SMART approach, leveraging the combined benefits of glass ionomer cements (GIC) and SDF for the effective management of dental caries in children. There are limited literatures comparing the tensile bond strength of resin cements and GICs to SDF-treated carious dentin. Hence, this study aims to assess and compare the tensile bond strength of three GICs—a conventional Type IX GIC, resin-modified glass ionomer cement (RMGIC), and a bulk-fill glass hybrid cement (EQUIA Forte)—used as restorative materials after SDF treatment of carious dentin.

Materials and Methods

A total of 54 premolars extracted for orthodontic purposes were used in this study. Each tooth was carefully inspected, and those showing signs of enamel hypoplasia, surface cracks, or visible white spots were excluded. Ethical committe approval was given by institutional ethics committe (ECR/956/Inst/KL/2016). The eligible teeth were cleaned using ultrasonic scalers and then stored in a 10% formalin solution for 2 weeks, ensuring that the total storage time did not exceed 1 month.

The occlusal surfaces of the extracted premolars were precisely sectioned using a high-speed diamond disc under water to expose the underlying dentinal substrate. The exposed dentin was then polished under running water using abrasive paper to obtain a standardized flat surface (Fig. 1). To confine the test area, the tooth surfaces were coated with nail varnish, leaving only the prepared dentin surface exposed. Horizontal reference grooves were created on the radicular portion of each specimen to aid in handling. Finally, the teeth were mounted onto self-cure acrylic resin blocks to facilitate manipulation during testing procedures (Fig. 1).

Prepared tooth samples with exposed dentin and mounted to acrylic

Induction of Artificial Caries by pH Cycling

Artificial carious lesions in dentin were induced using a pH-cycling protocol.13 The specimens were immersed in a demineralizing solution composed of 2.2 mM CaCl2, 2.2 mM NaH2PO4, and 50 mM acetic acid (adjusted to pH 4.8 using a pH meter) for 8 hours, followed by immersion in a remineralizing solution containing 1.5 mM CaCl2, 0.9 mM NaH2PO4, and 0.15 M KCl (adjusted to pH 7.0) for 16 hours.13 This cycle was repeated daily for 14 days at room temperature, without agitation.13

Using a simple random sampling technique, all the samples were then divided into group I (Type IX GIC + SDF), group II (RMGIC + SDF), and group III (EQUIA Forte + SDF). Group I was kept as control.

Application of Silver Diamine Fluoride and Placement of Restoration

For group I, conditioning of the dentin surfaces was done using 10% polyacrylic acid for 20 seconds, followed by thorough rinsing and gentle air-drying (Fig. 2). Subsequently, 38% SDF was applied with a microbrush for 2 minutes and air-dried. A T-band matrix was secured around the tooth to contain the restorative material, and Type IX GIC was incrementally placed to achieve a 4 mm thickness. A 26-gauge ligature wire was made into a loop and embedded into the unset cement to facilitate tensile testing. After complete setting of the cement, the matrix band was removed. Identical procedures were followed for group II, utilizing activated EQUIA Forte capsules (Fig. 3), and for group III (Fig. 4). All SDF-treated and restored specimens were then immersed in distilled water and stored for 7 days prior to testing (Fig. 5).

Testing of Tensile Bond Strength

The samples were mounted onto a universal testing machine (INSTRON 3345) (Fig. 6) running at a crosshead speed of 5 mm/minute. Tensile load was applied by engaging the wire loop until bond failure occurred at the restoration interface. The tensile bond strength values were recorded in MPa (megapascal).

Final testing of samples for bond strength using UTM

Statistical Analysis

The data were statistically analyzed. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. The Shapiro–Wilk test was employed to assess the normality of the data. As the data did not follow a normal distribution, nonparametric tests were applied. Comparison of groups was performed using the Kruskal–Wallis ANOVA, followed by Dunn's post hoc test for pairwise group analysis. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Early childhood caries (ECC) is an extensive dental problem among young children, causing severe tooth decay and irreversible oral health challenges. The highest prevalence and severity have been reported in Far East Asia, with India exhibiting particularly high rates—no state reports an ECC prevalence below 40%. Such a consistently elevated prevalence underscores ECC as a critical public health concern that demands targeted preventive measures and resource prioritization.14

The current paradigm in caries management reflects a transition from traditional surgical treatment toward a broader medical model prioritizing preventive care.15 This paradigm shift incorporates the use of fluoride, chlorhexidine, and sealants as key preventive measures. In addition, adjunctive therapies—including xylitol, amorphous calcium phosphate, SDF, systemic fluoride supplementation, and behavior-based interventions—are increasingly recommended by clinicians as effective alternatives for caries prevention.15,16

SDF has gained recognition as a cost-effective, simple, and safe topical agent with well-documented cariostatic and preventive efficacy. The American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (AAPD) supports its use, especially in patients at high risk for caries and in cases where traditional treatment approaches are difficult to implement.17

SDF was first introduced in dentistry at a concentration of 38%. While a 12% formulation is also available commercially, studies have shown it to be less effective in halting dental caries in children and in suppressing Streptococcus mutans biofilm formation when compared to the 38% concentration, which has demonstrated superior performance over other fluoride agents like acidulated phosphate fluoride.18 An 18-month clinical trial found that application of 38% SDF twice a year was significantly more successful in arresting caries in the primary teeth of preschool-aged children than the 12% solution.19 Based on these findings, the current study used the 38% SDF formulation to enhance its caries-arresting effectiveness.

A major limitation of SDF treatment is the noticeable dark discoloration it causes on carious tooth tissue, which often raises concerns among parents and caregivers.20 To address this esthetic limitation, various materials have been investigated for their ability to mask SDF-induced discoloration. Potassium iodide (KI) application immediately following SDF has been shown to reduce discoloration without significantly compromising its caries-arresting efficacy.20 However, KI is contraindicated for patients receiving thyroid treatment or those with allergies to potassium or iodine, which restricts its use.20 These constraints underscore the need for alternative discoloration-masking strategies that maintain the therapeutic benefits of SDF while improving its acceptability, particularly in pediatric patients.

The “SMART” technique involves SDF application followed by the placement of an esthetic restorative material, such as GIC, after selective caries excavation. SMART has been shown to effectively arrest carious lesions, reducing the risk of irreversible pulpitis or secondary infections, and improves the esthetic outcome by masking the dark discoloration associated with SDF application.8

The longevity of dental restorations is significantly influenced by the strength of the bond between the restorative material and the underlying tooth structure. Pretreatment of enamel or dentin with specific conditioning agents can substantially affect adhesion performance. Studies have demonstrated that the application of SDF leads to the formation of silver phosphate and calcium fluoride precipitates on the tooth surface.21 Fluoride ions contribute to the remineralization process, which is associated with increased microhardness of carious dentin. This enhanced hardness may contribute to improved bonding efficacy of restorative materials.21

Application of SDF after acid etching has been demonstrated to enhance the bonding of GIC to carious dentin. This improvement is believed to result from SDF's capacity to infiltrate dentinal tubules and the collagen matrix, thereby increasing the mineral content of dentin and strengthening the substrate to facilitate better adhesion.22 Additionally, acid etching has been reported to facilitate greater fluoride uptake in demineralized dentin.23 Based on this evidence, 10% polyacrylic acid was used in the present study. Conversely, some research indicates that the silver granules precipitated on denatured collagen fibrils following SDF treatment may adversely affect the bonding interface, potentially leading to decreased retention of the restorative material.24

In restorative dentistry, the bond strength to dentin is a critical factor in ensuring the retention and durability of restorations. The efficacy of adhesive systems in bonding to dentin is typically evaluated through bond strength testing. Various methods exist for this purpose, including macroshear, microshear, macrotensile, and microtensile bond strength tests.25 The pull-out microtensile bond strength test was employed in this study owing to its simplicity and widespread use in assessing bonding characteristics in dental research.

In this study, the pull-out test for tensile bond strength was performed on a universal testing machine (INSTRON 3345, Fig. 6). A load of up to 2,000 N was applied at a steady crosshead speed of 5 mm/min until bond failure was observed. Although intraoral forces differ in magnitude, speed, and direction, the forces in this experiment were standardized in both speed and direction to ensure controlled and reproducible measurements.

The mean tensile bond strength ± standard deviation (SD) for group I (Type IX GIC + SDF) was 1.578 ± 0.092 MPa. Group II (RMGIC + SDF) and group III (EQUIA Forte + SDF) exhibited tensile bond strengths of 2.397 ± 0.117 MPa and 2.617 ± 0.311 MPa, respectively. Normality assessment using the Shapiro–Wilk test indicated that the data were not normally distributed (Table 1). Consequently, the Kruskal–Wallis ANOVA, a nonparametric test, was employed to compare tensile bond strengths among the groups (Table 2). The analysis revealed a statistically significant difference between the groups' mean tensile bond strengths (p < 0.05). Within this in vitro study, the glass hybrid GIC (EQUIA Forte, group III) demonstrated the highest tensile bond strength to SDF-treated artificial carious dentin compared to groups I and II.

EQUIA Forte is a highly filled, chemically cured GIC that incorporates a nano-filled resin surface sealant, which imparts self-adhesive qualities.26 Its adhesion occurs via a combined chemical and micromechanical interaction with hydroxyapatite.26 Prior research has shown that EQUIA Forte possesses significantly greater compressive strength than other GIC formulations.26,27 The nano-filled resin coating further enhances its resistance to mechanical forces, thereby improving durability. These properties are likely responsible for the superior bonding of EQUIA Forte to SDF-treated carious dentin observed in this study. Notably, limited prior research has evaluated the tensile bond strength of EQUIA Forte on SDF-treated carious surfaces, highlighting the novelty and significance of the present findings.

Wang et al. and Puwanawiroj et al. reported that the use of SDF does not markedly affect the retention of RMGIC.24,28 RMGIC is widely recommended for restoring primary molars due to its better bonding to the tooth and reduced moisture sensitivity compared to conventional GIC, resulting in lower solubility and enhanced durability. Similar to adhesive resins, RMGIC achieves adhesion through both chemical and micromechanical bonding. The silver uptake into dentinal tubules following SDF application may occlude dentinal fluid flow, thereby enhancing the micromechanical bonding potential of the resin component and improving bond strength.23 These characteristics likely contributed to the higher tensile bond strength observed in group II (RMGIC + SDF) compared to group I (Type IX GIC) in this study.

Conventional GICs, including Type IX, are commonly considered the preferred restorative materials in pediatric dentistry because of their straightforward handling, ability to chemically bond to dental tissues, and fluoride-releasing properties. Nonetheless, their application is limited by factors such as vulnerability to moisture contamination during early setting, extended setting and limited working time, low resistance to fracture, and decreased wear durability.29 These inherent material limitations may account for the comparatively lower tensile bond strength observed in group I (Type IX GIC + SDF) relative to the other groups in this study.

EQUIA Forte and RMGIC can be considered as the restorative materials in the SMART technique for primary teeth after SDF application, rather than Type IX GIC, because of their higher and better bond strength as shown in this study.

Limitations of the Study

The bonding of GIC to SDF-treated carious dentin cannot be generalized to primary teeth, which have lower microhardness compared to the permanent teeth used in this study.

Literature shows that artificial caries induction by microbial methods has more clinical relevance than the method of pH cycling.

This study was designed to approximate clinical conditions with a high degree of standardization. Nonetheless, due to inherent limitations in replicating the biological complexity of the oral environment, the findings may not fully reflect in vivo outcomes. Therefore, longitudinal in vivo investigations are essential to substantiate and translate these results into clinical practice.

Clinical Significance

SDF is a minimally invasive and preventive strategy for treating ECC. However, staining caused by SDF is a major parental concern. Various materials are used to mask this staining. Using a restorative material with better bond strength to mask this staining is of primary importance in primary teeth. Hence, identifying a restorative material with higher bond strength and more retention in primary teeth is crucial. In this study, EQUIA Forte was found to have the highest bond strength and retention to SDF-treated carious dentin.

Conclusion

Recent developments in dental biomaterials, validated by scientific research, have expanded their use in pediatric dentistry. To enhance their properties, GICs have undergone various modifications. EQUIA Forte, a product utilizing glass hybrid technology, is one such advanced form of GIC. SDF is considered one of the most minimally invasive and preventive approaches for managing ECC. However, parental concerns about SDF staining are paramount. To conceal these stains, various materials are used. For primary teeth, it is crucial to mask this staining using a restorative material with greater bond strength. The present study revealed that EQUIA Forte exhibited the highest bond strength and retention to SDF-treated carious dentin.
