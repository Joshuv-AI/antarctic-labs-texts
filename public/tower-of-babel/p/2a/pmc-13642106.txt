Reversible and Dynamic Tuning Mechanism for Fabry–Pérot Polaritonic Resonators
Authors: Zhiqiang Shi, Yongqian Zhao, Ying Liao, Rao Fu, Chen Ge, Shuai Feng, Jianing Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPolaritons are quasiparticles formed via light‐matter interaction (e.g., with electrons or phonons). They confine light to subwavelength scales, enhance electromagnetic fields in Fabry–Pérot polaritonic resonators, and tune cavity resonance via adjusting external parameters (light frequency or dielectric environment). Thus, we propose tuning Fabry–Pérot polaritonic resonators by modifying the dielectric environment through substrate phase transitions. We perform mid‐infrared nanoimaging of α‐MoO3 nanocavities on phase‐change VO2 at varying temperatures. At 25°C (VO2 insulating phase), adjusting incident light frequency tunes the highly sensitive Fabry–Pérot phononic polaritonic resonator order from 10 to 2; at 90°C (VO2 metallic phase), the order tunes from 7 to 2. With incident light fixed at 992 cm−1, the α‐MoO3 nanocavity/VO2 heterojunction undergoes a low‐high‐low temperature cycle, showing a maximum Fabry–Pérot resonance order change of 3, along with reversible tuning ability and delayed recovery. Simulations indicate higher‐order Fabry–Pérot resonant modes can be achieved in thinner and wider α‐MoO3 nanocavities. This method provides new means and experimental support for designing tunable Fabry–Pérot resonant devices.Keywords: Fabry–Pérot resonators, near‐field optics, phonon polaritons, polaritons

Introduction

In modern nanophotonics, polaritons have emerged as a focal point of cutting‐edge research due to their ability to confine light beyond the diffraction limit and endow optical fields with matter‐like properties [1, 2, 3]. Moreover, polaritons can significantly enhance the optical field intensity in structures such as Fabry–Pérot (F‐P) resonators [4, 5, 6], providing a powerful platform for manipulating light–matter interactions at deep‐subwavelength scales [7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. The dynamic tunability of polaritons also enables flexible control of F‐P cavity resonance. These features not only overcome the limitations of conventional optics but also open new possibilities for nanophotonic device design [12, 13, 14]. Methods for the dynamic tuning of polaritons have advanced rapidly. Notably, several approaches stand out: driving topological transitions of hybrid polaritons via modulation of the graphene Fermi level [15], controlling polariton dispersion by adjusting the doping level in monolayer graphene [16] and dynamically tuning polaritons in monolayer and twisted bilayer structures through back‐gate voltage to modify the graphene Fermi level [17]. In recent years, polariton F‐P resonators based on materials such as hBN and metallic single‐walled carbon nanotubes have been reported [11, 18, 19, 20]. However, the exploration of external physical tuning methods for dynamically adjusting the characteristics of F‐P resonators has been limited in these studies. Therefore, achieving dynamic tuning of polariton‐based F‐P resonators remains a highly challenging task in this research field.

α‐MoO3, a van der Waals material [21, 22, 23, 24], has three Reststrahlen bands in the mid‐infrared region. These bands include Band 1 (545–851 cm−1) along the [001] crystal direction (y‐axis), Band 2 (820–972 cm−1) along the [100] crystal direction (x‐axis), and Band 3 (958–1010 cm−1) along the [010] crystal direction (z‐axis). In the Band 3 region, phonon polaritons (PhPs) can propagate along the [001] and [100] crystal directions [25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30]. When α‐MoO3 is fabricated into a nanocavity, the PhPs resonance of α‐MoO3 manifests as F‐P optical resonance modes.

Vanadium dioxide (VO2), as a typical and promising temperature‐induced phase‐change material, exhibits a reversible temperature‐induced insulator‐metal transition (IMT), accompanied by abrupt and rapid changes in dielectric properties and optical properties [31, 32, 33, 34]. Therefore, when performing mid‐infrared nanoimaging on α‐MoO3 nanocavities spin‐coated onto the surface of VO2 films using a scattering‐type scanning near‐field optical microscope (s‐SNOM, Attocube neaspec GmbH) equipped with a quantum cascade laser (QCL, DRS Daylight Solutions), the temperature of the air/α‐MoO3 nanocavity/VO2 three‐layer heterojunction system was simultaneously regulated. By tuning the incident light frequency ω (989.5–998.5 cm−1), the F‐P resonance order of the α‐MoO3 nanocavity decreased from 10 to 2 at 25°C and from 7 to 2 at 90°C. When ω is fixed at 992 cm−1 and the entire α‐MoO3 nanocavity/VO2 heterojunction system undergoes a complete temperature cycling process, a maximum shift of 3 resonance orders can be observed; during this process, the F‐P cavity resonance order exhibits reversible tuning ability and a delayed recovery phenomenon. Both experiments and simulations confirmed that phase transition control of PhPs F‐P resonance is effective. The mechanism of precisely tuning the F‐P resonant mode of phonon polaritons in α‐MoO3 nanocavity through the VO2 IMT provides a new strategy for the tuning of polaritonic resonant cavities.

Basic Optical Characterization of α‐MoO3 Nanocavity

Figure 1a shows the configuration diagram of this experiment in which the α‐MoO3 nanocavity is transferred onto a 50‐nm thick VO2 film grown on a c‐Al2O3 substrate, and the morphology is given under a scanning electron microscope. We used a s‐SNOM, equipped with a mid‐infrared monochrome continuously adjustable QCL, to perform near‐field optical imaging of a single α‐MoO3 nanocavity on VO2 under normal pressure. When the atomic force microscopy (AFM) scans the sample below, the probe is irradiated with continuous mid‐infrared monochromatic light, and the scattered optical signal is collected. The incident light is converted by the metallic tip and reflected at both ends of the cavity, and then scattered by the tip again. The scattered signal contains information about PhPs and topography. Figure 1b,c shows polar plots of the Raman spectrum of the α‐MoO3 nanocavity from 50 to 1050 cm−1 and its polarization Raman peak intensity in A g (158 cm−1) mode. Since the Raman peak in A g (158 cm−1) mode originates from vibrations of transverse rigid chains along crystal directions, its intensity shows significant dependence on the polarization angle [35, 36, 37, 38]. It can be concluded from Figure 1c that peak intensity decreases as the angle increases between 0° and 90°, confirming that the long axis of α‐MoO3 nanocavity is collinear with its [001] crystal direction. The real part of the relative permittivity for α‐MoO3 depicted in Figure 1d shows three Reststrahlen bands: Band 1 (545–851 cm−1), Band 2 (820–972 cm−1), and Band 3 (958–1010 cm−1) [39]. Among these bands, Band 2 cannot support PhPs propagation along the [001] crystal direction and is therefore not considered here. Both Band 1 and Band 3 support PhPs propagation along [001], whereas PhPs in Band 3 exhibit a substantially higher sensitivity to the illumination frequency [21]. Therefore, the special dispersion behaviors of PhPs in Band 3 area are the basis for precisely tuning the F‐P cavity of α‐MoO3 nanocavity PhPs.

Imaging of Non‐Constant Temperature Nanocavity PhPs and Analysis of Their Dispersion

To investigate the F‐P cavity characteristics of α‐MoO3 nanocavity at different temperatures, we selected an α‐MoO3 nanocavity with dimensions of 1302 nm in length, 160 nm in width, and 40 nm in thickness on a VO2 substrate as the research object (Figure 2a,b). It is worth noting that, according to the previous report, even when the VO2 substrate is in the metallic phase at 92°C, PhPs in α‐MoO3 propagating along the [001] direction still exhibit a propagation trajectory of up to ∼1.5 μm, which further increases to ∼2.5 μm when VO2 is in the insulating phase [27]. In this work, the high‐quality α‐MoO3 nanocavities used have cavity lengths of 900 and 1302 nm, which are substantially shorter than the propagation trajectory of PhPs on the metallic‐state VO2 substrate. Therefore, whether at high or low temperatures, the F‐P resonances along the [001] direction are well satisfied. Consistent with this analysis, well‐defined F‐P resonance modes are indeed observed experimentally. At 25°C (VO2 insulating phase), the nanocavity exhibits high‐contrast F‐P cavity interference patterns along the [001] crystal direction (Figure 2c,e). By scanning the ω (from 989.5 to 998.5 cm−1), the number of bright spots j was observed to range from 9 at 989.5 cm−1 to 1 at 998.5 cm−1 (more details see Supporting Information S1: Figure S1a,c). At the highest observable F‐P resonance order (i.e., at 989.5 cm−1), the electromagnetic confinement capability of the α‐MoO3 nanocavity reaches 35.58 (λ0/λp). When the ω were 992, 993, and 994 cm−1, the numbers of bright spots j distributed along the [001] crystal direction were 7, 6, and 5, respectively; according to the F‐P cavity resonance order relation (n=j+1), the corresponding n were 8, 7, and 6, respectively. The results show that in the mid‐infrared band, only a 1 cm−1 tuning of the ω is required to achieve a single‐step change in the resonance order (Δn/Δω=1), highlighting the extremely high sensitivity of this system for controlling the F‐P cavity. We simulated the dispersion relation of PhPs in α‐MoO3 nanocavity along the [001] crystal direction to analyze the influence of VO2 at 25°C and 90°C on the F‐P cavity resonance. In Figure 3a,b, we extracted the wave vector q (q=2π/λp) values of PhPs at 25°C and 90°C, respectively, from Figure 2c,d. At the same ω, the q of the PhPs at 90°C is smaller than that at 25°C. However, within the scanning ω range of Band 3 at both 25°C and 90°C, the PhPs follow a trend: as the ω increases, the q of the PhPs decreases, and the resonance order decreases. To investigate the detailed tuning mechanisms of the F‐P resonator for PhPs during the IMT process of VO2, we maintained the ω at 992 cm−1 and performed temperature rise‐fall cycles on the α‐MoO3 nanocavity/VO2 heterojunction, collecting near‐field images of the PhPs. The temperature range of the cycle was from 25°C to 90°C (more details see Supporting Information S1: Figure S3). During the heating part, the number of bright spots j of the α‐MoO3 nanocavity PhPs decreased as the temperature rose: from 7 at 25°C to 4 at 90°C (as shown in Figure 4a). During the cooling part, the number of bright spots j of the α‐MoO3 nanocavity PhPs increased as the temperature dropped, recovering from 4 at 90°C to 7 at 25°C (as shown in Figure 4b). Similarly, the F‐P resonance order of the PhPs followed this trend. Next, we extracted the q‐values of the α‐MoO3 nanocavity PhPs at each temperature gradient (as shown in Figure 4c) and found that within the range of 30°C–90°C, the q‐values of the PhPs decreased as the system temperature increased; during the cooling process, however, the q‐values recovered to their original values as the system temperature decreased. The temperature‐dependent q curve does not overlap with that of the heating process, showing a certain degree of hysteresis. The temperature at which VO2 undergoes IMT is approximately 68°C. At this temperature, its dielectric properties undergo a drastic change [31, 43]. We can observe from Figure 4c that the q‐values of PhPs change most sensitively when approaching this temperature. The temperature sensitivity of PhPs and their hysteresis recovery characteristics during the rise‐fall process provide a new mechanism for tuning F‐P cavities.

The Influence of Cavity Size Variation on F‐P Resonance

Given the relatively uniform dimensions and limited variations of high‐quality nanocavities, we investigated the effect of size on the F‐P mode via numerical simulations. Firstly, we changed the width of the α‐MoO3 nanocavity while keeping the incident light wavenumber at 992 cm−1, the nanocavity thickness fixed at 40 nm, and the length fixed at 1302 nm. We simulated the optical imaging of α‐MoO3 nanocavities of different widths before and after the IMT of VO2. As shown in Figure 5a,c, under the conditions of VO2 at 25°C and 90°C, respectively, it is observed that the resonance order of the F‐P cavity increases with the increase of the width of the α‐MoO3 nanocavity, whereas the value of λp decreases. This indicates that the variation in cavity width does indeed have a tuning effect on the F‐P resonance of PhPs (more images are shown in Supporting Information S1: Figure S4). The left and right parts of the red dashed box correspond to the images at 992 cm−1 under the same experimental conditions in Supporting Information S1: Figure S1a and Figure 2d, respectively. It can be seen that the simulated near‐field images are highly consistent with the experimental images. This also confirms from the simulation that the IMT of VO2 regulates the F‐P resonance order of the PhPs in the α‐MoO3 nanocavity.

Subsequently, we fixed the width of the α‐MoO3 nanocavity at 160 nm, the length at 1302 nm, and the incident light wavenumber at 992 cm−1, and then conducted simulations by varying the thickness. As shown in Figure 5b,d, for VO2 at 25°C and 90°C, with the increase of cavity thickness, the resonance order decreases and the λp of PhPs becomes longer. The simulation results show that the variation in cavity thickness can also tune the F‐P resonance mode of PhPs (more images are shown in Supporting Information S1: Figure S5). For cavity length, if it is excessively long, F‐P resonances cannot be sustained. Therefore, we selected a 900‐nm nanocavity and found that, under identical conditions, its resonance order is lower than that of the 1302‐nm nanocavity, as shown in Supporting Information S1: Figure S7. Simulations of a series of nanocavities with different widths and thicknesses, as well as optical imaging of nanocavities of different lengths, indicate that the cavity size has a tuning effect on the F‐P mode. Moreover, provided that the F‐P resonance condition is satisfied, wider, thinner, and longer nanocavities support higher‐order F‐P resonance. This geometric size tuning phenomenon can be attributed to the different electromagnetic confinement capabilities of α‐MoO3 of different sizes [44, 45, 46, 47, 48]. This provides a specific structural optimization direction for the design of high‐performance nanophotonic devices.

Conclusion

In summary, we have developed an intriguing method: by constructing α‐MoO3 nanocavity/VO2 heterojunctions, we realized the dynamically reversible modulation of Fabry–Pérot resonance of phonon polaritons in α‐MoO3 nanocavities via the dielectric property changes induced by the phase transition of VO2. Specifically, by using infrared nano‐imaging technology, we obtained the optical images of Fabry–Pérot resonance of phonon polaritons in α‐MoO3 nanocavities and identify their high sensitivity and precise tuning range. We analyzed the existence and state of phonon polaritons in α‐MoO3 nanocavities as well as their dispersion relations under near‐field imaging, the state of phonon polaritons before and after the phase transition of VO2, and the tuning effect of the nanocavity geometry on the Fabry–Pérot resonance of phonon polaritons. Our findings demonstrate that the phase transition of VO2 and the variation of α‐MoO3 nanocavity geometry exert controllable and reversible tuning effects on the Fabry–Pérot resonance modes of α‐MoO3 nanocavities; meanwhile, VO2 serves as a reliable platform for the resonance modulation of phonon polaritons in α‐MoO3 nanocavities. This achievement opens up a reliable new avenue for the tuning of Fabry–Pérot polaritonic resonators.

Methods

Experimental Section

A diluted colloid solution of α‐MoO3 nanocavities was evenly deposited onto the surface of a 50–nm VO2/Al2O3 heterojunction, and their optimal size was characterized via AFM. Near‐field optical properties were investigated using a commercial s‐SNOM, which is based on AFM technology. The experiment employed a Pt/Ir probe (Arrow‐NCPt‐50, Nano World) with a tip radius of approximately 20 nm. This probe operated in tapping mode, featuring a resonance frequency of around 255 kHz and a vertical oscillation amplitude of 100 nm. The illumination source was a tunable QCL. Its p‐polarized monochromatic beam was collimated using a parabolic mirror and focused onto the interaction zone between the probe and the sample. To minimize far‐field background interference, pseudo‐heterodyne interferometric phase detection was utilized to capture the light scattered by the probe. Background suppression was achieved through demodulation at the third harmonic of the tapping frequency.

Numerical Simulation

A systematic numerical investigation of the electromagnetic‐field distribution in an air/α‐MoO3 nanocavity/VO2 three‐layer structure was performed using the finite‐element method (FEM) implemented in COMSOL Multiphysics (Wave Optics Module). In the model, air constituted the top layer, the α‐MoO3 nanocavity served as the middle layer (with thickness and width treated as variables), and VO2 acted as the bottom layer. The optical response of α‐MoO3 was described by a Lorentz model, with parameters detailed in Supporting Information S1: Note S1, while the relative permittivities of VO2 in its insulating and metallic phases are shown in Supporting Information S1: Figure S6. The simulation geometry is illustrated in Supporting Information S1: Figure S8. The incident wavenumber was set to 992 cm−1 and could be varied as required. The out‐of‐plane (z‐direction) electric‐field intensity was extracted at a height of 50 nm above the α‐MoO3 nanocavity. In addition, the dispersion relations of the α‐MoO3 nanocavity along the [001] direction, before and after the VO2 phase transition, were calculated by solving the complex refractive index of the air/α‐MoO3 nanocavity/VO2 system using the Fresnel formalism, as described in Supporting Information S1: Note S2. These numerical results provide quantitative insights into the mechanism by which the VO2 phase transition modulates the F‐P cavity.
