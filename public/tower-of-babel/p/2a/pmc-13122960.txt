Study on a drama-based Relational Empathy and Communication Training (REACT) to improve medical student empathy
Authors: Bernard A Landry-Wegener, Amanda Bertram, Andrew Pastides, Stephen M Sozio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmpathy is pivotal in healthcare with implications for patient outcomes and provider wellness. While the importance of empathy is widely accepted, there are no current best practices in how to develop medical trainees’ empathy. This study assessed the outcomes of a novel drama-based workshop – Relational Empathy And Communication Training (REACT) – in developing medical students’ empathy.MethodsA cohort of 10 first-year medical students elected to participate in a 1.5-hour workshop that was led by a theatre professional and used non-clinical drama-based acting and improvisation exercises. Learner satisfaction was assessed with a post-intervention survey. Learners self-assessed any change in their awareness of their physicality or confidence in their communication skills post-intervention. Of those that attended, a majority reported increased awareness of their physicality. Background

A healthcare provider’s empathy is a foundational element of their relationship with patients and leads to better plan adherence, patient satisfaction, and outcomes [1–3]. Increased empathy also curbs provider burnout and improves wellness [1]. Empathy requires a varied skill set of verbal and non-verbal communication skills to understand, communicate and act on patients’ perspective [4]. Unfortunately, physicians are still underperforming in this important domain [3]. Additionally, medical students may experience a decrease in empathy during medical school [5].

While the literature supports empathy as a skill that trainees can develop, the overall quality of the literature on the subject is low and there are no widely adopted best practices for empathy development in medical education [3]. One possible framework for empathy training draws on the skill set of the dramatic actor [6]. The use of acting and improvisation exercises (AIE) offer trainees an experiential learning environment outside of the clinical space to develop empathy through embodied learning experiences. AIE have learners align cognitive tasks with physical representation of emotion to develop awareness of the verbal and non-verbal embodied elements of interpersonal skills.

This approach shows promise with a majority of the studies included in a recent scoping review on the use of drama training to develop medical trainees interpersonal and communication skills (ICS) reporting an outcome of increased empathy [6]. This embodied approach is further supported by literature that shows active empathy training (e.g. role-play) improves trainee empathy more than discussion-based exercises [7]. Despite this, there are no widely accepted best practices on how to incorporate drama training into medical training. To address this knowledge gap, we aimed to assess the effects of a drama-based Relational Empathy and Communication Training (REACT) on first-year medical students’ empathy. We hypothesized that an interactive workshop using AIE would improve the participants’ empathy and would be acceptable to learners.

Methods

Study design

We conducted an exploratory pilot using a non-randomized, single group pre- and post-test to assess changes in first-year medical students’ empathy following participation in REACT. We also assessed learner satisfaction and acceptability using a post-intervention survey.

Setting and participants

Participants were first year medical students at the Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine. REACT was conducted on campus as an elective workshop outside of the structured course time. Attendees for the workshop were recruited from the 120-member first year cohort via printed flyers and emails. Participants for the study were recruited from the registered workshop attendees via a separate email correspondence with a description of the study and offered a financial incentive for participation. Students had the option to attend the workshop without participating in the study.

Educational intervention

REACT was a single 1.5-hour workshop with a curricular goal of promoting medical students’ awareness of their physicality and emotional narrative. A member of the theatrical community with both professional performance credits and experience in theatre education aided in the design and implementation of the intervention (A.P.). Learning objectives included defining empathy, recognizing the importance of non-verbal communication to empathy, and the ability to analyze body language [8].

REACT was based in experiential learning theory [9]. The Kolbs’ model conceptualizes learning as a cyclical process in which learners engage in a concrete experience, reflect on that experience, develop abstract conceptualizations, and then actively experiment with new approaches. Iterative movement through this cycle allows learners to refine their understanding and gradually shift behavior. AIE operationalize this framework by engaging learners in embodied activities, followed by structured debriefing and deliberate repetition, creating opportunities for reflection, conceptualization, and experimentation.

The first part of the intervention consisted of a 1-hour embodied learning experience in which participants engaged in several non-clinical AIE. The session began with physical and spatial awareness exercises, in which learners moved freely around the room while attending to architectural elements, and then applied the same attention to maintaining uninterrupted eye contact with a peer for several minutes. To promote active listening and rapid responsiveness, participants engaged in exercises requiring quick responses to verbal and non-verbal cues. One example was a “line game,” in which learners stood in a line and were assigned a numerical rank from right to left. The facilitator called on participants randomly by rank in rapid succession, and the corresponding learner had to immediately respond with a tongue twister stating their rank and calling on another participant. Delayed responses resulted in moving to the end of the line, requiring all participants to remain attentive to their new position.

The session culminated in a “card game,” in which each learner was given a playing card, the value of which assigned them a rank. Participants were unaware of their own rank but could see the ranks of others. As they moved around the room pretending to be at a party, they interacted with peers based on the visible rank displayed, adjusting their behavior accordingly. Each interaction required learners to continually recalibrate their perceived position within the power hierarchy of the room. The game ended with learners lining up in their perceived rank order, after which their actual rank was revealed to them. The debrief focused on how perceptions of power influence non-verbal communication. These exercises collectively emphasized relational awareness, embodied attention, and adaptive responsiveness, skills directly applicable to clinical communication.

Following the AIE, participants were provided with didactic learning on empathy and non-verbal communication in clinical communication. Through this final debrief learners conceptualized how the AIE related to clinical communication and made plans for future experimentation in their clinical learning environments.

Measures

Contextual data

Participants’ demographic data collected pre-intervention was limited to age and gender. This was due to a lack of publicly available data on the broader class for comparison. Learner’s satisfaction was assessed post-intervention with a novel satisfaction survey. The survey was created by B.LW. following coursework in survey design as part of a master’s program and using exemplar language from survey design literature. While the survey did not undergo piloting, careful consideration was placed on item creation and clarity. Questions on acceptability included learner perception of safety and comfort during the intervention. Questions on satisfaction included whether participants would want to have access to more educational experiences like REACT and if REACT should be included in the formal curriculum. The post-intervention survey asked participants to self-identify as extroverted or introverted.

Empathic orientation

A participant’s empathic orientation was assessed via the Jefferson Scale of Empathy (JSE) student version (JSE-S) [10]. The JSE is a 20-item rating scale rubric with evidence to support its validity in assessing medical professionals’ and trainees’ empathic orientation, corresponding with Kirkpatrick level 2a outcomes of attitudes (affective domain) [10, 11]. Medical students with higher scores on the JSE-S have been shown to demonstrate more empathetic behaviors in post-graduate training [12]. We worked with the JSE copyright holders for permission on the initial instrument, later independently modifying two items on the JSE-S using language from the JSE Health Professions Student version. These changes were limited to removing the words “medical” in one item to explore “treatment” more broadly and changing the phrase “medical and surgical treatment” to “treatment outcomes” in another item to focus more on the patient.

Procedures

Participants completed the pre-intervention survey including demographic information and the JSE-S one-week prior to the workshop. The satisfaction survey and post-intervention JSE-S were collected one-month post-intervention to reduce immediate post-session score inflation. This study was reviewed by the Johns Hopkins Medicine Institutional Review Board (IRB00377036) and was determined to be exempt from further review under U.S. Department of Health and Human Services regulations 45 CFR 46.104(d). The IRB waived the requirement for informed consent as the study met criteria for exemption.

Data analysis

Pre- and post-intervention scores on the JSE-S were compared via paired samples t-test. A one-way ANOVA was performed to determine if changes in JSE-S scores post-intervention differed between participants that identified as introverted versus extroverted. The satisfaction survey was analyzed with descriptive statistics. Data were analyzed using a standard statistical software package with a statistical significance determined at a p-value of < 0.05.

Results

Participants

Out of the 120 member first-year medical student cohort, 10 (8.3%) participated in the intervention. All of those that shared their gender identity were female (n = 9). The ages of participants ranged from 22 to 27 years old. Nine participants completed the post-intervention satisfaction survey. A majority of those participants self-identified as introverted (n = 5/9, 56%).

In terms of learners’ self-assessed outcomes, three (33%) participants reported REACT increased their confidence in their communication skills quite a bit, five (56%) reported somewhat increased confidence, and one (11%) reported no change in confidence. A majority (78%, n = 7) reported that REACT increased their awareness of their physicality quite a bit.

Empathy scores

Eight participants completed both the pre- and post-intervention JSE-S. There was a statistically significant increase in JSE-S scores post-intervention (114.6 ± 11.4, 119.0 ± 11.5, p < 0.005) as found in Table 1. There was no statistical difference in the JSE-S sub-scales. There was no significant influence of learner’s self-identification as either being introverted or extroverted on change in JSE-S score (F-statistic = 0.088, p-value = 0.78).

Role in the medical curriculum

All responding participants (n = 9) requested access to more educational interventions like REACT, with 78% (n = 7) responding that trainings like REACT should be incorporated into the curriculum for all medical students.

Discussion

We found that the use of non-clinical experiential acting and improvisation exercises (AIE) may have contributed to a statistically significant increase in participants’ empathy as assessed by the JSE-S. Despite the non-traditional nature of the intervention, participants felt comfortable and safe building their interpersonal and communication skills (ICS) using AIE.

Our findings add to the growing body of literature showing that drama-based instructional strategies like AIE are effective at increasing medical trainees’ empathy [6, 13, 14]. We addressed the previously identified need for more rigorous data by using the JSE-S, an assessment tool with evidence to support its validity in predicting empathic behaviors of medical trainees [6]. While participants did not self-report increased confidence in communication skills, the more objective data on empathy from the JSE-S strengthens the argument to more widely incorporate drama-based instruction into ICS training for all medical trainees.

This approach builds on conceptual parallels between physicians’ ICS and the competencies actors use to authentically portray human experience on stage [15]. To authentically embody a character, an actor must understand the character’s emotional narrative and cultivate awareness of their own physical presence. To express affect they must regulate non-verbal cues while remaining attuned and responsive to scene partners in real time. These competencies parallel the relational empathy, embodied non-verbal communication, and attentive listening required in clinical encounters. Framing ICS development through this embodied lens moves beyond reductionist checklist approaches by emphasizing presence, relational attunement, and physical expression in addition to verbal technique [16, 17]. This approach also stands to enrich medical schools’ community engagement by incorporating the local theatrical community into the planning and facilitation.

While all participants expressed a desire for more access to trainings like REACT and 78% believed that AIE should be part of the formal curriculum, the size and demographics of this cohort prevent generalizing these findings to all medical students. The gender identity of the participants was all female as compared to 58% for the class of 2027 [18]. This is especially relevant given recent studies showing gender identity is associated with differences in empathy outcomes depending on the instructional strategy employed [19]. Also, 92% of the class chose not to attend the intervention, their reasoning for which remains unknown. This trend may suggest limited acceptability of drama-based ICS training; however recruitment for the workshop relied on flyers and emails, which may have failed to reach interested students. Future studies should investigate the rationale behind non-attendees’ reluctance to engage in drama-based ICS trainings and examine strategies to better integrate drama-based interventions with the formal curriculum to more fully assess acceptability.

Our study has several other limitations. The lack of a control group limits the ability to attribute the increase in empathy solely to the intervention. The single site and small sample size limits generalizability. While the JSE-S has evidence to support its validity, it is subject to self-report bias and limits the scope of a complex construct. Finally, no long-term outcomes were collected to determine sustained change over time, which is important given medical students’ decline in empathy during training [5].

The findings from this exploratory pilot study provide preliminary support for incorporating drama-based instructional strategies into medical curricula. Participants generally responded positively, including those who self-identified as introverted, and there is suggestive evidence that the intervention may enhance empathic orientation. These results, while limited by sample size and non-randomized design, justify further investigation using larger, controlled studies to determine the impact of drama-based interventions on trainee empathy. Future outcomes assessments should consider longitudinal measures and incorporate patients’ perspectives to better understand how training translates into clinical practice. Ultimately, such research can inform best practices for integrating the medical humanities into curricula and for preparing empathic physicians ready to partner with their future patients.

Conclusion

Despite empathy being an important competency for practicing physicians, current medical training inadequately prepares physicians to relate to their patients with empathy. Drama-based ICS training offers a promising way to develop empathy in medical trainees, with the potential to lead to better patient outcomes and provider wellness. Medical educators should partner with their local artistic community to design and implement multi-site randomized control trials to determine the generalizability and best practices of these innovative arts-based instruction. This will ensure future physicians are equipped with the empathy needed for competent medical care.
