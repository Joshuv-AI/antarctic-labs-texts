An image recognition agorithm for fine-grained high-frequency workpieces based on a multi-branch network architecture
Authors: Jiaqi Deng, Chenglong Sun, Jiajie Lin, Yichen He, Yipeng Yin, Qinlu Pu, Xu Yang, Liangzhong Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To address the low recognition accuracy of high-frequency workpiece images caused by complex intra-class textures and minor inter-class differences in top-surface features, we propose a Multi-Branch EfficientNet (MBEN) algorithm for recognizing fine-grained high-frequency workpieces. First, a weakly supervised region detection module is used to obtain discriminative regional images of the workpieces, which are then combined with global images to construct a multi-branch network that enhances the model’s multi-scale representational capacity. Next, by incorporating a weight-adjustment mechanism, we implement joint supervision using an adaptive cross-entropy loss and an adversarial center loss to guide the model toward intra-class compactness and inter-class separability of workpiece features. Finally, a branch fusion module is employed to augment the network’s attention to both global and local information, yielding improved fine-grained recognition performance. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed algorithm effectively discriminates fine-grained high-frequency workpieces and outperforms existing models and methods in recognition accuracy, achieving an accuracy of 98.75%.

Introduction

Manufacturing serves as a fundamental pillar of global economic development and plays a vital role in promoting employment and stimulating consumption. With the ongoing transformation and upgrading of global manufacturing toward high-quality development1, automation of processing technologies and the intelligence of industrial production have been substantially enhanced2. Among these advances, research on image recognition for high-frequency workpieces has become a core element driving progress in intelligent manufacturing. This technology not only facilitates automation, intelligence, and unmanned operation of production processes—thereby supporting the transition of manufacturing toward higher value—but also addresses the cost challenges associated with small-batch, multi-batch production, enabling more efficient resource allocation and improved production efficiency worldwide.

Classification and sorting of high-frequency workpieces constitute a typical scenario in intelligent manufacturing. As image recognition technologies mature and their practical applications expand, visual recognition methods are widely adopted to improve the efficiency and accuracy of high-frequency workpiece sorting. Image recognition is an interdisciplinary research area that integrates theories and methods from computer science, mathematics, image processing, and pattern recognition. By converting digital images into structured representations, image recognition systems automatically identify and analyze key elements such as objects, scenes, and characters. As a cutting-edge information processing technology, image recognition can extract target features from complex scenes and perform classification and inference based on those features. In recent years, advances in computational power and algorithmic innovation have accelerated the development of image recognition. While template-matching methods remain valuable in specific scenarios due to their simplicity and efficiency, deep learning–based convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have become the mainstream approach, demonstrating powerful feature-learning capabilities and strong generalization performance. Progress in these areas has driven wide application of image recognition in face recognition3, vehicle recognition4, autonomous driving5, security surveillance6, medical image analysis7, image retrieval8, and image processing9, injecting new momentum into social and technological development.

At present, image recognition of high-frequency workpieces faces several challenges: (1) intra-class textures can be highly complex; (2) different types of workpieces are often highly similar, with discriminative local regions occupying only a small portion of the image; and (3) image acquisition yields large pose variations. Consequently, the classification of high-frequency workpieces belongs to the domain of Fine-Grained Visual Categorization (FGVC)10. Fine-grained classification aims for more detailed categorization than conventional classification tasks—for example, distinguishing between different bird species rather than grouping all birds together—and consequently demands higher accuracy. FGVC has become a research hotspot in computer vision, with numerous researchers developing methods and techniques that have achieved significant progress.

Based on the strength of supervisory information available during training, fine-grained classification methods can be categorized into strongly supervised and weakly supervised approaches. Both classes introduce additional supervisory information concerning local discriminative regions, using the degree of annotated data to distinguish between specific fine-grained categories.

(1) Strongly supervised fine-grained classification assumes availability of detailed annotations for each image category during training, enabling direct training and evaluation of models. Some researchers in this direction have proposed novel feature-extraction methods such as deep convolutional neural networks (DCNN)11 and improved classification algorithms like Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)12 networks to better capture image details and improve classification accuracy. Methods based on strong supervision13,14 directly utilize manually annotated discriminative regions to train classification models, but they suffer from high annotation costs and substantial subjectivity.

However, obtaining ample strongly supervised annotations is often challenging in practice15. Therefore, weakly supervised fine-grained classification methods, which require only partial or coarse annotations, have emerged.

(2) Weakly supervised fine-grained classification leverages incomplete, imprecise, or noisy annotations. Researchers have proposed various approaches in this domain, for instance employing Generative Adversarial Networks (GAN)16 to synthesize pseudo-annotated data for training. Other studies employ clustering17 and data preprocessing18 to tackle weak supervision. Methods that rely solely19,20 on image-level labels construct network architectures that automatically attend to local discriminative regions, thereby avoiding the limitations of manual annotation; such weakly supervised methods have become mainstream in FGVC. Recent works have used Spatial Transformer Networks (STN)21,22 to provide weak supervision for local discriminative regions, effectively boosting classification performance. STNs enable networks to learn spatial transformations of input samples—such as scaling and cropping—that emphasize discriminative regions. Specifically, document23 proposed a spatial-transformation-based bilinear network for fine-grained fish image classification, where spatial transformation automatically localizes discriminative regions within the image and the bilinear network enhances the feature responses of local regions. This approach demonstrates outstanding performance in addressing the challenges of fine-grained fish classification; however, the model suffers from high computational cost and memory consumption, and spatial transformation may lead to information loss. In the field of remote sensing image classification, document24 introduced a network structure capable of simultaneously learning transformations in both geometric and spectral domains. The transformations correct geometric distortions and spectral aberrations in multispectral remote sensing imagery, significantly improving classification performance; however, the effectiveness of the model relies heavily on the quality and quantity of the dataset.

To address the problem of low recognition accuracy caused by complex intra-class textures and small differences in top-surface features among fine-grained high-frequency workpieces, this paper proposes a Multi-Branch EfficientNet (MBEN) algorithm. The algorithm adopts the foundational network Efficient-b025 and achieves improved fine-grained recognition results by integrating weakly supervised region detection module, loss-enhancement module, and branch-fusion module.

The main contributions of this article are as follows. We enhance multi-scale representations so the model can capture and learn both global and local discriminative features of workpieces. The proposed algorithm

Overall framework

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the proposed MBEN model is built upon EfficientNet-B0 and principally comprises three components: a weakly supervised region-detection module, a loss-augmentation module, and a branch-fusion module. The weakly supervised region-detection module constructs a local branch which is operated in parallel with a global branch, thereby strengthening the workpiece’s multi-scale representational capacity. MBEN extracts local and global discriminative features respectively from the discriminative-region image and the entire image, employs the loss-augmentation module to enhance the network’s ability to discriminate between easily confusable workpieces, and then uses the branch-fusion module to guide the model toward an effective balance in capturing global and local workpiece features.

Weakly supervised region-detection module

The weakly supervised region-detection module consists of two mechanisms: detection-localization and cropping. The detection-localization mechanism locates regions in high-frequency workpiece images that exhibit salient differences; the cropping mechanism then extracts local workpiece images from those regions and supplies them as inputs to the local branch, enabling the global and local branches to complement one another and improving the expressiveness of image features.

The detection-localization mechanism can be decomposed into four steps: channel attention, energy map aggregation, energy map structuring, and class-greedy boundary search, as shown in Fig. 2. First, multi-layer feature maps of the global workpiece image are produced by the EfficientNet-B0 backbone; these multi-layer feature maps are aggregated to form an energy map. The energy map is then decomposed along width and height into two structural vectors. Finally, class-greedy boundary search produces width boundary coordinates and height boundary coordinates that are used to crop the global image and yield the final local image.

Loss-augmentation module

The loss-augmentation module consists of two algorithms: self-adaption cross-entropy loss and contrastive-center loss27. These algorithms are improvements upon the cross-entropy loss function and the center loss function28, respectively.

The cross-entropy loss function computes the loss independently for each workpiece sample, without considering the impact of sample classes on network training. However, in the practical task of fine-grained high-frequency workpiece image recognition, different categories of samples often present varying levels of recognition difficulty. To help the model focus more on easily confused workpieces and improve overall network recognition performance, we propose an adaptive cross-entropy algorithm. The adaptive cross-entropy loss algorithm introduces a weight-adjustment mechanism into the loss computation, which automatically adjusts the contribution of each class’s loss based on its recognition difficulty. In Eq. (8), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${p_{{y_i}}}$$\end{document} represents the adaptive factor, which is initialized to 1 and, based on empirical practice, is set to the recognition accuracy of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${y_i}$$\end{document}-th class on the validation dataset after 15 training epochs. By setting the weight of each sample as the reciprocal of its class’s recognition accuracy and continuously updating it, the network is encouraged to focus on learning those workpiece classes that are more prone to confusion.

Considering the characteristics of fine-grained high-frequency workpieces, the discriminative features should exhibit intra-class compactness and inter-class separability. However, the center loss function only penalizes large intra-class distances without considering inter-class separability, leading to smaller distances between features of different classes. To address this issue, we propose the contrastive-center loss algorithm, which reduces intra-class distances for workpiece features while increasing inter-class distances. The feature centers are initialized using Xavier initialization and updated with an exponential moving average (EMA) strategy to avoid numerical instability. Branch-fusion module

To exploit both global and local information of workpiece images and to balance the relative contribution of these two information sources in recognition, a branch-fusion module is designed to fuse the outputs of the two branches and thereby further improve recognition accuracy. To more accurately extract workpiece features at different scales, the two channel-attention modules do not share parameters. Experimental results and analysis

Experimental dataset

The experimental images were acquired from high-frequency workpieces produced by an enterprise, collected under uncontrolled lighting conditions. A total of 3,600 high-frequency workpiece images were selected to form the experimental dataset, which includes extreme lighting scenarios and covers 20 workpiece categories. Using data augmentation techniques such as geometric transformations and random cropping, the dataset was expanded by a factor of two. After augmentation, each category contains 360 images, with each image having a resolution of 3822 × 2702 pixels. The augmented dataset was randomly divided into training, validation, and test sets in a ratio of 5:2:3. The detailed composition of the dataset is shown in Table 1.

Fine-grained high-frequency workpieces are characterized by complex internal textures and small feature differences between different types. Loss function comparison experiment

The loss function is a key factor guiding model optimization. To analyze the impact of the loss-augmentation module on model performance, we conducted a comparison experiment with four different loss functions: cross-entropy loss, adaptive cross-entropy loss, contrastive-center loss, and the joint loss. The experiments were conducted under the same environment, using the same model (MBEN) and parameter settings. Figure 5 shows the experimental results, where different categories of high-frequency workpieces are distinguished by different colors or shapes.

From the 2D deep feature distribution plots, the following observations can be made:Compared to cross-entropy loss, adaptive cross-entropy loss effectively increases the inter-class distance of high-frequency workpiece features. However, the feature vectors of the same class are more dispersed, resulting in lower intra-class compactness. Compared to cross-entropy loss, contrastive-center loss reduces the intra-class distance of high-frequency workpiece features, but the feature vectors of different classes remain dispersed. Some features of easily confused workpieces still show lower inter-class separability. The joint loss, which combines the characteristics of adaptive cross-entropy loss and contrastive-center loss, more effectively gathers feature vectors from the same category of workpieces and increases the inter-class distance between similar workpieces. During training, both losses complement each other, and as intra-class compactness and inter-class separability improve, the model’s ability to distinguish fine-grained high-frequency workpiece features is enhanced. Therefore, the selection of the joint loss function more effectively supervises and optimizes the model, leading to improved classification performance.

Since the performance of deep learning models is closely related to the data distribution and sample imbalance across different categories, this study did not consider potential data imbalance in the actual production process when applying the algorithm to fine-grained high-frequency workpiece image recognition. Future research will need to address the classification of high-frequency workpieces under conditions of data imbalance.

Conclusion

This paper addresses the problem of low recognition accuracy in high-frequency workpiece images, which is caused by complex intra-class textures and minimal differences in top-surface features. We propose a high-frequency workpiece image recognition algorithm (MBEN) that integrates a multi-branch network structure. First, a weakly supervised region-detection module is used to construct a multi-branch network, enhancing the model’s multi-scale feature representation. Next, we design an adaptive cross-entropy loss algorithm and a contrastive-center loss algorithm to promote intra-class compactness and inter-class separability for high-frequency workpiece features. Then, the branch-fusion module is employed to guide the model in effectively combining global and local information from the high-frequency workpieces. Finally, through model performance comparison experiments, module ablation experiments, attention visualization experiments, loss function comparison experiments, and model parameter selection experiments, we demonstrate that MBEN outperforms traditional algorithms in fine-grained high-frequency workpiece image recognition tasks.
