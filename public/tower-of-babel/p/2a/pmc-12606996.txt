A retrospective analysis of phase I and phase I/II investigator-initiated clinical trials conducted by the Swiss Group for Clinical Cancer Research (SAKK)
Authors: K-L Koster, I Colombo, S Hayoz, K Eckhardt, A Rojas Mora, D Hess, C Britschgi, S Haefliger, M Imbimbo, F Bertoni, K Homicsko, R von Moos, C Sessa, M Joerger, A Stathis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhase I trials represent the initial step in the clinical evaluation of a new drug or new drug combination. The main characteristics and designs of phase I trials have changed throughout the years to better support the development of drugs with different specificities compared with traditional chemotherapy.Patients and methodsWe reviewed early-stage investigator-initiated clinical trials conducted by the Swiss Group for Clinical Cancer Research (SAKK) within the last 20 years at a patient and clinical trial level. For efficacy analysis, patients were grouped into advanced/metastatic solid tumors, nonadvanced solid tumors, and lymphoma.ResultsThe study included 295 patients from 20 clinical trials. Systemic drug combinations were the most common treatment modality (39.3%), followed by multimodal treatments (29.8%) and monotherapy (24.7%). Dose-limiting toxicities were observed in 8.8% of patients [95% confidence interval (CI) 5.8% to 12.6%]. Grade ≥3 adverse events occurred in 60.3% of patients (95% CI 54.5% to 66.0%), most frequently in those receiving combinations (74.1%). No treatment-related deaths were observed. In advanced solid tumors, the overall response rate was 25.8% (95% CI 18.4% to 34.4%), with a median progression-free survival of 5.8 months (95% CI 4.8-8.0 months) and an overall survival of 1.5 years (95% CI 1.2-2.2 years). Nonadvanced solid tumors demonstrated a median progression-free survival of 78.8 months, while overall survival was not reached (NR) (95% CI 62.3-NR months). The overall response rate in lymphoma patients was 81.3% (95% CI 67.4% to 91.1%), with a median progression-free survival of 30.8 months and an overall survival was NR (95% CI 10.8-NR months).ConclusionThese findings highlight the safety profile, efficacy outcomes, and diversity of treatments in SAKK-conducted phase I trials, providing valuable insights into the investigator-initiated early clinical trials landscape in Switzerland.Key words: phase I investigator-initiated trials, safety and efficacy outcomes, solid tumors and lymphoma

Introduction

Phase I clinical trials represent the first step in the clinical evaluation of a new anticancer drug or new drug combinations.1,2 The designs and characteristics of phase I trials have been subject to changes over the last years due to the introduction of drugs with novel mechanisms of action compared with traditional chemotherapy, such as targeted therapies, immune checkpoint inhibitors, antibody–drug conjugates, and, more recently, bispecific antibodies and cellular therapies.3,4

If phase I trials were previously seen as mainly pharmacology studies to study tolerability and pharmacokinetics with very low expected response rates, several reports now show that this scenario has changed, and significant antitumor activity can be observed for patients enrolled in phase I trials.5, 6, 7, 8 This observation, coupled with an improved knowledge of the biology of cancer and the availability of better preclinical models and, therefore, identifying biomarkers of response, may support the inclusion of patients in phase I trials at earlier lines of therapy. This would be different from the more traditional approach, which consisted in including patients in phase I trials only at the later stages of their disease, when all prior anticancer therapies had failed.7,8

Another change concerns developing larger and more complex clinical trials, which often incorporate phase I/II designs, multiple expansion cohorts, dose optimization, and adaptive designs, to identify early activity signals and fast-track the drug development process.5,6 Finally, for many nonchemotherapy agents and more recently for antibody–drug conjugates, it has become evident that identifying the optimal dose (which is the main objective of phase I trials) is challenging, and this will require additional changes in phase I trial designs, as has been suggested by several recent works.9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16

The Project Group Developmental Therapeutics (former New Anticancer Drugs group) of the Swiss Group for Clinical Cancer Research (SAKK) is dedicated to developing and conducting early-phase clinical trials through a network of phase I centers in Switzerland. We reviewed early clinical trials conducted by SAKK over two decades, focusing on trial designs, patient characteristics, and outcomes.

Patients and methods

Study population

The data comprised phase I and phase I/II clinical trials initiated by SAKK between January 2001 and December 2021. For phase I/II clinical trials, only data from the phase I part of the trial were included.

Patient and disease characteristics

For each trial, baseline patient and disease characteristics were collected, including age, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status (PS), laboratory results, tumor type, tumor stage, and presence or absence of liver metastases at the time of inclusion. For hematological malignancies, the stage was defined as advanced for stage IV disease. For efficacy outcomes, we divided patients in all trials into three groups: patients with solid tumors in nonadvanced settings, patients with solid tumors in advanced settings, and patients with lymphoma. Data were extracted directly from the electronic data capture systems of the participating clinical trials, where information had been prospectively collected according to protocol-defined procedures.

Safety and efficacy endpoints

The definition of dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs) varied between trials; hence, we collected data on whether a DLT—as defined by the protocol—occurred on a trial basis. In addition, the type of DLT (hematological and/or nonhematological) was collected from each trial.

The occurrence of grade ≥3 adverse events (AEs) was defined as at least one AE of grade ≥3 according to Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) 3, 4, or 5, depending on the study. Further, the number of AEs of grade ≥3 was counted for each patient.

Standard response criteria were used depending on the tumor entity, including Cheson, Lugano, Dworak, Mandard, and RECIST.

Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the time from the registration date in the trial to the date of disease progression or the patient’s death, whichever occurred first. Patients who did not experience any event were censored at the last date the patient was known to be alive.

Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from the registration date of the trial to the date of the patient’s death. Patients who did not experience an event were censored at the last date the patient was known to be alive.

Statistical analysis

All time-to-event endpoints were analyzed using univariable and multivariable mixed-effects Cox regression models, and the hazard ratio, along with a 95% confidence interval (CI), is presented. The Kaplan–Meier method was used for the visualization of time-to-event endpoints.

Univariable and multivariable mixed-effects logistic regression models were used to assess the influence of the variables on binary endpoints, and the odds ratio along with the 95% CI is presented. Because of the heterogeneity between trials, mixed-effects models were applied with the trial as a random intercept for all analyses.

If a variable could not be fitted into the models, it was presented in the tables. Because of the heterogeneity of efficacy across different cohorts in our dataset, efficacy analyses were carried out separately for solid tumor patients in an advanced setting, solid tumor patients in a nonadvanced setting, and lymphoma patients. No pooled analyses were conducted for efficacy endpoints.

Given the exploratory nature of this analysis, P values were not corrected for multiple testing and must thus be interpreted with caution. Because of the heterogeneity of data availability across trials, summary statistics for categorical variables present some missing observations, and the percentages were calculated for the available population (denoted as n).

All the analyses were conducted in SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) and R version 4.2.2 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria).

Study protocol and ethical considerations

Approval from the Ethics Committee of Ticino was obtained before starting data analysis (BASEC No. 2022-02208).

Results

Types of trials

A total of 20 (10 phase I and 10 phase I/II) trials conducted between January 2001 and December 2021 were considered (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105876). Of these, 4 (20%) trials included patients with different tumor types, while 16 (80%) were disease-specific. Thirteen trials included patients with solid tumors, and seven included only patients with hematological malignancies (five specifically designed for lymphoma patients).

Baseline patient characteristics

A total of 312 patients were enrolled in the aforementioned phase I or phase I portion of the phase I/II trials. Seven patients were not eligible for the primary analysis in their respective trials, and ten patients participating in international trials and treated outside Switzerland were also excluded, given that the protocol for this analysis was approved only in Switzerland. After these exclusions, 295 patients were included in our study.

Baseline patient characteristics are presented in Table 1. The median age was 64 years (range 31-88 years), the female-to-male ratio was 131 (44.4%)/164 (55.6%), and the ECOG PS was 0-1 in 287 (97.6%) patients. Among patients with solid tumors (n = 215), 135 were treated in trials in an advanced setting and 80 in a nonadvanced setting (adjuvant or neoadjuvant). Among patients treated in trials in the advanced setting (locally advanced or metastatic), 78 (57.8%) had metastatic disease; among them, 57 had liver metastases.

Treatment patterns

A total of 116 (39.3%) patients received systemic drug combinations, while 73 (24.7%) received systemic monotherapy; 88 (29.8%) patients received multimodal treatment, 12 (4.1%) patients received radiotherapy as the sole therapy, and 6 (2.0%) had a local drug administration.

Among patients enrolled in the advanced setting (n = 135), 41 (30.4%) were treated in the first-line for their advanced disease. A first-line treatment was also investigated in 29 (48.3%) of the 60 patients with lymphoma. A multimodal treatment was employed in most patients (n = 74, 92.5%) in the adjuvant or neoadjuvant setting. In the advanced solid tumor setting, 73 (54.1%) patients were treated with a systemic drug monotherapy, followed by 36 (26.7%) treated with a systemic drug combination. All patients with lymphoma were treated with systemic drug combinations. Treatment patterns by cohort are reported in Table 3.

Safety results

As many as 26 (8.8%) patients (95% CI 5.8% to 12.6%) experienced a DLT, most frequently nonhematological in 20 (76.9%) patients; 5 (19.2%) patients had hematological DLTs, and only 1 (3.8%) had both.

In total, 178 (60.3%) patients experienced at least one grade ≥3 AE (95% CI 54.5% to 66.0%). With regard to treatment pattern, the emergence of grade ≥3 AEs was more frequent in patients treated with systemic drug combinations, 86 of 116 (74.1%; 95% CI 65.2% to 81.8%), followed by patients treated with multimodal treatments (53/88, 60.2%, patients; 95% CI 49.2% to 70.5%) and patients treated with one systemic drug (32/73, 43.8%, patients; 95% CI 32.2% to 55.9%). The number of patients experiencing at least one grade ≥3 AE was 105 out of 195 (53.8%; 95% CI 46.6% to 61.0%) among patients with ECOG PS 0 and 66 out of 92 (71.7%; 95% CI 61.4% to 80.6%) among patients with ECOG PS 1. The median number of unique AEs grade ≥3 per patient was 1 (range 0-8).

Overall, 72 (24.4%; 95% CI 19.6% to 29.7%) patients experienced a dose reduction due to AEs. A total of 161 (54.6%; 95% CI 48.7% to 60.4%) patients were registered as discontinued from their respective trial: 83 (51.6%) were discontinued due to progressive disease, followed by unacceptable toxicity in 19 (11.8%) patients (Table 4).

Reasons for treatment discontinuation

The factors significantly associated with grade ≥3 AEs in the univariable analysis in all patients included indication, type of treatment, and ECOG PS. However, none of these factors maintained significance in multivariable analysis (Supplementary Tables S2 and S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105876).

Efficacy results

Efficacy results are reported according to the three main cohorts of patients: patients with solid tumors participating in trials in the advanced setting, patients with solid tumors enrolled in nonadvanced setting trials, and patients with lymphoma. Because of the heterogeneity of response criteria in the trials for patients with solid tumors in the nonadvanced setting, we report response rates only for the categories of patients with solid tumors in the advanced setting and for lymphoma. Results are presented with a median follow-up time after registration of 1.5 years (95% CI 1.0-2.3 years) for all patients, 0.7 years (95% CI 0.5-1.1 years) for advanced setting solid tumor patients, 3.3 years (95% CI 3.1-3.7 years) for nonadvanced setting solid tumor patients, and 0.8 years (95% CI 0.5-2.2 years) for lymphoma patients.

Solid tumors in the advanced setting

In total, 11 out of 135 patients with solid tumors in the advanced setting had missing information regarding response to treatment. The overall response rate was 25.8% (95% CI 18.4% to 34.4%).

The median PFS was 5.8 months (95% CI 4.8-8.0 months). Age at registration was significantly associated with PFS in both univariable and multivariable analyses (Supplementary Tables S4 and S5 and Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105876) with a hazard ratio of 0.73, indicating longer PFS with increasing age.

Patients with solid tumors in an advanced setting had a median OS of 1.5 years (95% CI 1.2-2.2 years). The type of therapy, hemoglobin at baseline, neutrophil counts at baseline, and prior surgery were correlated with OS in univariable analysis (Supplementary Tables S6 and S7 and Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105876).

Solid tumors in the nonadvanced setting

Patients with solid tumors in a nonadvanced setting had a median PFS of 78.8 months [95% CI 62.3-not reached (NR) months]. Age at registration (in both univariable and multivariable analyses) and ECOG PS (in univariable analysis) were significantly associated with PFS (Supplementary Tables S8 and S9 and Figure S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105876). The median OS was NR (95% CI 3.0-NR years). ECOG PS was the only factor significantly associated with OS in univariable and multivariable analyses (Supplementary Tables S10 and S11 and Figure S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105876).

Patients with lymphoma

In total, 12 out of the 60 lymphoma patients had missing information regarding the response. These patients were excluded from these analyses based on the 48 patients with available data. The overall response rate was 81.3% (95% CI 67.4% to 91.1%). Patients with lymphoma had a median PFS of 30.8 months (95% CI 10.8-NR months). ECOG PS (in both univariable and multivariable analyses) and hemoglobin value at baseline (in the univariable analysis) were significantly associated with PFS (Supplementary Tables S12 and S13, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105876). The median OS was NR (95% CI 3.0-NR years). Hemoglobin at baseline was significantly correlated with OS in the univariable but not in the multivariable analysis (Supplementary Tables S14 and S15 and Figure S5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105876). A schematic overview of the included patients in the three categories is reported in the Supplementary Figure S6, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105876.

Discussion

This is the first and only retrospective analysis of phase I and phase I parts of phase I/II clinical trials conducted by the SAKK from 2001 to 2021, involving 295 patients across 20 trials. We have included all eligible SAKK phase I or phase I/II trials conducted within the investigated time frame. The study aimed to describe the phase I trial landscape in Switzerland, focusing on investigator-initiated trials conducted by SAKK. A predominance of disease-specific studies reflects the SAKK research strategy in the past two decades, which has mainly focused on investigator-initiated trials in selected tumor types. Patients were categorized into three groups: advanced solid tumors, nonadvanced solid tumors, and lymphoma. A limitation of our study is that the available information was restricted to the variables prespecified in the trial protocols, and additional clinically relevant data may not have been captured. By contrast, data were prospectively collected within clinical trial databases, ensuring standardized procedures and a reduced risk of missing or inconsistent information.

The treatment modality most investigated within the reviewed trials was systemic drug combinations. The observed DLTs were predominantly nonhematological, and no treatment-related deaths were observed. Grade ≥3 AEs occurred most frequently in patients receiving drug combinations. The prevalent reason for treatment discontinuation was disease progression. The longest median PFS was seen in patients with nonadvanced solid tumors. OS was NR in patients with nonadvanced solid tumors and lymphoma. The best overall response rate was achieved in patients with lymphoma.

We analyzed baseline patient, disease, laboratory, and type of therapy characteristics and correlated them with outcomes (both safety and efficacy). Type of therapy, hemoglobin at baseline, neutrophil counts at baseline, and prior surgery were significantly associated with OS in patients with solid tumors in the advanced setting. In patients with solid tumors in the nonadvanced setting, ECOG PS was significantly associated with OS in univariable and multivariable analyses, while in patients with lymphoma, hemoglobin at baseline was significantly associated with OS in an univariable analysis. Similar studies from larger series have evaluated baseline characteristics correlating with OS in patients participating in phase I trials.17, 18, 19, 20, 21 Given the heterogeneity of diseases and trials and the small number of patients considered for each category of our study, no firm conclusions can be drawn. In addition, our findings based on univariate analyses were conducted with an exploratory intent, and P values may overestimate the effect and should therefore be interpreted with caution.

With regard to clinical activity, phase I trials have historically been associated with low objective response rates (ORRs), often below 5%.22, 23, 24 For example, in 1986, Estey et al. reported a median phase I response rate of 4.3% for drugs that later showed activity in phase II trials.22 Similarly, Decoster et al.23 and Von Hoff and Turner24 reported ORRs of 4.5% and 6%, respectively.

In recent decades, these rates have markedly improved. Horstmann et al.5 analyzed 460 trials conducted between 1991 and 2002, reporting an ORR of 10.6%. More recently, Chihara et al.7 analyzed 465 phase I protocols undertaken between 2000 and 2019, reporting an ORR of 12.2% (95% CI 11.5% to 12.8%). Importantly, this study demonstrated an increase in ORR from 9.6% (95% CI 8.7% to 10.6%) during 2000-2005 to 18% (95% CI 15.7% to 20.5%) during 2013-2019. These findings suggest that recently investigated drugs are more effective than those tested earlier. The increased efficacy of drugs in phase I trials is partly attributed to advances in biomarker-driven strategies. Trials employing genomic biomarkers for patient selection have demonstrated improved response rates and PFS compared with non-biomarker-based selection strategies.25,26 This trend is observed not only in solid tumors, but also in hematologic malignancies.8,27 Our study found higher response rates in patients with solid tumors in the advanced setting and patients with lymphoma than in the recent larger retrospective series. However, the reported response rates and survival outcomes should be interpreted with caution and may primarily reflect the experience of our academic group, rather than being generalizable to the broader outcomes of early clinical trials. Indeed, the population of patients included in our study is not only heterogeneous due to the variety of trials included, but it also includes more patients treated in first-line and in disease-specific phase I trials. These factors may have contributed to the high response rates observed.

While these advances underscore the growing efficacy of novel agents, they also highlight the need for methodological adaptations in phase I trial design to properly evaluate them.

Developing new, more effective drugs has introduced phase I trial design challenges.9, 10, 11, 12 Traditional phase I trials often recommend a single phase II dose based solely on toxicity data.4 The Methodology for the Development of Innovative Cancer Therapies (MDICT) Taskforce guidelines, updated in 2022, advocate for dose ranges rather than single-dose recommendations. These guidelines also emphasize the need to consider population-specific dosing requirements.9 In addition, new drugs may be tested in earlier lines of therapy, including the perioperative and neoadjuvant setting. Sophisticated designs for early signal-seeking trials in the neoadjuvant setting are critical to improve cure rates while minimizing patient harm.28

Conclusions

There is growing evidence that newly developed drugs tested in phase I trials have become an increasingly efficient and also safe treatment alternative for patients with cancer. We provide a comprehensive insight into early-phase investigator-initiated trials conducted through the SAKK network in Switzerland, reporting their safety and growing efficacy. For specific cancer types, phase I trials now demonstrate response rates significantly exceeding the historical 5%. As treatments become increasingly personalized and biomarker-driven, we anticipate further improvements. The continued evolution of trial designs will be essential to maximize the potential of innovative therapies while ensuring patient safety.
