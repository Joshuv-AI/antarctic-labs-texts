Long-term outcomes of endoscopic pilonidal sinus treatment: A single tertiary centre experience
Authors: Lakshay Singla, Ramya Kalaiselvan, Ajai Samad, Michael Chadwick, Rajaganeshan Rajasundram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Pilonidal sinus disease commonly affects young adults and presents a clinical challenge due to high recurrence rates. This study aims to evaluate long-term outcomes following Endoscopic Pilonidal Sinus Treatment (EPSIT) performed at a UK district general hospital.Patients and Methods:We conducted a retrospective observational study of all adult patients who underwent EPSIT by a single colorectal surgeon between January 2015 and May 2022. Patients with acute abscesses or previous extensive surgery were excluded. Outcomes included healing rate, recurrence and need for further intervention, assessed through structured clinical follow-up and telephone review until March 2024.Results:Eighty-seven patients (62 men, median age: 31 years) underwent EPSIT. Twenty-one procedures were performed under local anaesthesia. The median follow-up was 62 months (range: 24–108). Short-term healing was achieved in 70 patients (84%), with 13 requiring additional intervention (repeat EPSIT or flap). At long-term follow-up, 52 patients were fully evaluated. Of these, 41 (79%) achieved durable healing after a single EPSIT. Six had recurrence and were managed conservatively, and four patients await surgery. Twenty-five patients were lost to follow-up. If assuming the best-case scenario (all healed), the overall long-term success rate would be 76%; worst-case scenario (none healed) gives 47%. Healing was confirmed clinically or through patient self-report of complete resolution of discharge and symptoms.Conclusion:EPSIT is a minimally invasive approach with promising long-term results when carefully selected patients are treated by trained surgeons. However, high attrition rates limit interpretation. Larger comparative studies with standardised outcome definitions and inclusion of patient-reported outcomes (pain and cosmesis) are required.

INTRODUCTION

Pilonidal sinus disease (PSD) is a chronic inflammatory condition that predominantly affects the sacrococcygeal region in young adults, particularly males. It is thought to arise from the penetration of hair and debris into the skin of the natal cleft, triggering a foreign body reaction.[1] PSD can lead to persistent discharge, pain, local infection and impaired quality of life. The condition has a reported incidence of approximately 26 per 100,000 population, with a male predominance of more than 2:1.[2]

Management of PSD remains controversial. Traditional treatments have included wide excision with secondary healing, primary midline or off-midline closure and various flap techniques. However, these procedures often result in significant post-operative discomfort, prolonged healing and time away from work or education. Minimally invasive techniques, such as pit picking, the Gips procedure, phenol injection and laser therapy, have gained popularity in recent years for their potential to reduce morbidity and improve recovery.[34]

Endoscopic pilonidal sinus treatment (EPSIT) is a video-assisted minimally invasive technique first described by Meinero et al. in 2014. It involves the endoscopic identification and removal of hair and granulation tissue from sinus tracts under direct vision, with simultaneous cauterisation of the tract.[5] Preliminary studies have shown EPSIT to offer advantages such as minimal post-operative pain, superior cosmetic results and lower recurrence rates.[67] However, most published series have limited follow-up durations, making long-term effectiveness uncertain.

In the UK, efforts to standardise management of PSD have led to initiatives like the Pilonidal sinus Treatment studying the Options (PITSTOP) study, which emphasises the need for evidence-based selection of surgical strategies tailored to disease complexity and patient preferences.[8] Despite this, long-term outcome data for EPSIT from UK-based cohorts remain scarce.

The objective of this study is to assess the long-term outcomes of patients undergoing EPSIT at a single UK centre, with particular attention to healing rates, recurrence and the need for subsequent interventions.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

A retrospective observational study was conducted at a single UK district general hospital. All consecutive adult patients (age >18 years) diagnosed with primary or recurrent PSD who underwent EPSIT between January 2015 and May 2022 were included. Patients with acute pilonidal abscesses, previous extensive surgical excision or significant tissue loss and scarring were excluded. Patient selection was based on shared decision-making following clinical assessment by a single-experienced colorectal surgeon.

EPSIT was performed under local or general anaesthesia depending on sinus complexity and patient preference. The Meinero fistuloscope was used to visualise the sinus cavity, remove hair and debris, perform thermal ablation and achieve haemostasis. Laser depilation was offered selectively based on natal cleft hair density.

Data were extracted from a maintained electronic database and included age, gender, smoking status, diabetes, anaesthesia type, operative time and need for further treatment. All patients received standardised post-operative instructions and analgesia, with no requirement for packing. Patients were advised on hygiene and physical activity and referred for laser depilation when appropriate.

Follow-up occurred at 6 weeks, 6 months and annually through in-person visits or structured telephone interviews. Healing was defined as complete resolution of symptoms (absence of discharge and pain), confirmed either clinically or through patient report. Recurrence was defined as the return of symptoms after initial healing. No formalised pain scoring or cosmetic assessment was performed.

Outcome data were collected up to 30 March 2024. The primary endpoints were short- and long-term healing rates, recurrence and need for further surgical intervention (repeat EPSIT or flap surgery). Patients lost to follow-up were documented, and sensitivity analysis was performed to assess best- and worst-case scenarios for long-term outcomes.

Technique of the procedure

The procedure can be performed under general anaesthesia (complex pilonidal disease or patient preference) or local anaesthesia (simple sinus disease).

RESULTS

A total of 87 patients underwent EPSIT between January 2015 and May 2022. The cohort included 62 men and 25 women with a median age of 31 years (range: 19–60 years). Twelve patients were smokers, and one had type 2 diabetes. Most patients were American Society of Anesthesiologists Grade I (n = 59), followed by Grade II (n = 25) and Grade III (n = 3). Procedures were performed as day cases, with 21 conducted under local anaesthesia and 66 under general anaesthesia. The mean procedural time was 34 min [Table 1 and Figure 4].

Follow-up

Short-term outcomes

Follow-up at a median of 6 months showed that 70 of 87 patients (84%) had achieved complete primary healing. Thirteen patients did not achieve initial healing: three underwent repeat EPSIT and healed, while 10 were referred to plastic surgery for flap reconstruction. Four patients were lost to short-term follow-up [Figures 5 and 6].

Long-term outcomes

By March 2024, 52 patients had complete long-term follow-up data (median duration: 62 months; range: 24–108 months). Among these, 41 (79%) had durable healing after a single EPSIT. Four of the initial failures underwent repeat EPSIT, with successful outcomes. Seven patients underwent flap surgery, five of whom achieved complete healing; two were lost to subsequent follow-up. Six patients experienced recurrence after initial healing. These were managed conservatively with hygiene measures and occasional antibiotics; four patients await further surgical intervention. Twenty-five patients were lost to long-term follow-up [Table 2 and Figures 7 and 8].

DISCUSSION

The present study demonstrates that EPSIT can achieve acceptable long-term outcomes in a selected cohort of patients. With a short-term healing rate of 84% and long-term durable healing of 47% using EPSIT alone, our findings support the use of minimally invasive approaches in appropriate cases. When adjunctive interventions such as repeat EPSIT or flap reconstruction were included, the long-term healing rate improved to 60% assuming none of the patients lost to follow-ups healed and 88% assuming all the lost to follow-ups healed.

Our results are slightly lower than those reported in several published EPSIT series, which cite healing rates between 70% and 90% at medium-term follow-up.[9] Giarratano et al. reported a long-term success rate of 92% following EPSIT, with repeat procedures being effective for recurrence.[10] Similarly, Ouali demonstrated rapid wound healing with 95% epithelialisation by 3 weeks and recurrence as low as 3.6% at 6 months.[11] Variability across studies may be explained by differences in patient selection, surgical expertise, length of follow-up and definition of treatment success. In contrast to some studies which focus on primary cases only, our cohort included tertiary referral of recurrent and previously treated patients from hospital around the region, which may partially explain the discrepancy. Nevertheless, our recurrence rates are comparable to other international reports, suggesting the reproducibility of this technique.

When compared with conventional surgical approaches, EPSIT offers favourable outcomes in terms of morbidity and recurrence. Traditional midline excision is associated with delayed wound healing and long-term recurrence rates of up to 67.9%.[12] Flap reconstructions, such as Limberg and Karydakis techniques, reduce the recurrence rates to 0%–5%[1314] and are associated with a low risk of surgical site infections and wound dehiscence when compared to primary midline closure.[15] In contrast, EPSIT provides a minimally invasive, repeatable option with lower perioperative morbidity and faster recovery, though it may not be universally curative.[16]

Repeat EPSIT in our cohort yielded satisfactory outcomes, aligning with prior literature suggesting that minimally invasive techniques can be effectively repeated in select patients.[101117] However, the requirement for flap surgery in 10 patients (11%) and additional recurrences in 6 patients reinforce that EPSIT may not be curative in all cases – particularly where sinus complexity, previous surgical scarring or chronic inflammation are present. This aligns with PITSTOP study in the UK, which advocate for tailoring treatment based on individual pathology, patient characteristics and shared decision-making.[18]

Loss to long-term follow-up (29%) remains a major limitation. As noted in other audits and the PITSTOP study, maintaining engagement with this young, mobile patient population can be difficult.[18] Although sensitivity analyses were conducted, these losses introduce uncertainty in interpreting the long-term effectiveness of EPSIT. Future studies should consider incorporating digital follow-up platforms or routine patient-reported outcome measures to improve data capture.

Other limitations of our study include its retrospective, single-centre design and the lack of a comparator arm. A further limitation is the lack of systematic assessment of pain, aesthetic outcome, time to return to normal activity and quality of life. While prior studies have reported favourable outcomes in these domains with EPSIT,[910111217] our data do not support these outcomes due to a lack of formal measurement. Randomised controlled trials or prospective registries incorporating these endpoints are essential to validate the broader benefits of EPSIT over conventional excisional or flap-based procedures.

The current findings should also be interpreted considering evolving surgical practice. In the UK, interest in tissue-sparing techniques such as pit picking, phenol application and laser ablation is growing. Recent UK data indicate that around 40% of pilonidal procedures are now performed using minimally invasive techniques such as pit picking, EPSIT or laser ablation, although wider uptake is limited by training availability and surgical habit.[19] However, standardisation of treatment algorithms remains lacking. EPSIT, with its enhanced visualisation, may offer a more comprehensive solution for select patients compared to blind or semi-blind approaches.[918] However, the cost of instrumentation and lack of widespread training opportunities remain barriers to widespread adoption.

CONCLUSION

EPSIT represents a promising minimally invasive option for the treatment of PSD, particularly in primary or recurrent cases without extensive tissue loss. In this long-term single-centre series, we demonstrate acceptable healing rates with a low complication profile and feasibility as a day-case procedure. Repeat EPSIT can offer success in cases of initial failure, delaying or avoiding more morbid interventions. However, success is influenced by careful patient selection, surgical expertise and structured follow-up. Given the limitations of retrospective data and follow-up loss, future prospective studies with comparator arms and inclusion of patient-centred outcomes are needed to establish EPSIT’s place within the broader surgical armamentarium for PSD.
