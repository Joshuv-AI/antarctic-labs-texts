Midwives’ perspectives on preventing unsafe abortion: a qualitative study
Authors: Leila Bozorgian, Solmaz Mohammadi, Firoozeh Nourimand, Mahmood Maniati, Marzieh Araban
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnsafe abortion poses severe risks to women’s health and well-being worldwide. In Iran, implementation of restrictive abortion rules in 2022 has intensified concerns about a potential rise in unsafe practices. This shift is likely to introduce new challenges for midwives, who are often the first point of contact in healthcare. Midwives are frequently the first healthcare professionals women consult when seeking abortion or post-abortion care, whether the services are legal or illegal. Thus, this study was conducted to explore midwives’ views on unsafe abortion.MethodsThis qualitative study was conducted in Yasuj, Iran, from 23 December 2024 to 29 April 2025. The participants were 14 midwives with practical experience in the region. Data were gathered from in-depth, semi-structured individual interviews with participants selected through purposive sampling. Data analysis involved conventional content analysis approach. Overall, 14 interviews with 14 midwives were done. Data were analyzed by MAXQDA. The trustworthiness was established.ResultsThe interview analysis revealed 4 main categories and 11 sub-categories: 1.“policy development” including subcategories: implementing a policy for caregivers and clients,” “re-orientation in legal abortion policies,” “implementing policy for clients”, and “re-orientation of the Family Planning Program, 2.“risk communication strategies” including subcategories like “providing risk education to clients and caregivers”, 3.“media education on parenting” including subcategories like “responsibility of couples and adherence to values”, and 4. “supportive approach” including sub-categories like “economic and social support”.ConclusionMidwives in this study advocated for revising restrictive abortion laws, expanding family planning programs, and creating clear clinical guidelines. They emphasized educating women about the dangers of unsafe procedures and the media’s role in disseminating accurate reproductive health information. In Iran’s sociocultural context, these themes guide strategies for preventing unsafe abortion and enhancing reproductive healthpromotion. The study calls for a holistic, rights-based, and woman-centered approach to reproductive health, positioning midwives as essential contributors to policy and program development. Only with such integrated strategies can we achieve sustainable improvements in women’s health and broader public health outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13406-0.Keywords: Midwives, Prevention, Unsafe abortion, Illegal abortion, Reproductive health, Iran, Qualitative research

Background

Unsafe abortion and global context

The WHO defines unsafe abortion as a method of terminating an unwanted pregnancy that is performed either by people who lack the necessary skills in an environment that does not meet minimum medical standards or both [1]. Unsafe abortion has been identified as the main cause of maternal mortality [2]. It is estimated that about 22 million unsafe abortions occur worldwide each year [3], and 97% of these occur in developing countries [4] like Ethiopia [5]. This rise in intentional abortion, in turn, poses risks to the health of both the family and society [6, 7].

Iran’s renewed focus on raising the birth rate could result in restrictions on contraception and abortion, potentially leading to a rise in unsafe abortions [8]. According to a 2024 study, the number of abortions performed annually in Iran ranges from 300,000 to 800,000 [9]. Although there is no accurate report of unsafe abortion incidents in Iran, global statistics show that about half of the abortions that occur annually in the world are unsafe [10]. Abortion has destructive effects on the health status of the family and affects the physical, emotional, and social health of women and their families [11, 12].

Complexity of abortion in healthcare settings

In Iran, abortion is a complicated medical issue with overlapping ethical, legal, and clinical aspects that have a massive impact on women’s rights and health. In 2021, the Family and Youth Protection Law was passed to increase the population in Iran. The law includes provisions for punishing abortion service providers and introduces legal concerns regarding restrictions on free access to family planning services [13]. Disagreements regarding women’s autonomy and the value of life, often framed as the pro-life versus pro-choice debate, constitute a significant ethical dilemma [14]. Additionally, the law bans permanent sterilization for both men and women and prohibits the free distribution of contraceptives, the implantation of contraceptive devices, and even the promotion of their use [15]. Clinical professionals frequently struggle to guarantee access to safe and effective care due to systemic challenges such as stigma, inadequate funding, and the lack of comprehensive reproductive health education [16]. These intertwined factors create a complex problem that impacts individual women, as well as the wider Iranian healthcare system and society.

While various aspects of abortion care have been discussed in different contexts, research consistently highlights the significant difficulties faced by healthcare providers. For instance, systematic reviews indicate that nurses and midwives often encounter stringent challenges in providing abortion-related care, frequently complicated by their personal beliefs and resource limitations [17, 18]. These reviews further reveal that nurses and midwives express a clear need for support to enhance their mental well-being and develop their professional capabilities in this area [19].

The role of midwives is particularly crucial, as they often serve as the first point of contact for women seeking reproductive health services [20]. Providers experiences highlight the need for better support and training to navigate the complexities of abortion care effectively [21], which points to the need for a multifaceted approach to training, legal reform, and support systems within abortion care [22].

In Iran and after implementing new restrict regulations regarding abortion in the year 2022, which allows for therapeutic abortion only when the mother’s life is in danger or there is a congenital abnormality incompatible with life before the 19th week of pregnancy [10]. This shift is likely to present midwives with new challenges like legal ambiguity, patient counseling, referral limitations, and alter their perceived needs concerning abortion and reproductive care [23]. Consequently, further research is essential to understand the implications of these new regulations and their perceived effects on abortion care within this context.

Despite the widespread implementation of safe motherhood programs in Iran, which include services to mothers during pre-conception, pregnancy, childbirth, and postpartum periods [24], abortion remains a critical reproductive health issue, continually presenting significant ethical and legal challenges [25]. Midwives, as essential frontline members of the healthcare team, are pivotal in women’s reproductive health, pregnancy, and abortion programs [26]. They also play a key role in informing and preparing women to make life-saving decisions [27].

Qualitative research offers unique insights into individuals’ attitudes and experiences. Given midwives’ crucial role in educating women about the complications and risks of unsafe abortion, this study aims to explore their perceptions toward unsafe abortions, thereby addressing a fundamental gap in the existing literature. Identifying the challenges and needs that midwife encounter in this specific setting can directly inform policy and practice, leading to improved reproductive health outcomes. Furthermore, considering the inherently controversial nature of this topic within religious and cultural contexts, this study highlights the imperative for further research and action to advance reproductive health equity globally.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a qualitative descriptive design, conducted from 23 December 2024 to 29 April 2025. Data were analyzed using conventional content analysis, an inductive approach particularly suited when existing theory or research on a phenomenon is limited. This method allowed for categories to emerge directly from the data rather than being predefined [28].

Participants and setting

Participants, who were recruited for individual, in-depth, semi-structured interviews, were midwives with private practices in Yasuj, southern Iran. All interviews (which included 14 interviews with 14 midwives) were conducted by the first author.

The list of midwives was obtained from medical council organization and local health care system. The lead researcher first obtained the addresses of midwives with offices by visiting the office of the Vice President of Medical Affairs at Yasuj University of Medical Sciences. From this list, participants were selected using purposive sampling. To make sure our sample was representative of the population of interest, we specifically targeted people with varying backgrounds and places of employment. The lead researcher made a formal phone call to invite the participants. Based on the participants’ and researcher’s agreement, a more convenient location was selected for the study. Inclusion criteria included: midwives with a practice experience of 5 years or more, willingness to participate in the study, and ability to provide rich and sufficient information on the subject under study.

Data collection

Appointments were scheduled for individual face-to-face interviews at times and locations convenient for each participant. All interviews were conducted in the midwives’ private offices in a quiet environment. All participants were assured that their information would remain confidential. The interviews began with general questions and progressed to more in-depth inquiries based on participants’ responses and the study’s objectives. Before the interviews, all participants completed a demographic questionnaire, and the interviews were recorded with their consent. An interview guide (additional file 1) was specifically designed for the study, starting with an open-ended question: “Could you share your experiences and perspectives as a midwife regarding the topic of unsafe abortion? What factors do you believe influence your views on this sensitive issue?” During the interviews, probing questions such as “Explain more,” “Make your point clearer,” and “Give an example” were asked to encourage the participant and gain more in-depth information. An interview guide was developed following a comprehensive literature review and consultation with an expert panel. Each interview lasted 45–60 minutes. After 14 interviews, data collection was terminated once saturation was achieved. Three additional interviews were conducted to confirm that no new information was being generated.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using MAXQDA software (version 10; VERBI GmbH, Berlin, Germany). We employed inductive data analysis concurrently with data collection, using the conventional content analysis method developed by Elo and Kyngäs [29]. It was done as follows:

To be more specific, we transcribed each interview verbatim immediately after it was conducted. Then, we repeatedly read the transcripts to identify and label “meaning units,” which were coded directly from the raw data. These codes were then classified based on their conceptual and semantic similarities, with categories and sub-categories being continuously compared until the main categories were finalized [30].

It is worth noting that coding was done by two authors separately, and then the results were shared. In case of any disagreement in coding, the results were provided to the research team, and disagreements were resolved through in-person meetings.

Trustworthiness

The initial coding was conducted by the first author who periodically met with the research team to discuss and review the coding scheme and its synthesis. To ensure data accuracy and reliability, we adhered to Lincoln and Guba’s four criteria: credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability [31] (See Table 1). Additionally, two peer debrieferswere engaged to identify potential biases, and critically examine the researchers’ assumptions and conclusions. These strategies collectively strengthened the rigor and trustworthiness of the study.

Policy development

Policy development is crucial for understanding healthcare professionals’ engagement with legal and ethical issues. Midwives often face an unclear and inconsistently enforced policy landscape, impacting caregivers, legal abortion policies, clients, and the Family Planning Program. This ambiguity can lead to legal fears for providers, unsafe choices for clients, and inconsistent care pathways. Establishing clear guidelines, realistic enforcement, and aligned family-planning services is essential to mitigate these challenges, reduce unsafe practices, and improve maternal health outcomes. Key recommendations include extending legal abortion timeframes, adjusting penalties, and enhancing referral and counseling protocols to normalize safe care and minimize harm.

Implementing a policy for caregivers

This subcategory examines policies affecting midwives’ practice. Midwives shared their experiences on regulations governing abortion care, highlighting heavy fines, legal penalties, and inadequate law enforcement.

“There should be strict penalties for offenders” (P.5).

" You know, imposing steep fines on midwives or anyone taking part in unsafe abortions would be a strong deterrent. " (P.11).

These statements reflect a desire for regulatory clarity in a context where informal and unregulated practices persist. Rather than advocating for punishment per se, midwives appeared to call for a coherent legal framework that distinguishes between licensed, ethical care and illicit, dangerous interventions. In this sense, demands for penalties may be interpreted as a professional strategy to protect the integrity of midwifery practice and reduce competition from untrained providers. However, midwives also recognized the gap between policy and practice:

" Sure, there are laws against unsafe abortions, but what’s really important is actually enforcing them. And let’s be real, that doesn’t always happen as strictly as it should. " (P.6).

The findings suggest that effective policy implementation is not merely about the severity of penalties but about fairness, consistency, and institutional support. Midwives’ calls for enforcement must therefore be understood not as a move toward criminalization, but as a plea for a functional, equitable system that protects both providers and clients.

Re-orientation in legal abortion policies

This subcategory highlights midwives’ critical perspectives on the rigidity of current abortion laws and their implications for clinical practice and public health. Participants emphasized that restrictive gestational limits—particularly the absolute prohibition of abortion after 20 weeks, regardless of fetal or maternal indications—force families into dangerous alternatives when severe fetal anomalies are diagnosed late in pregnancy.

" Sometimes, due to a physical problem with the fetus, the family may decide to have an abortion, but since abortion is not allowed in our country after 20 weeks under any circumstances, they resort to unsafe abortion” )P.1).

" If legal abortion is allowed, then no one will seek unsafe abortion for any reason” (P.9).

Thus, without policy adaptation to clinical and ethical realities, laws risk complicity in harm rather than protection of life. Midwives’ insights underscore the urgent need for legal frameworks that are not only health-centered but also responsive to the lived experiences of women and families.

Implementing policy for clients

This subcategory encompasses two semantic units: “punishing wrongdoers” and “encouraging women to continue unwanted pregnancies”. It highlights midwives’ concerns about the legal and ethical aspects of unsafe abortion. They may believe in holding individuals accountable for unsafe actions, viewing punishment as a deterrent and a means to reinforce social norms regarding reproductive health and awareness of the law. Conversely, this group acknowledges the challenges women face with unintended pregnancies and is committed to empowering them through support services, education, and alternative resources.

“If laws are considered to punish women and men who seek abortions without reason and people are educated about the existence of these laws, they might stop doing it“(P.7).

This statement reflects a normative view of law as a behavioral regulator, where legal sanctions are perceived not merely as retribution but as a means of shaping public awareness and discouraging “frivolous” or “unnecessary” abortions. However, the notion of an “abortion without reason” remains undefined, raising ethical concerns about who determines the legitimacy of a woman’s decision and how such judgments may reinforce stigma.

In contrast, other midwives explicitly rejected punitive approaches toward women:

“It certainly shouldn’t be punished. Sometimes encouraging those who had unwanted pregnancies but didn’t attempt abortions can have a positive impact on some families’ decision to stop” (P.13).

Here, the focus shifts from blame to encouragement, highlighting a strengths-based approach that values women’s agency when they choose to continue a pregnancy despite adversity. The suggestion to “encourage” those who carry unintended pregnancies reflects a desire to build positive social narratives around reproductive resilience, potentially countering dominant discourses of regret or failure.

The interviewed midwives also stressed the need for proactive, non-judgmental care:

" We need midwives and other providers to step up and offer the right kind of support to those with unexpected pregnancies, so they can continue the pregnancy rather than choosing abortion. " (P.14).

This shift emphasizes relational care over legal control. Rather than relying on punishment to shape behavior, participants advocate for a system in which access to counseling, financial aid, childcare support, and mental health services enables women to make informed, sustainable choices. Thus, policies that criminalize abortion without addressing its root causes fail both public health goals and ethical care. Conversely, policies that support women’s autonomy while expanding access to resources may reduce abortion rates more effectively—and more humanely—than punishment ever could.

Re-orientation of the family planning program

This subcategory highlights midwives’ recognition of family planning not merely as a technical service, but as a transformative public health strategy with direct implications for reducing unsafe abortion. Participants emphasized that a re-imagined family planning program—one that is comprehensive, accessible, and education-based—can fundamentally alter the reproductive landscape by addressing the root cause of unsafe abortion: unintended pregnancy.

“Comprehensive family planning services empower women to make informed choices and significantly reduce the demand for unsafe abortions. Access to education and resources enables women to plan their pregnancies more effectively, which will result in safer outcomes for everyone“(P.14).

This statement encapsulates a preventive and rights-centered logic: when individuals have the knowledge, tools, and autonomy to control their fertility, the need for abortion—particularly unsafe abortion—diminishes. Midwives view effective family planning as a form of primary prevention, where reproductive health is safeguarded before crisis decisions arise.

The call for “re-orientation” highlights that current family planning initiatives are insufficiently focused on counseling, gender dynamics, and long-term reproductive life planning, primarily emphasizing contraception. Midwives argue that effective programs should promote reproductive literacy, helping clients understand their bodies, navigate relationships, and align their fertility with life goals. This shift has both moral and structural implications. Midwives protect women’s bodies in public health policies by viewing family planning as empowerment rather than population control. This perspective aligns with principles of reproductive justice, which affirm both the right to prevent pregnancy and the right to have a safe and supported childbearing experience.

Risk communication approach

This category focuses on comprehensive reproductive health education. It involves informing clients and caregivers about the dangers of unsafe abortions, pregnancy risks, and available healthcare options. Midwives should tailor evidence-based communication to educate clients effectively. Consistent messaging helps women understand their options, potential risks, and safer alternatives, empowering them to make informed decisions. Integrating this approach with accessible contraception and post-abortion care enhances counseling and promotes safer choices.

Providing risk education to clients

This subcategory reveals midwives’ strong belief in reproductive health education as a foundational tool for prevention and empowerment. While clients may possess a basic awareness of the dangers of unsafe abortion—such as hemorrhage, infection, infertility, and maternal death—midwives argue that this knowledge is often fragmented, fear-based, or acquired too late. Therefore, they advocate for systematic, comprehensive, and accessible education that not only informs but transforms reproductive decision-making at the individual, couple, and community levels.

“Husbands and wives should be taught that they are both responsible for preventing unwanted pregnancies and should be taught to accept responsibility if they become involved in an unwanted pregnancy “(P. 3).

This statement signifies a shift from individualized to relational accountability in reproductive health. Midwives participating in this study challenged the traditional view of contraception as a woman’s sole responsibility. They advocate for educating couples together, which promotes shared decision-making and fosters a sense of collective responsibility for reproductive health. This approach not only advances gender equity but also addresses the power dynamics within relationships. By empowering both partners, it helps overcome the common challenge women face in negotiating contraceptive use, even when they understand the associated health risks.

Further, the midwives emphasized primary prevention over crisis intervention:

“The best thing to do, in my opinion, is to educate women about preventing unwanted pregnancies. It is better to solve the problem at its root” (P. 8).

The metaphor of “solving the problem at its root” positions education as a key public health intervention, emphasizing that addressing underlying issues like misinformation and gender inequality is crucial for effectively reducing unsafe abortions. This approach also expands the role of midwives beyond clinical care to that of health promoters.

This subcategory emphasizes that education goes beyond information transfer; it acts as structural support. Equitable access to reproductive knowledge promotes autonomy, reduces health disparities, and minimizes high-risk behaviors. Midwives advocate for comprehensive education to drive a cultural shift towards prevention and reproductive literacy, aiming for a reproductive health ecosystem where education is central to policy, media, and clinical practice, ensuring informed choice as a fundamental right.

Providing risk education to caregivers

This subcategory highlights midwives’ understanding that their education and professional preparedness are crucial for safe and client-centered reproductive care. Participants noted that caregivers are not only technical providers but also vital influencers in clients’ decision-making. Their knowledge, communication skills, and emotional competence significantly impact how clients perceive risks, understand options, and feel supported, especially in high-stakes situations like unintended pregnancy and abortion.

“More retraining classes on the consequences of unsafe abortion should be provided for midwives. (P. 4)”.

This calls for continuous professional development addresses knowledge gaps regarding the medical, psychological, and social effects of unsafe abortion. Midwives recognize that outdated training can lead to misinformation and inadequate counseling, potentially deterring clients from seeking care. Hence, regular retraining is seen as essential for delivering evidence-based, compassionate care that meets current public health needs.

Participants in this study also highlighted disparities in preparedness among practitioners, particularly newer midwives:

“Some new midwives who have their practice may be clumsy in dealing with these matters. I think that the practice license should be given to them after completing some necessary courses like this one” (P12).

This statement points out a quality assurance issue, indicating that current licensure processes may not adequately assess competence in reproductive care. The term “clumsy” reflects technical and communicative shortcomings. Midwives call for formal education on abortion care, focusing on risk communication and stigma reduction, to ensure caregivers meet a standardized readiness for independent practice.

Media education on parenting

This category examines how media shape couples’ views on shared responsibilities, influence cultural attitudes toward childbearing, and impact adherence to reproductive health values. Midwives emphasize that media significantly affect parenting norms, gender roles, and reproductive choices, influencing couples’ perceptions and potentially normalizing harmful practices. To address these challenges, media literacy initiatives and positive public messaging can encourage informed decision-making, reduce stigma, and support discussions about reproductive health within families and communities.

Responsibility of couples

The findings highlight the crucial role of media education in changing perceptions of reproductive responsibility among couples. The midwives in this study observed that when the media promote shared decision-making regarding contraception, pregnancy planning, and abortion, it leads to a more balanced distribution of responsibility between partners. Open communication about fertility fosters a supportive environment that enhances mutual accountability and informed choices, especially in complex situations like unintended pregnancy.

“Media education plays a vital role in shaping couples’ understanding of their shared responsibilities in reproductive health. When both partners actively participate in open discussions about contraception and pregnancy, a supportive environment is created—one that not only strengthens mutual accountability but also empowers them to make informed decisions regarding potential abortion.” (P. 12).

The interviewed midwives observed a gap between media portrayals and clinical realities. Despite increased media visibility of male involvement in reproductive health, many couples still adhere to traditional gender roles, with men often passive or absent in decision-making. This indicates that while media can spark cultural change, it needs to be supported by targeted counseling and structural improvements in healthcare.

Culture of childbearing

Media narratives were found to significantly influence cultural norms surrounding childbirth and motherhood. Participants highlighted that those mainstream representations often idealize natural childbirth, selfless mothering, and seamless transitions into parenthood, thereby constructing a narrow definition of what is considered “normal” or “desirable” in reproductive experiences.

“Media narratives play a significant role in shaping cultural beliefs about childbirth and motherhood. They often define what is considered ‘normal’ or ‘ideal.’ As midwives, we must critically examine these representations to understand their impact on our clients’ decisions and emotions related to their reproductive experiences.“(p.5).

This idealized portrayal significantly affects women facing unintended pregnancy or abortion, leading to guilt or moral conflict when their choices clashed with societal expectations. The midwives in this study noted that clients not fitting the “ideal mother” archetype often felt inadequate or ashamed, particularly regarding termination.

“The portrayal of childbearing in the media not only reflects cultural ideals but also reinforces stigma surrounding abortion. By recognizing and addressing these narratives, midwives can better support clients in making informed choices that honor their personal beliefs and circumstances.“(P. 2).

These findings underscore the need for critical media literacy within reproductive healthcare, enabling both providers and clients to deconstruct dominant narratives and acknowledge the diversity of valid reproductive experiences.

Adherence to values

The data reveal that media representations actively shape personal values related to motherhood and female identity. Midwives reported that repeated portrayals of motherhood as an essential, intrinsic component of womanhood generate profound social and psychological pressure on women, particularly those facing unintended pregnancies or considering abortion.

“The media often idealize motherhood as a core value. In doing so, they exert deep pressure on women to conform to these expectations. As midwives, we see how these images shape clients’ beliefs about motherhood, influence their decisions regarding pregnancy and abortion, and highlight the need for compassionate, values-based support.“(p.8).

Participants highlighted that the pressure surrounding pregnancy decisions is both external and internal, shaping women’s self-perceptions and choices. For many, terminating a pregnancy feels like a moral transgression linked to culturally defined roles, particularly influenced by traditional media that equates womanhood with motherhood. These insights suggest that midwifery practice should adopt a reflexive approach, integrating an awareness of media influence, supporting critical engagement with cultural narratives, and affirming reproductive autonomy.

Supportive approach

The midwives stressed the importance of a supportive approach—both economic and social—for improving reproductive health and reducing unsafe abortion reliance. They noted that reproductive decisions are influenced by broader structural and relational contexts. The midwives advocated for policies that tackle the root causes of reproductive vulnerability, particularly poverty and social isolation. The categories of “Economic Support” and “Social Support” represent distinct but interconnected pathways for operationalizing this support.

Economic support

The midwives participating in this research consistently identified financial instability as a primary driver of unwanted pregnancies and unsafe abortion. Economic hardship was described not only as a background factor but as an active force shaping reproductive decisions. Women from low-income households, particularly those without access to affordable contraception or prenatal care, were seen as disproportionately vulnerable to unintended pregnancy and its consequences.

“Many people who seek unsafe abortion do so because of the costs related to pregnancy and childbearing. If families are financially supported by the government and low-cost services are provided to them, many of them will give up abortion.” (P7).

Participants stressed that financial insecurity often leads individuals to perceive childbearing as an unaffordable burden, pushing them toward termination—even when the pregnancy is intended. Midwives argued that targeted economic interventions could shift this calculus, transforming pregnancy from a source of anxiety into a feasible life choice.

“Most people who seek an abortion are due to unwanted pregnancies. And usually, poor families have these problems. Financial assistance to families who cannot afford to have children can be a way to discourage them” (P.3).

“If the government provides monthly benefits to families who have children, many of those who decide to have an abortion due to unwanted pregnancies may be discouraged” (P. 9).

These insights suggest that economic support is not merely a welfare issue, but a reproductive justice imperative. Midwives viewed policies such as cash transfers, subsidized childcare, and accessible family planning services as essential tools for expanding reproductive autonomy and reducing health inequities.

Social support

While economic constraints operate at the structural level, the midwives emphasized the relational and cultural dimensions of reproductive decision-making. Social support—or the lack thereof—was described as a critical factor influencing women’s choices, particularly in contexts where motherhood is highly valorized or stigmatized.

“The authorities should address the poverty of the people. Poverty leads to many problems, such as unwanted pregnancy and then the decision to have an unsafe abortion “(P. 10).

This quote highlights how midwives see economic and social vulnerabilities as interconnected. A woman facing financial hardship and a lack of emotional support from her family may experience a compounded sense of isolation. On the other hand, midwives observed that when women receive understanding and encouragement from their partners, families, or communities, they are more likely to carry their pregnancies to term, even in challenging situations.

“Social support from families and communities can significantly influence women’s decisions about pregnancy and abortion. When families and communities provide emotional and practical support, women may feel empowered to make choices aligned with their values and circumstances” (P. 4).

This subcategory reveals that reproductive decisions are not solely rational or economic calculations, but are deeply emotional and socially mediated. The midwives described their role as not only providing clinical care but also helping women navigate familial expectations, stigma, and shame—particularly in cases of unintended pregnancy.

Discussion

This qualitative study explored midwives’ perspectives on preventing unsafe abortion in Yasuj, Iran. It identified four key categories: policy development, risk communication approaches, media education on parenting, and a supportive approach. These findings highlight midwives’ recognition of unsafe abortion as a complex, multifaceted issue requiring systemic, rather than isolated, interventions. Abortion policy is often influenced by the conflict between criminal law, ethical considerations, and clinical realities. Midwives called for comprehensive policy reforms, including revising restrictive laws, reorienting family planning programs, and establishing standardized care pathways. These recommendations align with WHO guidelines on safe abortion care, which emphasize integrating legal, educational, and service-delivery systems to protect women’s health [32, 33].

Unsafe abortion, especially in areas where it is criminalized (including Iran), is a major public health and ethical challenge. Criminalization drives people to perform unsafe and clandestine procedures, leading to high morbidity and mortality rates [34]. In Iran, abortion is legally permitted only under limited circumstances—such as threats to the mother’s life or severe fetal anomalies—leaving many women without legal recourse [35, 36]. Midwives play a crucial role in providing safe abortion care (SAC), especially in resource-limited settings. However, they often encounter ethical dilemmas due to legal restrictions, personal beliefs, and their professional responsibilities. This unique position enables midwives to witness the effects of unsafe abortion practices and to support prevention strategies effectively. There needs to be clear rules to protect both patients’ rights and healthcare providers’ rights. The lack of clear guidelines on conscientious objection complicates these issues, emphasizing the need for policies that balance midwives’ morals with patients’ rights to care [37]. For ethical practice, it is important to find a balance between giving advice and letting patients make their own decisions. A more in-depth look at these problems can help midwives get ready for the moral challenges of their job and give care that is both compassionate and knowledgeable.

Additionally, at the client service level, the criminalization of women for having “unnecessary” abortions and the associated social stigma were identified as barriers to accessing safe services. This finding aligns with the social stigma theories proposed by Goferman and Lynch, which suggest that negative social symbols, moral stigma, and discrimination hinder individuals from obtaining health services [38]. In contrast, participants highlighted the necessity of a supportive rather than punitive approach—one that aligns with the “Care through Compassion” model [39].

As far as family planning programs are concerned, the findings showed that access to contraceptive methods, specialized counseling, and integration of family planning services into prenatal care are considered primary prevention in reducing unsafe abortions. This finding is supported by reports from the Guttmacher Institute, which show that countries with widespread access to family planning have significantly lower rates of unsafe abortion [38].

While participants emphasized the importance of risk communication and client education, they cautioned against viewing education as a standalone solution. Improved health literacy is vital [40–42], but structural barriers —such as lack of partner support, stigma, and limited access to reproductive health services— remain powerful determinants. Economic hardship was frequently cited as a driver of abortion decisions [10, 43], though midwives recognized that reproductive choices are shaped by intersecting personal and social factors. Similarly, while the media were seen as a tool for promoting shared responsibility and cultural values around parenting [44, 45], they were not presented as essential or sufficient on their own, but rather as part of a broader, culturally sensitive communication strategy.

Participants emphasized the need for mandatory pre-licensure education and ongoing professional development programs, which aligns with FIGO (2020) recommendations to incorporate safe abortion education into nursing and midwifery curricula [46]. The change to midwifery curricula is an example responsive midwifery theme in our culture.

Communication skills in reproductive counseling have been identified as a crucial element. Many young or inexperienced midwives struggle with emotional competence during sensitive conversations, which can erode patient trust. This finding clarifies and expanded the theory of Sensitive Communication in Reproductive Health [47].

The innovative contribution of this research lies in its holistic and comprehensive framework for addressing unsafe and illegal abortion. This framework integrates policy development, risk communication, and multi-faceted support systems, encompassing revised legal frameworks, targeted education for clients and caregivers, and the promotion of responsible parenting. Crucially, the approach emphasizes the interplay of media education, cultural values, and socio-economic support as essential components for prevention. This integrated, culturally sensitive, and education-based paradigm represents a significant advancement, empowering midwives and communities to collaboratively confront reproductive health challenges, particularly unsafe abortion. These may contribute to “SDG Target 3.7 | Sexual and reproductive health: By 2030, ensure universal access to sexual and reproductive health-care services, including for family planning, information & education, & the integration of reproductive health into national strategies“ [48].

Although Iranian midwifery-related laws are documented, they are not always critically examined for their implications on midwifery practice. Our understanding of how legal frameworks affect midwives’ roles and responsibilities is limited by this omission. Empirical studies that evaluate how these laws affect the standard of care provided by midwives and the availability of services for women are of vital importance. We can more successfully advance legislative changes that strengthen midwifery practice as well as improve maternal and child health outcomes in Iran by taking into account the input of midwives and stakeholders.

Limitation

We should acknowledge several limitations of this study. It lacked perspectives from other healthcare providers and stakeholders in abortion care, such as gynecologists and legal experts. Including their viewpoints could have provided a more comprehensive understanding of the issue. Additional limitations include a geographical focus on Yasuj, a small sample size, and potential interviewer bias, as all interviews were conducted by the first author. Nevertheless, the insights gained provide a solid foundation for ongoing discussions and initiatives aimed at enhancing reproductive health policies. By addressing the critical areas identified, stakeholders can collaborate to develop communication and counseling programs for those seeking abortions and create initiatives to support the well-being of women and their families.

Conclusion

This qualitative study examined Iranian midwives’ views on preventing unsafe abortion, highlighting their vital role in health policy, maternal care, and community education. Key themes include the need for human rights-based health policies, effective risk communication, media-led public education, and comprehensive support frameworks for women facing unintended pregnancies. Midwives in this study advocated for revising restrictive abortion laws, expanding family planning programs, and creating clear clinical guidelines. They emphasized educating women about the dangers of unsafe procedures and the media’s role in disseminating accurate reproductive health information. In Iran’s sociocultural context, these themes guide strategies for preventing unsafe abortion and enhancing reproductive health promotion. The study calls for a holistic, rights-based, and woman-centered approach to reproductive health, positioning midwives as essential contributors to policy and program development. Only with such integrated strategies can we achieve sustainable improvements in women’s health and broader public health outcomes.
