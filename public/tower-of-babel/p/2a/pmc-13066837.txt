Three-dimensional pseudo-continuous arterial spin labeling magnetic resonance imaging: an effective imaging technique for early-stage kidney injury in patients with type 2 diabetes
Authors: Wenli Fang, Qiaowei Song, Fang Cao, Sixian Zhou, Zhicheng Liu, Yong Zhang, Xiaomin Xu, Xiangyang Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFunctional biomarkers cannot currently detect hemodynamic impairment in patients with early-stage kidney injury and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). This study evaluated the utility of three-dimensional pseudo-continuous arterial spin labeling (3D-pCASL) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in quantifying stage-specific renal blood flow (RBF) alterations in patients with T2DM.MethodsA total of 33 patients with T2DM, who were divided into normoalbuminuria (NAU; n=11), microalbuminuria (MAU; n=13), and diabetic kidney disease (DKD; n=9), along with 15 healthy volunteers (HVs), were recruited. All participants underwent renal contrast-enhanced MRI and 3D-pCASL examinations on a 3.0-T scanner. Two experienced radiologists independently segmented the kidneys based on the postcontrast cortical phase images and measured the cortical RBF. The cortical RBF values across the four groups were compared via one-way analysis of variance, with post hoc multiple comparisons performed to analyze differences between specific renal segments. Correlation analyses were conducted between cortical RBF values and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), as well as other renal function parameters.ResultsDespite having a normal eGFR, patients in the NAU stage exhibited significantly reduced RBF (right: 110.84±13.15 mL/100 g/min; left: 94.14±5.24 mL/100 g/min) compared to HVs [right: 133.78±19.19 mL/100 g/min; left: median, 109.00, interquartile range (IQR), 101.53–117.15 mL/100 g/min], with reductions of approximately 17–19% in the bilateral anterior segments (right: adjusted P=0.016; left: adjusted P=0.008). A progressive decline in RBF was observed across advancing disease stages in certain renal segments (e.g., posterior segments) in the NAU group (right: median, 104.57, IQR, 101.90–116.92 mL/100 g/min; left: 95.49±11.60 mL/100 g/min, MAU group (right: 92.07±13.13 mL/100 g/min; left: 82.40±6.76 mL/100 g/min), and DKD group (right: 69.52±17.16 mL/100 g/min; left: 65.75±19.36 mL/100 g/min). The posterior segment emerged as a consistently vulnerable region in the MAU and DKD stages (both adjusted P values <0.05), suggesting an anatomic predilection for injury, attributed to its terminal arterial supply with limited collateralization. Separate analyses indicated that in the NAU and MAU groups, RBF showed a positive correlation with eGFR (NAU: r=0.460, P=0.031; MAU: r=0.398, P=0.044; DKD: r=0.694, P=0.001) but no significant correlations with other clinical parameters. Furthermore, in the DKD group, RBF showed a positive correlation with the blood urea nitrogen–serum creatinine ratio (r=0.670, P=0.002) and inverse correlations with serum creatine (r=–0.633; P=0.005) and triglyceride level (r=–0.749; P<0.001).Conclusions3D-pCASL MRI enables detection of preclinical microvascular impairment in patients with T2DM, representing a sensitive biomarker for early DKD risk stratification. Quantification of RBF in the vulnerable posterior segment yields clinically actionable insights for targeted interventions before the onset of functional decline.Keywords: Diabetic kidney disease (DKD), arterial spin labeling magnetic resonance imaging (ASL MRI), renal blood flow (RBF), early kidney injury, microvascular impairment

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) constitutes a significant chronic disease burden globally (1). In 2021, the number of individuals with diabetes exceeded 537 million worldwide. Projections indicate a 46% increase in this number by 2045. Notably, prevalence rates are substantially higher in middle- and high-income countries than in low-income countries (2). Research indicates that impairment of renal function in patients with diabetes reflected by an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 typically signifies irreversible kidney damage. However, early stages of diabetic kidney injury [i.e., normoalbuminuria (NAU) and microalbuminuria (MAU)] are potentially reversible (3,4). Therefore, early diagnosis of diabetic kidney injury and objective assessment of renal function impairment severity are crucial for formulating effective treatment strategies and improving patient prognosis.

The pathological changes in diabetic nephropathy are progressive and heterogeneous. Even during the NAU stage, diffuse lesions, nodular lesions, mesangial dissolution, and occasionally interstitial lesions may occur in the kidney (5). This frequent discrepancy between the progression of diabetic kidney disease (DKD) and abnormalities in urinary albumin excretion or eGFR decline renders current clinical indicators for assessing renal function impairment in patients with T2DM (e.g., eGFR and urinary albumin levels) insufficient for accurately evaluating renal damage severity (6,7). Although novel tubular injury biomarkers (e.g., urinary neutrophil gelatinase–associated lipocalin and kidney injury molecule-1) can facilitate the earlier detection of renal dysfunction than urinary albumin, they are susceptible to interference from extrarenal factors (8,9).

Conventional imaging techniques for dynamic perfusion studies, including those based on computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), require exogenous contrast agents (10,11). Iodinated contrast agents carry risks of nephrotoxicity and expose patients to ionizing radiation. Although gadolinium-based contrast agents are generally regarded as safer, their use in patients with advanced renal insufficiency is generally avoided due to the risk of nephrogenic systemic fibrosis (NSF) and is reserved for cases for whom the diagnostic benefits outweigh the risks after careful assessment (12). Radionuclide imaging techniques [e.g., technetium-99m-diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid (99mTc-DTPA) renal dynamic imaging] allow for separate assessment of the glomerular filtration rate (GFR) for each kidney (13,14). However, their limited spatial resolution precludes quantification of cortical-medullary perfusion, and the associated ionizing radiation exposure limits their use in repeated examinations. Furthermore, while renal biopsy remains the gold standard for detecting early DKD, its invasive nature, risk of complications, and sampling variability preclude its routine diagnostic use (15). Consequently, the development and application of reliable, noninvasive techniques suitable for long-term dynamic renal function monitoring are need.

Arterial spin labeling (ASL) is a noninvasive MRI perfusion technique that employs magnetized arterial blood as an endogenous tracer to quantify tissue perfusion (16). Technical optimization of 3D pseudo-continuous ASL (3D-pCASL), including labeling pulse sequence and three-dimensional acquisition, significantly enhances image signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and spatial resolution (17). ASL has been increasingly applied in the investigation of renal hemodynamics in cases of acute kidney injury, kidney transplantation, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and metabolic syndrome (18-20). However, the value of ASL in quantitatively assessing early renal injury in patients with T2DM has not be extensively examined.

To address this need, we employed 3D-pCASL MRI to quantify early renal functional impairment in patients with T2DM. Through analysis of blood flow perfusion changes in both kidneys and certain renal segments, we sought to establish objective imaging evidence to support early screening and precise clinical intervention for patients with DKD. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-1776/rc).

Methods

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital (No. QT2025144). Informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Participants

A prospective, cross-sectional, single-center study was conducted in which patients diagnosed with T2DM according to American Diabetes Association (ADA) criteria were prospectively enrolled between February 2024 and February 2025 (21). Participants were placed into the following groups as determined by the albumin-creatinine ratio (ACR) and eGFR: (I) NAU group, ACR <30 mg/g and eGFR >60 mL/min/1.73 m2; (II) MAU group, ACR 30–300 mg/g and eGFR >60 mL/min/1.73 m2; and (III) DKD group, ACR >300 mg/g or eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2.

The participant inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) age 30–84 years; (II) a diagnosis of T2DM as per the ADA criteria ≥5 years before enrollment; and (III) no MRI contraindications. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) nondiabetic renal diseases (e.g., glomerulonephritis and polycystic kidney disease) or significant extrarenal comorbidities; (II) neighboring organ lesions compressing the kidney or renal blood vessels; (III) pregnancy or lactation; and (IV) recent use (within 48 hours) of nephroactive medications (e.g., diuretics, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors). A cohort of 15 sex- and age-matched healthy volunteers (HVs) was enrolled as the normal control group.

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Demographic and clinical data were obtained from medical records. We collected clinical parameters for all T2DM participants, including ACR, eGFR, serum creatinine (SCr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), uric acid, glycated hemoglobin, and triglyceride (TG) levels. Anthropometric measurements included height, weight, body mass index (BMI), and blood pressure.

MRI acquisition and analysis

MRI examinations

3D-pCASL MRI was performed with a 3-T MRI scanner and a 32-channel torso coil (Discovery MR750W, GE HealthCare, Chicago, IL, USA) and included a background-suppressed 3D spin-echo echo planar imaging (EPI) sequence. Participants fasted (including water restriction) 4–6 hours prescan and received standardized breathing instruction. During a single breath-hold, conventional axial and coronal T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) of both kidneys, as well as axial corticomedullary phase contrast-enhanced imaging, were acquired. The scanning range extended from the upper to the lower poles of both kidneys. Imaging parameters for T2WI were as follows: repetition time (TR), 2,857 ms; echo time (TE), 80 ms; field of view (FOV), 400×400 mm; slice thickness, 24 mm; and matrix, 384×256. Subsequently, renal perfusion was assessed with a free-breathing 3D-pCASL sequence. To mitigate respiratory motion artifacts, the acquisition employed prospective respiratory gating triggered at end expiration (22,23). All participants were fitted with respiratory bellows for monitoring and received real-time audio instruction to maintain a regular pattern and were told to breathe during the gaps between control and label image acquisitions. This approach enhanced patient comfort and clinical feasibility. The key imaging parameters were as follows: TR, 11,250 ms; TE, 17.7 ms; labeling duration, 4,000 ms; postlabelling delay, 700 ms; voxel size, 3.1×2.3×8.0 mm3; FOV, 300×300 mm2; matrix size, 96×128; slice thickness, 8 mm; and number of signal averages, 6. Coronal images were acquired with the labeling plane positioned 10 cm superior to the FOV center, with an orientation perpendicular to the aorta for descending aortic blood labeling (Figures S1,S2).

MRI data processing and analyses

Upon transfer to the m-Viewer workstation, bilateral RBF measurements were independently performed by two abdominal radiologists, each possessing >5 years of specialized experience. Both radiologists completed standardized training in region-of-interest (ROI) delineation methodology, achieving excellent interobserver agreement (intraclass correlation coefficient >0.85) during 15 prestudy calibration examinations. To ensure precise spatial correspondence between anatomical structure and functional perfusion, a linear co-registration was performed between the coronal T2-weighted images and the quantitative RBF maps derived from 3D-pCASL. This step was implemented using FMRIB’s Linear Image Registration Tool (FLIRT) within the FSL software library. The co-registered anatomical images then served as the direct reference for the subsequent manual segmentation of renal ROIs, thereby ensuring that all perfusion measurements were accurately localized within the correct anatomical boundaries. Initial segmentation included cortical-phase axial enhanced images, which were used to identify five key segmental arteries, with the following renal divisions being demarcated according to an established protocol (25): superior, upper anterior, lower anterior, inferior, and posterior. Cortical localization for each segment was subsequently verified with source coronal T2WI sequences. For each renal segment, two circular ROIs measuring 10–20 mm2 were meticulously traced, with vasculature, cystic structures, renal sinus adipose tissue, and imaging artifacts being deliberately excluded. These defined ROIs were subsequently transferred onto quantitative blood flow maps for perfusion value extraction. Technical constraints inherent to ASL slice acquisition precluded differentiation between upper-anterior and lower-anterior segments, necessitating their consolidation into a composite anterior segment. As a result, eight ROI values were obtained per kidney (16 in total for both kidneys). The final statistical value was calculated as the average of the measurements from the two radiologists (Figure 1).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed via the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and Levene test, respectively. Normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas nonnormally distributed data are reported as the median with interquartile range. Categorical variables were compared with the Chi-squared test.

For comparisons of demographic and clinical variables across the four study groups, the choice of test was based on data distribution: one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for normally distributed data with homogeneity of variance, and the Kruskal-Wallis H test was used for nonnormally distributed variables.

To evaluate intergroup differences in renal perfusion parameters, global cortical and segmental RBF values were first compared across all groups via one-way ANOVA (for normally distributed data) or the Kruskal-Wallis H test (for nonnormally distributed data). When a significant overall difference was detected (P<0.05), post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted to identify specific group differences. Bonferroni correction was applied following ANOVA, and the Dunn test was used following the Kruskal-Wallis H test.

For correlation analyses, the relationship between RBF and clinical parameters (e.g., eGFR) was assessed via Pearson correlation coefficients for normally distributed data and Spearman ρ for nonnormal distributions. The statistical significance threshold was established at a two-sided P value <0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the patients

This study initially enrolled 41 patients diagnosed with T2DM. Following the exclusion of 8 cases with structural renal abnormalities, the final cohort comprised 33 participants divided into three subgroups: NAU (n=11), MAU (n=13), and DKD (n=9). Additionally, 15 age-matched HVs (9 males and 6 females) constituted the healthy control group. No statistically significant differences in demographic characteristics or baseline clinical parameters were observed between the study groups, with the exception of SCr and eGFR levels (P>0.05) (Table 1).

Significant intergroup differences in renal cortical RBF were observed across the four study cohorts (HVs group, NAU group, MAU group and DKD group; P<0.01). Segmental distribution of cortical perfusion alterations

Pairwise post hoc comparisons revealed distinct patterns of segmental RBF reduction across disease stages. First, compared to HVs, the NAU group showed significantly diminished RBF specifically in the bilateral anterior segments (right: adjusted P=0.016, decrease of 17.2%; left: adjusted P=0.008, decrease of 13.6%), but not in the posterior segments. Second, the MAU group, as compared to the NAU group, had a significantly lower RBF in the bilateral inferior and posterior segments. The reductions in the inferior segments were 21.3% (right: adjusted P=0.032) and 18.0% (left: adjusted P=0.040), and the corresponding reductions in the posterior segments were 14.5% (right: adjusted P=0.024) and 13.7% (left: adjusted P=0.016). Finally, the DKD group, as compared with the MAU group, had a significantly lower RBF in the bilateral posterior segments (right: adjusted P=0.016, decrease of 24.5%; left: adjusted P=0.032, decrease of 20.2%) and the left anterior segment (adjusted P=0.024; decrease of 27.1%) (Table 2, Figure 3, and Tables S1-S3).

Correlation between RBF, eGFR, and other relevant clinical variables

RBF was significantly and positively correlated with eGFR across all the study groups (NAU group: P=0.031; MAU group: P=0.044; DKD group: P=0.001). We stratified patients with T2DM into NAU, MAU, and DKD subgroups for stage-specific correlation assessment of RBF with eGFR and clinical parameters. This design specifically evaluated ischemia sensitivity gradients across progressive DKD stages. There were no significant correlations found between RBF and other clinical measures in both the NAU and MAU subgroups (all P values >0.05). Conversely, within the DKD group, RBF exhibited significant inverse correlations with SCr (P=0.005) and TG (P<0.001) levels and a positive correlation with the BUN-SCr ratio (P=0.002; Table 3).

Discussion

Renal ASL MRI is being actively investigated in terms of its ability to evaluate microvascular alterations in patients with DKD. Our study demonstrated that 3D-pCASL MRI can be used as a noninvasive tool to quantify early microvascular compromise in patients with T2DM and to provide objective assessment of renal microcirculatory alterations. We observed significantly diminished cortical perfusion in patients with T2DM as compared to that in nondiabetic controls, a difference independent of age, sex, and BMI. Furthermore, perfusion abnormalities emerged during NAU stages prior to clinical proteinuria. Our results are consistent with previous smaller studies, suggesting that cortical hypoperfusion accompanies early DKD and confirming the stepwise reduction in global renal cortical RBF across the NAU, MAU, and DKD stages (26,27). This further indicates that ASL technology can sensitively detect dynamic changes in renal microperfusion during diabetes progression. We extend these findings by providing the first evidence of segment-specific heterogeneity in renal perfusion across the NAU-MAU-DKD continuum. This heterogeneity aligns with established pathophysiological cascades and follows distinct anatomical patterns.

The progressive RBF decline reflects hierarchical cascades of microvascular failure, which elucidates the pathophysiological basis of this condition to some extent. At the NAU stage, a 13–20% reduction in the RBF of the anterior and posterior segments may stem from primary microcirculatory disruption, in which endothelial damage compromises oxygen supply (28). This initiates subclinical hypoxia despite preserved eGFR, as hyperglycemia activates proximal tubular sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) transporters, increasing sodium-glucose cotransport and Na+/K+-ATPase oxygen consumption (26).

During disease progression to the MAU stage, the 13–21% reduction in RBF in inferior segments is driven by renin-angiotensin system amplification, a process in which enhanced proximal sodium reabsorption reduces macula densa sodium delivery, triggering renin release and angiotensin II (Ang II) generation (29). Ang II exerts a dual pathological effect: Efferent arteriolar vasoconstriction causes intraglomerular hypertension and reduced pos-glomerular flow, further increasing proximal tubular oxygen demand through stimulated sodium reabsorption. Concurrently, mitochondrial dysfunction from hyperglycemia-induced reactive oxygen species impairs adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthesis (30), while compensatory perfusion increases accelerate microvascular injury.

At the DKD stage, advanced hemodynamic impairment manifests as a severe reduction in RBF (24–45%) in the bilateral anterior/posterior segments and right renal regions. This perfusion collapse directly reflects the hypoxia-fibrosis vicious cycle: chronic hypoxia induces tumor growth factor beta (TGF-β)-mediated extracellular matrix dysregulation (31,32), while progressive interstitial fibrosis physically compresses microvasculature (33). Critically, these alterations elevate transcapillary hydraulic pressure gradients—a consequence mechanistically linked to hyperfiltration-induced glomerular basement membrane stress (34). Concurrently, sustained hyperfiltration drives hemodynamic decompensation (35), further compromising renal architecture through microvascular rarefaction and endothelial injury (36). Collectively, these pathophysiological cascades precipitate glomerulosclerosis and the degradation of the filtration barrier integrity, ultimately mediating both the decline in GFR and the progression to proteinuria.

Notably, the posterior segments emerged and remained as vulnerable regions during the MAU and DKD stages. This susceptibility originates from posterior segmental artery (PSA) anatomy. The posterior segments receive perfusion through the PSA, which arises directly from the main renal trunk (37) and is characterized by shorter trajectories, independent courses, and limited collateral networks. In contrast, the anterior division branches (e.g., superior/inferior anterior segmental arteries) permit collateral compensation through complex preparenchymal branching (38). Consequently, this anatomical configuration predisposes PSA territories to preferential ischemia during diabetic vascular stenosis or occlusion, and diminishing vascular compliance in PSA territories exacerbates glomerular hyperfiltration stress, promoting segmental glomerulosclerosis progression.

A consistent observation in our study was the systematically higher RBF in the right kidney compared to the left across all participant groups. This lateral asymmetry likely stems from established anatomical and hemodynamic disparities. Specifically, the left renal vein is notably longer and traverses between the aorta and the superior mesenteric artery, a configuration that predisposes it to mild physiological compression, potentially impeding venous outflow. The resultant relative increase in venous pressure may subsequently trigger a reduction in arterial inflow and cortical perfusion via renal autoregulatory mechanisms. In contrast, the shorter, direct course of the right renal vein into the inferior vena cava offers less resistance, thereby facilitating higher perfusion. This pathophysiological explanation is in line with previous multiparametric MRI research on HVs, which also reported findings of significantly higher RBF in the right renal cortex (39).

Beyond the anatomical import, the consistent right-left RBF asymmetry observed across all groups, including HVs, carries important clinical and pathophysiological implications. Primarily, it should be interpreted as a common anatomical variant rather than an indicator of unilateral pathology. Consequently, the detection of such laterality in clinical practice should not be overinterpreted as a pathological sign. Moreover, this finding underscores the necessity for side-specific baseline assessment when serial RBF measurements are employed for monitoring disease progression or treatment response. In such longitudinal evaluations, a significant deviation from an individual’s established left-right RBF difference may serve as a more sensitive indicator of altering renal hemodynamics than absolute perfusion values alone. Furthermore, this observation suggests the possibility that the kidney with lower baseline perfusion (typically the left) might exhibit differential vulnerability in bilateral diseases such as DKD, a possibility warranting further investigation. Therefore, acknowledging and accounting for this physiological variability is a crucial step toward achieving the precise and individualized clinical application of quantitative renal ASL.

There was robust positive correlation between RBF and eGFR, indicating a close association between the extent of renal perfusion and the severity of renal functional decompensation. Significant RBF-eGFR/SCr correlations emerged exclusively in patients with DKD, whereas the associations in the early stage were nonsignificant. In contrast, Zhang et al. (40) documented a stronger correlation between RBF and eGFR in patients with stages 1–3 CKD, which diminished in those with stages 4–5 CKD. This discrepancy is primarily attributable to the exclusion of patients with DKD, highlighting how distinct pathophysiology modulates perfusion-function relationships. Khatir et al. (41) demonstrated that during CKD progression, the kidney develops intrinsic energy compensation mechanisms. This adaptation, however, can result in a disproportionate reduction in renal oxygenation relative to RBF. It should be noted that Khatir et al.’s study exclusively enrolled patients with moderate-to-severe CKD (eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2), potentially limiting the generalizability of their conclusions. This observed limitation may be attributable to compensatory renal mechanisms (42), such as glomerular hyperfiltration, which can temporarily maintain renal function, thereby obscuring the relationship in earlier disease stages or milder CKD.

During the progression from diabetes to early-stage DKD, glomerular hyperfiltration and vascular remodeling transiently maintain eGFR. This contributes to a decoupling between RBF and functional parameters. When these compensatory mechanisms eventually collapse, a strong correlation between RBF and eGFR becomes evident. This pattern underscores the heightened clinical significance of hemodynamic parameters in early-stage DKD. These observations align with those of Klessens et al. (43), who proposed that early alterations in renal perfusion occur independently of traditional functional indicators.

In the DKD cohort, RBF also showed a significant inverse correlation with serum TG level. Although TG is less directly atherogenic than is low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, an elevated TG level is associated with endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and impaired vasodilation—factors that may exacerbate renal microvascular impairment (44,45). This observation suggests that lipid metabolism disturbances may interact with hemodynamic alterations in patients with advanced DKD, potentially accelerating perfusion decline. Although this inverse correlation does not imply causation, it suggests a plausible pathophysiological link in which dyslipidemia, a common comorbidity in patients with advanced DKD, may exacerbate microvascular endothelial injury and impair vasoregulation, thereby contributing to the observed perfusion deficit. Future studies are needed to clarify whether TG-lowering interventions can ameliorate renal perfusion in this population.

Although our study highlights the sensitivity of 3D-pCASL MRI in detecting early microvascular alterations, we acknowledge the current limitations of MRI as a widespread screening tool. Its cost, examination time, and limited availability restrict its use to carefully selected patients or research settings. Therefore, renal ASL is positioned not as a broad screening tool but as a targeted, second-line investigational modality. It may be most valuable for clinical tasks such as those evaluating patients with normoalbuminuric diabetes and a high cardiorenal risk for which conventional biomarkers are in effective or those monitoring therapeutic response in clinical trials targeting renal hemodynamics. Future efforts to simplify protocols, reduce scan time, and integrate machine learning for automated analysis may enhance the clinical feasibility of 3D-pCASL MRI.

Limitations

Four principal limitations should be acknowledged related to the application of advanced 3D-pCASL MRI technology in this study. First, the modest cohort size—particularly the DKD subgroup (n=9)—compromises the statistical power and potentially reduces the reliability and generalizability of the subgroup analysis. Prospective multicenter collaborations and expanded recruitment will address this constraint. Second, pathological verification was not feasible as renal biopsy is rarely indicated for mild renal impairment, necessitating reliance on internationally standardized clinical diagnostic criteria for cohort stratification. Third, the eight ROIs per kidney provided incomplete cortical coverage and omitted renal arterial variant considerations. Artificial intelligence-driven whole-kidney segmentation integrated with CT/magnetic resonance angiography will enhance spatial precision in future investigations (46). Fourth, ASL is inherently limited to the assessment of cortical perfusion and does not provide direct information on tubulointerstitial fibrosis—a central pathological feature of DKD progression. In a recent study, Wan et al. (47) demonstrated that diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) outperforms ASL in detecting renal allograft fibrosis. This finding suggests that a multiparametric MRI protocol integrating ASL with DTI and blood oxygenation level-dependent MRI sequences may offer complementary information and thereby improve diagnostic accuracy in patients with DKD.

Conclusions

3D-pCASL MRI offers a noninvasive approach for detecting early microvascular alterations in patients with T2DM, with RBF quantification in the posterior segment appearing particularly valuable. Our observations suggest this region may provide enhanced sensitivity for identifying disease progression and stratifying DKD risk as compared to whole-kidney assessments. Although methodological refinements are still needed, the posterior segment RBF has the potential to be a perfusion biomarker that can inform clinical management strategies—including early detection, personalized therapeutic approaches, and monitoring of interventions. Future longitudinal validation should be conducted through multicenter, large-cohort studies and ASL sequences optimized (48) (e.g., via the enhancement of spatial resolution and signal-to-noise ratio) to accelerate translational implementation in routine nephrology practice.
