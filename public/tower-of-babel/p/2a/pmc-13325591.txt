Societal perceptions of household air pollution and how it impacts children’s respiratory health: a systematic review of qualitative studies
Authors: Pranab Kalita, Abdul Haye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHousehold air pollution (HAP) represents a significant global health concern. Children are particularly vulnerable to HAP. Despite the known risks, there exists a “perception gap” between societal understanding and scientific evidence regarding HAP’s impacts. This systematic review aims to explore societal perceptions regarding HAP and its impacts on children’s respiratory health.MethodsFollowing the PRISMA guidelines, a comprehensive search was conducted across relevant databases including Embase, PubMed, Web of Science, and Google Open Searches. The search strategy incorporated both Medical Subject Headings and search terms, focusing on “perception,” “household air pollution,” “children,” and “respiratory health”. Studies were included if they reported qualitative data on societal actors’ perceptions of HAP’s impact on children’s (0–18 years) respiratory health. Titles, abstracts, and full texts were screened, followed by data extraction and quality assessment of included studies using CASP and AACODS tools. The findings across these publications were synthesized through thematic analysis.ResultsTen studies met the inclusion criteria, revealing three key themes: (1) Perceived impact of HAP on children’s respiratory health, (2) Perceived obstacles to implementation of solutions, and (3) Perceived drivers of change in behaviour to reduce exposure. The ‘perceptions’ highlight some important gaps in societal actors’ view of HAP and awareness of its impact on children’s respiratory health, particularly in rural areas. Financial constraints, low awareness, limited access to cleaner fuels and cultural practices are thought to be the key barrier amongst societal actors and stand as hurdles to change. Despite these, there is realization amongst societal actors of the need for practical solutions to mitigate HAP exposure. This points to the need for intervention strategies that not only targets HAP, but parallelly addressees’ community’s cultural norms, economic and educational realities.ConclusionThis review underscores the criticality of integrating societal insights with scientific evidence to tailor HAP mitigation strategies effectively. It reiterates the necessity for interventions to be culturally sensitive, economically feasible, and compatible with societal actors’ expectations to improve children’s respiratory health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27808-0.

Introduction

Household air pollution (HAP) is a ‘global health concern’ [1]. Children are particularly vulnerable to HAP due to their developing respiratory and immune systems, higher respiratory rates, and greater time spent indoors. Compared to adults, children’s airways are narrower, and their detoxification mechanisms are less mature. These factors collectively increase their exposure and susceptibility to environmental pollutants [2–8]. This poses a significant immediate and long-term health risks. Studies have demonstrated that early-life exposure to HAP is associated with an increased risk of developing respiratory illnesses in children, with adverse health effects that may extend into adulthood [2, 9–11]. Children’s actual exposure to pollution is shaped not only by emission sources but also by household decision-making influenced by caregivers’ beliefs, cultural norms, and risk perceptions. Choices regarding fuel type, cooking location, ventilation, and whether children remain nearby during cooking often follow tradition and gender roles rather than scientific evidence [1]. Biomass fuels, for instance, may be favored because they viewed as natural, affordable, or producing better-tasting food, while smoke is often accepted as a normal part of daily life [12–16]. These perceptions influence the adoption of clean stoves, improving ventilation, and protective behaviors. Thus, risk perception is a key pathway through which social and cultural factors determine children’s actual exposure levels and, subsequently, their health conditions.

The global burden of HAP-related diseases continues to remain a significant challenge. Approximately one-third of the world’s population continues to rely on open fires or inefficient stoves that use kerosene, biomass, and coal as fuel sources, all of which are major contributors to HAP in rural areas [17]. Household environments are also affected by indoor pollutants such as dust, dirt, germs, mold, mildew, and pollen [18]. HAP significantly contributes to respiratory morbidity and mortality in children, accounting for 237,000 of 3.2 million deaths in 2020 among those under five [1]. This high mortality rate is largely linked to severe respiratory conditions, such as lower respiratory infections, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and lung cancer [1].

The situation in Africa and South Asia is particularly severe. In South Asia and certain regions of Africa such as East, West, Central, and Southern, nearly 30% of deaths within the first month after birth are attributable to air pollution [6]. In 18 African countries, including Burundi, Mali, South Sudan, Niger, and Uganda, over 95% of the population depends on solid fuels for cooking, thereby increasing their exposure to harmful pollutants. In addition, countries like Nigeria, Ethiopia, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo have experienced an increase of over 20% in HAP exposure in the past decade [6].

Addressing the impact of HAP on children’s respiratory health requires a comprehensive strategy that includes the involvement of various societal actors (who influence household decision-making, childcare practices, and responses to HAP interventions, such as parents, caregivers, guardians, and community health worker) alongside the critical need for long-term, sustainable solutions to facilitate the adoption of effective measures aimed at reducing HAP [5, 19, 20]. Without significant policy changes and access to cleaner fuels, the population’s exposure to HAP is projected to remain unchanged by 2030 [21, 22]. Therefore, the development of such policies must prioritize a thorough understanding of all [23].

The concept of a “perception gap” is frequently discussed in the scientific literature, highlighting a divergence between how societal actors’ perceive and understand environmental hazards, and how these hazards are assessed by scientific and policy-making professionals [24]. Unlike the ‘objective’ scientific data-based understanding of experts, other societal actors’ views are regarded as ‘subjective’ and lacking in scientific evidence and more a product of social constructs [24–26].

Despite the well-documented health risks of HAP, there remains a significant gap in understanding how societal perceptions and attitudes influence both exposure and success of interventions. While existing quantitative studies have largely focused on pollutant levels and health outcomes, they often overlooked the complex social, cultural, and behavioral factors that shape community responses. Without a nuanced grasp of how societal actors perceive the risks and solutions related to HAP (particularly its impact on children’s respiratory health and practical implementation of policies at the grassroots level), public health initiatives may fail to achieve their intended impact. e.g., uptake remains low for cleaner technologies if the intervention does not align with community beliefs, social norms, or daily household priorities [22]. Hence, assessing whether societal actors’ perception of HAP align with actual pollution levels is essential for understanding public attitudes and behaviours toward air quality. To our knowledge, no peer-reviewed article has systematically summarized qualitative research in this area, which allows for a deeper exploration of these contextual factors, offering insights into the social realities that often missed by quantitative studies. Therefore, this study aims to systematically review existing literature on societal perceptions regarding of HAP’s impact on children’s respiratory health and to identify barriers to effective interventions in that context.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted as per the ‘Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA)’ guidelines [27].

Search strategy

This review employed a comprehensive search strategy across relevant electronic databases, including Embase, PubMed, Web of Science, and Google Open Searches. For the search, a combination of ‘Medical Subject Headings’ and search terms were used. Keywords included were related to “perception” AND “household air pollution” AND “children” AND “respiratory health” AND “low-middle income countries” AND “qualitative study”, along with their synonyms and variations to ensure a thorough literature capture (See supplementary file S1 for search strategy details). The search strategy was unrestricted by any timelines and only publications written in English were included. The search terms “qualitative study” and “low-middle income countries” were later excluded to expand the search. This ensured the inclusion of all relevant studies across both LMICs and HIC, and allowed the capture of studies that did not explicitly state their qualitative nature in the title or abstract.

Additional manual search was conducted through the reference lists of key publications included while incorporating grey literature from relevant databases such as conference proceedings, government reports, unpublished theses, and institutional repositories.

Criteria for eligibility

The review included primary studies that collected qualitative data on perceptions of HAP’s and its impact on respiratory health of children aged 0 to 18 years. Studies were included if they consisted of any societal actors’ perception explored through qualitative studies, mixed-method studies (qualitative part), household surveys with qualitative information, interviews, or expert consensus. Studies were excluded if they focused solely on adults, or were letters to the editor, non-human studies, comments, editorials, protocols, and case reports.

Study selection

The shortlisted publications’ titles were screened in Covidence. After removing duplicate publications, abstracts were screened to identify potentially eligible studies and retrieved for full-text review to identify the final selection of publications. Both authors (PK and AH) independently undertook the study selection process to minimize selection bias and enhance methodological rigor. This involved separately screening the titles and abstracts of all retrieved records against the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, followed by a full-text assessment of potentially eligible studies. Any discrepancies or disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus between them.

Data extraction

The key information from selected publications such as authors, publication year, locations, settings, methodologies, participant characteristics, HAP sources, societal perceptions and any other relevant information was collected.

Quality assessment

To assess the methodological quality of screened studies, two established critical appraisal tools was applied: the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) Checklist for qualitative studies [28] and AACODS Checklist for grey literature [29]. The CASP checklist consisted of 10 questions grouped into three sections, covering the validity of results, the rigor of data analysis, and the value of the research [30]. Each study was assessed using a structured checklist. Responses were marked as “Yes,” “No,” or “Can’t Tell.” Studies were rated as excellent if all responses were “Yes,” good if there was one “No,” and poor if two or more were “No.” Multiple “Can’t Tell” responses indicated methodological ambiguity and reduced trustworthiness. For grey literature, the AACODS checklist evaluated six domains: Authority, Accuracy, Coverage, Objectivity, Date, and Significance. Publications were rated high quality if ≥ 5 domains scored “Yes,” moderate for 3–4, and low if < 3. “Unclear” responses in Authority and Accuracy were flagged due to their importance in assessing credibility.

Synthesis

A thematic synthesis was conducted following the interpretative framework proposed by Thomas and Harden (2008), which is widely used in qualitative systematic reviews [31]. This approach involves three key stages: (1) the relevant original texts from each study were organized into different text codes based on relevance, meaning, and content, forming a collection of codes and sub-themes, (2) development of descriptive themes by grouping similar codes, and (3) generation of analytical themes that go beyond the content of the original studies to offer new insights.

Results

The primary search yielded 73 potential publications, from which 12 were eliminated due to duplication, and one German-language article was excluded. Google searches yielded 76 additional potential articles; of which 69 were excluded (deemed irrelevant based on their titles). Furthermore, citation search added 3 more articles. After screening abstracts from the remaining 70 papers, 35 papers were removed for not meeting eligibility criteria. Of the 35 full-text papers assessed for eligibility, 25 were excluded, leaving 10 studies retained for inclusion (Fig. 1, PRISMA flow diagram).

Quality assessment of the included studies

In CASP assessment, four were rated as excellent, three as good, and two were deemed poor quality. The poor rating was primarily due to one study [12] not reporting ethical considerations and another [32] not obtaining ethical approval, arguing it had an observational design with anonymous data based on focused group discussions. Despite this, being indexed in PubMed, suggests both having ethical standards in place, though not well described in the papers. Consistent with established qualitative evidence synthesis methodology (CASP and Cochrane Qualitative & Implementation Methods Group), methodological limitations alone do not necessitate exclusion when studies offer conceptual richness or relevance [30, 33]. These studies were therefore retained for their valuable stakeholder perspectives, with their limitations transparently acknowledged and their interpretations weighted cautiously to maintain analytical integrity (Table 1).

Study characteristics

Seventy percent (7/10) studies were based in rural or slum settings and conducted across diverse geographical locations in East and South Africa, Southern Asia, and North America [25, 27–30, 32–33]. Specifically, two studies were conducted in Ethiopia [13, 14]; one study each in Alaska, USA [34], South Africa [12], Malawi [15], India [36], Uganda [16]. Two studies, one in New Jersey, USA [37] and another in Nepal [35] were conducted in semi-urban or urban settings; and one study conducted in France did not clearly specify its location or setting [32]. Among the selected studies, six clearly specified the ages of the children, while four did not, limiting the ability to interpret age-related differences in exposure or vulnerability. The studies primarily employed qualitative methodologies, such as semi-structured interviews, focus group discussions (FGD), observations, and cross-sectional survey evaluating societal actor’s perceptions of HAP’s impact on children’s health. The studies engaged diverse societal actors, such as family members, caregivers, mother, or parents [12, 15, 34, 35, 37], community members [13], midwives, and village health teams/healthcare workers [16], general practitioners (GPs) [32], pregnant women [14], and households [36].

Four studies gathered societal actors’ views as part of evaluation of interventions to improve respiratory health of family members including children i.e., High Efficiency Particulate Air (HEPA) purifier [34], clean improved cookstove [25, 30], increase ventilation during cooking, limit children’s proximity to fires, reduce the duration of solid fuel use [25] and ‘midwife-led biomass smoke education program for mothers’ [16].

The studies identified various HAP sources in rural settings, primarily linked to cooking practices and fuel sources, such as wood [34], biomass fuel [12–16] and liquid fuel, predominantly kerosene [36]. However, dust, dirt, germs, mold or mildew, and pollens were perceived as major contributors of poor indoor air quality in urban setting [37]. These differences in exposure profile likely shape stakeholder perceptions and experiences in distinct ways and should be considered when interpreting the synthesized findings.

Perceived impact of HAP on children’s respiratory health

Knowledge about the existence of problem

Seven out of ten studies revealed a consistent concern among societal actors regarding the impact of HAP on children’s respiratory health, with most identifying respiratory issues as a significant consequence of HAP exposure, reflecting a good level of perceived risks and existence of this issue [13, 15, 35, 36]. Societal actors especially women reinforced that infants and children remain in the kitchen with them and there is a need to protect children from HAP exposure [13, 35]. For instance, in a study, 65% of households acknowledged HAP, with 45% identifying it as a major health issue for children under five years [36]. Parents of African Americans living in urban settings also identified household pollutants, including dust, mold, and household cleaners, which they believed exacerbated asthma symptoms among children [37].

In a mixed-method study utilizing qualitative interviews, pregnant women themselves perceived that they were at risk, reporting that kitchen smoke exacerbated respiratory conditions and expressing concerns that HAP could harm both the mother and foetus [14].

One study noted a generational difference amongst GPs specializing in maternal and pediatric health, where younger GPs were more focused to emerging indoor pollutants such as mold, formaldehyde, particulate matter, and mites, while more experienced GPs tended to focus on traditional pollutants like tobacco smoke. Notably, 6% of GPs had environmental health training, which influenced their understanding and communication about HAP risks in vulnerable groups. This variation reflects differences in training and shifting public health priorities [32]. In addition, societal actors expressed concerns about the outdoor pollution entering their houses from especially vehicular smoke, and fumes from neighbouring houses troubled them more. They emphasized that despite their efforts to maintain ventilation by keeping doors open, the persistent infiltration of pollutants remained a pressing issue [35]. Mothers were deeply affected by illnesses of their children due to HAP exposure [16].

Perceived obstacles to the implementation of solutions

Cultural and traditional perspectives

Cultural and traditional beliefs significantly influence how communities perceive and respond to HAP. For example, in rural societies across Ethiopia, Nepal, and Malawi, individuals (particularly mothers, home cooks, and other household decision-makers) hold diverse views shaped by local customs and lived experiences. While some studies found awareness of smoke-associated health risks, others revealed that smoke was perceived as harmless or culturally normative. In a qualitative study conducted in rural Ethiopia (Butajira), societal actors (male farmers, female house wife, and mothers) viewed smoke as beneficial for warmth, hygiene, and cultural significance [13]. Smoke was perceived as heating the house during rainy seasons and night-time, controlling pests, preserving the fabric of traditional tukul houses, as a natural way of conditioning the living space, as a symbol of a household’s social status and the cooking skills of the wife in rural Ethiopia [13]. Societal actors in southern Nepal, including home cooks and mothers of young children, expressed beliefs that the polluted air has a divine source and that effective solutions to mitigate HAP may not be feasible [35]. In contrast to these cultural beliefs, some concerns were more practical in nature. Some parents in one rural community in Malawi (Chilumba) perceived the improved clean stoves to have a higher risk of fire hazards, which deterred their use [15].

Lack of awareness/Low education levels

While societal actors acknowledged the presence of HAP, few recognized it as a major health issue, indicating a critical gap that must be addressed [36]. Studies highlights a general intention to transition from traditional biomass cooking methods; however, many households cited low community awareness and competing priorities as significant barriers. For instance, community members in rural Ethiopia and Malawi expressed low concern due to poor education and limited awareness of the long-term effects of traditional stoves [13, 15]. Nearly one-fifth of improved stove users in rural Malawi mention health-related benefits only when prompted, suggesting that these were not a primary concern compared to other advantages of the cookstove like reduced cooking times and fuel consumption [15]. Furthermore, behavioural factors also contributed to the persistence of HAP. Some societal actors preferred traditional stoves, as that allowed for multitasking such as breastfeeding while cooking, leading to a reluctance to adopt new cooking technologies [13]. Furthermore, households in rural Alaska reported discontinuing the use of air purifiers, as some families indicated they only used purifier when their children fell sick [34].

Low socio-economic status

Economic limitations emerged as a key barrier to addressing HAP [32]. A study in slums of Kolkata observed that houses were densely packed with shared walls, leaving no windows and lacking well-defined rooms, with a common room serving as living area and kitchen, thereby exposing children to HAP [35]. The family members believed that “little had been done” to mitigate the problem, attributing this to “insufficient money” [35]. In another study, mothers were made to prioritize other activities over those that could reduce air pollution, reflecting a sense of helplessness in the face of economic constraints [16]. Conversely, some mothers in a study conducted in rural Ethiopia reported that improving their cookstoves led to financial savings by reducing firewood consumption and healthcare costs related to smoke exposure [14].

Perceived drivers of accepting changes to reduce exposure

Adoption of preventive measures

Eight out of ten studies emphasized a collective acknowledgment by societal actors of health benefits derived from adopting interventions and strategic behaviours aimed at reducing HAP [12, 14–16, 32, 34, 36, 37]. Healthcare workers and mothers observed health improvements after implementing preventive measures against HAP, e.g. reduced respiratory ailments and improved general health among children [16]. It was perceived that households using air purifiers and improved cooking stoves experienced lesser HAP related health issues [14, 15]. This growing acceptance of technologically improved solutions (such as air purifiers and cleaner cooking stoves) among end-users reflects a positive shift in attitudes toward adopting interventions that mitigate the effects of HAP on children’s respiratory health. Additionally, the importance of spatial arrangements and ventilation in minimizing exposure was echoed by societal actors in a study in Nepal advocating for adequately ventilated or outdoor cooking spaces [35]. Three household behaviours that were perceived to be beneficial for children’s respiratory health were highlighted by mothers in rural villages in the Northwest Province of South Africa: Firstly, maintaining double ventilation by opening ‘at least 2 sources of ventilation for extended periods while a fire is burning was deemed effective’; Secondly, ‘keeping children at more than 1.5 m away from fires’ helped minimize their exposure; Lastly, ensuring that ‘fires were burned within 1.5 meters of functioning ventilation systems’ further contributed to protecting children’s respiratory health [12].

Moreover, two studies highlighted the evolving perceptions and behaviours towards creating healthier living environments, particularly for vulnerable populations such as pregnant women, thereby protecting children from HAP [14, 32]. For instance, GPs in France reported an increasing awareness among parents about indoor environmental risks, leading to proactive measures such as avoiding exposure to pollutants during pregnancy and improving household ventilation [32]. Similarly, in rural Ethiopia, pregnant women expressed a growing recognition of the health risks associated with traditional cooking methods and showed a willingness to adopt improved stoves, motivated by perceived benefits for both maternal and child health [14].

Opportunities to increase acceptance

Societal actors emphasized that individual end-users and policymakers should demonstrate the needs and willingness to implement changes to reduce HAP exposure amongst children [35]. Mothers in rural Ethiopia using improved cook stoves had positive attitudes towards this and spoke of the benefits on the reduction of the amount of wood used and time needed to cook food. They also stated that the level of smoke emitted and the exposure to flame and direct fire burning was low compared with the traditional ones [13]. Health extension workers noted that mothers learned about the advantages of improved cooking stoves by observing others and began requesting them for their households. Furthermore, mothers perceived health benefits was associated with less smoke production from these stoves [15]. Some women took proactive measures to decrease HAP, including using cooking stoves outdoors or keeping doors open [36].

At the same time, societal actors highlighted the necessity for raising awareness regarding the effects of biomass and traditional cook stoves, indicating that the problem may not be viewed as serious enough to warrant investment in interventions [13]. Practical information and community education were found to empower mothers to effect change and educate others [16]. Societal actors suggested that any technology provided should be user-friendly and locally maintainable at affordable price [13].

Discussion

This systematic review examines societal perceptions related to the effects of HAP on children’s respiratory health, offering valuable insights into how various societal actors understand both the harms caused by HAP and the efforts aimed at mitigating those harms. The findings indicate a varied level of awareness on the existence of HAP, identify key obstacles to progress, and highlight opportunities for advancing initiatives aimed at mitigating the harmful effects of HAP (Fig. 2).

Consistent with the global observations that highlight women and children from rural areas are predominantly exposed to prolonged HAP [19, 38–41]. This review primarily includes studies from rural settings, where solid biomass fuels like wood and kerosene, etc. majorly contribute to HAP [12–16, 34, 35]. These findings are consistent with earlier studies that have documented the widespread use of solid fuels (such as wood, coal, and biomass) for cooking and heating in rural households, leading to elevated levels of HAP and associated health risks [42, 43].

For instance, the World Health Organization (WHO) and other global reviews have highlighted that a substantial proportion of the rural population globally still relies on polluting fuels, resulting in disproportionately high exposure to harmful pollutants, which is linked to increased rates of respiratory and cardiovascular diseases, particularly among women and children who spend more time near cooking areas [1, 44]. In contrast, urban populations face exposure to distinct air pollutants including dust and dirt originating from vehicular emissions and industrial discharges [37]. Understanding this rural–urban divide is crucial for designing effective public health interventions. Rural areas require solutions focused on clean cooking technologies and fuel transitions [42], while urban areas benefit more from traffic regulation, industrial emission controls, and urban planning [45]. Recognizing these differences ensures that mitigation strategies are context-sensitive, equitable, and targeted toward the most vulnerable populations.

Many societal actors recognized the detrimental effects of HAP on children’s respiratory health, highlighting the urgent need for remedial actions [13–15, 35, 36]. These findings are consistent with earlier literature e.g., a systematic reviews and meta-analyses have shown that both prenatal and postnatal exposure to harmful pollutants significantly elevate the risk of acute respiratory infections and long-term pulmonary impairment [46]. Additionally, pregnant women express concerns that HAP also poses risks to both maternal and foetal health. These finding are also aligns with available evidence indicating that exposure to unclean cooking fuels during pregnancy is associated with adverse outcomes such as low birth weight, preterm birth, and stillbirth [47]. This highlights the importance of parental knowledge regarding the vulnerability of children to HAP-related respiratory diseases, particularly in developing countries where traditional cooking methods prevail [6, 19]. Furthermore, various societal actors, including women and healthcare workers, reported perceived impacts of HAP on their own health and productivity, while also expressing concern for its effects on children’s well-being [35, 36], consistent with existing literature [48].

As per WHO, there are multiple strategies as cleaner cooking, improving housing, ventilation design, economic subsidies and education programs, being implemented globally for reducing exposure of HAP and accelerating the transition to clean household energy [49]. This review synthesizes societal actors’ perceptions of HAP interventions, highlighting both observed benefits and behavioral responses. For instance, healthcare workers and mothers in several studies reported noticeable improvements in children’s respiratory health and sleep following the adoption of preventive measures [12, 16, 34]. These findings are consistent with previous literature suggest improvements in respiratory outcomes with reduced HAP exposure in children [50, 51]. Studies also enumerate similar impact from multiple local policy initiatives [52] e.g., LPG subsidies through India’s Ujjwala program [53], tax incentives for clean technologies in Malawi [54], were perceived to have a positive impact on household fuel choices, aligning with global evidence on the role of financial incentives in promoting clean energy transition [55]. Mothers employed strategies to mitigate children’s exposure, including maintaining adequate ventilation, keeping children away from cooking areas, and ensuring fires were burned near functioning ventilation systems [12]. These behavioral adaptations reflect a growing awareness of HAP risks and are supported by studies that link caregiver knowledge to improved household practices [56]. GPs noted an increased interest in avoiding pollutants during pregnancy, and parents undertook thorough cleaning regimes to mitigate asthma triggers [14, 32]. Furthermore, community recognize the critical role of practical information and community education in empowering mothers [16]. Findings of this study indicate potential behavioral and social shifts. Through a midwife-led education program, mothers were not only informed about the health risks of biomass smoke but were also empowered to adopt safer practices and share this knowledge within their communities. The focus of the education was therefore not only to raise awareness but also to overcome the social and economic barriers that often prevent sustained behavior change in low-income settings. These findings underscore the importance of sustained knowledge dissemination and culturally tailored education to ensure long-term acceptance and effectiveness of HAP interventions.

This review highlights that household decisions regarding sources of HAP and related mitigation strategies are shaped by a range of socio-economic and contextual factors, including affordability, accessibility, awareness, and cultural beliefs, rather than reflecting a clearly defined decision-making process. These findings aligns with other studies that highlight how air quality initiatives often overlook the complex interplay of visible and hidden factors influencing solid fuel use, thereby limiting the sustainability of interventions [57, 58]. This study underscores the urgent need to address cultural and traditional views simultaneously to enhance the effectiveness of mitigation strategies, while also highlighting the complex relationship between poverty and household decisions regarding the use of improved solutions, as highlighted in other studies [19, 59]. It emphasizes the role of a holistic strategy encompassing education, economic support, and behavioural change to confront HAP comprehensively. For example, in India, integrated programs such as India’s Pradhan Mantri Ujjwala Yojana (PMUY) combine financial subsidies for clean cooking fuels with awareness campaigns and community engagement efforts. These initiatives aim not only to improve access to cleaner technologies but also to shift cultural norms and household behaviors, thereby enhancing the long-term effectiveness of HAP mitigation strategies [60].

Although the studies included in this review offer valuable insights, they mainly remain limited to rural population. Future research should focus on understanding urban perspectives, particularly in low-income neighbourhoods where pollution sources may be different. Additionally, future studies should also consider the combined effects of household and ambient air pollution (another major contributor to respiratory illness). Addressing both sources will provide a more comprehensive understanding of respiratory health risks in affected populations.

This systematic review synthesizes qualitative studies across diverse geographical and socioeconomic contexts; however, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the review included only English-language publications, which may have excluded relevant studies from African and Asian regions where research is often published in local or regional languages, introducing potential language bias. Second, the evidence base is relatively small (only ten studies) with uneven representation across countries. For example, although Ethiopia is a frequently discussed setting in the literature, only two Ethiopian studies met the inclusion criteria, limiting the depth of contextual interpretation. Third, participant representation was skewed toward women, predominantly mothers, with limited perspectives from men, community leaders, or adolescents, potentially narrowing the range of stakeholder viewpoints captured.

Additionally, the included studies span a wide timeframe, with some published in the early 2000s, meaning that the perceptions reported may not reflect more recent policy and technological developments such as improved access to LPG or national clean-fuel initiatives. Exposures and experiences also varied substantially across settings, and synthesizing studies from both high-income and low- and middle-income contexts required careful interpretation, as underlying structural and environmental conditions differ markedly. Furthermore, several studies lacked detailed reporting on child ages or recruitment methods, which may affect interpretability and transferability. Finally, as with all qualitative syntheses, the findings rely on subjective accounts and researcher interpretation, which may introduce interpretive bias despite efforts to mitigate this through team discussions and reflexive engagement. Collectively, these limitations indicate that while the review provides valuable insights, the findings may not fully capture the full breadth or contemporary dynamics of household air pollution perceptions globally.

Implication for future practices

This review carries significant implications for future public health practice, policy development, and implementation strategies. First, the recognition of HAP’s impact on children’s respiratory health by various stakeholders highlights the need for targeted health education and awareness campaigns, especially in rural communities. Policymakers should prioritize expanding access to clean cooking technologies and fuels, such as LPG, through subsidies and infrastructure development. Implementation strategies must also consider cultural and behavioral factors that influence household decisions, ensuring interventions are contextually appropriate and community driven. Additionally, integrating HAP mitigation into broader maternal and child health programs can enhance uptake and sustainability. Strengthening intersectoral collaboration between health, energy, and environmental agencies will be crucial to designing holistic policies that address both the health and environmental dimensions of HAP exposure.

Conclusion

This systematic review highlights the need for multi-level intervention strategies that address both household and community dynamics, while actively incorporating societal perceptions. To ensure relevance and effectiveness, socio-demographic and socio-cultural factors (such as gender roles, traditional beliefs, and economic constraints) must be considered. Based on the thematic findings, behavioral interventions should aim to raise awareness of health risks associated with household air pollution, challenge cultural norms that normalize smoke exposure, and promote the adoption of cleaner cooking practices. These efforts must be supported by solutions that are affordable, durable, and user-friendly, and implemented through coordinated actions involving families, community leaders, and policymakers to safeguard children’s health.
