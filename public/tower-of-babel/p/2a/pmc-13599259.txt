PDLSC-CM targets HAPLN1 in macrophages to reduce root resorption in delayed replanted teeth
Authors: Lu Jia, Zengbo Zhao, Ying Zhang, Liping Zhang, Yan Liu, Hongyu Ma, Peng Zhang, Xiangdong Liu, Lu Zhang, Hang Yang, Zihan Yang, Zhe Ma, Huimin Wang, Zhiyong Hou, Huijie Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTooth avulsion is the most serious dental trauma, mainly affecting anterior teeth in adolescents. Delayed tooth replantation is the most common situation in clinical, but root resorption is a common complication that can lead to tooth loss. As implant is not feasible in adolescents, strategies to mitigate root resorption remain a major challenge in dentistry. We aim to evaluate the therapeutic effect and potential target of periodontal ligament stem cell-conditioned medium (PDLSC-CM) to reduce root resorption in delayed replanted tooth.MethodsClinical samples from delayed tooth replantation patients were analyzed by X-ray and histological staining. A rat model of delayed tooth replantation was established, with PDLSC-CM applied as the tooth preservative and irrigating solution. Root resorption was assessed by micro-CT and histopathology. Proteomic screening followed by Western blot and immunofluorescence was used to identify and validate key molecules. Macrophage polarization and osteoclastogenesis were examined in vitro using RAW264.7 and bone marrow-derived macrophages. Recombinant adeno-associated virus (rAAV) was used to validate the key protein molecules.ResultsHuman avulsed tooth with delayed replantation shows obvious root resorption and high expressions of inflammatory cytokines and osteoclast-related factors in periapical tissue. PDLSC-CM reduces the root resorption and inhibits the expressions of inflammatory cytokines and osteoclast-related factors in root and periapical tissue of delayed replantation of avulsed tooth in rats. PDLSC-CM downregulates the expression of HAPLN1 mainly in macrophages, thereby inhibits inflammation and osteoclastogenesis of delayed replanted tooth root. Overexpression of HAPLN1 abolishes the protective effect of PDLSC-CM on delayed replanted tooth root.ConclusionHAPLN1 reduction in macrophages plays a pivotal role in PDLSC-CM reducing root resorption. PDLSC-CM is a promising preservation medium for avulsed teeth with potential clinical applications, and the development of drug candidate targeting HAPLN1 has favorable clinical prospects.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13287-026-05154-7.

Introduction

Tooth avulsion, as the most severe type of dental trauma, accounts for 0.5%-16% of such injuries [1, 2]. Millions of teeth, especially anterior teeth, are avulsed annually from various incidents, including sports, traffic accidents and fist fights [3]. Tooth avulsion is particularly common in children due to their high activity levels [4]. Immediate replantation of the autogenous tooth is preferred for pediatric patients due to their underdeveloped jaws, which cannot accommodate artificial tooth implant [5]. However, delayed tooth replantation, where a detached tooth is preserved dry for over an hour before replantation, is common due to emergency situations, limited access to medical care and varying levels of patient medical knowledge [6]. This markedly raises the risk of root resorption, which can result in tooth loss, with severe impacts on patients’ aesthetics, physical and mental health [7]. Therefore, reducing root resorption in delayed replanted teeth is a significant challenge in dentistry.

Macrophages, crucial for skeletal homeostasis [8], are considered to be the most important cell type involved in root resorption [9]. Tissue injury disrupts protective barriers, permitting microbial invasion and activating macrophages. These macrophages phagocytose debris and pathogens, release pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β), stimulate receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL) expression, and promote their differentiation into osteoclasts [9, 10]. Osteoclasts adhere to the root surface, secreting proteins like tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) and matrix metalloprotease 9 (MMP9) to degrade mineralized tissue [11]. Therefore, exploring treatments that diminish inflammation and reduce osteoclast formation is critical for minimizing root resorption.

Scholars have attempted to transplant periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSC) to promote healing and reduce root resorption in replanted teeth, leveraging their multi-directional differentiation potential, self-renewal capacity, and immune-regulatory functions [3]. Nonetheless, limitations such as the shortage of donor stem cells, uncertainty in transplanted cell fate, potential immune rejection, and ethical concerns hinder clinical applications [12]. Interestingly, studies show that PDLSC-derived conditioned medium (CM), which contains growth factors, cytokines, chemokines, enzymes, exosomes and extracellular vesicles, significantly enhances periodontal tissue regeneration by promoting osteogenic differentiation in periodontal defect models [13, 14]. However, it remains unclear whether PDLSC-CM can inhibit osteoclast and thus reduce root resorption in delayed replantation of avulsed tooth.

Hyaluronan and proteoglycan link protein-1 (HAPLN1), initially identified in proteoglycan components from bovine articular cartilage, is recognized as a cartilage junction protein with multiple biological functions [15]. Recent researches reveal its diverse roles in various tissue, such as promoting tumor progression and metastasis [16–18], and enhancing cardiomyocyte proliferation and regeneration [19]. Additionally, HAPLN1 is overexpressed in rheumatoid arthritis patients and promotes pro-inflammatory responses [20], while its knockdown inhibits osteoclast differentiation. Knocking down HAPLN1 protein in osteoclast precursor cells significantly inhibits osteoclast differentiation [21]. This suggests that HAPLN1 is closely related to inflammation and bone resorption in bone related diseases. However, whether HAPLN1 plays a key role in the root resorption of replanted teeth remains unclear.

The aim of the present study is to evaluate the therapeutic effect of periodontal ligament stem cell-conditioned medium (PDLSC-CM) to reduce root resorption in delayed replanted tooth and clarify its mechanism and translational value. Based on clinical observations of root resorption, we designed an integrated study including a rat model of delayed tooth replantation and in vitro experiments to explore the therapeutic effect and mechanism of PDLSC-CM (Fig. 1).

Methods

Ethics statement

The human study of this research was conducted in accordance with the principles expressed in the Declaration of Helsinki. The collection of human samples was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Stomatology Hospital of Hebei Medical University (Approval No. 2024-004). The approved project was entitled “Study on the effect of PDLSC conditioned medium on delayed replanted tooth root resorption” and was approved on February 23, 2024. All participants provided written informed consent. The animal experimental protocols used in this study conformed to National Research Council’s (NRC) Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the ARRIVE 2.0 Guidelines and received approval from the Animal Ethics Committee of Hebei Medical University (No. IACUC-Hebmu-2024035). The approved project was entitled “Study on the root resorption mechanism and prevention strategies in delayed replantation of avulsed teeth” and was approved on May 17, 2024.

Human specimens

The non-replantation specimens were obtained from patients who underwent extraction of residual root of her right upper first molar due to chronic apical periodontitis caused by severe caries, with mild root surface resorption resulting from long-term chronic inflammation. The delayed replantation specimens were obtained from patients who underwent delayed tooth replantation due to tooth avulsion, and the replanted teeth could not be preserved due to severe root resorption. All patients signed the informed consent form. The collection of specimens did not pose any clinical risks or harm to the patients.

Animals

Male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (200–230 g) were purchased from Hebei Experimental Animal Center, Shijiazhuang, China. Before experiments, the rats were housed in a SPF facility under controlled temperature, humidity and a 12-hour light/dark cycle, with free access to water and normal rodent food. A total of 48 rats were included, with 6 animals per group in each experimental set. All animals completed the study as planned. For the surgical procedures, rats were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane. At the end of the experiments, the rats were euthanized under deep anesthesia with 5% isoflurane, followed by thoracotomy and transcardial perfusion with chilled saline, and subsequently, tissue specimens were obtained.

Preparation of PDLSC-CM

PDLSCs were isolated and cultured in accordance with our previous study [22]. PDLSC-CM preparation followed previously published methods [23]. PDLSCs at passages 3–5 were seeded at 1 × 10⁶ cells per T25 culture flask, conditioned in serum-free α-MEM for 48 h, then centrifuged and filtered. CM was normalized to 100 µg/mL total protein by BCA assay. To validate reproducibility, three independent batches of PDLSC-CM were prepared separately, and their effects were confirmed to be consistent in preliminary experiments. EV-depleted CM was obtained by ultracentrifugation at 100,000 g for 2 h at 4 °C. For protein depletion, CM was treated with proteinase K (100 µg/mL) at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by PMSF (1 mM) to terminate digestion.

Delayed replantation of avulsed tooth rat model

The rats were randomly divided into the following three groups: the control group (Ctrl) received no delayed replantation of avulsed tooth model surgery, the normal saline group (Saline) received normal saline for detached tooth preservation and alveolar cavity flushing, and the CM group (CM) received CM for the same purposes. Each group consisted of six rats. A rat model for delayed replantation of avulsed tooth was established based on previously published methods [24, 25].

Micro-CT analysis

Root resorption was analyzed using a micro-CT scanner (SkyScan 1174, Bruker, German) with a resolution of 12.5 μm, an energy of 76 kV and a current of 278 µA. Scanning data were reconstructed and analyzed using Skyscan software to calculate the root resorption [24]. Specifically, a standardized three-dimensional volume of interest (VOI) was defined around the target dental root. A consistent grayscale threshold was then applied to classify voxels within the VOI as either “intact root hard tissue” or “resorption area”. Finally, the software directly calculated the total volume of the resorption area. To account for individual variations, this resorption volume was normalized to the total volume of the dental root itself, yielding the final “root resorption ratio”.

Histology analysis

The degree of root resorption and the number of periapical osteoclasts were statistically analyzed based on H&E and TRAP staining results [24, 26]. After micro-CT scanning, specimens were decalcified in 14% EDTA, gradient dehydrated in ethanol, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 4 μm slices. H&E and TRAP staining were performed following the protocols provided by the respective kits (Baso, China; Servicebio, China).

Immunohistochemistry staining

After the sections were routinely dewaxed and hydrated, they were subjected to antigen repair with Tris-EDTA solution (pH = 8.0, Servicebio, China) at 100 ℃ for 10 min, and then rinsed three times with phosphate buffer solution (PBS). Subsequently, endogenous peroxidase was inactivated with 3% H2O2 for 20 min at room temperature and washed three times with PBS. After blocking with 5% BSA (BioFroxx, Germany), the sections were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with rabbit anti-TNF-α (1:100, Arigo, China), rabbit anti-IL-1β (1:100, Arigo, China), rabbit anti-RANKL (1:100, Santa Cruze, USA), rabbit anti-MMP9 (1:100, Huabio, China) or rabbit anti-HAPLN1 (1:100, Huabio, China) antibodies, respectively. After rinsing with PBS again, the sections were incubated with the corresponding goat anti-rabbit second antibody (1:200, Servicebio, China) for 60 min at room temperature. Finally, the sections were stained with DAB solution (Servicebio, China) and nuclei were stained with hematoxylin. Images were acquired using an upright microscope (DM6000, Leica, German) and quantified using ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence staining

The basic process of immunofluorescence staining is similar to immunohistochemistry staining except that the secondary antibody was changed to a fluorescent secondary antibody and the nuclei were stained with DAPI. Information of primary antibodies are as follows: rabbit anti-HAPLN1 (1:100, Huabio, China) and mouse anti-CD68 (1:100, Santa Cruze, USA) antibodies. The fluorescent secondary antibodies are as follows: Alexa Fluor® 488-conjugated alpaca anti-rabbit second antibody (1:500, Jackson ImmunoResarch, USA), Alexa Fluor® 594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit second antibody (1:500, Jackson ImmunoResarch, USA) and Alexa Fluor® 594-conjugated goat anti-mouse second antibody (1:200, Servicebio, China). Images were acquired using a confocal microscope (FV3000, Olympus, Japan) and quantified using ImageJ software.

Cell culture of RAW264.7

RAW264.7 cell line was purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) and cultured in DMEM medium (Gibco, USA) containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin and streptomycin in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 ℃. The RAW264.7 cell line was divided into five groups: the control group (Ctrl) treated with regular DMEM basic culture medium, the lipopolysaccharides (LPS) group (LPS) treated with DMEM basic culture medium containing 100 ng/mL LPS (Sigma, USA), the LPS + CM group treated with CM containing the same concentration of LPS, the LPS + EV-dep-CM group treated with EV-depleted CM containing the same concentration of LPS, and the LPS + P-dep-CM group treated with proteins-depleted CM containing the same concentration of LPS. After 6 h, cells were collected for subsequent qRT-PCR, and the supernatant was collected for subsequent ELISA detection.

Osteoclastogenic assay in vitro

Based on previously published methods [27], bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMMs) were isolated from femur of SD rats and seeded at 5 × 104 cells/well in 96-well plates. Osteoclast differentiation was induced with α-MEM culture medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin and streptomycin, 25 ng/mL M-CSF (R&D Systems, USA) and 10 ng/mL RANKL (R&D Systems, USA) at 37 ℃ in a 5% CO2 incubator for 5 days. TRAP staining performed on day 5 (TRAP kit, Servicebio), cells with ≥ 3 nuclei counted as osteoclasts. BMMs were divided into four groups: the control group (Ctrl) cultured in conventional osteoclast induction medium, the conditioned medium group (CM) cultured in CM containing the same concentration of M-CSF and RANKL, the EV-depleted CM group cultured in EV-depleted CM containing the same concentration of M-CSF and RANKL, and the protein-depleted CM group cultured in protein-depleted CM containing the same concentration of M-CSF and RANKL. After 5 days of osteoclast differentiation, cells were collected for subsequent TRAP staining, Phalloidin staining, qRT-PCR and immunofluorescence staining.

qRT-PCR

qRT-PCR was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocols (Zhongshi Tontru, China), with β-actin as the internal reference. The 2−ΔΔCt method was used for qRT-PCR data analyses (Table 1).

Western blotting

Western blotting was performed according to standard protocols and manufacturers’ guidelines. Information of the primary antibodies are as follows: rabbit anti-TNF-α (1:1000, Arigo, China), rabbit anti-IL-1β (1:1000, Arigo, China), rabbit anti-RANKL (1:1000, Santa Cruze, USA), rabbit anti-MMP9 (1:1000, Huabio, China), rabbit anti-HAPLN1 (1:1000, Huabio, China) and rabbit anti-β-actin (1:10000, ABclonal, China). The secondary antibodies are as follows: HRP-conjugated AffiniPure goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (1:10000, Proteintech, USA) and HRP-conjugated AffiniPure goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (1:10000, Proteintech, USA).

Proteomics analysis

The protein of tooth root tissue was extracted using TCA/acetone precipitation and SDT lysis method, followed by SDS-PAGE electrophoresis and FASP enzymatic hydrolysis. Then, mass spectrometry analysis was performed using the Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Finally, non-labeled quantitative calculations were performed on the proteomics data using the Label free algorithm in Maxquant. Different proteins were screened with a fold-change threshold of < 1.2 and P values < 0.05, and bioinformatics analysis was conducted. This part of the bioinformatics experiment was completed by the assistance of Genechem (Shanghai, China).

AAV construction and transfection

In order to overexpress HAPLN1 in the tissue around the tooth root of rats, rAAV-CAG-rHapln1-EGFP (Brain Case, China) or its control vector rAAV-CAG-EGFP (Brain Case, China) was injected into the periodontal ligament tissue using a microinjector. The virus titer was 1 × 1012 v.g./mL, and the injection volume was 10 µL per site. The rats were divided into two groups in this part of experiment: rats received the control vector injection group (Vector) and rats received the HAPLN1 overexpression AAV injection group (HAPLN1OE). After 3 weeks, Western blotting was performed to verify the overexpression efficiency. Then, these animals underwent the modeling surgery as described above, using CM as the preservation medium for the detached tooth. The rats were divided into two groups in this part of experiment: rats received the control vector injection and CM treatment group (Vector-CM) and rats received the HAPLN1 overexpression AAV injection and CM treatment group (HAPLN1OE-CM). After 28 days, animal samples were collected for micro-CT scanning and analysis, histology analysis, immunohistochemistry staining, and Western blotting as described above.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in a blinded manner by two independent investigators to ensure reproducibility. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments included 6 biological replicates and 2 technical replicates per group. Data were expressed as means ± SEM. All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used to compare data among multiple groups, and unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used to compare data between two groups. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Human avulsed tooth with delayed replantation exhibits obvious root resorption and high expressions of inflammatory cytokines and osteoclast-related factors in the periapical soft tissue

During clinical practice, many delayed replanted teeth experienced root resorption, as illustrated in the 8 cases shown in Fig. 2A. The roots of healthy adjacent teeth remained smooth and intact, while the surfaces of the replanted teeth exhibited pitted resorption (indicated by the red arrows in Fig. 2A). In severe cases, the roots were completely absorbed, leaving only the crowns, which led to tooth loosening and obviously impaired oral health and aesthetics.

Non-replanted and delayed replanted teeth with no preservation value were collected from patients. The root of non-replanted tooth was smooth and intact, with limited inflammatory granulation tissue surrounding the root apex. In contrast, the root of delayed replanted tooth exhibited obvious resorption, with only one-third of the cervical portion remaining. Moreover, the root surface of delayed replanted tooth showed silkworm-like erosion (Fig. 2B). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of the dental tissue revealed localized shallow pits and minor surface resorption on the cementum, with no osteoclast formation observed in non-replanted tooth. Conversely, the delayed replanted tooth showed obvious root resorption, with numerous multinucleated osteoclasts present on the root surface (Fig. 2C). H&E staining of the soft tissue at the root tips indicated that both groups of teeth exhibited apparent infiltration of inflammatory cells. In the non-replantation group, connective tissue encapsulation and fibrous tissue repair were observed around the inflammatory area. In contrast, the delayed replantation group displayed loose, aggressively growing inflammatory tissue, with no fibrous tissue repair observed (Fig. 2D).

The immunohistochemical staining results showed that compared with non-replantation group, both TNF-α and IL-1β exhibited evident upregulation in the periapical soft tissue of the delayed replanted tooth (Fig. 2E, F). Our immunohistochemical staining results showed that compared with non-replantation group, RANKL and MMP9 were upregulated in the periapical soft tissue of delayed replanted tooth (Fig. 2G, H).

These clinical findings prompted us to establish an in vivo model to verify whether PDLSC-CM could alleviate root resorption in a controlled setting.

PDLSC-CM reduces root resorption in the delayed replantation of avulsed teeth in rats

Flow cytometry analysis confirmed positive expression of mesenchymal stem cell markers CD90 (97.5%), CD105 (97.2%), CD73 (96.2%), CD29 (97.4%) and negative expression of hematopoietic/endothelial and leukocyte markers CD34 (0.32%), CD45 (2.42%) (Figure S1). Combined with the tissue origin, these cells were identified as PDLSC [28]. To verify the clinical observations, we established a rat model of delayed tooth replantation that recapitulates the clinical root resorption process. A delayed replantation of avulsed tooth model was established in rats as shown in the flowchart (Fig. 3A). The micro-CT results 28 days after surgery showed significant root resorption in both the Saline group and the α-MEM group, with no statistically significant difference between these two groups. In contrast, the CM group exhibited substantially reduced root resorption in both the middle root region (F(2,6) = 8.239, P = 0.0190) and the root apex region (F(2,6) = 14.23, P = 0.0053; Figure S2). These data exclude the influence of FBS present in the CM. Since saline solution is the standard storage medium for detached teeth and alveolar flushing solution in clinical practice, we use saline as the control condition for all subsequent experiments.

Specimen photos at 28 days postoperatively revealed that the periapical tissue in the Ctrl group was healthy, pink, and tough. The Saline group exhibited a dark purple color, indicating a severe inflammatory response, with the root canal filling visible through the root due to thin cementum resulting from root resorption. In contrast, the tissue in the CM group appeared pink, and the root tissue seemed thicker, partially covering the root canal filling materials, which was much healthier than that of the saline group (Fig. 3B).

Micro-CT images revealed significant root resorption and continuous interruptions (indicated by the yellow arrows in Fig. 3C) on the palatal side of the middle root, and the palatal, mesial, and distal sides of the root apex in the Saline group. In contrast, the CM group exhibited mostly intact roots, with only minor discontinuities on the palatal side (Fig. 3C). Quantitative analysis of the micro-CT images in the middle root and root apex were conducted, defining the percentage of discontinuous root length relative to the total root circumference as root resorption degree. The data indicated substantial root resorption in the Saline group compared to the Ctrl group. However, root resorption in the CM group was significantly reduced in both the middle root (F(2,15) = 142.0, P < 0.0001, Fig. 3D) and the root apex region (F(2,15) = 294.4, P < 0.0001, Fig. 3D) compared to the Saline group.

H&E staining showed that the roots of the Ctrl group were intact and smooth, surrounded by dense and healthy tissue. However, the Saline group exhibited increased root resorption and infiltration of inflammatory cells in the surrounding tissue. The CM group showed a significant reduction in root resorption compared to the Saline group, with relatively mild inflammation in the adjacent tissue (F(2,15) = 57.88, P < 0.0001, Fig. 3E, F).

Masson staining revealed that the periodontal ligament in the Ctrl group was densely and neatly arranged, while it was damaged and lacked a normal structure in the Saline group. In contrast, the CM group showed an arrangement close to normal (Figure S3). Col-I immunofluorescence staining showed that the Ctrl group had a neat and dense arrangement of periodontal ligament with abundant Col-I, while the Saline group had disordered periodontal ligament with a significant decrease in Col-I. In contrast, the CM group had a significantly reduced degree of disordered periodontal ligament arrangement, and the Col-I was significantly increased compared to the Saline group (F(2,15) = 15.18, P < 0.0001, Figure S4).

The results indicated fewer TRAP-positive osteoclasts in the Ctrl group, with all osteoclasts located on the alveolar bone side and none present on the tooth root surface. In the Saline group, there was a significant accumulation of osteoclasts on the resorption surface of the root. Conversely, in the CM group, the number of osteoclasts on the root surface was significantly lower compared to the Saline group (F(2,15) = 40.55, P < 0.0001, Fig. 3G, H).

PDLSC-CM inhibits the expressions of inflammatory cytokines and osteoclast-related factors in the root and periapical tissue of delayed replantation of avulsed tooth in rats

The immunohistochemical staining results of inflammatory cytokines in the root and periapical tissue showed that TNF-α and IL-1β were upregulated in the Saline group compared to the Ctrl group. However, CM treatment significantly reduced the expressions of TNF-α and IL-1β compared to the Saline group (TNF-α: F(2, 15) = 40.88, P < 0.0001; IL-1β: F(2, 15) = 147.4, P < 0.0001; Fig. 4A-D). Consistent with the immunohistochemical data, Western blotting results also showed that TNF-α and IL-1β levels were significantly higher in the Saline group than in the Ctrl group, while they were significantly lower in the CM group than in the Saline group (TNF-α: F(2,15) = 22.81, P < 0.0001; IL-1β: F(2,15) = 17.63, P = 0.0001; Fig. 4E-G).

Then, the expressions of osteoclast-related factors, RANKL and MMP9 in the root and periapical tissue were assessed. Both immunohistochemical staining and Western blotting results showed that the expressions of RANKL (IHC: F(2,15) = 12.76, P = 0.0006; Western blotting: F(2,15) = 5.996, P = 0.0122) and MMP9 (IHC: F(2,15) = 38.48, P < 0.0001; Western blotting: F(2,15) = 5.686, P = 0.0145) were significantly higher in the Saline group than those in the Ctrl group, while it was significantly lower in the CM group than those in the Saline group (Fig. 4H-N).

PDLSC-CM inhibits the expressions of inflammatory cytokines and osteoclast formation in vitro

In order to further investigate the active ingredients involved in PDLSC-CM, we employed an ablation strategy to remove EV or proteins from CM. RAW264.7 macrophages cell line was applied to investigate the effects of CM on inflammatory response in vitro. LPS markedly elevated mRNA levels of Tnf-α and Il-1β, which were significantly suppressed by CM. EV depletion abolished the inhibitory effect of CM on Tnf-α and Il-1β (Tnf-α: F(4, 25) = 25.23, P < 0.0001; Il-1β: F(4, 25) = 43.08, P < 0.0001; Fig. 5A). Similarly, CM upregulated mRNA expression of Tgf-β and Il-10. EV depletion attenuated induction of Il-10, while protein digestion impaired upregulation of Tgf-β (Tgf-β: F(4, 25) = 21.28, P < 0.0001; Il-10: F(4, 25) = 46.21, P < 0.0001; Fig. 5B). At the protein level, LPS increased TNF-α and IL-1β secretion, whereas CM reduced TNF-α and IL-1β and enhanced TGF-β and IL-10 levels. EV depletion abolished effects on TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-10, whereas protein digestion abolished effects on TGF-β (TNF-α: F(4, 25) = 105.8, P < 0.0001; IL-1β: F(4, 25) = 43.23, P < 0.0001; TGF-β: F(4, 25) = 21.77, P < 0.0001; IL-10: F(4, 25) = 11.72, P < 0.0001; Fig. 5C, D).

We further evaluated the functional roles of EV and proteins in CM-mediated inhibition of osteoclastogenesis. Treatment with intact CM significantly suppressed osteoclastogenesis, as evidenced by reduced TRAP⁺ cell area and F-actin rings. EV depletion partially abrogated the inhibitory effects of CM on osteoclast formation, with a modest recovery in TRAP⁺ cell area and F-actin rearrangement. In contrast, protein digestion did not alter the inhibitory effect of CM on osteoclastogenesis (F(3, 20) = 45.56, P < 0.0001; Fig. 5E-G). Consistently, the mRNA levels of Mmp9, Nfatc1, Ctsk and Atp6v0d2 in the CM group were significantly lower than those in the Ctrl group, EV depletion attenuated CM-mediated suppression of them, while protein digestion did not change the inhibitory effects on them (Mmp9: F(3, 20) = 54.69, P < 0.0001; Nfatc1: F(3, 20) = 10.21, P = 0.0003; Ctsk: F(3, 20) = 10.83, P = 0.0002; Atp6v0d2: F(3, 20) = 8.612, P = 0.0007; Fig. 5H).

PDLSC-CM downregulates the expression of HAPLN1 mainly in macrophages, thereby reducing root resorption in the delayed replantation of avulsed teeth

Next, the protein expression profiles of the root and periapical tissue in the Saline and the CM groups were analyzed using proteomics to investigate the mechanism by which PDLSC-CM reduces root resorption. By comparing the protein expression levels in the Saline group and the CM group with a fold change > 1.2 and P < 0.05 as the threshold cut-off, 22 upregulated and 40 downregulated proteins in the CM group were identified (Fig. 6A, B). HAPLN1 ranked the third among downregulated proteins (Fig. 6C). Notably, the average expression abundance of HAPLN1 in the CM group was significantly lower than that in the Saline group (t(10) = 2.983, P = 0.0148, Fig. 6D). Gene ontology enrichment analysis revealed that these proteins were involved in multiple biological processes, including cellular process, biological regulation, and response to stimulus, etc. (Fig. 6E).

Discussion

The most salient finding of our study is that the decreased HAPLN1 in macrophages plays a critical role in the protective effect of PDLSC-CM on reducing root resorption in delayed replanted teeth (Fig. 10). Proteomics data revealed that HAPLN1 is one of the most significantly downregulated differentially expressed proteins in CM treated detached teeth. Furthermore, immunofluorescence co-localization of HAPLN1 and CD68 localized HAPLN1 expression to macrophages. And quantitative image analysis subsequently demonstrated that CM treatment significantly reduced HAPLN1 levels in these macrophages. Our study also demonstrated that PDLSC-CM can inhibit macrophage inflammatory response through reducing the HAPLN1 expression, thus reducing the inflammatory response in delayed replanted teeth. While previous studies have shown that PDLSCs or their secretions can significantly inhibit macrophage inflammatory response [23, 29–31], our study demonstrated for the first time that suppressing HAPLN1 in macrophages is crucial for the inhibitory effect of PDLSC-CM on inflammatory response.

Surprisingly, we also demonstrated that overexpression of HAPLN1 not only significantly upregulated the expressions of inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β, but also increased the expressions of osteoclast-related factors such as RANKL and MMP9 in the roots and periapical tissue of delayed replantation of avulsed tooth in rats. Previous study has shown that plasma HAPLN1 level is significantly high in rheumatoid arthritis patients, and that overexpression of HAPLN1 in rheumatoid arthritis-fibroblast-like synoviocytes upregulates pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6 and MMP through AMPK pathway activation [20]. Additionally, down-regulation of HAPLN1 in BMMs inhibits osteoclast differentiation in vitro [21]. These studies support our view that upregulation of HAPLN1 enhances inflammation and osteoclastogenesis in replanted teeth rat model. Thus, our study indicates that by downregulating HAPLN1 in macrophages, PDLSC-CM reduces inflammation and osteoclastogenesis, ultimately decreasing root resorption in delayed replanted teeth of rats.

Maintaining the detached root in a condition similar to the physiological environment is crucial for the prognosis of replanted teeth. The International Association of Dental Trauma recommends that avulsed teeth should be stored in a special medium such as milk or Hank’s balanced salt solution promptly [2]. Unfortunately, patients often store avulsed teeth improperly by keeping them dry or even wrapping them in tissues, which greatly increases the risk of root resorption in replanted teeth [32, 33]. Our human specimens’ results showed that the inflammatory cytokines and osteoclast-related factors in periapical tissue are significantly elevated in replanted tooth roots, leading to a shortened life-span of the replanted tooth. In contrast, the non-replanted tooth root exhibited a healthier status with limited inflammatory response and no active osteoclasts.

In fact, researchers have explored various methods, such as ultrasound or laser [34, 35], growth factors [36, 37], or drug applications [38, 39]. Despite these efforts, outcomes remain significantly below clinical expectations. Recent advancements in stem cell therapy suggest that PDLSCs can promote the healing of replanted teeth [3]. However, limitations such as donor stem cell shortages, uncertainties about transplanted cell outcomes, potential immune rejection, and ethical issues impede their clinical use. Strikingly, our research indicates that PDLSC-CM significantly reduces root resorption in delayed replanted teeth, offering valuable insights for clinical prevention and treatment of PDLSC-CM.

PDLSC-CM contains growth factors, cytokines, and various active substances, offering a convenient cell-free therapy option with significant potential clinical application [40–42]. However, the intriguing question about which specific components in CM causes these effects has long puzzled many scholars. Extracellular vesicles are important components of CM and play a key role in its treatment. Exosomes extracted from PDLSCs of healthy periodontal ligaments significantly decrease inflammatory bone loss in periodontitis [43]. Several studies have analyzed the secretion protein content of PDLSC-CM using proteomics methods, describing possible key bioactive proteins [44]. Although miR-31-5p and circ:0000722 in PDLSC exosomes promote osteogenesis and inhibit osteoclastogenesis in vitro, their impact on bone healing remains unclear [45, 46]. Future research should focus on exploring the components of PDLSC-CM and developing specific medicines targeting HAPLN1 in macrophages to reduce root resorption of delayed replanted teeth.

Our findings position PDLSC-CM as superior to conventional storage media [2] and more readily translatable than cell-based therapies [40–42], while identifying HAPLN1 as a novel, pharmacologically targetable mediator of macrophage-driven bone resorption. Relative to empirical current management and single-factor interventions with inconsistent outcomes, this multi-factorial, mechanism-defined strategy offers a rational path toward clinical implementation in delayed tooth replantation.

While our findings establish PDLSC-CM as a promising cell-free therapy for reducing root resorption in delayed replanted teeth, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, ethical constraints precluded the use of healthy human teeth as controls. Chronic apical periodontitis specimens were used to illustrate clinical phenotype, not as mechanistic controls. Second, while EVs and proteins were identified as active fractions, further proteomic characterization is still needed for clinical standardization. Third, the combined administration of PDLSC-CM for tooth storage and socket irrigation means the individual contribution of each route remains to be clarified in future work. Finally, although HAPLN1 was identified as a key candidate mediator supported by published evidence, only gain-of-function experiments were performed. Additional loss-of-function studies are required to fully validate its causal role and mechanism. These points warrant further investigation to facilitate clinical translation.

In conclusion, we present our striking finding that HAPLN1 reduction plays a pivotal role in PDLSC-CM reducing root resorption by alleviating inflammatory reactions and inhibiting osteoclast generation in delayed replanted teeth. The development of PDLSC-CM and its active ingredients is highly beneficial for the prognosis of delayed replanted tooth. Moreover, the study of the pathogenic mechanism of HAPLN1 in root resorption and the development of targeted drugs to inhibit HAPLN1 have great clinical prospects. Our finding may also inspire orthodontic treatment and intentional replantation, as well as the management of long-term chronic apical periodontitis, which share a similar pathogenesis with delayed replanted teeth.
