CSDE1 in tumour immunology: spatiotemporal translational reprogramming and immune microenvironment remodeling
Authors: Danhui Wang, Zilan Xie, Jiye Yin, Xinyang Li, Huashan Zhou, Zhi Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe tumour immune microenvironment (TIME) can be considered a dynamic ecosystem, which requires rapid proteomic adaptation for immune evasion. While transcriptional control is undoubtedly significant, post-transcriptional mechanisms, most notably translational regulation, facilitate immediate proteome rewiring. Cold Shock Domain Containing E1 (CSDE1), an RNA-binding protein, has emerged as a pivotal upstream regulator of translational control, orchestrating multiple facets of mRNA metabolism, including translation initiation, elongation, and stability. While its context-dependent roles in tumourigenesis are recognised, the function of CSDE1 in immune regulation remains incompletely understood.Main bodyThis review synthesises emerging evidence to position CSDE1 as a central architect of immunosuppression through spatiotemporal translational reprogramming. The present study delineates the manner in which CSDE1 facilitates tumour-intrinsic immune evasion by suppressing antigen presentation and neutralising immunogenic signalling (e.g. cGAS-STING), while concurrently dictating the fates of immune cells – ranging from the development of haematopoietic stem cells to the differentiation of T and B cells. These findings are integrated to propose a novel conceptual framework of spatiotemporal translational reprogramming. This framework posits that CSDE1 decodes the cellular context to dynamically configure translational output across temporal (tumour initiation, progression, therapy resistance) and spatial (cytoplasm, nucleoplasmic reticulum, stress granules, immunological synapse) dimensions. Finally, we explore the therapeutic targeting of CSDE1 through combinatory genotoxic therapy, neoantigen vaccines, immunotherapy sensitisation, and physical stimulation, proposing CSDE1 as a novel therapeutic nexus to overcome resistance in tumour immunotherapy.ConlusionsCSDE1 has been identified as a central context-decoder and a promising therapeutic nexus in the TIME. However, its duality and essential physiological roles pose significant translational challenges.

Background

The dynamic and plastic nature of the tumour immune microenvironment is a crucial determinant of tumour progression and therapy response. This adaptability necessitates the rapid and precise regulation of gene expression. While transcriptional regulation is important, post-transcriptional mechanisms, most notably mRNA translational control, have been shown to be central to the process of rapid adaptation. This is due to the ability of these mechanisms to enable cells to swiftly alter their proteome in response to environmental signals without necessitating the synthesis of new mRNA.

Recent studies have recognized RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) as pivotal regulators of this process, controlling immune cell fate and tumour-immune crosstalk through the mechanisms of mRNA stability, localization, and translation. For instance, Human antigen R (HuR) modulates the stability and translation of mRNAs encoding proliferative and immunomodulatory factors, influencing tumour progression and immune evasion [1]. Conversely, Regnase-1 exerts a powerful inhibitory influence on inflammation by promoting the decay of cytokine mRNAs in immune cells [2]. Similarly, Y-box binding protein 1 (YBX1) is a multifunctional RBP that regulates the expression of oncogenes and modulates the tumour immune microenvironment, thereby impacting response to therapy [3]. These examples highlight the crucial function of particular RBPs in regulating post-transcriptional gene expression programs within the TIME.

In the extensive range of RBPs that regulate post-transcriptional networks, CSDE1 (also known as Unr) is a particularly prominent translational regulator. CSDE1 plays a fundamental role in modulating various aspects of mRNA metabolism, including translation initiation, internal ribosome entry site (IRES)-mediated translation, and RNA stability [4–13]. Structurally, CSDE1 is characterized by its nine cold shock domains (CSDs) and functions as a molecular scaffold that coordinates gene expression by enabling the simultaneous binding of specific mRNA and effector proteins [5]. Recent advances have begun to elucidate the mechanistic basis of CSDE1 function. Ciocia et al. proposed that CSDE1 acts as a versatile “molecular adaptor” that coordinates post-transcriptional regulatory complexes in a context-dependent manner, enabling its diverse roles in cancer hallmarks including proliferation, invasion, and immune modulation [14]. This adaptor function is rooted in CSDE1’s unique structural architecture, comprising nine cold shock domains (CSDs)—including both canonical and non-canonical CSDs—that collectively mediate RNA binding and protein-protein interactions [14, 15]. High-resolution NMR structures of individual CSDs (e.g., CSD4, PDB: 2YTY; CSD5, PDB: 2YTV) have provided atomic-level insights into the RNA recognition mechanism [16]. CSDE1 exhibits relaxed RNA-binding specificity with a preference for purine-rich motifs (core sequence AAGUA/G or AACG), and its target selection is further influenced by interactions with protein partners such as PABPC1, STRAP, and G3BP1 [12, 14]. Additionally, CSDE1 undergoes extensive post-translational modifications, including phosphorylation and ubiquitylation, which preferentially localize to non-canonical CSDs and may modulate its function through structural adjustments [14]. The existence of multiple protein isoforms further contributes to its functional diversity. This adaptor function is exemplified by the recent structural characterization of the CSDE1-eIF3a-RPA2 ternary complex [17]. In this “zipper-like” cross structure, the CSD1 domain serves as a central hub, simultaneously binding the PCI domain of eIF3a and a specific 34-nucleotide sequence within the RPA2 IRES. Key residues mediating these interactions have been mapped, including R48, H52, D76, and R78 of CSD1 for RNA recognition, and N57, N59, D62 for PCI binding. Together, these features—differential CSD binding preferences, a purine-rich consensus motif, and modulation by protein partners—enable CSDE1 to selectively recognize distinct mRNA targets including PTPN2, RPA2, VIM, and RAC1 in a context-dependent manner [17–19]. Its role in oncogenesis is complex and tissue-specific: while it primarily acts as an oncoprotein by promoting proliferation, invasion, metastasis and apoptosis [20–22], it can also function as a tumour suppressor in contexts such as cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma by inducing cellular senescence and suppressing YBX1 synthesis [15].

Beyond its roles in tumour biology, CSDE1 has long been recognized as a key player in viral infections and host antiviral responses. Early studies established CSDE1 as an IRES trans-acting factor (ITAF) that binds to the internal ribosome entry sites (IRESs) of several viral RNAs, including rhinovirus, poliovirus and human immunodeficiency virus-1, thereby regulating cap-independent translation initiation [23–27]. A seminal work by Hunt et al. demonstrated that CSDE1 is required for the internal initiation of translation of human rhinovirus RNA, acting synergistically with PTB to stimulate IRES-dependent translation [23]. Subsequent studies have confirmed CSDE1 as a major regulator of type I IRES activity in poliovirus-like viruses [28]. This function positions CSDE1 as a critical host factor for viral replication. Indeed, CSDE1 has been shown to facilitate the replication of vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) by interacting with the viral genome, acting as a major cellular co-factor for VSV replication [29, 30]. Under therapeutic selective pressure, a point mutation in CSDE1 (CSDE1P5S) confers resistance to oncolytic virotherapy by disrupting viral transcription, while simultaneously generating a neo-epitope that can be targeted immunotherapeutically.

However, despite the established role of CSDE1 in tumour - intrinsic processes, its specific functions and mechanisms in shaping the TIME remain largely unexplored and lack systematic elaboration. Intriguingly, mounting evidence indicates that the repertoire of CSDE1 target mRNAs includes numerous genes encoding powerful immunoregulators, including cytokines, chemokines and immune checkpoint proteins. This striking association positions CSDE1 at the nexus of tumour - immune cell crosstalk, suggesting it may be a pivotal master regulator of immune homeostasis within the TIME.

In this review, we aim to bridge this critical knowledge gap by synthesising emerging evidence into a novel ‘Spatiotemporal Translational Reprogramming Framework’. This framework proposes that CSDE1 functions as a central decoder of microenvironmental cues, dynamically configuring mRNA translation across both temporal (e.g., tumour initiation, progression, therapy resistance) and spatial (e.g., cytoplasm, nucleoplasm) dimensions to orchestrate immunosuppression. We will first analyze the multifaceted roles of CSDE1 in tumour cells, immune cells, and the TIME, then elaborate on this unifying framework, and finally explore its therapeutic implications.

The multifaceted roles of CSDE1 in tumour immunity

In this section, the multifaceted roles of CSDE1 in sculpting the immunosuppressive TIME (See Table 1) are systematically analyzed. First, we examine its tumour-intrinsic functions in driving immune evasion (Sect. “CSDE1 as a driver of tumour cell immune evasion”). Then, we elaborate on its cell-intrinsic regulation of immune cell development and function (Sect. “CSDE1 as a regulator of immune cell function”). Finally, we synthesise how these actions converge to architect the broader immunosuppressive niche (Sect. “CSDE1 as an architect of the immunosuppressive niche”).

Multifaceted roles of CSDE1 in tumour cells, immune cells, and the tumour immune microenvironment

CSDE1 as a driver of tumour cell immune evasion

Within tumour cells, elevated CSDE1 has been observed to bind to and stabilize protein tyrosine phosphatase non-receptor type 2(PTPN2) mRNA, thereby leading to increased T-cell protein tyrosine phosphatase (TCPTP) protein. TCPTP dephosphorylates and inactivates the transcription factor signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1), resulting in the downregulation of MHC class I and antigen-processing machinery (e.g., TAP1, TAP2, TAPBP). This impairment in antigen presentation reduces tumour cell immunogenicity and facilitates immune escape [18].

This suppression of adaptive immune recognition represents a fundamental mechanism of immune evasion. Intriguingly, tumours employ a broader arsenal of complementary strategies to subvert immunity. For example, the recently characterised CD24-Siglec10 axis functions as a potent ‘don‘t eat me’ signal, enabling tumour cells to evade innate immune surveillance by inhibiting macrophage phagocytosis, a mechanism distinct from but potentially complementary to antigen presentation suppression [35]. The potential of CSDE1’s translational regulatory network might extend to modulate such non-redundant checkpoints (e.g., CD24) warrants further investigation. This prospective positions CSDE1 not merely as an isolated regulator, but as a prospective central orchestrator with the capacity to coordinate a cohesive immunosuppressive program across both adaptive and innate immune axes.

The study by Wu, Yin, et al. establishes a crucial mechanism linking CSDE1 to genotoxic drug resistance and immune evasion [17]. This work reveals that CSDE1 is a critical promoter of genotoxic drug resistance in tumour cells by significantly enhancing their DNA repair capacity. It has been demonstrated to specifically upregulate two major DNA damage repair pathways: nucleotide excision repair (NER) and homologous recombination (HR). It is imperative to note that this regulatory process occurs post-transcriptionally: CSDE1 knockdown or overexpression exerts no significant effect on RPA2(replication protein A2) mRNA levels, but specifically enhances RPA2 protein expression through the formation of a unique ternary complex. In this complex, CSDE1 has been demonstrated to bind directly to the IRES region of RPA2 mRNA and to recruit the translation initiation factor eIF3a. This process has been shown to enhance the translation of RPA2, a crucial component of the DNA repair machinery, to a significant degree. As a result, tumour cells with high CSDE1 expression exhibit more efficient repair of drug-induced DNA damage, as evidenced by reduced levels of DNA double-strand breaks (measured by γH2AX foci and comet assays), and ultimately achieve significantly increased survival rate upon genotoxic drug treatment.

Beyond its role in translational reprogramming, CSDE1 also influences viral transcriptional regulation. Kottke et al. delineate a novel mechanism of acquired treatment resistance wherein CSDE1 evolves from a crucial host factor for oncolytic vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) replication into a potent driver of immune evasion. Under the selective pressure of suboptimal VSV-IFNβ therapy, APOBEC3B-mediated mutagenesis has been shown to induce a point mutation (CSDE1C−T), resulting in a proline-to-serine substitution at position 5s (CSDE1P5S). This gain-of-function mutant protein dominantly inhibits VSV replication by disrupting viral transcription, specifically impairing polymerase disengagement at the P/M intergenic region. This results in a significant decrease in unicistronic M mRNA and protein synthesis, thus facilitating the escape of tumour cells from viral oncolysis and enabling aggressive relapse. This work establishes CSDE1P5S as a predictable and high-frequency driver of an escape phenotype in response to oncolytic virotherapy [29].

Saltel et al. demonstrated that CSDE1 is essential for the formation of a novel subnuclear compartment, the Unr-rich nucleoplasmic reticulum (Unr-NR), in polyploid cells under genotoxic stress (e.g., chemotherapy) [31]. These structures function as efficient hubs for mRNA translation, potentially allowing for the rapid production of pro-survival proteins [22]. It is hypothesized that CSDE1 promotes a stress-adaptive response, thereby facilitating the survival of a subpopulation of treatment-resistant tumor cells. These surviving cells, which frequently exhibit a polyploid giant cancer cell (PGCC) phenotype, are known to evade immune surveillance and contribute to relapse. Consequently, CSDE1-mediated Unr-NR formation does not directly inhibit immune effector function, but rather establishes the cellular substrate for escape by ensuring the viability of tumour cells in the face of environmental pressures that would otherwise be lethal. This indirect pathway serves to expand the repertoire of mechanisms by which CSDE1 fosters an immunosuppressive tumour ecosystem.

CSDE1 as a regulator of immune cell function

Beyond its role in enabling tumour cell-intrinsic evasion, CSDE1 also acts as a master regulator of immune cell function, with its influence extending from the differentiation of haematopoietic stem cells to the effector functions of lymphocytes within the TIME.

Foremost, CSDE1 acts as a crucial post-transcriptional brake in CD4 + T cells by binding to and promoting the degradation of Retinoic Acid Receptor-related Orphan Receptor γt (RORγt) mRNA, thereby constraining Th17 polarization. However, under inflammatory conditions (e.g., IL-23 stimulation), CSDE1 itself undergoes Tripartite Motif-containing protein 21(TRIM21)-mediated degradation, which results in the release of this brake and enables robust Th17 responses. This positions CSDE1 as a central regulatory node whose dysregulation contributes to autoimmunity, such as inflammatory bowel disease [5]. Notably, this context-dependent regulatory mechanism of CSDE1 may extend beyond autoimmune settings to influence immune responses within the TIME. In the TIME, the dual functionality of Th17 cells—capable of mediating both anti-tumour immunity and pro-tumour inflammation—creates a precarious balance that could be swayed by CSDE1’s “brake-and-release” dynamics. In conditions of steady-state or tolerogenicity, the sustained expression of CSDE1 has been observed to exert a marked effect on the polarization of Th17 cells, potentially attenuating their function in the recruitment of effector lymphocytes and the stimulation of anti-tumour cytokine production. Conversely, in highly inflammatory tumours with elevated IL-23 or other signals that trigger TRIM21-mediated CSDE1 degradation, the loss of this post-transcriptional brake could lead to unchecked Th17 expansion and functional skewing towards a pathogenic, pro-angiogenic, and immunosuppressive phenotype, thereby fostering tumour progression and immune evasion.

Beyond T cells, CSDE1 exerts precise temporal control over B cell development. In the study conducted by Chen et al., it was established that CSDE1 forms a functional complex with the serine-threonine kinase receptor-associated protein (STRAP), thereby orchestrating the germinal center response and terminal differentiation of B cells into antibody-secreting plasma cells [32]. This work highlights a fundamental mechanism in which post-transcriptional control of key transcriptional regulators determines cellular fate in the adaptive immune system.

Mechanistically, the CSDE1-STRAP complex has been demonstrated to bind directly to the coding sequences (CDS) and 3’ untranslated regions (UTRs) of target mRNAs, thereby coupling their translation with decay and exerting precise temporal control over protein expression. A principal target of this regulation is BTB and CNC homolog 2 (Bach2), a transcription factor that suppresses the plasma cell differentiation program. The CSDE1-STRAP complex ensures a rapid but transient pulse of Bach2 protein expression following B cell activation, which is essential for initial proliferation. Subsequently, it facilitates the rapid degradation of Bach2 mRNA, enabling for the timely downregulation of Bach2 protein required for the initiation of differentiation.

Conditional deletion of CSDE1in mature B cells has been shown to uncouple this translation-decay cycle, resulting in sustained and elevated Bach2 expression. This resulted in a severe block in plasma cell generation, impaired germinal center formation, and a profound defect in T cell-dependent, high-affinity antibody responses. The study also identified other targets, such as Atp2a2, whose expression is modulated by the complex, thus highlighting its role in tuning the proteome for successful differentiation.

Beyond its role in plasma cell differentiation, emerging evidence implicates CSDE1 in regulating B cell infiltration within the tumour microenvironment. A very recent study by Li et al. demonstrated that systemic Csde1 knockout in a murine NSCLC model significantly increased tumor-infiltrating B lymphocytes (TIL-Bs), accompanied by elevated immunoglobulins (IgA, IgD, IgG) in plasma and tumour tissues. Functionally, B cell depletion reversed the tumour-suppressive effect of Csde1 knockout, confirming that the antitumor activity of CSDE1 deficiency is B cell-dependent [36]. These findings expand the immunoregulatory repertoire of CSDE1, positioning it as a key modulator of B cell recruitment and function within the TIME.

At the most fundamental level, CSDE1 governs the very origin of the immune system by regulating haematopoietic stem/progenitor cell (HSPC) generation. It is highly expressed in hemogenic endothelium and directly binds ctnnb1 mRNA, encoding β-catenin. Instead of stabilising the transcript, CSDE1 has been shown to promote the translation initiation of ctnnb1 by facilitating the recruitment of the key initiation factor eIF4a1a. This has been demonstrated to boost β-catenin protein synthesis and amplify Wnt signalling, a pathway indispensable for HSPC emergence. Consequently, loss of CSDE1 has been shown to compromise HSPC production and subsequent lymphoid development, thus revealing a critical post-transcriptional checkpoint in the foundation of the immune system [33].

The essential role of CSDE1 in developmental hematopoiesis is well established, and its function as a translational enhancer of Wnt/β-catenin signaling in HSPCs also suggests its potential role in tumour and therapy-related hematopoiesis. A robust hematopoietic output is imperative for maintaining effective anti-tumour immunity, and chemotherapy or radiotherapy frequently compromises HSPC function, resulting in myelosuppression. Consequently, augmenting the CSDE1-mediated Wnt pathway could be a viable strategy to expedite hematopoietic recovery and immune reconstitution subsequent to cytotoxic treatments. Furthermore, it is possible that tumours may exploit or inhibit this regulatory node in order to suppress the generation of immune cells, thus contributing to the creation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment. The investigation of CSDE1’s activity within HSPCs in tumour settings has the potential to reveal the mechanisms of therapy-induced cytopenias and immune escape. This, in turn, could inform approaches to bolster haematopoietic resilience and anti-tumour immunity.

The seemingly opposing roles of CSDE1 in suppressing Th17 differentiation while promoting B cell differentiation raise the question of how these functions integrate within the TIME [5, 32]. Recent evidence suggests that despite its role in B cell differentiation, CSDE1 limits B cell infiltration into tumors [28], thereby preventing effective humoral immunity at the tumor site. Together with its suppression of Th17 polarization and antigen presentation [5, 18], these mechanisms converge to create a dominant immunosuppressive milieu in CSDE1-high tumors, characterized by reduced T cell effector function and restricted B cell accumulation. This integrated evidence explains how CSDE1-high tumour cells navigate potential immune activation—promoting B cell development systemically while excluding them from the TIME.

In summary, CSDE1 deploys a multi-tiered strategy to regulate immunity: it controls the development of immune cells at their source (HSPCs), dictates the differentiation of lymphocytes (B and T cells), and ultimately defines the functional state of the TIME. Beyond its roles in immune cell function, CSDE1 is also indispensable for the development of non-immune blood lineages. For instance, CSDE1 is critical for erythroid differentiation, as its deficiency impairs erythropoiesis and leads to anaemia, highlighting its broad importance in hematopoietic development [37].

CSDE1 as an architect of the immunosuppressive niche

In addition to directly regulating immunomodulatory proteins, CSDE1 sculpts the immunosuppressive niche through a more indirect but equally profound mechanism: the suppression of immunogenic signaling. In addition to its role in conferring cell-intrinsic resistance to genotoxic stress, the enhanced DNA repair orchestrated by CSDE1 has profound implications for tumour-immune crosstalk, primarily through the suppression of the innate immune cGAS-STING (cyclic GMP-AMP Synthase- Stimulator of Interferon Genes) pathway. The mechanistic logic is as follows: genotoxic agents (e.g., chemotherapy, radiation) cause nuclear and mitochondrial DNA damage. A proportion of the damaged DNA leaks into the cytosol, where it is recognised by the sensor cGAS. cGAS activation has been demonstrated to catalyse the production of cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP), a second messenger that activates the adapter protein STING. This triggers a signaling cascade leading to the production of type I interferons (IFNs) and other inflammatory cytokines, which are critical for dendritic cell maturation, T cell priming, and the overall anti-tumour immune response.

The study by Cui et al. provides direct experimental evidence supporting this mechanism [17]. Using BrdU labeling to visualize DNA fragments, the authors demonstrated that CSDE1 knockdown significantly increased cytosolic DNA accumulation in H1299 and A549 cells following bleomycin-induced DNA damage, whereas CSDE1 overexpression markedly reduced DNA fragmentation [17]. Consistent with these findings, CSDE1 depletion enhanced the expression of cGAS and STING, while CSDE1 overexpression suppressed their expression [17]. Functional validation using the cGAS-STING pathway inhibitor RU.521 confirmed that this pathway mediates CSDE1-driven genotoxic drug resistance: in the presence of RU.521, CSDE1 knockdown no longer significantly reduced IC50 values for cisplatin, 5-FU, epirubicin, or daunorubicin [17]. Thus, CSDE1 suppresses cGAS-STING activation by reducing cytosolic DNA accumulation through enhanced DNA repair.

Thus, CSDE1 does not merely help tumor cells survive treatment; it actively engineers an immunologically silent or “cold” tumour microenvironment by intercepting a critical cell-intrinsic immunogenic circuit. This places CSDE1 in a pivotal role as a master architect of immunosuppression, leveraging DNA repair machinery to simultaneously ensure survival and evade immune detection.

CSDE1 has been demonstrated to play a pivotal role in shaping the immune microenvironment also through its regulation of the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) [34]. It has been demonstrated that this protein acts as a crucial post-transcriptional enhancer of the SASP by directly binding to and stabilizing the mRNAs of key immunomodulatory factors, including cytokines such as Interleukin-6(IL-6) and Colony-Stimulating Factor 1 (CSF1), and adhesion molecules such as Intracellular Adhesion Molecule-1 (ICAM1). This function ensures the robust production and secretion of a potent array of soluble signalling molecules. The resultant CSDE1-dependent SASP is biologically active and capable of inducing paracrine senescence, triggering cell death and proliferation arrest in recipient cells. Consequently, the absence of CSDE1 dismantles this critical communicative axis, abolishing the SASP’s capacity to recruit and instruct immune cells. This disruption impairs the establishment of an immunostimulatory microenvironment, ultimately compromising immune-mediated surveillance and the clearance of pre-malignant cells, thereby facilitating tumor immune evasion.

It is important to reconcile the tumour-suppressive role of CSDE1-enhanced SASP in senescence with the immunosuppressive function of CSDE1 in established tumors. SASP exhibits well-established context-dependent duality: acute SASP promotes immune-mediated clearance of senescent cells (senescence surveillance), whereas chronic SASP can foster a pro-inflammatory, tumour-promoting microenvironment [38]. In the oncogene-induced senescence (OIS) model, CSDE1 directly enhances SASP factor mRNA stability, contributing to paracrine senescence and tumor suppression [34]. However, in established tumours where CSDE1 promotes immune evasion via other mechanisms (e.g., cGAS-STING suppression, antigen presentation inhibition), its role in SASP regulation has not been directly demonstrated. Whether CSDE1-mediated SASP shifts from an acute, tumor-suppressive to a chronic, pro-tumourigenic program during tumor progression remains to be investigated. Thus, the function of CSDE1 in SASP regulation may be context-dependent and warrants further study in the tumor microenvironment.

A spatiotemporal framework for CSDE1-mediated translational reprogramming in the TIME

Based on the evidence discussed, we know that CSDE1 drives tumour immune evasion through diverse yet seemingly disconnected roles. Current evidence lacks a unified mechanism explaining how it dynamically coordinates these processes. In this study, we propose a ‘Spatiotemporal Translational Reprogramming Framework’ in which CSDE1 functions as a master regulator that decodes microenvironmental cues to orchestrate mRNA translation across both a temporal axis (e.g., tumour initiation, progression, therapy resistance) and a spatial axis (e.g., cytoplasm, Unr-NR, stress granules, immunological synapse). This framework also integrates tumour-intrinsic evasion with immune cell regulation to sculpt an immunosuppressive TIME and confer therapy resistance.

The proposed framework comprises two interwoven dimensions: a temporal axis, which charts the functional evolution of CSDE1 across disease stages from tumour initiation and progression to therapy resistance; and a spatial axis, which maps its functional localization to specific subcellular compartments such as the cytoplasm, Unr-NR, stress granules and immunological synapse. The concerted action of these axes dictates the final proteomic output, thereby enabling context-specific immunoregulatory reprogramming.

Within this framework, “decode” refers to the process by which CSDE1, through alterations in its expression level, post-translational modifications (e.g., phosphorylation), subcellular localization, or formation of specific complexes with co-factors (e.g., eIF3a, STRAP), senses and responds to specific microenvironmental cues (input). This results in the differential regulation of target mRNA translation, stability, or localization (output). The aforementioned input cues encompass, but are not limited to, genotoxic stress (e.g., chemotherapy), cytokine signalling (e.g., IL-23), oncogenic pathway activation, intercellular contact, and various cellular stress conditions (see Table 2 for further details). For instance, during the early stages of tumourigenesis under conditions of microenvironmental stress, there is an increase in the expression of CSDE1 (an alteration in expression level). This, in turn, binds directly to and stabilises PTPN2 mRNA (decoding action), which leads to an increase in the synthesis of TCPTP protein (output). This ultimately results in the suppression of STAT1 signalling and antigen presentation machinery [18].

Temporal axis of CSDE1-mediated translational reprogramming framework

Phase 1: Tumour initiation and early immune editing: During the initial phases of tumorigenesis, CSDE1-mediated post-transcriptional reprogramming is critical. CSDE1 expression is epigenetically upregulated in nascent tumour cells, particularly under mechanotransduction stress within the microenvironment [18]. The expression of this gene is controlled by epigenetic modification, specifically H3K4 trimethylation of the CSDE1 locus, which is mediated by the methyltransferase SET and MYND domain containing 3 (SMYD3). This epigenetic modification leads to heterogeneous expression of CSDE1 in tumour cells, which in turn influences their immunogenicity. During this phase, high CSDE1 expression is closely associated with immune evasion of tumor cells, whereas low CSDE1 expression renders tumor cells more susceptible to immune recognition and elimination. Notably, CSDE1high and CSDE1low subpopulations can interconvert, thus emphasising a dynamic and reversible epigenetic landscape. These findings not only elucidate the regulatory mechanism governing CSDE1 during early tumorigenesis, but also provide novel insights into the dynamic process of tumour immune escape (See Fig. 1a).

Phase 2: Tumour progression and metastatic: During tumour progression and metastasis, CSDE1 shifts its functional output to orchestrate a pro-invasive and pro-metastatic translatome primarily through direct translational control of key mRNAs encoding effector proteins of these processes. This post-transcriptional rewiring facilitates the rapid acquisition of aggressive phenotypes without the delay of new transcription.

The role of CSDE1 in overcoming translational barriers to enhance the synthesis of pro-metastatic proteins is best exemplified in melanoma. In this study, the use of iCLIP-seq analyses reveal that CSDE1 binds to the 3’UTRs of transcripts such as VIM (vimentin) and RAC1(Ras-related C3 botulinum toxin substrate 1), potently enhancing their translation elongation without altering mRNA abundance. Ribosome profiling confirms this mechanism, showing a redistribution of ribosomes toward the 5’ end of these mRNAs upon CSDE1 depletion, indicative of stalled elongation. This reprogramming is complemented by CSDE1’s ability to suppress tumor suppressors like PTEN(Phosphatase and Tensin Homolog) and to counteract repressive pathways, such as miRNA-mediated silencing of PMEPA1(Prostate Transmembrane Eprotein, Androgen-induced 1) [19]. Furthermore, its phosphorylation-dependent association with ribosomes positions CSDE1 to directly facilitate the translation of its target mRNAs [21, 39]. This paradigm of CSDE1-driven translational control of pro-metastatic genes extends to other tumor types. In glioma, CSDE1 has been shown to bind the 3’UTR of RPL9 mRNA, upregulating RPL9 protein expression and subsequently promoting vimentin-mediated cell migration [45], further illustrating the conserved nature of CSDE1-regulated metastatic programs. A parallel mechanism has been identified as a driver of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) progression, whereby CSDE1-mediated translational upregulation of VIM and RAC1 activates the ERK(Extracellular Signal-Regulated Kinase) signalling pathway, thereby enhancing invasive and metastatic capabilities [46].

This paradigm of CSDE1-driven translational control has been observed in other tumours. Although upstream regulatory mechanisms differ—ranging from loss of tumour-suppressive miRNAs (e.g., miR-371b-5p in Triple-Negative Breast Cancer (TNBC)) to lncRNA-mediated miRNA sponging (e.g., LINC00205, LINC01234)—the functional outcome of CSDE1 overexpression consistently converges on promoting invasion and metastasis [47–49]. The identification of the pro-metastatic gene RAC1 as a key downstream target across diverse tumours (e.g., TNBC) suggests a conserved oncogenic network orchestrated by CSDE1 at the translational level.

Although less delineated, evidence from pancreatic, thyroid and colorectal cancers further underscores the critical role of CSDE1 in promoting invasion [20, 40, 50]. In colorectal cancer (CRC), CSDE1 knockdown downregulates c-MYC protein without affecting its mRNA levels, and its correlation with EMT (Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transition) markers (VIM, β-catenin, SNAIL) and chemoresistance strongly implies a pivotal role for translational reprogramming in driving oncogenic networks, warranting further investigation.

The regulation of CSDE1 during this phase is multi-layered. Upstream, CSDE1 expression is modulated by loss of tumor-suppressive miRNAs (e.g., miR-371b-5p) and lncRNA-mediated stabilization (LINC00205, LINC01234), as documented in breast and lung cancer models [23–25, 47–49]. At the post-translational level, phosphorylation of CSDE1 enhances its association with ribosomes and target mRNAs, fine-tuning its pro-metastatic activity [8, 21, 39]. These regulatory inputs collectively sustain CSDE1-driven oncogenic networks during tumor progression.

In summary, during the progression phase, CSDE1 functions as a central oncoprotein whose upregulation, achieved through diverse upstream mechanisms, is shown to drive metastasis primarily through direct translational control of a network of mRNAs that execute invasion and metastasis programs(See Fig. 1b).

Phase 3: Therapy-induced stress and adaptive resistance: Beyond its roles in baseline evasion, CSDE1 is a key mediator of the dynamic adaptive response to genotoxic therapy. The study by Cui et al. illustrates how this RNA-binding protein functions as a stress-responsive hub, enabling tumours to rapidly counteract treatment-induced damage [17]. Under the selective pressure of chemotherapy or radiation, the CSDE1-eIF3a-RPA2 axis is engaged to mount a dual defence: 1) Rapid Damage Control: It mediates a post-transcriptional surge in RPA2 translation, accelerating the repair of DNA lesions to ensure immediate cell survival. 2)Preemptive Immune Silencing: By efficiently repairing nuclear DNA, it prevents the cytosolic leakage of DNA fragments, thereby preemptively suppressing cGAS-STING activation and the ensuing interferon response. Thus, CSDE1 facilitates a quintessential adaptive resistance: it simultaneously manages the cell-intrinsic cytotoxic threat and neutralizes the cell-extrinsic immune danger signal, allowing persisting cells to thrive post-therapy.

In addition, under genotoxic assault, CSDE1 drives the formation of the Unr-rich nucleoplasmic reticulum (Unr-NR), effectively creating a protected nuclear translation hub [31]. This compartmentalization facilitates the rapid and efficient synthesis of pro-survival proteins, thereby enabling tumour cells to withstand the acute cytotoxic pressure of treatment. This mechanism represents a direct, inducible adaptation that sustains a durable polyploid cell population, which is a cornerstone of acquired resistance and eventual disease relapse(See Fig. 1c).

The stress-responsive function of CSDE1 in this phase is directly activated by genotoxic stress itself. DNA damage induced by chemotherapy or radiation triggers two parallel pathways documented in the literature: assembly of the CSDE1-eIF3a-RPA2 ternary complex to enhance DNA repair [17], and formation of Unr-NR structures that compartmentalize pro-survival translation [31]. Thus, genotoxic stress serves as the primary upstream signal, reorganizing CSDE1’s protein interactions and subcellular localization to mount an adaptive survival response.

The spatiotemporal regulation of gene expression by CSDE1 is not a phenomenon exclusive to tumourigenesis. Emerging evidence indicates that CSDE1 plays an equally critical and dynamic role in the development and function of immune cells. For instance, within the haematopoietic system, CSDE1-mediated translational control is pivotal for the fate determination of hematopoietic stem cells, the functional differentiation of T cells, and the antibody response of B cells [5, 32, 33]. The precise timing and subcellular localization of CSDE1 activity in these immune compartments underscore its broad role as a master regulator of cell identity and function across diverse biological systems. The specific mechanisms and functional impacts of CSDE1 in the regulation of immune cells have been detailed in a previous section.

Translation on membrane-bound organelles

CSDE1 has been identified as a central regulator of cytoplasmic post-transcriptional gene expression (See Fig. 2a). Its function as an RNA-binding protein is characterised by its predominant subcellular localisation within the cytoplasm, where it associates with polysomes and target mRNAs to modulate their translational output [5]. Robust evidence, including cellular fractionation studies and ribosomal profiling, demonstrates that CSDE1 controls the efficiency of translation elongation and IRES-dependent initiation for a specific network of transcripts [5, 21]. This regulation occurs post-nucleocytoplasmic export of its target mRNAs, firmly positioning CSDE1 as a key determinant of the cytoplasmic proteome, whose spatial function is fundamental to its biological impact.

The concept of CSDE1-mediated translational reprogramming gains a profound spatial dimension with the discovery of the Unr-NR. Saltel et al. provide definitive evidence that CSDE1 is not merely a diffuse cytoplasmic translator, but rather a master organiser of a dedicated translational subnuclear compartment [31]. The Unr-NR signifies a remodelling of the nuclear envelope, thereby engendering an intranuclear territory that is both continuous with the cytoplasm and topologically distinct. Within this spatially confined niche, CSDE1 co-localises with its target poly(A) RNAs, translation initiation/elongation factors (eIF4E, eEF2), and ribosomes to form a highly efficient “translation factory.” This spatial partitioning facilitates the selective and potentially regulated translation of specific mRNA subsets away from the bulk cytoplasm. However, whether specific mRNAs are selectively enriched within Unr-NRs compared to the bulk cytoplasm remains to be determined. While Unr-NRs concentrate poly(A) RNA and translation machinery [31], direct evidence for target selectivity is currently lacking. These structures may primarily serve as a stress-adaptive platform to enhance the translation of pro-survival transcripts under genotoxic conditions, without stringent mRNA selection [22]. It is hypothesised that the Unr-NR is the physical manifestation of CSDE1’s ability to orchestrate translation in a spatially restricted manner. This paradigm that could allow for the rapid and localised production of proteins that are critical for stress response, cell fate decisions, or, in the context of tumour, pro-metastatic and survival factors(See Fig. 2b).

The functional significance of this spatially compartmentalised translation mechanism is strongly validated in the clinical context of melanoma. Indacochea et al. demonstrated that the intracellular distribution of CSDE1—rather than its total expression level—serves as a determinant prognostic factor in melanoma [51]. By developing a highly specific monoclonal antibody, they confirmed that although total CSDE1 protein levels decrease with tumour progression, its cytoplasmic-to-nuclear (C/N) ratio significantly increases. An elevated CSDE1 C/N ratio was closely associated with more aggressive tumour characteristics, such as nodular phenotype and high mitotic index. Furthermore, the ratio was also associated with a poorer progression-free survival rate in patients.

It is hypothesised that the observed increase in the C/N ratio in clinical samples represents the histological manifestation of an aberrant spatially restricted translation program mediated by CSDE1 and driven by the Unr-NR. However, this interpretation requires caution. The increased C/N ratio could alternatively reflect altered nucleo-cytoplasmic shuttling, enhanced cytoplasmic retention, or increased nuclear export of CSDE1, independent of its assembly into nuclear invaginations [31, 51]. Indeed, Unr-NRs are induced under specific stress conditions and are not constitutively present in all cells with high CSDE1 cytoplasmic levels [22, 31]. Further studies are needed to dissect the relationship between CSDE1 subcellular distribution and the formation of functional Unr-NRs in tumors. The process of malignant transformation may depend on the recruitment of CSDE1 to a perinuclear/cytoplasmic compartment, thereby enabling the efficient operation of its “translation factory.” This factory preferentially facilitates the localized translation of key mRNAs involved in invasion, metastasis, and immune evasion, including PD-L1.

While the predominant characterisation of CSDE1 is as a cytoplasmic translational regulator, emerging evidence suggests a role for CSDE1 in stress-responsive pathways that are intimately linked to membrane-bound organelles. Functional studies demonstrate that CSDE1 controls the expression of core endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress response factors, including GRP78(Glucose-Regulated Protein 78), CHOP(CCAAT/Enhancer-Binding Protein Homologous Protein), and PERK(PKR-like Endoplasmic Reticulum Kinase), and modulates cell survival during unfolded protein response [39, 41]. This, coupled with its well-established function as a molecular bridge that recruits protein complexes to specific mRNAs, suggests a plausible, albeit not yet definitively proven, role for CSDE1 in orchestrating localized translation at organellar interfaces such as the ER membrane(See Fig. 2c). Further spatial proteomic and high-resolution imaging evidence is required to validate the precise subcellular localization and mechanistic activity of these membrane proteins.

Translation regulation by membraneless organelles

The subcellular spatial organisation of mRNA-binding proteins into biomolecular condensates, such as stress granules (SGs) and processing bodies (PBs), is now recognised as a crucial layer of translational control. Groundbreaking high-density proximity mapping studies have begun to elucidate the intricate architectural principles governing these granules. A significant finding is that the core SG proteome, which includes master regulators such as GTPase Activating Protein (SH3 Domain)-Binding Protein 1 (G3BP1), pre-assembles into a network of stable sub-complexes even in the absence of external stress. This pre-ordained interactome, rich in proteins with intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs), serves as a molecular blueprint, enabling the rapid, stimulus-induced liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) that drives SG formation. Within this spatially organized framework, CSDE1 emerges as a pivotal scaffold and functional effector. It is imperative to the structural integrity of the granule, as its loss has been shown to disrupt SG assembly. It operates within a network that connects mRNA translation, decay, and storage machineries [42].

The spatial sequestration of specific mRNA transcripts into CSDE1-containing SGs constitutes a fundamental mechanism of translational reprogramming. The process under discussion has been demonstrated to effectively halt global translation while selectively promoting the cap-independent translation of mRNAs encoding pro-survival factors, such as oncoproteins and immune checkpoint molecules like PD-L1(See Fig. 2d). The selective enrichment of mRNAs within SGs is governed by RNA-intrinsic features and protein interactions. Transcriptomic analyses show that mRNAs with longer coding regions, extended UTRs, and lower basal translation efficiency are preferentially recruited to SGs [52]. Within this framework, CSDE1 functions as a core scaffold protein that, together with G3BP1, forms a pre-assembled interaction network enabling rapid SG assembly [42]. Once sequestered, mRNAs are protected from decay through exclusion from the cytoplasmic degradation machinery [52]. Notably, immune checkpoint molecules such as PD-1 are translated from SG-enriched mRNAs following stress resolution, a process requiring microtubule-dependent transport via kinesin 1 and association with G3BP1-containing SGs [43]. Thus, SG-mediated mRNA sequestration not only protects transcripts from degradation but also enables their selective translation upon stress recovery. Consequently, this CSDE1-mediated spatial switch in translational output equips tumour cells with a powerful adaptive response to therapeutic stresses, including chemotherapy and radiotherapy, fostering traits like proliferation, metabolic reprogramming, immune evasion, and ultimately, therapy resistance [52]. Therefore, the spatial partitioning of translational control by CSDE1 within SGs is not merely a passive stress response but an active mechanistic strategy that tumour cells exploit for survival, positioning the CSDE1-SG axis as a promising therapeutic target for overcoming treatment resistance.

Localized translational control is a well-established paradigm in specialized cellular compartments. In neurons, RNA-binding proteins orchestrate synapse-specific protein synthesis by transporting mRNAs to distal dendrites and regulating their translation in response to synaptic activity. Disruption of this process—exemplified by loss-of-function CSDE1 mutations—is linked to neurodevelopmental disorders, underscoring its role in spatiotemporal translational regulation [44].

Given the conceptual parallels between neuronal and immunological synapses—both requiring rapid, spatially restricted protein synthesis to modulate cell-cell communication—we propose a speculative model in which CSDE1 may serve an analogous function in immune cells. According to this model, at the immunological synapse, localized translation of mRNAs encoding cytokines and signaling molecules could provide a rapid mechanism to fine-tune T cell receptor signaling (See Fig. 2e). This hypothesis is supported indirectly by CSDE1’s established roles in T cell polarization and B cell differentiation, both of which involve dynamic remodeling of the synaptic interface [5, 32]. However, direct experimental evidence for CSDE1-mediated localized translation at the immunological synapse is currently lacking. Future studies combining high-resolution imaging with synapse-specific ribosome profiling are warranted to directly test whether CSDE1 mediates localized translation at the immunological synapse.

The concept of “functional integration across spatial compartments” is one that has been a subject of much debate in recent years. The specialised roles of CSDE1 in distinct locales – such as pro-survival translation within the Unr-NR and stress-adaptive reprogramming in SGs – prompt the question of how these spatially segregated functions are integrated. A speculative model posits that CSDE1 may coordinate a tiered response to escalating stress. For instance, an initial genotoxic insult might preferentially trigger Unr-NR assembly for rapid damage management. Should stress persist, CSDE1 could be progressively recruited to SGs to implement a broader translational switch. This potential spatial hierarchy or functional relay would enable CSDE1 to mount precise, context-appropriate proteomic responses, underpinning its putative role as a master regulator of spatiotemporal adaptation in the TIME. Experimental validation of this hierarchical model is required.

Translational medicine: targeting CSDE1 for immunotherapy

The multifaceted roles of CSDE1 in fostering an immunosuppressive TIME and conferring therapy resistance render it an attractive therapeutic target. In the following section, we will explore several promising strategies that are aimed at either inhibiting CSDE1 function or exploiting its mechanisms for the purpose of therapeutic gain (See Fig. 3).

Sensitizing tumors to genotoxic therapy via CSDE1 inhibition

The phenomenon of resistance to genotoxic chemotherapeutic agents, primarily driven by hyperactivation of DNA damage repair (DDR) pathways, remains a significant clinical challenge. While DDR inhibitors such as Poly (ADP-Ribose) Polymerase (PARP) inhibitors have shown promise, the development of other agents has been hampered by limited efficacy and significant systemic toxicities, exemplified by the hematological adverse events associated with Ataxia Telangiectasia mutated and Rad3-related protein (ATR) inhibitors. This emphasises the critical need for the development of novel, safer therapeutic targets to overcome chemoresistance.

The study by Wu, Yin, et al. positions CSDE1 as a highly attractive candidate in this regard [17]. They demonstrated that CSDE1 knockdown significantly sensitises tumours to genotoxic drugs. Most notably, CSDE1 depletion appears to exhibit a favorable safety profile: histological analysis of major organs from Csde1-knockout mice revealed no abnormalities in the absence of DNA damage, and no baseline cell death was observed. Bioinformatic analysis of clinical samples revealed no association between CSDE1 expression levels and homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) scores in ovarian and breast cancers, suggesting that CSDE1 loss does not induce widespread genomic instability.

This compelling safety profile is strongly complemented by functional evidence across multiple tumour types. Independent studies have demonstrated that CSDE1 silencing enhances sensitivity to platinum-based chemotherapy in ovarian tumour cells and potentiates camptothecin-induced apoptosis in colorectal cancer models, without eliciting significant toxicity as a single agent [20, 53]. The findings, which have been consistent across a range of different types of tumours, highlight the role of CSDE1 as a key part of the cellular response to genotoxic stress. Inhibition of CSDE1 selectively hinders the survival of tumour cells during chemotherapy.

The combination of robust efficacy—potentiating genotoxic drug sensitivity across tumour types—and a potentially wide therapeutic window positions CSDE1 as a promising and novel therapeutic target. Consequently, pharmacological inhibition of CSDE1, utilised in conjunction with conventional genotoxic chemotherapy, emerges as a viable and promising strategy to overcome treatment resistance without incurring the debilitating toxicities associated with current DDR inhibitors (See Fig. 3a).

Preventing relapse by vaccinating against CSDE1 escape mutants

As demonstrated by Kottke et al., the study indicates that the very mechanism of escape can be therapeutically exploited [29, 30]. The CSDE1P5S mutation has been shown to generate a heteroclitic neo-epitope, constituting an escape-associated tumor antigen (EATA). This antigen is recognised by non-tolerized T cells and has the capacity to elicit cross-reactive responses against wild-type CSDE1. The authors strategically leveraged this predictable treatment-induced neo-antigenesis to develop a “trap and ambush” immunotherapy approach. They engineered an oncolytic virus expressing the mutant CSDE1P5S antigen (VSV-IFNβ-CSDE1C−T), which, despite attenuated replication, potently primes CSDE1P5S -specific T cells. This preemptive immunity has been shown to successfully target tumours as they evolve the escape phenotype, thereby effectively preventing relapse and achieving cures, particularly when employed in conjunction with immune checkpoint blockade. This paradigm of targeting therapy-induced immunopeptidomes presents a promising strategy to overcome acquired treatment resistance across multiple tumour modalities (See Fig. 3b).

Sensitizing tumors to checkpoint blockade by reversing CSDE1-mediated immunosuppression

Strong clinical and preclinical evidence underscores the role of CSDE1 as a determinant of response to tumour immunotherapy. Integrated analysis of TCGA data across multiple tumour types (e.g., Stomach Adenocarcinoma, Breast Carcinoma, Bladder Carcinoma) has revealed a significant negative correlation between CSDE1 expression and levels of MHC-I and antigen-processing machinery (e.g., Transporter associated with processing 1(TAP1)) [18]. This inverse relationship was validated at the protein level in human breast cancer and melanoma samples via immunohistochemistry. A retrospective analysis of cohorts from anti-PD-1 immunotherapy trials demonstrated that melanoma patients with high CSDE1 expression had significantly poorer overall survival, thus establishing its value as a potential predictive biomarker. This finding was functionally confirmed in vivo: mice bearing Csde1-knockout melanoma or breast cancer tumours exhibited a markedly improved response to anti-PD-1 treatment compared to those bearing control tumours. Mechanistically, this enhanced response is attributed to the restored immunogenicity of CSDE1-deficient tumour cells, as evidenced by their increased susceptibility to killing by antigen-specific T cells (e.g., Pmel-1 T cells) both in vitro and in vivo. Collectively these data position CSDE1 as a core mediator of immune escape through the suppression of antigen presentation, thus establishing it as a promising therapeutic target to overcome resistance to checkpoint blockade immunotherapy. Therefore, targeting CSDE1 presents a promising strategy to reverse tumour cell-induced immune suppression and enhance the efficacy of existing immunotherapies (See Fig. 3c).

Collectively, these strategies form a multi-pronged approach to target the CSDE1 pathway. The utilisation of small molecule inhibition provides a direct approach to the elimination of its oncogenic functions, particularly in the context of combination therapies. Neoantigen vaccines exemplify an ingenious immunological approach to preemptively targeting escape variants. Finally, leveraging CSDE1 inhibition to convert immunologically ‘cold’ tumors into ‘hot’ ones presents a powerful strategy to expand the efficacy of ICB. The choice of strategy may ultimately depend on tumour context, such as CSDE1 expression levels, subcellular localization, and the specific immunotherapeutic landscape.

The selection of a CSDE1-targeting strategy is contingent upon the particular characteristics of the tumour under consideration. Beyond the selection of the most effective molecular intervention, an emerging paradigm involves the active remodelling of the TIME itself to enhance therapeutic efficacy. Physical stimulation modalities, including targeted ultrasound, radiotherapy, and phototherapy, offer a non-invasive means to exert precise spatiotemporal control over the TIME. It has been demonstrated that these agents have the capacity to transiently disrupt physical barriers (e.g. the extracellular matrix, abnormal vasculature), modulate local immune responses, and enhance drug delivery [54]. A synergistic treatment sequence could be created by strategically combining such physical “priming” of the TIME with subsequent CSDE1-targeted therapies (e.g. small molecule inhibitors or vaccines). This approach would first reshape the immunosuppressive landscape spatially and temporally, then deploy precision molecular interventions within this optimised context, potentially overcoming delivery hurdles and primary resistance mechanisms.

Enhancing B cell-mediated antitumor immunity via CSDE1 inhibition

A recent study by Li et al. has unveiled a previously unrecognized role of CSDE1 in regulating B cell infiltration and function within the tumor microenvironment [36]. Using systemic Csde1 knockout mice and single-cell RNA sequencing in a NSCLC model, the authors demonstrated that CSDE1 depletion significantly increased TIL-Bs and enhanced humoral immunity, as evidenced by elevated IgA, IgD, and IgG levels in both plasma and tumour tissues. Importantly, this antitumor effect was B cell-dependent: depletion of B cells using an anti-CD20 antibody completely reversed the tumor-suppressive phenotype observed in Csde1 knockout mice. These findings suggest that targeting CSDE1 may exert dual therapeutic benefits: (1) relieving tumor-intrinsic immunosuppression (e.g., restoring antigen presentation and suppressing DNA repair-mediated immune evasion, as discussed above), and (2) fostering a B cell-rich tumor microenvironment that supports adaptive antitumor immunity. Given the emerging role of tumor-infiltrating B cells and tertiary lymphoid structures in predicting favorable responses to immune checkpoint blockade, combining CSDE1 inhibition with strategies that promote B cell activation or TLS neogenesis may represent a promising approach to enhance immunotherapy efficacy, particularly in tumors with limited T cell infiltration (See Fig. 3d).

Technical considerations for CSDE1 targeting

The therapeutic strategies outlined above highlight the potential of CSDE1 inhibition, yet their clinical translation necessitates critical assessment of available targeting technologies. Several approaches are under active investigation.

RNA-targeted strategies represent the most validated approach to date. siRNA-mediated CSDE1 knockdown has been shown to suppress invasiveness in pancreatic cancer cells and enhance chemosensitivity in lung cancer models, with CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing further confirming its therapeutic potential [45, 50]. However, delivery to solid tumors remains a major hurdle, requiring advanced formulations such as lipid nanoparticles. Off-target effects also demand careful optimization of sequence design and chemical modifications (e.g., 2’-O-methyl) to enhance specificity.

Small molecule inhibitors offer an alternative modality. Notably, the antibiotic clofoctol has been identified as a CSDE1-binding compound, exhibiting antitumour activity in glioma and prostate cancer models [45, 55]. Recent structural elucidation of CSDE1’s cold shock domains and its interaction with eIF3a and RPA2 provides a foundation for rational drug design, yet achieving selectivity over other cold shock domain-containing proteins remains challenging [17].

PROTACs (proteolysis-targeting chimeras) represent an emerging frontier. While no CSDE1-targeting PROTACs have been reported, the successful application of oligonucleotide-based PROTACs to degrade other RNA-binding proteins (e.g., FMRP) demonstrates proof-of-concept [56, 57]. These bifunctional molecules could enable sustained CSDE1 degradation with sub-stoichiometric dosing, potentially overcoming compensatory upregulation. However, identifying suitable CSDE1 ligands for PROTAC conjugation and optimizing linker chemistry remain key challenges.

Preclinical validation of these approaches must also address context-dependent duality of CSDE1 function and potential on-target toxicities given its essential roles in hematopoiesis and development. Despite these challenges, the expanding toolbox of RBP-targeting technologies positions CSDE1 as a tractable therapeutic target.

Limitations and future validation

Several limitations of this review should be acknowledged. First, the proposed Spatiotemporal Translational Reprogramming Framework, while integrative, remains largely hypothetical. Direct evidence demonstrating that CSDE1 dynamically shifts its functional output across defined temporal phases within the same tumour model is currently lacking. In particular, the mechanisms governing the transitions between phases—whether from immune evasion (Phase 1) to pro-invasive functions (Phase 2), or from progression to therapy resistance (Phase 3)—remain to be experimentally defined. These transitions may involve evolving microenvironmental cues, post-translational modifications, or changes in CSDE1-interacting partners, but such links are currently speculative. Longitudinal studies tracking CSDE1 expression, post-translational modifications, and target engagement over disease progression are needed to validate this temporal axis. Second, the spatial dimensions of CSDE1 function—particularly at the immunological synapse and within stress granules—require experimental substantiation through techniques such as spatial translation reporters or synapse-specific ribosome profiling. Third, the molecular basis of CSDE1-RNA recognition remains incompletely characterized; the sequence specificity, binding affinities, and structural determinants governing target selection have yet to be systematically defined. Fourth, the context-dependent duality of CSDE1 (pro-tumorigenic in most settings but tumor-suppressive in others) warrants caution in therapeutic targeting, and the physiological consequences of systemic inhibition require careful evaluation. Finally, the generalizability of the proposed framework across diverse tumor types remains to be established through pan-cancer analyses. Despite these limitations, the framework provides a heuristic foundation for generating testable hypotheses and guiding future mechanistic and therapeutic investigations.

Conclusion and future perspectives

CSDE1 has been identified as a master regulator of spatiotemporal translational reprogramming within the TIME, thereby enabling immune evasion through tumour-intrinsic mechanisms (e.g. suppressed antigen presentation, enhanced DNA repair) and extrinsic niche sculpting (e.g. SASP). Concurrently, it calibrates adaptive immunity by controlling immune cell development and differentiation. The proposed Spatiotemporal Translational Reprogramming Framework unifies these disparate roles, positioning CSDE1 as a critical decoder of microenvironmental context and a central mediator of therapy resistance. This framework expands the repertoire of master immunoregulatory RBPs which includes key players such as HuR, Regnase-1, and YBX1.

It is imperative to contemplate the translational potential of targeting CSDE1, whilst acknowledging the considerable challenges that are concomitant with this approach. A primary challenge is posed by its context-dependent duality, which functions as an oncoprotein in most settings while exhibiting tumour-suppressive activity in others (e.g., cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma). This inherent complexity, in conjunction with its indispensable functions in normal haematopoiesis and immunity, gives rise to valid concerns regarding its potential for on-target toxicity and systemic inhibition. Furthermore, while the current evidence is compelling, it is primarily derived from specific tumour models, and a comprehensive map of its target networks (“regulon”) across major human tumours is still evolving. Finally, the development of specific pharmacological inhibitors against RNA-binding proteins such as CSDE1 remains a technically challenging endeavour.

The following key priorities have been identified for future consideration: (1) Decoding the Context-Specific Regulon: Precisely mapping CSDE1’s target networks and binding dynamics across different tomour types and microenvironments to resolve its dual roles and identify actionable, disease-specific vulnerabilities. (2) Developing Context-Specific Therapeutic Strategies: Advancing modalities—such as degraders, neoantigen vaccines, or combination regimens—that can disrupt its oncogenic functions while sparing essential physiological roles, guided by robust biomarker development. 3)Comparative and Network Biology with Other Immunoregulatory RBPs: Investigating potential functional interactions between the CSDE1 regulon and those of other key RBPs (e.g., HuR, Regnase-1, YBX1) will elucidate the systems-level architecture of post-transcriptional control in the TIME. 4) Exploring Systemic Impact: Investigating its role in intercellular communication (e.g., via extracellular vesicles) to understand potential systemic effects on metastasis and immune modulation. 5) Integrating with Physical Modulation of the Tumor Microenvironment: Exploring the combination of CSDE1-targeted therapies with physical stimuli (e.g., ultrasound, radiotherapy) to dynamically prime the TIME and enhance therapeutic efficacy through spatiotemporally coordinated interventions. 6) Elucidating CSDE1’s Role in B Cell Biology: The recent identification of CSDE1 as a regulator of tumor-infiltrating B cells warrants further investigation into the molecular mechanisms by which CSDE1 controls B cell recruitment, differentiation, and function within the TIME.7) investigating CSDE1 at the Immunological Synapse: The proposed model of CSDE1-mediated localized translation at the T cell-APC interface, based on parallels with neuronal synapses and its roles in immune cells, requires direct experimental validation using synapse-specific imaging and ribosome profiling.

In summary, targeting CSDE1-mediated reprogramming represents a promising but intricate frontier in tumour immunotherapy. A clear-eyed appraisal of the associated challenges is essential for translating this knowledge into safe and effective therapies designed to overcome adaptive resistance and restore antitumour immunity.
