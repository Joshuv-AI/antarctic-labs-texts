Statin therapy in end-stage renal disease patients undergoing dialysis: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yun-Hui Huang, Fang-Pei Tsai, Ta-Wei Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectives:Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of mortality in dialysis patients. While clinical trials have suggested uncertain benefit of statin therapy in this population, several large-scale observational studies have reported an association between statin use and reduced all-cause mortality. To further explore this issue, we aimed to assess the effectiveness of statin therapy in dialysis patients by synthesizing real-world data.Materials and Methods:We systematically searched PubMed and Embase for observational studies comparing adult statin users to nonusers in maintenance dialysis patients. Outcomes included all-cause mortality, cardiovascular death, stroke, myocardial infarction (MI), and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE).Results:Nineteen studies with 310,370 dialysis patients were included. Statin use was associated with a lower risk of all-cause mortality (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 0.82; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.77–0.87) and cardiovascular death (aHR, 0.83; 95% CI, 0.74–0.94). However, sensitivity analyses showed attenuated associations (aHR, 0.92; 95% CI, 0.89–0.96 for all-cause mortality; aHR, 0.90; 95% CI, 0.82–0.99 for cardiovascular death). No significant associations were observed between statin use and the risk of stroke, MI, or MACE.Conclusion:Statin therapy modestly reduced the risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in patients undergoing maintenance dialysis but was not associated with a reduced risk of stroke, MI, or MACE.

INTRODUCTION

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the primary cause of death among chronic kidney disease (CKD) patients on dialysis, with cardiovascular mortality being 10–20 times higher than that of the general population [1]. Statins (hydroxymethylglutaryl coenzyme A reductase inhibitors) are widely used to prevent primary and secondary CVDs by reducing low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol and have been shown to be effective in CKD patients [23].

The 2013 Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) lipid management guideline does not recommend initiating statin-based therapy in dialysis-dependent CKD patients [4]. However, it suggests continuing statin therapy in patients already receiving treatment at the time of dialysis. These recommendations are based on findings from three large-scale randomized clinical trials (4D, AURORA, and SHARP) [567], which reported a relatively small and uncertain reduction in cardiovascular events of statin use in dialysis patients.

Despite these guidelines, statin use remains widespread among patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) undergoing dialysis. Over the past decade, several real-world observational studies have reported potential benefits of statin therapy in this population. In a retrospective study, Kim et al. found that statin initiation was associated with a reduced risk of all-cause mortality in dialysis patients [8]. Streja et al. reported a similar association in a retrospective study of patients receiving statin therapy before transition to chronic dialysis [9]. This study compared patients who continued statin with those who discontinued statin after starting dialysis, finding that persistent statin use was associated with a lower risk of all-cause mortality. In another study, Lo et al. found that statin therapy in dialysis patients and concomitant peripheral artery disease was associated with reductions in all-cause mortality and cardiovascular death [10]. On the other hand, a retrospective study conducted by Shavadia et al. found that the benefit of statin therapy in incident dialysis patients with atherosclerotic heart disease in the reduction of mortality and CVD was modest [11].

To further explore this issue in a real-world setting, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies to assess the effectiveness of statin therapy in patients with ESRD undergoing dialysis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This systematic review and meta-analysis were reported in line with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guideline and Meta-analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology [1213]. Two authors (YHH and TWW) independently performed study selection, data extraction, and quality assessment. Disagreements were resolved through consensus. The study protocol was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024505411).

Data sources and searches

We searched PubMed and Embase from inception to December 31, 2024, without language restriction for relevant observational studies. Terms such as “chronic kidney disease,” “end stage renal disease,” “renal replacement therapy,” “dialysis,” “statin,” and “HMG-CoA reductase inhibitor” were searched as both Medical Subject Heading terms and text words. The complete search strategies are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Study selection

We included cohort and case–control studies that compared adult statin users with nonstatin users among patients with ESRD undergoing maintenance hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis, for any of the following outcomes: all-cause mortality, cardiovascular death, stroke, myocardial infarction (MI), or major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE). The following types of publications were excluded: conference abstracts, letters, editorials, case reports, case series, reviews, systematic reviews, and guidelines. The specific operational criteria for including and excluding citations are listed in Supplementary Table 2. If two or more studies with overlapping populations reported the same outcome, the study with the more recent inclusion period or a more general population would be selected.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data were extracted using a standardized form, including the first author, publication year, country, data sources, inclusion period, study design, dialysis type, exposure, number of patients, mean age, sex, comorbidities, estimates of targeted outcomes, and follow-up duration. The quality of each study was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), a tool validated for evaluating the risk of bias in observational studies [1415]. The scale assesses the risk of bias in studies across three domains: selection of cohorts, control of confounding factors, and measurement of exposure and outcome. Detailed assessments of each NOS item are listed in Supplementary Table 3. Studies with an NOS score of 7–9 were considered at low risk of bias, those scoring 4–6 at moderate risk, and those scoring <3 at high risk.

Statistical analysis

The meta-analysis was performed using RevMan software (Cochrane Review Manager Version 5.4, Oxford, UK) to investigate the associations between statins use and mortality or cardiovascular events. Pooled adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) were calculated for cohort studies, and pooled odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for case–control studies. Heterogeneity across studies was assessed using Cochran’s Q test with a significance threshold of P < 0.10, and quantified using the I2 statistic, with values of 25%, 50%, and 75% representing low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. Pooled estimates were calculated using inverse variance weighting and DerSimonian and Laird random-effects models, which is accounting for the expected heterogeneity [16]. Meta-regression analyses were conducted using EZR software (version 1.68) to explore potential sources of heterogeneity [17]. Subgroup analyses were performed according to dialysis modality, type of statin exposure (new or continued use), geographic region (Asian or Western), follow-up duration, and baseline cardiovascular risk. To ensure the robustness of the findings, sensitivity analyses were conducted by including studies with low risk of bias. Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots for meta-analyses that included 10 or more studies [18]. Egger’s and Begg’s tests were also performed using Comprehensive Meta-Analysis software to further assess publication bias.

RESULTS

Study selection and population characteristics

The search and selection process of the included studies is shown in Figure 1. Of the 14,853 citations identified through electronic searches, 103 studies were assessed for eligibility, and 84 studies were excluded for reasons described in Figure 1. Seven studies were excluded due to overlapped inclusion population, and the reasons for exclusion are summarized in Supplementary Table 4 [19202122232425]. One study was excluded due to a small proportion of statin users, as only 23% patients in the treatment group were prescribed statin [26]. Finally, 19 cohort studies [891011272829303132333435363738394041] encompassing 310,370 ESRD patients on dialysis, with 141,381 exposed to statins, were included in the systematic review. Seven of these studies [81034364041] involved overlapping populations but were retained as they focused on specific populations distinct from the general population. Results from these specific populations were included in the subgroup analysis. The characteristics of the included studies are detailed in Table 1. The mean age of the included studies ranged from 53 to 69 years, with a mean follow-up period of 1–9 years. In terms of statin exposure, five studies [829313436] included new statin users who initiated statin treatment after starting dialysis, while one study [9] included continuous statin users who maintain statin therapy before and after dialysis. The remaining studies included both new and continuous statin users. Most studies did not specify the type of statin used, except for one study [35], in which all patients were prescribed pitavastatin.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed using the NOS. The total NOS scores for each study are listed in Table 1, whereas the NOS scores for each item are summarized in Supplementary Table 5. Overall NOS scores ranged from 5 to 9 among the 19 included studies. 14 studies were at low risk of bias (NOS score of 7–9), and 5 studies were at moderate risk of bias (NOS score of 4–6).

Association between statin therapy and all-cause mortality

The pooled analysis of 11 cohort studies revealed that dialysis patients treated with statin therapy were associated with a significantly lower hazard ratio for all-cause mortality (aHR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.77–0.87; I2 = 90%), as shown in Figure 2a. Due to the substantial heterogeneity, we performed a meta-regression analysis to identify potential sources of heterogeneity and identified two statistically significant variables: NOS score (<7 vs. ≥7; P < 0.001) and follow-up duration (<4 vs. ≥4 years; P = 0.019). Since follow-up duration is one of the criteria included in the NOS score, and four out of the five studies with NOS <7 also had follow-up durations of <4 years, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by excluding studies with a higher risk of bias, as detailed in the sensitivity analysis section. Similar associations were observed across various subgroups, such as new and continuous statin users, as well as patients with general or high cardiovascular risk, but not in patients on peritoneal dialysis (aHR, 0.74; 95% CI, 0.52–1.07; I2 = 81%), as shown in Table 2.

Association between statin therapy and cardiovascular death

The pooled analysis of eight cohort studies showed that dialysis patients treated with statin therapy were associated with a lower hazard ratio for cardiovascular death (aHR, 0.83; 95% CI, 0.74–0.94; I2 = 86%), as shown in Figure 2b.

Association between statin therapy and stroke

The pooled analysis of three cohort studies found that dialysis patients treated with statin therapy were not significantly associated with a reduced hazard ratio for stroke (aHR, 0.86; 95% CI, 0.54–1.35; I2 = 94%), as shown in Figure 2c.

Association between statin therapy and myocardial infarction

The pooled analysis of three cohort studies showed that dialysis patients treated with statin therapy were not associated with a reduced hazard ratio for MI (aHR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.89–1.18; I2 = 40%), as shown in Figure 2d.

Association between statin therapy and major adverse cardiovascular events

The pooled analysis of three cohort studies showed that dialysis patients treated with statin therapy were not associated with a reduced hazard ratio for MACE (aHR, 0.93; 95% CI, 0.72–1.21; I2 = 67%), as shown in Figure 2e.

Publication bias

Funnel plots were generated for outcomes synthesized from more than 10 studies. Visual inspection of the funnel plot for the association between statin therapy and all-cause mortality [Figure 3] revealed potential asymmetry, with a relative absence of studies showing nonsignificant results in areas with smaller standard errors. To further assess potential publication bias, we conducted Egger’s test (P = 0.002) and Begg’s test (P = 0.304), which yielded inconsistent results. This asymmetry may reflect heterogeneity among studies or a small study effect, where smaller studies tend to report larger treatment effects. A sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess its potential influence.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses by excluding studies with a higher risk of bias for each outcome are presented in Supplementary Table 6. For outcomes with substantial heterogeneity, the I2 statistic decreased after the exclusion of studies with a higher risk of bias (all-cause mortality: from 90% to 71%; cardiovascular death: from 86% to 80%; stroke: from 94% to 0%), and the results remained consistent. Notably, the treatment effects for both all-cause mortality (aHR, 0.92; 95% CI, 0.89–0.96; I2 = 71%) and cardiovascular death (aHR, 0.90; 95% CI, 0.82–0.99; I2 = 80%) were attenuated. Sensitivity analyses for subgroup analyses on the association between statin therapy and all-cause mortality are presented in Supplementary Table 7, with overall reduced heterogeneity across most subgroups.

For MACE, since all included studies had a NOS score above 7, we conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding the Horikoshi study [38] because its definition of MACE differed from the others by not including stroke. After this exclusion, the I2 statistic decreased from 67% to 0%. This result suggested a possible benefit of statin therapy for MACE outcomes. However, the small number of the included studies limits the reliability of this finding. Although this sensitivity analysis reached statistical significance, the effect size suggests only a modest clinical benefit. Therefore, we remain cautious in interpreting this finding.

To address the possible small study effect for the association between statin therapy and all-cause mortality, it is recommended to compare the fixed effects (aHR, 0.93; 95% CI, 0.92–0.94; I2 = 91%) and random effects estimates (aHR, 0.81; 95% CI, 0.76–0.87; I2 = 91%) since the random effects model assigns more weight to smaller studies [18]. Similar results were observed when excluding studies that contributed <1% to the overall weight (aHR, 0.93; 95% CI, 0.91–0.96; I2 = 91%). In addition, a trend was noted between smaller study populations and higher risk of bias, with five of the seven small-population studies rated as having a moderate risk of bias. After excluding studies with a higher risk of bias, the association remained (aHR, 0.91; 95% CI, 0.87–0.95; I2 = 84%), suggesting that the impact of the small study effect may have been reduced.

DISCUSSION

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the effectiveness of statins in maintenance dialysis patients by synthesizing real-world data. Our study demonstrated that statin use in this population was associated with a lower risk of all-cause mortality and cardiovascular death, but not with stroke, MI, or MACE. Sensitivity analyses suggested that the benefit of statins in reducing all-cause and cardiovascular mortality was modest.

Statins are known to reduce cardiovascular events through the lowering of LDL cholesterol in the general population. However, their effectiveness appears to diminish as kidney function declines [42]. One proposed mechanism is that, in dialysis patients, uremic conditions may induce carbamylation of LDL, enhancing its atherogenic properties and potentially reducing responsiveness to statin therapy [43]. In addition, an observational study suggested that statin use may be associated with accelerated coronary artery calcification in patients with ESRD [44]. Nevertheless, statins are also believed to exert pleiotropic effects, such as reducing oxidative stress and inflammation [4546], and one observational study reported significant benefits in dialysis patients with elevated high-sensitive C-reactive protein levels [38]. These findings underscore the complex interplay between kidney dysfunction, uremia-induced LDL modifications, and the multifaceted mechanisms by which statins may influence cardiovascular outcomes in this population.

While previous meta-analyses of clinical trials [42474849] have reported little or no benefit of statin on survival and cardiovascular outcomes in dialysis patients, our findings suggest a modest reduction in both all-cause mortality and cardiovascular death. The discrepancy may be explained by the larger sample size and greater statistical power of the included observational studies. Mortality in dialysis patients arises from diverse causes. Approximately 50% of deaths are due to noncardiovascular causes, while the other 50% are cardiovascular-related events. Among cardiovascular deaths, sudden death accounts for roughly half, with the remainder attributable to stroke, MI, heart failure, and other CVDs [50]. Given these reasons, detecting a survival benefit from statin use may require greater statistical power [51].

In our study, statin therapy was found to modestly reduce the risk of cardiovascular mortality but was not associated with a reduced risk of stroke, MI, or MACE. A potential explanation may lie in the distinct distribution of cardiovascular causes of death among the dialysis population. As mentioned above, sudden cardiac death is the most frequent cause of cardiovascular mortality in dialysis patients, accounting for 55.5%, whereas stroke and MI account for only 13.3% and 8.8%, respectively [50]. Therefore, the modest reduction in cardiovascular mortality associated with statin use may not be attributable to the benefits related to stroke, MI, or MACE. Furthermore, based on the proposed mechanism that uremic conditions may induce carbamylation of LDL and reduce responsiveness to statin therapy [43], the observed benefit may instead be related to the pleiotropic effects of statins, such as reducing oxidative stress and inflammation. On the other hand, the relatively small number of events contributing to the outcomes of stroke, MI, and MACE may limit the statistical power to confirm the robustness of these findings.

Compared to earlier clinical trials, including 4D, AURORA, and SHARP [567], the studies included in our analysis encompassed a broader region of population, incorporating data from the United States, Italy, Japan, Taiwan, and South Korea, thus including more evidence from studies involving Asian populations. In addition, our analysis included 11 studies that enrolled patients undergoing peritoneal dialysis, whereas the 4D and AURORA trials excluded such patients, and only 16.3% of participants in the SHARP trial were on peritoneal dialysis. With regard to statin exposure, the 4D trial implemented a 4-week washout period before randomization, and both the AURORA and SHARP trials excluded patients with prior statin use. In contrast, the studies included in our analysis encompassed both new statin and continuous users. In terms of CVD history, approximately 30% of participants in the 4D trial, 40% in the AURORA trial, and 7% in the SHARP trial had a prior history of CVD. In our meta-analysis, six studies specifically included populations with preexisting CVD. Subgroup analysis of all-cause mortality showed results consistent with the primary analysis across most variables, such as patients from Western and Asian countries, new and continuous statin users, as well as patients with general and higher cardiovascular risks. Among patients on peritoneal dialysis, the nonsignificant findings may be attributed to the limited number of studies included, rendering the results more vulnerable to the influence of a single study. As a result, the effect of statins in this population remains inconclusive.

This study has some limitations. First, although heterogeneity decreased after excluding studies with a higher risk of bias or through subgroup analyses, residual heterogeneity may still be attributed to differences in statin exposure, such as statin type (hydrophilic or lipophilic), statin intensity, or adherence, which could not be further stratified in this systematic review due to a lack of data in most included studies. Second, baseline LDL levels were not reported in most included studies, except for four studies [9293238]. As an alternative, we performed subgroup analyses focusing on patients at higher cardiovascular risk, such as those with acute MI, atherosclerotic heart disease, or peripheral artery disease. Third, bias may still exist in studies with a low risk of bias (NOS score <7) according to its deduction items of NOS score. Fourth, potential selection bias inherent to observational studies due to unmeasured confounding factors cannot be entirely ruled out. Finally, the possible small study effect was detected in the association between statin use and all-cause mortality. However, sensitivity analyses showed that the association remained consistent after excluding studies with low weighting or higher risk of bias. These findings suggest that the influence of the small study effect may have been attenuated.

CONCLUSION

This meta-analysis suggested that statins may have a modest effect in reducing all-cause death or cardiovascular death in dialysis patients, but they do not appear to provide benefit for stroke, MI, or MACE. Further investigation is needed to confirm these findings.
