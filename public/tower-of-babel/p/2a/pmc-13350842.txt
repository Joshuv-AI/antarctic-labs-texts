P. gingivalis-host interactions direct antibiotic adjuvants for periodontitis antimicrobial therapy
Authors: Xiya Liu, Zuoying Yuan, Zhuo Wan, Xiaojing Yuan, Yue Wang, Yike Gao, Linxue Zhang, Rui Song, Jingyi Sang, Yang Wang, Yiming Han, Yuming Zhao, Cun-yu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis) invades host cells to evade antibiotics, driving the recurrence of periodontitis. However, current clinical treatment strategies remain ineffective in addressing this intracellular invasion by the bacteria. Here, we report a host-directed strategy that blocks bacterial invasion by targeting key host factors, including collagen I (using FT011), lipid rafts (using simvastatin), integrin β1 (using ATN-161), and FAK signaling (using YH-306). Inhibiting these targets significantly reduced P. gingivalis infection in human oral keratinocytes and periodontal ligament stem cells. Crucially, simvastatin restored the osteogenic ability of P. gingivalis-infected PDLSCs. These drugs subsequently functioned as effective antibiotic adjuvants. The combination of simvastatin or YH-306 with metronidazole, a standard-of-care antibiotic for periodontitis, exhibited potent synergistic effects, significantly eradicating intracellular bacteria at reduced antibiotic dosages. In a rat periodontitis model, simvastatin-metronidazole therapy yielded superior outcomes, significantly mitigating bacterial load, inflammation, and alveolar bone loss. The repurposing of clinically available drugs, such as simvastatin, as antibiotic adjuvants represents a promising, readily translatable therapeutic approach for periodontitis.

Introduction

Periodontitis is an infectious disease primarily driven by dental plaque bacteria, affecting approximately 350 million adults globally.1,2 Among these pathogens, Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis) creates a pathological microenvironment critical for disease progression.3,4 This bacterium produces virulence factors, such as gingipains, and releases lipopolysaccharide (LPS) primarily during cell lysis or membrane disruption, which trigger persistent host immune responses and damage periodontal tissues.5–8 Current mechanical and antibiotic treatments often fail to manage severe cases effectively. P. gingivalis can invade and colonize host cells,9,10 where it may enter a metabolically dormant state.11,12 In this state, even high antibiotic doses cannot eliminate these bacteria completely.13,14 Instead, it increases the risk of drug resistance.15 Following treatment, intracellular bacteria can reactivate and exit the cells, causing reinfection.16 Therefore, preventing P. gingivalis invasion is essential to enhance treatment efficacy and minimize antibiotic dependency.

The infection process involves complex, multi-stage interactions between bacteria and the host. The periodontal ligament features a highly organized collagen fiber network that serves as both a primary defense and a target for bacterial breach.17 Initially, bacteria secrete gingipains to degrade the collagen-enriched extracellular matrix (ECM), exposing deeper tissues. Membrane-bound collagen further mediates cell-bacteria interactions.18–20 Our colleague’s previous findings indicate that collagen II and XV overexpression enhances adhesion under hypotonic and hypertonic conditions, respectively.21 Furthermore, bacterial interaction with spatially confined cell monolayers shows heterogeneity; bacteria preferentially adhere to the outer edges where collagen IV is abundant.22 Beyond collagen, P. gingivalis also exploits lipid raft microdomains for non-specific entry.23

During the specific bacterial invasion of host cells, P. gingivalis binds to integrin β1 via adhesins such as the long fimbriae FimA and short fimbriae Mfa1.24–27 This high-affinity ligand-receptor interaction secures bacterial attachment and, crucially, triggers host cell endocytosis. Upon binding, intracellular signaling molecules associated with integrins (specifically paxillin and focal adhesion kinase (FAK)) are recruited, inducing actin cytoskeleton rearrangement through the Rho GTPase pathway.28 Consequently, characteristic membrane ruffles form, facilitating the active internalization of P. gingivalis.

Targeting bacterial invasion represents a promising host-directed therapeutic strategy.29 In our previous work, we used a fibronectin inhibitor (Arg-Gly-Asp-Ser, RGDS peptide) as a host-directed antibiotic adjuvant. This approach reduced Staphylococcus aureus adhesion to epithelial cells and cut the required antibiotic dosage by 75%.30 Yet for periodontitis, few studies have explored blocking P. gingivalis adhesion and invasion as a treatment approach.

In this study, we evaluated the efficacy of several invasion inhibitors as antibiotic adjuvants in periodontal host cells (Fig. 1). The inhibitors included: FT011, which targets type I collagen (COL-Ⅰ) fibers (over 70% of periodontal ligament composition);31,32 ATN-161, an α5β1 integrin inhibitor;33 YH-306, which inhibits the FAK pathway;34 and simvastatin, an HMG-CoA reductase inhibitor that reduces lipid raft expression.35 First, we established an in vitro model of P. gingivalis ATCC 33277 infection using human oral keratinocytes (HOK) and periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs). Among the tested inhibitors, YH-306 and simvastatin showed the best safety profile while effectively reducing bacterial adhesion. We further demonstrated the effectiveness of YH-306 and simvastatin as adjuvants for various antibiotics (including amoxicillin, tetracycline, and metronidazole) in reducing the required doses of antibiotics commonly used in periodontal treatment. Finally, using a rat periodontitis model, we validated that these inhibitors enhanced the in vivo antibacterial efficacy of low-dose antibiotics, effectively reducing the load of P. gingivalis, alleviating local inflammation, and inhibiting jaw bone resorption in rats with periodontitis.Fig. 1Mechanism of P. gingivalis-host cells interactions. Results

Molecular docking

To elucidate the interaction mechanisms governing P. gingivalis adhesion and invasion, we performed molecular simulations to predict binding modes and affinities between P. gingivalis fimbrial protein FimA and specific host targets: COL-Ⅰ, integrin β1, and caveolin-1 (Fig. 2a, c, e). The caveolin-1/FimA complex exhibited the highest affinity, with a docking score of −281.52 and a binding free energy of −68.33 kcal/mol (confidence score: 0.932 8). Similarly, the binding free energies for COL-Ⅰ/FimA and integrin β1/FimA complexes were −38.87 and −58.9 kcal/mol, respectively, surpassing the threshold of −10 kcal/mol, suggesting highly stable protein interactions. Based on these findings, we identified four potential adjuvant agents to inhibit bacterial adhesion and invasion: the COL-Ⅰ inhibitor FT011 (Fig. 2b), the integrin β1 protein inhibitor ATN-161, the FAK pathway inhibitor YH-306 related to integrin β1-mediated internalization of P. gingivalis (Fig. 2d), and the lipid raft inhibitor simvastatin (Fig. 2f). Biosafety profile and antibacterial efficacy of adjuvant drugs

To assess the cytotoxicity of the selected agents, a CCK-8 assay was performed on two primary periodontal cell types: Periodontal Ligament Stem Cells (PDLSCs) and Human Oral Keratinocytes (HOK). While low concentrations of all these drugs were well-tolerated, extended incubation revealed that cell proliferation was compromised in the high-concentration YH-306 (50 μmol/L) and simvastatin (10–40 μmol/L) groups, in which cell viability was significantly reduced relative to the control group (Fig. 2g, h).

Subsequently, the intrinsic antibacterial properties of the four drugs were evaluated in planktonic P. gingivalis cultures (Fig. 3a). Optical density (OD) measurements at 48, 72, and 96 h indicated that only high concentrations of YH-306 (50 μmol/L) and simvastatin (40 μM) exerted a significant inhibitory effect on bacterial growth during the initial phase ( < 48 h). However, bacterial regrowth occurred by 96 h (Fig. 3b–d). Adjuvant drugs regulate P. gingivalis-host cells interactions

First, we verified the inhibitory effects of the selected adjuvant drugs on target proteins within host cells. The results showed (Fig. S7) that these agents effectively suppressed the expression of cellular target proteins. Notably, as a multi-target drug,36–40 simvastatin not only inhibited caveolin-1 but also suppressed integrin β1 expression. To clarify whether these candidate adjuvant drugs regulate the interaction between host cells and bacteria, an in vitro P. gingivalis infection model was established. In this model, HOK and PDLSCs monolayer cells were pretreated with diverse concentrations of the candidate drugs prior to P. gingivalis infection (Fig. 4a). Quantitative analysis (Fig. 4b) revealed that a 24-hour pretreatment of PDLSCs resulted in a significant attenuation of P. gingivalis infection in groups treated with 5 μmol/L simvastatin, 10 μmol/L ATN-161, 10 μmol/L FT011, and 0.5 μmol/L YH-306 (Fig. S4). Furthermore, confocal fluorescence image analysis confirmed that the number of P. gingivalis infections was significantly reduced in these pretreated PDLSCs (Fig. 4c, d). In addition, simvastatin also enhanced the clearance of already internalized bacteria. As shown in Fig. S10, following infection with P. gingivalis and subsequent antibiotic elimination of extracellular bacteria, treatment with simvastatin (5 μmol/L) significantly reduced the number of viable intracellular CFUs. This result clearly indicates that simvastatin not only reduces bacterial adhesion and invasion but also actively aids host cells in clearing established intracellular infections. It is worth noting that a similar reduction in infection levels was also observed in the epithelial cells HOK (Fig. S5), but the inhibitory effect of ATN-161 on P. gingivalis infection was poor in epithelial cells. To further evaluate the broad-spectrum potential of this host-directed strategy, we assessed the effects of the candidate inhibitors (simvastatin, YH-306, and FT011) on host cell invasion by other key periodontal pathogens. The results demonstrated that these compounds also markedly suppressed the invasion of PDLSCs by Fusobacterium nucleatum and Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans (Figs. S12, S13). Against Fusobacterium nucleatum, FT011 exhibited the strongest inhibitory effect, reducing intracellular infection to 47.2% of the control level. All three compounds significantly limited invasion by Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, with FT011 again showing the most potent activity (51.4% of control). These findings indicate that the anti‑invasive efficacy of these host‑targeting compounds is not limited to P. gingivalis but extends to other invasive pathogens implicated in polymicrobial periodontal disease, supporting their potential as broad‑spectrum host‑directed therapeutics.

Osteogenic potential of P. gingivalis-infected PDLSCs under pharmacological intervention

The recruitment and osteogenesis of endogenous stem cells are a prerequisite for achieving alveolar bone regeneration. Given the pivotal role of PDLSCs in periodontal tissue repair, we assessed how P. gingivalis infection and potential therapeutic agents influence their osteogenic capacity (Fig. 5a). Under uninfected conditions, drug treatments did not significantly impede PDLSC migration (Fig. S6). Notably, treatment with 5 μmol/L simvastatin alone significantly promoted osteogenesis. By day 7, ALP activity and RUNX-2 expression increased 2.1-fold and 4.3-fold, respectively, compared to the standard osteogenic induction group. To elucidate the impact of P. gingivalis on cellular behavior, we monitored PDLSC status post-infection. While cell viability remained unaffected for up to 72 hours across different MOIs, migration was visibly compromised (Fig. S1, S2). When infected cells (MOI = 100) were subjected to osteogenic induction, we observed a biphasic response: expression of osteogenic markers (ALP and RUNX-2) initially surged at day 7. Subsequently, it declined below control levels by day 14. These findings suggest that while P. gingivalis may trigger a transient, stress-induced proliferative and differentiation response, it impairs migration and ultimately suppresses long-term osteogenesis (Fig. 5b).

Subsequently, we investigated the restorative effects of adjuvant drugs on infected PDLSCs (Fig. 5c–f). Among candidates, 5 μmol/L simvastatin proved most effective in rescuing the osteogenic phenotype. It elevated ALP expression by 7.9-fold (day 7) and 1.8-fold (day 14) relative to the untreated osteogenic group. Similarly, RUNX-2 expression was upregulated by 2.9-fold and 1.1-fold, respectively (Fig. 5e).

Use of adjuvant agents to potentiate antibiotic efficacy

Since host-directed agents lack intrinsic bactericidal activity, we hypothesized that they function as antibiotic adjuvants. Consequently, we investigated the synergistic potential of simvastatin, FT011, and YH-306 in combination with standard periodontal antibiotics (metronidazole, tetracycline, amoxicillin) against intracellular P. gingivalis (Fig. 6a). To this end, we determined in vitro efficacy dose-responses for these combinations (Fig. 6b–e). The data demonstrate that simvastatin significantly lowers the intracellular half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of both metronidazole and tetracycline against intracellular bacteria. Specifically, simvastatin reduced the metronidazole IC50 from 414.8 μg/mL to 239.4 μg/mL (2 424 μmol/L to 1 399 μmol/L), representing a 42.29% reduction in antibiotic load. Similarly, the simvastatin-tetracycline combination lowered the IC50 from 136.6 μg/mL to 69 μg/mL (284 μmol/L to 144 μmol/L), representing a 49.49% reduction. While YH-306 reduced IC50 values across all three antibiotics, its efficacy remained inferior to simvastatin. Discussion

Periodontitis recurrence often stems from P. gingivalis invading and persisting within host cells, evading antibiotics.41,42 In this study, we screened four targets involved in P. gingivalis invasion: type I collagen, lipid rafts, α5β1 integrin, and the downstream FAK pathway. Blocking any of these four targets significantly reduced infection. Specifically, blocking type I collagen, lipid rafts, or the FAK pathway cut invasion rates by more than half. FAK serves as a central signaling hub: the FimA subunit of P. gingivalis fimbriae binds to α5β1 integrin on host cells,25 recruiting paxillin and FAK to attachment sites and driving cytoskeletal reorganization to facilitate bacterial entry.28 YH-306, an FAK pathway inhibitor, downregulated FAK and paxillin mRNA expression,34 thereby impairing FAK-Src complex formation.43 This reduced paxillin phosphorylation at Tyr118,44 disrupting focal adhesion turnover and the actin cytoskeletal reorganization essential for bacterial invasion.45,46 However, blocking α5β1 integrin was less effective. Integrin signaling supports cell survival and proliferation through pathways such as PI3K-Akt.47 Blocking integrin may compromise cell viability,48–50 weakening intrinsic resistance to pathogens.

As an inhibitor primarily targeting α5β1, the differential effects of ATN-161 observed between PDLSCs and HOK in this study suggest its action may be cell-type specific. This difference is likely because the distinct integrin expression profiles in the two cell types, PDLSCs, are known to express high levels of integrins such as α5β1,51 whereas HOK predominantly express subtypes such as α6β4.52 Notably, oral pathogens may preferentially exploit specific integrins as receptors for invasion or adhesion during infection. For instance, P. gingivalis has been reported to interact with host cells via integrins, including α5β1 and α5β3.53 Thus, differences in integrin expression profiles across cell types may not only influence drug response but also determine susceptibility to particular bacterial infections. Consequently, we excluded α5β1 integrin and focused on type I collagen, lipid rafts, and the FAK pathway.

Simvastatin, FT011, and YH-306 show weak direct antibacterial activity. They partially block bacterial adhesion, but even under high extracellular bacterial loads, host cells can still be infected. To address this, we used these inhibitors as adjuvants with conventional antibiotics (metronidazole, tetracycline, and amoxicillin). Our aim was to maintain intracellular bacterial clearance with lower antibiotic concentrations,54,55 thereby minimizing host cell toxicity.30 Metronidazole, a nitroimidazole antibiotic, is the most commonly used antibiotic for periodontitis. As a prodrug, its bactericidal activity depends on bacterial nitroreductases to reduce the nitro group into reactive intermediates that cause DNA damage.56 While metronidazole is lipophilic and can penetrate mammalian cells,57 its efficacy against intracellular P. gingivalis may be limited if the bacteria enter a metabolically dormant state.58 Thus, the intracellular survival of P. gingivalis likely results from its transition into a non-growing, persistent state; although metronidazole enters the cell, it cannot be activated by these dormant intracellular bacteria. In our study, simvastatin proved the most effective adjuvant for metronidazole, reducing the required antibiotic dose by nearly 50%. This synergy may not only involve direct antibacterial actions but could also stem from simvastatin’s ability to block cellular invasion, thereby preventing the formation of the intracellular persister reservoir at its source and undermining the bacterium’s key tolerance mechanism. Conversely, FT011 showed the strongest synergy with amoxicillin. These findings indicate that synergy between host-directed drugs and antibiotics depends on specific targets. Importantly, this antibiotic dose-sparing effect of host-directed therapy offers a significant ecological advantage. By reducing antibiotic exposure, it minimizes collateral damage to commensal bacteria and helps preserve oral microbiome homeostasis, which is critical for preventing dysbiosis and disease recurrence.59,60 Moreover, by targeting host-cell invasion rather than directly killing bacteria, this approach exerts minimal selective pressure on non-invasive commensals.61 Thus, host-directed adjuvants support a more ecologically rational therapeutic paradigm for periodontitis.

Antagonism is also possible, as seen with simvastatin and amoxicillin. This reflects the conflict between a bacteriostatic agent and a bactericidal agent. Amoxicillin, a β-lactam bactericidal agent, acts by inhibiting peptidoglycan synthesis in the cell wall of actively dividing bacteria, leading to cell wall defects, osmotic imbalance, and bacterial lysis.62 Simvastatin, conversely, may potentially exert bacteriostatic activity by inhibiting bacterial HMG-CoA reductase,63 thereby disrupting the synthesis of isoprenoids,64 which are crucial for bacterial membrane function and growth.65 This could induce bacteria into a metabolically slow or dormant state. When bacterial growth is arrested due to the action of simvastatin, their susceptibility to antibiotics that depend on active bacterial division (such as amoxicillin) is significantly reduced, diminishing the latter’s bactericidal efficacy. This interaction carries direct clinical implications. In periodontitis treatment, systemic or local use of β-lactam antibiotics (e.g., amoxicillin) is a common approach for managing acute infections.66 Our findings strongly suggest that simvastatin should not be used concurrently with β-lactam antibiotics to avoid antagonism that could compromise overall antimicrobial efficacy. Future studies designing combination therapies must carefully evaluate such drug interactions to ensure the effectiveness and safety of treatment strategies.

In the present study, simvastatin and YH-306 were employed as adjunctive agents to metronidazole within a rat periodontitis model challenged by P. gingivalis infection. Our findings demonstrate that both combination therapies significantly attenuated bacterial burden, with a particularly pronounced reduction in intracellular bacterial load. Concomitantly, this bacterial suppression led to the downregulation of local inflammatory mediators, a decreased proportion of osteoclasts, and the significant upregulation of osteogenic markers. Ultimately, these molecular changes resulted in substantial increases in bone volume and density relative to the control group. Notably, the combined regimen of simvastatin and metronidazole exhibited the most potent therapeutic effect. This superior outcome is ostensibly attributable to the intrinsic osteogenic activity of simvastatin, a property that has been extensively corroborated by our prior in vitro investigations and established literature.67–69 It’s worth noting that statins, including simvastatin, have precedents for topical use (e.g., periodontal treatment,70 bone defect repair,71 and wound healing72,73) where they exert direct bioactive effects. Such a local application bypasses the need for hepatic CYP3A4‑mediated conversion into active metabolites.74,75 This study aims to exploit simvastatin’s ability to directly inhibit the interaction between P. gingivalis and host cell proteins, a mechanism that does not require its conversion into a systemically active form.

Although we found that topical application of 5 μmol/L simvastatin effectively inhibits P. gingivalis infection and demonstrates synergistic, dose-reducing effects with antibiotics like metronidazole, its clinical translation faces significant challenges. The limitations of this work include: first, the investigation did not encompass all potential host targets (e.g., fibronectin and distinct collagen subtypes) or explore a broader range of antibiotic classes;76–78 second, the local delivery approach used here presents notable pharmacokinetic differences compared to standard oral administration, and systemic drug exposure was not assessed; third, a major practical hurdle lies in maintaining an effective local concentration within the dynamic periodontal pocket microenvironment, where gingival crevicular fluid flow impedes sustained delivery; and fourth, simvastatin shows cytotoxicity at 10 µmol/L, indicating a narrow therapeutic window that complicates precise dosing.

Nevertheless, we believe our in vivo results provide strong confidence for the therapeutic potential of this strategy. To address these limitations and facilitate clinical translation, future work should pursue two complementary directions. First, high‑throughput screening should be leveraged to systematically identify optimal drug synergies tailored to specific pathologies, thereby expanding the scope of targets and antibiotic combinations. Second, to achieve precisely sustained local drug release, we will focus on developing a synergistic controlled release system co-delivering simvastatin and metronidazole. We have already successfully prepared poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) nanoparticles co-loaded with both drugs, and the complete study on this system will be published in the near future.

Conclusion

In summary, this study proposes a novel strategy for periodontitis treatment based on host-pathogen interactions. The core of this strategy lies in applying host-directed adjuvant therapy to interfere with the pathogenic process. Here, we found that adjuvant targeting COL-Ⅰ (FT011), fibers FAK pathway (YH-306), and lipid raft (simvastatin) significantly reduced bacterial adhesion without cytotoxicity. These adjuvants offer a dual benefit: they can reduce antibiotic dosage, which helps to prevent the development of resistant bacteria, and certain adjuvant drugs (e.g., simvastatin in this work) themselves can promote osteogenesis. After being combined with an antibiotic (e.g., metronidazole in this work), the reduced bacterial load and enhanced tissue repair were simultaneously achieved in a rat periodontitis model. Given that both metronidazole and simvastatin are widely used in clinical practice, this combination treatment holds significant potential for rapid translation, offering a new, promising direction for the clinical management of periodontitis.

Materials and methods

Materials

Metronidazole was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Amoxicillin, tetracycline, and gentamicin were obtained from Aladdin (China). Simvastatin, FT011, YH-306, and ATN-161 were sourced from MCE (USA). All other reagents and solvents used were of analytical grade and provided by Beijing Chemical Reagents Company (China).

Cell culture

Human periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs) were a gift from the Oral Stem Cell Bank (China, Beijing). These cells were characterized by this public institution and stored in liquid nitrogen vapor for long-term cryopreservation. Briefly, PDLSCs were thawed and incubated in α-MEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (100 U/mL and 100 μg/mL, Invitrogen, UK) in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C, and PDLSCs at passages 2–6 were used for subsequent experiments.

Human immortalized oral keratinocytes (HOK) were purchased from ScienCell (Carlsbad, CA, USA). HOK cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, USA) containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

For all bacterial-host interaction experiments, both α-MEM and RPMI 1640 media were supplemented with 1% FBS but without penicillin-streptomycin solution.

Bacterial strains and bacterial culture

P. gingivalis ATCC 33277 was cultured anaerobically at 37 °C for 48 hours in Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) broth supplemented with yeast extract (1 mg/mL), hemin (5 μg/mL), and menadione (vitamin K, 1 μg/mL). Following incubation, bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation at 6 000 × g for 10 minutes at 4 °C. The resulting pellet was washed and resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Bacterial concentration was determined by measuring the optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) using a spectrophotometer and was subsequently adjusted to the desired concentration for subsequent experiments.

Molecular docking simulation

Molecular docking simulations to predict the interactions between P. gingivalis adhesin FimA and host target proteins were performed using HDOCKlite (version 1.1). The three-dimensional structure of the receptor protein, P. gingivalis FimA (UniProt ID: B2RH54), was retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 6JZK). The structures of the human host target proteins were obtained as follows: the caveolin-1 module (CAV1_HUMAN, Q03135) from PDB ID: 7SC0, the type I collagen module (CO1A1_HUMAN, P02452) from PDB ID: 7E7B, and the integrin beta-1 module (ITB1_HUMAN, P05556) from PDB ID: 7CEB. For each FimA-target pair, all possible binding modes were sampled without predefined binding sites. The docking simulations used the server’s default grid parameters for sampling and scoring. The resulting poses were ranked using the integrated scoring function. The primary outputs included a docking score (where more negative values indicate higher binding affinity) and a confidence score. A confidence score was also calculated; a value > 0.7 was considered to indicate a high probability of binding. The top-ranked docking model for each complex was selected for further analysis. Protein-protein interactions at the binding interface, including hydrogen bonds, salt bridges, and hydrophobic contacts, were analyzed in detail using the PLIP (Protein-Ligand Interaction Profiler) web server and visualized with PyMOL (The PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 2.5). The molecular graphics and surface representations of the complexes were generated using PyMOL to illustrate the binding interface and complementary surface areas.

Cell viability

To evaluate the potential cytotoxicity of four compounds—simvastatin, ATN-161, FT011, and YH-306—on PDLSCs and HOK, cell viability was measured using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Briefly, PDLSCs and HOK cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5000 cells per well and cultured for 24 hours to allow complete adhesion. The culture medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing serial concentrations of simvastatin, ATN-161, FT011, or YH-306, incubated for 24, 48, or 72 hours. After treatment, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for an additional 1 hour at 37 °C. The optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher, USA).

Wells containing only culture medium and CCK-8 reagent were used as blank controls, and wells with cells cultured in drug-free medium served as the negative control. Antibacterial assay

To evaluate the growth inhibitory effects of four compounds (simvastatin, ATN-161, FT011, and YH-306) against P. gingivalis, a preliminary screening was performed using optical density (OD₆₀₀) measurements, followed by precise quantification via the plate colony counting method.

Cell migration

For the wound-healing assay, PDLSCs were cultured in a 6-well plate until they reached 90% confluence. A wound approximately 1.5 mm wide was created using a blue pipette tip, and the cells were washed three times with PBS.

To evaluate the effect of adjuvant drugs on cell migration, fresh medium containing the four compounds (simvastatin, ATN-161, FT011, and YH-306) with 2% FBS was added to the wells, and the cells were incubated for 24 h. In parallel, to examine the impact of P. gingivalis infection on cell migration, PDLSCs were infected with P. gingivalis at different MOI for 2 hours prior to wound creation. After infection, the bacterial suspension was removed, and cells were washed thoroughly with PBS before adding fresh medium with 2% FBS for 12-24 h incubation.

The extent of wound healing in all experimental groups was observed under an optical microscope and analyzed using ImageJ software.

Bacterial infection assay

To evaluate the effects of four compounds (simvastatin, ATN-161, FT011, and YH-306) on the ability of P. gingivalis to infect host cells, a bacterial infection assay was performed.

PDLSCs and HOK cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 10⁵ cells per well and cultured for 24 hours to allow complete adhesion. The medium was then replaced with fresh antibiotic-free medium containing different concentrations of the four test compounds, and cells were pretreated for 24 hours. Cells without drug treatment served as the control group.

Prior to infection, P. gingivalis ATCC 33277 was centrifuged at 6 000 × g for 5 minutes at 4 °C. The bacterial pellet was washed with PBS and resuspended in antibiotic-free cell culture medium. The bacterial suspension was adjusted to an optical density of approximately 1.0 at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) for subsequent use.

After 24 hours of pretreatment, the drug-containing medium was aspirated, and cells were gently washed with PBS to remove residual compounds. Each well was then inoculated with the prepared bacterial suspension at an MOI of 100. The infected cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere for 2 hours.

Following infection, cells were gently washed three times with PBS to remove non-adherent and non-internalized bacteria. To lyse the cells and release internalized bacteria, 1 mL of sterile distilled water was added to each well, and the plates were incubated at room temperature for 10 minutes. The resulting lysates were serially diluted, and appropriate dilutions were spread onto BHI blood agar plates. The plates were incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 5–10 days, after which bacterial colonies were counted and quantified as CFU per well.

Intracellular bacterial clearance assay

To evaluate the effect of simvastatin on the clearance of established intracellular P. gingivalis infection, an intracellular bactericidal assay was performed. Briefly, PDLSCs were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured to form a confluent monolayer. Cells were infected with P. gingivalis at an MOI of 100 and incubated at 37 °C under 5% CO₂ for 2 hours. After infection, the cells were gently washed with PBS to remove non-adherent bacteria. Extracellular adherent bacteria were then eliminated by incubating the cells in α-MEM containing 1% FBS supplemented with metronidazole (200 μg/mL, 1.17 mmol/L) and gentamicin sulfate (300 μg/mL, 0.52 mmol/L) for 1 hour. Following the antibiotic treatment, the infected cells were divided into two groups: a control group and a simvastatin-treated group (5 μmol/L). The cells were further cultured for 24 hours. Subsequently, to release intracellular bacteria, the cells were lysed with sterile distilled water for 10 minutes at room temperature. The lysates were serially diluted, plated onto blood agar plates, and incubated under anaerobic conditions at 37 °C for 5–7 days. CFUs were counted to assess the number of viable intracellular bacteria. The intracellular bacterial load in the simvastatin-treated group was compared with that of the control group to evaluate the effect of simvastatin on the survival and proliferation of intracellular P. gingivalis.

Immunofluorescence staining

To visually observe and quantify the effect of drug pretreatment on P. gingivalis infection in PDLSCs, immunofluorescence staining was performed.

PDLSCs were seeded onto confocal dishes. Cells were pretreated for 24 hours with the four test compounds (simvastatin, ATN-161, FT011, and YH-306). Subsequently, cells were infected with P. gingivalis at an MOI of 100 for 2 hours.

After infection, cells were washed three times with PBS to remove unbound bacteria. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 minutes at room temperature and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 minutes. Non-specific binding sites were blocked with goat serum for 1 hour at room temperature.

Cells were then incubated with diluted primary antibody (rabbit anti-P. gingivalis polyclonal antibody, Sigma, [SAB4200832]) overnight at 4 °C. After washing three times with PBS, cells were incubated with fluorescent secondary antibody (Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG, Invitrogen) for 1 hour at room temperature in the dark to label P. gingivalis.

To visualize the cytoskeleton and nuclei, cells were subsequently stained with TRITC-phalloidin (for F-actin) for 30 minutes and DAPI (for nuclei) for 10 minutes at room temperature in the dark. Finally, fluorescence images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, TCS-SP8 STED 3X, Germany). The number of green fluorescent signals (representing P. gingivalis) in the observed areas was quantified using ImageJ software to analyze the effects of different drug treatments on P. gingivalis infection of PDLSCs.

Gene expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the cells in the 6-well plates and reverse transcribed into cDNA. qRT-PCR was then performed on the cDNA samples using GAPDH or β-actin as the reference gene. The primer sequences for osteogenic markers (RUNX-2 and ALP) and adjuvant drugs targeted proteins (Caveolin-1, COL1A1, ITGB1, and FAK) are listed in Table S1 and Table S2, respectively. The experiment was repeated three times to ensure the accuracy of the results.

Antibacterial combination assay

To evaluate the bactericidal effects of three antibiotics (amoxicillin, metronidazole, tetracycline) alone or in combination with three chemical compounds (simvastatin, FT011, YH-306) against P. gingivalis infecting host cells, a combination antibacterial assay was conducted.

PDLSCs were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured for 24 hours to form confluent monolayers. Prior to infection, P. gingivalis suspensions were prepared in antibiotic-free cell culture medium according to experimental groups: 1) antibiotics alone at serial dilutions; 2) chemical compounds alone at fixed concentrations (determined as safe concentrations by cell viability assays); 3) combination groups (fixed concentrations of adjuvant drugs + serially diluted antibiotics). The prepared P. gingivalis suspensions containing corresponding agents were added to cell cultures at an MOI of 100, with an infected control group without any drugs. Plates were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 2 hours.

Post-infection, cells were washed three times with PBS to remove drugs and non-adherent bacteria. Cells were then lysed with sterile distilled water to release intracellular bacteria. The lysates were serially diluted and plated on BHI blood agar plates. After 5–10 days of anaerobic incubation at 37 °C, bacterial colonies were counted.

The antibacterial rate was calculated by comparing viable bacterial counts between treatment groups and the infected control group. The half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) was determined by fitting the antibiotic concentrations (alone or in combination) and their corresponding intracellular bacterial inhibition rates to nonlinear regression (dose-response curve) using GraphPad Prism software, representing the antibiotic concentration required to kill 50% of intracellular bacteria.

Rat Periodontitis Model

Healthy 8-week-old male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats were used to establish a periodontitis model. Prior to experimentation, all rats were acclimatized for one week under standard conditions.

Under isoflurane inhalation anesthesia, a 2.0 mm ligature wire was securely tied around the cervical area of the maxillary first molars (M1) using needle holders. To induce infectious periodontitis, P. gingivalis suspension (1×10⁹ CFU/mL) was applied into the gingival sulcus around the ligated teeth79. Concurrently, 20 μL of corresponding therapeutic drug solutions were administered via a micro-syringe to the buccal and palatal gingival sites of the first molars in respective groups. Ligature wires were checked every 4 days, and drug solutions were injected into the gingival sulcus between the first and second molars every 5 days. Ligatures were removed after 10 days.

Rats were euthanized at 10 or 21 days post-surgery. Maxillae were collected and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin. Micro-CT scanning was performed for imaging and three-dimensional reconstruction. Quantitative analysis included bone volume fraction (BV/TV) and bone mineral density (BMD). Tissue specimens were embedded in paraffin and sectioned. The sections were subjected to Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining, Masson’s trichrome staining, immunohistochemical staining (for IL-6, TNF-α, and OCN), and Tartrate-Resistant Acid Phosphatase (TRAP) staining. These staining procedures were employed to systematically evaluate the severity of gingival inflammation, collagen fiber distribution, osteoblast activity (as indicated by OCN), and osteoclast activity (as indicated by TRAP), thereby providing a comprehensive assessment of periodontal tissue pathology and alveolar bone formation.

Furthermore, the absolute quantities of extracellular and intracellular P. gingivalis in gingival tissues were determined using TaqMan probe-based quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR). Additionally, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) was performed on tissue sections using a specific fluorescent probe (Fubo, China, [FBFP1342]) targeting P. gingivalis 16S rRNA to analyze and localize the bacterial load and distribution within the gingival tissues.

Bacterial extraction and quantification from rat gingival tissue

Different bacterial populations were separated through sequential physical elution and antibiotic pretreatment. (1) Extracellular bacteria: Fresh gingival tissue was gently agitated in PBS, briefly centrifuged to pellet tissue debris, and the supernatant containing extracellular bacteria was collected. (2) Intracellular bacteria: A separate tissue aliquot was incubated at 37 °C for 1 hour in PBS containing metronidazole (200 μg/mL, 1.17 mmol/L) and gentamicin sulfate (300 μg/mL, 0.52 mmol/L) to eliminate extracellular bacteria. After washing thoroughly with PBS to remove antibiotic residues, the tissue was homogenized and centrifuged; the resulting supernatant, containing bacteria released from intracellular compartments, was collected. Genomic DNA was then extracted from each of the two supernatants (extracellular and intracellular fractions). Absolute quantification of P. gingivalis was performed by TaqMan probe-based qPCR targeting the species-specific arg-gingipain gene (RgpA)80; primer and probe sequences are listed in Table S4. A standard curve was generated using a serially diluted pUC57 recombinant plasmid carrying the target amplicon, allowing precise calculation of bacterial load in tissue samples based on cycle threshold (Ct) values.

Statistical Analysis

Data were collected and analyzed using SPSS 24.0 software. All quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with a sample size of n ≥ 3. Statistical analyses were performed using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s analysis. A significance level of *P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, while **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.000 1 were considered highly significant differences.
