Percutaneous Bleomycin Sclerotherapy: A Useful Therapeutic Option for Ganglion Cysts
Authors: Kihyuk Shin, Won-Ku Lee, Sang-Hyeon Won, Hyang-Suk You, Hyun-Chang Ko, Byung-Soo Kim, Moon-Bum Kim, Hoon-Soo Kim
Published: 2023

Dear Editor:

Ganglion cysts are mucin-filled cysts mostly found in the wrist, hand, ankle, and foot. Treatment options include aspiration, incision and drainage, cryotherapy, sclerotherapy, and surgery. There are no consistently successful treatment modalities, and recurrence is common1. Sclerotherapy is a noninvasive treatment modality used to disrupt the target tissue. It is simple to perform, inexpensive, and has good cosmetic results. Various sclerosing agents have been used to treat venous and lymphatic malformations2.

Bleomycin is an antitumor, antibacterial, and antiviral agent isolated from the actinomycotic soil fungus Streptomyces verticillus. It has cytotoxic antitumor and sclerotic activities possibly associated with DNA synthesis inhibition3. Historically, it was a proven effective treatment for lymphatic malformations but has also been found to be a potent therapeutic option for venous malformations, potentially more effective than other agents in certain scenarios2. One study reported that bleomycin was superior to sodium tetradecyl sulfate (STS) when used as an intralesional sclerotherapic agent in soft tissue vascular lesions4. STS has shown efficacy in ganglion cyst treatment. However, no published report has assessed the therapeutic effect of bleomycin on ganglion cysts. Here, we report our experience of treating 19 cases of ganglion cysts with percutaneous bleomycin sclerotherapy to evaluate its efficacy and safety. Institutional review board approval was provided by the Ethics Committee of Pusan National University Hospital (H-1801-015-063), and all patients gave written consent before collecting data.

Fifteen milligrams of bleomycin powder (Bleocin; Nipponkayaku Co., Ltd.) was reconstituted with 15 ml of 0.9% normal saline yielding a 1 mg/ml concentration. Ultrasonography was performed to examine the communication between the cystic space and the joint cavity. Without local anesthesia, a gelatinous material was aspirated from the cystic lesion with an 18- or 21-gauge needle with a disposable syringe, preceded by cleaning the treatment area with a povidone-iodine solution (Fig. 1A). While keeping the tip of the aspiration needle within the cyst lumen, the syringe with gelatinous material was exchanged for that with 1% bleomycin. The injected amount was comparable to the amount of extracted gelatinous material, approximately 0.5 to 2 ml (Fig. 1B). External compression was applied for at least 24 hours. Patients were followed up every two months after treatment. The second and third procedures were performed when the patient was found to have had a recurrence at the 2- and 4-month follow-up visits, respectively.

Gelatinous material (inlet) was aspirated from the cystic lesion with an 18- or 21-gauge needle with a disposable syringe (A). Bleomycin solution (1%) was injected into the cystic space with a similar amount of extracted gelatinous material (B). The ganglion cyst showed on the medial side of the left wrist in case 5 (C) and complete resolution was observed in two months after 2 times of percutaneous bleomycin sclerotherapy (D).

Nineteen patients received bleomycin sclerotherapy, including 11 males and 8 females, with a median age of 55.4 years (range, 24~77 years; Table 1). Patients with a digital mucous cyst on the periungual region were excluded because of the well-known risk from bleomycin injection in that region. Eleven patients (57.9%) experienced complete resolution (CR) in two months, and five patients (26.3%) experienced CR in two months after the second procedure. Of the three patients with recurrence after the second procedure (Fig. 1C, D), two underwent a third procedure resulting in CR, and the other refused further treatment. We measured the largest diameter of each lesion in millimeters (Table 1) and found that the size of each ganglion cyst had nothing to do with CR. No recurrence developed in eighteen patients who underwent the procedure with a follow-up period of 21.2 months (range, 12~41 months after the last treatment). One patient reported a fibrotic remnant and three reported hyperpigmentation that spontaneously disappeared without scarring. No serious complications related to the joint or tendon were observed.

The therapeutic mechanism of bleomycin is unknown. However, we consider that bleomycin induces an inflammatory response and fibrosis in ganglion cysts and a regenerative process to prevent further mucous formation. Sclerotherapy has a risk of a sclerosant passing into the joint cavity and tendon sheath. However, several experimental studies with intra-articular injections of sclerosing solutions reported no gross changes in the joint cavity and ligament5. Ogose et al.6 reported that sclerosant injection to a ganglion cyst communicating with the joint cavity was safe. However, the treatment is not recommended for digital mucous cysts in the periungual region because of the nail deformity risks. Our patients showed good tolerance without major adverse effects. Bleomycin as an intralesional sclerosant for ganglion cysts is easily available, cost-effective, cosmetically excellent, and more tolerable than surgery. This procedure can be performed easily by any dermatologist in the outpatient clinic. Therefore, we suggest bleomycin sclerotherapy as an effective and safe therapeutic option for ganglion cysts, decreasing the need for invasive surgery. Further study is needed between sclerosants, comparing their effect on ganglion cysts.
