Takotsubo Syndrome Following Pulmonary Vein Isolation With Pulse Field Ablation
Authors: Nobunari Tomura, Keitaro Senoo, Yusuke Kakei, Kohei Kawai, Masahiro Makino, Jun Munakata, Hirokazu Shiraishi, Satoaki Matoba
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTakotsubo syndrome (TTS) is triggered by emotional or physical stress and characterized by transient left ventricular wall motion abnormalities in the absence of coronary culprit lesion.Case SummaryA 76-year-old woman underwent pulse field ablation (PFA) for paroxysmal atrial fibrillation. History of Presentation

A 76-year-old woman was referred to our hospital with recurrent palpitation due to drug-refractory paroxysmal atrial fibrillation (AF). On presentation, the patient was afebrile, with blood pressure 135/62 mm Hg, regular heart rate of 60 beats/min, and oxygen saturation 98% in room air. Physical examination revealed no cardiac murmurs, respiratory crackles, or edema. Initial laboratory examination revealed slightly elevated creatinine level (0.92 mg/dL) and brain natriuretic peptide (68.4 pg/mL). Electrocardiography (ECG) showed sinus rhythm with normal ST-segment level and QT interval. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated no focal asynergy with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 77%. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography showed no thrombus in the left atrial appendage. She underwent pulse field ablation (PFA) for paroxysmal AF.

During the procedure, deep sedation with propofol and dexmedetomidine hydrochloride was performed. Pentazocine was routinely used for relieving airway irritation due to laryngeal tube and ventilator. Heparin was administered to maintain an activated clotting time of >300 seconds. A 20-electrodes catheter (BeeAT, Japan Lifeline) was placed in the coronary sinus vein for pacing, recording, and internal cardioversion. Following the transseptal puncture under guidance with intracardiac echocardiography (ViewFlex Extra), each pulmonary vein was ablated with an 8-application protocol (4 in flower and 4 in basket configuration) by a 31-mm PFA catheter (Farawave, Boston Scientific) (Figure 1). Atropine was used before the first application, but any catecholamine was not utilized. After the completion of pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) was confirmed using an Orion/Rhythmia system (Boston Scientific), we noticed that ECG showed ST-segment elevation in leads V2-V3 (Figure 2A), and TTE detected a new severe hypokinesis in mid-anteroseptal wall and apical hyperkinesis (Figure 2B, Video 1). The ECG changes occurred after PFA for the right inferior pulmonary vein (38 minutes after the start of the procedure) (Figure 3).

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of chronic kidney disease and was being treated for overactive bladder with mirabegron, which is a beta-3 adrenergic receptor agonist.

Differential Diagnosis

Coronary vasospasm was a leading concern. Another potential diagnosis was coronary embolism and Takotsubo syndrome (TTS).

Investigations

We performed emergent coronary angiography (CAG) to establish a definitive diagnosis. While CAG showed no significant stenosis (Figure 4, Video 2), ST-segment elevation and left ventricular wall motion abnormality still remained after injecting nitroglycerin. Left ventriculography was not performed in consideration of poor renal function. The serum levels of creatine kinase and high-sensitive troponin I were elevated to 218 U/L and 2,618.7 pg/mL, respectively. The next day, follow-up ECG revealed T-wave inversion and prolonged QT interval in leads V1-V3 (Figure 5A), and left ventricular wall motion was almost normalized on TTE (Figure 5B, Video 3).

Management

The patient was diagnosed with TTS due to PFA because of typical ECG changes and transient left ventricular wall motion abnormality beyond the distribution of a single coronary artery. Fortunately, her symptoms and hemodynamics were stable. In addition to stopping a mirabegron, an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor and a beta-blocker were initiated because left ventricular ejection fraction decreased to 35% to 45%. Her cardiac function improved within a day of onset, so the risk of left ventricular thrombus was low.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Cardiac magnetic resonance performed on hospital day 6 identified myocardial edema from the anteroseptal to lateral wall of the mid-left ventricle with T2 mapping (Figure 6A) but also the absence of late gadolinium enhancement suggesting the lack of substantial necrosis due to severe coronary vasospasm or embolism (Figure 6B). Iodine-123-meta-iodobenzylguanidine scintigraphy on hospital day 7 revealed impaired tracer uptake in apex and high washout in the septum and anterolateral walls, where the hypokinetic left ventricular region at the onset of TTS is located (Figure 7). The ECG T-wave inversion and prolonged QT interval gradually improved. The patient was discharged without developing heart failure and recurrent AF on hospital day 9.

Discussion

TTS is an acute heart failure syndrome triggered by emotional or physical stress and typically characterized by transient regional wall motion abnormalities of the left ventricle in the absence of a culprit lesion on CAG.1 Although its pathophysiological mechanism is not yet completely understood, sympathetic nervous stimulation is known to play a critical role. So far, a few cases of TTS after radiofrequency or cryoballoon ablation have been reported.2 However, to our knowledge, this is the first case of TTS following PFA.

Recently, PFA has emerged as a novel nonthermal treatment technology for paroxysmal AF patients. Unlike the traditional ablation energy sources, PFA has demonstrated a high rate of durable PVI with limited effect on surrounding structures such as the esophagus, phrenic nerves, and pulmonary vein tissue through a more selective process to myocardial tissue.3 On the other hand, severe coronary vasospasm has been observed when PFA was delivered in proximity to a vessel.4 In the present case, we first suspected the development of coronary vasospasm because new ST-segment elevation and left ventricular dysfunction were detected during the procedure using PFA. As a result, emergent CAG showed no stenosis with TIMI flow grade 3, but these abnormal findings remained despite injecting nitroglycerin. In addition, TTE revealed atypical midventricular type of TTS, excluding epicardial coronary events. The findings of cardiac magnetic resonance and iodine-123-meta-iodobenzylguanidine scintigraphy strengthened the diagnosis of TTS.

PFA for AF has several potential risks of TTS. First, PFA can stimulate the phrenic nerve and bronchus, which causes diaphragmatic contraction and dry cough.5 Frequent coughing accompanied by arousal may stimulate the sympathetic nervous system. In this case, diaphragmatic contraction was recognized for each application by PFA, and we had to add sedatives frequently during the procedure. The observation of ST-segment changes in ECG after PFA for right inferior pulmonary vein may also support this hypothesis because the right-sided phrenic nerve is anatomically close to the right pulmonary vein. Consideration should be given to the use of narcotics or muscle relaxants to relieve discomfort. Second, myocardial injury by a mechanism of irreversible electroporation may be associated with the development of TTS. PFA has been reported to cause significantly higher cardiac enzymes than cryoballoon ablation,6 and it can induce a TTS episode through the microvascular dysfunction due to inflammation. This case developed TTS despite completing the procedure with a recommended total of 32 applications. Therefore, it may be better to adjust the amount of energy or catheter types of PFA depending on the patients' age and physical condition. Third, autonomic ganglionated plexi (GP) damaged by PFA leads to an enhanced adrenergic tone. It has been reported that acute vagal effects were observed in approximately half of subjects who underwent PFA while it had no long-term impact on GP.7 Perhaps, this temporary response pattern to GP could result in very early improvement in left ventricular dysfunction of TTS.

In addition, this patient was taking mirabegron, which may have been potentially stimulating the sympathetic nerves. Although mirabegron showed high intrinsic activity for beta-3 adrenergic receptor, it has been reported to cause events such as increased blood pressure and heart rate, suggesting the partial involvement of other beta-adrenergic receptors.8 Therefore, we stopped mirabegron after the onset of TTS and treated in accordance with the expert consensus document,9 leading to her benign course.

Conclusions

This is the first report of TTS after PVI according to PFA. TTS should be recognized as a complication of PFA, and it remains important to assess the intraoperative ECG changes and cardiac function by echocardiography. PFA may be associated with greater emotional or physical stress than conventional thermal ablation, requiring the caution of TTS development.
