Development of a personalized, modular intervention to promote dignity, meaning and mindfulness in palliative patients in a swiss acute-care hospital
Authors: Katharina Klatte, Annina Seiler, Reka Schweighoffer, Gunther Meinlschmidt, Silke Walter, Sandra Stephanie Mai, Josef Jenewein, Harvey M Chochinov, Gary Rodin, Rainer Schaefert, Sandra Eckstein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with serious, life-limiting diseases often experience substantial emotional and spiritual distress, death anxiety, depressive symptoms, and loss of dignity. Fostering dignity and meaning and relieving psychological distress have been identified as core objectives of palliative care. To address the individual needs of palliative patients, a personalized, modular intervention approach may be advantageous.AimBased on previous work, we aimed to develop a personalized, modular approach to improve dignity, meaning, and mindfulness in palliative care patients.MethodsAn expert panel of international specialists in palliative care, dignity therapy, psychotherapy, clinical psychology, psychiatry, and psychosomatic medicine was convened to develop the proposed modular approach. Intervention development is described following the “Template for Intervention Description and Replication” TIDieR guidance and is based on results from a literature search on previous work on dignity, meaning-centered therapy, and mindfulness-based interventions, as well as on in-depth needs assessments.ResultsBased on the expert consensus, a three-modular approach was formulated, with evidence-based modules providing Dignity Therapy, Managing Cancer and Living Meaningfully (CALM) therapy, and mindfulness-based interventions, each addressing complementary aspects.ConclusionA personalized, modular intervention to promote dignity, meaning, and mindfulness in palliative care patients was developed by a panel of international experts using existing dignity-fostering end-of-life interventions. The resulting modular approach was designed with the aim to address patients’ individual needs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-026-02088-y.Keywords: Dignity, Meaning, Mindfulness, End-of-life, Life-threatening diseases, Palliative care

Introduction

Advanced disease brings substantial physical and psychological challenges, including multiple physical symptoms and progressive physical deterioration that may contribute to psychological distress, anxiety, and depressive symptoms [1]. In the advanced stages of disease, patients and their relatives are confronted with a variety of stressors that may affect all areas of life and that can lead to high levels of psychological and social distress. For the patient, an advanced disease stage is often associated with changes in appearance, functional limitations or losses, and an increasing dependence on others. This can pose threats to the patient’s sense of control, identity, meaning, and dignity. Disease burden, depressive symptoms, death anxiety, and loss of the sense of meaning, and purpose have all been associated with high levels of distress and a desire for hastened death [2–5].

There are a number of psychological interventions that aim to promote meaning and dignity at the end of life, which can be roughly divided into the following: dignity-centered and life-review focused therapies, supportive-expressive and cognitive-behavioral therapies, mindfulness-based therapies, as well as ‘complementary therapies’, integrating elements from neighboring fields, such as music or art therapy [6, 7]. Some aspects of dignity such as symptom management, privacy, respect, and a calm environment, can be effectively addressed and provided through established methods of general palliative care [8]. In contrast, deeper, more individualized dimensions of dignity – such as the feeling of being human and being self, having meaningful relationships, and experiencing existential satisfaction – cannot be fully met by commonly-provided core palliative care services alone. These dimensions often require targeted interventions to meet the unique emotional and existential needs of each patient [9–13]. Evidence from recent research indicates small to large effects in the reduction of depression, anxiety, and distress in palliative care patients through supportive-expressive psychotherapy and cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) interventions, mindfulness-based interventions, and life-review interventions, also known as dignity and meaning-centered interventions [6, 7, 14, 15]. Conversational and cognitive-based therapies are designed to target and modify automatic negative thoughts and behavioral patterns that contribute to and exacerbate emotional challenges, specifically depression and anxiety. These interventions involve the identification and replacement of maladaptive patterns with more rational thoughts and behaviors [16, 18–22]. Mindfulness-based interventions encompass diverse contemplative practices and incorporate components that encourage the deliberate focus on present-moment experiences, fostering non-judgmental awareness and an attitude of acceptance [17]. Life review interventions provide patients the opportunity to reflect on significant life experiences, attitudes, and values they wish to share with others, fostering a sense of generativity.

Interventions suitable for patients with a life expectancy of less than 18 months must meet specific requirements due to the acute and often unstable conditions of the patients. Importantly, patients with advanced disease are a heterogeneous group being diverse in medical health conditions and mental states. Against this background, these patients might benefit from different interventions to address their individual needs and to maintain their sense of dignity and meaning in life.

Notably, while all evaluated approaches aim to foster dignity and the sense of meaning in patients with advanced diseases, they are usually applied as standardized and manualized stand-alone interventions [6]. This renders their application difficult, when aiming to address the complex individual needs of palliative care patients in a personalized manner. Previous psychotherapy research has demonstrated that evidence-based treatments for depression, anxiety, and conduct problems are more effective when structured as flexible, freestanding modules following a component-based approach. These modular treatments outperformed standard-evidenced-based treatments across multiple clinical outcomes [23–27].

Akin to personalized medicine for physical conditions, personalized and individualized interventions for mental health issues rely on accurately assessing clinically relevant individual characteristics. This may involve prioritizing or integrating multiple predictors of treatment engagement or overall outcome, such as comorbidity, motivation for change, and treatment history [26]. Instead of prioritizing or integrating multiple clinical predictors, personalization may also involve selecting a suitable form of psychotherapy based on physician recommendations and patient preferences. Directly involving patients in the choice of therapy increases their willingness to initiate and engage in treatments, leading to more positive outcomes [28]. Moreover, active patient involvement ensures that interventions are based on an understanding of individual disease/ symptom/ mental burden, which is highly relevant in palliative care [29].

Therefore, we aimed to identify which existing interventions are suitable for a personalized, modular approach in the acute-care setting. The target patient population included individuals with an advanced, incurable, life-limiting disease who required multimodal support and were mentally and physically able to attend the offered sessions.

The modular approach builds on previous work on dignity, supportive-expressive, and cognitive therapies, and mindfulness-based interventions in palliative care, with the overall aim to improve dignity, the sense of meaning, and mindfulness for palliative care patients. In sum, the modular approach offers a promising way to tailor interventions to individual needs of palliative care patients, leveraging the strengths of evidence-based treatments while enhancing their application and effectiveness.

Literature reviews

As the first step of the study, an exploratory literature review was conducted to identify psychological interventions published until the year 2017, which would fit a modular intervention for palliative care patients in acute-care settings. The database PubMed was searched by two experienced researchers (AS, SE), titles were screened and eligibility of full-text articles was discussed within research group meetings. Relevant interventions were extracted and their suitability for the initial concept of the modular approach was assessed in group discussions.

In 2021 a systematic review investigating the effectiveness of psychological short-term interventions in inpatient palliative care settings was conducted in five databases (Embase, PubMed, PsycINFO, Web of Science, and CINAHL) from their inception to 10th of September 2021. Results from this systematic review refined the final modular intervention protocol. For more detailed information on the methodology, the systematic review including a Bayesian network meta-analysis is publicly available [32].

Local stakeholder involvement and contextual analysis

We set up a local, multidisciplinary research group including experts from the field of palliative care with expert knowledge of patient population, patient needs and local infrastructure as well as expertise in psychotherapeutic interventions. Regular meetings of the local stakeholder group, consisting of the core research group (SE, SW, AS) and 2 experts in psychosomatics and psychology/ psychotherapy (RaS, GM), addressed the choice of modular interventions, feasibility in the local setting and patient population, as well as the scope and organization of the international stakeholder involvement. The choice of the three modular interventions was guided by the consideration of different target populations, different mental and physical conditions and expected benefits for patients with different areas of distress in the palliative care setting (Table 1). The chosen modular interventions should be complementary and address the needs of a wide variety of patients in the acute palliative care setting.

Wrap-up of the modular intervention development

During the final development process of the modular intervention, the contributing elements – including literature reviews, contextual analysis, local stakeholder involvement, and international stakeholder workshop – were discussed within the local stakeholder research group in Basel, and through email exchanges with international stakeholders. Results

Results from literature reviews

Results from the initial exploratory review and the subsequent systematic review provided information on availability and effectiveness of interventions for patients in the acute palliative care setting. The identified studies indicated that supportive-expressive and cognitive-behavioral therapy, mindfulness-based interventions, and life review interventions, also referred to as dignity and meaning-centered interventions, exhibited varying degrees of effectiveness in alleviating depression, anxiety, and distress among individuals with progressive and life-limiting illnesses [6, 21]. The meta-analysis of the systematic review indicated that life review interventions were the best-ranked treatment for improving anxiety and distress, while the top ranked treatment for reducing depression was Outlook intervention [32]. While life review interventions are effective in addressing existential distress, they may also hold promise in mitigating end-of-life anxiety and depression [6]. Outlook therapy requires three in person interviews during a one-month period after the baseline interview and is therefore less feasible in the acute care setting. Conversational and cognitive-behavioral based therapy was ranked second in the treatments for anxiety and depression, while mindfulness-based interventions were estimated as the second-best treatment option for distress. Substantial empirical support underscored the efficacy of supportive-expressive, and cognitive based therapies across various disorders and patient populations, particularly in mitigating symptoms of depression and anxiety [33–35]. Mindfulness-based interventions have demonstrated notable efficacy in reducing stress, pain, anxiety, depression, and restlessness [40].

Wrap-up of the modular intervention development

Based on these findings, the research group selected three psychological interventions, which were deemed most suitable for a modular intervention in the acute palliative care setting, namely dignity therapy (module 1), CALM therapy (module 2), and mindfulness-based therapy (module 3). The three selected modules were chosen (i) since they differ in terms of their content and patients’ needs addressed, (ii) since they can be offered modularly in two sessions, and (iii) are based on published evidence on their effectiveness. The three selected therapies are summarized in Table 1. Evidence of effectiveness has been gathered in clinical research for all three selected therapies:

‘Dignity Therapy’ is a brief, individualized psychotherapeutic intervention, which is designed to relieve distress and enhance the end-of-life experiences of terminally ill patients by addressing dignity-conserving factors [43]. Dignity therapy is based on the construct of generativity and gives patients the chance to record meaningful aspects of their lives and leave behind a personal legacy that can benefit their loved ones [43]. Dignity therapy has already been evaluated several times in the context of specialized palliative care settings [14, 44]. Patients who received dignity therapy reported a heightened sense of dignity, an increased will to live, increased feeling of hopefulness. Patients also deemed it helpful for their friends and relatives [45–51]. A literature review from 2021 attested the effectiveness of dignity therapy, and suggested further trials to support the potential to address psychosocial distress at the end of life [52]. Furthermore, a recent RCT on Dignity Therapy showed the potential to increase health related quality of life and prevent further mental health deterioration, illness burden and suffering in terminally-ill patients [53].

CALM therapy is a brief, supportive-expressive psychotherapy, and was originally designed to alleviate distress and to promote psychological well-being and personal growth in patients with advanced cancer [22]. CALM therapy has since been applied in a wider spectrum of palliative-care patients [36, 37]. CALM therapy aims to help patients manage the disease, symptoms, and treatment and to find a sense of meaning and purpose in life despite the disease. Furthermore, CALM therapy supports patients in preparing for the future, in sustaining hope while facing the end of life, and at the same time also facilitating communication with healthcare providers [20, 38, 39]. Clinical outcomes that have been shown to be sensitive to the effects of CALM include depressive symptoms, distress about dying and death, and preparation for the end of life [19, 36]. Qualitative outcomes that have been described by CALM participants include the experience of the therapist as a secure base, feeling seen as a whole person, feeling more capable of facing the end of life, and a sense of partnership with the therapist, who is perceived as an “insider” in the oncological treatment setting [38].

Description of modular intervention approach

The modular intervention approach is aimed at palliative care patients suffering from life-limiting diseases (life expectancy ≤ 18-month) and follows evidence-based intervention strategies that have been shown to promote dignity and meaning, reduce distress, and improve quality of life among patients with advanced life-threatening diseases. All three modules will be available for palliative care patients (cancer and non-cancer patients). The interventions are structured as freestanding modules that represent a selection of options for clinicians and patients. The modules can be adapted to the patients’ needs and preferences (Fig. 2). Due to the patient population, the modules can only be carried out in individual sessions - group sessions are not possible.

Assessment and module selection

Prior to the module selection for patients, a multidimensional palliative care assessment [57, 58] is performed screening for underlying diagnosis, understanding of disease, symptom burden, functional status, demographic factors, distress, and spiritual needs. This basic assessment is conducted by the palliative care consulting team in the acute hospital, enabling the identification of patients’ individual needs and preferences. The assessment is also an evaluation of the physician if the patient is mentally and physically able to benefit from one of the interventions. Subsequently, the three modules are thoroughly introduced to the patient, who then chooses one of the introduced interventions based on her/his personal preference. The physician can discuss the choices and give a recommendation based on the assessment. Modular intervention

Each module will be conducted within two structured sessions of 60 min each by a trained therapist (requirements of training are provided in Table 2). The modules are provided either at patient bedside or during outpatient visits.

Module 1: generativity

The module “generativity” consists of Dignity Therapy, which gives the patient the opportunity to create a legacy document (generativity document) that can be passed along to the significant other or to the family [11, 43] Based on the DT question protocol (Supplementary Table 3) [43], patients alone or together with their accompanying relatives are guided through the conversation by a trained therapist. The question protocol consists of 10 questions and is meant to provide a flexible framework, shaped according to the patient’s needs and specifications, designed to elicit messages that patients want to tell and pass on to their loved ones [43]. A German version was successfully tested and implemented [44]. The narrative may include reflections on facets of the patients’ personal histories, their most important achievements, social roles, hopes, wishes or dreams for their loved ones, and anything else they would want to pass along or have remembered. The patient’s narration is audiotaped. Upon completion, the audiotape is transcribed verbatim and edited to provide a clear and readable narration called the “generativity document”. In a second session, the generativity document is read out loud to the patient to ensure that there are no errors needing correction. The final version of the document is given to the patient, who can share it with their loved ones.

Module 2: living meaningfully

The module “living meaningfully” is based on the CALM therapy and is aimed at patients at high risk for death anxiety and distress [20, 22, 60]. CALM is delivered proactively and has preventive value. The therapy covers four domains:

Symptom control and communication with health care providers: Cooperation and improvement of communication with health care providers, and support with medical decision-making to ensure the best care and control of symptoms are addressed.

Symptom control and communication with health care providers: Cooperation and improvement of communication with health care providers, and support with medical decision-making to ensure the best care and control of symptoms are addressed.

Self-concept and important relationships: Supporting the adjustment of self-esteem and identity to cancer-related changes, also regarding relationships with close others, and sustaining and providing required care and support are addressed.

Self-concept and important relationships: Supporting the adjustment of self-esteem and identity to cancer-related changes, also regarding relationships with close others, and sustaining and providing required care and support are addressed.

Spiritual well-being/ meaning and purpose in life: understanding the individual meaning of suffering and dying and evaluating priorities and goals facing in light on an advanced disease are addressed.

Spiritual well-being/ meaning and purpose in life: understanding the individual meaning of suffering and dying and evaluating priorities and goals facing in light on an advanced disease are addressed.

Preparing for the future, sustaining hope, and facing mortality: Acknowledgment of anticipatory fears, balancing life and death, planning advanced treatment, and preparing the process of dying.

Preparing for the future, sustaining hope, and facing mortality: Acknowledgment of anticipatory fears, balancing life and death, planning advanced treatment, and preparing the process of dying.

In each session, the patient decides which one of the four CALM-domains is discussed and to what extent the topics should be addressed by the therapist. According to the patient’s needs and individual preferences, different topics within the CALM manual will be explored and discussed within two individual therapy sessions (duration: approximately 45–60 min each) delivered over a period of two weeks. In addition, the patient has the option to invite his or her significant other or a close family member to one or both sessions.

The process of CALM is as important as the content of the domains. The process includes the formation and maintenance of a therapeutic relationship, the empathic understanding of the patient’s experience, and mentalization, which entails making sense of the illness experience in the context of the patient’s life history and enhancement of the capacity to entertain alternative perspectives. Aims of the intervention are to enhance “double awareness” of the possibilities, richness and beauty of living as well as facing the end of life. Another goal is to provide support to renegotiate attachment security, and address challenges, burdens, and decisions of advanced and progressive disease [60].

Module 3: mindfulness

The module “mindfulness” is aimed at patients who have the preference to reduce distress through a mindfulness-based intervention. Patients in the mindfulness module will attend two 60-minute sessions over a period of 2 weeks. During these sessions, patients are instructed to focus their attention on the present moment, on the “here and now”. The therapist guides the patients through the exercise, reminding the patient to gently bring their focus back to the present moment when their mind drifts away. The patient simply tries to observe his or her experience of “being in the present moment” without judging or modifying it [55]. Mindfulness is also suited for patients whose verbal communication is limited due to disease progression or physical decline [42]. The mindfulness-based intervention could prove especially useful in palliative care patients, who experience inner distress, a loss of dignity, anxiety, or restlessness [42].

Discussion

To date, no modular psychological approach exists for palliative care patients in an acute-care hospital setting that can be tailored to patients’ immediate needs. This article describes the development of a personalized, modular approach with the overall purpose of more precisely addressing the needs of palliative care patients nearing the end of their life and thereby improving patient outcomes. In a multi-step process, including literature reviews, expert consensus of international specialists in palliative care, a three modular intervention approach, consisting of dignity therapy, CALM therapy, and mindfulness-based therapy was developed. All three interventions are empirically grounded and have been utilized in the context of palliative care.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of our study are the systematic and structured process of development of the modular approach, which included the involvement of local and international stakeholder groups and the performance of a comprehensive contextual analysis. In addition, the development was based on prior evidence gathered through literature searches and the consideration of patient’s individual needs. The treatment protocol within the modules was developed through a clear, systematic approach tailored to meet the specific needs and preferences of palliative care patients.

Our study has the following limitations: (1) Initially, we conducted an exploratory literature review, which was subsequently updated with a systematic review that included meta-analyses. (2) Second, an expert panel comprising international authorities in palliative care, dignity therapy, nursing, psychotherapy, clinical psychology, psychiatry, and psychosomatic medicine discussed the modular intervention approach during a one-day workshop. Despite its strengths, this method has potential limitations: The dynamic of such workshops may raise concerns about the influence of moderators on the group’s outcomes. Moderators may unintentionally introduce their biases into the discussion, potentially skewing the results. Additionally, dominant individuals, including moderators, might steer the group towards certain assumptions or conclusions. Moreover, participants could withhold their genuine opinions out of concern for contradicting the moderator or peers, leading to a less candid and comprehensive exchange of ideas [61]. With regard to these potential limitations, we tried to minimize these effects by an appropriate structure of the panel and reasonable workshop moderation.

(3) Third, patient needs were considered through the experience of the expert panel rather than direct input from patients. Gathering patient input on their opinions and needs will be an essential component of the feasibility study in order to verify the alignment between expert-identified needs and patient-perceived needs.

(4) Fourth, limitations to the proposed modular approach include the lack of evidence for the enhancement of dignity and meaning in end-of-life patients by mindfulness-based interventions. This modular intervention was specifically designed for an acute-care hospital setting, where resources relating to infrastructure, such as single rooms for therapy, are often limited. Another limitation is that the personalized, modular approach in its current form is rather resource intensive and may involve education and training, since each module can only be carried out by a specially trained healthcare professional. Therefore, a feasibility study is needed to shed light on the acceptability and feasibility of the proposed personalized, modular approach. The personalized, modular approach has yet to be tested and evaluated in a clinical sample of palliative care patients with various underlying diseases.

Comparison with similar studies and frameworks

Various assessments are available to identify the needs of palliative care patients [62]. In this context, approaches focusing on needs rather than prognosis seem to be more effective in facilitating timely palliative care [62]. However, these assessment tools usually do not include a recommendation of the scope and direction of palliative care that would be most helpful for the individual patient. Timely and personalized palliative care as described by Hui takes into account standardized referral criteria focused on needs, which also consider availability of palliative care resources to deliver personalized, timely patient-centered care [63, 64] Raising the awareness of the array of supportive care options that are available and identifying potentially helpful interventions is highly relevant for patients with a very limited life expectancy. We want to address these challenges in the acute palliative care setting by providing effective short-term interventions that meet the patient’s most relevant and urgent needs.

Considering patient’s preference has been shown to increase the effectiveness of psychotherapeutic treatment [28]. By offering three specific options of different therapy approaches in the modular intervention and providing resources for the immediate realization of the patients choice, we are considering timely personalized palliative care with a strong patient involvement. NHS England also sought to enhance personalized palliative and end of life care by using the “Comprehensive Personalized Care Model” to view the improvements needed to support increased choice and control at the end of life and better experience of care. This also includes choices made for the mental end of life care as well as the physical [65]. Proposing models and frameworks for palliative care is an important step to ensure the improved implementation and quality of palliative care options [66]. Interprofessional guidance for assessment and holistic care of patients with palliative care needs, promotes support to the family, and supports high quality personalized palliative care throughout the palliative trajectory [67]. Our approach based on the involvement of local and international stakeholders further supports these initiatives and underscores the significance of interventions that address the individual needs of this diverse patient population while actively involving patients in selecting their therapy.

By including patient’s preference and offering three different options within the modular intervention we want to provide an important component for an integral approach of dignity, meaningfulness and mindfulness in the acute care setting and investigate the potential of further improvement of palliative care for the individual patient.

Implications for further research

The modular intervention presented in this paper serves as the basis for a feasibility study, which aims to evaluate the implementation and acceptance of the personalized, modular approach among palliative care patients in acute hospital care (Supplementary Table 2). Measurement parameters to determine the feasibility and effectiveness of the modular intervention, which were discussed in the expert workshop, will be applied and described in detail in the subsequent feasibility study and related publications. Quantitative as well as qualitative outcome measures will be relevant to assess the needs and preferences of palliative care patients, usefulness and acceptance of the modular intervention, and feasibility in an acute care setting. In order to assess the impact of the modules on patient’s sense of dignity, death anxiety and distress and overall health, we are including the Patient Dignity Inventory (PDI), Death and Dying Distress Scale (DAADS) and Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) questionnaires in our feasibility study. In addition, patients will be interviewed in a semi-structured interview that includes Likert scales as well as open questions to evaluate patient’s perception of the therapies and allow for suggestions of improvement.

Importantly, patient-centered care cannot be practiced without patients participating in their own health care decisions and in the research, which informs such decisions. Treatment protocols should be continuously elaborated and redefined by considering feedback from its application in clinical practice [31]. Engaging patients in clinical trials is important and can potentially help researchers to ensure that the study design of their research is relevant, applicable, participant friendly and ethically sound [68, 69]. Thus, within the planned feasibility study, the personalized, modular approach will be further improved through patient involvement by listening to the patients’ perspectives on the applicability and usability of the intervention and by identifying palliative care patients’ individual needs and values.

Conclusion

A modular intervention approach to promote dignity, meaning, and mindfulness in patients receiving palliative care was developed through existing dignity-fostering, meaning-making end-of-life interventions redesign, informed by literature review and a panel of international experts and their clinical experience. This modular intervention serves as the basis for a phase I feasibility study, which will investigate its application and acceptance in palliative care patients in acute hospital care. It is our hope that this patient-centered modular approach will be able to address the needs of patients nearing the end of their lives more precisely and thereby improve patient outcomes.
