Computer-assisted planning combined with bone crest positioning to guide lateral acromioplasty: methods and clinical application
Authors: Yanbo Wang, Biao Guo, Yanlong Liu, Yong Hu, Dongqiang Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study investigates preoperative CT three-dimensional reconstruction and computer-assisted planning, combined with bone crest positioning, to guide lateral acromioplasty for subacromial impingement syndrome (SAIS). It evaluates this approach’s effectiveness in enhancing surgical accuracy, improving shoulder function, and optimizing imaging parameters.MethodsThis study retrospectively analyzed 40 patients diagnosed with SAIS who underwent surgical treatment in our hospital between May 2022 and December 2023. Patients were divided into two groups: preoperative planning acromioplasty (group 1) and conventional acromioplasty (group 2), with 20 patients each. Preoperative and postoperative shoulder CT 3D reconstructions were performed to measure critical shoulder angle (CSA), acromion index (AI), acromio-humeral interval (AHI), and lateral acromial angle (LAA). Clinical function scores and visual analogue scale (VAS) were recorded preoperatively, at 6 months postoperatively, and at final follow-up (September 30, 2024). Postoperative MRI or CT assessed acromial fracture, deltoid insertion tears, and other major complications. Continuous variable data with a normal distribution were analyzed using the t-test; the rank sum test was used for continuous variable data with a non-normal distribution; and count data were analyzed using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests.ResultsWith an average follow-up of 19.70 ± 7.39 months (9–28 months). Postoperative comparison between groups showed that the CSA of group 1 was significantly lower than that of group 2 (P < 0.05). AI was lower and AHI was higher in group 1 versus group 2 postoperatively (P < 0.05). Group 1 achieved superior Constant and UCLA scores at final follow-up, with no significant differences in ASES or VAS scores between groups, and no cases of acromial fracture, deltoid insertion tears, or other major complications were observed postoperatively in either group.ConclusionThe use of preoperative CT 3D reconstruction and computer-assisted design for precise grinding planning, combined with an intraoperative bone ridge positioning method enhances surgical accuracy, can significantly improve clinical outcomes and imaging parameters, and protect deltoid insertion integrity, offering clinical value.Keywords: Computer simulation, Arthroscopy, CSA, Acromioplasty

SAIS is one of the most common shoulder diseases, accounting for 50%−70% of all shoulder pain symptoms [1]. Its main clinical manifestations are chronic shoulder pain and movement disorders [1, 2]. The pathogenesis of this disease is usually closely related to the narrowing of the subacromial space caused by osteophyte hyperplasia under the acromion and the thickening of the bursa tissue [2, 3]. This anatomical change leads to repeated impingement between the greater tuberosity of the humerus and the acromion during overhead movements, which may eventually develop into chronic non-traumatic rotator cuff tears [3]. By removing osteophytes or impingement areas from the acromion, mechanical impingement factors are eliminated, thus improving the symptoms of patients.

The CSA is defined as the angle between the Line connecting the lateral point under the acromion to the lower margin of the glenoid fossa on a standard anteroposterior X-ray film and the Line connecting the upper and lower margins of the glenoid fossa. Its safe range is generally considered to be between 30º and 35º [4, 5]. Studies have shown that a higher CSA is an important risk factor for shoulder impingement syndrome and has a high accuracy in predicting rotator cuff tears [6, 7]. In recent years, many studies have achieved good clinical results by grinding or removing acromial osteophytes during surgery to reduce CSA [8, 9]. However, traditional acromioplasty still has certain limitations in accurately controlling CSA to the ideal range; abnormally large CSA or insufficient correction may be associated with a higher retear rate and insufficient abduction strength after healing [10]. Currently, there is limited research on how to accurately assess the position and range of impingement osteophytes preoperatively and guide surgical procedures. Due to the lack of precise guidance, it is difficult for surgeons to efficiently and completely remove impingement osteophytes and adjust CSA to the ideal range under arthroscopy to achieve satisfactory surgical results.

Based on this, this study plans to use three-dimensional reconstruction technology combined with computer-assisted technology to perform a preoperative precise assessment of patients, including the position and range of impingement osteophytes, and simulate the range of bone to be ground during surgery to guide 20 cases of arthroscopic acromioplasty. On this basis, we compared this method with conventional acromioplasty to explore its feasibility and effectiveness in improving clinical efficacy and optimizing surgical outcomes.

Materials and methods

General information

Inclusion criteria

① Complete three-dimensional reconstruction of shoulder joint CT and shoulder joint MRI examination before surgery; ② Preoperative critical shoulder angle (CSA) ≥ 35°; ③ Preoperative MRI confirms rotator cuff tear (small-to-medium full-thickness rotator cuff tears, <3 cm), requiring arthroscopic rotator cuff repair; ④ Follow-up for more than 6 months.

Exclusion criteria

① Massive irreparable rotator cuff tears; ② There is a history of shoulder trauma, accompanied by shoulder fracture or dislocation; ③ The affected shoulder has a history of surgery; ④ Accompanied by periarthritis of the shoulder, glenohumeral osteoarthritis (OA) or other inflammatory arthritis; ⑤ Rupture of the long head tendon of the biceps brachii; ⑥ Injury to the insertion point of the deltoid muscle.

Case data

Forty patients with rotator cuff tears admitted to our hospital from May 2022 to December 2023 were selected as research subjects. Of these, 13 were male and 27 were female; 13 had left shoulder involvement and 27 had right shoulder involvement; the age range was 42–72 years, with an average of (55.53 ± 8.26) years; the average follow-up time was (19.70 ± 7.39) months (9–28 months).

Methods

This study is a retrospective, single-center analysis. Eligible participants were divided into the preoperative planning acromioplasty group (group 1) and the conventional acromioplasty group (group 2) based on the surgical approach documented in their medical records. All included patients underwent three-dimensional CT reconstruction and MRI examination of the shoulder joint before and after surgery. 3D models was performed using CT data by the Orthopedic 3D Printing Center of our hospital before and after surgery. The study methods of the two groups are shown in Fig. 1. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the hospital, and all patients received informed consent.

Preoperative design and surgical techniques

Preoperative design

Group 1: Preoperatively, the target joint data of the German Siemens 64-row CT scan (tube voltage 120 kV, tube electrical current 300 mA, scanning layer thickness 0.6 mm) were used for 3D virtual reality and multi-planar reconstruction. The CT data were imported into the 3D simulation software (9yuan3D Digital Orthopedics System, Shanghai Jiao Tong University 3D Printing Innovation Research Center) in DICOM format to reconstruct the scapula, and the upper edge of the glenoid was marked as A and the lower edge was marked as B. The plane where the anterior and posterior edges of the glenoid overlapped was taken as α, and a plane β perpendicular to plane α was made through line segment AB. A straight line m perpendicular to plane α was made through point B in plane β, and plane β was rotated around straight line m toward the acromion side. When the plane passed the outermost point of the acromion, its rotation angle was the CSA measured in the three-dimensional structure. When plane β rotated 34° around straight line m toward the acromion, it intersected with the anterior and posterior edges of the acromion at points C and D, respectively. At this time, the outer side of line segment CD is the range of the acromion bone that is estimated to be ground to achieve the ideal CSA angle before surgery. Point E is marked as the intersection of the bone crest and the acromion, and the distances between point E and the anterior edge (point C) and the posterior lateral edge (point D) of the acromion are measured respectively.(Fig. 2).

Surgical method

All surgeries were performed by two orthopedic surgeons with senior professional titles, using identical arthroscopic setups and standardized protocols.

Lateral decubitus position with the healthy side down, the shoulder is positioned at 30° posterior tilt, 60° abduction, and 30° forward flexion, followed by traction fixation. The scapular spine, lateral segment of the clavicle, acromion, and anterior/posterior approaches are marked on the skin using a surgical marker.

Group 1: General anesthesia, controlled hypotension during surgery, arterial systolic pressure was controlled below 100 mmHg, and 0.5 ml of epinephrine was added to every 3000 ml of saline lavage fluid. The conventional posterior approach was used to enter the joint cavity. After the glenohumeral joint was explored and cleaned, the patient entered the subacromial space. The anterolateral and lateral approaches were added. The bursa of the subacromial space was cleaned with a planer, and the subacromial surface was cleaned with an electrocoagulation knife to determine the medial edge of the hyperplastic bone ridge on the subacromial surface (Fig. 3a). Enter the camera from the posterior approach for observation, use the ruler to enter from the anterior lateral approach, hook the top of the bone ridge and the intersection of the medial edge of the acromion and the anterior edge of the acromion (point E, Fig. 2f), find the anterior acromion mark point according to the preoperative measurement data (line segment CE and DE, Fig. 2f), and use a hollow puncture needle to mark the anterior acromion positioning point on the body surface (Fig. 3b-c), then switch to the lateral approach for observation, enter the ruler from the posterior approach to find the posterior acromion mark point, and also use a hollow puncture needle to mark the positioning on the body surface (Fig. 3d-f), use the PDS line from the inner core of the hollow puncture needle to mark the required grinding range from front to back (Fig. 3g-h), then enter the grinding head from the lateral channel, grind the lateral acromion bone according to the marked range, and after the grinding of the lateral acromion bone is completed, grind and remove the residual osteophytes on the lower surface of the acromion (Fig. 3i). After the acromion is processed, the rotator cuff repair surgery is performed routinely.

Group 2: The same group of doctors did not use intraoperative positioning technology based on experience and performed acromion shaping routinely.

Postoperative functional rehabilitation exercise

After surgery, continuous ice therapy was applied during the 24 h postoperatively, with each session lasting 20 min every 2 h, and use an abduction brace to support the shoulder in the scapula plane with an abduction position of 30°~45° for 4 weeks. The 4-week period is the maximum protection stage. During this stage, the hands, wrists, and elbows are actively moved, and pendulum movements are practiced. The 4–8 weeks are the moderate protection stage after surgery, mainly for further training of scapula stability structure, rotator cuff muscle isometric exercises, and active range of motion of the glenohumeral joint. Wall climbing and resistance training can be performed. After surgery, 9–12 weeks are the minimum protection stage, and closed chain exercises are performed to strengthen the rotator cuff muscles, subscapularis, biceps, deltoids, pectoralis major and other muscles that stabilize the scapula; open chain exercises, proprioception and strengthening exercises, and posture recovery of the movement chain are performed 13–16 weeks after surgery. The preoperative activity level is allowed 6 months after surgery.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0 statistical software was used to analyze the data. The Shapiro-Wilk method was used to test the normality of the data. The t-test was used for continuous variable data with normal distribution, and the measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation(‾x ± s); the rank sum test was used for continuous variable data with non-normal distribution, and the measurement data were expressed as median and interquartile range [M༈P25, P75༉]; count data were presented as counts (n) and percentages (%), and group comparisons were conducted using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Comparison of general data of the two groups of patients

Of the 87 patients who initially met the inclusion criteria, 15 were excluded due to the presence of large or massive rotator cuff tears. Furthermore, 27 patients were excluded because of concurrent shoulder conditions, such as fractures, dislocations, or various types of shoulder joint inflammation. Four patients were excluded due to a history of prior surgery on the affected shoulder, and one patient was excluded because of pre-existing damage to the deltoid insertion site identified before surgery. A total of 40 patients were ultimately included in this study and were divided into two groups based on the surgical techniques employed.

There was no significant difference in gender, age, shoulder impact side, height, weight, BMI, diabetes, and rotator cuff tear type between the two groups of patients (P > 0.05), and they were comparable (Table 1).

Discussion

Some scholars believe that a larger CSA is closely related to the occurrence of SAIS. Patients with SAIS often show a significant increase in acromial thickness and larger CSA [5, 14]. Therefore, arthroscopic acromioplasty to improve CSA is considered the “gold standard” surgery for the treatment of SAIS [15]. We used computer-assisted planning combined with the bone crest positioning method to guide the lateral acromionoplasty to treat SAIS. Postoperative analysis revealed that in group 1, CSA was reduced to a safe range in all patients, and 80% of the patients were accurately adjusted to the ideal target angle (34 °). In contrast, group 2 relied solely on surgeon experience, resulting in 50% of patients failing to reach the preoperative target angle, and 5 patients had a postoperative CSA exceeding 35°, potentially increasing the risk of poor recovery or recurrence. Additionally, group 1 showed significantly lower AI and higher AHI compared to group 2 (P < 0.05). While no significant differences in clinical functional scores were observed between groups at 6 months postoperatively, at the final follow-up, group 1 demonstrated significantly better Constant and UCLA scores (P < 0.05). These findings highlight that this technique enhances surgical precision, effectively reduces CSA, improves clinical outcomes and holds substantial clinical value for improving shoulder function.

Currently, anteroposterior and lateral X-rays and three-dimensional CT are usually used clinically to identify the location of acromial osteophyte formation [16]. However, these methods have certain limitations in preoperative planning, especially in accurately assessing the volume and extent of bone that needs to be ground during surgery [17, 18]. Toro et al. used X-rays for preoperative planning to guide lateral acromionoplasty and measured the corrected CSA through postoperative X-rays. The study results showed that only 41.5% of patients had CSA reduced to 35° or lower, while only 29.3% of the accuracy in achieving preoperative planning goals was rated as good, and the accuracy in 46.3% of cases was unsatisfactory [17]. This result may be related to the insufficient accuracy of X-ray measurement and planning, and the inability to effectively translate preoperative planning into intraoperative operations. Billaud et al. used three-dimensional reconstruction based on CT scans to measure the size of the acromion to be removed before acromioplasty [19]. Their study showed that 3D preoperative planning can accurately and reproducibly assess the acromion resection volume. However, the study did not explain in detail how to accurately adjust the CSA to a specific target value (such as 33°), nor did it explore how to effectively connect preoperative planning with actual surgical operations. Long et al. proposed a precise acromioplasty technology based on a 3D-CT model and software analysis for preoperative planning, and compared the effects of different acromioplasty techniques on reducing CSA [18]. Their research analysis showed that compared with traditional lateral or anterior acromioplasty, Compared with acromioplasty, precision acromioplasty technology is more effective in reducing CSA to the target value, which is consistent with our findings. However, this study is still mainly based on measuring CSA in a two-dimensional plane (2D) in a 3D-CT model, this may have a certain impact on the accuracy of the measurement results and their guiding significance.

In this study, we further developed preoperative planning-guided acromioplasty technology. By conducting a three-dimensional CT reconstruction examination of the shoulder joint on the patient and importing the image data into the computer software system, a complete 3D model of the shoulder joint was generated. In the model, measure the CSA in the three-dimensional state, adjust the CSA to the ideal range, and obtain the bone range that needs to be ground based on the measurement results. The difficulty of this technology is how to completely transform preoperative planning into precise grinding of the acromion bone during surgery. Some scholars have found that during intraoperative operations, it is difficult to accurately determine the resection axis under the arthroscopic field of view based on the preoperatively planned grinding range, and it is also impossible to ensure the direction of the resection [17]. Long et al. proposed a skin marking method, which is to determine three specific points on the acromion (anterior, posterior and lateral), measure the distance between them, and mark them on the skin as reference points [18]. The needle is inserted vertically at three points, and the resection line is drawn from the anterior point to the posterior point during arthroscopy. The author believes that this marking method may be affected by factors such as patient position, marking, and needle insertion operations, which may increase the grinding error. In long-term clinical practice, we have observed that in patients with acromion impingement syndrome, there are often varying degrees of bony crests between the edge of the impingement area on the lower surface of the acromion and the surrounding normal bone. The existence of this structure was further verified through the preoperative 3D model, and it was found that the bony ridge appears as a bony step-like protrusion extending from the anterior edge of the acromion to the posterior side. It is worth noting that the intersection between this bony crest and the anterior edge of the acromion is relatively easy to identify during preoperative imaging evaluation and intraoperative operation, which provides an important anatomical landmark for surgical planning and precise positioning. Therefore, this study proposes a bony crest localization method to apply preoperative planning to intraoperative procedures. Use a measuring ruler with a scale to measure the distance from the inner edge apex of the “iconic ridge” formed by the osteophytes to the preset marker point on the lower surface of the acromion. After finding the marker point position that meets the designed distance, stick it closely to the body surface. The needle is inserted vertically into the bone at the edge of the acromion to preset marking points, and then the measuring ruler is used to locate the anterior and posterior marking points, and the PDS Line is tightened from the anterior marking point to the posterior marking point and marked. The bone crest positioning method directly marks the preoperative plan on the acromion bone under arthroscopy, reducing other operations as much as possible and ensuring the accuracy of grinding to the greatest extent. Comparing the postoperative measurement of CSA with the preset angle of preoperative planning, the maximum deviation was 1.89º, and the minimum deviation was 0.23º, which initially proved the effectiveness of this method. The adoption of 3D CT planning and bone crest positioning requires surgeons to become proficient in both preoperative planning software and intraoperative navigation techniques. This learning curve can be attributed to familiarity with preoperative planning tools, spatial understanding of anatomical structures, and other factors. To address these challenges, training programs incorporating 3D simulation software and familiarity with arthroscopic landmarks could help shorten the learning curve.

Bigliani et al. classified the acromion into three types: type I is flat, type II is arc-shaped, and a type III is hook-shaped [20]. They also pointed out that type III acromion is more likely to cause rotator cuff injury or even tear. Based on this, some scholars believe that type III acromial morphology is a risk factor for poor prognosis without acromioplasty [21]. Studies have further shown that performing acromioplasty at the same time as rotator cuff repair can effectively reduce the risk of postoperative reoperation. Especially for patients with type III acromion, the benefits of this combined surgical approach may be more significant [22]. Therefore, accurate assessment of acromial morphology is of great significance in preoperative planning and postoperative evaluation of effects. In this study, all patients in group 1 who were diagnosed with type III acromion preoperatively were successfully converted to type II, while there was still one patient in group 2 who maintained type III acromion after surgery. Fully grinding related. This result further verifies the importance of formulating the grinding range based on preoperative 3D model analysis, and suggests that surgical results can be significantly optimized through standardized preoperative planning and intraoperative application, thereby improving patient prognosis.

The deltoid muscle is an important stabilizing structure for shoulder movements, and damage to its insertion point may affect the stability and function of the shoulder joint [23]. In lateral acromioplasty, maintaining the integrity of the deltoid insertion is a prerequisite for successful surgery. The empirical operation of traditional acromioplasty may lead to two potential risks: on the one hand, excessive resection may cause deltoid insertion injury or acromion fracture; on the other hand, insufficient resection may not fully relieve the symptoms of acromion impingement, causing the patient’s symptoms to persist [24, 25]. Therefore, accurately determining the scope of bone resection to optimize the surgical effect and reduce the risk of complications is an important issue that needs to be solved in current clinical practice. In this study, at the final follow-up after surgery, the deltoid insertion point was checked in the transverse and coronal views by re-examination of MRI, and no discontinuity in the deltoid insertion point was observed. However, studies have shown that traditional acromioplasty cannot adequately reduce the CSA if the preoperative measurement exceeds 40° [26]. Therefore, to reduce the high CSA to the ideal range, more acromial bone may need to be ground. If the surgeon performs grinding based on experience, this may result in insufficient grinding due to concerns about damaging the deltoid insertion point. If detailed planning can be carried out before surgery, it will help to clarify the specific scope of grinding, so that the surgeon can be calmer during the operation. In this study, we observed three patients in group 1 and two patients in group 2, all of whom had preoperative CSA exceeding 40°. The postoperative results showed that the CSA of patients in group 1 was controlled within 35°, and the CSA of two patients reached the ideal range, and neither deltoid insertion point tear occurred, while the CSA of two patients in group 2 still exceeded 35° after surgery. 35°. This preliminary study shows that this surgical method is safe and effective for patients with high CSA.

This study also has some limitations. Firstly, this study has limitations related to selection bias and a small sample size, the small sample size may reduce statistical power, and the limited sample size may lead to insufficient representativeness of the study population, increasing the risk of selection bias, although we have used consistent inclusion/exclusion criteria, identical surgical setup, and a uniform physiotherapy protocol to minimize them. In addition, we used the bony crest as an intraoperative landmark to guide the surgical operation. This specific anatomical structure of the bony crest is not present in all patients, which leads to potential limitations in the application of bony crest localization methods. However, our preliminary observations suggest that most patients with acromial impingement have a bone ridge, and regardless of its degree of development, as Long as this structure exists, we can clearly identify it through the 3D model of preoperative planning and intraoperative arthroscopy. This finding still needs to be further studied through larger sample sizes and multi-center studies. Verify its universality. Secondly, we only reviewed the MRI to check the deltoid insertion point within 2 days after surgery, which may affect the MRI imaging results due to the inflammatory reaction and edema of local tissues in the early postoperative period, thereby interfering with the assessment of the integrity of the deltoid insertion point. In addition, the difference between the two groups may not reach the minimal clinically important difference (MCID), so we do need Longer follow-up to verify greater differences between the two groups. In this study, we arbitrarily selected 34° as the target angle for the CSA, aiming to balance the reduction of mechanical stress with the preservation of acromial morphology and shoulder function. However, this selection may introduce a degree of bias, and further research will be conducted to determine the optimal target angle in the future.

Conclusion

The use of preoperative CT three-dimensional reconstruction and computer-assisted preoperative planning, combined with an intraoperative bone ridge positioning method to guide lateral acromionoplasty surgery, can effectively reduce CSA to the ideal range, and both clinical functional scores and imaging evaluations have achieved good results. The result is that there is no obvious damage to the insertion point of the deltoid muscle.
