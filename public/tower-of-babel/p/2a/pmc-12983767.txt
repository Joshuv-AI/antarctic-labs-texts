Which front-of-pack nutritional label (FOPNL) is most effective in promoting healthier dietary choices? An experimental study in Indonesia
Authors: David Colozza, Kathryn Backholer, Liliana Orellana, Dwiana Andayani, Ati Widya Perana, Fatcha Nuraliyah, Astrid Citra Padmita, Mamadou Ndiaye, Carolina Venegas Hargous
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMandatory interpretative front-of-pack nutrition labels (FOPNLs) are yet to be adopted in Indonesia. This study compares Indonesians’ objective understanding of five FOPNLs and explores perceptions on four interpretative labels.MethodsA randomised experiment was conducted in 4039 participants aged 16–78 years. Participants were assigned to view food products with one of five FOPNLs: Guideline Daily Amounts, Nutri-Score, multiple traffic light (MTL), warning labels (WL) or health star rating (HSR). Objective understanding was compared pre-label and post-label exposure by correctly ranking products by healthiness (Outcome 1) and identifying the healthiest (Outcome 2) and least healthy products (Outcome 3) using logistic mixed models. Healthy purchase intention post-label exposure (Outcome 4) was compared using logistic models. Perceptions of the four interpretative labels were explored.ResultsAll FOPNLs improved Outcomes 1, 2 and 3. WL and MTL performed better across all three outcomes, with no significant differences between them. Exposure to WL led to improvements of 16.3% points (95% CI 13.5 to 19.0), 9.5% points (95% CI 6.7 to 12.3) and 14.5% points (95% CI 11.3 to 17.7) for Outcomes 1, 2 and 3, respectively. MTL led to increases of 13.9% points (95% CI 11.2 to 16.6), 10.4% points (95% CI 7.4 to 13.4) and 16.8% points (95% CI 13.5 to 20.0), respectively. Proportion of participants intending to purchase the healthiest products (Outcome 4) was highest for MTL (39.6%, 95% CI 36.3 to 43.0), HSR (38.9%, 95% CI 35.5 to 42.2) and WL (35.6%, 95% CI 32.3 to 38.9), with no significant differences between them. Compared with WL and HSR, MTL was perceived as the most liked, useful, helpful and easy to identify.ConclusionsMTL and WL significantly improved participants’ understanding of products’ nutritional quality and promoted healthier food choices. MTL may offer the best balance between effectiveness and user acceptability. These findings can inform national FOPNL policy revisions and broader efforts to improve food environments.Keywords: Obesity, Preventive Medicine, Public Health, Public Health Practice

Introduction

Indonesia is facing rising rates of overweight, obesity and diet-related non-communicable diseases (NCDs). In 2023, these conditions affected approximately one in five children aged 5–12 (19·7%), one in seven adolescents aged 13–18 (14·3%) and one in three adults (37·8%).1 These trends, coupled with persistent undernutrition, have resulted in a pronounced triple burden of malnutrition.2 A key contributor is the growing prevalence of unhealthy food environments.3 4 The widespread availability, affordability and marketing of ultra-processed products (UPPs) high in unhealthy fats, salt and sugar, driven by transformations in the food supply and the expansion of modern retail outlets such as supermarkets and convenience stores, are of particular concern.3 5 Data show that, between 2007 and 2021, the sales of processed foods in Indonesia grew from around 7 kg to 10 kg per capita (46% increase) and those of non-alcoholic beverages, including packaged and ready-to-drink products, from around 10 L to 22 L per capita.6 These trends mirror observations from community-based studies, suggesting a high prevalence of UPPs and ready-made meals in the local diet.7 8

A critical factor behind this shift towards unhealthy food environments is the lack of robust regulatory frameworks. Indonesia has yet to adopt many of the WHO’s recommended ‘best buy’ policies for NCD prevention, including mandatory interpretive front-of-pack nutrition labels (FOPNL). Unlike non-interpretative systems, which present raw nutritional data without indicating overall healthiness, interpretative labels provide a simplified, evaluative assessment of a product’s nutritional quality.9 Designed to be easily understood by consumers with varying levels of nutrition literacy, interpretative FOPNL have generally been found to be more effective in guiding healthier choices.10 11 Interpretative FOPNL also play a supporting role in broader food policy efforts, such as regulating the marketing of unhealthy foods, by providing a simple mechanism to identify products that would be subject to the regulation based on their nutritional content.12 In Indonesia, two voluntary FOPNL schemes exist: a monochrome, non-interpretative Guideline Daily Amount (GDA) label and an interpretative 'healthier choice’ logo applied to a limited range of UPPs. However, available evidence suggests that neither label type is likely to have a meaningful public health impact. Non-interpretative labels, such as the GDA, are often difficult to interpret for consumers, while endorsement logos, such as the ‘healthier choice’ label, focus only on identifying the healthiest options within restricted product categories.13

In contrast, mandatory, interpretative FOPNL have shown to shape consumer behaviour through different mechanisms, such as deterring purchases of unhealthy products (aversion) or encouraging healthier ones (attraction). For example, warning labels used in Chile and Mexico are particularly effective at helping consumers avoid products high in nutrients of concern, such as sugar, sodium and saturated fat.14 Colour-coded systems, such as multiple traffic light or Nutri-Score, adopted in several European countries, may promote both aversion and attraction effects.13 However, research has also shown a possible ‘halo’ effect, with products featuring these labels perceived as healthier, and with items labelled favourably because low in target nutrients (eg, sugar) may still be nutritionally poor overall, increasing the risk of inadvertently encouraging consumption of unhealthy UPPs.15 16 The choice of a label format should therefore reflect policy goals, whether to primarily discourage unhealthy choices, promote healthier alternatives or both.13 Given the mixed evidence on the relative effectiveness of different label types across countries and populations,17 18 context-specific research is essential to inform locally appropriate policies.

Indonesia is currently in the process of revising its food labelling policies, with the stated aim to reduce the prevalence of obesity, diabetes and other diet-related NCDs,19 and has proposed the phased implementation of a mandatory traffic-light labelling system called Nutri-level in certain processed foods and drinks with excessive content of sugar, salt and fat, starting with ready-to-drink, non-alcoholic beverages.20 However, evidence of its potential effectiveness in the Indonesian context is lacking. This study aimed to generate evidence from Indonesia on the effectiveness of five FOPNL in supporting healthier food choices, including the (1) GDA currently used in Indonesia, and four interpretative labels that have demonstrated positive public health impacts in other settings: (2) Nutri-Score (NS), (3) health star rating (HSR), (4) multiple traffic light (MTL) and (5) a monochrome warning label (WL).

The five FOPNL were selected to reflect current regulatory practice in Indonesia (GDA) and evidence-based, internationally recognised interpretative labelling approaches representing plausible options for future regulatory consideration (MTL, NS, WL and HSR). This selection aimed to address a key evidence gap in the Indonesian context, where no empirical data currently exist on the relative effectiveness of these schemes in guiding healthier consumer choices. Including both the existing GDA and alternative interpretative labels enabled direct comparison across labelling approaches, generating policy-relevant evidence to inform ongoing discussions on strengthening FOPNL regulations in Indonesia.

The specific objectives were (1) to compare the objective understanding of the five FOPNL, (2) to assess the influence of the FOPNL on intention to purchase the healthiest products and (3) to explore consumers’ perceptions of the four interpretive labels currently not in use in Indonesia. The findings aim to inform ongoing efforts to revise FOPNL regulations in Indonesia and contribute to broader strategies to tackle the growing burden of overweight, obesity and diet-related NCDs.

Materials and methods

Trial design

We conducted a randomised experiment in a sample of Indonesian adolescents and adults, with participants randomly allocated (1:1:1:1:1) to one of the five FOPNL (figure 1).1718 21,23 Participant recruitment and data collection took place between September 2024 and February 2025 via an online panel survey, administered by PureProfile, under the supervision of the study team. Study results are reported following the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials for Non-Pharmacologic Treatments guidelines.24

Participants

Eligible participants were Indonesian residents aged 16–80 years who reported purchasing at least two of the following UPP categories at least once in the past 3 months: sweetened drinks, savoury and sweet snacks, instant noodles and ready-to-eat meals.

Experiment

Survey design followed WHO guidance,25 and the Front-Of-Pack International Comparative Experimental (FOP-ICE) methodology.13 All eligible participants were exposed to a pre-label condition (baseline) and a post-label condition. Participants’ objective understanding of the five FOPNL was assessed by testing their ability to correctly rank three products by healthiness and to identify the healthiest and least healthy options across three food categories, both at baseline and following label exposure.

To minimise brand familiarity bias, we created three photo sets of mock-up packages using a fictional brand, ‘Nikmati’. The mock-ups resembled real products from three food categories commonly consumed in Indonesia: packaged drinks, instant noodles and ice creams. These food categories were selected based on market data showing high sale volumes and growth in recent years.26 For FOPNL based on graded or categorical signals rather than interpretation of specific nutrient values (NS, HSR and WL), labels were applied to explicitly represent three levels of healthiness (eg, NS A, C and E; no WL, one WL and two WL), consistent with the intended interpretation of each scheme. For labels displaying nutrient-specific information (GDA) or based on nutrient threshold calculations (MTL), product healthiness was defined using real-world nutritional data from the Indonesian market to identify nutritionally plausible ranges for key nutrients of concern (ie, energy, sodium, saturated fat and sugars).

Based on these ranges, each set of three mock-up products was designed to represent clearly differentiated levels of overall healthiness (healthiest, moderately healthy and least healthy). For the MTL, UK Department of Health guidelines27 were used to assign appropriate colour coding. Across all food categories, product A was always the healthiest, product B moderately healthy and product C the least healthy. Mock-up packages displayed only the brand name, flavour and net weight. No nutritional information was shown beyond the FOPNL under experimental conditions. Therefore, at baseline (pre-label condition), there were no visible cues indicating product healthiness.

Nutritional values for instant noodles were assessed on an ‘as sold’ basis, consistent with how nutrient information is presented on packaged foods in Indonesia and how FOPNL are encountered at the point of purchase. All nutrient profiling models, including HSR and NS, were therefore applied using the same ‘as sold’ data to ensure consistency across products and labelling schemes. This approach reflects the real-world context in which consumers encounter FOPNL prior to preparation or consumption.

In the pre-label condition, participants were shown unlabelled versions of the three-product sets and asked to rank them by perceived healthiness and indicate which product they were most likely to purchase. These questions were repeated for each food category. In the post-label condition, participants were shown the same three-product sets with the FOPNL corresponding to their randomised allocation (see figure 2 for an example of the MTL condition and online supplemental file 1 for details on all the labels and mock-up packages used in the study) and asked the same questions again. The order of food category and the sequence of products within each set were randomised. Participants received no explanation about the FOPNL tested, simulating real-world exposure to FOPNL in the absence of prior education or guidance.

Mock-up packages shown to participants in the pre-label and post-label conditions for the group randomised to the multi-traffic light label. The multiple traffic light label was created specifically for this study, drawing on technical guidance for the traffic light label implemented in the UK in 2013 issued by the UK Food Standards Agency under an Open Government Licence. These guidelines are disseminated under an Open Government Licence: www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/doc/open-government-licence/version/3/

Additional questions covered participants’ sociodemographic characteristics, including age, gender, education and income.

A sub-sample of participants (those who were randomised to NS and WL, n=1608) completed an extended version of the survey that included perception measures for the four interpretative FOPNL not currently implemented in Indonesia (MTL, NS, WL and HSR). This sub-sampling approach was implemented due to resource constraints. Participants rated each label on likability, acceptability, attractiveness, visibility, usefulness, helpfulness, cognitive workload and processing time, using 5-point Likert scales. The full survey is provided in online supplemental file 2.

Outcome measures

Objective understanding of five FOPNL

For each food category (drinks, noodles and ice creams), binary indicators (0 = ‘No’, 1 = ‘Yes’) were created at both baseline and post-label exposure to assess whether participants (a) correctly ranked the three products by healthiness; (b) correctly identified the healthiest product; and (c) correctly identified the least healthy product. The information across the three food categories was used to create the following binary outcomes:

Outcome 1: correctly ranked products by healthiness in at least two of the three food categories.

Outcome 1: correctly ranked products by healthiness in at least two of the three food categories.

Outcome 2: correctly identified the healthiest products in at least two of the three food categories.

Outcome 2: correctly identified the healthiest products in at least two of the three food categories.

Outcome 3: correctly identified the least healthy products in at least two of the three food categories.

Outcome 3: correctly identified the least healthy products in at least two of the three food categories.

Influence on purchase intention of five FOPNL

For each food category, binary indicators were created to reflect participants’ intention to purchase the healthiest product post-label exposure and the following binary outcome was defined:

Outcome 4: intention to purchase the healthiest products in at least two of the three food categories post-label exposure.

Outcome 4: intention to purchase the healthiest products in at least two of the three food categories post-label exposure.

Perceptions of the four proposed FOPNL currently not available in Indonesia

Perceptions on the four interpretative FOPNL were assessed on likability (“I like this label”), acceptability (“I would like to see the label on the package”), attractiveness (“The label catches my attention”), visibility (“The label is easy to spot on packages”), usefulness (“The label is useful”), helpfulness (“The label helps me choose healthier products”), cognitive workload (“The label is too complex to understand”) and processing time (“The label takes too long to understand”). Responses used a Likert scale with anchors ‘Strongly disagree’ (1) and ‘Strongly agree’ (5). Responses were transformed into binary indicators (1 if participant selected ‘Agree’ or ‘Strongly agree’, and 0 otherwise. These were analysed as Outcomes 5–12. Corresponding survey items are listed in table 4 and online supplemental file 2.

Covariates

Covariates included age (≤25 years or >25 years), gender (female or male), university degree (no or yes) and monthly income (<6 million Indonesian Rupiah (IDR) or ≥6 million IDR).

Sample size considerations

For any binary outcome measured at post-intervention, a sample of 4000 participants (800 per FOPNL group) achieved >80% power of rejecting the null hypothesis (expected proportions are the same across the five labels) under alternative hypotheses that assume different plausible scenarios with differences between groups no larger than 7 percentage points (see online supplemental file 3). Power calculations were conducted using PASS 2021 software (V.21.09), a test for multiple proportions in a one-way design, χ2 test, 4 degrees of freedom, α=0·05.28

Randomisation

Participants were randomly assigned to conditions or question orders using the survey platform’s built-in algorithm. The platform applied simple randomisation at the individual level, without restrictions such as blocking or stratification. Allocation was fully automated, and the sequence was concealed by default.

Blinding

Given that this was an online self-administered survey, participants were not informed about the randomisation or the existence of alternate survey versions. This ensured participant blinding. Researchers became aware of the post hoc randomisation during data analysis.

Statistical analyses

Participant characteristics were summarised overall and by label group, with categorical variables compared across groups using the χ2 test.

This study assessed FOPNL effectiveness across multiple food categories rather than within individual categories. While performance may vary by product type, outcomes were defined at the aggregate level to evaluate whether labels improved overall participants’ performance on the different tasks across commonly consumed foods. Effects were estimated by comparing changes in performance before and after label exposure across label types, thereby reducing the risk of conflating product-specific effects with label effectiveness.

To assess changes in objective understanding (Outcomes 1–3), mixed logistic models were fitted. Models included time (baseline (T0), post-label (T1)), label type (five levels), and their interaction as fixed effects, and participant as a random effect to account for repeated measures. For each outcome, we reported the estimated percentage of participants at T0 and T1, the within-label percentage point difference between T1 and T0, and the difference-in-differences between labels, with 95% CIs. Logistic models were fitted to compare purchase intention (Outcome 4) across label types. For perception measures (Outcomes 5–12), mixed logistic models were fitted including label type as a fixed effect and participant as a random effect. For each outcome, we reported estimated percentages and percentage point differences between label types, with 95% CIs.

Sensitivity analyses: Models for outcomes 1–4 were additionally adjusted by age (≤25 years; >25 years) to account for age imbalances at T0 in the total sample. Models for outcomes 5–12 were not adjusted by age, as age distribution was balanced within the analytic subsample. All analyses were conducted using Stata V.18.

Role of the funding source

The funding source had no involvement in the study conceptualisation; trial design; in the collection, analysis and interpretation of data; in the writing of the report; nor in the decision to submit the paper for publication.

Patient and public involvement

No patient involved.

Results

Sample characteristics

The sample comprised 4039 individuals aged 16–78 years, representing Indonesia’s major island groups and reflecting diverse socio-economic backgrounds. Slightly more than half were male (53.4%), over 25 years of age (55.5%), had no university degree (58.3%) and around two-thirds had a monthly income of <6 million IDR (68.4%). Participants’ healthy purchase intention after exposure to five FOPNLs

Across all label types, between 31.4% and 39.6% of participants reported an intention to purchase the healthiest product in at least two of the three food categories (Outcome 4). The highest proportions were observed among those exposed to MTL (39.6%; 95% CI 36.3 to 43.0) and HSR (38.9%; 95% CI 35.5 to 42.2), followed by those exposed to WL (35.6%; 95% CI 32.3 to 38.9), with no significant differences between them. Exposure to MTL showed a statistically significant advantage over both GDA and NS, while exposure to HSR was only significantly better than GDA (table 3).

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses adjusting for age (online supplemental files 4 and 5) produced results virtually identical to those presented in tables2 3, suggesting that the estimated label effects for Outcomes 1–4 were not driven by the uneven distribution of age across experimental arms.

Perceptions of the four proposed FOPNLs currently not available in Indonesia

Among the four proposed labels, NS and MTL were rated most favourably in terms of likability (Outcome 5), attractiveness (Outcome 7), usefulness (Outcome 9) and helpfulness (Outcome 10), with no significant differences between them. NS was perceived as the most acceptable (Outcome 6), followed by MTL, with 80.9% and 75.3% of participants, respectively, indicating they would like to see these labels on food packages. In terms of visibility (Outcome 8), both NS and MTL were rated highly, although MTL was perceived as significantly less visible than NS. NS was also considered the least complex (Outcome 11) and least time-consuming to understand (Outcome 12). No statistically significant differences were observed among MTL, HSR and warning label in terms of cognitive workload or processing time (table 4).

Discussion

The widespread availability and promotion of unhealthy UPPs is a key driver of rising rates of overweight, obesity and NCDs globally, including in LMICs across Asia and the Pacific.29 A critical challenge to public health, particularly in LMICs, is the absence of strong regulatory frameworks to discourage the overconsumption of these products. Many countries, including Indonesia, have yet to implement the WHO’s recommended package of cost-effective interventions for obesity and NCD prevention, such as taxes on unhealthy products, restrictions on marketing to children and mandatory interpretative FOPNL.30

Interpretative FOPNL have emerged as an effective policy tool to support healthier food choices. Evidence from several countries, including Chile, Mexico, France, the UK, Thailand and Singapore, shows that interpretative labels, including WL, NS and MTL, can improve consumer understanding, reduce purchases of less healthy products and incentivise reformulation.1131,34 Yet despite their potential, adoption remains limited globally, slowed by industry interference and weak regulatory action, particularly in Southeast Asia.14 In Indonesia, this represents a significant policy gap and a missed opportunity to address the growing burden of obesity and NCDs, including among children.

Our findings suggest that interpretative FOPNL, if implemented effectively, could help Indonesian consumers make healthier choices. Across all label types tested, participants were more likely to correctly rank products by healthiness and identify the healthiest and least healthy products when labels were present, compared with unlabelled products. Participants exposed to MTL and WL performed consistently better across all three outcome measures than those exposed to the GDA label. This aligns with global evidence indicating that interpretative schemes are more effective than numeric or non-interpretative labels, especially in settings with varied levels of nutrition literacy.18 22 35 This is particularly relevant in Indonesia, where dietary behaviours and health literacy vary widely across population groups.

Interestingly, both the MTL and WL were similarly effective in helping participants rank products by healthiness and identify the healthiest and least healthy options. This is consistent with findings from Chile and Mexico, where WL improved understanding of whether products were high in sugar, calories or other nutrients of concern.31 36 Similarly, MTL, along with other colour-coded systems like NS, has been associated with greater consumer understanding and healthier food choices across different contexts, including in a global study focused on 18 countries across Europe, the Americas and Asia,13 and in country-specific studies from India,17 the UK37 and Australia.10 Conversely, in our study, both GDA and NS improved participants’ performance relative to the pre-label condition; however, no significant differences were observed between the two label types for ranking products by healthiness or identifying the healthiest options. Notably, NS performed best when participants were asked to identify the least healthy options, outperforming GDA in this specific task. This is consistent with experimental evidence from the UK showing that colour cues are more effective at signalling less healthy products than healthier ones.37 The greater salience of red indicators in NS may therefore have enhanced participants’ ability to identify the least healthy products compared with GDA. Contextual factors may also help explain these findings. As GDA is already implemented in Indonesia, greater familiarity with this format may have reduced the relative advantage of NS in our sample. In addition, evidence from India suggests that the letter grades used in NS (A–E) can be misinterpreted by some consumers as referring to vitamins rather than overall nutritional quality,17 which may further explain the comparatively weaker performance of NS in our study.

In terms of purchase intent, MTL showed the highest percentage of participants intending to purchase the healthiest products, followed by HSR and the WL, with no statistically significant differences between the three. MTL was also the only label that performed significantly better than both GDA and NS in influencing purchase intention. NS was not significantly associated with improvements in ranking or identifying healthier products. This contrasts with findings from other countries, where NS has often performed well, although MTL has generally followed closely behind.13 18 Direct comparisons between MTL and NS are limited in the Southeast Asia region; however, experimental evidence from Singapore suggests a better performance of MTL over NS in reducing total calorie, fat and sugar intake, potentially because MTL explicitly displays nutrient-specific information, whereas NS provides only an overall summary indicator.38 If policy objectives prioritise reductions in total energy intake or specific nutrients of concern, MTL may therefore represent a more effective option.

When asked about their perceptions of the labels, participants rated NS and MTL as the most liked, most attractive and most helpful and as the easiest to spot on packaging. While MTL and WL performed best in terms of objective understanding, they were perceived as more cognitively demanding and time-consuming to interpret than NS. These findings are consistent with a recent qualitative study among urban Indonesian adolescents, which found that a traffic-light-type label was perceived as more informative, attractive and ‘believable’ than both WL and the ‘Healthier Choice’ logo implemented in the country.39 The stronger performance of traffic-light-type labels on perception indicators may also reflect greater familiarity with this label format in Indonesia, where proposals to introduce such a system have been under discussion in recent years. Our findings also echo international evidence suggesting that visual design, such as colour versus monochrome schemes, or summary versus nutrient-specific formats, can influence how well labels attract attention and improve nutritional understanding.18 Taken together, our findings suggest that MTL may offer a more favourable balance between effectiveness and ease of interpretation—an important consideration in a country as diverse as Indonesia.

International evidence suggests that the effectiveness of interpretative FOPNL varies by label design and regulatory context. In Latin America, mandatory WL policies have been associated with high industry compliance and substantial changes in the food supply and consumer behaviour. In Chile, WL implementation led to high compliance,40 sustained reductions in products classified as ‘high in’ critical nutrients and reformulation over time,41 alongside declines in purchases of high-in foods and beverages42 and reductions in children’s intake of sugars, sodium and saturated fats in school settings.43 Similar early successes have been reported in Mexico.44 By contrast, evidence for traffic-light-type labels is mixed. Short-term evaluations from the UK and Ecuador found no immediate reduction in the healthiness of food or beverage purchases following implementation,45 46 although longer-term shifts towards lower-sugar options were observed in Ecuador.45 Overall, this body of evidence highlights the importance of label format, mandatory implementation and enforcement, and time horizon when considering FOPNL reforms.

Introducing a mandatory, interpretative FOPNL in Indonesia would also support wider regulatory efforts. FOPNL not only inform consumers but can also serve as a foundation for other policy tools. For example, nutrient profiling models based on FOPNL criteria, whether adapted from existing models or developed locally, could be used to identify products to restrict in child-focused marketing or remove from school canteens.12 25 This approach has been adopted in countries such as Chile and Mexico, where WL have informed broader regulatory action, including school bans and marketing restrictions.11 47 These experiences are particularly relevant in Indonesia, where unhealthy food environments in schools,48 49 and the widespread marketing of unhealthy products to children,5 50 remain key challenges. In this context, a mandatory, interpretative FOPNL scheme could play a catalytic role in strengthening food environment policies and promoting healthier diets.

We acknowledge several limitations to our study. First, the experiment was conducted via a web-based survey rather than a real-life shopping simulation. As many factors influence food purchasing decisions, consumers’ understanding and use of FOPNL in this setting may differ from how they would respond in actual shopping environments. Similarly, the labels were tested on simplified mock-up packages, which may have amplified their visual appeal; in real-world settings, FOPNL must stand out against complex packaging designs and competing marketing cues, which may affect their visibility on the package, consumers’ perceptions and label effectiveness. Second, our study focused on assessing objective understanding of the FOPNL, rather than exploring the underlying reasons for consumers’ perceptions. As such, it does not offer insights into why certain FOPNL were more or less liked or perceived as more or less useful or difficult to understand. Future qualitative research could help explore these perceptions in greater depth, including preferences related to label placement, size and colour. Third, we removed all additional nutritional information from the mock-up packages to isolate the influence of the FOPNL—an approach aligned with previous studies. However, in real-life settings, consumers have access to more comprehensive information (eg, back-of-pack nutrition facts), which may influence how FOPNL are interpreted and used.

Conclusions

Indonesia faces a triple burden of malnutrition, with rising rates of overweight, obesity and diet-related NCDs compounding persistent undernutrition issues. A major driver of these trends is the rapid shift towards unhealthy food environments, alongside critical gaps in regulatory action. Our findings indicate that mandatory, interpretive FOPNL could contribute to addressing these challenges by supporting consumers to make more informed food choices. Among the systems tested, MTL labels emerged as the most promising option for Indonesia, demonstrating strong performance in improving objective understanding while also achieving high levels of consumer acceptability; WL were similarly effective for understanding but less well received. Evidence from countries that have successfully implemented interpretive FOPNL systems, including traffic-light and WL approaches, suggests that such policies are feasible and can be implemented at scale. Together, these findings support consideration of a mandatory MTL system as a pragmatic and effective option for Indonesia, while recognising that broader food-environment policies will be required to achieve sustained improvements in diet and health.
