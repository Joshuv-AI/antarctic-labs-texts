Emergency medicine in Ethiopia: key updated statistics and insights
Authors: Mikiyas G Teferi
Published: 2026

I graduated from medical school in Addis Ababa in 2024 into an emergency medicine system that barely existed a decade ago. Working in Ethiopia’s emergency departments has underscored that building emergency care infrastructure in a resource-limited country requires creativity, governmental commitment and acceptance that progress will be uneven and incomplete for years. One illustration of this reality occurred during periods when commercial inhaler spacers were unavailable, when clinicians locally fabricated simple spacer devices using clean plastic bottles to facilitate bronchodilator administration for patients in acute respiratory distress. Used as a temporary mitigation rather than a substitute for standard equipment, this approach illustrates how frontline teams adapt care processes when essential resources are lacking.

Ethiopia’s emergency medicine expansion was driven by necessity. Ethiopia faces one of the world’s highest injury mortality rates at 94 per 100 000 population, substantially higher than the sub-Saharan African average of 30–40 per 100 000 and more than 10 times rates in high-income countries.1 2 Road traffic injuries account for 79% of injury deaths, with an RTI fatality rate exceeding 56 deaths per 10 000 vehicles, among the highest globally.3 Simultaneously, Ethiopia’s epidemiological transition brought surging cardiovascular emergencies, strokes and diabetic complications alongside persistent infectious diseases like malaria and pneumonia.4 This dual burden overwhelmed general practitioners rotating through emergency areas, making emergency medicine a distinct specialty essential.

Building the workforce: rapid expansion from a low baseline

One of the most instructive developments has been the rapid expansion of the emergency medicine physician workforce from a very low baseline. In 2013, Ethiopia had only four trained emergency medicine specialists for over 100 million people, approximately 0.004 per 100 000 population. High-income countries typically have 12–15 emergency physicians per 100 000. Ethiopia therefore had roughly 3000 times fewer emergency physicians per capita than the USA.5 The Ministry of Health responded by dramatically expanding residency training. Between 2010 and 2024, emergency medicine programmes grew from one institution to eight, and trained specialists increased to 201 with over 200 physicians currently in training.5 6 While the current ratio of approximately 0.17 per 100 000 remains far below international standards, this represents a 40-fold increase in just over a decade. The lesson: political will and dedicated funding for graduate medical education can achieve rapid workforce expansion even in resource-constrained settings.

However, specialist physicians alone cannot staff an emergency system. Emergency nursing workforce development proved equally critical. Tertiary emergency departments typically operate with only five specialised emergency nurses (masters-level) and approximately 15 general nurses, which is far below national guidelines of 1:1 nurse-to-patient ratios in resuscitation areas and 1:3 in observation areas.7 Ethiopia does not yet have a nationally standardised or widely deployed paramedic workforce. While paramedic training initiatives and services exist in some private, urban or pilot settings, prehospital care in many areas remains limited and inconsistently staffed, particularly outside major cities. Emergency care system development therefore requires coordinated training across multiple cadres simultaneously rather than the sequential development of individual workforce groups.6 8

One model that may be relevant to other resource-constrained settings is the use of combined emergency medicine and critical care training pathways, which aim to build acute care capacity within a limited training infrastructure. In many systems, emergency medicine and critical care are developed as distinct specialties; in Ethiopia, a combined pathway has been used as a pragmatic approach to workforce constraints and service needs. However, this model also carries important trade-offs, including the risk of role dilution, variable critical care exposure across sites and challenges in maintaining depth of supervision and competency assessment as programmes scale. For these reasons, its value should be considered context dependent and accompanied by clear competency standards and ongoing evaluation.5 9 10

Daily realities: crowding and system bottlenecks

Emergency medicine practice in Ethiopia is characterised by crowding and limited resources. Ethiopian emergency departments routinely experience average lengths of stay of 3 days, with nearly 60% of patients remaining beyond 24 hours.11 For comparison, high-income countries define prolonged ED stay as exceeding 4–6 hours.12 This 3-day average is extraordinary even globally and reflects severe inpatient bed shortages. In 62% of cases, unavailability of inpatient beds was the primary cause of prolonged boarding.11 Emergency patients often wait over 24 hours for ICU admission, receiving emergency-level care in hallways rather than definitive critical care. I learnt that emergency department crowding in low-resource settings fundamentally differs from crowding in high-income countries. This realisation emerged during shifts when critically ill patients remained in the emergency department for prolonged periods due to the absence of inpatient and intensive care capacity, requiring ongoing resuscitation, monitoring and decision-making within spaces designed for rapid turnover. In this context, crowding was not driven primarily by patient volume, but by downstream system constraints that transformed the emergency department into a de facto inpatient care area. These observations suggest that crowding is not simply an isolated emergency department problem, but a manifestation of interacting constraints across the hospital and the broader health system.12 13 While limited inpatient and critical care capacity are major contributors, persistent crowding also reflects upstream pressures such as inadequate prevention, limited primary and community-based care, delayed access to outpatient services, and unmet social and safety-net needs that increase avoidable emergency care utilization. Accordingly, efforts to reduce crowding should extend beyond emergency department or inpatient expansion alone, and include system-level strategies that strengthen hospital capacity while also improving prevention, continuity of care, and community-based support.

Prehospital care: building prehospital infrastructure

Ethiopia does not yet have a fully organised prehospital emergency medical services system. In many rural areas, emergency transport often relies on taxis, private vehicles or other informal means. Government procurement of ambulances in 2018–2019 expanded the national fleet to estimated 1400–1500 vehicles, corresponding roughly to one ambulance per 70 000–80 000 people.14 While significant progress, this remains grossly inadequate compared with high-income nations. Moreover, simply providing ambulances does not create effective EMS.7 Ethiopia had no unified emergency dispatch number (fragmented between 907 for ambulances, 911 for police, with variable local systems), inconsistent radio communication, and minimal prehospital provider training. The Ministry has since developed national dispatch protocols and is piloting coordinated call centres, but as of 2025 no unified nationwide system exists.

These observations highlight that prehospital system development requires simultaneous investment in vehicles, communication infrastructure, dispatch coordination and provider training. Ethiopia’s phased approach, which initially prioritised transport capacity and subsequently focused on dispatch development with more recent efforts to introduce advanced life support training, may be pragmatic in resource-limited settings and also implies prolonged periods of suboptimal prehospital care.

Government leadership and strategic training

Government leadership has been an important enabling factor in the development of emergency care in Ethiopia. National coordination by the Ministry of Health has helped elevate emergency and critical care within broader health system priorities and has provided a framework for aligning partners, training activities and service development. Rather than relying on fragmented or ad hoc initiatives, emergency care strengthening has been informed by periodic system assessments and national planning processes supported by international partners.15

Workforce development has included the use of short-course training programmes designed to rapidly strengthen foundational emergency care competencies while longer specialist training pathways continue to mature. Approaches such as the WHO Basic Emergency Care course have been implemented through collaborative models, with national coordination by the Ministry of Health and delivery supported by implementation partners and professional stakeholders.16 17 These initiatives aim to expand basic emergency care skills across diverse clinical settings, while recognising that short courses alone cannot substitute for comprehensive specialty training required for advanced care and system leadership.

In practice, professional advocacy has complemented government-led initiatives by supporting implementation at the clinical and educational levels. The Ethiopian Society of Emergency Medicine and Critical Care has contributed through clinician education, professional networking and feedback from frontline practice, helping translate national priorities into locally feasible approaches within emergency departments.5 6 Together, these experiences underscore the importance of sustained governmental coordination alongside professional engagement, while recognising that short-course training and specialist education play distinct and necessary roles in the development of emergency care systems.

Lessons for other countries

Ethiopia’s experience offers several lessons that may be relevant for other low and middle-income countries. Nationally coordinated leadership has been important in guiding emergency care development, while workforce expansion has required attention to physicians, nurses and prehospital providers in parallel. Combined training pathways, such as emergency medicine-critical care, may represent a pragmatic approach in settings where resources and training infrastructure are constrained. Importantly, improvements within emergency departments alone have limited impact when inpatient and critical care capacity remains insufficient. Substantial challenges persist. The physician to population ratio remains low, shortages of emergency nurses and prehospital providers continue, and organised prehospital systems are still evolving. Nevertheless, progress has been sustained through continued governmental engagement. Countries facing similar burdens may find value in both Ethiopia’s advances and its ongoing constraints. Ultimately, the sustainability of emergency medicine in Ethiopia will depend less on the continued expansion of emergency departments themselves than on strengthening the inpatient and critical care capacity required to support them.
