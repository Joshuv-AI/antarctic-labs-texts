Deciphering the role of SNP variants of co-stimulatory genes in systemic lupus erythematosus: from genotype to protein expression
Authors: Ding-Ping Chen, Gem Huai-Chueh Wu, Wei-Tzu Lin, Kuang-Hui Yu
Published: 2026

However, the specific mechanisms by which these SNPs influence gene and protein expression and ultimately affect disease pathology remain unclear. Background

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a multi-organ involved autoimmune disease with strong genetic predisposition and an estimated heritability of up to 66% [1, 2]. Increasing evidence underscores the importance of cellular immunity in the pathogenesis of SLE, particularly in immune cell activation and tolerance through co-stimulatory molecules.

Co-stimulatory molecules regulate the activation of T cells and B cells and maintain immune tolerance [3]. Immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting CTLA-4/CD28/CD80/CD86 axis and PD-1/PD-L1 axis have been widely applied in oncology field and gradually demonstrated their possible application in rheumatology. CD28 is essential for the initial activation of T cells [4], while the inhibition of CTLA-4 can help prevent an excessive immune response [5]. PD-1 can inhibit the activity of T cells [6], and dysfunction of both axis in SLE has been shown to correlate with disease activity [7].

Single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the genes of co-stimulatory and co-inhibitory molecules have been extensively reported to be associated with the susceptibility and progression of SLE [8–10].

These SNPs are in the non-coding regions of the genes, such as the promoter and 3’ untranslated region (3’ UTR). Variations in these regions may influence transcriptional or translational activity, increasing or decreasing gene expression [11, 12]. We have also utilized reporter assays to investigate the effects of promoter SNPs in the CD28, CTLA4, and PDCD1 genes on transcriptional activity [13–16].

However, the specific mechanisms by which these SNPs influence gene and protein expression and ultimately affect disease pathology remain unclear. Although SNP variations with biological functions cannot directly account for final protein abundance, numerous mechanisms, such as epigenetics [17], play a role in regulating protein expression. Furthermore, the association between SNPs and diseases does not directly clarify how they contribute to disease pathogenesis [18]. To address these issues, this study first analyses the correlation between genotypes and protein expression. Subsequently, we integrate findings from genetic association studies, functional analyses, and protein expression data. This multi-level approach offers a deeper understanding of how genetic variations lead to immune dysregulation in SLE.

Methods

Patients

The study included 38 SLE outpatients in separate groups, along with 34 healthy controls. The diagnosis of SLE was established by clinicians based on the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR)/American College of Rheumatology (ACR) 2019 criteria [19]. Among the SLE patients, 34 were female and 4 were male, with a mean age of onset of 34.3 ± 12.2 years. Among the patients, 18 were identified as severe SLE and 16 as mild SLE. Patients who have received immunosuppressant therapy such as azathioprine (AZA), cyclophosphamide (CYC), or mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) are classified as having severe systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). Conversely, patients who have not received such treatment are considered to have mild SLE. The control group consisted of 31 females and 3 males, with a mean age of 28.7 ± 8.8 years.

Polymorphism genotyping

DNA was obtained from 200 µL of whole blood using the QIAamp® DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen GmbH, Hilden, Germany). We use direct sequencing method on the promoter region of the CD28, CTLA4, and PDCD1 genes, as well as their research hotspots, including rs11571315 T > C, rs733618 T > C, rs4553808 A > G, rs11571316 G > A, rs62182595 G > A, rs16840252 C > T, rs5742909 C > T, rs231775 A > G, rs3087243 G > A, rs11571319 G > A, rs1879877 G > T, rs3181096 C > T, rs3181097 G > A, rs3181098 G > A, rs3116496 T > C, rs5839828 G > del, rs36084323 C > T, rs41386349 G > A, rs6705653 T > C, rs2227982 G > A, and rs2227981 A > G, rs10204525 C > T (Supplementary Table 1). The primers and PCR program used to amplify the region covering these SNPs are shown in Supplementary Table 2. Due to technical issues such as DNA purity or PCR specificity, sequencing noise occurred, and some genotypes could not be clearly determined, resulting in missing genotype data in certain cases. The genotype data used in this article are from [8], and all control groups were in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium.

Membrane protein expression level

Before collecting data, fluorescence compensation was conducted using single-stained samples. Following this, cells were labelled with anti-CD3 and anti-CD4 antibodies. CD3⁺CD4⁺ T cells were identified by gating the double-positive cell populations, and a consistent gating template was applied across all samples. The peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from whole blood were isolated through Ficoll-Paque PLUS (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA). Then, transfer 5 × 10^5 to 1 × 10^6 unstimulated and stimulated cells into flow cytometry tubes. Add 20 µL of anti-CD3_FITC (IM1281U, Beckman), 10 µL of anti-CD4_APC-Alexa Fluor 750 (A94685, Beckman), 10 µL of anti-CD28_PC7 (B23313, Beckman), 10 µL of anti-CD279 (PD-1) APC (B30633, Beckman), and 20 µL of anti-CD152_PE (IM2282, Beckman). Vortex the mixture for 3–5 s, then incubate in the dark at room temperature for 15–30 min to allow for staining. Add 3 mL of PBS, vortex for 3–5 s, and centrifuge to remove any unbound antibodies. Resuspend the cells in 300 µL of PBS for analysis using the Navios EX flow cytometer (Beckman). Perform fluorescence compensation using single-stained samples for calibration before analysis. We analysed 10,000 cells per sample. The expression levels are reported as median fluorescence intensity (MFI). Histograms are utilized to display the expression of individual proteins, while dot plots are employed to assess expression levels in positive cells. The percentages of CD3+ CD4+ cells that are positive for each co-stimulatory or co-inhibitory molecule within the population are also indicated. The expression level of positive cells was assessed using the fluorescence intensity (x-median) within the target gate on the dot plot. The percentage of positive cells is determined by the value in the upper right quadrant of the left dot plot. The expression of a single protein is evaluated using the fluorescence intensity (x-median) on the histogram (Fig. 1). Fluorescence intensity was expressed as MFI without additional normalization or transformation, as all samples were processed and acquired under identical instrument settings. Single-stained controls were used to perform fluorescence compensation. A uniform standard was maintained across all samples, and the analysis was conducted using an identical template for all to minimize variability caused by the operator.

Assessment of protein expression and positive cell percentage by flow cytometry. Percentage of positive cells was determined from the upper right quadrant of the left dot plot. Expression of the target protein was assessed by the fluorescence intensity (x-median) on the corresponding histogram

Cell culture

To assess CTLA-4 and PD-1 expression, PBMCs need to be activated. The samples were thawed in a 37 °C water bath, and 5 × 10^5 cells were cultured in a 96-well plate with 200 µL of RPMI 1640 medium, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution, and 2 mM L-glutamine. Phytohemagglutinin (PHA)-L (2 µg/mL), interleukin-7 (IL-7) (1 µg/mL), and interleukin-2 (IL-2) (50 IU/mL) were added to stimulate the cells, and the plate was incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 48 h. After incubation, the cells were collected for further analysis [20, 21].

Statistics

Flow data collection is based on 50,000 cells or 10,000 lymphocytes, and samples containing fewer than 500 CD3+CD4+ cells are excluded from the analysis. We analysed the expression levels (exp: expression) and the percentage of positive cells (per: percentage) for CD28, CTLA-4, and PD-1 in CD3+CD4+ cell populations. Data for each variable were initially processed to eliminate outliers using the interquartile range (IQR) method (values below Q1 − 1.5 × IQR or above Q3 + 1.5 × IQR were excluded). Statistical analyses were conducted after excluding outliers to reduce the influence of extreme values. Then, the Mann-Whitney U-test was employed for pairwise comparisons between the two genotypes. Due to multiple comparisons among three genotypes (AA, Aa, and aa), the significance threshold was set at p < 0.05/3 = 0.017. This adjustment helps control the Type I error rate when performing multiple statistical tests, minimizing the probability of detecting a false positive.

Results

Association between CD28 genotypes and CD28 protein expression

When incorporating data from healthy controls and SLE, the analysis showed no significant variations in cell percentages or protein expression for CD28 SNPs. However, only in SLE patients (Table 1), a statistical association was identified between the CD28 rs1879877 genotypes and the expression levels of CD3+CD28+ cells within CD3+CD4+ cells. Individuals with the GT genotype had 17.3% higher CD3+CD28+ mean expression level than those with the GG genotype (p = 0.017).

There is no haplotype block in CD28 and CTLA4 SNPs (Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7), but there was a haplotype block in the PDCD1 gene, in which rs2227982, rs6705653, and rs41386349 form a strong LD group, which can be regarded as representative markers (tag SNPs) of the same genetic segment. Discussion

We analysed the association between CD28, CTLA-4, and PD-1 expression level and its cell percentage in CD3 + CD4+ cells from 38 SLE patients and 34 healthy controls. The allele frequencies of the selected SNPs in SLE patients and controls from the same population have been previously reported in our earlier study [8]. Following the methodology described by Ligers et al. [22], we conducted genotype–protein expression correlation analyses using two distinct approaches: one focusing solely on SLE patients and the other including both SLE patients and healthy controls. Analysing only SLE patients highlights disease-specific immune characteristics and the potential amplification of genotype effects, while the combined analysis offers a broader perspective on general regulatory effects and cell subset distribution. At last, the data were integrated with our previous functional analyses of SNPs to elucidate the role of co-stimulatory gene variants in SLE.

Among the CD28 genotypes, the rs1879877 variant was found to be associated with CD3⁺CD28⁺ expression levels in CD3⁺CD4⁺ T cells exclusively in SLE patients. The TT and GT genotypes showed higher expression levels than GG. Previous study has shown that CD4⁺CD28⁺KIR⁺CD11a^hi T cells were expanded in SLE and exhibit pro-inflammatory characteristics [23, 24]. These findings suggest a potential link between CD28-related genetic variation and pro-inflammatory T cell subsets, although further investigation is required. In addition, factors such as chronic inflammation, epigenetic reprogramming, and altered transcription factor activity in SLE may amplify the effects of these genotypes [25].

The rs733618 SNP showed a significant association with CD3+CTLA4+ cell percentage in SLE-only and combined groups. The association between rs733618 and SLE has been widely published [8, 26]. This SNP has previously been linked to decreased soluble CTLA-4 levels [27], decreased promoter activity [15], and altered transcription factor binding [28]. Our data are consistent with these observations and suggest that rs733618 may be associated with variation in CTLA4 transcription and T-cell activity.

Additionally, the rs16840252 in the 5’ region of the CTLA4 was associated with the expression of CTLA-4 in CD3⁺CD4⁺ T cells within the combined dataset. Its location in a regulatory region supports its potential to modulate transcription factor binding and promoter activity [29]. Furthermore, its association with other immune-related diseases and cancers [30] suggests that it may exert stable regulatory effects beyond SLE.

The PDCD1 SNP rs2227982 was consistently associated with PD-1 protein expression and CD3⁺PD1⁺ cell proportions in SLE and combined groups, even after stimulation. The CC genotype showed higher expression levels. As a nonsynonymous SNP (Val215Ala), rs2227982 may alter the structure and function of PD-1 [31], which may be related to variation in immunosuppressive mechanisms or affecting T-cell responses [32, 33].

Similarly, rs36084323 was associated with PD-1 expression in SLE and combined groups, even after stimulation. This SNP has been linked to reduced transcriptional activity [16] and increased SLE risk [8]. The weaker association in SLE may reflect interference from disease-related epigenetic changes [25]. We also observed no linkage between rs36084323 and rs5839828 in our dataset, suggesting that rs36084323 may contribute independently to variation in PD-1 expression.

However, rs5839828 was associated with PD-1 expression in the resting state but not after activation, suggesting that its regulatory effect is limited to non-activated immune contexts, possibly through the basal transcription machinery [25, 34].

Epigenetic mechanisms under disease conditions may alter gene expression and affect the regulatory impact of SNPs [35–37]. rs1879877 showed a disease-specific effect, while rs733618, rs36084323, and rs2227982 exhibited stable effects across both groups, indicating context-dependent SNP-gene interactions. Since CTLA-4, CD28, and PD-1 are central immune checkpoint molecules, variants in these genes are linked to various immune-related conditions and responses to immunotherapy [9, 30, 38], rather than being specific to SLE. Further investigation at the single-cell level (e.g., RNA-seq, ATAC-seq) may help elucidate their cell-type-specific functions.

We also incorporated previous findings (see Supplementary Table 4), including associations between SLE [8], SLE and protein expression [39], and the functional effect of the SNPs [13–16]. Most SNPs affecting protein expression did not directly associate with SLE susceptibility, as also reported in other studies [40–42], suggesting that protein regulation involves complex mechanisms beyond single SNP effects, such as epigenetic or post-transcriptional modifications [43, 44]. Additionally, we found that rs733618 and rs36084323 are not only associated with SLE risk but also possess functional regulatory potential. These SNPs influence promoter activity, and their genotypes are significantly associated with protein expression levels and the proportions of specific cell subsets. This suggests that they may contribute to disease development and immune regulation by altering gene expression.

This study has several limitations. Multiple comparisons across different SNPs and protein expression parameters were still performed, which may increase the likelihood of false-positive findings. However, applying a very strict correction to every comparison could cause us to miss truly meaningful results. In addition, the outcomes may be specific to a particular demographic and could result from undetected gene-environment interactions or epigenetic factors. The cross-sectional design imposes limitations on causal inferences, and the disease microenvironment including disease activity measures and clinical data may influence the relationship between genotype and gene expression. Further research should consider these factors, including lifestyle, infections, medications, and disease stage. Flow cytometry analyses were carried out with an adequate number of cells per sample to ensure reliable measurements. However, statistical comparisons were made on an individual basis. As a result, findings derived from smaller subgroups should be interpreted cautiously, and validation in larger cohorts remains necessary. Additionally, analyses based on genetic models, such as dominant, recessive, or additive models, were not conducted. Instead, direct comparisons among genotype groups were used to evaluate their associations with co-stimulatory molecule expression levels and cell proportions. While this method helps uncover genotype-specific effects, it may miss inheritance patterns that influence these genetic associations. Future research employing diverse genetic models is essential for a more comprehensive understanding of these relationships.

Conclusion

This study reveals that SNPs in CTLA4 and PDCD1 exhibit general regulatory effects on protein expression, while the effect of CD28 rs1879877 is specifically amplified in SLE. Notably, rs733618 and rs36084323 not only associate with SLE risk but also contribute to disease pathogenesis and immune regulation by modulating gene expression. Our findings provide valuable insights into genotype-protein associations; however, these results should be treated with caution when analyzing small subgroups and need to be validated through studies of larger cohorts.
