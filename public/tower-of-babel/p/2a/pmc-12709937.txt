Chemical composition and neuroprotective activity of hemp seed aqueous extract and their chemotaxonomic significance
Authors: Siqing Bu, Jianbo Ji, Mei Yang, Jieyue Sun, Jiaozhen Zhang, Shunmeng Qian, Hongxiang Lou, Peihong Fan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractFructus Cannabis (hemp seed) is important in food and traditional Chinese medicinal applications. Several studies have shown it has antioxidant, antiaging, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective properties. Studies have reported its anti-Alzheimer's disease effects. However, its active substances have not been defined, and little is known about the chemical constituents of the aqueous extract. The chemical profile of the aqueous extract of Fructus Cannabis (EFC) was obtained via isolation, structural identification, and qualitative and quantitative analyses. Twenty-seven compounds were identified, including seven nucleosides (1–7), five phenylpropanamides (8–11, and 24), three alkaloids (15, 16, and 26), two cyclic dipeptides (17 and 25), and one pyrimidine (19). Compounds 1, 3–7, 12, 14–19, and 21–27 were not reported previously in the Cannabis genus. Therefore, their chemotaxonomic significance is discussed. Neuroprotective activity screening revealed that EFC and the isolated compounds, particularly 9, 11, and 17, showed significant neuroprotective effects in PC12 ​cells (rat pheochromocytoma cells). The novel object recognition experiment and Nissl staining showed that EFC improved cognitive impairment in APP/PS1 mice and that EFC intervention reduced the number of senile plaques. These findings will contribute to the utility of Fructus Cannabis.Keywords: Hemp seed, Aqueous extract, Cyclic dipeptides, Neuroprotective effects, Chemotaxonomic significance, APP/PS1 AD model mice

Introduction

The genus Cannabis, an essential member of the Moraceae family, comprises two subspecies and four varieties worldwide. The two subspecies are C. sativa subsp. sativa (L.) and C. sativa subsp. indica (Lam.). Fructus cannabis (Hemp seed) is the dry and mature fruit of Cannabis Sativa L., widely distributed in Heilongjiang, Gansu, Yunnan, Guangxi, and other regions of China. In China, Fructus cannabis has a history of at least 3000 years as a food and traditional Chinese medicine [1]. “Hemp seed longevity soup” is a recipe of the Bama people in Guangxi with a history of thousands of years. Hemp seeds have attracted much attention because of their nutritional value as oil, proteins, fiber, vitamins, and minerals [2,3].

Hemp seed is also an essential component of traditional medicine. In the Chinese Pharmacopoeia, hemp seeds are prescribed for treating constipation; their extracts have antioxidant, anti-aging, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective properties [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a devastating neurodegenerative disorder clinically characterized by amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles (NFT), leading to progressive cognitive impairment and memory loss. Publications have provided evidence for the potential of extracts of Fructus Cannabis to improve the learning and memory ability of aging model rats. Lin et al. [8] reported that the petroleum ether, ethyl acetate, n-butanol, and water extracts reduced Aβ1-40 protein accumulation in established AD mice models after intraperitoneal injection of D-galactose and sodium nitrite.Chen Ningyuan et al. [9] found that the extract of Fructus Cannabis activated the SIRT1-ADAM10-APP and SIRT1-YY1-CREB-BDNF signaling pathways by up-regulating the expression of SIRT1 in a D-galactose-induced aging model. Other anti-aging mechanisms were also revealed, including suppressing the activation of astrocytes and the NF-κB signaling pathway, reducing the oxidative stress response, down-regulating the mRNA expression of APP, Tau, and PS1 genes in rat hippocampus, the phosphorylation of Tau protein, and the expression level of PS1. These studies indicate that hemp seed aqueous extract has potential applications in improving learning and memory ability and delaying aging.

In addition to oils and proteins, more than 200 small molecules, including various lignanamides, have been isolated from hemp seeds [7]. Lignanamides are mainly isolated from hemp seed extracts of medium polarity (such as the ethyl acetate fraction) and have biological activities such as anti-inflammatory and antioxidative [10]. However, little is known about the chemical compositions of aqueous extracts most commonly used in food, traditional medicine, and experimental research.

To better understand their chemical composition and pharmacological substances, a systematic phytochemical study was conducted on the aqueous extract of Fructus Cannabis (hemp seed) (EFC). The chemotaxonomic significance of the identified compounds was discussed, and the chemical profile of the extract was determined using isolated compounds as a reference. The neuroprotective activity of the isolated compounds was evaluated, and the neuroprotective effect of EFC was assessed in APP/PS1 mice as an AD model.

Instruments

NMR spectra (1H, 13C, HSQC, HMBC) were generated using a 600 ​MHz DD2 spectrometer (Bruker, Switzerland). Methanol-d4 (CD3OD) and dimethyl-d6 sulfoxide were used as analytical solvents (Sigma-Aldrich). The ESI-MS and MS/MS spectra were recorded using an LTQ-Orbitrap XL high-resolution mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Co.). HPLC analyses were performed with a 1260 series quaternary pump equipped with a DAD detector (Agilent Technologies), using a C18 column (250 ​mm ​× ​10 ​mm i.d., YMC-Pack ODS-AS-5 μm) with a guard column (4 ​mm ​× ​4 ​mm i.d.) of the same material for semi-preparation (the flow rate was 1.5 ​mL/min) and the C18 column (250 ​mm ​× ​4.6 ​mm i.d., 5 ​μm, ODS-A-HG C18) with a guard column (4 ​mm ​× ​4 ​mm i.d.) of the same material for analysis (the flow rate was 0.8 ​mL/min). Microplate analysis was performed using a BioTek multimode microplate reader (Biotek, USA).

Animals

The study used 36 27-week-old male SPF-level APP/PS1 double transgenic mice,and 12 wild-type C57BL/6J 27-week-old male mice of the same genetic background; the license number is scxk (Beijing) 2019–0008. All the mice were obtained from Beijing Huafukang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All animal studies were performed strictly with the Chinese Society of Laboratory Animal Sciences guidelines to minimize animal suffering, and approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethical and Welfare Committee of Shandong University Cheeloo College of Medicine (Approval No. 2000-6).

Extraction, fraction, and isolation

For the chemical constituent analysis, air-dried hemp seeds (19 ​kg) were crushed, soaked in purified water for 12 ​h, and extracted three times (1 ​h each) using boiling water. The extract (340 ​L) was vacuum-concentrated to 500 ​mL and defatted four times with a petroleum ether 1:1 extraction. Finally, the water layer was concentrated to obtain 625 ​g of extract. A portion of the crude extract (225 ​g) was suspended in water and processed with a column of D101 macroporous adsorption resin (1 ​kg). Seven fractions (Fr1–Fr7) were obtained by elution with water, 10%, 30%, 50%, 75%, and 95% ethanol. Among them, two fractions (Fr1–Fr2) were obtained by washing with water, and Fr1 was discarded. Qualitative and quantitative analysis of EFC using HPLC

Preparation of sample solutions for HPLC analysis

EFC solution. Hemp seeds (1 ​kg) were subjected to extraction twice with boiling water, and the extraction solution was collected and concentrated. The dried extract (100 ​mg) was dissolved in 4 ​mL 50 ​% MeOH aqueous solution and filtrated through a 0.22 ​μm filtration membrane.

Standard HPLC solutions for content determination. The uracil (19) and adenosine (2) powders were accurately weighed and dissolved in 10 ​mL volumetric bottles to obtain the standard reserve solution of uracil and adenosine (142 ​μg/mL and 131 ​μg/mL, respectively). The purity was analyzed by HPLC and found to be 99.08% and 98.64%, respectively. The standard reserve solutions (1 ​mL) of uracil and adenosine were taken and carefully diluted with 50 ​% methanol to obtain seven standard solutions.

HPLC detection conditions

Gradient elution was performed using H2O (A)–MeOH (B) as mobile phase at 0 ​min, 0% B; 30 ​min, 30% B; 45 ​min, 50% B; 50 ​min, 100% B. The column temperature was 25 ​°C, and the flow rate was 0.8 ​mL/min. The detection wavelength was 254 ​nm. The injection volume was 10 ​μL.

Methodological verification

Linearity, limit of detection (LOD), and limit of quantification (LOQ)

Seven standard solutions of different concentrations were used for detection under set chromatographic conditions. The concentration (X) was taken as the abscissa, and the corresponding peak area (Y) was used as the ordinate to draw the regression curve and calculate the regression equation. The limits of quantification (S/N ​= ​10) and detection (S/N ​= ​3) of the components were also determined.

Precision, repeatability, and stability

Standard solutions were prepared. For intra-day precision, six consecutive injections within the same day were performed under set conditions. For inter-day precision, injections were administered once a day for six days. The peak areas of uracil and adenosine were calculated. The RSD of the peak area of the uracil and adenosine were measured and calculated. For repeatability, six samples of the same test solution were prepared and tested under set conditions, and the peak areas and RSD of uracil and adenosine were measured and calculated. For stability, the test solution was injected at 0, 6, 12, 24, 36, and 48 ​h, and the RSD of the peak areas of uracil and adenosine were measured and calculated.

Investigation of the recovery rate

Nine samples (∼100 ​mg) of EFC were weighed, and 4 ​mL 50 ​% MeOH–H2O were added for ultrasonic dissolution. High, medium, and low concentrations of uracil and adenosine reference solutions were added, and each concentration was injected in parallel three times. The peak area was measured, and the recovery rate and RSD were calculated.

Content determination

Six portions of EFC (each 100 ​mg) were weighed precisely and dissolved in 4 ​mL of 50% MeOH via ultrasonic dissolution. The peak areas of uracil and adenosine contents were recorded and calculated under set conditions.

Neuroprotective potential assay

Cell viability

Glu (glutamic acid)-injured PC12 ​cells were established and used in this study. The cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 ​× ​106 ​cells/mL in three parallel wells per group and cultured for 24 ​h. Subsequently, in the control and model groups, only fresh complete DMEM without the drug was exchanged. The medium containing the compounds at specific concentrations was added to the PC12 ​cells in the drug groups. Glu (12 ​mM) was added to PC12 ​cells, except for the control group. After 24 h-culture, 10 ​μL of MTT solution was added to each well; 96-well plates were kept in an incubator at 37 ​°C, 5% CO2 for 4 ​h. Then, the supernatant was removed, 100 ​μL of DMSO was added to each well, and the absorbance of each well was measured at 570 ​nm using a microplate analysis system. Cell viability = ((ATreated - ABlank)/(AControl - ABlank)) ​× ​100%.

Apoptosis

The cells were cultured as described in section 2.6.1. After culturing for 24 ​h, trypsin-digested cells without EDTA were collected by centrifugation at 72 rcf, stained with the annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit for 15 ​min, and centrifuged. The supernatant was discarded, and the cells were suspended in PBS for flow cytometry analysis.

In vivo study

Grouping of animals and administration of EFC

After five days of adaptation, 36 mice were randomly divided into three groups: 12 in the EFC low-dose group (EFC-L), 12 in the EFC high-dose group (EFC-H), and 12 in the model blank control group (Tg). A wild-aged control group (WT.8m) was established. EFC-L (0.6 ​g/kg/d) and EFC-H (1.2 ​g/kg/d) were intragastric administered for six weeks. The blank and wild control groups were given a blank solvent, 1% sodium carboxymethyl cellulose. The mice were weighed every two days during the study.

New object recognition experiment

Novel object recognition test evaluates cognitive function; the memory of the tested animals is evaluated according to the length of time spent exploring familiar objects they have seen and new objects that they have not seen [11]. The test environment was kept quiet, the room temperature constant, and the light uniform. Before and after the mice were tested, the test box and objects were scrubbed with 70% ethanol to prevent odor interference.

The experimental process, lasting three days, was divided into habituation, training, and testing periods. The first day was the habituation period; no object was in the box, and the mice were placed in the middle of the behavior box in the same position and allowed to explore freely for 5 ​min. The second day was the training period; two identical brown cylinders with a height of five cm and diameter of 2.8 ​cm were placed at one end of the box. The center of each cylinder was eight cm away from the two sidewalls of the box. The mice were placed in a box and allowed to explore freely for 10 ​min. On the third day, one of the cylinders was replaced with a red cube three cm wide and five cm high. The two objects' exploration times TN (time of new object) and TF (time of familiar object) were recorded. An Xeye Aba (Xinruan, Shanghai, China) monitoring system was used for identification. The discrimination index (DI) and recognition index (RI) of the mouse's ability to recognize objects were used as detection indicators and calculated using the formulas DI = ((TN - TF)/(TN ​+ ​TF)) ​× ​100% and RI = (TN/(TN ​+ ​TF)) ​× ​100%.

Nissl dyeing

Nissl staining was applied following standard procedure: washed twice with double distilled water, 2 ​min each time; dyed with Nissl's stain solution for 5 ​min, washed twice with distilled water for several seconds each time; soaked in 95% ethanol for 5 ​s, washed twice with 70% ethanol, and dehydrated by soaking in 95% ethanol two times for 2 ​min each time; then made transparent by soaking in xylene two times, 5 ​min each time, and applying neutral gum to seal the slides. Images were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope.

Thioflavin-S staining

Senile plaques were detected using thioflavin S staining following standard procedure: incubated with 0.3% thioflavin-S in 50% alcohol, filtered and incubated at room temperature for 8 ​min; washed in PBS (pH 7.4) for 5 ​min three times, dried slightly, DAPI dye solution drop added, and incubated at room temperature for 10 ​min in the dark; washed in PBS (pH 7.4) for 5 ​min three times, slightly shaken to dry, and sealed with anti-fluorescence quench. Photographs were taken under an inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Statistical analysis

The significance of the differences between means was analyzed using a two-sided t-test. Sample sizes are indicated in the figures or figure legends. The plotted mean was calculated using the GraphPad software. Data were shown as mean ​± ​SD. P-values below 0.05 are marked as ∗; below 0.01 are marked as ∗∗; below 0.001 are marked as ∗∗∗; and below 0.0001 are marked as ∗∗∗∗; ns indicates no significant difference.

Results

Isolation and identification of chemical constituents of EFC

Although the aqueous extract of Fructus cannabis is often used in food and traditional medicine, little is known about its chemical constituents except for oils and proteins. In this study, we first separated the aqueous extract of Fructus Cannabis (EFC) using various chromatographic methods. Twenty-seven isolated compounds (structures shown in Fig. 2) were identified through 1H and 13C NMR spectral analyses and compared with data reported in the literature. The identified compounds were seven nucleosides: 2′-O-methyluridine (1) [12], adenosine (2) [13], thymidine (3) [14], 2′-O-methyladenosine (4) [15], 2′-O-methyl guanosine (5) [16], guanosine (6) and N, N-dimethyl-guanosine (7) [17]; five phenylpropanamides: 4-[(E)-p-coumaroylamino]butan-1-ol (8) [18], N-trans-coumaroyltyramine (9) [19], N-trans-caffeoyltyramine (10) [20], N-trans-feryroyltyramine (11) [21], and (E)-N-(2-hydroxybutyl)-3-(4-hydroxyphenyl) (24); three organic acids: dihydroferulic acid (12) [22,23], vanillic acid (13) [24], and phenylalanine (14) [25]; three alkaloids: 1-methyl-1,2,3,4-tetrahydro-β-carboline-3-carboxylic acid (15) [20], orychophragines-A (16) [26], and perlolyrine (26) [27,28]; two cyclic dipeptides: cyclo(Phe-Ser) (17) [29] and cyclo(Tyr-IIe) (25) [30]; one pyrimidine: uracil (19) [31]; one amino acid: tryptophan (18) [32]; and five other classes of compounds: p-hydroxybenzaldehyde (20) [33], p-cresol (21) [34], butyl 1H-imidazole 4-carboxylate (22) [35], 1-(β-D-ribofuranosyl)-1H-1,2,4-triazone (23) [36], 2-(furan-2-yl)-5-(2,3,4-trihydroxybutyl)pyrazine (27a), and 2-(furan-2-yl)-6-(2,3,4-trihydroxybutyl)pyrazine (27b) [37,38]. 27a and 27b were isolated and identified as isomeric mixtures (27).

Structures of compounds isolated and identified in hemp seed.

Among these 27 compounds isolated and identified, 20 compounds that were first isolated from hemp seed involved various types, including nucleosides (1–7), phenylpropanamides (8–11, 24), alkaloids (15, 16, 26), cyclic dipeptides (17 and 25), pyrimidine (19), amino acid (18), and organic acids (12–14), of which cyclic dipeptides, pyrimidine, and amino acid were reported for the first time from Cannabis genus.

Qualitative and quantitative analysis of EFC using HPLC

The chemical profile of the EFC was qualitatively and quantitatively analyzed using HPLC (Fig. 3). Seven known compounds 2′-O-methyluridine (1) (Rt ​= ​19.20 ​min), adenosine (2) (Rt ​= ​24.39 ​min), 2′-O-methyladenosine (4) (Rt ​= ​28.74 ​min), 2′-O-methyguanosine (5) (Rt ​= ​21.73 ​min), guanosine (6) (Rt ​= ​17.69 ​min), tryptophan (18) (Rt ​= ​23.47 ​min), and uracil (19) (Rt ​= ​8.29 ​min) were identified on-line by comparing Rt and UV spectra with isolated references.

HPLC chromatograph profile of EFC and peak identification using UV and Rt (A. HPLC chromatograph and UV spectra of reference compounds detected by DAD detector; B. HPLC chromatograph of EFC).

The profile revealed that nucleosides and pyrimidines were abundant in the EFC. Uracil (19) and adenosine (2) were selected as standards, and HPLC quantitatively analyzed EFC. We first verified the HPLC method. At S/N ​= ​3, the concentrations of uracil and adenosine were 25.92 ​ng/mL and 9.57 ​ng/mL, respectively. When S/N ​= ​10, the concentrations of uracil and adenosine were 86.4 ​ng/mL and 31.92 ​ng/mL, respectively. The RSD of uracil and adenosine in the intra- and inter-day precision (Table S1) was less than 1% RSD of repeatability (Table S2), and stability (Table S3) was less than 1%. The linear regression analysis (Table S4, Fig. S1) showed that the linear range of uracil was 2.22–142.00 ​μg/mL, and that of adenosine was 2.05–131.00 ​μg/mL. A good linear relationship existed between the concentration and peak area in the linear range. The high, medium, and low-level recovery rates of uracil were between 101.90 and 104.27%, while the high, medium, and low-level recovery rates of adenosine were between 100.76 and 101.74%, with RSD less than 2%. Finally, the contents of uracil and adenosine in EFC were determined as 1.30 ​mg/g and 2.04 ​mg/g, respectively.

Neuroprotective activity of EFC and the isolated compounds

Glutamic acid (GLU) treatment induces apoptosis and autophagy in PC12 ​cells [39]. Here, the neuroprotective activity of EFC and its components was explored in a PC12 ​cell model of Glu-induced injury. Firstly, Glu at 12 ​mM concentration was verified as a suitable damage level (Fig. S2). GM1, a glycosphingolipid (GSL), was the positive neuroprotective control. The cell viability of PC12 was significantly reduced after treatment with 12 ​mM Glu, while treatment with 100 ​μM GM1 attenuated the damage caused by Glu. EFC (0.125, 0.25, and 0.5 ​mg/mL) showed a protective effect (Fig. 4A). The protective effects of some of the compounds isolated from EFC were evaluated. Compounds 11 ​at 1.11–10 ​μM, 9 ​at 5–20 ​μM, and 17 ​at 5–10 ​μM showed the most potent protective effects comparable to those of GM1 at 100 ​μM (Fig. 4B–D). As shown in Fig. 5, Glu treatment significantly induced apoptosis in PC12 ​cells. GM1 positive control was used to attenuate apoptosis. Compounds 11 and 17 reduced cell apoptosis in the injured model, especially compound 11, attenuating apoptosis significantly at 1.1 ​μM, 3.3 ​μM, and 10 ​μM (Fig. 5A and B). These findings revealed the neuroprotective potential of EFC.

New object recognition experiment results

To explore the activity of EFC in vivo, APP/PS1 mice were used as an AD model. The initial evaluation used behavioral and histological experiments.

Compared to the traditional water maze experiment, the most significant advantage of this behavioral experiment is that it requires less physical strength. If a mouse recognizes a familiar object, it will tend to preferentially explore new objects, expressed as a “recognition index.” As shown in Fig. 6A, the mice's new object recognition index in the model blank group (Tg) was significantly reduced compared to the wild-type group. Compared to the Tg group, the different dose administration groups significantly increased the recognition index of the model mice (P ​< ​0.05), and the low-dose administration group significantly increased the cognitive index of the model mice. The low-dose group showed the most significant difference (P ​< ​0.01). The results showed that EFC enhanced the recognition and memory of new objects in APP/PS1 mice.

Nissl staining and thioflavin-S results

Nissl bodies reflect the functional activity of the nerve cells; we stained Nissl bodies to detect the number and distribution of Nissl bodies in the hippocampus to determine whether EFC improves the functional activity of damaged neurons. As shown in Fig. 6B, the nerve cells of the wild mice (WT.8m) were neatly arranged, the intracellular Nissl bodies were densely arranged and large, and the cytoplasm was abundant and patchy. The neuronal cells in the hippocampus of mice in the Tg group were loosely arranged, lightly stained, irregular in shape, and inconspicuous in nucleoli. The morphology and staining depth of hippocampal cells in the EFC administration group were similar to those in the wild mice after Nissl body staining, indicating that EFC increased the number of Nissl bodies in the hippocampus of AD model mice. Aβ was labeled with thioflavin-S and showed green fluorescence (Fig. 6C). Compared with the wild-type group (WT.8m), the blank model group (Tg) showed prominent senile plaques. The number of senile plaques decreased significantly after the EFC intervention.

Discussion

Because most of the compounds we isolated were found in cannabis for the first time, we discuss the chemotaxonomic significance of the compounds in the genus cannabis. Among the three types of nucleosides (1–7) from hemp seeds, compounds 1 and 3 have been isolated from several plants, including the marine drugs Sargassum pallidum, Sargassceae [40], Phyllostachys edulis, and Gramineae [41]. Compounds 2 and 4 have been previously reported in Capparis spinosa, Capparaceae [42], Cordyceps sinensis, Hepialidae [43] and many other plants. Compounds 5–7 have been identified in several plant species, including Stellaria media, Caryophyllaceae [44] Panax Panaxnotoginseng, Araliacea, Carthamus tinctoriuse [17]. Thus, compounds 1–7 are broadly distributed in various plants, including the genus Cannabis, and are not characteristic of the genus. According to the literature, phenylpropanamides is hemp seed's most widely reported component. Compounds 8–11 and 24 were obtained from Cannabis [10] in our laboratory. Thus, phenylpropanamides may be helpful taxonomic markers for Cannabis. Compounds 12, 14, 18, 19, and 21 were isolated from the Cannabis genus for the first time; however, compounds 12–14 and 18–21 are common in many plants and are, therefore, not considered valuable markers to support the taxonomic classification of Cannabis. Among the three alkaloids, compound 15 was previously isolated from the Starfish Asterias rollestoni Asteriidae [16]. This is the first report of compound 15 being isolated from the family Moraceae. Therefore, compound 15 can be considered a specific marker of Moraceae. Compound 16 has been previously isolated from Orychophragmus violaceus, Brassicaceae [26]. Compound 26 has been identified in Tribulus terrestris L and Zygophyllaceae [28]. Compounds 15, 16, and 26 provide new information on the chemistry of Moraceae. Compounds 22 and 23 were found in Peganum nigellastrum, Zygophyllaceae [35], Glyptocidaris crenularis, and Phymosomatidae [45]. Interestingly, compounds 22 and 23 were only isolated from hemp seeds in the Moraceae family and thus appeared to be characteristic compounds of hemp seeds identified from other plants in the Moraceae family. Two cyclic dipeptides, 17 and 25, commonly found in highly polar plants, were isolated from hemp seeds for the first time but not from other plants of the Cannabis genus and Moraceae families. Hence, the two cyclic dipeptides may serve as chemotaxonomic markers for the Cannabis and Moraceae families. The discovery of these two cyclic dipeptides provides new information on the chemotaxonomy of Moraceae.

EFC and isolated compounds (9 (N-trans-coumaroyltyramine), 11 (N-trans-feryroyltyramine), and 17 (cyclo (Phe-Ser)) were found to have neuroprotective activity (MTT assay) in the PC12 ​cell injury model at 0.125–0.5 ​mg/mL. The neuroprotective activity (apoptosis assay) of EFC and compounds 11 and 17 attenuated the apoptosis induced by Glu.

Using the isolated compounds as references, the chemical profile of the EFC was determined, and the contents of the main compounds, uracil and adenosine, were measured for quality control of the extract in animal experiments. Hemp seeds have been reported to have a possible anti-AD effect in a d-galactose-induced senescence model. We evaluated the health-promoting effect of EFC in APP/PS1 mice as an AD model. EFC significantly enhanced the new object recognition ability of APP/PS1 mice, increased the number of hippocampus of APP/PS1 mice, and reduced the deposition of senile plaques in the hippocampus.

Overall, this study supplemented the database of the chemical components and the neuroprotective activities of the aqueous extract of hemp seeds. However, the mass of the compounds obtained in this study was relatively small, possibly because of the aqueous extract's complex composition and low content.

Compared with the previously published studies of Fructus cannabis, ours presents more comprehensive research from chemistry to health effects studies. Although the direct material basis for its health effect has not been disclosed, this study provides the detailed chemical constituents of the aqueous extract qualitatively and quantitatively. It proves their effect in cellular and APP/PS1 AD models. Fructus cannabis is both a food and a commonly used traditional medicine; our study contributes to its development in both the food and medicine fields.

Conclusions

In summary, aqueous extraction of air-dried fruits of hemp seeds, followed by various separation techniques, produced 27 compounds. Compounds 1, 3–7, 12, 14–19, and 21–27 are the first-reported components of the genus Cannabis; compounds 8–11, 22, 15–17, 22, 23, 25, and 26 are considered characteristic of the Cannabis genus. Identifying these compounds documented the chemical diversity of cannabis seeds, providing a basis for further research into their chemical classification. In addition, the neuroprotective effects of EFC and the isolated compounds were revealed, suggesting that EFC might offer therapeutic activity in neurodegenerative diseases; the presented in vivo data support this hypothesis.
