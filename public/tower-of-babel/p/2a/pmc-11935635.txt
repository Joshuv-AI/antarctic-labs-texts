Timing Is Everything, Even for the ENCHANTED National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale
Authors: Arjun K. Rangan, Gillian Gordon Perue
Published: 2024

When the National Institute of Neurology Disorders and Stroke recombinant tissue plasminogen activator stroke work group developed the now ubiquitous National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) in the 1990s, 1 , 2 I doubt they could have foreseen the enduring impact this research scale would have on everyday stroke clinical practice, stroke program certification, Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services–related regulations, or physician certification requirements. In fact, in 2007 Dr Lyden, a member of the National Institute of Neurology Disorders and Stroke stroke work group, presented a cautionary tale 3 and suggested limiting the use of the NIHSS beyond its intended research purposes. Other authors have pushed back on the inclusion of NIHSS certification as part of our regulatory requirements, arguing against the rigorous requirement for biannual examiner recertification. 4 , 5 These publications were not enough to extinguish the stroke community's enchantment with the NIHSS.

Designed initially as a research stroke deficit rating scale for the landmark 1995 recombinant tissue plasminogen activator clinical trials I and II, the NIHSS has now become the de facto gold standard for stroke severity assessments in clinical trials. 1 These trials ultimately led to the Food and Drug Administration approval of intravenous alteplase for acute ischemic stroke. The NIHSS has stood the test of time, with the original NIHSS version being more reliable and better validated than the modified NIHSS. 6 During its development, the NIHSS was validated against magnetic resonance imaging–based brain infarct volumes with a normal score of 0 up to a maximum of 42, with greater scores corresponding to greater infarct volumes. 1 , 7 The NIHSS has been shown to have internal consistency 6 and high interrater reliability, can be extrapolated from the medical records, 2 is time efficient, and can be reliably used by non‐neurologists who undergo training. 3 Its utility is that it quantifies the neurology examination, ensuring speed and ease of communication among multidisciplinary teams, and it identifies more severe stroke types on the basis of the score. This allows clinicians to identify patients at highest risk of having large‐vessel occlusions, assesses the effectiveness of emerging therapies and classifies the severity of stroke treatment‐related complications such as symptomatic hemorrhagic transformation. The NIHSS has now become foundational for many acute ischemic stroke outcome prediction tools and is used by certification bodies and payers alike to define the quality of care of a stroke program.

In this issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA), You et al 8 explore yet another utility of the NIHSS in the ENCHANTED (Enhanced Control of Hypertension and Thrombolysis Stroke) trial, that of predicting 90‐day stroke outcomes among patients treated for acute ischemic strokes. The ENCHANTED trial was a 2×2 prospective, randomized, open‐label study evaluating the outcome between low‐ versus high‐dose alteplase and intensive or guideline recommended blood pressure control for patients with acute ischemic strokes. This new analysis of ENCHANTED uses the data of 4410 participants comparing the performance of NIHSS measured at baseline and 24 hours or change in NIHSS (absolute or percentage) in predicting the 90‐day favorable functional outcomes. The study ultimately determined that a 24‐hour NIHSS ≤4 was the most reliable and best predictor of 90‐day functional outcomes among these 4 options with an area under the curve of 0.86 for a modified Rankin Scale score of 0 to 2.

The astute reader will recall other trials that have used NIHSS as a predictive tool. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 Most of these used only baseline NIHSS 11 , 15 , 16 rather than the 24‐hour score, and several studies combined the NIHSS with other clinical or demographic information to develop a predictive model. 9 , 10 , 16 , 17 The current study provides a new perspective on the role of the NIHSS focusing the clinician not only on the predictive power of the NIHSS but also on the timing of its completion. The 24‐hour NIHSS demonstrates a higher predictive ability (AUC, 0.86 [95% CI, 0.85–0.88]) than the baseline score (AUC, 0.76 [95% CI, 0.74–0.77]).

One might ask why does the timing of the NIHSS have such an impact on outcomes? As authors, we offer the hypothesis that the NIHSS does more than just predicting outcomes. The NIHSS defines the disease severity and categorizes patients into mild stroke with nondisabling symptoms versus moderate to severe stroke. These 2 categories of patients arguably have different underlying stroke burden, stroke pathophysiology, and, ultimately, stroke outcomes. For example, we now know that patients who arrive with mild stroke and nondisabling symptoms defined as NIHSS <4 will have favorable outcomes, with no additional benefit derived from interventions such as intravenous thrombolysis 11 , 18 or mechanical thrombectomy. 19 A higher NIHSS score at presentation portends a more devastating illness with a high risk of a poor 90‐day outcome.

Understanding this categorization then provides another perspective. For those patients who present acutely with a higher NIHSS score, teams should offer interventions early and assertively with the goal of securing a 24‐hour NIHSS of <4 and ultimately a better 90‐day outcome. Both the absolute change (AUC, 0.69 [95% CI, 0.67–0.71]) and percentage change in NIHSS (AUC, 0.76 [95% CI, 0.75–0.78]) were modestly predictive of good outcomes but 24‐hour NIHSS outperforms them both because it includes the population of patients with mild stroke at presentation.

Strengths of the current analysis include its population size, which on average is 1.5 to 2 times larger than several other predictive studies previously mentioned. Additionally, when compared with other prediction models, the 24‐hour NIHSS performs favorably among other predictive studies 16 , 17 , 20 , 21 (Figure). However, there are limitations to the studies’ generalizability having been conducted primarily in an Asian population and varying dose of alteplase (both the 0.6 mg/kg and 0.9 mg/kg) being used, which may have impacted outcomes. It is also conventional that predictive models once developed in 1 cohort are later validated in a separate cohort, which was not the case in this analysis. Future validation studies of the performance of the 24‐hour NIHSS in a larger, more diverse population are warranted. Should the predictive value of the 24‐hour NIHSS be confirmed in other populations, could it become the new outcome biomarker of clinical trials? This has the potential to reduce the cost of clinical trials that follow patients out to 90 days to collect the 90‐day modified Rankin Scale score. The use of 24‐hour NIHSS as an outcome biomarker could lead to more rapid trial results, hastening the speed of our science.

This analysis also raises some other interesting questions. Should the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services incentivize the completion of the NIHSS with a Current Procedural Terminology code/reimbursement given its pivotal role in stroke care? There is a pertinent discussion to be had regarding this, especially as the regulatory requirements around stroke care continue to increase with time. Should the NIHSS be taught in medical schools and certification provided by medical schools even for those physicians destined to become nonneurologists as a routine part of their foundational training? Is it time to do away with the formal paid certification requirement for the NIHSS? No other neurology skill requires this paid recertification requirement. Is the timing right to remove this certification requirement especially for neurologists with >5 years of experience performing the scale? Or would this paradigm shift in NIHSS training and certification reduce its reliability and consistency and, in so doing, its enchantment?

While we do not have answers for these questions, we do know that the NIHSS will likely be a standard part of stroke clinical practice for years to come. The final impact of this pivotal work by the National Institute of Neurology Disorders and Stroke work group is yet to be fully realized.
