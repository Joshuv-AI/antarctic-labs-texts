Single-stapled versus non-single-stapled colorectal anastomosis in minimally invasive anterior resections: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: S Jain, R Abweni, M Iacob, R Labinoti, A Malik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnastomotic leak remains a major cause of morbidity after anterior resection for rectal cancer. The single-stapled technique (SST) has been proposed to eliminate crossover of intersecting staple lines in conventional non-single-stapled techniques (NSST). However, evidence comparing the two approaches in minimally invasive anterior resections remains limited.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted according to PRISMA 2020 guidelines (PROSPERO CRD420251053461). Randomised and observational studies comparing SST versus NSST in adult minimally invasive anterior resections were included. Primary outcomes were anastomotic leak and length of hospital stay. Secondary outcomes included operative time, postoperative morbidity (Clavien–Dindo ≥ III), and mortality. Random-effects models were used to pool effect estimates.ResultsFive observational studies (n = 1647; SST = 587, NSST = 1060) met inclusion criteria. Pooled analysis demonstrated that SST significantly reduced anastomotic leak rates compared with NSST (OR 0.43; 95% CI 0.26–0.70; p = 0.0007; I2 = 23%). Length of stay was shorter with SST (SMD − 0.43; 95% CI − 0.75 to − 0.11; p = 0.008; I2 = 88%). Operative time, blood transfusion requirements, and major postoperative complications did not differ significantly. Mortality was zero in all reporting studies.ConclusionsSingle-stapled anastomosis in minimally invasive anterior resection is associated with a lower pooled risk of anastomotic leak compared with non-single-stapled techniques. Despite heterogeneity in secondary outcomes, SST appears to be a promising technical refinement; however, randomised trials are required before routine adoption.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10151-026-03380-y.Keywords: Single stapled, Colorectal anastomosis, Anterior resections, Minimally invasive

Introduction

Anastomotic leak remains one of the most serious complications following anterior resection for colorectal disease. Despite ongoing refinement in minimally invasive techniques, leak rates continue to range from 3% to 15% in contemporary series, influenced by patient comorbidities, tumour characteristics, neoadjuvant treatment, and operative technique [1–4]. Anastomotic failure contributes to substantial morbidity, reoperation, prolonged hospitalisation, stoma formation, and impaired long-term oncological outcomes [5, 6].

Stapling devices are now integral to colorectal surgery, with linear and circular staplers enabling several anastomotic configurations. The conventional non-single-stapled technique (NSST) divides the rectal stump with a linear stapler before applying a circular stapler, creating intersecting staple lines. These “dog-ears” may compromise local blood flow and increase leak risk [7, 8].

The single-stapled technique (SST) was developed to eliminate staple-line intersections. By closing the dog-ears with a purse-string suture and creating only one final circular staple line, SST may reduce focal ischaemia and promote improved healing. Adoption has increased in minimally invasive anterior resection—both laparoscopic and robotic—particularly for mid- and low-rectal cancers [9–12]. Observational studies have suggested lower leak rates and shorter recovery with SST, although they vary in definitions, inclusion criteria, and perioperative protocols.

Given these differences, a synthesis of contemporary evidence is needed. This systematic review and meta-analysis compared SST with NSST in minimally invasive anterior resection, evaluating anastomotic leak, operative outcomes, postoperative morbidity, and length of hospital stay.

Methods

This review adheres to the guidelines outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [13]. Furthermore, it has been registered on PROSPERO under the registration number PROSPERO 2025 CRD420251053461.

Studies published in or translated to the English language from their inception until August 1, 2025 were included in the search. The initial criteria for inclusion involved randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and observational studies that compared SST to NSST, for elective adult anterior resections. Systematic reviews were excluded from the primary analysis, but primary studies included within these systematic reviews were considered for inclusion if they met the predefined inclusion criteria. Only studies focusing on adult anterior resections for elective patients and involving a stapled anastomosis were eligible for inclusion in this review. The exclusion criteria included no animal/cadaveric studies, non-English studies, paediatric population, palliative surgery, metastatic rectal cancer disease, faecal incontinence preoperatively, and non-stapled anastomosis.

The primary outcomes of interest encompassed anastomotic leak rates and length of stay in hospital. Secondary perioperative outcomes included the operative times, need for blood transfusions, and 30-day postoperative complication rates with Clavien–Dindo classification score ≥ 3. It is important to note that all these outcomes were assessed at the endpoint of the respective follow-up periods for the included studies, unless otherwise explicitly specified.

Electronic database searches were carried out in Ovid Medline, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews (CDSR). These searches were conducted by a senior information specialist from the library department of the Royal College of Surgeons of England on July 3, 2023. Furthermore, an additional search was undertaken through snowballing, referencing relevant articles. A final review was conducted before concluding the literature search on August 15, 2025.

The primary aim of the study was to assess whether SST, in elective adult anterior resections for rectal cancer surgical patients, confers any benefits over NSST in reducing anastomotic leak rates. To guide our search comprehensively, we employed the Patient-Intervention-Control-Outcome (PICO) framework, as outlined in Supplementary Table 1. The thorough search strategy is depicted in Supplementary Table 2a, b.

Abstract screening and full text review was performed by two independent blinded reviewers [SJ and RA], with conflict resolution by a third senior reviewer [RL], to generate a final list of eligible studies for inclusion in the meta-analysis. Demographic, clinical and outcome data in both treatment arms from individual studies were extracted by two independent researchers [MI and SJ] and cross-checked with another independent researcher for adequacy and accuracy [RA].

Observational studies were categorized following the criteria established by Mathes and Pieper [14]. The assessment of the risk of bias in observational studies utilised the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) assessment tool [15]. In this case with the JBI appraisal tool, the overall risk of bias for a specific study was determined by the number of questions answered with “yes”, “no”, or “unclear”. Studies were considered to have a low concern of bias if there was an unfavourable answer to one question or fewer, a moderate concern if there were unfavourable answers to two to three questions, and a high concern if four or more questions received unfavourable answers.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarised as counts and ratios; continuous data were reported as mean (SD). When studies reported medians with IQRs or ranges, means and SDs were estimated using the method reported by Wan et al. [16].

Meta-analyses for categorical variables used risk ratios (RR) or odds ratios (OR) with 95% CIs. Continuous outcomes were synthesised using standardised mean difference (SMD). Heterogeneity was evaluated using the I2 statistic, with I2 > 50% considered significant. Random-effects models (Mantel–Haenszel) were used when heterogeneity was present [17].

Handling of confounding factors

Patients and disease characteristics were highlighted and compared in SST and NSST comparisons.

Operational definitions

Linear stapler division of the rectum, followed by purse-string closure of dog-ears and creation of a single circular staple line. Alternatively a combined transabdominal and transperineal approach could be done where suitable for a TTSS approach. However, the mechanics of both are different. The type of suture used to invert the dog-ears was not specifically looked at as they all yielded the same type of configuration, achieving a circular staple line only without any intersections.

One or more linear stapler firings with subsequent circular stapling, leaving intersecting staple lines.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

The study selection process is summarised in the PRISMA diagram (Fig. 1). Eighty-seven records were identified on the initial search after excluding eight duplicates. Out of 12 studies that were eligible for full text review, six studies were excluded for either not fulfilling the study question criteria, no comparison performed, incorrect study intervention, and incorrect publication type to produce a final list of six studies.

Primary outcomes

Anastomotic leak

A random-effects meta-analysis of all the included studies [7, 8, 18–21] in the meta-analysis produced the pooled estimate that odds ratio (OR) for anastomotic leak (SST vs NSST) was 0.43 (95% CI 0.26–0.70; p = 0.0007) meaning that SST was associated with an approximately 43% relative reduction in reported anastomotic leak (Fig. 2a); and low statistical heterogeneity I2 = 23% indicating consistency of direction of effect across the included series. A subset analysis of the techniques to yield a single complete circular stapled anastomosis without TTSS was also done which showed a pooled leak rate reduction of 50% (OR 0.50; 95% CI 0.30–0.82; p = 0.006).

Kim et al. [7] had reported a higher leak rate with SST than seen in any of the other studies included here which is likely due to two reasons: first, it was the first study population and the surgeons were still learning how to master this new technique and the rates started to decrease over a period of time; second, they had a higher risk due to higher proportion of patients with previous abdominal surgeries, therefore increasing the difficulty of the surgeries as well.

Ninety-six percent of patients in both groups in the study by Foppa et al. [19] had a defunctioning ileostomy created despite which the anastomotic leak rates were significantly higher in this study than the other studies, reinforcing that an ileostomy is not protective. However, the malignancies in this study were more advanced in both groups compared to other studies which could be a potential contributor due to higher neoadjuvant rates and local reaction of the cancer itself. Raju et al. [18], on the other hand, showed zero anastomotic leaks in the SST group which is due to the rather higher number of pathologies being benign and more refined experience of the surgeons.

Secondary and other outcomes

Operative time

The overall operative time (mean ± SD) was available for five studies [7, 8, 18, 19, 21] and it showed no significant differences between the two groups on pooled analysis using standard mean differences (SMD 0.05; 95% CI − 0.23 to 0.33; p = 0.75) and a high heterogeneity was noted I2 = 83% (Fig. 2b). The operative times in the study by Kim et al. [7] showed that the new interventional technique SST took longer, while the operative times were lower in the SST group in the study by Raju et al. [18]. These differences are again attributable to the fact that the experience of the surgeons over a period led to faster operative times and the number of patients with previous abdominal surgeries was lower.

Length of hospital stay

Length of hospital stay (days) was recorded in all studies [7, 8, 18–21] and SST showed a significantly shorter length of hospital stay compared to NSST (SMD − 0.43; 95% CI − 0.75 to − 0.11; p = 0.008) (Fig. 2c), although the spread of the data was very heterogeneous with I2 = 88%. The length of hospital stay was significantly heterogeneous in the study by Foppa et al. [19] with potential outliers as well skewing the data which is likely because there were more anastomotic leaks also reported in the NSST group in the study. Therefore, the findings of this particular outcome will have to be treated cautiously.

Mortality

Mortality was recorded only by two studies [7, 8] which both showed zero mortalities. The other studies did not record any data on mortalities.

Discussion

Main findings

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesising data from five observational cohorts suggests that SST is associated with a significantly lower rate of anastomotic leak observed compared with NSST in minimally invasive anterior resections. The pooled odds ratio indicated nearly a 43% relative risk reduction (OR 0.43; 95% CI 0.26–0.70), with minimal heterogeneity (I2 = 23%). A subset analysis done excluding the TTSS technique showed that the OR was different and the mechanics of the two techniques are variable. However, this current available data does not allow us to directly make a comparative conclusion. Secondary analyses revealed a shorter hospital stay in SST cohorts, although findings for operative time and overall morbidity were heterogeneous.

Comparison with existing evidence

Anastomotic leak rates after low anterior resection remain as high as 15% despite advances in technique and perioperative optimization [15]. The theoretical rationale for SST—eliminating intersecting staple lines and associated dog-ear ischaemia—has been supported by experimental perfusion studies demonstrating improved microvascular flow at the staple line compared with double-stapled configurations [22, 23]. Recent clinical reports by Foppa et al. (2023) [19] and Harji et al. (2023) [20] similarly found lower leak rates with SST in laparoscopic and robotic anterior resections, corroborating the present pooled effect.

Earlier comparative work in open surgery yielded mixed results [7, 8], likely reflecting older stapling technology, variable definitions of leak, and absence of enhanced recovery protocols. The present findings therefore extend current knowledge by demonstrating a consistent protective association in the minimally invasive era. Importantly, the observed low heterogeneity strengthens the credibility of the leak outcome despite the non-randomised design of included studies.

Potential mechanisms

By producing a single staple line, SST avoids crossing linear and circular staple intersections, potentially preserving circumferential perfusion and reducing focal stress concentration [18]. This technical advantage could translate into improved anastomotic integrity. Moreover, SST facilitates precise tissue alignment and minimizes manipulation in the narrow pelvic space of minimally invasive rectal surgery [24]. However, the success of SST remains operator-dependent; inadequate purse-string closure or poor visualization during transanal stapler introduction may offset these benefits.

Clinical implications

The present data provide a signal that SST may reduce anastomotic leak after minimally invasive anterior resection. However, given the observational nature of the evidence and the heterogeneity in secondary outcomes, the findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Should these results be validated, SST could represent a practical refinement of minimally invasive anterior resection, potentially lowering leak-related morbidity and associated reoperation rates. Nonetheless, patient selection remains essential—particularly in irradiated, obese, or ultra-low rectal cases where technical exposure may limit applicability. They should continue to adhere to core anastomotic principles and use diverting stomas selectively. SST should therefore be viewed as an adjunct to, not a substitute for, meticulous surgical technique, adequate perfusion, and tension-free anastomosis.

Technical considerations and standardisation

The practical application of the single staple line (SSL) technique is heavily influenced by anatomical constraints and patient factors, which must be considered to ensure successful adoption. Applying a purse-string or a bunching stitch to the distal rectal stump after specimen extraction is straightforward for upper rectal cancers where the anastomosis lies at or above the peritoneal reflection, particularly in open surgery. However, technical difficulty increases significantly in the distal third of the rectum, especially in the context of a narrow male pelvis where the sacral curvature and the rigid prostate create a challenging angle of approach. In such cases, the prolapse of seminal vesicles or pelvic side wall fat into the operative field after excision can further hinder articulation of the circular stapler. Conversely, the distal rectum is often more accessible in women and in patients with a low BMI. While some surgeons advocate reinforcing the linear staple line with interrupted or continuous sutures, this manoeuvre is often impractical in the lowest third of the rectum where pelvic access is most constrained. Emerging approaches such as transanal total mesorectal excision (TaTME) and transanal transabdominal surgery (TTSS) can facilitate anastomotic construction in this region, but they require additional perineal setup and a dedicated skillset.

Standardisation of the SSL technique and a structured approach to the learning curve are essential for safe dissemination. In our experience, the SSL technique—specifically the placement of a purse-string or omega stitch to incorporate the lateral dog-ears of the linear staple line—can be reliably performed via an abdominal approach in both laparoscopic and robotic low rectal cancer surgery, provided the surgeon has adequate familiarity with minimally invasive pelvic dissection. We have previously demonstrated the technical details of this manoeuvre to facilitate single-staple anastomosis [25]. For surgeons adopting this method, we recommend initiating practice with high anterior resections where pelvic access is less restrictive. In our institutional experience, proficiency in applying the stitch in the low rectum was achieved within three cases, suggesting a short learning curve for surgeons already competent in advanced rectal cancer surgery. This progression aligns with the IDEAL framework for surgical innovation, where technical refinement and operator proficiency are critical precursors to widespread implementation and before evaluating outcomes in larger comparative studies.

Conclusion

Using the study-level data, our analysis shows that single-stapled anastomosis after minimally invasive anterior resection is associated with an observed lower pooled risk of anastomotic leak compared with non-single-stapled techniques. Secondary outcomes are heterogenous and less certain. The body of evidence is largely observational and variably at risk of bias. Definitive adoption of SST as a standard technique will require confirmatory randomised evidence; currently, on the basis of observational study analysis, SST should be adopted with caution in the interim. Meanwhile, SST may be considered a valid option in the armamentarium for colorectal surgeons where anatomically appropriate following the core principles of a good anastomosis.
