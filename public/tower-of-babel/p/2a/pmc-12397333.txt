Infrared and ultraviolet spectroscopic characterization of a key intermediate during DNA repair by (6-4) photolyase
Authors: Daichi Yamada, Ai Kadono, Tatsumi Maeno, Wataru Sato, Sachiko Yanagisawa, Toshihiko Hamamura, Yasuteru Shigeta, Junpei Yamamoto, Minoru Kubo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUltraviolet (UV) irradiation of DNA causes genotoxic photolesions, such as carcinogenic pyrimidine(6-4)pyrimidone photoproducts ((6-4)PPs). In many organisms, (6-4)PPs are repaired by (6-4) photolyases, which contain a flavin chromophore and use blue light energy to initiate the catalytic reaction. Although (6-4)PP repair has been shown to require the input of two successive photons, details of the mechanism remain elusive. Here, we applied recently developed time-resolved UV and infrared (IR) spectroscopic techniques to capture a key intermediate that awaits the second photon in the repair reaction of Xenopus laevis (6-4) photolyase. The results indicate that the intermediate is formed within 500 μs following the initial absorption of a photon and contains a four-membered oxetane ring that bridges two adjacent pyrimidine bases. These findings shed light on the molecular basis of DNA repair involving two-photon activation.Subject terms: Enzyme mechanisms, Biophysical chemistry

Introduction

The ultraviolet (UV) component of sunlight induces the formation of genotoxic photoproducts in DNA, leading to harmful effects such as growth inhibition and skin cancer1–4. Major types of these lesions include cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers (CPDs) and pyrimidine(6-4)pyrimidone photoproducts ((6-4)PPs), both involving the formation of covalent bonds between adjacent pyrimidine bases. In many organisms, these DNA photolesions are repaired by flavin-containing DNA photolyases that harness blue light energy. For example, CPD photolyases repair CPDs with a quantum yield of ≥50% via photoinduced electron transfer (ET) from fully reduced flavin adenine dinucleotide (FADH−) to the CPD, triggering the opening of the cyclobutane ring5–7. Very recently, this catalytic process was visualized by time-resolved serial femtosecond crystallography (TR-SFX), enhancing our understanding of the mechanism underlying the repair of CPDs8,9. On the other hand, (6-4)PPs are specifically repaired by (6-4) photolyases (Fig. 1). Similar to CPD photolyases, photoinduced ET from FADH− to the (6-4)PP triggers the repair reaction; however, the mechanism remains poorly understood, which is attributable in part to experimental difficulties arising from the low repair efficiency of (6-4)PPs (≤10%)10–12.

The lesion is repaired by (6-4) photolyase, which uses blue light to excite its FADH− cofactor, initiating an electron transfer–driven repair reaction. The active site structure, including two conserved His residues denoted His1 and His2, is shown in the inset.

A key structural distinction between CPDs and (6-4)PPs is that the latter feature an oxygen atom transferred from the 3ʹ pyrimidine to the 5ʹ pyrimidine during their formation (Fig. 1). Accordingly, the key question in repairing (6-4)PPs is how this oxygen atom returns to its original position in the 3′ base. Notably, in the UV-induced formation of (6-4)PP, an oxetane-bridged compound arises transiently (Fig. 1)13–18. Additionally, a thietane, a sulfur analogue of the oxetane, has been shown to be in equilibrium with (6-4)PP in water19. These led an early study11 to hypothesize that, during repair, (6-4)PP may form an oxetane upon binding to the enzyme, which is subsequently cleaved by photoinduced ET, as in CPD repair. However, crystallographic analysis later revealed that (6-4)PP remains structurally unchanged in the enzyme active site20, stimulating theoretical studies that do not presuppose oxetane formation. To date, three mechanisms of (6-4)PP repair have been proposed theoretically21. Two of them are largely similar, involving direct oxygen transfer from the 5′ pyrimidine to the 3′ pyrimidine following photoinduced ET from FADH− to the (6-4)PP22,23. These are classified as one-photon mechanisms because a single photon suffices to complete the repair. By contrast, the third mechanism involves two sequential photoexcitations24. Following initial ET from FADH− to the (6-4)PP, a key intermediate containing an oxetane bridge between the pyrimidine bases is formed, accompanied by back ET to the FAD cofactor. This oxetane intermediate is metastable unless a second photon becomes available. The second photoexcitation of FADH− again induces ET from FADH− to the intermediate to open the ring as in the case of CPD repair, completing the oxygen transfer. This mechanism is referred to as the two-photon mechanism.

Experimentally, ultrafast spectroscopy has been used to capture the early events of the repair process within a few nanoseconds, including a proton transfer step12,25. A competing ultrafast back ET from the (6-4)PP●− species to FADH● has also been observed, which contributes to the low quantum yield of (6-4)PP repair, yet the number of photons required for (6-4)PP repair remained unclear. Subsequently, a spectroscopic experiment using successive laser flashes was conducted to examine the two-photon mechanism. This experiment provided direct evidence that (6-4)PP repair by Xenopus laevis (6-4) photolyase requires two photoexcitations26. Additionally, a long-lived intermediate denoted “intermediate X” was kinetically identified upon single photoexcitation. Intermediate X is formed in ~40 μs27 and persists for a lifetime of about 100 s, awaiting a second photon to complete the repair26. Thus, it is evident that intermediate X is a key species and its characterization is crucial to understand how (6-4)PP lesions are repaired.

In the present study, we employed time-resolved (TR) UV and infrared (IR) spectroscopies to investigate the nature of intermediate X formed during the first photoexcitation of Xenopus laevis (6-4) photolyase. While previous studies employed repeated laser flashes and monitored absorption changes at selected UV or visible wavelengths to establish the two-photon mechanism26,27, our method is based on a single-pulse excitation with sample exchange and uses UV and IR probes covering a relatively broad spectral range for the detection of spectral features associated with intermediate X. This approach, however, poses a significant challenge, in that TR spectroscopic observations are generally restricted to systems that can be repeatedly measured without the need for sample exchange, such as photocycle proteins. To overcome this limitation, we recently developed novel sample-exchange systems designed to capture enzyme intermediates during catalytic turnover, and we applied them here for the TR detection of intermediate X in (6-4) photolyase. For TR-UV spectroscopy, a microflow-flash system28 was used to monitor UV absorption changes of the (6-4)PP substrate. For TR-IR spectroscopy, a fixed-target system combined with a Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) microscope29 was utilized to probe the vibrational signatures of intermediate X. A particular focus of this study was whether intermediate X possesses an oxetane structure, as predicted by the two-photon repair mechanism.

Results and discussion

TR-UV spectroscopy

To monitor the formation of intermediate X, the UV spectral changes were measured after single pump-pulse illumination (Fig. 2). Single-pulse illumination was ensured by performing the measurements on a continuously flowing sample, such that each excitation flash illuminated fresh, unexcited sample. It should also be noted that the pump pulse duration was 6 ns. This is sufficiently short to avoid the photoexcitation of intermediate X, which is formed on a timescale of tens of microseconds. At 10 µs, a sharp positive peak at 274 nm and a negative peak at 300 nm were observed, corresponding to FADH● and FADH‒, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S1). The appearance of these peaks indicates ET from FADH‒ to the (6-4)PP, which is consistent with the reported ET timescale of 0.2 ns12. At 500 µs, the bands corresponding to FADH● and FADH‒ had disappeared, and a new peak and a new trough had appeared at 250 and 328 nm, respectively, which persisted up to 20 ms (the maximum delay time of the present instrument). Because the completion of (6-4)PP repair should yield a peak and trough at ~268 and 328 nm (Supplementary Fig. S2), the spectral features observed at 500 µs can be ascribed to an intermediate species. The disappearance of the FADH● and FADH‒ signals indicates that FADH‒ was restored by electron return. Therefore, the broad positive peak at 250 nm can be reasonably assigned to intermediate X.

TR-UV spectral changes of X. laevis (6-4) photolyase during (6-4)PP repair upon single-pulse photoillumination.

The enzyme and substrate concentrations were 300 and 600 µM, respectively, and the optical pathlength was 100 μm.

UV spectral simulation of intermediate X

Intermediate X has been postulated to be an oxetane species in the two-photon mechanism24. To examine this possibility, the UV difference spectrum of oxetane minus (6-4)PP was simulated using density functional theory (DFT). As a control, the difference spectrum of intact TT minus (6-4)PP was first calculated to assess the computational reliability (Fig. 3, blue trace). Although the calculated absorption spectrum was blue-shifted by 10–16 nm relative to the experimental one (Supplementary Fig. S2), the overall spectral features were well reproduced. The difference spectrum of oxetane minus (6-4)PP was then simulated, which is overlaid in the same figure (Fig. 3, red trace). Notably, the oxetane exhibited relatively weak absorption at 244 nm. Taking into account the systematic blue shift in the calculated spectra, this feature is in good agreement with the experimental spectrum of intermediate X (Fig. 2), in line with its identification as an oxetane species.

Calculated UV difference spectra of DNA structures.

The difference spectra of intact TT minus (6-4)PP and oxetane minus (6-4)PP are presented in blue and red, respectively. The corresponding structures used in the calculations are shown beneath the spectra.

The negative peak in the intermediate X spectrum at around 328 nm (Fig. 2) corresponds primarily to the loss of (6-4)PP. On the basis of the bleaching at 325 nm (ε = 6.0 mM−1 cm−1)26, the formation efficiency of intermediate X was estimated to be ~4%, in accordance with a previously reported value26. However, it should be noted that this value is likely an underestimate due to a small but appreciable absorbance contribution from oxetane, as suggested by the UV spectral simulation (Supplementary Fig. S3).

IR characterization of the intermediate

IR spectroscopy is a powerful tool for molecular structure assignment. Oxetanes are known to exhibit strong IR absorption bands below around 900–1000 cm−1, a characteristic feature of their four-membered ring30. To gain detailed insight into these vibrational features, the IR spectrum of the oxetane was simulated using DFT (Supplementary Fig. S4a). The spectrum of the 18O-labeled oxetane, with the ring oxygen replaced with 18O, was also calculated. Experimentally, a D2O-based buffer is required to observe the low-frequency region below 1000 cm−1 because it reduces the background absorption of water. Therefore, to enable direct comparison with experimental data, the dissociable protons of the nucleobases were deuterated in the DFT calculations. As expected, isotope shifts were observed below 1000 cm−1, originating from the C5–O4′–C4′ bending (928/920 cm−1) and stretching (904/892 and 876/868 cm−1) modes of the oxetane ring, where the wavenumbers are given as 16O/18O pairs, as summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Guided by these calculations, IR measurements were performed focusing on the low-frequency region (<1000 cm−1) using a D2O-based buffer and an optical pathlength of 30 μm. A fixed-target scheme, originally developed for TR-SFX31, was employed for sample exchange. The sample solutions were placed in the microwells (Ø500 μm) of a fixed-target-type cell mounted in an FTIR microscope29, and rapid-scan measurements were carried out for each well under single-pulse excitation. Spectra before and after the single-pulse photoillumination were accumulated for 60 s each and used to calculate the light-minus-dark difference spectrum, which is shown as the red trace in Fig. 4. It is worth noting that the long lifetime of intermediate X (ca. 100 s)26 enabled such extended signal averaging, which facilitated the detection of its IR signature during these measurements.

The light-minus-dark difference spectra for 16O-(6-4)PP and 18O-(6-4)PP are shown by the red and blue traces, respectively. Subsequently, an isotopically labeled (6-4)PP was synthesized, bearing 18O at the OH group of the 5′ pyrimidine—the oxygen atom transferred during lesion formation (Fig. 1). The corresponding light-minus-dark difference spectrum is plotted as the blue trace in Fig. 4. Finally, the difference spectrum between the 16O-containing and 18O-labeled samples was generated to highlight the 18O-isotope-sensitive bands, which is shown as the green trace. It should be noted here that although minor spurious peaks due to the photo-oxidation of FADH− (<5%)26 were observed above 1000 cm−1, no such features appeared below 1000 cm−1 (Supplementary Fig. S5).

For comparison, a theoretical difference spectrum (black trace in Fig. 4) was obtained by subtracting the 16O-minus-18O difference spectrum for (6-4)PP from that for the oxetane, although the contribution from (6-4)PP was negligible (Supplementary Fig. S4). The experimental difference spectrum largely matched the theoretical one, with the peak positions consistent within 30 cm−1, enabling a one-to-one correspondence between the two sets of peaks as indicated by the dashed lines in Fig. 4 (see also Supplementary Table S1). The experimentally observed 16O/18O peak pair at 928/910 cm−1 was assigned to the C5–O4′–C4′ bending mode, whereas the pairs at 893/881 and 874/841 cm−1 corresponded to the C5–O4′–C4′ stretching modes. Together with the TR-UV results, these vibrational signatures confirm the formation of a four-membered ring involving the transferred oxygen, providing compelling experimental evidence that intermediate X is the oxetane species, a critical missing piece in the proposed reaction mechanism of (6-4)PP repair.

Reaction mechanism of (6-4)PP repair

On the basis of the present TR-UV and IR results, we outline a reaction scheme for the repair of (6-4)PPs by (6-4) photolyase (Fig. 5). Upon the first photoexcitation, ET from FADH‒ to the (6-4)PP lesion occurs, followed by proton transfer12,25. Subsequently, the electron transiently returns to the FAD cofactor (FADH●), resulting in the formation of the long-lived intermediate X, a species that has so far remained uncharacterized. Our spectroscopic results indicate that this intermediate contains a four-membered oxetane ring, implying that its formation during repair requires a transient electron donation from FADH‒, unlike in the UV-induced formation of (6-4)PP. Notably, the formation of the neutral oxetane species paired with FADH‒ is energetically much more favorable than that of the oxetane radical with FADH●24, consistent with the electron return observed in the present TR-UV experiments. A second photon is then required to drive the repair forward: another photoinduced ET from FADH‒ to the 5′ pyrimidine base of the oxetane intermediate is proposed24, initiating cleavage of the covalent bonds within the oxetane ring. This final step yields the repaired DNA product, corresponding to the original TT bases.

Putative reaction scheme of (6-4)PP repair by X. laevis (6-4) photolyase.

The first photoinduced ET from FADH‒ to the (6-4)PP initiates the repair reaction and, via back ET, leads to the formation of a long-lived intermediate X containing a four-membered oxetane ring. A second photon is then required to induce productive cleavage of the oxetane ring and complete the repair, yielding intact TT. The dashed arrow indicates a reverse reaction that occurs in the absence of a second photon. The time constants shown are based on previous studies12,26,27.

The oxetane intermediate detected here is chemically identical to the species transiently formed as a result of excited-state dynamics during the UV-induced formation of (6-4)PP14–18. By contrast, in the repair process catalyzed by (6-4) photolyase, the oxetane is generated through a ground-state reaction initiated by electron injection from FADH‒ under enzymatic control. This convergence on a common intermediate from distinct initial states and chemical environments reflects the mechanistic significance of oxetane in the structural conversion between the damaged and the repaired DNA. However, it is noteworthy that the oxetane intermediate formed during repair exhibits a markedly longer lifetime (ca. 100 s) compared to the much faster (<ms) rearrangement to (6-4) PP observed during lesion formation32. This pronounced difference suggests that the enzyme stabilizes the oxetane intermediate, giving sufficient time for second-photon absorption to enable subsequent repair. This highlights the enzyme’s distinctive role in modulating the stability and reactivity of the oxetane intermediate, with His1 and His2 likely involved (Fig. 1).

Conclusions

In summary, we employed TR-UV and IR spectroscopies in combination with custom-built sample-exchange systems to characterize intermediate X, a key species in the catalytic cycle of (6-4) photolyases. The molecular identification of intermediate X is of great importance for gaining insight into the photorepair mechanism of (6-4)PPs, one of the major UV-induced DNA lesions. This work, to the best of our knowledge, presents the first direct experimental identification of the oxetane nature of intermediate X, validating a critical step in the two-photon repair mechanism of (6-4)PPs. While this work focused on the (6-4) photolyase from Xenopus laevis, our experimental approach is broadly applicable to photolyases from other organisms, including that from Drosophila melanogaster, for which a crystal structure is available20. Such a structurally characterizable system may allow future efforts to combine spectroscopic and crystallographic analyses of the oxetane intermediate. Although its formation yield is limited, continuous photoirradiation could be used to enhance its population. Pursuing such an approach could offer deeper mechanistic insight into the DNA photorepair mechanism, including the structural basis for the remarkable stability of the oxetane intermediate under enzymatic conditions.

Methods

Expression and purification

Xenopus laevis (6-4) photolyase was expressed in Escherichia coli strain C41(DE3) as a fusion enzyme with a 6×His tag at the N-terminus33. The enzyme was purified using a TALON metal affinity resin column (Takara Bio) and a HiTrap Heparin HP column (Cytiva). The enzyme expressed in E. coli does not bind a second chromophore.

Preparation of DNA lesions

(6-4)PP was synthesized as described previously26 and incorporated at the center (underlined) of the single-stranded DNA oligonucleotide carrying the following sequence: 5ʹ-CGATTGAT-3ʹ. For isotopic labeling, an 18O4-thymidine derivative was synthesized using H218O (Taiyo Nippon Sanso Corp.) with 88% 18O enrichment (Supplementary Fig. S6) and further derivatized to the 18O-(6-4)PP phosphoramidite for oligonucleotide synthesis.

Preparation of reaction samples

A solution containing the oxidized enzyme and cysteine (reductant) was illuminated for 25 min with a 100 W halogen lamp (PHL-100, Mejiro Precision) through a light guide (400–800 nm), coupled with a heat-absorbing filter (S76-HA50, Suruga Seiki), to reduce the FAD cofactor in the enzyme. Full reduction of FAD was confirmed by UV–visible spectroscopy (NP-80, Implen). The enzyme concentration was estimated from the absorption band at 384 nm (ε = 5.8 mM−1 cm−1)26. Next, aliquots of the (6-4)PP substrate solution were added to the enzyme solution containing reduced FADH− under dim red light to achieve an [enzyme]:[substrate] molar ratio of 1:2. The substrate concentration was estimated from the absorption band at 325 nm (ε = 6.0 mM−1 cm−1)26. For TR-UV spectroscopy, a buffer containing 25 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8), 50 mM NaCl, 100 mM cysteine, and 2.5% glycerol was used. For FTIR spectroscopy, a D2O-based buffer (50 mM Tris-DCl, pD 8) containing 100 mM NaCl, 100 mM cysteine, and 5% glycerol was used to avoid strong background absorption by H2O. All procedures were performed in a glovebox at an O2 concentration of <10 ppm (Vinyl Anaerobic Chamber Type A, Coy Laboratory). Samples were handled at 4 °C using an aluminum block bath (CTU-Mini, Taitec).

TR-UV spectroscopy

Flow-flash measurements were performed using a continuously flowing sample solution. Sample excitation was achieved with a 6 ns, 390 nm pump pulse from an optical parametric oscillator (OPO) operating at 5 Hz (NT230, Ekspla). TR spectra were recorded at 10 Hz using microsecond probe pulses from a Xe flash lamp (L11316-11-11, Hamamatsu Photonics). Because the pump laser operated at half the probe repetition rate, nearly simultaneous acquisition of the pump-on and pump-off (reference) spectra was achieved at 100 ms intervals. Continuous flow of the sample was accomplished using a microflow cell and a syringe pump (PHD Ultra Syringe Pump, Harvard Apparatus). The flow channel width and thickness (i.e., optical pathlength) were 150 and 100 µm, respectively. The sample flow rate was set to 4 µL min−1, ensuring complete sample exchange within 98 ms. Under these flow conditions, the detectable time window was limited to 20 ms because the pump-illuminated sample exited the observation area after this period. The pump and probe focal diameters at the sample point were Ø300 and Ø110 µm, respectively.

The enzyme concentration was 0.3 mM and the pump photon density was reduced to 14 mJ cm−2, such that the number of absorbed photons was 0.53 times the number of enzyme molecules in the pump-irradiated sample volume. The pump illumination was controlled using a beam shutter (SH05, Thorlabs). The pump and probe light source, beam shutter, and spectrometer (Flame-S, Ocean Optics) were synchronized with a pulse generator (DG645, Stanford Research Systems), providing a time resolution of 10 µs. The spectral data were analyzed using Igor Pro 6 (WaveMetrics). All measurements were performed at 10 °C.

IR spectroscopy

The sample solution was loaded into an optical cell consisting of a stainless steel spacer with 100 wells (Ø500 μm) sandwiched between two BaF2 windows. The spacer thickness (i.e., optical pathlength) was 30 μm. Sample excitation was induced by a 6 ns, 390 nm pump pulse from the OPO (NT230, Ekspla) passed through a beam shutter (SH05, Thorlabs). FTIR spectra were acquired in the transmission mode using a Bruker Vertex 70 spectrometer coupled with a Hyperion 2000 microscope, which was equipped with a 15× Cassegrain objective/condenser pair and a mercury cadmium telluride (MCT) detector (D316-025). The IR probe area was confined to a single well using a knife-edge aperture. A germanium filter (68-653, Edmund Optics) was placed at the back of the Cassegrain objective to protect the MCT detector from pump light scattering. Spectra were recorded in the rapid-scan mode with a single-sided data acquisition mode and a scanner speed of 160 kHz (120 ms/spectrum). The spectral resolution was 4 cm−1 with an aperture of 6 mm. A reference spectrum was collected for 60 s prior to pump illumination. Subsequently, a spectrum was recorded for 60 s after single pump-pulse illumination, from which the reference spectrum was subtracted to yield the light-minus-dark difference spectrum. For data accumulation, measurements were repeated across multiple wells by scanning the sample cell. The pump focal size at the sample was Ø500 µm. The enzyme concentration was 2 mM and the pump photon density was 21.9 mJ cm−2, such that the number of absorbed photons was 0.90 times the number of enzyme molecules in the pump-irradiated sample volume. To improve the signal-to-noise ratio, the pump photon density was set higher than that used in the TR-UV measurements. The spectral noise level was estimated using the dark-minus-dark difference. The spectral data were analyzed using Igor Pro 6 (WaveMetrics). All measurements were conducted at 10 °C.

Simulation of UV and IR spectra

To simulate the experimentally obtained UV and IR spectra, three model systems of the intact TT, (6-4)PP, and oxetane species were constructed and their spectra were computed. The models were optimized in water at the B3LYP/6-311 + G(d,p) level of theory with empirical dispersion correction (GD3BJ)34,35, where the solvent effects of water were treated by the solvation model based on density within an implicit polarizable continuum model36. UV spectra were calculated for the optimized structures by time-dependent DFT with the same exchange-correlation functional. IR spectra were calculated for the optimized structures, with the dissociable protons of the nucleobases deuterated to allow comparison with experimental data. Peak widths were set to a half-width at half-maximum of 4 cm−1 using GaussView 6 (Gaussian Inc.). Gaussian 16 revision C01 was utilized throughout the calculations37. The optimized structures used for the spectral simulations are available as Supplementary Data 1 (intact TT), Supplementary Data 2 ((6-4)PP), and Supplementary Data 3 (oxetane).
