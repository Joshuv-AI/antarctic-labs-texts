Knowledge of pregnancy danger signs and associated factors among pregnant teenagers in Western Hararghe, Ethiopia: a facility-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Kassata Kakse, Dawit Tamiru, Selemaye Zenebe, Ribka Mamaru, Kasiye Shiferaw
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObstetric danger signs are often unpredictable, making timely recognition critical for preventing maternal complications. Enhancing maternal knowledge, ensuring quality maternal care, and promoting prompt health-seeking behavior are essential strategies for reducing maternal mortality. While previous studies have primarily focused on the general population of women of reproductive age, empirical evidence regarding pregnant teenagers’ knowledge of these signs, and the factors influencing it, remains limited and largely unexamined in Ethiopia.ObjectiveThis study aimed to assess the level of knowledge regarding pregnancy danger signs and identify associated factors among pregnant teenagers in public health facilities in Western Hararghe Zone, Ethiopia, from October 1 to 30, 2024.Methods and materialsA facility-based cross-sectional study was carried out among 402 pregnant teenagers attending antenatal care (ANC) in public health facilities within the Western Hararghe zone. A total of seven facilities, comprising five hospitals and two health centers, were selected via simple random sampling, while study participants were recruited using systematic random sampling techniques. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using a pre-tested structured questionnaire administered by six diploma-level midwives. The collected data were coded and entered into EpiData version 3.1, then exported to SPSS version 27 for analysis. Variables with p-value < 0.25 in bivariable analysis were entered into multivariable logistic regression model. The strength of associations was measured using adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals, and statistical significance was declared at p < 0.05.ResultsThis study demonstrated that 23% (95% CI: 19.03–27.26) of pregnant teenagers possessed good knowledge of pregnancy danger signs. Several factors were significantly associated with higher knowledge levels: achieving a secondary education or higher [AOR = 3.1; 95% CI (1.52, 6.17)], having a spouse with formal education [AOR = 5.52; 95% CI (2.36, 12.66)], being multigravida [AOR = 5.96; 95% CI (2.91, 12.19)], and residing within 30 min of a health facility [AOR = 2.8; 95% CI (1.33, 5.88)].ConclusionIn conclusion, the level of knowledge regarding pregnancy danger signs among pregnant teenagers was poor. These findings underscore the urgent need for coupled-based educational health program to enhance awareness. Furthermore, tailored interventions should prioritize primigravida teenagers and those residing in remote areas to bridge the existing gaps in maternal health literacy.Keywords: Pregnant teenagers, Danger signs, Knowledge, Ethiopia

Introduction

Pregnancy danger signs (PDSs) are serious warning indicators that can arise during pregnancy. These include seizures, high fever, severe headache, vaginal bleeding, blurred vision, a lack of fetal movements, a sudden gush of fluid per vagina, and swelling of the face or hands [1, 2]. Such complications pose grave risks to both mother and baby, making awareness essential. While these complications aren’t always predictable, early recognition is key to preventing life-threatening outcomes [1, 3].

Each year, approximately 211 million pregnancies occur worldwide, with 15% of women facing obstetric complications [4, 5]. A lack of awareness regarding danger signs often exacerbates these risks, a problem that is particularly acute among teenagers in Sub-Saharan Africa. In this region, cultural beliefs and limited healthcare access frequently hinder the recognition of critical warning signs [5]. Furthermore, even when services are accessible, teenagers often receive lower-quality care and support than adults [4]. Ultimately, this knowledge gap delays life-saving obstetric care, increasing the danger for both mother and baby [6, 7].

Teenage pregnancies come with significant social and health burdens. Complications during pregnancy and childbirth remain the leading causes of death for young women aged 15–19. Compared to adults, teenagers are disproportionately affected by conditions like preeclampsia and eclampsia, facing 1.2 and 3.2 times the risk, respectively [8, 9].

Research has highlighted several factors that influence how much women of reproductive age know about pregnancy danger signs, including their age, location, education, job, economic status, and previous obstetric history [10–13]. While national strategies in Ethiopia aim to empower women, awareness remains inconsistent across the country; for instance, knowledge levels are lowest reported in Dire Dawa and highest in Mizan Aman [14, 15].

Despite their vulnerability, the level of knowledge regarding pregnancy danger signs among Ethiopian teenagers remains largely unexamined. This evidence gap poses a significant risk, as unrecognized warning signs can lead to preventable morbidity and mortality for both mothers and newborns. Identifying the extent of this knowledge gap and its underlying determinants is essential for designing targeted interventions to improve survival rates. Consequently, this study provides contemporary evidence on knowledge of pregnancy danger signs and associated factors among pregnant teenagers in Eastern Ethiopia.

Methods and materials

Study design, period, and setting

A multi-center, health facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted from October 1 to October 30, 2024. The study focused on pregnant teenagers attending ANC services at public health facilities within the Western Hararghe Zone of the Oromia Region in Eastern Ethiopia. Based on the most recent available population projections (2017), the total population of the Western Hararghe Zone was estimated at 2,435,350, comprising 1,243,462 men, and 1,191,888 women. Of these, 259,563 residents were urban dwellers.

The zone’s healthcare infrastructure includes two general hospitals, five primary hospitals, 85 health centers and a non-governmental clinic operated by the Family Guidance Association (FGA), all of which provide antenatal care (ANC) services and comprehensive maternal health packages.

Population and eligibility criteria

All pregnant teenagers attending ANC clinics in public health institutions within the Western Hararghe Zone served as the source population. The study population consisted of those pregnant teenagers who sought ANC services at the specifically selected public health institutions during the study period.

Teenagers who were unable to participate due to illness were excluded. This specifically applied to individuals with significant hearing or communication impairments, as well as those experiencing acute mental health problems that hindered ability to respond.

Sample size and sampling technique

The sample size was determined using the single population proportion formula. Due to the lack of prior studies conducted in similar population a proportion (p) of 50% was used with a 95% confidence level, and a 5% margin of error (d). After accounting for a 5% non-response rate, the final sample size was established at 402 pregnant teenagers.

A multi-stage sampling technique was employed to recruit the study participants. Within the study area, ANC services are provided across two general hospitals, five primary hospitals, and eighty-five health centers. From these, seven facilities were selected using a lottery method to ensure representativeness: Chiro General Hospital, Gelemiso General Hospital, Azebot Primary Hospital, Badesa Primary Hospital, Hirna Primary Hospital, Chiro Health Center, and Kuni Health Center. The total sample size was proportionally allocated based on each facility’s average monthly caseload of pregnant teenagers (CGH = 112, APH = 98, BPH = 93, HPH = 92, CHC = 102, GGH = 95, and KHC = 84). Finally, participants were selected using systematic random sampling with a calculated sampling interval of K = 2. The initial participant at each site was chosen via a lottery, with subsequent eligible teenagers recruited sequentially until the required quota was met.

Data collection tool and procedures

Data was collected through face-to-face interviews with pregnant teenagers using pretested, structured questionnaires. Six trained diploma-level midwives served as data collectors. The instrument was adapted from peer-reviewed literature and tailored to the local context to ensure all relevant variables assessed [16–18]. The tool comprises four primary sections: socio-demographic, obstetric, knowledge-based, and health service–related factors. To ensure accuracy, the questionnaire was translated into the local language and independently translated back to English to verify linguistic consistency.

Variable and measurement

Pregnancy Danger signs: Presence of a condition that increases the chances of a pregnant woman and/ or her unborn child dying or having poor health. The most common Danger signs are vaginal bleeding, convulsions, high fever, persistent severe headache, blurred vision, absence of fetal movements, gush of fluid from the vagina, Swollen hands and face [2].

Teenage pregnancy: refers to pregnancy in women between the ages of 13–19 years old, irrespective of parity [19].

Multigravida teenager: refers to teenagers in their second pregnancy.

Knowledge of PDS: refers in this study to mean awareness or familiarity of mothers about danger signs of pregnancy that a pregnant woman could face during her gestational period. It will be evaluated depending on the mothers’ answers to the questions that evaluate knowledge, i.e.;

Good knowledge of PDS: those participants who answered three or more questions out of the 8 knowledge-related questions [18, 20].

Poor knowledge of PDS: those participants who answered fewer than three questions out of the 8 knowledge-related questions [11, 15].

Data quality assurance

Six female diploma-level midwives, selected for their community familiarity and local language proficiency, were recruited for data collection. They were supervised by two senior BSc midwives with prior research experience. Both collectors and supervisors attended a one-day intensive training session covering the study objectives, respondent confidentiality, questionnaire content, and ethical recruitment techniques. To ensure data integrity, questionnaires were reviewed and cross-checked at the end of each day for completeness and consistency, allowing for immediate corrective measures. Additionally, the tool was pretested on 5% of the total sample (20 pregnant teenagers) at Albarekate Health Center to validate the instrument and ensure the study’s internal validity.

Data processing and analysis

Data was coded and cleaned using EpiData 3.01 before being exported to SPSS version 27 for analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics, while the prevalence of knowledge regarding pregnancy danger signs was dichotomized into “good” or “poor” levels. To identify independent predictors, both bivariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed. Multicollinearity among independent variables was ruled out using the variance inflation factor (VIF < 5), and model’s goodness-of-fit was confirmed by the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (p = 0.733). Finally, adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals and a p-value < 0.05 were utilized to determine factors statistically associated with the outcome.

Factors associated with knowledge of pregnancy danger signs

Nineteen variables were initially entered into the bivariable analysis; subsequently, eight variables (age, residence, maternal educational status, husband’s educational status, family income, ANC follow-up, gravidity, and travel time to health facility) met the criteria (p-value < 0.25) for inclusion in the multivariable model. After adjusting for confounders in the multivariable binary logistic regression, only maternal education, husband education, gravidity, and walking time remained independently associated with outcome.

The odds of possessing good knowledge of pregnancy danger signs were significantly higher among pregnant teenagers with secondary education or above compared to those with less education (AOR = 3.1; 95% CI: 1.52–6.17; p = 0.002). Similarly, teenagers whose husbands had formal education were five times more likely to have good knowledge than those whose husbands lacked formal education.

Multigravida teenagers also showed a greater likelihood of having good knowledge of pregnancy danger signs compared to primigravida peers (AOR = 5.96; 95% CI: 2.91–12.19; p < 0.001). Finally, teenagers living within a 30-minute walk of a health facility were more likely to be knowledgeable than those residing further away (AOR = 2.8; 95% CI: 1.33–5.88; p = 0.006). Discussion

This study aimed to determine the level of knowledge regarding pregnancy danger signs and its associated factors within public health facilities in West Hararghe, Ethiopia. The findings revealed that only 23% of pregnant teenagers possessed good knowledge of these signs. Factors significantly associated with higher knowledge levels included a secondary education or higher, having a husband with formal education, being multigravida, and residing within a 30-minute walk of a health facility.

This finding aligns with research conducted in the Eastern Democratic Republic of Congo (21.9%) [21]. The current study also indicates that maternal educational attainment is a significant predictor of good knowledge regarding pregnancy danger signs, a result consistent with findings from Ubungo Municipality, Tanzania [10] and Indonesia [22]. This association likely exists because education enhances a woman’s ability to access and comprehend health information, navigate healthcare services, and interpret health messaging via mass media. Furthermore, higher education levels are associated with increased ANC utilization and improved cognitive processing skills, which facilitate better retention of critical health warnings [23, 24].

Teenagers whose husbands had a formal education were five times more likely to possess good knowledge of pregnancy danger signs than those whose husbands had no formal education. This finding aligns with previous research indicating that a husband’s educational level positively influences a woman’s awareness of obstetric risks [25]. This link may be attributed to more equitable household decision-making, better resource allocation for healthcare, or shared exposure to health-related media. Consequently, these results suggest that partner involvement in maternal health education is a critical strategy for reducing maternal mortality and pregnancy-related complications.

Multigravida teenagers were more likely to have good knowledge of pregnancy danger signs than the primigravida teenagers. This finding corroborates previous researches [21, 26] and may be attributed to cumulative exposure gained through prior pregnancies. Repeated interactions with healthcare providers, firsthand life experience, and the exchange of information with peers likely contribute to this enhanced awareness over time.

Teenagers residing within 30-minute walk of a health facility were significantly more likely to possess good knowledge of pregnancy danger signs compared to those living further away. This finding aligns with studies conducted in Chiro town [27] and Yirgacheffe town [28], Ethiopia. This suggests that geographic proximity to health facilities may bolster awareness by lowering transportation barriers, facilitating more frequent interactions with healthcare providers, and increasing the likelihood of receiving consistent health education.

The development of policies that actively promote partner involvement in antenatal care counseling is essential. In parallel, community-level awareness campaigns led by health professionals and health extension workers should be strengthened to facilitate the early recognition and timely management of pregnancy-related complications. Additionally, tailored Information, Education, and Communication (IEC) materials must be designed and disseminated to teenagers and their families to bridge the awareness gap. Finally, further community-based research is recommended to capture the experiences of teenagers with poor health-seeking behaviors who may have been missed in this facility-based study.

Strengths and limitations

The study focuses on a vulnerable and underserved demographic, enhancing its relevance and potential public health impact. By employing a robust sample size, the study ensures reliable statistical analysis and improved precision. Furthermore, it addresses critical maternal health issues that align with international health goals and policy priorities, providing evidence to strengthen antenatal education programs.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. As the study only included teenagers already attending antenatal care, it may have excluded those with the most limited access to services or information. Additionally, while a short recall period was used to minimize recall bias, its influence cannot be entirely ruled out. Finally, the cross-sectional design means that a causal or temporal relationship between the variables cannot be definitively established.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study reveals that overall knowledge of pregnancy danger signs among pregnant teenagers is notably poor. The findings emphasize that expanding educational initiatives for both women and their partners is fundamental to improving awareness. Furthermore, there is a clear need for targeted interventions, specifically for primigravida teenagers and those facing geographic barriers to healthcare. However, given that this was a facility-based study, these conclusions primarily apply to those already accessing care; therefore, extending outreach to teenagers currently outside the healthcare system remains a critical priority for future public health effort.

The study was conducted in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations of the Declaration of Helsinki on medical research ethics. Ethical approval was obtained from Haramaya University, College of Health and Medical Sciences Institutional Health Research Ethics Review Committee (IHRERC) (Ref. No. IHRERC/232/2024). To maintain confidentiality, data was collected anonymously. Participants were informed that all information provided would be treated with the utmost confidentiality and that their participation would involve minimal risk.
