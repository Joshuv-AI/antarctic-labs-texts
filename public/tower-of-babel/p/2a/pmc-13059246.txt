Cardiac ochronosis with severe aortic and mitral valve stenosis: a challenge case
Authors: Mukan Kağan Kuş, Burak Bozkurt, Talib Durak, İsmail Yücesin Arslan, Mehmet Kaplan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ochronosis is a hereditary disorder characterized by black pigmentation in tissues. Cardiac ochronosis is most commonly associated with aortic stenosis, while mitral stenosis is a less frequently observed involvement. In this case presentation, we will discuss ochronosis diagnosed during open heart surgery in a patient who had not been previously diagnosed. Our patient underwent surgery for aortic valve and mitral valve replacement. The aortic root, mitral valve, and aortic-mitral curtain were found to be calcified, and a mandatory Commando procedure was performed. The coronary buttons could not be mobilized, and the Cabrol procedure was applied. In cases of ochronosis, it is a fact that decalcification can lead to significant complications, and the involvement of the aortic-mitral curtain must also be considered by the surgeon.

Introduction

Ochronosis is an autosomal recessive tyrosine metabolism disorder resulting from a defect in the homogentisic acid oxidase enzyme. One of its characteristic features is the darkening of urine upon exposure to air. Clinically, it most commonly presents with spondyloarthropathy, arthritis in large joints, black pigmentation in cartilage, and alkaptonuria [1]. Although there is no definitive data on the frequency of cardiac ochronosis, its incidence ranges between 1:250,000 and 1:1,000,000 in many literatures [3].The accumulation of homogentisic acid (HGA) in tissues typically manifests after the fourth decade of life. According to most authors, alkaptonuria affects areas with high mechanical stress. In the skeletal system, joints are commonly affected, while in the cardiovascular system, the aortic valve is most frequently involved [4]. A large majority of patients have a history of joint prosthesis surgery. Cardiac ochronosis is most frequently associated with aortic stenosis, though mitral stenosis is less commonly observed. Coronary arteries may also be involved simultaneously. In our case, the involvement of both the aortic valve and the mitral valve represents a rare instance of cardiac ochronosis.

Case

A 66-year-old male patient presented with dyspnea and echocardiography showed severe aortic stenosis with 67/47 mmHG gradient and severe mitral stenosis 24/14 gradient and mitral annular calcification. The patient’s ascending aorta measured 35 mm. His medical history included bilateral knee prosthesis surgery. Preoperative coronary angiography revealed no coronary artery disease. His ejection fraction was 60%. The patient had no previously diagnosed systemic diseases.

The patient was taken for surgery, and median sternotomy was performed. The black appearance of the sternum periosteum raised suspicion for alkaptonuria. After arterial cannulation from the ascending aorta and bicaval venous cannulation, an aortic cross-clamp was placed. Cardiac arrest was achieved with cold blood cardioplegia.

Aortotomy was performed. The endothelial and medial layers of the aorta were completely black and fragile Fig 1. The aortic valve was thickened and calcified with a trileaflet structure. Valve resection was initiated. The aortic valve, mitral valve anterior leaflet, aortic-mitral continuity, and coronary ostia were found to be block calcified and black-pigmented. During decalcification, aortic annulus and aorticomitral curtain scattered like sand and it was destructive and unstable tissue for suturing. With the preservation of the right and left fibrous trigones, the aortic valve, mitral valve, and aortic-mitral curtain were resected Fig 2. A mitral prosthetic valve was placed using pledgeted sutures on the posterior annulus of the mitral valve. A Dacron graft was configured to form the mitral valve anterior annulus, the non-coronary aortic annulus, and the roof of the left atrium (Commando procedure). Since the coronary ostia could not be mobilized, interposition of the left and right coronary arteries was performed with an 8-mm Dacron graft. The intercoronary Dacron graft was side-to-side anastomosed to the ascending aortic graft (Cabrol procedure). We use 23/26 no Carbomedics valved conduit and 27 no Carbomedics for mitral valve. The distal aortic anastomosis was performed under cross-clamp. The cross-clamp was removed. Cross clamp time was 387 min. Despite prolonged cardiopulmonary bypass support and high-dose inotropes, the patient was unable to achieve adequate contractility and succumbed to death while remaining in a post-cardiotomy shock state and total operative time was 16 h. Pathological examination revealed involvement of both the aortic and mitral valves, with ochronotic infiltration affecting the valve leaflets and annular structures, closely associated with extensive calcification extending to the aorto-mitral curtain Fig3.

Discussion

Ochronosis is a rare hereditary disorder caused by a defect in the homogentisic acid oxidase enzyme, leading to a disruption in tyrosine metabolism. This results in the accumulation of homogentisic acid (HGA) in various tissues, particularly in connective tissues such as cartilage, skin, ocular, genitourinary and the cardiovascular system, where it manifests as black pigmentation. The accumulation causes progressive tissue damage, with a particularly destructive course in connective tissues. The condition is inherited in an autosomal recessive manner [2]. A characteristic feature of ochronosis is the darkening of urine when exposed to air, which occurs due to the alkalinization of homogentisic acid. The most common clinical presentation of the disease is arthropathy, with patients typically undergoing joint replacement surgeries, especially knee or hip prosthesis, around the age of 55 [5].

The disease remains asymptomatic during adolescence and early adulthood, which contributes to its late diagnosis. Treatment with phenylalanine and tyrosine-low diet nitisinone and vitamin C has been attempted, but their effectiveness is still uncertain [3]. Most patients experience a relatively mild disease course, although progressive tissue damage due to the accumulation of HGA is inevitable.

Taniguchi et al. reviewed 28 cases of cardiac ochronosis in the literature and found that all of them had aortic stenosis, with 6 cases also having concomitant ischemic heart disease [5]. Cardiac involvement in ochronosis is most frequently observed as aortic stenosis, caused by the calcification and thickening of the aortic valve. Similar to the literature, aortic valve involvement is observed in our case; however, the mitral valve involvement, which has only been reported in a few cases previously, makes this case more rare and complex. The presence of concomitant severe mitral stenosis and widespread calcification increased the extent of the surgical field required for double valve replacement, which also led to a prolonged cross-clamp time.

Preoperative echocardiography did not show any sign of ocronotic tissue. If surgeon has any suspect of this disease transesophageal echocardiography(TEE) and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging(MRI) may be helpful. Calcification-like but atypical echogenity can be seen at TEE and valvular low signal intensity on cardiac MRI. Although cardiac ochronosis is usually diagnosed intraoperatively, gold standart biochemical confirmation can be achieved preoperatively by detecting elevated urinary homogentisic acid levels.If mitral annular calcification is observed in preoperative echocardiography and the patient has a history of joint prosthesis surgery, cardiac ochronosis should be suspected, and the possibility that the extent of surgical resection may increase should be considered. If cardiac ochronosis is identified preoperatively, transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) may represent a viable alternative, particularly in high-risk patients, by avoiding extensive surgical manipulation of heavily infiltrated and rigid valvular tissue. In cases where the diagnosis is established intraoperatively, a more conservative surgical approach may be considered, including limited resection or the use of sutureless aortic valve prostheses to minimize annular trauma or staged approach. In the presence of severe mitral annular calcification, valve implantation over a pericardial patch or limited intervention rather than complete replacement may be an appropriate strategy to reduce the risk of annular disruption and perioperative complications. In younger patients, the use of a mechanical valve would be more appropriate due to the potential accumulation of alkaptonuric deposits on the bioprosthetic valve.

While most reported cases of cardiac ochronosis describe isolated aortic valve involvement with favorable outcomes after conventional valve replacement, the present case was distinguished by concomitant mitral valve involvement and marked tissue rigidity, leading to significant technical challenges, particularly during coronary mobilization. These features highlight the heterogeneity of cardiac ochronosis and underscore the need for careful surgical planning in such complex presentations.

Conclusion

Cardiac ochronosis should be suspected in patients presenting with aortic stenosis, especially when there is a history of early-onset joint prostheses or systemic pigmentation. Preoperative diagnosis can be supported by 24-hour urine homogentisic acid measurement, genetic testing, or tissue examination. Identifying the condition before surgery allows careful planning of valve selection and intraoperative strategies, potentially improving surgical outcomes in these high-risk patients.
