Temperature-independent and photocrosslinkable hydrolyzed GelMA with tunable mechanics for biofabrication
Authors: JuneHyun Kyeong, Dayi Jeong, Hojae Bae
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThermoresponsive and photo-crosslinkable hydrogels are highly desirable for biofabrication; however, conventional gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) exhibited thermal gelation below 30 °C and high viscosity, restricting its compatibility with concentration-dependent tunability and lower critical solution temperature-type systems. To address these issues, hydrolyzed GelMA (hGelMA) was developed by subjecting GelMA to controlled enzymatic degradation, effectively eliminating thermal gelation while maintaining photo-crosslinkability and enabling broader control of the mechanical properties through concentration adjustment. The resulting hGelMA was characterized for viscosity, mechanical tunability, and compatibility with digital light processing bioprinting. Cell compatibility was evaluated, and the ability of hGelMA to integrate with thermoresponsive hydrogels, such as hydroxybutyl-methacrylated chitosan, was assessed to demonstrate its suitability for composite bioink systems. hGelMA showed significantly reduced viscosity, supported high-resolution digital light processing bioprinting across a wide stiffness range, and maintained excellent cell compatibility. In addition, its temperature-independent behavior facilitated its integration with thermoresponsive hydrogels exhibiting lower critical solution temperature (LCST) behavior, thereby broadening its potential for composite bioink systems. These findings indicate that hGelMA could serve as a practical and adaptable material for use in bioink formulations for tissue engineering and bioprinting applications.Keywords: Hydrolyzed GelMA, Tunable stiffness, Hydrogels, Biofabrication, Bioprinting, Digital light processing

Introduction

Hydrogels have emerged as key platform materials for various biomedical applications, including tissue engineering, drug delivery, wound healing, and 3D bioprinting, owing to their high water content and extracellular matrix-like properties [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA), which is derived from gelatin, has attracted significant attention as an optimal hydrogel for 3D tissue modeling, customized biofabrication, and in vitro tissue culture. GelMA maintains its biocompatibility, cell-adhesion properties, and biodegradability while incorporating photo-crosslinking capabilities through the introduction of methacrylic groups [5,6]. However, both gelatin and GelMA undergo sol-gel transition below 30 °C due to their unique crosslinking mechanism, which leads to the formation of partial triple-helix structures [7]. This characteristic hinders manipulation at room temperature and restricts the development of temperature-responsive composite materials. In addition, GelMA exhibits high viscosity at room temperature, limiting its application in bioprinting processes that require high-concentration printing and precise microstructure control [8,9]. These temperature-dependent properties present major limitations to the broader application of GelMA-based hydrogels.

Various approaches have been explored to overcome these limitations, including chemical modification and the use of composite materials. A high degree of substitution (DS) in GelMA can lower the transition temperature, as methacrylate groups create steric hindrance that disrupts the hydrogen bonding necessary for triple-helix structure formation and interferes with the hydrogen bonding potential of hydroxyproline residues [10]. Additional strategies involve reacting GelMA in urea-containing buffers to decrease the transition temperature by weakening protein-protein interactions, or mixing with synthetic polymers such as F127 diacrylate, followed by tannic acid treatment to improve mechanical properties and resistance to deformation [11,12]. However, these approaches may introduce challenges such as reduced biocompatibility, complex post-processing of composite materials, and increased costs.

In the case of the gelatin precursor of GelMA, hydrolytic strategies employing acids, alkalis, enzymes, ultrasound, high pressure, or high temperature have been investigated to generate peptides with various biological activities, including antimicrobial and antioxidant properties [13,14]. However, peptides generated by hydrolysis have very low molecular weights (LM), and non-specific or excessive reduction in molecular weight significantly compromises the photo-crosslinking capability and mechanical integrity of GelMA [15]. Moreover, several studies have reported the generation of LM GelMA through chemical or physical treatments to reduce viscosity and facilitate handling at low to moderate concentrations [16,17]. While these approaches have demonstrated technical feasibility, concerns remain regarding potential protein denaturation, uncontrolled degradation, and limited reproducibility. Accordingly, strategies are required that achieve controlled molecular weight reduction under mild and well-defined conditions while preserving methacryloyl substitution, thereby ensuring predictable viscosity modulation, suppression of undesired thermal gelation, and reinforcement of mechanical robustness in GelMA-based bioinks.

Hydrogels exhibiting a lower critical solution temperature (LCST) undergo reversible phase transitions in response to temperature changes, making them suitable for use as thermally responsive systems [18,19]. Prototypical synthetic LCST polymers include poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAAm), poly(N-vinylcaprolactam), and Pluronic F127; thermoresponsive chitosan derivatives such as hydroxybutyl chitosan (HBC) have also been explored [[20], [21], [22]]. Nevertheless, these polymers exhibit inherent limitations, including fixed transition temperatures near physiological conditions, restricted biodegradability, and inadequate biocompatibility [23,24]. In practical applications, the restricted thermal range often leads to reversible swelling-shrinkage, compromising dimensional stability during bioprinting and culture, while the lack of intrinsic adhesion motifs necessitates additional biofunctionalization.

To address these limitations, hybrid LCST systems have been engineered by integrating thermoresponsive networks with bioactive or photocrosslinkable matrices (e.g., PNIPAAm–biopolymer composites, graft-modified polysaccharides, or LCST multilayers stabilized through covalent crosslinking) [25,26]. These approaches have improved cell-material interactions and enabled partial modulation of mechanical properties [25,27]. Despite these advances, many hybrid systems continue to exhibit restricted cell proliferation in the absence of supplemental ligands and insufficient stiffness under physiological conditions, unless additional reinforcement strategies are employed [[28], [29], [30]]. Furthermore, the dependence on multiple components and stabilization chemistries increases compositional complexity, thereby compromising reproducibility and hindering scale-up. Accordingly, there remains a compelling need for hybrid LCST platforms that integrate high cytocompatibility with broadly tunable mechanical properties, while preserving structural integrity and enabling controlled dimensional responses under thermal cycling.

In this study, we present hydrolyzed GelMA (hGelMA), engineered through controlled enzymatic hydrolysis to reduce molecular weight while preserving methacryloyl functionality. This approach eliminates undesired thermal gelation, decreases viscosity, and broadens the range of applicable concentrations for tuning mechanical properties. Furthermore, hGelMA was integrated with Hydroxybutyl-methacrylated chitosan (HBC-MA) to construct hybrid LCST hydrogels with enhanced cytocompatibility and mechanically adaptable networks. Collectively, these advances establish a versatile bioink platform that addresses critical limitations of conventional GelMA and LCST systems, offering broad potential for 3D bioprinting and tissue engineering applications.

Materials and methods

Materials

Protamex® (protease from Bacillus sp.), gelatin (Type A, 300 bloom from porcine skin, suitable for electrophoresis and cell culture), chitosan (50–190 kDa, 20–300 cP, 1 wt% in 1 % acetic acid, 75–85 % deacetylated), 1,2-epoxybutane, methacrylic anhydride, lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP, ≥95 %), lysozyme (40,000 U/mg) and the EZBlue® Gel Staining Reagent were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Tartrazine, used as a photoabsorber, was purchased from GreenTech (Anyang-si, Daejeon, South Korea). Fetal bovine serum, penicillin/streptomycin, high-glucose Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), Dulbecco's phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS), phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4), and a 0.05 % trypsin–EDTA solution were procured from WelGene (Daegu, Gyeongbuk, South Korea). Anhydrous sodium carbonate and sodium bicarbonate were purchased from Daejung Chemical and Metals (Siheung-si, Gyeonggi-do, South Korea). The StarPAGE Bis-Tris Protein Gel (4–20 %, 12-well), a protein marker (GPM 2700), and the MOPS-SDS running buffer were obtained from Genestar (Seoul, South Korea). The Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) was purchased from Dojindo Laboratories (Kumamoto, Japan). All other chemicals used in this study were of analytical grade.

Synthesis of GelMA

GelMA was synthesized as previously described [31]. Briefly, 10 % (w/v) Type A porcine skin gelatin was dissolved in a 0.25 M carbonate-bicarbonate (CB) buffer (100 mL) at 50 °C. The pH of the solution was adjusted to 9.4 using 5 M NaOH, followed by the gradual addition of 1 % (v/v) methacrylic anhydride at a rate of 1 mL/min under continuous mechanical stirring (500 rpm) at 55 °C for 4 h. The reaction was terminated by adjusting the pH to 7.4 with 1 M HCl. The mixture was dialyzed against distilled water for 24 h at 50 °C using a Sartorius® Vivaflow 200 tangential flow filtration system equipped with a 10-kDa polypropylene membrane. The purified GelMA was lyophilized for 4 days, yielding a solid product with a purity exceeding 85 %.

Synthesis of HBC-MA

HBC-MA was synthesized as previously described [20]. Briefly, hydroxybutyl chitosan (HBC) was prepared by dissolving chitosan (1 g) in 0.1 M acetic acid solution (60 mL). The pH was adjusted to 6.0 using 5 M NaOH, followed by the addition of 1,2-epoxybutane (20 mL, 99 %), and the reaction was conducted at 55 °C for 24 h. Residual 1,2-epoxybutane was removed by centrifugation, and the resulting HBC solution was lyophilized for 4 days.

For methacrylation, HBC (1 g) was dissolved in distilled water (60 mL) overnight. Then, 15 % (v/v) methacrylic anhydride was gradually added dropwise at 1 mL/min, initiating a reaction under continuous mechanical stirring (500 rpm) at pH 7.0 and 55 °C for 4 h. Unreacted methacrylic anhydride was removed via dialysis using a 12–14-kDa MWCO membrane in distilled water for 7 days. Purified HBC-MA was lyophilized for 4 days.

Preparation of hGelMA bioinks

hGelMA was prepared using GelMA and Protamex® enzyme. A 30 % (w/v) GelMA solution was completely dissolved in DPBS (5 mL, pH 7.4) at 50 °C. Protamex® was added at concentrations of 0 (P0, control), 0.125 (P125), 0.25 (P250), 0.5 (P500), or 0.75 mg/mL (P750), followed by reaction in a dry bath at 55 °C for 1 h on a shaker (100 rpm). The enzymatic activity was terminated by heating to 95 °C for 10 min, then rapidly cooled on ice. The hGelMA samples were lyophilized for 7 days. The lyophilized hGelMA was then dissolved in deionized water containing 0.5 % (w/v) LAP (photoinitiator) and 1 % (w/v) yellow pigment (tartrazine, photoabsorber).

Preparation of HBC-MA/hGelMA bioinks

HBC-MA and hGelMA (HBC-MA/hGelMA) bioinks were prepared using HBC-MA and P500, respectively. First, a 2 % (w/v) HBC solution was prepared, followed by the addition of P500 at varying concentrations. The sample compositions were as follows: 2 % (w/v) HBC-MA (H2, control), 2 % (w/v) HBC-MA with 5 % (w/v) P500 (H2hG5), 2 % (w/v) HBC-MA with 10 % (w/v) P500 (H2hG10), and 2 % (w/v) HBC-MA with 15 % (w/v) P500 (H2hG15). For the P500-containing samples (H2hG5, H2hG10, and H2hG15), the solvent was prepared by mixing DPBS and deionized water. The mixing ratio was adjusted for each sample based on the calculated concentrations of pre-existing salts and ions. The bioinks were alternately dissolved at 4 and 30 °C to ensure complete solubilization. Once fully dissolved, 0.5 % (w/v) LAP (photoinitiator) and 1 % (w/v) yellow pigment (tartrazine; photoabsorber) were added.

1H NMR spectroscopy

1H Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy was used to determine the DS of GelMA and HBC-MA. Gelatin, GelMA, and hGelMA were dissolved in deuterium oxide (D2O) at a concentration of 1.5 % (w/v), while chitosan, HBC, and HBC-MA were dissolved in D2O at a concentration of 1 % (w/v). All samples were analyzed using a 500-MHz Fourier transform NMR spectrometer (Varian, Palo Alto, CA, USA). DS values were calculated by integration after baseline correction using MestRenova software. For the DS calculation, the polymer concentration was kept identical to that used at the time of measurement.

SDS-PAGE

The electrophoretic patterns of hGelMA were analyzed using a Bis-Tris protein gel (StarPAGE Bis-Tris Protein Gel) with a 4–20 % (w/v) concentration gradient. The hGelMA samples (P0, P125, P250, P500, and P750) were mixed with a sample loading buffer at a 4:1 (v/v) ratio, heat-treated at 95 °C for 5 min, and rapidly cooled. The prepared samples were then loaded into gel wells. Electrophoresis was performed at a constant voltage of 70 V for 1 h. Following SDS removal by washing with distilled water, the gels were stained with the Gel Staining Reagent (EZBlue®) for 1 day. The molecular weights of the proteins were estimated using a protein marker (GPM 2700).

Photorheological analysis

Photorheology tests were conducted using a HAAKE MARS 40 rheometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) equipped with an OmniCure® LX 505 UV light source (Lumen Dynamics, Mississauga, ON, Canada) emitting 405-nm light at an intensity of 2.45 mW/cm2. The bioink was loaded into a 100-μm gap between the light-transmitting glass and rheometer base. hGelMA and HBC-MA/hGelMA samples were equilibrated for 1 min at 37 and 20 °C, respectively. Oscillatory tests were performed at a frequency of 1 Hz and an oscillatory shear strain of 0.01 %.

Rheological analysis of the apparent viscosity and gelling temperature

The apparent viscosity and gelling temperature were measured using a HAAKE MARS 40 rheometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The apparent viscosity, defined as the ratio of shear stress to shear rate, was determined using rotational rheology in the controlled rate mode with a shear rate sweep ranging from 0.01 to 100 s−1 at 37 °C. Measurements were performed using a 35-mm parallel plate geometry with a 1-mm gap. hGelMA samples (1 mL) were loaded and equilibrated for 5 min to ensure thermal stability. The gelling temperature, defined as the crossover point where the storage (G′) and loss (G″) moduli intersect, was analyzed using oscillatory rheology in the controlled deformation mode with a temperature sweep. hGelMA samples were cooled from 40 to 4 °C, while HBC-MA/hGelMA samples were heated from 4 to 40 °C, both at a rate of 1 °C/min. Measurements were conducted at a strain of 0.1 % and frequency of 1 Hz using a 35-mm parallel plate geometry with a 0.5-mm gap. Samples (0.5 mL) were loaded and equilibrated for 5 min to ensure thermal stability. All rheological experiments were performed at least three times (n = 3).

Differential scanning calorimetery (DSC)

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was performed on P0, P125, P250, P500, and P750 using a DSC 200 F3 (Netzsch, Germany) at the Biopolymer Research Center for Advanced Materials at Sejong University (NFEC-2023-07-289071). Heating scans were conducted from 0 to 80 °C at 5 °C/min under a nitrogen atmosphere. The samples were analyzed as lyophilized (freeze-dried) powders.

Modeling and DLP printing

3D modeling for evaluating physical properties, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), swelling ratio, printability, degradation, cell encapsulation, and tissue-engineered scaffolds was performed using Fusion 360 (Autodesk, San Rafael, CA, USA). Prior to digital light processing (DLP) printing, hGelMA and HBC-MA/hGelMA bioinks were preheated to 37 and 20 °C, respectively. DLP printing was performed with a layer thickness of 100 μm using an IM2 DLP 3D printer (Carima, Seoul, Republic of Korea) with an optical pixel size of 30 μm. The crosslinking time for each layer was determined based on the optimized photorheological data from previous measurements. After printing, samples were immediately washed with DPBS to remove residual bioink.

Compressive modulus analysis

The compressive modulus values of hGelMA and HBC-MA/hGelMA were measured using a CT3 Texture Analyzer (Brookfield, Toronto, ON, Canada). Cylindrical hGelMA samples (8 mm diameter × 2 mm height) were fabricated via DLP printing. HBC-MA/hGelMA samples were printed using the same DLP printing method. All samples were tested immediately after printing using a 12.7-mm diameter probe, with a trigger load of 0.05 N and test speed of 0.05 mm/s. The compressive modulus was calculated from the linear elastic region (5–15 % strain) of the stress–strain curve. All compression tests were performed at least five times (n = 5).

SEM

Cylindrical hydrogel structures (8 mm diameter × 2 mm height), prepared as described in a previous section, were used for SEM imaging. Hydrogels were flash-frozen at −70 °C to preserve their porous architecture. Prior to lyophilization, the frozen samples were bisected with a precooled scalpel. Cross-sectional surfaces were imaged using an SU-8010 SEM (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). The pore size was quantified from SEM images at 300 × magnification using ImageJ software.

Printability test

Printability was assessed to compare GelMA and hydrolyzed GelMA (hGelMA). Two 3D models were designed for resolution testing: (i) an array of circular through-holes and (ii) an array of square through-holes. Each model contained one hole at each nominal diameter of 900, 800, 700, 600, 500, 400, 300, 200, 100, 90, 80, and 70 μm. Specimen thickness was 1 mm. Constructs were fabricated by DLP at 30 % (w/v) for each material, using the printing conditions described in Section 2.11. After printing, samples were rinsed with DPBS, imaged by optical microscopy, and hole diameters were quantified in ImageJ to calculate the deviation between measured and nominal diameters. All procedures were performed in triplicate (n = 3).

Subcutaneous implantation

Short-term in vivo biocompatibility testing was conducted to evaluate immune rejection and in vivo compatibility of GelMA and hydrolyzed GelMA (hGelMA; P500). Both materials were prepared at 30 % (w/v) and fabricated into cylindrical constructs (6 mm diameter × 1.5 mm height) using digital light processing (DLP). Printed 3D constructs were stored in DPBS containing 1 % (v/v) penicillin–streptomycin until use. C57BL/6 mice were used. GelMA (positive control) and hGelMA (experimental) constructs were implanted subcutaneously on the dorsal side of randomly assigned mice, and a negative control received no implant (n = 4). After 7 days, mice were euthanized, tissues were collected, and sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histological assessment. The study was approved by the ANRP Science Inc. IACUC.

Temperature-responsive swelling and shrinking test

To investigate the thermally responsive contraction behavior, HBC-MA/hGelMA samples were printed into cylindrical 3D structures (8 mm diameter × 2 mm height) using DLP printing. Samples were swollen in PBS at 37 °C for 24 h until equilibrium. All samples underwent three temperature cycles between 25 and 37 °C, with diameter measurements performed after each temperature interval. The diameter retention ratio at each time point was calculated as the ratio of the diameter at each time (Lt) to the initial diameter (L0), expressed as Lt/L0.

Cell encapsulation

GelMA was prepared at 10 % (w/v) by dissolving in DPBS. HBC-MA/hGelMA samples were prepared as described in a previous section. NIH-3T3 cells and HaCaT cells were suspended in each bioink at a density of 2 × 106 cells/mL prior to printing. The CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan) was used to quantify metabolic activity at 450 nm. Results

1H NMR spectroscopy

The DS of hGelMA was determined using 1H NMR spectroscopy. Samples containing 30 % (w/v) GelMA were treated with Protamex® at concentrations of 0 (P0, control), 0.125 (P125), 0.25 (P250), 0.5 (P500), and 0.75 mg/mL (P750). Comparative analysis with gelatin revealed distinct functional group signatures (Fig. 1A). Peaks at 5.3–5.7 ppm (a + b) are attributed to acrylic protons from methacrylate and methacrylamide vinyl groups, while a distinct signal at 1.9 ppm (d) corresponds to the methyl groups in methacryloyl moieties. The lysine amine proton signal (2.9–3.1 ppm, c) exhibits a reduced intensity in GelMA and hGelMA, consistent with methacryloylation at primary amines.

(B) Schematic diagram illustrating the enzymatic cleavage mechanism of Protamex, a serine endoprotease, acting on GelMA. (C) SDS-PAGE analysis showing a decrease in the molecular weight with increasing Protamex concentration. (D) Apparent viscosity of hGelMA samples decreases with increasing Protamex concentration.

The DS values were determined by integrating the proton signals at 2.9–3.1 ppm relative to the backbone groups and were consistent across all hydrolyzed samples, ranging from 85.6 % to 87.4 % (Supplementary Table 1 and Fig. 1) [31]. Notably, no significant reduction in DS was observed even at higher levels of enzymatic hydrolysis, confirming that the methacryloyl functional groups were preserved during peptide bond cleavage. These results demonstrate that enzymatic hydrolysis selectively cleaves peptide bonds while preserving methacryloyl functional groups, thereby maintaining the photo-crosslinking capability of hGelMA.

Molecular weight and apparent viscosity of hGelMA

Protamex®, a serine endoprotease, exhibits broad substrate specificity by cleaving internal peptide bonds via a serine residue at its active site, effectively reducing the molecular weight of GelMA (Fig. 1B) [32,33]. To evaluate the molecular weight distribution as a function of the protease concentration, SDS-PAGE was performed using a gradient gel (Fig. 1C). Native gelatin exhibited a broad molecular weight distribution above 35 kDa, with a significant proportion exceeding the upper detection limit of 245 kDa. In contrast, GelMA without protease treatment (P0) displayed a distribution ranging from 15 to 245 kDa, with fewer HM components (>245 kDa) and a dominant distribution range between 35 and 180 kDa, compared with unmodified gelatin.

In the protease-treated samples (P125, P250, P500, and P750), HM fractions (>245 kDa) were nearly absent. As the protease concentration increased, a clear shift toward LM was observed. P125 and P250 exhibited molecular weight distributions shifted below 245 kDa relative to P0, whereas those of P500 and P750 showed a distinct shift toward fractions below 140 kDa, indicating a greater extent of proteolytic degradation. Specifically, P125 and P250 exhibited increased intensities at and below 75 kDa, whereas P500 and P750 showed marked shifts below 45 kDa. These results differ substantially from the molecular weight profile of P0, which showed a broad distribution from 15 to 245 kDa, or even higher.

To further assess the effect of molecular weight reduction on the rheological behavior, the apparent viscosity of the hGelMA samples was measured under identical conditions using a shear-rate ramp (Fig. 1D). P0 exhibited the highest apparent viscosity, followed by progressively lower values for P125, P250, P500, and P750. Higher protease concentrations led to lower molecular weight and reduced viscosity.

Evaluation of the low-temperature applicability and photo-crosslinking behavior of hGelMA

Rheological analyses were conducted to determine the minimum protease concentration required for low-temperature processability of hGelMA (Fig. 2A). Since the sol-gel transition of GelMA occurs upon cooling, temperature sweeps were performed from 40 to 4 °C. The gelling temperature was defined as the crossover point where the storage (G′) and loss (G″) moduli intersect. At temperatures above the transition point, all hGelMA samples exhibited a higher G″ than G'. As the temperature decreased, P0, P125, and P250 exhibited a more pronounced increase in the G′ compared with the G″, whereas this behavior was not observed in P500 and P750. The shear stress values of each sample across different temperatures exhibited trends consistent with those of apparent viscosity. Notably, at 4 °C, P500 and P750, neither of which underwent thermal crosslinking, showed a significant difference in shear stress (Supplementary Fig. 2). Crossover points were identified at 23.7, 16.6, and 7.8 °C for P0, P125, and P250, respectively, indicating gelation, while no crossover was detected for P500 and P750, suggesting the absence of thermally induced gelation at low temperatures.

To further validate these findings, tube inversion assays were conducted under heating–cooling cycles (Fig. 2B). Consistent with the rheological results, P0, P125, and P250 exhibited physical gelation upon cooling, whereas P500 and P750 remained in the sol state, demonstrating the loss of thermal gelation behavior associated with triple-helix formation [34]. In parallel, differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) analysis revealed a progressive broadening and diminished intensity of the endothermic peaks (Supplementary Fig. 3). These results indicate that protease-mediated hydrolysis reduced the stability of triple helices and disrupted their cooperative melting [35,36]. Consequently, the thermal transition became increasingly indistinct and was ultimately absent in highly hydrolyzed samples (P500 and P750), thus confirming the complete loss of thermal gelation in hGelMA.

Although protease activity does not directly affect the methacryloyl substitution sites on GelMA, it reduces the molecular weight and decreases effective chain interactions, thereby impacting the photo-crosslinking behavior [37]. Therefore, the photo-crosslinking characteristics of samples with different protease concentrations were evaluated (Fig. 2C–G). The photo-crossover point—defined as the time at which the G′ and G″ moduli intersect during light exposure—was measured for each sample. P0, P125, P250, P500, and P750 reached their photo-crossover points at 15.7, 17.6, 19.2, 20.2, and 21.7 s, respectively. While all samples retained their photo-crosslinkable properties, the photo-crosslinking time increased with higher protease concentrations, implying slower network formation due to reduced molecular weight. This trend was consistently reproduced in experiments conducted at different time points, showing a maximum variation of less than 2 % between batches (Supplementary Fig. 4).

In photo-crosslinking rheology, the storage modulus G′ measured under linear SAOS is frequency-dependent [38], and because the compressive modulus is sensitive to boundary conditions, geometric scaling, and timescale, the theoretical correspondence between G′ and bulk compression holds only when temperature, the characteristic timescale (frequency/strain rate), and the linear regime are matched [39]. In practice, differences in test protocols—e.g., geometry and specimen dimensions, definition of the LVR, and destructive vs. non-destructive shear—shift effective boundaries, scaling, and timescale, so measured values can deviate from theory. Notably, for thin specimens with a small radius, the required torque can approach the instrument's minimum torque limit, artifactual lowering of the measured G′ [40].

GelMA can form a double-network gel structure through physical crosslinking at low temperatures and chemical crosslinking under light irradiation. However, because of partial hydrogen bonding and triple-helix formation at low temperatures, GelMA cannot be mixed with LCST systems in dual-functionality bioinks. By contrast, in hGelMA, increasing the protease concentration led to a stepwise reduction in the molecular weight, which in turn disrupted triple-helix formation, abrogated thermally induced physical crosslinking at low temperatures, and restricted the crosslinking mechanism to photo-crosslinking (Fig. 2H). For compatibility with LCST-based systems, photo-crosslinkable bioinks must have suppressed thermal gelation at low temperatures while retaining their photo-crosslinkability. Among the evaluated conditions, P500 and P750 met both criteria, suggesting their suitability for use in LCST-integrated biofabrication (Fig. 2I).

Evaluation of the mechanical properties of hGelMA

SEM analysis

SEM analysis demonstrated pronounced differences in the pore architecture as a function of both the molecular weight and polymer concentration (Fig. 3A). As illustrated in Fig. 3A (a), HM GelMA (P0) exhibited a densely crosslinked network owing to its high degree of polymerization. This was reflected in the SEM image of 30 % (w/v) P0 (Fig. 3A (b)), which displayed the most compact microstructure among the 30 % hGelMA samples. By contrast, LM GelMA (P125–P750) exhibited a reduced network-forming capability owing to its shorter chain lengths, resulting in a lower crosslinking density (Fig. 3A (c)). As the protease concentration increased from P125 to P750, larger pore sizes were observed, indicating a further reduction in the crosslinking density (Fig. 3A (d–g)). These SEM micrographs clearly demonstrate that protease-induced molecular weight reduction directly affects the pore size. Increasing the concentration of LM GelMA enhanced the chain density and intermolecular packing, facilitating the formation of a more cohesive microstructure despite its lower molecular mass (LM) (Fig. 3A (h)) [41]. SEM images of 40 % and 50 % (w/v) P500 confirmed this observation, with the 50 % formulation exhibiting a notably compact architecture, which was even more condensed than that of 30 % P0 (Fig. 3A (i, j)). Quantitative analysis of the pore dimensions further supported these morphological observations. The average pore size of P0 was 25.4 ± 3.5 μm, representing the most compact microstructure, whereas LM GelMA samples exhibited progressively larger pore sizes with increasing protease concentration: 64.4 ± 10.1 (P125), 101.3 ± 7.7 (P250), 124.3 ± 11.2 (P500), and 154.7 ± 15.8 μm (P750) (Fig. 3B). These results demonstrate a clear molecular weight-dependent variation in pore size, suggesting that microstructural porosity can be effectively tuned via the protease-mediated degradation of GelMA. The 40 % (w/v) P500 sample exhibited an average pore size of 43.6 ± 9.4 μm, while 50 % (w/v) P500 showed a pore size of 20.5 ± 1.7 μm, indicating a denser, more compact structure at higher concentrations (Fig. 3C).

Mechanical properties of hGelMA

To evaluate the effect of enzymatic degradation on the physical and mechanical properties of hGelMA, we investigated the swelling behavior, compressive modulus, and 3D printability of the hydrogels at different protease concentrations and polymer concentrations. In the swelling behavior analysis, all 30 % (w/v) hGelMA samples reached swelling equilibrium within 2 h, which was then maintained. Among the samples, P750, which was treated with the highest protease concentration, exhibited the highest equilibrium swelling ratio (SEq) of 5.04 ± 0.14 %, whereas P0 had the lowest SEq at 3.58 ± 0.12 %. The SEq decreased progressively with decreasing protease concentration across the hGelMA samples (Fig. 4A).

To evaluate mechanical properties as a function of protease concentration, compressive modulus measurements were performed on the hGelMA samples (Fig. 4B). Among the 30 % (w/v) hGelMA groups, the P0 sample exhibited the highest compressive modulus at 621.2 ± 22.7 kPa. As the protease concentration increased, the compressive modulus of P125, P250, P500, and P750 gradually decreased to 489.3 ± 25.4, 387.2 ± 35.9, 311.1 ± 25.6, and 186.4 ± 20.6 kPa, respectively. Protease-treated samples exhibited reduced mechanical stiffness, corresponding to a decrease in molecular weight.

Notably, although 30 % (w/v) P500 exhibited a lower compressive modulus than that of P0, increasing its polymer concentration to 40 % and 50 % (w/v) significantly enhanced the stiffness to 764.0 ± 84.8 and 1233.1 ± 32.5 kPa, respectively. These values surpassed those of 30 % (w/v) P0, indicating that LM hydrogels at higher concentrations can exhibit superior compressive properties compared with those of HM hydrogels at lower concentrations.

The trends of the compressive modulus with respect to protease concentration were consistent with the pore size variations observed in SEM images (Fig. 4). At the lowest protease concentration (P0), the smallest pore size (25.4 ± 3.5 μm) and highest compressive modulus (621.2 ± 22.7 kPa) were observed, whereas at the highest concentration (P750), the largest pore size (154.7 ± 15.8 μm) and lowest modulus (186.4 ± 20.6 kPa) were obtained. Thus, at the same concentration (30 %), higher protease levels consistently resulted in larger pores and lower compressive modulus. However, in the high-concentration P500 groups, a discrepancy between pore size and compressive modulus was observed, which may be attributed to the distinct structural characteristics of hGelMA. In conventional GelMA, the formation of triple helices at low temperatures is considered to contribute to additional physical crosslinking, resulting in a denser network structure and reduced pore sizes [42]. In contrast, enzymatic degradation may disrupt these triple helix-forming domains in hGelMA, potentially leading to reduced physical crosslinking and increased pore sizes. Nevertheless, at sufficiently high polymer concentrations, it is likely that increased chain density and effective photo-crosslinking compensate for the loss of physical crosslinking, allowing hGelMA hydrogels to maintain mechanically robust structures despite the presence of relatively large pores.

Evaluation of the potential of hGelMA for tunable mechanical properties using 3D bioprinting

To evaluate the potential of hGelMA hydrogels with tunable mechanical properties for use in photo-crosslinking-based bioprinting, DLP-based 3D bioprinting was employed to fabricate tissue-mimetic constructs exhibiting distinct stiffness profiles. The compressive modulus of hGelMA was effectively modulated by adjusting the polymer concentration, yielding values of 30.1 ± 1.9 kPa (P500 15 %), 64.6 ± 6.2 kPa (P500 20 %), and 1233.1 ± 32.5 kPa (P500 50 %) (Fig. 4C). These values correspond to the mechanical properties required for the lungs (soft tissue), heart (muscle tissue), and cartilage (load-bearing tissue) [43,44].

Using these formulations, anatomical 3D models of the lungs, heart, and nose were printed via DLP printing, demonstrating high geometric fidelity and structural precision across the full range of stiffnesses (Fig. 4D). Notably, despite the increased polymer content, hGelMA maintained a relatively low viscosity, enabling higher concentrations than those typically achieved with conventional GelMA. Using this high-concentration formulation (50 % w/v), scaffolds with compressive modulus exceeding 1 MPa were successfully fabricated. In addition, constructs with lower mechanical stiffness were also printed by adjusting the hGelMA concentration.

To evaluate whether hydrolysis affected print resolution, 3D models containing circular and square pores of decreasing diameters were printed using GelMA and hGelMA (P500) at 30 % (w/v) (Supplementary Fig. 5). Both samples accurately reproduced features down to 200 μm, and the measured dimensions closely matched the theoretical values, with no significant differences observed between GelMA and hGelMA across the tested range. These results indicate that hydrolysis did not compromise print resolution or fidelity.

Short-term in vivo biocompatibility

Early tissue responses to GelMA and hGelMA (P500) hydrogels were evaluated in a 7-day dorsal subcutaneous implantation model. The non-implanted (naïve) control maintained normal skin architecture with only sparse inflammatory cells (Supplementary Fig. 6A, control). Fragments of the GelMA and hGelMA hydrogel matrix were identifiable on H&E staining, consistent with the persistence of photocrosslinked covalent networks. Both GelMA and hGelMA elicited a localized implant-associated host response at the material–tissue interface (Supplementary Fig. 6B and 6C, GelMA and hGelMA), characterized by a mixed inflammatory infiltrate (neutrophils and mononuclear cells) adjacent to residual hydrogel, with early fibroplasia in the overlying dermis and subcutaneous tissue. There was no histological evidence of extensive necrosis or abscess formation in either group. Neovascular structures with erythrocyte-filled lumina were identified in representative interface fields. The epidermis remained intact without ulceration, and no widespread tissue destruction was noted.

Collectively, histology at day 7 is consistent with a subacute, localized implant-associated host response and indicates acceptable local tolerance. Relative to GelMA, hGelMA exhibited no histological evidence of additional or formulation-specific adverse tissue reactions.

Evaluation of the low-temperature printability of HBC-MA/hGelMA composite inks

HBC-MA possesses biodegradability, biocompatibility, thermal responsiveness, and photo-crosslinkability, but remains biologically limited due to poor cell adhesiveness [21,22]. To improve the cytocompatibility of HBC-MA, a composite bioink was formulated by integrating HBC-MA with hGelMA (HBC-MA/hGelMA). Thermal characterization was subsequently conducted to investigate whether the thermoresponsive behavior of hGelMA, which differs from that of conventional GelMA, modulates the phase-transition profile of HBC-MA and to evaluate the feasibility of low-temperature crosslinking. The degrees of hydroxybutylation and methacrylate substitution of HBC-MA were determined to be approximately 81.3 ± 0.5 % and 15 ± 1.2 %, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 7). Assuming that sol-gel transitions occur upon heating, rheological measurements were performed from 4 to 40 °C on 2 % (w/v) HBC-MA (H2, control) and its composites containing 5 %, 10 %, and 15 % (w/v) P500 (denoted H2hG5, H2hG10, and H2hG15, respectively) (Fig. 5A). In all samples, both the G′ and G″ increased proportionally with the P500 content, and the rate of increase in the G′ was positively correlated with temperature. Crossover points, indicative of sol-gel transitions, were observed at approximately 29.8 °C for H2 and H2hG5, 27.1 °C for H2hG10, and 25.8 °C for H2hG15. A decrease in the sol-gel transition temperature was observed with increasing P500 concentration.

The photo-crosslinking kinetics of HBC-MA/hGelMA were evaluated at 20 °C, a temperature selected to reflect the thermoresponsive properties of HBC-MA relevant to LCST-integrated applications (Fig. 5B). The photo-crosslinking crossover points were measured at 26.6 s (H2), 21.6 s (H2hG5), 19.5 s (H2hG10), and 18.2 s (H2hG15), showing a concentration-dependent decrease in gelation time with increasing P500 content.

The compressive modulus was measured at 23.5 ± 4.9 kPa (H2), 35.5 ± 1.3 kPa (H2hG5), 48.2 ± 5.4 kPa (H2hG10), and 84.9 ± 7.4 kPa (H2hG15), with higher values observed at increased P500 concentrations (Fig. 5C). To evaluate the dimensional stability, the diameter retention ratios at 37 °C relative to those at 25 °C were measured for each formulation. H2 exhibited the greatest reduction (82.1 ± 0.2 %), whereas the retention ratios increased with higher P500 concentrations, reaching 91.6 ± 0.5 % (H2hG5), 97.4 ± 0.5 % (H2hG10), and 99.7 ± 0.2 % (H2hG15) (Fig. 5D). In the absence of hGelMA (H2), the hydrogel exhibited distinct thermoresponsive behavior, where heating induced hydrophobic dehydration and rearrangement of hydrogen bonding, leading to molecular aggregation and structural shrinkage [21,45]. By contrast, incorporation of hGelMA progressively mitigated this thermal sensitivity. Notably, the degree of swelling–shrinkage fluctuation was inversely correlated with hGelMA concentration, such that H2hG10 and H2hG15 maintained nearly complete dimensional retention with minimal deformation due to the additional photocrosslinkable sites and reinforced internal network provided by hGelMA.

Cytocompatibility of HBC-MA/hGelMA hydrogels in 3D culture

The cytocompatibility of HBC-MA/hGelMA hydrogels was investigated using NIH-3T3 fibroblasts. Live/Dead staining revealed that all GelMA and hGelMA-containing groups (GM10, H2hG5, H2hG10, H2hG15) exhibited high initial viabilities above 75 % on day 1, while the H2 group displayed a markedly lower value (54.5 ± 6.4 %). Beginning on day 3, cell viability in GelMA and hGelMA-containing hydrogels exceeded 83 % and progressively increased to >96 % by day 7, which was comparable to 2D culture controls (Fig. 6A and B). In contrast, H2 maintained significantly lower viability throughout the culture period, reaching only 74.1 ± 5.3 % on day 7.

Analysis of cell spreading confirmed progressive proliferation in GelMA and hGelMA-containing groups, with clear hGelMA concentration-dependent effects. By day 7, H2hG15 reached spreading areas of 96.8 ± 0.7 % and 96.5 ± 0.7 %, respectively, values comparable to GM10 (96.1 ± 1.7 %). In contrast, H2 remained significantly restricted, with only 40.7 ± 1.3 % spreading at the same time point (Fig. 6C). These results indicate that incorporation of hGelMA significantly enhances cellular proliferation and matrix affinity.

Quantitative CCK-8 assays further confirmed these findings (Fig. 6D). GM10 and high-hGelMA groups (H2hG10, H2hG15) exhibited robust proliferation, reaching absorbance values of 2.61 ± 0.12, and 2.65 ± 0.02 by day 7, comparable to GM10 (2.51 ± 0.07). By contrast, H2 showed consistently lower absorbance values (0.98 ± 0.10 on day 7), indicating limited proliferation. Collectively, these results demonstrate that hGelMA incorporation provides a favorable microenvironment that supports cell survival and proliferation in 3D culture.

To expand the evaluation, HaCaT keratinocytes were also encapsulated and analyzed (Supplementary Fig. 8). HaCaT cells exhibited viability and proliferation trends similar to NIH-3T3 cells, with higher hGelMA concentrations promoting significantly improved outcomes. H2hG15 showed proliferation comparable to GM10 by day 7, whereas H2 consistently demonstrated low viability and proliferation in both NIH-3T3 and HaCaT assays.

Furthermore, the potential cytotoxicity associated with photoinitiator concentration (0.5 % w/v LAP) and light exposure conditions (405 nm, 2.45 mW/cm2) was evaluated. Live/Dead assays conducted within before and within 2h after printing revealed an approximate 10 % reduction in viability immediately post-printing in both NIH-3T3 and HaCaT cells (Supplementary Fig. 9). Importantly, all GelMA and hGelMA-containing groups recovered rapidly, with viabilities exceeding 90 % by day 5 (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Fig. 9). These results demonstrate that the printing and crosslinking parameters adopted in this study are applicable without inducing significant acute cytotoxicity [46,47].

Evaluation of lysozyme-mediated degradation as a function of hGelMA concentration

To evaluate enzymatic degradation of HBC-MA in the printed HBC-MA/hGelMA constructs, samples crosslinked with P500 were incubated in lysozyme-containing solution. GelMA and gelatin components remained stable, while HBC-MA exhibited degradation, consistent with lysozyme's activity on β-1,4-glycosidic bonds [48,49]. On day 1, residual mass quantification revealed values of 67.3 ± 3.1 % (H2), 56.2 ± 7.0 % (H2hG5), 76.0 ± 1.0 % (H2hG10), and 83.0 ± 2.0 % (H2hG15), with H2hG15 exhibiting the highest retention (Fig. 7A). Both H2hG10 and H2hG15 retained over 75 % of their residual mass on day 1. H2hG5 exhibited lower residual masses on days 1 and 3 compared with those of the pure HBC-MA structure (H2), likely due to the combined effects of the low crosslinking density, irreversible disruption of the P500-derived network caused by thermal contraction at 37 °C, and progressive degradation of internal HBC-MA, leading to mechanical destabilization and accelerated structural collapse.

H2 exhibited progressive mass loss through day 7 and was completely degraded by day 14, at which point the residual mass could no longer be quantified. By day 21, residual mass values were 46.8 ± 5.4 % (H2hG10) and 80.7 ± 4.1 % (H2hG15), while the H2hG5 samples had completely collapsed by day 7 and were no longer quantifiable. H2hG15 showed the smallest change in residual mass from day 1 to day 21 among all groups, indicating high structural stability throughout the enzymatic degradation period. By contrast, H2hG10 exhibited a reduced structural stability relative to that of H2hG15, with gradual degradation and mass loss resulting from the lysozyme-mediated hydrolysis of HBC-MA. These findings suggest that higher hGelMA concentrations enhance the crosslinking density, improve the internal structural stability, and confer greater resistance to enzymatic degradation.

Pore size distribution analysis using SEM revealed that both H2hG10 and H2hG15 showed an increase in pore size with longer enzymatic treatment times, although H2hG15 maintained smaller pores than H2hG10 (Supplementary Fig. 10). Furthermore, the H2hG10 sample was successfully printed into a construct replicating the original 3D model in shape and dimension (Fig. 7B).

Discussion

To address the processing limitations of conventional GelMA, we developed a temperature-independent and photocrosslinkable hydrogel platform through controlled enzymatic hydrolysis, resulting in the generation of hydrolyzed GelMA (hGelMA). This approach aimed to mitigate the thermal gelation behavior and high viscosity of GelMA under ambient conditions, which significantly restrict its applicability in thermoresponsive polymer systems and high-resolution bioprinting (Fig. 1).

Our results demonstrate that Protamex®, a serine endoprotease, effectively cleaved the peptide backbone of GelMA while preserving the integrity of its methacryloyl functional groups, as confirmed by the consistent degrees of substitution across all hGelMA formulations (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 7) [32,33]. SDS-PAGE and rheology showed that higher protease levels reduced molecular weight and apparent viscosity, enabling low-viscosity bioinks and suppressing the thermally induced sol–gel transition typical of conventional GelMA (Fig. 2). Highly hydrolyzed samples (P500 and P750) did not gel below 30 °C, indicating substantial disruption of hydrogen-bond-mediated triple-helix formation. At the molecular level, controlled proteolysis shortens GelMA chains and weakens the re-formation of hydrogen-bonded triple helices at low temperature, thereby reducing helix fraction and lowering/broadening the helix melting transition. Short-chain fragments introduce steric and connectivity limitations that reduce effective helix-mediated crosslinking density and chain entanglement, thereby attenuating long-range helical network percolation. Accordingly, as hydrolysis increases, the dominant contribution to the mechanical response shifts from physical, helix-mediated associations to a covalent, photocrosslinked network [35,50,51].

By performing methacrylation prior to hydrolysis, we preserved methacryloyl functionalities while enabling the generation of a broader and more tunable molecular weight distribution. This sequence minimized batch-to-batch variability, maintained photocrosslinking capacity, and ensured reproducibility across preparations (Supplementary Fig. 4).

The swelling ratio represents a key parameter reflecting the intrinsic physical properties and internal network architecture of hydrogel systems [52,53]. In the present study, swelling trends of hGelMA paralleled SEM-observed pore morphology and the compressive modulus (Fig. 3, Fig. 5), indicating that proteolysis-driven reductions in molecular weight modulate crosslinking density and, in turn, microstructure and mechanics.

The optimal stiffness requirements for biomaterial scaffolds are highly tissue-specific, ranging from soft to highly elastic matrices [43,44]. hGelMA provided broad, concentration-dependent tunability of compressive modulus and enabled successful 3D bioprinting of mechanically relevant constructs (Fig. 4). Increasing polymer concentration enhanced intermolecular interactions and the integrity of the crosslinked network; notably, hGelMA (P500) spanned ∼30 kPa at 15 % (w/v) to >1 MPa at 50 % (w/v). The observed range of mechanical properties indicates the potential of hGelMA for applications ranging from soft tissue engineering to structurally demanding hard-tissue constructs, supporting its applicability in mechanically demanding environments [54].

hGelMA incorporation into HBC-MA accelerated photocrosslinking in a concentration-dependent manner; higher hGelMA (P500) showed earlier rheological crossover, indicating faster network formation (Fig. 5). This accelerated polymerization is attributed to the higher density of methacrylate-functionalized polymer chains in P500, which increases the availability of vinyl groups and facilitates more efficient radical propagation during photoinitiated crosslinking [55,56]. Furthermore, rheological analyses revealed that the integration of hGelMA not only improved the photopolymerization efficiency but also shifted the sol-gel transition of HBC-MA-based composites to lower temperatures. This thermal modulation is primarily attributed to the increased hydrophilicity and hydrogen bonding capacity introduced by hGelMA, which enhanced water retention and promoted hydrophobic interactions among HBC-MA chains [57]. In addition, the degree of swelling-shrinkage fluctuation was inversely correlated with hGelMA concentration, indicating that hGelMA effectively mitigates thermoresponsive deformation by reinforcing the hydrogel network and stabilizing volumetric responses during thermal cycling. The improved mechanical stiffness and dimensional stability at physiological temperatures further support the structural robustness of the composite hydrogel system, particularly at elevated hGelMA concentrations [58,59]. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the thermoresponsive behavior of the composite hydrogel system can be precisely modulated by adjusting the hGelMA concentration.

From a biological perspective, the incorporation of hGelMA into HBC-MA–based hydrogels markedly enhanced the viability and morphological spreading of NIH-3T3 fibroblasts and HaCaT (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 8), as supported by the quantification of proliferation area and CCK-8 absorbance measurements. This improvement is primarily attributed to the presence of cell-adhesive motifs inherently derived from gelatin, which are otherwise absent in HBC-MA [60]. Notably, constructs containing the H2hG15 formulation exhibited cellular proliferation profiles comparable to those observed in unmodified GelMA (GM10), indicating that the presence of hGelMA at appropriate concentrations enables cellular activity in matrices lacking inherent bioactivity. While higher stiffness provides structural stability, the dense hydrogel network inherently restricts cell survival and proliferation within the interior [61]. Therefore, further studies are required to optimize the relationship between internal network architecture and cellular growth.

Printing conditions (405 nm light and LAP) are another critical factor influencing cell survival within hydrogels. To evaluate this effect, cell viability was assessed immediately before and after printing. Both NIH-3T3 and HaCaT exhibited a decrease of approximately 10 % in viability immediately after printing, likely due to ROS from LAP, light exposure, and temperature fluctuations (Supplementary Fig. 9) [46,47]. Although lowering LAP to 0.25 % can reduce ROS and improve initial viability, it also prolongs gelation and compromises print fidelity under low-irradiance conditions [62]. Therefore, LAP at 0.5 % (w/v) was selected as a balanced condition, ensuring both acceptable viability and robust print performance (Fig. 6, Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9) [63]. The subsequent recovery of high viability indicates that the initial reduction was due to transient stress rather than irreversible damage.

The degradation analysis employing lysozyme, which specifically degrades the chitosan backbone of HBC-MA, further emphasized the tunability of the hydrogel system (Fig. 7). A higher content of hGelMA was associated with attenuated enzymatic degradation, due to enhanced crosslinking density and mechanical robustness [64]. This property is particularly relevant for applications that demand controlled degradation kinetics, such as temporally controlled tissue remodeling or sacrificial bioink strategies [48,49]. Consistent with reports on methacrylated chitosan–based hydrogels, formation of a swollen, photocrosslinked network can increase enzymatic accessibility to the polysaccharide backbone, thereby accelerating lysozyme-mediated chain scission and mass loss; with prolonged exposure (>6 weeks), such hydrogels have been reported to degrade to the point of becoming difficult to handle [65,66].

For clinical translation, the potential toxicity of degradation products is an important consideration. GelMA exhibits well-established biocompatibility with low cytotoxicity under standard synthesis and purification, and proteolytic degradation of GelMA yields gelatin-derived peptides and amino acids that are generally reported to be non-cytotoxic under typical exposure conditions [67,68]. In our study, hGelMA constructs supported high NIH-3T3 and HaCaT cell viability (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 8), which suggests that, under the present formulation and purification conditions, degradation of GelMA is unlikely to introduce intrinsic cytotoxicity. For the HBC-MA component, lysozyme cleavage of the chitosan backbone generates glucosamine and N-acetylglucosamine oligosaccharides with low intrinsic toxicity, and hydroxybutyl-substituted chitosan hydrogels are reported to be cytocompatible and enzymatically degradable in vitro and in vivo [21,69,70]. While these considerations support a degradation-product profile consistent with low intrinsic toxicity for the hGelMA/HBC-MA system, clinical applicability should be further evaluated by quantitative characterization of degradation products and longer-term biocompatibility studies under application-relevant conditions.

Overall, the development of hGelMA represents a straightforward and efficient approach to broaden the applicability of GelMA-based hydrogels. Importantly, enzymatic hydrolysis enabled molecular weight modulation while preserving methacryloyl substitution, thereby maintaining photocrosslinking capacity. This balance allowed stable DLP bioprinting even at high concentrations up to 50 % (w/v), which is not feasible with conventional GelMA, and conferred widely tunable mechanical properties. Given its absence of temperature-dependent gelation, compatibility with thermoresponsive polymers, and favorable cytocompatibility, hGelMA establishes a robust platform for designing composite hydrogel systems. Collectively, these attributes suggest the potential of hGelMA as a versatile material for emerging applications in biofabrication and tissue modeling.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully developed a bioink capable of low-temperature photocrosslinking by controlling the molecular weight of GelMA through simple enzymatic degradation using a serine protease. This molecular weight control enabled fine-tuning of the temperature responsiveness, photoreactivity, and viscosity. The LM GelMA (hGelMA) did not undergo physical crosslinking at low temperatures, allowing effective blending with thermoresponsive materials. In composite formulations with HBC-MA, hGelMA maintained the excellent cell affinity characteristics of GelMA. Furthermore, hGelMA exhibited the ability to achieve diverse mechanical properties while maintaining a low viscosity even at high concentrations. These features facilitated the fabrication of customized scaffolds tailored to various tissue types and enabled the production of high-stiffness tissue constructs. Overall, hGelMA overcame temperature constraints while preserving its photoreactivity and cell compatibility, alongside facile tunability of its physical properties. Therefore, hGelMA represents a promising platform for a broad range of applications in 3D bioprinting and tissue engineering.
