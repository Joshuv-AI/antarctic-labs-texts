Increased frequency of remote and off-site work impacts incident learning workflows in radiation oncology
Authors: Ethan Steele, Matthew Whitmill, Ross McGurk, Xianming Tan, Alison Amos, Zachary Oaks, Michael Dance, Ashley Weiner, Lukasz Mazur, Lawrence B. Marks, Bhishamjit Chera, Shivani Sud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Recent events have led to shifts in culture in favor of increased remote work.•Workflows in radiation oncology have adapted to facilitate remote work.•Changed workflows are associated with changes in event reporting.•Communication is frequently cited as a contributor to reported incidents.•Event severity has not increased despite these changed workflows.

Introduction

Quality assurance (QA) is of utmost importance in maintaining patient safety, especially in the context of substantial changes in work environment. For example, highly publicized misadministration of radiation events around the time of the widespread adoption of intensity modulated radiation therapy were found to be related to outdated workflows and ultimately led to an overhaul of quality, safety, and reporting standards in radiation oncology [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]. Viral-transmission mitigation strategies associated with the COVID-19 pandemic similarly led to changes in clinical workflows including remote work, increased reliance on electronic forms of communication, virtual meetings, telehealth visits, and an increased utilization of hypofractionated treatment schedules [10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17]. Many of these changes have persisted even as the acuity of the pandemic has subsided [18].

Incident learning has been recognized to be an important component of robust quality assurance (QA) programs aimed to maintain safety. For example, incident learning helps to guide changes to existing or new workflows, and promotes a culture where all workers are empowered to report events without fear of repercussion. A rich collection of reported events allows a multidisciplinary committee to better understand their system’s shortcomings and guides modifications to workflows [19], [20]. However, event reporting is imperfect; underreporting of events is common, and many reports lack actionable details [21].

We herein assess the impact of remote work on our incident learning workflows and event reporting program.

Methods and materials

Incident learning program

For more than a decade, our institution has developed a robust Incident Learning System (ILS) that sets patient safety at the center of all clinical proceedings. To cultivate a culture of safety, any member of the department can report an event which is reviewed by a multidisciplinary team (e.g. physicians, physicists, dosimetrists, therapists, nurses, and administrative staff) at weekly Quality and Safety Committee meetings. Reported events are submitted to the department ILS in our Communify Health online portal. Events are triaged by trained members of the Quality and Safety team with relevant expertise for review and preliminary investigation. New events are reviewed by the full Committee on a weekly basis. The Committee includes representation from key stakeholders in clinician, nursing, radiation therapist, physicist, dosimetry, education, resident and administrative teams. The review process includes a root cause analysis aiming to identify where and how the event was discovered, where in the treatment pathway an event occurred, the contributing or associated factors and real or potential consequences of the event. Further, the committee assists in determining whether the event warrants escalation beyond the department, for example, to the hospital or healthcare system administration or American Society for Radiation Oncology (ASTRO) Radiation Oncology Incident Learning System (RO-ILS). This process facilitates consideration of potential procedural changes or implementation of new safety barriers. At our institution, the specifics of each event, additional information gathered, committee deliberation are documented and stored in the Communify Health ImprovementFlow online software.

Source of data

Data were obtained from the Communify Health online repository of departmental event reports for the purposes of analysis in the current study. Data were additionally obtained from the departmental record and verify system (MOSAIQ®) to quantify the number of external beam planning simulations each month from March 2019 through February 2022.

The following details were assigned to each case prior to the current analysis. The severity of all events is assessed on a patient-oriented scale from 1 to 6 as follows: 1 = no patient impact; 2 = mild: reached patient but no direct clinical impact; 3 = moderate: clinical impact unlikely; 4 = severe: altered intended treatment; 5 = life-threatening; 6 = death. The clinical workflow from when the patient is scheduled for a consultation, through completion of radiotherapy, is divided into eight main timepoints including pre-visit, patient assessment, imaging for radiation planning, treatment planning, pretreatment review and verification, treatment delivery, on-treatment quality management, and post-treatment completion. For the purpose of the current analysis, these timepoints are combined into four windows of time: patient assessment; imaging and treatment planning; pretreatment review and verification; and treatment delivery and management. For events that relate to the delivery of external beam radiation therapy, both the timepoints at which each event originated and was discovered are determined. Other events are characterized as “not in the external beam radiation workflow.” To highlight important keywords related to each event, the reviewers of event reports assign one or more associated/contributing factors from a pre-specified index list. A summary of the situational details that led to the origin and/or the discovery of the event are additionally included.

Statistical analysis

The COVID-19 pandemic and associated push toward remote work began to impact our radiation oncology clinic in March 2020. Three years were thus defined: (1) the year prior to the pandemic from March 2019-February 2020 (pre-COVID); (2) the initial year of the pandemic from March 2020-February 2021 (during-COVID); and (3) the second year after declaration of the pandemic from March 2021-February 2022 (COVID-adapted). Descriptive statistics were obtained for the number of simulations, number of event reports, severity of reported events, and timepoint of origin and discovery of reported events during each of the three years of the study. Chi-square test was performed to make comparisons of event severity and timepoint of origin and discovery across COVID eras. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics 28 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY).

Results

778 events were reported across the three years studied. Decreases in the absolute number of reports per year, and reports per patient simulated were noted (Fig. 1, Table 1). The severity of reported events varied significantly between the three eras (p < 0.001; Table 1). There was an overall decrease in the severity of events: An increase in the proportion of events with no patient impact was observed between the pre-COVID (40.3%) and during-COVID (57.6%) eras (Fig. 2a). There were no highest-severity incidents that resulted in patient harm or death, and the low rate of severe incidents per simulated patient that altered the intended treatment was relatively stable across the three eras (Fig. 2b). The workflow timepoints at which events originated or were discovered varied significantly across the three years of the study (p < 0.001 both comparisons; Table 1). Event detection generally moved earlier in the treatment pipeline during COVID which was sustained into the COVID-adapted era as well. Imaging and treatment planning were more commonly identified as the origin of events through the pandemic (42%, 57%, and 63% progressing through the three eras) yet also had an increased role in the discovery of events. Events were more commonly discovered during pre-treatment review and verification during the COVID-affected years of the study (14% of events pre-COVID up to 26% and 22% in the subsequent two years respectively) while there was a decrease in the rate of event discovery while patients were on treatment in the during-COVID era from 41% down to 26% of events from pre-COVID to during-COVID (Table 1; Fig. 3).Fig. 3The rate (number of events per 100 simulations) that events originated or were discovered at each timepoint of the treatment pathway in each of the three time periods.

The frequency of specific indexed associated/contributory factors varied across the three time intervals. Highly cited indexed factors were noted as follows: Simulation (23%, 4%, 1% in the Pre-COVID, During-COVID, and COVID-Adjusted eras respectively), workload (25%, 11%, 10%), self-report (voluntary reporting of events related to their own work; 18%, 19%, 20%), communication (16%, 25%, 7%), COVID-19 (0%, 16%, 4%), cross-coverage (referring to when one colleague covers the tasks for which a different colleague is the primary responsible task owner or the most familiar; 4%, 4%, 8%). Workload and simulation were frequently cited issues pre-COVID while communication, COVID-19, and rapid-turnaround were more commonly indexed in the during-COVID and COVID-adapted eras (Table 1).

Discussion

This study assessed changes in event reporting in a radiation oncology department in response to workflow changes associated with the COVID-19 pandemic (namely remote work) and by doing so joins a very limited body of literature assessing impacts of these altered workflows on incident learning and event reporting in radiation oncology. Key departmental systems remained static over this time interval including consistency in machinery or treatment equipment, computer systems, treatment planning policies and procedures and longstanding incident learning practices. Though less tangible practices, changes in staffing, and attitudes could have evolved over this time interval as well. We found four main outcomes from the data: A decrease in the absolute number of reported events over time, a relative increase in the reporting of low-severity events that did not impact patient care, shifts in the primary timepoints of event origin and discovery across time, and changes in associated keywords that reflect changes in the nature of reported events.

First, the three year-long time intervals demonstrated modest decreases in the number of submitted events which could be attributed to several factors. One reason is that the number of simulations, and by extension, patients undergoing treatment, decreased during the pandemic thus resulting in lowering the absolute number of event reports. However, the rate of reports per simulation also decreased during the pandemic: Reasons for this decreased rate of event reporting was likely multifactorial and hard to concretely ascertain from the current analysis. Fewer planned treatments (as illustrated by the reported decrease in the number of external beam planning simulations), increasing utilization of hypofractionation leading to shorter on-treatment duration (as recommended by published guidelines) [10], [11], [12], [13], changes in faculty/staff (our institution had multiple staff changes over this time interval), and remote work leading to decreased engagement of some staff in department QA projects are possible explanations. Prior reports have described a similar magnitude of decreased rates of event reporting in recent years, although the underlying reasons for these consistent decreases remain unclear. Volpini et al postulated that their observed decrease in reporting was likely associated with decreased team member engagement in the incident learning system while adapting to altered work conditions and changing hospital policies [22]. Jacqmin and Crosby at the University of Wisconsin found similar decreases in event reporting among physicists, dosimetrists, and therapists despite varying degrees of in-person vs remote work by job title which may suggest that hybrid work was likely not a primary cause of this decreased reporting [23].

Second, there were significant differences in event severity noted across the three years of the study. Compared to pre-COVID, a higher proportion of events reported in the latter two years of the study were rated as having the lowest severity with “no patient impact” which could speak partially to more qualitative changes in the nature of reported events. Some anecdotes include improper mask-wearing among patients, difficulty maintaining 6-feet social distancing with multiple individuals in the clinic room (example, learners, patient support, Department of Corrections guards for incarcerated patients), educational or didactic conference operational events, among other events related to changes resulting from the pandemic but unlikely to impact oncologic outcomes. We also noted a decrease in mild-to-moderate-severity events, i.e., those that may have reached the patient but with little to no impact on clinical care or outcome which is consistent with the overall lower number of submitted events to the ILS. A decrease in moderate-severity events could be associated with decreased clinical business as represented by the decreased number of external beam planning simulations. There was no appreciable change in severe incidents across the three time periods. Interestingly, while they found a similar magnitude in the overall decrease in reported events, Jacqmin and Crosby found a notable decrease in reporting of low-severity events [23]. This finding is in contrast with our results that demonstrate increased reporting of low-severity events and may ultimately suggest the impact individual team members and institutional culture have on the nature of reported events.

Third, there were changes in the timepoint of origin of events, and the points at which those events were discovered. Across the time periods, the origin of events appears to mainly increase in the imaging and treatment planning stages. Discovery of events relied less heavily on on-treatment safeguards and instead they were discovered more frequently in the pre-treatment review and verification steps in the workflow. In the COVID-adapted era the frequency with which events were discovered begins to more closely resemble the pre-COVID baseline (Fig. 3). The changing nature of event severity, origin, and detection demonstrates the importance of multiple layers of safeguards to avoid patient harm [24]. The often referenced “swiss cheese model” provides a conceptual framework of how policy, culture, and institutional structure contribute to medical errors being missed resulting in detrimental outcomes [24], [25]. The change in dependence on different safeguards under varying conditions highlights the importance of a strong multi-layer network of safeguards that engage a multi-disciplinary team. Prior work by Mazur et al. evaluated the utility of various safety barriers in the radiation treatment pathway and how multiple layers are needed to detect events as many events may pass through certain barriers without detection [20]. The present study suggests that the utility of individual safety barriers may change given the context of altered workflows.

Fourth, the underlying reasons for why events occurred was seen to change over the chosen three time periods based on changes in associated event keywords. Keywords associated frequently with events pre-COVID were “workload” and “simulation” while “communication,” “COVID-19,” and “self-report” were more frequently associated with events in the during-COVID and COVID-adapted eras. Our department maintained a high rate of self-reported events in all COVID eras with self-reports accounting for about a fifth of reports. Modes of communication changed through the latter years of the study. In response to the pandemic, our institution adopted various masking and social distancing policies. Physicians were required to work on-site on clinic days to see patients and help manage patients on treatment, but many consultations and follow up visits were transitioned to a virtual format. Physicians were encouraged to work from home 1–2 days per week during dedicated administrative, academic, or research days. High quality communication became essential to allow for effective clinical cross-coverage, a common practice in health care wherein a team provides medical coverage and management for a patient with whom they are not the primary, familiar team. For clinicians this included imaging verification, simulations, and on-treatment symptomatic concerns for the patients of off-site providers. Cross-coverage was also increased among dosimetry, therapy and physicists. For example, dosimetrists transitioned from working on site every weekday to a majority working remotely with only one designated dosimetrist on site per day. Communication regarding treatment planning between physicians, physicists, and dosimetrists therefore changed from in-person discussions to primarily telephone and email discourse. A hybrid model balancing on-site and remote work remains in place for many of the personnel in our department. Daily simulation/contour review has been a longstanding QA measure at our institution, but these sessions have increased importance to bridge communication between groups in the virtual world. Work by Judy et al. before the pandemic demonstrated the role of miscommunication in contributing to errors that impact patient care in radiation oncology [24]. The present study further emphasizes the need to reduce inter-provider variations and adopt consistent standards for communication and treatment.

Our analysis has several weaknesses. First, the definition of the three year-long time windows is somewhat arbitrary. While the COVID-19 pandemic had a relatively well-defined start date in March 2020 in the United States, the nature of varying incidence and mortality rates through the multiple viral surges and the complexity of the social and political response, it is difficult to define a timepoint when the impact of the pandemic meaningfully lessened, let alone ended. Many of the changes instituted in the interest of social distancing persist to date despite dramatically decreased COVID-19 incidence and an end of most masking or other viral mitigation policies [18]. In spite of these considerations, we believe the three time intervals used to address these issues are reasonable. Second, our department experienced some changes in staffing over the study period and the change in staffing for individuals heavily engaged in reporting may notably impact the number and nature of reported events. Third, attendance at our institutional weekly Quality and Safety Committee meeting is variable and attendees participate in discussion that ultimately contributes to factors such as severity assessment, event origin and discovery evaluation, and assignment of associated/contributing factors. As a result, changes in engaged participants in our incident learning program could result in shifts in event data that partially reflect the changes in observers rather than the inherent nature of the events. Fourth, for the purpose of this analysis we used the number of simulations as a surrogate for the total patient volume through our departmental treatment pathway and acknowledge that this may imperfectly approximate reality. Fifth, this analysis reflects the experience of a single academic institution with a historically robust incident learning system and therefore these results may not be generalizable to all practice settings. Finally, the indexed factors or “keywords” associated with each event are assigned by the reviewer of the event and two individuals could reasonably choose to assign different keywords to the same event report.

Despite the mentioned limitations, the current study of the effects of changed workflows resulting from the COVID-19 pandemic on incident learning leverages the robust experience of a public academic radiation oncology department with longstanding incident learning experience that treats a diverse complement of diseases using various modalities and therefore we expect these results to be applicable to a range of clinical settings.

Conclusion

Analysis of our departmental incident learning program demonstrates changes in the quantity and nature of event reports over time in line with changes in workflows related to remote work and electronic communication born out of the COVID pandemic. Future work is needed to better understand the reduction in events reported in recent years and to evaluate if the rate of reporting will rebound in the future.
