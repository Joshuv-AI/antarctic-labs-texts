Cross-modal deep learning enhanced mixed reality accelerates construction skill transfer from experts to students
Authors: Chenyu Wang, Meng Zhu, Sharifah Akman Syed Zakaria
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents a novel approach for transferring expert tacit knowledge to construction students through a cross-modal deep learning supported mixed reality training system. Traditional construction training faces challenges in effectively capturing and transmitting the implicit expertise of experienced practitioners. We developed a framework that integrates multimodal data collection from construction experts with cross-modal deep learning algorithms to identify critical knowledge patterns and represent them in an immersive mixed reality environment. Experimental validation with 80 participants demonstrated that students trained using this system achieved 32.4% faster skill acquisition, 22.4% higher accuracy in construction tasks, and significantly better knowledge retention compared to traditional methods. The system particularly excelled in transferring situational awareness and adaptive problem-solving capabilities. Results indicate that cross-modal deep learning approaches can successfully formalize expert tacit knowledge into learnable representations, providing a scalable solution for construction education that addresses industry challenges of workforce development and knowledge preservation. Despite these promising results, the study acknowledges limitations including computational latency challenges during complex interactions and hardware ergonomic constraints during extended training sessions.Keywords: Cross-modal deep learning, Mixed reality, Construction training, Tacit knowledge, Knowledge transfer, Skill acquisitionSubject terms: Computer science, Civil engineering, Electrical and electronic engineering

Introduction

Construction industry training faces significant challenges in the era of digital transformation, particularly in effectively transferring practical skills from experienced professionals to novice students. Traditional construction training methods often struggle with resource limitations, safety concerns, and the gap between theoretical knowledge and practical application1. The complexity of modern construction projects demands increasingly sophisticated skillsets from graduates, creating an urgent need for innovative training approaches that can meet industry demands while ensuring student safety2.

One of the most critical challenges in construction education is the effective transmission of tacit knowledge—the implicit, experience-based knowledge that experts possess but often cannot articulate explicitly3. This tacit knowledge encompasses intuitive decision-making, situational awareness, and contextual understanding that is typically acquired through years of on-site experience. Traditional classroom-based instruction frequently fails to capture these nuanced aspects of construction expertise, resulting in graduates who possess theoretical knowledge but lack practical competence4.

Mixed Reality (MR) technology has emerged as a promising solution for bridging the gap between theory and practice in construction education. Previous studies have demonstrated MR’s effectiveness in construction safety training and equipment operation simulation, with early implementations showing 15–25% improvement in hazard identification accuracy compared to traditional methods5. By seamlessly blending virtual elements with the physical environment, MR creates immersive training scenarios that simulate real-world construction challenges without associated risks. When integrated with cross-modal deep learning algorithms, MR systems can analyze expert behaviors across multiple sensory dimensions, including visual, auditory, and kinesthetic inputs, to identify and codify tacit knowledge patterns that would otherwise remain inaccessible6.

While previous research has explored multimodal approaches in vocational education through virtual reality environments, and deep learning techniques have been applied to knowledge transfer in other domains, the integration of cross-modal deep learning with mixed reality for tacit knowledge transfer in construction remains largely unexplored.

This research addresses the following key questions: (RQ1) How can cross-modal deep learning effectively capture and formalize expert tacit knowledge in construction? (RQ2) What is the comparative effectiveness of MR-based versus traditional training methods for construction skill acquisition? (RQ3) How can mixed reality environments optimize the transfer of tacit knowledge to improve long-term retention?

This research aims to develop and evaluate a novel cross-modal deep learning supported mixed reality training framework for construction education that effectively captures and transfers expert tacit knowledge to students. The framework utilizes multimodal data collection from expert practitioners, deep learning analysis to identify critical knowledge patterns, and mixed reality interfaces for immersive knowledge transfer7. This study is guided by three primary hypotheses: (H1) cross-modal MR training will significantly accelerate skill acquisition compared to traditional methods, (H2) knowledge transfer efficiency will correlate with expert model representation fidelity, and (H3) procedural knowledge retention will be superior in the experimental group.

The significance of this research extends beyond immediate educational outcomes to address broader industry challenges, including the aging workforce, skills shortages, and the need for rapid upskilling in response to technological advancement8.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section II reviews relevant literature on construction training challenges, tacit knowledge transfer, and mixed reality applications. Section III presents the methodology for the proposed framework, while Section IV details the implementation and experiments. Section V presents results and discussion, followed by conclusions and future work directions in Section VI.

Theoretical foundation and literature review

Fundamentals of cross-modal deep learning

Cross-modal deep learning represents a significant advancement in artificial intelligence by enabling machines to process, interpret, and correlate information across different modalities such as visual, auditory, and textual data9. Unlike traditional unimodal approaches, cross-modal systems can establish meaningful connections between heterogeneous data sources, mimicking the human ability to integrate sensory information from multiple channels10. This capability is particularly valuable in construction training where expert knowledge manifests through diverse modalities including visual demonstrations, verbal explanations, and tactile guidance.

In educational contexts, cross-modal deep learning has demonstrated remarkable effectiveness for capturing and transmitting complex knowledge. Construction domain expertise encompasses a substantial proportion of tacit knowledge—implicit understanding that is difficult to formalize and communicate through conventional means. This specialized tacit knowledge can be categorized into three primary dimensions: technical know-how (embodied skills and construction techniques), contextual awareness (site-specific adaptations and environmental interpretations), and social-collaborative knowledge (team coordination practices and communication protocols)21. The complex, multimodal nature of construction expertise presents significant challenges for traditional educational approaches, as approximately 67% of critical knowledge in experienced practitioners remains undocumented and primarily transferred through direct observation and guided practice22.

Mixed reality technology applications in construction

The integration of Mixed Reality (MR) technology into construction education has evolved significantly over the past two decades, progressing from simple visualization tools to sophisticated interactive training environments23. Early implementations in the early 2000 s primarily focused on Augmented Reality (AR) for visualizing 3D building models and construction sequences, with limited interactivity and predominantly used in classroom settings rather than on-site24. The emergence of head-mounted displays (HMDs) around 2015 marked a pivotal advancement, enabling more immersive experiences where virtual building components could be overlaid onto physical spaces with increasing precision25.

Currently, MR applications in construction site training encompass diverse functionalities including safety hazard identification, equipment operation simulation, assembly sequence training, and quality control procedures26. Industry adoption has grown substantially, with approximately 67% of large construction firms implementing some form of MR-based training by 2023, though implementation varies significantly in sophistication and scope28.

Despite promising advancements, MR technology faces several critical challenges in construction applications that must be addressed for effective implementation. Hardware limitations including field of view restrictions, device weight, and battery life constraints significantly impact extended training sessions in dynamic construction environments29. The fidelity gap between virtual and physical interactions remains problematic, particularly for tasks requiring haptic feedback and fine motor skills30. The proposed cross-modal deep learning approach offers potential solutions to some of these challenges by optimizing content delivery based on user attention patterns and reducing computational overhead through intelligent scene management.

Comparative analysis between MR-based and traditional construction training reveals complementary strengths rather than a straightforward superiority of either approach. Traditional methods excel in providing authentic material interactions and environmental conditions but suffer from resource constraints, safety risks, and limited repeatability32. A mixed-methods study involving 142 construction management students demonstrated that MR-based training yielded 28% higher knowledge retention rates for safety procedures and 34% improved performance in equipment operation compared to conventional approaches33. However, MR systems showed limited effectiveness in conveying tactile aspects of construction materials and environmental conditions such as weather impacts34.

The integration of pedagogical frameworks with MR technology has emerged as a critical factor in successful implementation, with constructivist and experiential learning approaches showing particular promise when coupled with scaffolded MR experiences that gradually decrease guidance as learner competence increases2. This structured progression from guided to autonomous practice within MR environments provides a mechanism for addressing the scaffolding requirements that Vygotsky identified as essential for skill development, while maintaining the safety advantages of virtual training35.

Research on tacit knowledge transfer in construction expertise

Given the substantial tacit knowledge components in construction expertise, effective capture and transfer methods become critical for educational advancement.

Traditional methods for capturing and transferring tacit construction knowledge have predominantly relied on apprenticeship models, communities of practice, and mentorship relationships36. These approaches, while effective in transmitting contextual nuances and embodied skills, are inherently limited by resource constraints, expert availability, and the inability to scale across large student populations37. Evaluating the effectiveness of tacit knowledge transfer remains challenging due to the implicit nature of the transferred skills. However, significant gaps remain in effectively transferring the intuitive decision-making abilities that characterize master-level construction practitioners, suggesting the need for more sophisticated cognitive modeling approaches that can capture the heuristic reasoning patterns underlying expert judgment in complex, ambiguous construction scenarios44.

System implementation and validation experiments

Experiment design and participants

The validation experiments were designed to evaluate the effectiveness of the cross-modal mixed reality training system for transferring expert tacit knowledge to construction students compared to traditional training methods66. Three primary research hypotheses guided the experimental design: (H1) students trained using the cross-modal MR system will demonstrate higher skill acquisition rates compared to traditional methods; (H2) the knowledge transfer efficiency will correlate positively with the fidelity of expert model representation; and (H3) retention of procedural knowledge will be significantly higher in the experimental group at four-week follow-up assessments.

Participants were recruited from undergraduate and graduate construction-related programs at three universities using stratified random sampling to ensure balanced distribution of academic backgrounds, prior experience levels, and demographic characteristics67. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The experimental protocols were approved by the University Sains Malaysia Human Research Ethics Committee (IRB Approval #2024–05-127). Informed consent was obtained from all participants following these institutional research ethics protocols. The experimental group (n = 42) received training through the cross-modal MR system, while the control group (n = 38) participated in traditional classroom and workshop-based training covering identical construction tasks and concepts. Additionally, an expert group (n = 12) established performance baselines for comparative analysis. Participant demographic and background information is presented in Table 4.

The experimental protocol consisted of four phases implemented over an eight-week period: (1) pre-training assessment of baseline construction knowledge and skills; (2) training intervention (either MR-based or traditional) consisting of twelve 90-min sessions covering scaffolding assembly, concrete formwork, and structural connection techniques; (3) immediate post-training competency assessment; and (4) four-week follow-up retention assessment68. Performance evaluations employed standardized task completion scenarios assessed by blinded expert evaluators using validated assessment rubrics.

Data collection encompassed multiple dimensions including task performance metrics (completion time, error rates, quality scores), psychomotor parameters (movement precision, tool handling proficiency), knowledge assessment scores, and subjective experience measures69. Quantitative performance data was captured through the instrumented training environment, while qualitative feedback was collected through semi-structured interviews and standardized questionnaires examining cognitive load, training satisfaction, and self-efficacy.

The experimental environment was configured to ensure ecological validity while maintaining experimental control, with the MR training conducted in a dedicated laboratory space equipped with spatially mapped construction elements and safety provisions. The traditional training was delivered in university workshop facilities with industry-standard equipment and materials. Environmental conditions including lighting, noise levels, and temperature were standardized across training sites to minimize confounding variables. Particular attention was given to ethics considerations regarding data privacy, with all performance recordings anonymized during analysis and stored on secure, encrypted servers in compliance with institutional data protection protocols.

Discussion

The study acknowledges several technical and methodological limitations that warrant consideration. Real-time cross-modal processing demands occasionally created latency issues exceeding the 20ms threshold, particularly during complex multi-user interactions. Hardware ergonomics presented challenges for extended sessions, with participants reporting moderate discomfort after 60 + minutes of continuous use. The controlled laboratory environment, while ensuring experimental validity, may limit direct generalizability to dynamic construction sites with environmental variabilities such as weather conditions, noise levels, and spatial constraints.

The findings provide substantial implications for construction education and industry practice. The demonstrated 32.4% improvement in skill acquisition rates suggests significant potential for cost reduction in training programs while enhancing graduate employability. Educational institutions can leverage the system’s scalability to address resource constraints while maintaining instructional quality. For industry applications, the framework enables accelerated employee onboarding and standardization of expert practices across organizations.

From a theoretical perspective, this work advances tacit knowledge formalization by demonstrating that implicit expertise can be computationally captured and systematically transmitted through immersive technologies. The cross-modal deep learning framework establishes a foundation for analyzing complex human behaviors and extracting transferable patterns from expert demonstrations across various skill domains.

Conclusions and future work

This research successfully developed and validated a novel cross-modal deep learning-supported mixed reality training system for transferring expert tacit knowledge to construction students. Experimental validation with 80 participants confirmed all three research hypotheses: significantly faster skill acquisition (H1), positive correlation between model fidelity and transfer efficiency (H2), and superior knowledge retention in the MR-trained group (H3). The experimental results demonstrate significant improvements in learning outcomes across multiple dimensions, with the most pronounced effects observed in situational awareness, procedural knowledge acquisition, and adaptive problem-solving capabilities.

The cross-modal MR approach achieved a 32.4% reduction in time-to-proficiency compared to traditional training methods while simultaneously improving knowledge retention rates and transfer to novel construction scenarios84. The integration of multi-modal expert behavior modeling with immersive mixed reality visualization provides a scalable framework for capturing and transferring the critical tacit knowledge components that have traditionally been accessible only through extended apprenticeship experiences.

While the system effectively transfers procedural and situational knowledge, certain socio-cultural aspects of construction expertise proved more resistant to computational modeling and virtual transfer85.

Future research directions should address these limitations through both technological and methodological advancements. Integration of edge computing architectures could reduce system latency while preserving model complexity. Longitudinal studies tracking professional performance over extended periods would provide more definitive evidence regarding the persistence of learned skills. The expansion of the knowledge representation framework to incorporate broader contextual factors, including cultural and organizational dimensions of construction practice, represents an important frontier for comprehensive expertise modeling86. Additionally, the development of automated assessment capabilities based on continuous performance monitoring could enable more adaptive, personalized learning pathways.

The implications for construction education practice are substantial, suggesting a paradigm shift from traditional apprenticeship models toward technology-enhanced knowledge transfer systems that make expert tacit knowledge more accessible, analyzable, and transferable. This approach addresses critical industry challenges including workforce shortages, standardization of best practices, and preservation of specialized knowledge from retiring experts. As mixed reality and cross-modal deep learning technologies continue to mature, their integration into formal construction education programs offers a promising pathway for enhancing workforce readiness and maintaining the transmission of critical tacit knowledge across generations of construction professionals.
