The contribution of the Philips 66 brainstorming technique to nursing students’ academic engagement and belonging-related outcomes: a single-group pretest–posttest study
Authors: Sevinc Tastan, Hulya Firat Kilic, Gülsen Müsteyde Altan Denizer, Vildan Budak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcademic belonging is considered an important prerequisite for academic success and is closely linked to students’ academic engagement. Together, these constructs positively influence motivation, willingness to learn, and achievement.AimsThis study aimed to examine the potential contribution of the Philips 66 brainstorming technique to nursing students’ academic engagement and belonging-related outcomes.MethodA single-group pretest–posttest quasi-experimental design was conducted in Northern Cyprus with 85 nursing students. Data were collected using a demographic form and the Academic Engagement Scale before and six weeks after a Philips 66 brainstorming session, which was integrated into the practical component of the Nursing Management course. Data were analyzed using paired-samples t-tests, with effect sizes reported as Cohen’s d (α = 0.05), with small to moderate effect sizes.ResultsStudents showed a statistically significant improvements in total and subscale scores on the Academic Engagement Scale after the intervention (p < .05). The findings suggest that the Philips 66 brainstorming technique may be associated with improvements in academic engagement and may also be related to students’ sense of belonging.ConclusionThe Philips 66 brainstorming technique appears to be a promising approach for supporting nursing students’ academic engagement. Integrating such active learning strategies into nursing education may support students’ institutional commitment and professional competencies.Trial registrationThis study was an educational intervention using a single-group pretest–posttest quasi-experimental design and did not meet the criteria for a clinical trial requiring registration in a WHO-approved clinical trial registry. Therefore, trial registration was not applicable.

Background

The sense of belonging can be defined as an individual’s connection and engagement with a subject, place, community, or entity [1]. Maslow (1954) identified belonging as a fundamental human need, ranking it third in his hierarchy of needs [2]. Definitions of the sense of belonging often refer to “an individual’s sense of identity or positioning within a group or the university community,” which leads to an emotional response [3]. In higher education, belonging requires the interaction of environmental, social, and cognitive factors that enhance students’ sense of connection to their university and support a sense of purpose in relation to their academic work and career goals [4].

In recent years, belonging has been considered an indicator of institutional success, and student belonging is viewed as a key prerequisite for academic achievement [5, 6]. Previous research has emphasized that a strong sense of belonging among university students is crucial for academic motivation, satisfaction, performance, and retention [7].

Students’ attitudes, values, desires, and behaviors related to the university’s physical environment, educators, and ongoing academic and social activities influence their adjustment to the institution. This adjustment, in turn, affects their academic performance [8, 9]. School belonging is based not only on behavioral factors, such as attending classes and participating in social and academic activities, but also on affective components. These include a sense of connection to the school climate and alignment with institutional values [10]. Participation in academically oriented learning activities is considered a key determinant of university students’ success [11]. Previous studies have identified several internal factors that influence academic engagement, including academic self-efficacy, academic satisfaction, academic performance, self-esteem, motivation, and personal values. In addition, external factors such as gender, instructors’ teaching styles, and grades also play a significant role [12, 13]. Maintaining a high level of academic engagement among university students remains a critical goal for educational institutions. Despite the growing emphasis on active learning approaches in higher education, relatively limited research has specifically examined the role of structured brainstorming techniques, such as the Philips 66 brainstorming technique, in relation to academic engagement and belonging-related outcomes among nursing students. In particular, there is a need for studies that explore how brief, structured, and interactive classroom interventions may influence engagement-related behaviors that are closely associated with students’ sense of belonging.

National and international nursing organizations emphasize the importance of investing in nursing education. Such investments aim to enhance the academic success of nursing students and to meet the health needs of the population. Therefore, it is essential to implement a variety of strategies to support and improve students’ academic performance [14]. Developing strategies to strengthen academic belonging—shown to have a positive impact on academic achievement—is also expected to contribute to the development of core competencies needed in nursing practice. In particular, academic engagement in nursing education directly supports the development of critical thinking, clinical decision-making, and patient care skills [15]. Although the primary focus of this study is academic belonging, this construct was not measured directly. Instead, academic engagement was assessed as the main outcome. Academic engagement and academic belonging are conceptually distinct but closely related constructs. In this study, engagement-related behaviors, such as participation in academic activities, interaction with faculty members, and involvement in institutional processes, were considered as belonging-relevant indicators rather than direct measures of belonging.

In this context, the potential influence of the Philips 66 brainstorming technique on these outcomes may be explained through its emphasis on interactive and participatory learning processes. The technique facilitates peer interaction, encourages students to express their ideas (voice), enhances visibility within the group, and promotes collective problem-solving. These processes may strengthen students’ connection to the academic environment and support engagement-related behaviors, which are considered to be closely associated with the development of a sense of belonging over time. Therefore, this study aims to contribute to the literature by providing empirical evidence on the potential role of a structured brainstorming technique in supporting engagement-related outcomes that are closely linked to students’ sense of belonging. Methods

Study design

This study employed a single-group pretest–posttest quasi-experimental design. No control or comparison group was included, and no random allocation was performed at the study design level. This design was preferred in order to implement the intervention within the natural structure of the educational setting without disrupting the course flow. However, its limitations in terms of causal inference were acknowledged. Given the absence of a control group, the study may be subject to potential threats to internal validity, including history, maturation, testing effects, course-related exposure during the six-week period, and possible Hawthorne effects. Accordingly, the observed changes cannot be confidently attributed solely to the Philips 66 brainstorming technique intervention. In addition, as the intervention was embedded within an ongoing course and the post-test was conducted six weeks later, routine course progression and other educational experiences may have influenced the outcomes. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines.

Setting

The research was conducted during the Spring Semester of the 2023–2024 academic year in the Department of Nursing at a university located in Northern Cyprus.

Participants and sample size

The study population consisted of senior undergraduate nursing students enrolled in the course “Nursing Services Management” (N = 90). No sampling method was employed; all students who voluntarily agreed to participate were included in the study. Inclusion criteria were enrollment in the “Nursing Services Management” course, participation in the Philips 66 session, and completion of both pre- and post-assessments within the specified time frame. Exclusion criteria were non-attendance at the intervention session, incomplete pre- or post-questionnaires, or withdrawal of consent. A total of 15 groups were formed, each consisting of 6 students, in accordance with the Philips 66 brainstorming technique. Students were divided into small discussion groups using simple random drawing solely for the implementation of the Philips 66 brainstorming technique. This grouping procedure was not intended as randomization at the study design level, as the study employed a single-group pretest–posttest quasi-experimental design without a control group. Of the 90 students, 85 completed both the pre-test and post-test, and their data were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). A priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power (version 3.1.9.2; Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany) to determine the required sample size for a paired-samples t-test. The calculation was based on an assumed medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5), a significance level of α = 0.05, and a statistical power of 95% (1–β = 0.95). Based on these parameters, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 54 participants. Accordingly, the final sample size exceeded the minimum estimated sample size required for the analysis. The sample size calculation was based on the standard formula for detecting mean differences in paired measurements, where Zα represents the critical value corresponding to the significance level, Zβ represents the critical value corresponding to the desired statistical power, σ denotes the standard deviation of the difference scores, and δ represents the expected mean difference. In this study, these parameters were incorporated using the standardized effect size (Cohen’s d = δ/σ) within the G*Power framework. Of the 90 participants initially recruited, 5 did not complete the data collection instruments and were excluded from the analysis. Therefore, the final analysis was conducted with 85 participants, with no missing data in the analyzed dataset. As all eligible students enrolled in the course were included, the study represents a census of a single class cohort rather than a representative sample.

Data collection tools

Although this study is conceptually grounded in academic belonging, the primary outcome measured was academic engagement. Academic engagement and academic belonging are conceptually distinct but closely related constructs. In this study, academic engagement was used as a belonging-related behavioral indicator rather than a direct measure of academic belonging. Academic engagement reflects students’ active participation in academic and social activities, which is considered an important behavioral and experiential component associated with the development of a sense of belonging, particularly in terms of students’ interaction with the academic environment, faculty members, and institutional activities. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution, as academic engagement does not directly capture the multidimensional construct of academic belonging.

Demographic information form

The demographic information form was developed by the researchers based on existing literature [16, 17]. It contained questions regarding participants’ descriptive characteristics, including age, gender, mother’s education level, father’s education level, regular study habits, daily study hours, willingness to choose the nursing profession, and satisfaction with the nursing profession.

Academic engagement scale

The Academic Engagement Scale was developed by Kızılkaya and Doğan in 2022. The scale consists of 25 items divided into three sub-dimensions: “Class Attendance, Library, and Resource Access” (items 1–9), “Communication with Faculty Members” (items 10–17), and “Participation in Scientific and Cultural Activities” (items 18–25). The overall Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient of the scale is 0.91, with sub-dimensions ranging from 0.78 to 0.90. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale: “Always” (5), “Frequently” (4), “Sometimes” (3), “Rarely” (2), and “Never” (1). Scores can range from 25 to 125. The scale does not have a cutoff point. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were 0.94 for the overall scale and ranged from 0.84 to 0.91 for the sub-dimensions [5].

Intervention

The intervention was implemented within the practical component of the “Nursing Services Management” course and was conducted by the course instructor, who was also a member of the research team. The session was delivered once as part of the scheduled course activities. A standardized instruction was provided to all students. The problem statement, “How can nursing students’ sense of institutional belonging be improved?”, was presented, and students were asked to apply the Philips 66 brainstorming technique. Students were divided into 15 groups of six members each using simple random drawing for educational purposes. Each group selected a leader and engaged in a structured six-minute small-group discussion to generate solutions to the given problem. During this period, the instructor did not actively intervene and maintained a facilitator role. Following the small-group discussions, group leaders presented their ideas to the entire class. All presentations were listened to by the larger group, and a class-wide discussion was conducted. The instructor facilitated the discussion and provided feedback to support reflection and collective problem-solving. Although the Philips 66 brainstorming technique is based on short, structured small-group discussions, in this study, it was implemented within a broader interactive learning context to encourage students’ active participation, peer interaction, and reflection. The intervention was conducted as a single-session educational activity, and no formal follow-up actions were taken based on students’ suggestions. Data collection was performed face-to-face before the intervention (pre-test) and six weeks after the session (post-test), during which the course continued as part of the regular curriculum.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Frequency analysis was used to determine the distribution of participants’ descriptive characteristics. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test (all pre/post distributions met normality assumptions; p > .05) and Q–Q plots were also inspected. Primary analyses were conducted using paired-samples t-tests (two-tailed, α = 0.05). Effect sizes were computed as Cohen’s d for dependent means (mean change ÷ SD of the change scores), with 95% confidence intervals.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the university’s ethics committee (Date: 26 March 2024; Approval No: ETK00-2024-0081). Students were informed that participation was entirely voluntary and that withdrawal at any stage would not affect their education or course evaluations. Written and verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants. Confidentiality was ensured by using pseudonyms, and all data were pseudonymized before analysis. The intervention was conducted during a scheduled class session; however, participation in data collection was optional. The course instructor was also a member of the research team; however, several measures were implemented to minimize potential coercion, including emphasizing voluntary participation, ensuring that non-participation would not affect academic evaluation, and separating research participation from course grading processes. Students were clearly informed that their decision to participate or withdraw would have no impact on course grades, as grading criteria were predefined in the curriculum and calculated independently of research participation.

Results

The descriptive characteristics of the students are presented in Table 1. The mean age of the students was 23.19 ± 1.80 years, and 57.6% were women. It was found that 48.2% of the students’ mothers and 36.5% of their fathers had completed primary school. While 48.2% of the students reported having regular daily study habits, 52.9% indicated that they studied regularly for more than one hour per day. Additionally, 55.3% of the students voluntarily chose the nursing profession, and 63.5% expressed satisfaction with the nursing profession.

When examining the sub-dimensions, post-test scores were also significantly higher than pre-test scores for “Participation in the Course, Library, and Resource Access” (t = − 3.80, p < .001), “Communication with Faculty Members” (t = − 2.46, p = .016), and “Participation in Scientific and Cultural Activities” (t = − 3.64, p < .001). The observed effect sizes ranged from small to moderate (Cohen’s d = 0.25–0.42), indicating modest improvements in academic engagement following the intervention.

Discussion

The findings of this study indicate that the Philips 66 brainstorming technique was associated with improvements in students’ academic engagement scores following the intervention. Academic engagement reflects students’ active participation in learning processes and is considered an important indicator of meaningful learning experiences. Previous research conducted with nursing students has suggested that high levels of academic engagement are associated with active participation in academic activities and may also be linked to students’ motivation and attitudes toward learning [15]. Interactive and student-centered teaching approaches have been shown to support students’ engagement in academic activities [15, 18]. While increased engagement may be conceptually related to students’ sense of belonging within the educational environment, it should be noted that belonging was not directly measured in this study. Therefore, the observed changes should be interpreted as improvements in engagement-related behaviors and perceptions rather than direct evidence of enhanced belonging.

In the present study, 55.3% of students reported voluntarily choosing the nursing profession, and 63.5% expressed satisfaction with their choice. Students’ satisfaction levels are influenced by factors such as institutional opportunities, the learning environment, student engagement, and the services provided. Investigating student satisfaction offers educational institutions an opportunity to assess the quality of education and to support the achievement of educational goals for both instructors and students [16]. Ergün et al. (2021) reported that nursing students’ overall satisfaction with their education was at a moderate level, and that not only professional nursing education but also the opportunities and facilities provided to students influenced their satisfaction [17]. Additionally, a study by Mohamed et al. (2023) suggested that students’ awareness of university rules, resources, and values may be associated with their sense of belonging [11].

The Philips 66 brainstorming technique is a structured method that encourages students’ active participation in idea generation and enables them to propose solutions within a short time frame. Although the Philips 66 brainstorming technique is applied within a limited time frame, its potential effects may be understood in relation to the active learning processes it supports. Short-term, structured group interactions may facilitate cognitive engagement and peer communication and may increase students’ awareness of their roles within the academic environment [18]. In particular, sharing ideas, listening to peers, and participating in collective discussions may contribute to students’ sense of visibility, inclusion, and connection, which are considered to be components related to academic belonging [22, 27]. Moreover, the use of the Philips 66 brainstorming technique in large groups may provide opportunities for broader student participation in the learning process, which may otherwise be limited in traditional classroom settings. This type of participation may help students feel more involved in the academic environment. The literature suggests that participatory learning strategies are associated with students’ cognitive and affective engagement [19, 20]. In the present study, following sessions conducted using the Philips 66 brainstorming technique, increases were observed in students’ scores in the sub-dimensions of “Participation in the Course, Library, Resource Access” and “Communication with Faculty Members.” Academic interaction refers to the sharing of knowledge, attitudes, and skills between educators and students [21]. While such interactions may be related to students’ experiences within the educational environment, it should be noted that a sense of belonging was not directly measured in this study.

Rockinson-Szapkiw et al. (2014) conceptualized belonging as a form of social integration within the institution, characterized by feelings of attachment and the development of relationships with peers and faculty members [22]. In this context, the increase observed in the “Communication with Faculty Members” sub-dimension in our study may reflect more positive interactions between students and faculty members. Such interactions have been discussed in the literature as factors that may be associated with students’ sense of belonging. Similarly, the increase observed in the “Class Attendance and Resource Access” sub-dimension may indicate greater student participation in academic activities and more effective use of available resources. Indeed, Taff and Clifton (2022) emphasized the importance of strategies such as orientation programs, peer mentoring, and empathetic approaches by faculty members in supporting students’ experiences within higher education environments [23]. However, they also highlighted the need for further research to better understand the effectiveness of these strategies. From this perspective, the Philips 66 brainstorming technique applied in this study may be considered as a student-centered approach that supports interaction and participation in the learning process. Nevertheless, its potential relationship with students’ sense of belonging should be interpreted cautiously, as belonging was not directly measured in this study.

It has been reported that university activities, including academic and social events, are among the factors associated with students’ perceptions of belonging [24, 25]. In the present study, the increase observed in the “Participation in Scientific and Cultural Activities” sub-dimension may indicate greater student involvement in extracurricular academic and social activities. This finding appears to be consistent with previous literature. A qualitative study conducted with nursing and midwifery students reported that participation in extracurricular scientific activities may contribute to students’ competence and academic participation [25]. Similarly, participation in voluntary extracurricular group activities has been associated with students’ sense of institutional belonging and personal development [26]. From this perspective, the educational technique used in this study may be considered a structured, student-centered approach that facilitates active participation in group discussions.

In this study, the increase observed in nursing students’ total academic engagement scores following brainstorming sessions using the Philips 66 technique may indicate greater involvement in academic activities and interaction within the learning environment. In their systematic review and meta-analysis, van Kessel et al. (2025) reported a moderate to low positive relationship between university belonging and academic achievement and well-being [27]. Similarly, a systematic review by Allen et al. (2024) emphasized that belonging is a multidimensional concept influenced by institutional approaches, inclusive learning environments, faculty practices, and quality relationships [28]. The same study identified effective strategies to support belonging, including the use of technology, the creation of inclusive environments, and the implementation of diverse teaching methods. Within this context, the Philips 66 brainstorming technique used in this study may be considered as a student-centered approach that facilitates active participation and interaction in the learning process. Such features may be associated with students’ experiences within the academic environment.

It is recommended to integrate social interaction-based learning approaches into the education process to support students’ academic development [29]. The teaching technique used in this study, which involved considering students’ ideas, engaging them in generating solutions, and increasing their visibility within the group, may have facilitated students’ active participation and interaction in the learning process. Such features may be associated with students’ experiences within the academic environment and could be conceptually related to a sense of belonging. From this perspective, the Philips 66 brainstorming technique may be considered not only as a teaching method but also as an approach that supports interaction and participation in learning environments [30].

The Philips 66 brainstorming technique, which is used to develop students’ problem-solving skills, has also been applied in nursing education to support clinical reasoning and decision-making abilities. For example, a mixed-methods study combining the Philips 66 technique with case-based learning reported that active learning approaches were associated with improvements in students’ confidence and clinical judgment skills [31]. Additionally, Borrott et al. (2016) found that nursing students’ job satisfaction during clinical practice was associated with their sense of belonging [32]. These findings suggest that active learning approaches may support various aspects of students’ learning experiences, including academic skills and confidence. Within this context, the Philips 66 brainstorming technique used in the present study may be considered as an approach that facilitates active participation and engagement in the learning process.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted with a specific group of students at a single university, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Second, the use of a single-group pretest–posttest quasi-experimental design without a control group restricts internal validity and makes it difficult to attribute observed changes solely to the Philips 66 brainstorming technique. Potential confounding factors, such as students’ ongoing course experiences, academic interactions, and the natural progression over the six weeks, may also have influenced the results. In addition, reliance on self-reported data may introduce response bias, and the study was limited to a specific time frame, which may not capture longer-term effects. Another important limitation concerns the scope of measurement. Although this study is conceptually grounded in academic belonging, academic engagement was used as a proxy measure. While these constructs are closely related, they are theoretically distinct. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution, as the Academic Engagement Scale does not fully capture the multidimensional nature of academic belonging.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that the Philips 66 brainstorming technique is a promising method for supporting nursing students’ academic engagement. Given the limited literature on the use of this technique in relation to academic belonging, this study provides a meaningful contribution by highlighting its potential role within nursing education. Considering that a sense of belonging has been reported in the literature to be associated with academic success and various positive educational and professional outcomes, the integration of active learning strategies, such as the Philips 66 brainstorming technique, into nursing curricula may support students’ engagement and learning experiences and offer a practical approach for incorporating active, student-centered learning strategies into nursing education. Future studies employing randomized controlled or comparative designs are recommended to more rigorously evaluate the effectiveness of the Philips 66 brainstorming technique and to strengthen causal interpretations. Additionally, incorporating qualitative or mixed-method approaches may provide a deeper understanding of how and why this technique influences students’ learning processes and experiences within educational settings.
