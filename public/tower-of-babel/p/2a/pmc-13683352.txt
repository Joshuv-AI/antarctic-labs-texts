Multi-omics reveals tumor microenvironment heterogeneity and therapeutic vulnerabilities in peritoneal metastasis of gastric cancer
Authors: Wen Xie, Zhenfa Wang, Peiyu Lv, Jia Cheng, Lingyun Lin, Jingjing Hou, Xujin Wei, Kunzhai Huang, Huiqin Zhuo, Wangyu Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Peritoneal metastasis of gastric cancer (PMGC) is associated with a poor prognosis, with approximately one-third of patients presenting with peritoneal metastasis at diagnosis. Current systemic therapies have limited efficacy, underscoring the need for precise therapeutic targets in PMGC. We employed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to characterize the cellular composition of the PMGC microenvironment, reconstruct epithelial cell developmental trajectories, and analyze intercellular communication networks. Spatial transcriptomics and multiplex immunohistochemistry were integrated to validate targets, and in vivo mouse models were used to assess therapeutic efficacy. Single-cell analysis identified a peritoneal metastasis (PM) signature associated with cancer cell differentiation origin and poor outcomes. We classified cancer cells as PM-high or PM-low based on this signature, with the PM-high group enriched in multiple oncogenic pathways and the PM-low group overexpressing CLDN18. Intercellular communication analyses showed epithelial cells and diverse subsets interact with regulatory T, natural killer, and proliferative T cells via PVR-TIGIT and NECTIN2-TIGIT. In vivo studies indicated that dual targeting of TIGIT and Claudin 18.2 (CLDN18.2) inhibited PMGC progression and ascites formation. These findings indicate that CLDN18.2 and TIGIT are potential therapeutic targets, whose combined inhibition suppresses PMGC progression and ascites formation, providing valuable insights for PMGC treatment.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) ranks fifth worldwide in both incidence and cancer-related mortality1. The peritoneum is a common site of metastasis in GC, with nearly one-third of patients presenting with peritoneal metastasis at diagnosis, and a median survival of less than six months without treatment2. Despite current therapies demonstrating some efficacy in treating PMGC patients, their overall therapeutic benefit remains limited3,4. In addition, PD-1 and PD-L1 expression is relatively low in ascitic cells from PMGC5, indicating limited therapeutic potential in this context. Therefore, elucidating the molecular heterogeneity and immunosuppressive mechanisms of PMGC is essential for optimizing existing therapies and developing novel targeted strategies.

GC cells reach the peritoneum and interact with its unique microenvironment, creating a niche that promotes tumor seeding and colonization, thereby emphasizing the specialized characteristics of the PMGC tumor microenvironment (TME). During the progression of metastatic cancer, cancer cells dynamically alter their immunogenic characteristics, resulting in the remodeling of immune cells and ultimately creating an immune-evasive TME6,7. Recent studies have analyzed intratumoral heterogeneity and lineage diversity in primary gastric cancer and peritoneal metastatic sites8,9; however, direct access to peritoneal metastasis samples from PMGC patients remains rare. Furthermore, the spatiotemporal heterogeneity, plasticity, and reciprocal interactions within the multicellular ecosystem of PMGC, particularly regarding the evolution of therapeutic targets and immune checkpoints, create substantial challenges in effectively targeting these cells and reprogramming them into an antitumor phenotype. These challenges highlight the need for comprehensive profiling of the TME in PMGC, which can be gradually addressed through high-throughput scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics.

Recent advances in PD-1/PD-L1–based immunotherapies have significantly improved the treatment landscape for advanced GC10. However, the effective management of metastatic GC remains a considerable challenge11,12. With the identification of novel immunoregulatory targets and mechanisms, emerging therapies offer potential for enhancing clinical outcomes in patients with PMGC. TIGIT, a T cell immunoreceptor with immunoglobulin and ITIM domain, interacts with CD155 (PVR cell adhesion molecule, PVR) and CD112 (Nectin cell adhesion molecule 2, NECTIN2) in certain cancers, inhibiting T cell activation and NK cell function13–15. Notably, TIGIT-targeted therapies have shown promising initial results in several clinical trials involving cancer16,17; however, studies specifically addressing their therapeutic potential in the context of PMGC remain limited, underscoring the need for further research to explore their potential in the immunotherapy of PMGC.

Claudin 18.2 (CLDN18.2), a tight junction protein expressed in non-malignant gastric epithelium, is retained and exposed on tumor cell surfaces during malignant transformation18. Furthermore, its presence in a significant proportion of both primary gastric cancers and metastatic lesions makes it a promising target for GC therapy19. Recent results from two Phase III trials of zolbetuximab, a monoclonal antibody targeting CLDN18.2, showed significant improvements in progression-free and overall survival in patients with unresectable gastric cancer, suggesting CLDN18.2 as a viable therapeutic target for CLDN18.2-positive cancers20,21. However, the dynamics of CLDN18.2 expression in malignant epithelial cells during the progression of PMGC, as well as the feasibility of CLDN18.2-targeted therapy for PMGC, require further investigation.

In this study, we employed high-throughput scRNA-seq to characterize 41,118 high-quality cells derived from peritoneal metastasis samples of PMGC patients. By integrating scRNA-seq analysis with spatial transcriptomics and multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC), we revealed the heterogeneous characteristics of PMGC malignant cells and the immunosuppressive TME mediated by the TIGIT-PVR/NECTIN2 axis. Functional analyses in humanized PMGC mouse models confirmed the therapeutic efficacy of dual targeting of TIGIT and CLDN18.2, highlighting this approach as a promising strategy for PMGC immunotherapies.

Results

Construction of a single-cell transcriptomic atlas of human PMGC

To investigate the cellular landscape of the PMGC TME, we performed scRNA-seq on tumor samples from four PMGC patients (Supplementary Table 1) and integrated these data with previously published scRNA-seq datasets from four additional patients (Fig. 1A)8,22. After quality control and filtering, 41,118 high-quality cells from PMGC samples were identified for subsequent analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1). The cell clusters were annotated based on canonical marker genes, including T cells (CD2, CD3E, CD3D), EMF cells (comprising epithelial cells, mesothelial cells, and fibroblasts marked by KRT18, TFF1, EPCAM, KRT19, MSLN, UPK3B, LUM, DCN, COL1A1), myeloid cells (CD68, CD14, CD163), endothelial cells (PECAM1, PLVAP, VWF), myocytes (MYL9, ACTA2, NDUFA4L2), plasma cells (IGHG1, MZB1, DERL3), B cells (MS4A1, BANK1, CD79A) and mast cells (CPA3, TPSB2, KIT) (Fig. 1B, 1C). To further investigate the EMF cells, we re-clustered and annotated the cells using known marker genes, classifying the cells into epithelial cells (EPCAM, TFF1, MUC1), mesothelial cells (MSLN, CALB2, KRT19), and fibroblasts (LUM, MMP2, THY1) (Fig. 1D, 1E). Epithelial cell heterogeneity and developmental trajectory in PMGC

Given the high heterogeneity of malignant epithelial cells in PMGC, we re-clustered epithelial cells and identified three subclusters (Epi1–Epi3; Fig. 2A), which differed in expression of stemness-related genes (CD44, SOX9) and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT)-related genes (MMP7, LAMC223) (Fig. 2B). Subsequently, CytoTRACE was used to predict the differentiation states of epithelial cells, revealing that Epi1 had the highest differentiation potential, followed by Epi2, with Epi3 exhibiting the lowest (Fig. 2C). This suggests that malignant epithelial cells may undergo differentiation changes during PMGC progression, leading to increased heterogeneity. Building on these findings, we conducted pseudotime analysis to further explore the differentiation patterns of epithelial cells. Consistent with the CytoTRACE results, we observed a distinct pseudotime trajectory, with the Epi1 cluster located at the starting point and Epi2 and Epi3 positioned at the middle and later stages of the trajectory (Fig. 2D, 2E). Considering the dynamic nature of peritoneal colonization and cancer cell metastasis, we visualized gene modules that co-express across pseudotime and defined pseudotime-dependent genes enriched at the start of the trajectory as the PM signature (Fig. 2F), representing the gene expression profile of cancer cells at the onset of peritoneal metastasis in GC. To assess the prognostic impact of the PM signature in GC patients, we stratified the TCGA STAD cohort into two groups based on PM signature scores: high (n = 160) and low (n = 177), with the high PM signature group associated with a poorer prognosis (Fig. 2G). Molecular identity of epithelial cells in PMGC

To further investigate the classification and molecular characteristics of epithelial cells in PMGC, we calculated a peritoneal metastasis (PM) signature score for each epithelial cell. Based on this score, epithelial cells were classified into PM-high and PM-low epithelial states, representing cells with relatively high versus low PM signature scores, respectively, using the median value as the cutoff. The analysis showed that the PM-high group included nearly all Epi1 cells, the majority of Epi2 cells, and a small portion of Epi3 cells, whereas the PM-low group was predominantly composed of Epi3 and Epi2 cells (Fig. 3A, 3B). To explore the correlation between PM-high cells, which represent malignant epithelial cells at the early stage of the pseudotime trajectory during PMGC progression, and ascitic GC cells, we analyzed published scRNA-seq data from ascites associated with PMGC5. Given the crucial roles of EMT, TGF-β, and Hippo signaling pathways in the peritoneal metastasis of GC3,24,25, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) based on differentially expressed genes between PM-high and PM-low epithelial cell populations. This analysis revealed that genes upregulated in PM-high epithelial cells were significantly enriched in EMT, TGF-β, and Hippo signaling pathways (Supplementary Fig. 2A-C, Supplementary Data 1), with higher gene set scores observed in PM-high compared with PM-low cells (Fig. 3D). These findings suggest that PM-high epithelial cells may play a pivotal role in the initiation and progression of peritoneal metastasis in GC. Next, we inferred the transcriptional regulatory networks for each epithelial cell group. The results showed that PM-high cells specifically exhibit the highest transcriptional activity of TEAD1 (Fig. 3E, 3F), which has been reported to bind to YAP/TAZ, thereby promoting the development of a pro-tumor phenotype and facilitating peritoneal metastasis26,27. Notably, differential expression analysis revealed that CLDN18 was upregulated in PM-low epithelial cells (Fig. 3G). A consistent trend was observed in an independent scRNA-seq cohort (Supplementary Fig. 3A-E). Given that CLDN18.2, a splicing variant of CLDN18, is predominantly expressed in gastric epithelium18,28, we performed histological analysis of its expression in distant metastases of GC. The mIHC staining suggested a heterogeneous distribution of CLDN18.2 among CK-positive cells within metastatic lesions (Supplementary Fig. 4A). To further validate our findings, we analyzed an independent cohort (Cohort 2) consisting of 42 primary GC and 27 distant metastatic lesions. While CLDN18.2 expression was slightly reduced in metastatic sites compared to primary tumors, this reduction was not statistically significant (Supplementary Fig. 4B-D), indicating that GC metastatic lesions continue to exhibit some of the CLDN18.2 expression features of the primary tumor. Taken together, these results suggest that the progenitor malignant epithelial cells may exhibit multiple pro-tumor and metastatic molecular features while potentially partially retaining CLDN18.2 expression features similar to those of gastric epithelium and primary GC.

TIGIT-mediated immunosuppressive intercellular communication in the PMGC TME

Immune cells in the TME are able to modulate tumor progression, influencing both tumor behavior and response to treatment29–31. We analyzed the predominant cell population in the TME, the T cell cluster, and identified non-Treg CD4 T cells (nT CD4, CD4, CCR7), regulatory T cells (Treg, FOXP3, CTLA4), CD8 T cells (CD8B, GZMK), natural killer (NK) cells (FCGR3A, TYROBP), and proliferative T cells (Pro T, MKI67, TOP2A) (Fig. 4A, 4B). To further investigate immunosuppressive intercellular communication within the TME, CellChat was used to analyze receptor-ligand interactions among the major cell populations. We observed that Treg cells and PM-high epithelial cells engage in communication with NK and Pro T cells via the PVR–TIGIT ligand–receptor pair, whereas interactions mediated by the NECTIN2–TIGIT axis involved multiple cell types within the tumor microenvironment, including epithelial cells (PM-high and PM-low), fibroblasts (Fib), mesothelial cells (Meso), myocytes (Myo), myeloid cells, and endothelial cells (Endo), interacting with Treg cells, NK cells, and Pro T cells (Fig. 4C–F). Similar tumor cell–Treg interaction patterns involving PVR–TIGIT and NECTIN2–TIGIT were observed in an independent cohort (Supplementary Fig. 3F–G). To further characterize the spatial context of these immunosuppressive interactions in PMGC, we performed deconvolution of spatial transcriptomics data to delineate tumor-enriched regions (PM_high_Zone and PM_low_Zone) and Treg-enriched regions (Fig. 4G, 4H). Next, we validated the spatial expression patterns of PVR, NECTIN2, and TIGIT in PMGC tissues. Similar to the single-cell communication analysis, spatial transcriptomics revealed that NECTIN2 was most widely distributed in PMGC tissues, followed by PVR, with TIGIT sporadically distributed throughout the tumor (Supplementary Fig. 5E). Dual Targeting of TIGIT and CLDN18.2 to Inhibit PMGC Progression

Building on our findings that epithelial cells in PMGC establish an immunosuppressive TME via TIGIT-mediated signaling, and that a subset of these cells expresses the gastric epithelial-specific protein CLDN18.2, we noted that a previous study demonstrated potent antitumor activity of CLDN18.2-targeted therapy in CLDN18.2-positive solid tumors, an effect further enhanced by combination with PD-1 blockade28. We therefore hypothesized that dual targeting of TIGIT and CLDN18.2 might enhance tumor suppression in PMGC. To test this hypothesis, we constructed a PMGC mouse model through intraperitoneal injection of NUGC4-hCLDN18.2-Luc cells into huHSC-NCG-hIL15 humanized mice, with monoclonal antibody (mAb) administration initiated 6 days post-implantation. (G1: Vehicle, G2: hTIGIT mAb, G3: hTIGIT mAb+hCLDN18.2 mAb) (Fig. 6A). We evaluated the hCD45+ cell proportion, tumor burden, and body weight across all groups of mice prior to treatment, with no significant differences detected (Supplementary Fig. 7A-C). While tumor progression was attenuated in both the G2 and G3 groups, the G3 group achieved a higher tumor growth inhibition (TGI), indicating a more pronounced inhibitory effect (Fig. 6B-D; the data in Fig. 6C are also shown in Supplementary Fig. 7D). Additionally, regardless of whether evaluated by fluorescence intensity or metastatic spread, mice in the G3 group showed a lower peritoneal metastatic burden (Fig. 6E). Given that ascites formation is a prevalent symptom in patients with peritoneal metastasis32, we further evaluated the ascites volume in mice at the experimental endpoint. Although no significant differences in body weight were observed among the groups at the experimental endpoint (Fig. 6F), the G3 group exhibited the least ascites accumulation (Fig. 6G). Notably, the ascites volume in the G2 group was comparable to that in the G1 group, indicating that the combination of TIGIT mAb and CLDN18.2 mAb suppresses ascites formation in PMGC. Single-cell analysis reveals distinct expression patterns in the metastatic microenvironment of humanized mice after targeted therapy

To investigate transcriptional changes in cellular subpopulations within the metastatic microenvironment, we performed scRNA-seq on metastatic niches from different treatment groups (three mice per group), identifying human cells for subsequent analysis (Fig. 8A, Supplementary Fig. 8). To characterize transcriptional profiles of malignant epithelial cells across treatment groups, we employed non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) to define co-expressed gene programs within each tumor, which represent gene modules highly expressed by malignant epithelial cells in each tumor. We identified 56 such programs from nine samples and further hierarchical clustering analysis revealed four distinct meta-programs (MPs) (Fig. 8B). Each MP exhibited a distinct gene signature, prompting us to perform functional enrichment analysis on the four MPs. MP1 was predominantly associated with oncogenic signaling pathways, including TGF-β, Wnt, and MAPK, thereby reflecting malignant tumor characteristics within the cancer cells. MP3 was characterized by genes involved in the regulation of adaptive immune responses, innate immune responses, and NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity. The higher gene set score for MP3 was observed in the G3 combination therapy group, suggesting an immune activation state and functionality associated with targeted therapy. To assess treatment-associated changes in PM-high and PM-low composition, PM signature scores were used to classify epithelial cells as PM-high or PM-low across treatment groups (Supplementary Fig. 10A–B). Among epithelial cells remaining after treatment, the G1 showed a higher prevalence of PM-low epithelial cells relative to PM-high cells. Following G2, the relative fraction of PM-high epithelial cells was highest among the three groups, suggesting that treatment-associated tumor cell loss may leave a residual subset with malignant potential and enriched for PM-high like features. In the combination group (G3), the PM-high fraction was reduced compared with that in G2. This observation may reflect anti-CLDN18.2 further depleting a subset of malignant epithelial cells that persist after anti-TIGIT alone, while leaving a residual population characterized by malignant potential and PM-high like features. Following G3 treatment, we analyzed cell–cell communication between residual malignant tumor cells and human immune cells in the humanized model. In this setting, residual malignant cells classified as PM-high or PM-low continued to show interactions involving PVR–TIGIT and NECTIN2–TIGIT with Tregs and ProT cells (Supplementary Fig. 10C–F). A subset of interaction patterns reported in human PMGC, including those involving NK cells and signaling between Tregs and NK or ProT cells, were not observed in the G3 dataset. Overall, these results suggest that treatment targeting TIGIT and CLDN18.2 may remodel the TME of PMGC, enhancing the immune response.

Discussion

The prognosis for patients with PMGC remains poor, partly due to the intricate cellular composition of the TME and the limited investigation in this area. Furthermore, the limited efficacy of current therapeutic approaches highlights the urgent need for multi-omics analyses to delineate the TME of GC metastatic lesions and identify more effective therapeutic targets. In this study, we used scRNA-seq to uncover the heterogeneity and dynamic evolution of GC cells in peritoneal metastasis. Through the integration of spatial transcriptomics with mIHC analysis of PMGC samples, we identified CLDN18.2 as a specific target in a subset of cancer cells, and TIGIT as a key target mediating immune suppression within the TME. In vivo experiments using humanized mouse models indicated that dual targeting of TIGIT and CLDN18.2 inhibited PMGC progression and reduced ascites formation. These findings suggest that the combined inhibition of TIGIT and CLDN18.2 may attenuate PMGC progression and offer a promising therapeutic strategy.

In terms of the heterogeneity and dynamic evolution of GC cells in peritoneal metastasis, our data constructed a cellular atlas of PMGC and identified three distinct malignant epithelial cell clusters. CytoTRACE analysis revealed that the Epi1 cells exhibited pronounced stemness and differentiation potential. Consistently, pseudotime analysis showed that Epi1 cells were primarily located at the starting point of the pseudotime trajectory, leading us to hypothesize that the Epi1 cluster may represent the progenitor population of cancer cells in metastatic lesions. Moreover, we defined a set of highly expressed genes at the initiation point of the pseudotime trajectory as the PM signature, which includes genes such as NNMT, CD44, FN1, integrin-related genes, MMP7, and LAMC2. NNMT has been shown to promote gastric cancer by depleting S-adenosylmethionine, reducing H3K27 trimethylation, and activating the Wnt pathway, enhancing AQP5+ cells stemness and tumor development34. CD44, well-established as a stem cell marker in tumor biology35,36, also binds to hyaluronan on peritoneal mesothelial cells, playing a pivotal role in mediating adhesion during peritoneal metastasis37. Additionally, previous studies have shown that tumor cells can adhere to peritoneal mesothelial cells via integrins38 and promote fibronectin deposition, leading to peritoneal fibrosis and increased tumor cell adhesion39. Moreover, MMP7 and LAMC2 have been implicated in promoting tumor invasion and metastasis at the invasive front of GC40. Collectively, these findings support the notion that the elevated expression of PM signature genes in epithelial cells of PMGC contributes to establishing a niche that promotes peritoneal metastasis.

Recently, accumulating evidence has shown that oncogenic pathways, including EMT, TGF-β, and Hippo, are highly expressed in cancer cells from ascites in gastric cancer peritoneal metastasis5,41. In line with these findings, we observed increased expression of these pathways in epithelial cells from PMGC tissue samples, with particularly high levels in the PM-high group. This suggests that epithelial cells in the PM-high group may have enhanced metastatic potential and malignant characteristics. Meanwhile, CLDN18 was selectively upregulated in epithelial cells from the PM-low group. Previous studies have demonstrated that CLDN18.2 is predominantly expressed in differentiated gastric mucosal epithelial cells, absent in the gastric stem cell zone, and remains expressed during malignant transformation42. This is consistent with our findings, where the PM-high group represents a subpopulation with pronounced stem-like features, while the PM-low group shows elevated CLDN18 expression. Given the epithelial heterogeneity driven by distinct transcriptional profiles in PM-high and PM-low cells, the retained expression of CLDN18.2 in metastatic lesions, and its potential as a specific therapeutic target in GC, CLDN18.2 may represent a promising treatment target for PMGC patients.

Immune cells within the TME can exhibit both immunosuppressive and tumor-promoting functions43,44, contributing to the complexity of cellular interactions that define the TME. Previous reports have shown that in colorectal cancer patients with liver metastasis, the Treg-IL10 and Treg-CTLA4 cells are elevated in primary tumors compared to those without liver metastasis45. Additionally, it has been reported that Treg cells are more abundant in metastatic lesions of gallbladder cancer compared to primary tumors46. These findings suggest that tumors may develop a more immunosuppressive microenvironment, characterized by increased Treg cell activity. In this study, we observed that Treg cells were extensively involved in cell-to-cell communication within the TME of PMGC. Treg and PM-high epithelial cells communicated with NK and Pro T cells via the PVR-TIGIT axis. Various TME cell types interacted with Treg, NK, and Pro T cells through NECTIN2-TIGIT interactions. Additionally, both PVR and NECTIN2 were upregulated in metastatic lesions compared to primary tumors. The PVR-TIGIT axis was shown to not only promote immune evasion within tumors47,48 but also enhance metastasis by impairing the immune surveillance function of NK cells14. Similarly, the NECTIN2-TIGIT axis has been shown to play a pivotal role in establishing an immunosuppressive microenvironment across various tumor types15,49–51. Our data provide evidence that interactions among various cell subpopulations and immune cells within the TME of PMGC may contribute to the establishment of an immunosuppressive niche, promoting immune evasion and reducing therapeutic efficacy.

NK cells are a key component of the innate immune system and play a critical role in tumor surveillance, with their cytotoxic activity inhibiting tumor metastasis52,53. Previous studies have demonstrated an increased proportion of cytotoxic NK cells during metastasis compared to primary tumors, underscoring their importance in tumor control54. In our therapeutic study conducted in vivo with animal models, we observed that NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity, upregulation of cell-killing pathways, and innate immune regulation were significantly enhanced in the treatment group compared to controls, suggesting that NK cells may be pivotal mediators of the therapeutic response. Moreover, the expression of GZMA and GZMB, well-established markers of NK cell cytotoxicity55–57, was markedly elevated in the treatment group, indicating a strengthened anti-tumor immune response following therapy. Notably, combined inhibition of TIGIT and CLDN18.2 increased XCL1, a chemokine that recruits cDC1 into the TME, enhancing anti-tumor immunity33. Consistent with our findings, a previous study has shown that blocking TIGIT prevents NK cell exhaustion and enhances anti-tumor immune responses58. Together, these data suggest that NK cells play an essential role in tumor control during metastasis and warrant further investigation.

While this study validated key insights from our single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses, the scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics cohort (Cohort 1) did not include patient-matched primary tumors, thereby limiting direct comparison of microenvironmental programs during the transition from primary tumor to peritoneal metastasis. To partially address this limitation, we performed mIHC-based validation of key protein targets in an independent cohort (Cohort 2) comprising primary GC specimens and distant metastatic lesions, including peritoneal metastases. Future studies should prospectively collect matched primary tumors and peritoneal metastases from the same patient, with sampling across clearly defined clinical time points. Analysis of these paired specimens would facilitate direct assessment of microenvironmental remodeling and metastatic progression. Furthermore, current technological limitations confine single-cell and spatial transcriptomics to small regions of tumor tissue, preventing a complete assessment of the TME heterogeneity in resected specimens. Given the substantial inter- and intratumoral heterogeneity of tumor tissues, developing more advanced and cost-effective approaches for comprehensive analysis of entire tumor specimens will be crucial for identifying personalized therapeutic targets. Additionally, the observations in this study capture only a static view of the TME in PMGC, lacking dynamic monitoring or longitudinal tracking of peritoneal metastasis in GC. Future research should focus on establishing spontaneous mouse models of PMGC to elucidate the origins and progression of metastasis. This would provide deeper insights into the cellular composition and interactions within the TME at different stages, facilitating the development of immunotherapeutic strategies tailored to specific TME components.

Overall, we revealed the tumor heterogeneity and potential immune evasion mechanisms underlying PMGC. Through multi-omics approaches, we identified CLDN18.2 as a target specifically expressed in a subset of epithelial cells within PMGC. Furthermore, interactions between PVR-TIGIT and NECTIN2-TIGIT receptor-ligand pairs may contribute to the establishment of an immunosuppressive microenvironment, promoting PMGC progression. In vivo mouse models indicated that dual inhibition of CLDN18.2 and TIGIT suppressed PMGC progression and ascites formation. These findings offer valuable insights for the development of targeted therapeutic strategies for PMGC.

Methods

Human specimens

Specimens from four patients with PMGC (Cohort 1; Supplementary Table 1), collected at Zhongshan Hospital of Xiamen University, were subjected to scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomic analysis. These specimens were histologically confirmed as PMGC through intraoperative frozen-section analysis and routine postoperative pathological evaluation. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to sample collection, and the study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Hospital, Xiamen University (No. XMZSYYKY2021092). All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed. Paraffin-embedded tissue microarrays, comprising 42 primary GC specimens and 27 distant metastatic lesions from Stage IV GC patients (Cohort2), were obtained from the National Engineering Research Center for Biochips (Outdo Biotech, Shanghai, China). The metastatic lesions included 9 peritoneal metastases (PMGC) and metastases to the liver (n = 6), colon (n = 4), soft tissues (n = 6), pancreas (n = 1), and pubis (n = 1).

Mice and cell lines

Age-matched female huHSC-NCG-hIL15 mice (huCD34+HSC-NOD/ShiLtJGpt-Prkdcem26Cd52Il2rgem26Cd22Il15em1Cin(hIL15)/Gpt) were obtained from GemPharmatech (Jiangsu, China). Age-matched male and female C57BL/6 mice were obtained from the Xiamen University Laboratory Animal Center. All animal experiments performed in this study were approved by the animal ethics committee of the Xiamen University Laboratory Animal Center (No. SYXK2018-0009). All mice were maintained, and experimental procedures were conducted in strict accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Because this intraperitoneal model develops multiple peritoneal lesions and ascites rather than a single externally measurable tumor mass, tumor volume could not be reliably quantified by linear distance measurements in living mice. For the intraperitoneal tumor models, the maximal tumor burden was defined according to prespecified humane endpoint criteria based on the overall clinical condition of the mice, including >20% loss of baseline body weight, marked abdominal distension with increased abdominal wall tension or labored breathing, markedly reduced spontaneous activity with hunched posture, ruffled fur, or lethargy, or inability to eat or drink for >24 h. Mice were to be humanely euthanized if any of these criteria were met. No mice exceeded the maximal permitted tumor burden in any experimental group.

The NUGC4-hClaudin18.2(Tg)-Luciferase human gastric cancer cell line, generated by lentiviral transduction of parental NUGC4 cells with human CLDN18.2 cDNA and a luciferase reporter, was obtained from GemPharmatech (Jiangsu, China), and the MC38 cell line was purchased from Cyagen Biosciences (Suzhou, China), authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and confirmed to be mycoplasma-free.

ScRNA-seq library preparation and sequencing

Freshly resected tissue samples were cleaned to remove surface contaminants and residual blood, and subsequently cut into approximately 1 mm³ fragments. The fragments were enzymatically digested and filtered to obtain a single-cell suspension, followed by red blood cell lysis. The resulting cells were resuspended in PBS buffer containing BSA and subsequently counted. The scRNA-seq libraries were prepared according to the manufacturer’s protocol (10x Genomics), using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3ʹ GEM, Library & Gel Bead Kit and Chromium Next GEM Chip G Single Cell Kit for human tissue samples, and the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 5’ Kit and Chromium Next GEM Chip K Single Cell Kit for mouse tissue samples. Sequencing was conducted on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using a 150 bp paired-end strategy (performed by CapitalBio Technology, Beijing).

ScRNA-seq data processing and quality control

ScRNA-seq data from eight human PMGC samples were analyzed, comprising four generated in this study and four sourced from the previously published datasets (GSE1839048 and GSE16355822). Seurat objects were created in R (v4.3.1) using Seurat (v4.4.0). For each cell, the proportions of mitochondrial and erythrocyte gene expression were calculated. DoubletFinder (v2.0.3) was subsequently employed to detect potential doublets in each sample. Following doublet removal, cells were filtered for downstream analysis based on the following quality control criteria: (1) genes expressed in at least three cells; (2) cells expressing between 500 and 6000 genes; (3) cells with fewer than 50,000 UMIs; (4) mitochondrial gene content below 20%; and (5) erythrocyte gene content below 0.1%.

To validate our findings in an independent cohort, we analyzed a publicly available scRNA-seq dataset of peritoneal metastasis from gastric adenocarcinoma9 (EGA accession: EGAS00001004443). One sample (Pt13) was excluded based on cohort-level inspection. Raw data were processed using a standardized quality-control pipeline, removing cells with low gene complexity (nFeature_RNA ≤ 500), excessively high gene or UMI counts (top 1% by nFeature_RNA or nCount_RNA), or high mitochondrial transcript content (percent.mt > 20%).

Cells of human origin were extracted from the scRNA-seq data of humanized mice and further filtered according to the following criteria: (1) genes expressed in at least three cells; (2) cells expressing between 500 and 6500 genes; (3) cells with fewer than 40,000 UMIs; (4) mitochondrial gene content below 20%; and (5) erythrocyte gene content below 0.1%.

ScRNA-seq data integration and cell annotation

The filtered data were normalized using the SCTransform in Seurat. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed with RunPCA. Multiple samples were integrated, and batch effects were corrected using Harmony (v1.0). Clustering analysis was conducted with FindNeighbors and FindClusters, and the clusters were visualized using UMAP.

To annotate cell types within each cluster, the FindAllMarkers function in Seurat was applied to identify cluster-specific differentially expressed genes. Clusters were then assigned to cell types based on canonical marker genes. In the cell annotation process, subpopulations were classified as ‘EMF other,’ ‘Tcell other,’ or ‘Other,’ representing cell subsets that could not be identified.

Trajectory analysis

We employed Monocle2 (v2.22.0) to investigate the developmental lineage of distinct epithelial cell clusters, utilizing the DDRTree algorithm to reconstruct their developmental trajectories. To determine the root of the trajectory, we incorporated CytoTRACE (v0.3.3), selecting the subpopulation with the highest CytoTRACE score as the starting point. The expression dynamics of pseudotime-dependent genes along the pseudotime trajectory were visualized using a heatmap.

Survival analysis

Bulk RNA sequencing data and clinical information for the GC cohort (TCGA-STAD) were obtained from UCSC-XENA for subsequent survival analysis. Patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups based on GSVA enrichment scores for the PM signature gene set, using the optimal cut-off value. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated using the Survival (v2.43-3) and Survminer (v0.4.4) packages, with statistical significance assessed by the log-rank test.

PM signature

The PM signature was derived based on pseudotime trajectory analysis using Monocle2. Epithelial cells were ordered along pseudotime, and pseudotime-associated genes were identified using the differentialGeneTest function in Monocle2 and ranked based on FDR-adjusted q values. The top 100 pseudotime-associated genes were grouped into three co-expression modules using plot_pseudotime_heatmap (num_clusters = 3). Genes belonging to the second module (Cluster 2) were defined as the PM signature, and the complete gene list is provided in Supplementary Data 2.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

Gene set enrichment analysis was performed to assess pathway-level differences between PM-high and PM-low epithelial cells. Genes were ranked by average log2 fold change (avg_log2FC) from the PM-high versus PM-low differential expression analysis within the epithelial compartment. Enrichment analysis was conducted using the MSigDB Hallmark (H) and C6 (Oncogenic signatures) collections, implemented with the clusterProfiler package (v4.2.2). Enrichment plots were generated using the enrichplot package (v1.22.0). Statistical significance was evaluated using FDR-adjusted p values < 0.05.

Signature scores

Signature scores were calculated at the single-cell level using AUCell (v1.16.0). Gene expression rankings were constructed for each cell based on the normalized RNA expression matrix, and signature activity was quantified using AUCell_calcAUC. For each gene set, the resulting AUC value was used as the corresponding signature score. For the PM signature, epithelial cells were further stratified into PM-high and PM-low groups based on the median PM signature AUC value across all epithelial cells. The ascites signature score was derived from a gene set curated based on a previously published study5, and signaling pathway activities were assessed using EMT59, TGF-β59, and YAP60 gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB). Details of these gene sets are provided in Supplementary Data 2. The MP signature score for each MP was calculated using a gene set consisting of 350 characteristic genes specific to that MP.

Gene regulatory network inference

The pySCENIC (v0.10.3) was employed to infer gene regulatory networks. GRNboost was used to identify potential target genes, followed by RcisTarget for enrichment analysis of regulatory regions to infer regulons. AUCell calculated AUC scores to assess regulon activity in individual cells. Cluster-specific regulons were visualized based on specificity scores.

Multiplex immunohistochemistry

The mIHC staining was performed using the Opal 7-color IHC Kit (PerkinElmer, NEL801001KT) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, tissue sections were baked at 63 °C, deparaffinized with xylene, and rehydrated. Following antigen retrieval, the sections were incubated in blocking buffer for 10 min, then incubated with primary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, followed by secondary antibodies for 10 min. Tyramide signal amplification (TSA) with Opal fluorophores was applied for 10 min. The primary and secondary antibodies were then removed, and the cycle was repeated. After all antibody labeling steps were completed, nuclei were stained with DAPI. The primary antibodies used included PVR (Abcam, Cat#ab267788), NECTIN2 (Abcam, Cat#ab233384), CLDN18.2 (Abcam, Cat#ab222512), Pan-CK (Abcarta, Cat#PA125), TIGIT (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat#ZRB1454), and CD3 (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat#85061S). Imaging was performed using the TissueFAXS Spectra imaging system (TissueGnostics).

Region-based quantification of immunofluorescence signals was performed using Fiji (ImageJ v1.54p, National Institutes of Health). Background signal was estimated from adjacent regions lacking specific staining and subtracted from raw intensities. Background-corrected values were used for subsequent analyses.

Immunohistochemistry

Tissue sections were baked at 63 °C for 1 h, deparaffinized in xylene, and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed in EDTA buffer, followed by natural cooling to room temperature. The sections underwent endogenous peroxidase blocking, followed by separate incubation with primary antibodies against TIGIT (1:100; Biointron) or CLDN18.2 (1:100; Biointron) overnight at 4 °C. After being brought to room temperature for 45 min, sections were incubated with the appropriate secondary antibody. Immunoreactivity was visualized using diaminobenzidine (DAB), followed by hematoxylin counterstaining, differentiation in 0.25% acid alcohol, and dehydration prior to mounting with neutral resin.

Intercellular communication analysis

To explore the potential cell-cell communication networks among cell types in the TME of PMGC, CellChat (v1.4.0) was utilized for intercellular communication analysis. The analysis object was constructed using the createCellChat function, and potential receptor-ligand interactions within the microenvironment were inferred based on the CellChatDB.human database.

Spatial transcriptomics library preparation and sequencing

Tissue samples were cleaned to remove surface contaminants and residual blood, taking care to avoid excessive compression of the tissue. The samples were snap-frozen using isopentane and liquid nitrogen, followed by embedding in OCT. After RNA quality assessment and tissue morphology evaluation, frozen sections were cut into 10 μm-thick slices. Spatial libraries were constructed using the Visium Spatial Gene Expression Slide & Reagent Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (10x Genomics). The final libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with paired-end 150 bp reads (performed by CapitalBio Technology, Beijing).

Spatial transcriptomics analysis

The spatial transcriptomics data and image information for each sample were imported using the Load10X_Spatial function from Seurat. Data normalization was performed using SCTransform, followed by PCA. Clustering analysis was then conducted using FindNeighbors and FindClusters, and the results were projected into UMAP. The SpatialFeaturePlot function was used to visualize specific gene expression.

Spatial transcriptomic deconvolution analysis

Spatial transcriptomic data were processed using Seurat. Cell-type deconvolution was performed using Robust Cell Type Decomposition (RCTD) implemented in the spacexr package (v2.2.1), with the annotated PMGC scRNA-seq dataset used as the reference. Deconvolution was performed in “full” mode to allow mixed cellular compositions within individual spots. The resulting normalized weights were mapped to spatial spot coordinates for downstream analyses.

Mouse model and treatment

To establish a humanized mouse model of PMGC, huHSC-NCG-hIL15 mice were intraperitoneally injected with 1 × 106 NUGC4-hCLDN18.2-Luc cells suspended in PBS. CLDN18.2 overexpression in NUGC4 cells was confirmed by flow cytometry (Supplementary Fig. 11), and the cells were mixed at a 1:1 ratio with wild-type NUGC4-Luc cells prior to inoculation. The day of tumor inoculation was designated as Day 0 (D0). Tumor burden was assessed on day 6 post-inoculation, after which the mice were randomly allocated into three groups of five animals each. The treatment groups included the vehicle control group, the anti-human TIGIT mAb (hTIGIT mAb, Biointron, B338101) group, and the combination group receiving hTIGIT mAb and anti-human CLDN18.2 mAb (hCLDN18.2 mAb, Biointron, B535601). The dosing regimens for anti-TIGIT61–64 and anti-CLDN18.228,65,66 monoclonal antibodies were selected based on prior preclinical studies. Immunohistochemical staining in human GC tissue sections was performed for the anti-TIGIT and anti-CLDN18.2 antibodies (Supplementary Fig. 12). Intraperitoneal injections were administered on D6, D9, D13, D17, and D20, with hTIGIT mAb dosed at 5 mg/kg and hCLDN18.2 mAb at 2 mg/kg. Bioluminescence imaging was conducted on D6, D9, D12, D16, and D21 to monitor tumor progression. On day 22, the mice were euthanized for ascites collection and organ imaging.

To establish a C57BL/6 PMGC model, mice were intraperitoneally injected with a 1:1 mixture of MC38 cells stably expressing human CLDN18.2 (MC38-hCLDN18.2-Luc) and wild-type MC38-Luc cells (total 2 × 106cells) suspended in PBS. Both male and female mice were used. The MC38-hCLDN18.2-Luc cells were generated by lentiviral transduction. The day of tumor inoculation was designated as Day 0 (D0). Tumor burden was assessed on day 10 post-inoculation, after which the mice were randomly allocated into four groups of five animals each. The treatment groups included the vehicle control group, the anti-hCLDN18.2 mAb (Biointron, B535601) group, the anti-mouse TIGIT mAb (mTIGIT mAb, MedChemExpress, HY-P990155) group, and the combination group receiving both hCLDN18.2 mAb and mTIGIT mAb. Intraperitoneal injections were administered on D10, D13, D16, D19, D22, and D25, with hCLDN18.2 mAb dosed at 1 mg/kg and mTIGIT mAb dosed at 5 mg/kg. Bioluminescence imaging was conducted on D10, D14, D18, D22, and D27 to monitor tumor progression. On day 27, the mice were euthanized.

Flow cytometry

Peripheral blood was collected from mice via the facial vein, and erythrocytes were lysed using ACK lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A single-cell suspension was then prepared according to standard protocols and stained with anti-human CD45 antibody (hCD45, BD Biosciences, Cat#555483). NUGC4-hCLDN18.2-Luc tumor cells were stained with an anti-CLDN18.2 antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat# MA5-43155). Data analysis was performed with FlowJo software (v 10.8).

Meta-programs

To identify the potential expression signatures of tumor cells in humanized mouse scRNA-seq data, we subsetted tumor epithelial cells from the single-cell datasets of nine mice and applied non-negative matrix factorization using the cNMF package (v 1.3). Raw count matrices for each sample were obtained from Seurat objects, and the top 2000 highly variable genes were selected for downstream analysis. The optimal k-value for each sample was determined to define the number of factors for decomposing each tumor dataset. We identified 56 transcriptional programs and subsequently grouped them into four meta-programs via hierarchical clustering based on Pearson correlation. For each meta-program, 350 feature genes were selected, and functional enrichment analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler package (v4.2.2).

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were conducted using R (v4.3.1), Python (v 3.9.12), and GraphPad Prism (v8.2.1). Group comparisons were performed using t-tests, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, or ANOVA with appropriate multiple-comparison procedures. Correlations were assessed using Pearson correlation coefficients. Survival analysis was conducted using the log-rank test. A p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant and denoted as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001, while p > 0.05 was indicated as ns (not significant).
