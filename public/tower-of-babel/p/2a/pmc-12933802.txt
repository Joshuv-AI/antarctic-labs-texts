Serotonin transporter SERT: a novel immune checkpoint for CD8+ T cell antitumor therapy
Authors: Qing Xiao, Xinchun Zhang, Xiaolong Liang, Gangfeng Yu, Yingrui Li, Ruoyang Du, Huiyu Xiang, Zailin Yang, Yao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis Highlight describes a key observation where serotonin transporter (SERT) was shown to suppress CD8+ T cell antitumor responses via depletion of intratumoral 5-HT. While selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs)—among the most widely used antidepressants—significantly inhibit tumor growth and enhance T cell-mediated antitumor immunity in both mouse models and human xenografts, showing remarkable synergy with anti-PD-1 therapy. These findings emphasize the importance of intratumoral 5-HT signaling, establish SERT as an immune checkpoint, and identify SSRIs as promising candidates for cancer immunotherapy.Keywords: Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor, Immunotherapy, T cell

In a recent study published in Cell, Bo Li and his colleagues identified the serotonin transporter (SERT) as a novel immune checkpoint that negatively regulates the antitumor effector functions of CD8+ T cells.1 They found that SERT is upregulated in tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells and suppresses T-cell effector activity by depleting intratumoral serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT). Using both genetic knockout models and pharmacological approaches, the authors demonstrated that blocking SERT with selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) markedly enhanced CD8+ T cell-mediated antitumor responses across multiple syngeneic mouse tumor models. Mechanistically, activated CD8+ T cells concomitantly upregulated SERT, tryptophan hydroxylase 1 (TPH1; a key enzyme in serotonin biosynthesis), and monoamine oxidase A (MAO-A; a serotonin catabolic enzyme), establishing an autocrine serotonin signaling circuit that is negatively regulated by SERT.

Moreover, single-cell RNA sequencing revealed that SSRI treatment expanded effector/proliferative T-cell populations and boosted mitochondrial functions and metabolic pathways through mechanisms distinct from those associated with anti-programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) therapy. These fundamental discoveries were then extended to validation in human immune cells. SSRI treatment significantly enhanced effector functions in human CD8+ T cells, closely mirroring observations in mouse models. Importantly, SSRIs showed strong therapeutic synergy with PD-1 blockade, with particularly pronounced efficacy in melanoma models resistant to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB). Clinically oriented analyses further indicated that high intratumoral SERT expression correlates with poorer patient survival across multiple cancer types (figure 1). Collectively, this work fundamentally reshapes our understanding of SERT—not merely as a mood regulator in the brain, but as a key immunoregulatory switch within the tumor microenvironment—and provides a solid rationale for repurposing SSRIs as sensitizers for cancer immunotherapy.

The serotonin signaling pathway in CD8+ T cells in the TME. The SERT suppresses CD8+ T cell-mediated antitumor immunity by inhibiting an autocrine 5-HT signaling axis within solid tumors. Following antigen recognition, tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells increase expression of TPH1, which produces 5-HT and secretes it into the TME to promote T cell activation via 5-HT receptor signaling. Concurrently, these T cells also upregulate SERT, which acts as a negative-feedback mechanism by importing extracellular 5-HT back into the cell, thereby attenuating T cell activation. Pharmacological blockade of SERT with SSRIs leads to accumulation of 5-HT in the TME, triggering the 5-HTR-MAPK-TCR signaling cascade and enhancing the antitumor reactivity of CD8+ T cells. 5-HT, serotonin; MAO-A, monoamine oxidase A; MHC, major histocompatibility complex; SERT, serotonin transporter; SSRIs, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors; TCR, T-cell receptor; TME, tumor microenvironment; TPH1, tryptophan hydroxylase 1.

While immune checkpoint inhibition remains a cornerstone strategy, its limited efficacy, achieving responses in only 15–25% of patients, highlights the urgent need to identify new immunoregulatory targets.2 3 As a systemic signaling molecule, 5-HT exerts pleiotropic functions in both central and peripheral immunity through a sophisticated regulatory network involving TPH1/2, MAO-A, various 5-HT receptors, and the transporter SERT—the primary target of SSRI antidepressants.4 The immunomodulatory effects of 5-HT are complex and context-dependent: while it can promote inflammation by enhancing dendritic cell and macrophage activation, antigen presentation, and T cell proliferation under inflammatory conditions,5 6 it also exhibits anti-inflammatory properties through specific receptors like 5-HTR4 and 5-HTR7, demonstrating its functional dependence on tissue microenvironment.7 8 Although 5-HT is generally considered to have proinflammatory or immune-activating effects in immune cells, its precise role in cancer immunity remains poorly characterized.

While ICB has transformed the treatment pattern for multiple cancers, its overall efficacy remains limited by bottlenecks such as “cold tumors”, T cell exhaustion, and metabolic suppression.9 Most prior work on immune checkpoints has centered on classical inhibitory receptors, such as PD-1 and CTLA-4. The study by Li et al expands the concept of “checkpoints” to a broader notion of microenvironmental resource availability. SERT shapes T cell effector function and exhaustion fate by altering local 5-HT availability through its transport mechanism. In other words, this represents a type of “metabolic/neurotransmitter checkpoint”—it does not directly “brake” via inhibitory receptor signaling but indirectly raises the threshold for immune responses by altering the spatial distribution and receptor accessibility of critical small-molecule ligands. This perspective offers a new angle for understanding the variability in ICB responses. Even if PD-1 is blocked, the antitumor immune response may still struggle to cross the “activation threshold” if T cells within the tumor lack the local neurotransmitter/metabolic signals necessary to sustain their effector functions and long-term persistence. Therefore, we speculate that the SERT–5-HT axis functions more like a “licensing” step upstream of ICB: when this checkpoint is released, more T cells may enter a state amenable to reinvigoration by ICB, thereby facilitating synergistic effects.

Can antidepressants enhance the efficacy of tumor immunotherapy? As early as 2021, two important studies provided affirmative clues, although from entirely different perspectives. Wang et al focused on T cells within the tumor, discovering that inhibiting MAO-A could unleash the antitumor capacity of T cells and synergize with PD-1 inhibitors.10 Meanwhile, Schneider et al turned their attention to the systemic level, demonstrating that reducing serotonin produced by platelets and circulating in peripheral blood could also inhibit tumor growth and improve immunotherapy outcomes.11 These two studies collectively highlighted the central role of serotonin signaling in regulating tumor immunity. Building on this foundation, Li et al found that the core issue may lie in the local “availability” of serotonin within the tumor in this study. Tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells highly express SERT, which functions like a “pump”, actively lowering the serotonin concentration around the T cells. This suppression does not occur through traditional “braking” signals but rather by creating a scarcity of serotonin, indirectly inhibiting T cell function and making them more prone to exhaustion. Consequently, SERT itself can be viewed as a “metabolic” type of immune checkpoint. This study suggests that the effect of serotonin is entirely context-dependent, hinging on its source and site of action. Systemic or tumor cell-derived serotonin may foster an immunosuppressive environment, whereas serotonin produced by T cells themselves or present locally acts as essential “nourishment” to sustain their combat effectiveness. By creating a local scarcity around T cells, SERT precisely deprives them of this critical support. This heterogeneity may explain the differential responses to SSRI treatment and underscores the need to develop biomarkers to identify patients most likely to benefit.

Compared with the concurrent HCC study, Li et al emphasize that SERT suppresses CD8+ T cell responses by reducing locally available serotonin, and thus, inhibition can be relieved by blocking SERT. In contrast, the HCC study suggests that 5-HT2A signaling itself acts as a negative regulator of the cytotoxic program in CD8+ T cells and is associated with downstream MAPK transcriptional regulation.12 Both studies point to a common conclusion: the immunomodulatory effects of serotonin exhibit clear cell type-dependence and tissue microenvironment-dependence. In different tumor microenvironments, the source of serotonin and the receptor repertoire may determine whether increasing serotonin availability ultimately favors immune activation or suppression. The future challenge may lie in stratifying patients based on the “serotonin signaling profile” of different tumors to precisely select interventions targeting either the “transporter” or the “receptor” node, ultimately enabling personalized immunotherapy based on neurotransmitter networks.

In summary, this study not only revealed SERT’s crucial role in tumor immune regulation but also proposed the innovative repositioning of SSRIs as immunotherapy adjuvants. Considering SSRIs’ established safety profiles as approved clinical medications, confirming their antitumor efficacy could dramatically accelerate translational timelines. Simultaneously, this study opens up a broader frontier for exploring how neurotransmitters regulate tumor immunity. Beyond serotonin, signaling pathways involving dopamine, norepinephrine, and acetylcholine are increasingly recognized for their immunomodulatory roles within tumors. The concept of “neuro-immune checkpoints” may expand to encompass multiple neurotransmitter systems that tumors exploit to evade immune surveillance.
