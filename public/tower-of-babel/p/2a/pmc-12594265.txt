The Association of Retinal Capillary Density and Retinal Thickness in Diabetic Retinopathy
Authors: Rose Tan, Anoush Shahidzadeh, Ana Collazo, Xuejuan Jiang, Andrew A. Moshfeghi, Jeremy Wolfe, Neal Palejwala, Viet-Hoan Le, Ruikang Wang, Amir H. Kashani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe purpose of this study was to evaluate the relationship between retinal vascular density and retinal thickness (RT) using optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) in subjects with diabetic retinopathy (DR).MethodsOCTA scans were obtained on non-diabetic (control) and subjects with DR. DR was classified as mild nonproliferative DR (NPDR) or referable DR (moderate NPDR and worse) based on color fundus photographs. Full, superficial, and deep RT were quantified. Corresponding layer-specific capillary densities were quantified as vessel skeleton density (VSD) with and without large vessels. Tissue perfusion density (TPD) was defined as the ratio of layer-specific VSD-to-RT. Generalized estimating equations were used to adjust for inter-eye correlation, age, sex, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia.ResultsIn total, 147 subjects were recruited including 44 control subjects (73 eyes, 34% male subjects) and 103 subjects with subjects (144 eyes, 56% male subjects). In the superficial retinal layer, we observed: (1) capillary VSD and TPD were significantly lower with increased DR severity (P < 0.001), (2) no significant difference in full RT among study groups, (3) a novel significant association between VSD and RT among controls (r = 0.439, P < 0.001) that was absent or attenuated among subjects with diabetes. Similar findings were replicated in analyses of the full and deep retinal layers.ConclusionsThere is a significant correlation between retinal capillary density and thickness in control subjects which is absent or attenuated in DR. This relationship is reflected in a lower TPD measure that is primarily driven by a decrease in retinal capillary density in our cohort.

Diabetic retinal disease (DRD) is a significant microvascular complication in patients with diabetes and is one of the leading causes of blindness in the world.1 DRD consists of multiple components. One component is the widely recognized diabetic retinopathy (DR) component that is essential in staging of clinical disease.2 The other significant components involve neurodegenerative changes that are not evident clinically (at least in the early stages of the disease) and diabetic macular edema.3 Histopathologic studies in diabetic animal models demonstrate pathologic changes in retinal capillaries, increased apoptosis of neurosensory cells, and gliosis, as among the earliest changes that are detectable in DRD.4 Similar histopathologic changes have been demonstrated in the human retina even before clinically evident DR.4,5 Therefore, understanding the relationship between vascular and neurodegenerative changes in DRD is an important area of study that will shed new light on the pathophysiology and clinical management of DRD.

Clinical correlations of subclinical histopathologic findings in DRD have been demonstrated using optical coherence tomography (OCT) or OCT angiography (OCTA). For example, retinal thinning or sublayer thinning has been shown in animal models6 and human studies7 even in patients with minimal or no clinically evident DR. Neuronal degeneration has been demonstrated by thinning of the retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL), ganglion cell layer (GCL), and inner plexiform layer (IPL).7–10 Similarly, studies using OCTA have demonstrated impaired capillary perfusion in subjects without any detectable DR11,12 as well as in early stages of DR.13–15 Because changes in the retinal microvasculature may affect the retinal thickness (RT), or vice versa, evaluating the relationship between these two parameters contemporaneously is an important goal.

In this study, we report concurrent RT and vascular density metrics on a cohort of diabetic and non-diabetic controls. Importantly, we performed these analysis using methods that allow (1) precise registration of vascular and RT measures and (2) separate analyses of capillaries from larger caliber arterioles and venules. We then examined the relationship between retinal vascular and thickness measures. We propose a novel tissue perfusion density (TPD) measure to help report the complex relationship between these variables in DR.

Methods

Study Population

This is a cross-sectional study approved by the respective institutional review boards (IRBs) of each participating site and Johns Hopkins University. Written informed consents were obtained from all subjects and adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Subjects were recruited from the USC Roski Eye Institute (Los Angeles, CA, USA), Retinal Consultants of Arizona (Phoenix, AZ, USA), Associated Retinal Consultants (Royal Oak, MI, USA), and Los Angeles County and University of Southern California (LAC + USC) Medical Center (Los Angeles, CA, USA).14 Subject’s eligibility criteria included age 40 years and older. Control subjects were defined as those with no self-reported history or clinical history of diabetes mellitus (DM) and confirmed with no signs of retinopathy during clinical ophthalmological examination. Controls were recruited from the following sources: cornea clinic, general ophthalmology clinic, optometry clinic, or non-diabetic volunteers from the hospital. Subjects with DR had a clinical diagnosis of DM. DR was graded into two categories including subjects with mild nonproliferative DR (NPDR) and subjects with moderate or worse DR (referable DR) based on clinical examination.

Exclusion criteria for all groups included the presence of glaucoma, significant media opacity, such as nuclear, cortical, or posterior subcapsular cataracts grade 3 or worse according to the Lens Opacity Classification System III,16 or other ophthalmic diagnosis except dry eye, vitreous syneresis, posterior vitreous detachment, asymptomatic epiretinal membrane without retinal distortion, peripheral retinal tear not requiring incisional surgery, and trauma without any notable sequelae. Diabetic macular edema (DME) was defined by the presence of any intraretinal cysts or subretinal fluid on review of individual B-scans in each OCT volume. Subjects with DME were excluded from this study to avoid variability in layer segmentation. Demographic and clinical history, including age, sex, and diagnoses of hyperlipidemia, hypertension, and diabetes, were obtained from the medical records.

OCTA and OCT Data Acquisition

All subjects had both 3 × 3 mm and 6 × 6 mm OCTA images taken with a CIRRUS AngioPlex 5000 (Carl Zeiss Meditec, Dublin, CA, USA), consisting of 245 horizontal B-scans and 245 individual A-scans per B-scan. Because 3 × 3 mm SD-OCTA scans are of significantly higher transverse resolution than 6 × 6 mm scans, for purposes of this study, only results from 3 × 3 mm OCTA scans are reported in the main text unless specifically stated otherwise. Corresponding results from 6 × 6 mm scans are provided in the Supplementary figures and tables and, unless otherwise indicated, demonstrate similar trends or findings. OCT data were acquired from the underlying OCT scans simultaneously taken during OCTA imaging. OCTA images were included in subsequent analysis if the following criteria were met: (1) signal strength was greater than 7, (2) the fovea was centered, (3) there were no significant hypo- or hyper-reflective artifacts on enface structural images, and (4) there were less than 10 motion artifacts displacing a major vessel by more than one vessel width. All OCT B-scan segmentation lines were checked for superficial retinal layer (SRL) segmentation errors, and no images required manual re-segmentation.

Retinal Thickness Analysis

The Iowa Reference Algorithm (Iowa OCT Explorer 3.8, Iowa City, IA, USA) was used to automatically segment OCT volumes into 10 retinal layers from the ILM to the outer boundary of retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) as shown in Figures 1A and 1B for 3 × 3 mm scans (and Supplementary Fig. S1 for 6 × 6 mm scans).17 All B-scans were exported as IMG files from the OCTA scan volume and resized into 200 voxels × 1024 voxels × 200 voxels using a custom MATLAB algorithm before being segmented and quantified using the Iowa software. RT was evaluated for the full RT (FRT) as well as SRL thickness, and deep retinal layer (DRL) thickness which correspond to the boundaries used for segmentation of retinal vascular measures. FRT is the sum of layer thickness measured from the internal limiting membrane (ILM) to the RPE. SRL RT is the sum of measurements from the ILM to the outer boundary of IPL. DRL RT is the sum of measurements from the outer boundary of IPL to the outer boundary of outer plexiform layer (OPL).

Vessel Skeleton Density Analysis

Figures 1C to 1J illustrate qualitative images of the VSD analyses and have been previously described.13,18,19 To calculate the vessel skeleton density (VSD) for each image, a previously validated, semi-automated, custom MATLAB (MathWorks Inc., Natlick, MA, USA) algorithm was applied to a grayscale bitmap image of the OCTA slabs exported from the device (see Figs. 1C–E).20 Briefly, VSD is a vascular length measurement that is calculated by binarizing and skeletonizing all capillary segments to a width of one pixel and dividing that number by the total number of pixels in the image,21 including the foveal avascular zone (FAZ).

Layer-specific capillary VSD was calculated in layers corresponding to the same retinal layers as described above for RT (FRT, superficial, and DRLs). For this study, we quantified capillary VSD measures after separating large vessels (arterioles and venules) from the image as previously described.18,22 Large vessels were defined by an empirically defined threshold width that most consistently masked the end-arteriole or end-venule segment with minimal or no overlap onto contiguous capillary beds in non-diabetic subjects. Figure 1F illustrates an example of the vessel mask used to define large vessels on a raw 3 × 3 mm OCTA scan. Figure 1G illustrates binarized and skeletonized OCTA images including both large vessels and capillaries on the FRT. Figure 1H shows the large VSD map. Figures 1I and 1J show the capillary VSD with the large vessels removed in the SRL and DRL, respectively. Supplementary Figure S1 illustrates similar panels for scans. Projection artifacts were removed from all DRL slabs using the commercially available algorithm available on the device.

Tissue Perfusion Density Assessment

To facilitate a relatively simple way to report the relationship between retinal VSD and RT, we calculated the TPD, as follows: TissueperfusiondensityAU/m=VesseldensityAURetinalthicknessm×10,000

As shown, TPD values were multiplied by 10,000 to make the interpretation easier. TPD is used to evaluate the relationship between retinal blood supply and tissue thickness. It is important to note that one unique advantage of this study is that RT and VSD were quantified in exactly the same 3 × 3 mm or 6 × 6 mm region because the measures were derived from the same scan. It is also important to note that because TPD is a ratio, it is agnostic to minor variations in image scaling from interindividual differences in axial length or refractive error.

Statistical Analysis

We analyzed our data from three study groups: control, mild NPDR, and referable DR. The clinical characteristics of subjects were analyzed at the eye level and compared using generalized estimating equations (GEEs) corrected for inter-eye correlation, followed by a post hoc analysis with Bonferroni correction. VSD, RT, and TPD in each retina slab (SRL, DRL, and FRT) were analyzed and compared among the study groups using GEE that controlled for inter-eye correlation, age, sex, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia. To minimize type 1 errors, a significant P level was adjusted using Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. We evaluated the relationship between RT and the VSD using Pearson correlation. The primary study outcome was to determine the correlation between VSD and RT in the SRL and DRL. Secondary outcomes were the association between FRT large vessel VSD and FRT, and between FRT total VSD and FRT. All statistical analysis were performed using SPSS software version 29.0 (IBM/SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Table 1 summarizes the clinical characteristics of the study subjects. Two hundred seventeen eyes of 147 subjects who had eligible 3 × 3 mm scans were included in this study. Seventy-three eyes of 44 subjects were in the control group, 86 eyes of 58 patients were in the mild NPDR group, and 58 eyes of 45 subjects were in the referable DR group. Of 58 eyes in the referable DR group, 27 (47%) were moderate NPDR, 16 (28%) were severe NPDR, and 15 (25%) were proliferative DR (PDR). Age and spherical equivalent (SE) were not significantly different among the study groups. Cataract was more prevalent in the DR groups than in the control group. As Table 1 shows, there were differences in the proportion of male subjects, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia among the three study groups which were adjusted for in subsequent analyses. The results of a separate analysis of participants with eligible 6 × 6 mm scans are compiled in Supplementary Table S1.

Capillary Vessel Skeleton Density

To accurately capture the capillary level changes, large caliber vessels (arterioles and venules) were masked from the original OCTA images, as described in the Methods section, and reported in a separate analysis below. Table 2 shows that the SRL capillary VSD was significantly different among all study groups (P < 0.001). As expected, control subjects had the highest VSD, and subjects with diabetes had progressively lower VSD with worsening DR status. Similarly, the DRL capillary VSD was also significantly lower with increasing DR severity (P < 0.001). In several secondary analyses, these findings were confirmed. For example, when assessing VSD in the non-segmented (full) thickness retina that included large vessels, VSD was still significantly lower in patients with diabetes compared with healthy controls and with increasing DR severity (P < 0.001).

We also examined VSD in the 6 × 6 mm scans. In general, all the findings we reported in the 3 × 3 mm VSD measures were present in similar data from 6 × 6 mm scans although with decreased magnitude and significance in some parameters (Supplementary Table S2). This is likely due to the decreased resolution of 6 × 6 mm scans patterns compared with 3 × 3 mm scans. Therefore, there is a robust and highly significant decline in VSD with increasing DR severity regardless of how VSD was measured on both the 3 × 3 mm and 6 × 6 mm scans.

Large Vessel Skeleton Density

In a separate analysis of only large vessels (as shown in Fig. 1H), we observed that there is a significant increase in the density of large vessel segments among patients with diabetes compared with the healthy controls (P < 0.001; see Table 2). This is consistent with the general observation of vascular dilatation with progressive DR.

Retinal Thickness

There was no significant difference in the RT for the full, SRL, or DRL among the study groups as summarized in Table 2. Some studies have reported thinner retinal sublayers in patients with diabetes,3,23,24 so we performed an exploratory analysis of all 10 sublayers that are summarized in Supplementary Table S3. In this analysis, the IPL was significantly thinner in the referable DR group compared with the mild NPDR and control groups. The RPE was also significantly thinner in the referable DR group than the control group. These findings were present in both 3 × 3 mm and 6 × 6 mm scan patterns (see Supplementary Table S3) suggesting this was not an artifact of the segmentation error.

Capillary Tissue Perfusion Density

As illustrated in Table 2, SRL capillary TPD was significantly lower with increasing severity of DR (P < 0.001). Similarly, when calculated for the DRL capillary TPD or FRT (including large vessels), the TPD was significantly lower with increasing DR severity (P < 0.001). Therefore, at least in this cohort, subjects with progressively more severe DR seem to have greater attenuation in capillary perfusion than RT. Correlation Between VSD and RT

Figure 3 shows the correlation between VSD and RT for all eyes. Specifically, there was a significant correlation between capillary VSD and RT for the SRL in the controls (r = 0.439, P < 0.001) but this relationship was not present in either the mild NPDR group (r = 0.098, P = 0.37) or the referable DR group (r = 0.133, P = 0.32). There was also a similar significant correlation in RT and capillary VSD for the DRL in the controls (r = 0.309, P = 0.008) that was attenuated or absent in the mild NPDR and referable DR groups. In the secondary analyses, this same pattern was confirmed when analyzing the capillary VSD and RT in the full thickness retina and in images where large vessels were not masked, as shown in Figure 3.

Receiver Operating Characteristic of OCTA parameters

To determine the diagnostic value of the OCTA measures we have described, we performed an area under the curve (AUC) analysis, as shown in Table 3. Among all OCTA parameters, SRL capillary VSD consistently showed the highest AUC for classifying control versus different DR severity groups among all OCTA metrics. Notably, corresponding TPD measures performed very similarly to VSD measures for both the 3 × 3 mm and 6 × 6 mm images (Supplementary Table S4).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study demonstrates novel and significant findings between contemporaneous retinal neurosensory thickness and corresponding retinal capillary perfusion measures. Importantly, we implement methodological improvements over previous studies including: (1) an improved method for comparison of co-registered retinal regions and layers that are derived from the exact same scan pattern, and (2) separate analyses of larger vessels (arterioles and venules) and capillaries. Our results demonstrate robust correlations between layer-specific RT and corresponding layer-specific capillary density in contemporaneous OCT and OCTA scans of non-diabetic subjects. These correlations were absent or severely attenuated in subjects with diabetes, even with mild NPDR. These novel findings were persistent regardless of how we evaluated capillary density (e.g. capillary only VSD, large vessel VSD, or total VSD) or retinal thickness (3 × 3 mm and 6 × 6 mm scans). Therefore, neurodegeneration and impairment in retinal perfusion in diabetes may not necessarily occur in parallel, at least as measured by OCT and OCTA. We also propose TPD, the ratio of layer-specific VSD-to-RT, as a simple and novel measure to capture the important relationship between RT and vascularity for the exact same retinal region.

Our study significantly expands previous findings and overcomes major limitations of previous reports on comparing measures of RT and OCTA-derived capillary density in subjects with diabetes.23,25,26 One major limitation of nearly all previous studies is comparison of dissimilar or unregistered RT and vessel density measures. This is one likely cause of inconsistent results among studies. For example, Sung et al. derived the vessel density in the entire superficial retinal layer (ILM to IPL) of ETDRS sectors from 3 × 3 mm foveal centered OCTA scans and compared that to GC-IPL thickness from 6 × 6 mm OCT scans without accounting for the differences in scan size or sectoral quantification.25 They did not report on deep retinal layer measures. Similarly, Marques et al. reported on vessel density measures from 3 × 3 mm scans and retinal thickness measures from 6 × 6 mm cube scans.27 They also compared GC-IPL thickness with capillary density from the more posteriorly located DRLs which likely accounts for the variable correlation they observe over time.28 Lavia et al. also did not compare RT and vessel density in the same segmented retinal layers which may account for the lack of correlation between these measures in the DRLs in their study.26 Aschauer et al. reported longitudinal RT measures from Heidelberg Spectralis device and vessel density measures from OptoVue but did not report on any correlation between these measures.29

RT changes are thought of as a proxy of underlying neural degeneration which is recognized as an important component of DRD. We did not find such a difference in our study (despite careful analyses of RT and manual correction of segmentation), except for the IPL (differences up to 3.4 um) and RPE layers (0.3 um difference). Although similar findings have been reported by others24 there is widespread inconsistency in reports of RT measures in early DR. Several explanations for this discrepancy, which are not mutually exclusive, can be suggested by the data and are worth considering. First, it may be possible that (at least in some patients with diabetes), capillary degeneration is the primary driver of retinopathy and therefore shows more profound differences than RT. This possibility is consistent with reports of different DR “phenotypes.”30 Second, it is possible that RT changes are too small in magnitude to be detected by spectral-domain OCT (SD-OCT). This possibility is supported by the relatively small differences in RT compared to capillary density in studies conducted by Marques et al. and Aschauer et al.29,31 Third, it is possible that any RT changes from neural degeneration are offset by diffuse (non-cystoid) and subclinical retinal edema (at least in some patients with diabetes). This possibility has been hypothesized in diabetic animal models where paradoxical retinal thickening has been observed.6

Our study demonstrates a significant novel association between layer-specific RT and vessel density among controls which are absent or attenuated in patients with diabetes. Specifically, we found a significant correlation between capillary VSD and RT among controls that was present regardless of how we performed the analysis in the 3 × 3 mm images. This relationship disappeared in DR or was severely attenuated. Similar findings were present in the 6 × 6 mm images although they were driven primarily by the larger vessels and DRL VSD. This difference may explain why there are conflicting results between studies that do not distinguish between large vessels and capillaries. This is especially true in studies using different image sizes (e.g. 3 × 3 mm versus 6 × 6 mm) which differ not only in the prevalence of larger caliber arterioles and venules but also in the transverse resolution that is necessary to reliably detect capillaries. In our study, capillary level findings were attenuated or absent, but large vessel findings were evident in 6 × 6 mm images. Last, conflicting results among studies can also be attributed to the use of incongruous circular Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) grids for assessment of RT and the full square OCTA scan patterns for assessment of VSD. Our study is the first study that we are aware of using the same image size (3 × 3 mm or 6 × 6 mm) for quantification of both VSD and RT and therefore demonstrate robust correlations.

To better illustrate the relationship between retinal capillary density (VSD) and RT, we developed a measure of TPD which is the ratio of VSD-to-RT. This represents a more direct measure of the relationship between vessel perfusion and neural degeneration. Our findings showed that the TPD measures accurately capture the relative difference in capillary and RT attenuation in both 3 × 3 mm and 6 × 6 mm scan patterns. Such a TPD measure can provide a useful tool to concisely represent the dynamic and complex relationship between neurodegeneration and capillary density in DRD. This TPD measure suggests that there is dysregulation between blood supply and neurosensory metabolic demand and is also supported by studies of abnormal retinal blood flow32–35 and function36–38 even in early diabetes without retinopathy.

Our study has several limitations. As mentioned above, because the study was cross-sectional, we cannot infer that changes in retinal capillary density cause or always precede changes in the neurosensory retinal tissue. Given the complexity of DM management, it is likely that the relationship between capillary density and RT may also be more dynamic than is captured with a single OCT scan. As with any OCT based study, minor segmentation differences of retinal layers may contribute noise that can wash out small signals. In our study, we minimized the impact of segmentation errors by excluding subjects with any macular edema on any individual B-scan and we manually reviewed all scans to ensure accurate segmentation.39,40 Differences between commercial devices may also make it difficult to compare between different studies. In our study, all scans were derived from the same manufacturer to ensure consistency in scan patterns and parameters.

There were a few parameters, such as axial length and A1c, that were not available for all subjects. Approximately one third of our sample size did not have spherical equivalent data, but our sensitivity analysis (see Supplemental Table 5) showed no differences in the trend of VSD and RT findings between groups with and without SE values. In addition, we highlight that using TPD (the ratio of VSD-to-RT) that is quantified on the exact same scan size is agnostic to interindividual and lateral magnification differences in image scale and quality.41–45 Supplementary Table S9 shows the results of a sensitivity analysis on a subset of our cohort with a complete SE value (see Supplementary Table S9). We performed two models, without SE correction (model 1) or with SE values (model 2). Both models revealed the same results for all the TPD parameters.

Our moderate sample size was likely not large enough to detect changes in retinal thickness of <5 microns which are usually cited in studies of retinal neurodegeneration.7 However, this problem is unavoidable when evaluating individual patients and suggests that detection of real RT changes on an individual level in DRD will be very challenging unless new methods are developed.46 A change of a few microns may be statistically significant when evaluating hundreds or thousands of patients but is not measured reliably on an individual basis using current OCT devices.

In summary, our results show that capillary and large vessel changes are readily detected across a population of patients with diabetes with increasing severity of clinically assessed DR whereas neurodegenerative changes (as assessed by RT measures) are not detectable or modest at best. We believe that the clinical correlation between retinal capillary level pathology and neurodegeneration is complex and should be investigated further in a longitudinal study.
