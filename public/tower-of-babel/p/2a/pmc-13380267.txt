Prognostic and Clinical Significance of Peripheral Blood IL-2, IL-8, and TNF-α in Children with Severe Pneumonia
Authors: Jiachen Xu, Huihui Liu, Shuyang Zheng, Yadan Li, Shuyue Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveSevere pediatric pneumonia remains a major clinical burden and early prediction of deterioration is essential for timely risk stratification. This retrospective study evaluated baseline peripheral blood interleukin-2 (IL-2), interleukin-8 (IL-8), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and the modified PIRO (mPIRO) score for predicting progression to severe pneumonia in hospitalized children.MethodsWe analyzed 143 children with initially non-severe community-acquired pneumonia admitted between October 2020 and February 2022, together with 60 age-matched healthy controls. During hospitalization, 121 children maintained an uncomplicated course and 22 progressed to severe pneumonia. Serum IL-2, IL-8, and TNF-α levels were measured within 24 h of admission using residual serum from routine clinical testing. Logistic regression, ROC analysis, and a nomogram were used to assess predictors and internal cohort discriminative efficacy.ResultsChildren with pneumonia had significantly higher IL-2, IL-8, and TNF-α levels than healthy controls (all P < 0.001). Among children with pneumonia, those who progressed to severe disease had higher baseline IL-2 (85.7 ± 14.9 vs 63.0 ± 26.2 pg/mL), TNF-α (91.9 ± 12.5 vs 76.6 ± 9.88 pg/mL), and mPIRO scores (6.0 [5.0–6.0] vs 5.0 [4.0–5.0]; all P < 0.001), whereas IL-8 did not differ significantly (P = 0.622). IL-2, TNF-α, and mPIRO remained independent predictors in multivariable analysis. The combined model showed the highest apparent AUC (0.935, 95% CI 0.877–0.993), compared with mPIRO alone (0.858), TNF-α (0.826), and IL-2 (0.778).ConclusionBaseline IL-2, TNF-α, and mPIRO were associated with subsequent progression to severe pneumonia in children. The combined cytokine-mPIRO model may provide preliminary incremental prognostic information, but prospective multicenter validation and comparison with established biomarkers are required before routine clinical implementation.Keywords: IL-2, IL-8, TNF-α, children with severe pneumonia, risk stratification, prognostic assessment

Introduction

Pneumonia remains a leading infectious cause of childhood morbidity and mortality. The World Health Organization reported that pneumonia accounted for 14% of all deaths in children under 5 years of age and caused 740,180 deaths in 2019.1 Among hospitalized children, a clinically important proportion progress to severe pneumonia, and severe cases may develop hypoxaemia, respiratory failure, sepsis, myocardial injury, or require intensive monitoring and advanced respiratory support.2–5 Although vaccination, antimicrobial treatment, and supportive care have improved overall outcomes, early recognition of children at risk of deterioration remains challenging. There is still a need for pragmatic tools that can support early risk stratification, referral or discharge decisions, and individualized monitoring in pediatric pneumonia.

Previous studies suggest that progression to severe pneumonia is closely related to excessive activation of host inflammatory responses, with multiple inflammatory mediators participating in tissue injury and systemic complications.6 CRP and procalcitonin (PCT) are widely used in clinical practice, but their ability to discriminate non-severe from severe pediatric community-acquired pneumonia is variable, and they may not fully reflect adaptive immune activation or cytokine-mediated inflammatory burden.7–9 IL-2, IL-8, and TNF-α were selected because they represent complementary inflammatory pathways: T-cell activation, neutrophil recruitment, and systemic inflammatory injury. Nevertheless, compared with CRP or PCT, cytokine assays have potential limitations in assay standardization, reproducibility, cost, turnaround time, and routine clinical availability; therefore, their value should be interpreted as incremental and exploratory rather than as a replacement for established markers.

This study aimed to investigate whether baseline peripheral blood levels of IL-2, IL-8 and TNF-α, together with the mPIRO score, can predict progression from non-severe to severe pneumonia in hospitalized children. We further explored whether a combined “cytokines + mPIRO” model could provide incremental prognostic information over mPIRO alone, thereby addressing the specific knowledge gap of early deterioration prediction rather than severity classification at presentation.

Methods

Ethics Statement

This retrospective observational study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Qinhuangdao First Hospital and performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The ethical approval scope of this study included both hospitalized children with pneumonia and healthy children undergoing routine physical examination. Written informed consent was obtained from all legal guardians prior to enrollment, after detailed explanation of the research purpose, specimen usage, and research safety.

All serum samples in this study were residual specimens obtained from routine blood tests or physical examinations. No additional venipuncture or extra blood sampling was performed for research purposes. All detections of inflammatory cytokines in this study were performed merely for academic research, and the results did not interfere with clinical diagnosis, treatment strategies and follow-up management of all enrolled children. All clinical data and biological samples were completely anonymized to protect patient privacy.

Study Design and Participants

This was a retrospective study conducted at Qinhuangdao First Hospital (Qinhuangdao, China) from October 2020 to February 2022. We included 143 children with community-acquired pneumonia, of which 121 had an uncomplicated course and 22 progressed to severe pneumonia.

Inclusion criteria: (1) hospitalisation for community-acquired pneumonia; (2) availability of baseline clinical data and serum samples; (3) non-severe pneumonia at admission. Exclusion criteria: (1) hospital-acquired pneumonia; (2) repeated admissions; (3) incomplete data; (4) children who had received glucocorticoids or immune regulating drugs within two weeks before admission; (5) children combined with congenital immune deficiency diseases; (6) children with severe systemic infectious diseases recently.

Detection of Peripheral Blood Inflammatory Cytokines

Approximately 5.0 mL of venous blood collected for routine clinical purposes was centrifuged, and residual serum was stored at −80 °C until analysis. Serum IL-2, IL-8 and TNF-α levels were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Beijing Wallace Biological Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Modified PIRO Score (mPIRO)

The modified PIRO (mPIRO) score was used to assess disease severity within 24 hours of admission, following the definition proposed by Valentania et al10 The score comprises 10 dichotomous variables (1 point if present, 0 otherwise): (1) age < 6 months; (2) presence of relevant comorbidities (eg, chronic lung or heart disease, immunodeficiency); (3) hypoxia (SpO2 < 90% on room air); (4) age-specific hypotension; (5) clinical sepsis; (6) multilobar radiographic involvement; (7) complicated pneumonia (eg, pleural effusion, empyema); (8) hepatic insufficiency; (9) renal insufficiency; and (10) acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). The total score ranges from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating more severe illness. Although mPIRO has been proposed for pediatric pneumonia severity assessment, evidence in Chinese pediatric populations and in prediction of in-hospital progression remains limited.

Severity Definition and Clinical Data Collection

All enrolled patients were clearly diagnosed with non-severe community-acquired pneumonia at the time of hospital admission, and the final severe pneumonia classification was determined retrospectively according to the worst clinical condition during the whole hospitalization period. For all pneumonia patients, blood sampling for IL-2, IL-8 and TNF-α and calculation of the mPIRO score were performed within 24 hours of admission and were considered baseline measurements. Severity classification (severe vs uncomplicated pneumonia) was determined retrospectively based on the worst clinical status during the entire hospitalization, according to the guideline-based criteria described above.

Severe pneumonia was defined according to the 2019 national guidelines for community-acquired pneumonia in children issued by the National Health Commission of China and the World Health Organization (WHO) Pocket Book of Hospital Care for Children.11,12 In brief, children were classified as having severe pneumonia if they presented with cough or difficulty breathing plus at least one of the following: (1) hypoxaemia, defined as pulse oxygen saturation (SpO2) < 90% on room air; (2) severe respiratory distress (eg, grunting, nasal flaring, severe chest indrawing, apnea); (3) clinical signs of hemodynamic instability (such as prolonged capillary refill time, weak peripheral pulses, or hypotension) that required fluid resuscitation and/or vasoactive support; or (4) radiographic evidence of multilobar involvement and/or complicated pneumonia (eg, pleural effusion, empyema). These criteria are consistent with current international and national recommendations and have been widely used in previous pediatric pneumonia studies, in which hypoxaemia and severe respiratory distress were strongly associated with in-hospital mortality and other adverse outcomes.5,13 Uncomplicated pneumonia was defined as community-acquired pneumonia not meeting any of the above criteria.

Observation Indicators

The observation indicators in this study included: (1) baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of controls, uncomplicated pneumonia, and severe pneumonia; (2) peripheral blood IL-2, IL-8 and TNF-α levels and mPIRO scores in different subgroups; and (3) independent factors associated with severe pneumonia identified by logistic regression.

Statistical Methods

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 23.0. Normality was assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test; normally distributed measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation () and compared using one-way ANOVA, whereas non-normally distributed data were expressed as median [IQR] and analyzed using non-parametric tests. Categorical data were expressed as number (%) and compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Logistic regression used progression to severe pneumonia as the dependent variable. Univariate statistically significant variables and clinically meaningful indicators were included in the multivariate logistic regression model. Variables with obvious multicollinearity were excluded to ensure the stability of the final regression model. Given the limited number of severe cases, the final model was kept parsimonious to reduce overfitting risk; multicollinearity was checked using variance inflation factors (VIF <5), and model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. ROC curves were used to calculate apparent AUCs and 95% CIs, and the combined model was plotted based on predicted probabilities from regression. Because this was an exploratory single-center study with a small number of outcome events, ROC comparisons were interpreted descriptively and require validation in independent cohorts. Optimal cut-off values were determined using the Youden index. Sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative likelihood ratios (LR+ and LR-), positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated at these thresholds. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Comparison of Baseline Characteristics Between Children with Uncomplicated and Severe Pneumonia

A total of 143 children with pneumonia were included, of whom 121 (84.6%) had an uncomplicated course and 22 (15.4%) progressed to severe pneumonia during hospitalization. As shown in Table 1, baseline demographic characteristics and chronic comorbidities were generally comparable between the two groups. The mean age was 3.54 ± 0.87 years in the uncomplicated group and 3.39 ± 0.68 years in the severe group (P = 0.343). The proportion of boys was 51.2% and 36.4%, respectively (P = 0.293), and mean body weight was 14.0 ± 3.62 vs 13.7 ± 2.84 kg (P = 0.670). The prevalences of preterm birth (11.6% vs 18.2%), asthma (7.44% vs 4.55%), congenital heart disease (3.31% vs 4.55%), and anemia (9.09% vs 4.55%) did not differ significantly between the two groups (all P > 0.05). Admission white blood cell count and CRP levels were also similar (P = 0.189 and P = 0.319, respectively). The conventional routine inflammatory markers such as WBC and CRP showed no significant difference at admission, indicating that traditional inflammatory indicators were difficult to early predict subsequent disease deterioration. Notably, children who later developed severe pneumonia already showed slightly more pronounced respiratory compromise at admission, with a lower median SpO2 on room air (95.8% [94.7–96.6] vs 97.0% [95.8–98.4], P = 0.002) and a higher mean respiratory rate (35.4 ± 4.83 vs 31.3 ± 4.62 breaths/min, P = 0.001) compared with those who remained uncomplicated.

Comparison of Peripheral Blood IL-2, IL-8, TNF-α Levels and mPIRO Scores Between Uncomplicated and Severe Pneumonia

Within the pneumonia cohort (N = 143), children who progressed to severe pneumonia had markedly higher baseline serum IL-2 and TNF-α levels than those who remained uncomplicated (Table 2). The mean IL-2 concentration was 85.7 ± 14.9 pg/mL in the severe group versus 63.0 ± 26.2 pg/mL in the uncomplicated group (P < 0.001), and the mean TNF-α concentration was 91.9 ± 12.5 versus 76.6 ± 9.88 pg/mL, respectively (P < 0.001). In contrast, baseline IL-8 levels were similar between the two groups (161 ± 32.3 vs 165 ± 29.4 pg/mL, P = 0.622). No significant difference in baseline IL-8 level was observed between the two groups, suggesting that single peripheral blood IL-8 detection has limited early predictive value for the deterioration of pediatric pneumonia in this cohort. The median mPIRO score at admission was also higher among children who later developed severe pneumonia compared with those who maintained an uncomplicated course (6.00 [5.00–6.00] vs 5.00 [4.00–5.00], P < 0.001), indicating greater early physiological derangement in the severe group.

Multivariable Analysis of Baseline Predictors of Progression to Severe Pneumonia

Variables that were significantly associated with progression to severe pneumonia in the univariable analyses (P < 0.05) - namely baseline IL-2, TNF-α, mPIRO score, admission SpO2 on room air and admission respiratory rate - were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model (Table 3). To minimize overfitting given the limited number of outcome events, only variables with strong clinical relevance and minimal collinearity were retained in the final model. After mutual adjustment, higher IL-2, TNF-α and mPIRO scores remained independent predictors of severe pneumonia. Each 1 pg/mL increase in IL-2 was associated with a 6.9% increase in the odds of developing severe pneumonia (OR = 1.069, 95% CI 1.012–1.148, P = 0.030), and each 1 pg/mL increase in TNF-α was associated with a 19.2% increase in the odds (OR = 1.192, 95% CI 1.073–1.388, P = 0.005). The mPIRO score showed the strongest association: each 1-point increase was associated with an approximately eight-fold higher odds of progression to severe pneumonia (OR = 8.057, 95% CI 2.445–47.15, P = 0.005). In contrast, admission SpO2 and respiratory rate did not remain statistically significant in the multivariable model (SpO2: OR = 0.515, 95% CI 0.220–1.000, P = 0.075; respiratory rate: OR = 1.154, 95% CI 0.943–1.430, P = 0.164).

Development of a Nomogram for Individualized Prediction of Severe Pneumonia

Based on the multivariable logistic regression model, a nomogram incorporating IL-2, TNF-α and the mPIRO score was constructed to visualize the estimated risk of progression to severe pneumonia (Figure 1). In the nomogram, each predictor is mapped to a point scale; the sum of points corresponds to the predicted probability of severe pneumonia. Higher baseline IL-2 and TNF-α concentrations and higher mPIRO scores contribute more points and translate into a higher predicted risk. Because the model was derived from a single-center retrospective cohort, the nomogram should be regarded as an exploratory visualization tool pending external validation rather than a ready-to-use clinical decision instrument.

Discriminative Performance of IL-2, TNF-α, mPIRO and the Combined Model

In the cohort of 143 children with pneumonia, ROC analysis showed that baseline biomarkers and the mPIRO score had moderate to good apparent ability to discriminate children who would progress to severe pneumonia from those who remained uncomplicated (Figure 2). Among single predictors, the mPIRO score achieved the highest AUC (0.858, 95% CI 0.793–0.924), followed by TNF-α (AUC 0.826, 95% CI 0.716–0.937) and IL-2 (AUC 0.778, 95% CI 0.687–0.869). The combined model integrating IL-2, TNF-α and mPIRO yielded the highest apparent discrimination, with an AUC of 0.935 (95% CI 0.877–0.993). These AUC comparisons should be interpreted descriptively because formal internal and external validation were not available in this retrospective cohort. All predictive efficacy results in this study belong to single-center apparent efficacy, which cannot be directly applied to wide clinical practice before external cohort verification. Clinically informative cut-off values were IL-2 ≥80.73 pg/mL, TNF-α ≥91.04 pg/mL, and mPIRO score ≥5.5; corresponding sensitivities, specificities, and likelihood ratios are provided in Table S1.

Discussion

Severe pneumonia remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in children worldwide. The immaturity of the paediatric immune system, together with children’s limited ability to self-report symptoms and cooperate with examinations, means that clinical deterioration can occur rapidly and may be accompanied by serious complications such as respiratory failure, sepsis and myocardial injury. These features substantially increase the uncertainty of early assessment and treatment decisions. Although advances in antimicrobial therapy, respiratory support and intensive care have improved overall outcomes, early identification of children at high risk of deterioration remains difficult. This gap hampers timely triage, escalation of monitoring and rational allocation of intensive care resources. Consequently, there is a need for pragmatic prognostic indicators that can complement, rather than replace, routine clinical assessment in paediatric respiratory medicine.

From an immunopathological perspective, the occurrence and progression of severe pneumonia are closely linked to dysregulated host inflammatory responses. Pathogens and their products activate innate and adaptive immune cells, leading to cytokine release, leukocyte recruitment, and subsequent tissue injury.6,14 Among the numerous inflammatory mediators, IL-2, IL-8 and TNF-α were selected because they represent complementary biological pathways. IL-2, mainly secreted by activated T cells and natural killer cells, promotes lymphocyte proliferation and differentiation and is regarded as a central marker of T-cell activation.15 IL-8, produced by monocytes/macrophages, endothelial cells and airway epithelial cells, acts as a neutrophil chemoattractant and amplifies local inflammatory reactions.6 TNF-α is a pivotal mediator of systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction and multi-organ injury.6 However, cytokine assays are less widely standardized than CRP or PCT testing. Therefore, circulating IL-2, IL-8 and TNF-α should be viewed as potential complementary markers of inflammatory burden rather than as substitutes for established clinical and laboratory indicators.

In parallel, the PIRO (Predisposition, Insult, Response, Organ dysfunction) framework has been increasingly adopted as a structured method for staging infectious diseases and sepsis.10 The modified PIRO (mPIRO) score, adapted to paediatric characteristics, incorporates age, comorbidities, physiological derangements, radiographic findings and organ dysfunction, and has been proposed as a promising tool for the severity assessment of community-acquired pneumonia in children.10 However, the mPIRO focuses primarily on clinical and organ-based variables and does not explicitly capture the magnitude of the underlying inflammatory response. Combining cytokines with the mPIRO score may therefore provide a more comprehensive representation of the host-pathogen interaction along the “inflammatory burden-organ dysfunction” axis, but its clinical applicability depends on validation, assay availability and reproducibility.

In the present study, we restricted our analysis to children who were non-severe at admission and then retrospectively classified them as uncomplicated or severe pneumonia based on the worst clinical status during hospitalisation. This design reflects the clinical question of whether baseline information can identify children at risk of subsequent deterioration, rather than simply differentiating obviously severe cases at presentation from mild cases. At baseline, demographic characteristics and chronic comorbidities were broadly comparable between groups. Traditional inflammatory markers such as white blood cell count and CRP were also similar, whereas children who later developed severe pneumonia already showed slightly lower SpO2 and higher respiratory rates at admission. These findings are consistent with guideline-based concepts that early hypoxaemia and increased work of breathing are key clinical harbingers of severe disease.5,13

Our main findings can be summarised as follows. First, compared with healthy controls, children with pneumonia had significantly elevated IL-2, IL-8 and TNF-α levels, confirming that these cytokines participate in the inflammatory response to lower respiratory tract infection. Within the pneumonia cohort, however, only IL-2, TNF-α and the mPIRO score differed significantly at baseline between children who remained uncomplicated and those who progressed to severe pneumonia, while IL-8 levels were similar between groups. The non-significant IL-8 result deserves attention. Although IL-8 has a plausible biological role in neutrophil recruitment, its circulating level may be influenced by pathogen type, timing of blood sampling, prior treatment, local pulmonary production, and inter-individual variability. Thus, the negative IL-8 finding does not exclude a role in pneumonia pathophysiology, but suggests that a single baseline serum IL-8 measurement may have limited value for predicting progression in this cohort. Second, in multivariable logistic regression, baseline IL-2, TNF-α and the mPIRO score remained independent predictors after adjustment for candidate variables. Together, these results support a complementary role of “cytokines + mPIRO”: cytokines reflect immune activation and inflammatory injury, while the mPIRO integrates multidomain clinical information on physiological derangement and organ dysfunction.

Regarding discriminative performance, ROC analysis showed that each single predictor had moderate to good apparent ability to distinguish children who would progress to severe pneumonia from those who would not. The AUCs for IL-2, TNF-α and the mPIRO score were 0.778, 0.826 and 0.858, respectively. The combined model integrating IL-2, TNF-α and the mPIRO score achieved an apparent AUC of 0.935 (95% CI 0.877–0.993), higher than any single marker in this dataset. However, this improvement should be interpreted cautiously because the number of severe cases was small and no independent validation cohort was available. The high AUC may therefore partly reflect optimism from model derivation in the same dataset. From a pragmatic perspective, TNF-α and the mPIRO score alone already showed useful discrimination, whereas the full combined model may be more appropriate for further validation in tertiary-care settings where cytokine testing is available.

The thresholds derived in this study also have potential clinical interpretability. For example, IL-2 ≥80.7 pg/mL, TNF-α ≥91.0 pg/mL and an mPIRO score ≥5.5 were associated with positive likelihood ratios ranging from approximately 3.3 to 12.8 and negative likelihood ratios around 0.35–0.39. According to commonly used interpretative frameworks, a positive likelihood ratio above 10 provides strong evidence to “rule in” disease, whereas a negative likelihood ratio below 0.1 provides strong evidence to “rule out” disease. Although our negative likelihood ratios did not reach this stringent threshold, they still indicate a reduction in risk. These thresholds should not be applied as definitive decision cut-offs before prospective validation and assessment across different etiological and clinical subgroups.

The clinical implications of our findings should therefore be considered preliminary. Combining cytokine measurements with a bedside clinical score may help identify children who warrant closer observation or earlier escalation of monitoring, particularly when clinical status is evolving. However, real-world implementation would require confirmation that the model adds value beyond routinely available markers such as CRP and PCT, and that cytokine testing is feasible in terms of cost, laboratory availability, turnaround time, inter-laboratory reproducibility, and large-scale standardization. The nomogram should be viewed as a hypothesis-generating visualization of the model rather than as an immediately implementable bedside tool.

This study has several limitations. First, the number of children progressing to severe pneumonia was small (n = 22), which limited statistical power and increased the risk of overfitting. Second, this was a single-center retrospective study without external validation; thus, the predictive performance may be overestimated. Third, some items in the mPIRO score overlap with the definition of severe pneumonia, which may cause potential bias and inflate prognostic performance. Fourth, pathogenic typing was not available, and we did not compare the model’s performance with procalcitonin (PCT). Fifth, only baseline biomarkers were measured without dynamic monitoring during hospitalization. Finally, cytokine detection is limited by cost, availability, and standardization. Future prospective, multicenter studies with larger samples, serial measurements, and head-to-head comparisons with conventional biomarkers are needed before clinical application.

Conclusion

In a cohort of hospitalized children with initially non-severe community-acquired pneumonia, elevated baseline IL-2 and TNF-α levels and higher mPIRO scores were associated with subsequent progression to severe pneumonia, whereas IL-8 showed no discriminatory value in this cohort. A combined “cytokines + mPIRO” model showed the highest apparent prognostic performance, but the findings should be considered preliminary and hypothesis-generating because of the retrospective single-center design, small number of severe cases, lack of validation, and absence of direct comparison with PCT. Prospective multicenter validation is required before the model can be recommended for routine clinical decision-making.
