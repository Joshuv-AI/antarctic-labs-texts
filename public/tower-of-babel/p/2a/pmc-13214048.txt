Cultural evolution of beauty standards
Authors: Louis Boucherie, Sagar Kumar, Katharina Ledebur, August Lohse, Karolina Śliwa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceWho gets to be beautiful, and who decides? The media and fashion industry set aspirational body standards with measurable consequences for body dissatisfaction and eating disorders. Calls for greater diversity have grown louder, and the industry has responded. Using 25 y of data across runway shows, advertisements, and magazine covers, we document a contradiction: Headline measures of diversity have improved, yet the aspirational body is essentially unchanged. Broader casting has emerged, but remains confined to outliers that do not shift the norm. Models cast in these roles are also disproportionately non-White, revealing a symbolic diversification in which the industry satisfies multiple representational demands by concentrating them on already-marginalized individuals rather than changing who counts as aspirational.

The media and fashion industries promote narrow beauty ideals, emphasizing extremely thin female bodies and lean, muscular male physiques (1–3). Repeated exposure to these ideals in the contemporary hypermediated cultural landscape can have harmful consequences for self-perception through social comparison and objectification (4, 5). Viewers evaluate themselves against models who embody unrealistic ideals and typically feel they fall short (6, 7). As a result, body dissatisfaction rises, increasing the risk of psychological distress and disordered eating symptoms (1, 8), which can lead to elevated mortality (9). These effects are consistent across genders and age groups (10, 11). Despite these consequences, industry practices have normalized unsafe body sizes among professional models (3).

However, standards for beauty are not limited to body size alone. Modeling hiring practices have been shown to prioritize both thinness and racial exclusion (2). More broadly, across media and cultural industries, skewed visibility normalizes the marginal status of underrepresented groups. This legitimizes their exclusion from visibility, voice, and opportunity in adjacent markets and institutions (12). Media representation also operates as a signifying practice: Casting, styling, and framing produce meaning by marking which bodies and identities are considered aspirational or professional. This shapes hiring, taste formation, and the allocation of attention and capital (13). Through cultivation, sustained exposure creates entrenched racial stereotypes (14), and shifts shared beliefs about what kinds of people are typical or desirable (15). Together, these processes allow narrow representational regimes to reinforce social stratification while exporting psychological and health costs to the wider public.

Despite the public salience of these concerns, quantitative evidence capable of guiding policy and industry standards remains limited. Prominent assessments rely on industry-commissioned audits (16, 17), while academic studies are limited by small sample size (2, 18), region-specific focus (19–21), and an inability to track intersectional representation over time (22, 23). As a result, we lack system-level estimates of how representation evolves across media channels and identities, and how far model physiques deviate from those of the general population.

We address this gap by assembling a large, long-horizon dataset spanning the principal visual media sites where ideals have been historically established and disseminated— advertisements, magazine covers, editorials, and runway shows. The dataset comprises 793,199 work records spanning 25 y (2000–2024), linking anthropometric measurements, visible traits (e.g., skin tone, hair/eye color), national origin, and images (Materials and Methods) of models. We leverage this dataset to quantify representation and body composition over time and across intersecting identities. We provide system-level estimates that clarify where diversity has expanded, where it has stalled, and how far model physiques remain from general population. Finally, to explore how regulations may affect body representation, we compare two policy interventions: the 2006 Milan BMI floor, which banned female models below 18.5 kg m−2 from the runway (24), and the 2017 French medical certificate law, which required a health attestation but imposed no numeric threshold (25).

Body Measurement Means Remain Stable While Diversity Increases.

Cross-sectional means for height, waist, hips, and Relative Fat Mass (RFM; a body composition index that estimates body fat percentage from height, waist circumference, and sex (26); see Materials and Methods) are constant across all work types, i.e. fashion shows, advertisements, magazine covers, and editorials for females (Fig. 1A). Only the bust circumference shows a consistent decline from an average of 85 cm to 82 cm. In contrast, dispersion increases significantly (Fig. 1B and SI Appendix, Fig. S19). Since the early 2000s, the SD of all measurements has roughly doubled (for example, hip circumference s.d. from 2 cm to 4 cm), with similar patterns across work types, indicating a broader range of represented body sizes even as central tendencies remain stable. Appearances and origin traits diversify. Hair and eye color distributions shift away from the dominance of lighter phenotypes: The prevalence of blonde hair and blue eyes declines while darker shades gain share (Fig. 1 C and D). This coincides with a rise in diversity of national origins, as shown by increasing entropy over time (SI Appendix, Fig. S1) with growing contributions from Sub-Saharan Africa, East Asia, and South Asia (Fig. 1E). Eastern European origins peak in the early 2000s and subsequently decline. Western Europe and North America remain the largest sources, albeit with modest relative declines. These trajectories are consistent with the gradual broadening of racialized features in fashion imagery reported in prior content analyses (28–30).

Increasing Diversity Is Due to Outliers.

Distributional diagnostics show that the increase in variability is concentrated in the upper tail. The interquartile ranges for bust, waist, hips, and RFM remain essentially flat (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S20), indicating stability in the median and bulk-physique. By contrast, higher-moment statistics are increasing. Skewness is shifting from near zero to positive, and excess kurtosis is rising for size measures (Fig. 2 B and C and SI Appendix, Figs. S21–S22). This indicates a heavier right tail with more large-size observations. These patterns are consistent with a strategy of selective entry of plus-size models (defined using the industry standard as US dress size ≥12; see SI Appendix, section 2A.3), which expands the tail while leaving central quantiles unchanged (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Male representation in media and fashion remains limited (2, 3), a condition mirrored in our dataset, where male samples are smaller and more irregular than female ones (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). Despite this reduced coverage, the overall trend shows continuity rather than change. Male models remain distinctly lean and muscular compared with the general population, and no meaningful broadening of represented body types is visible (SI Appendix, Fig. S18). This stability aligns with recent media and industry reports noting the continued absence of size diversity in men’s advertising and runway imagery (31), and with psychological evidence that exposure to idealized physiques maintains narrow appearance norms (8, 10, 32).

Models Remain Physiologically Distinct from the Population.

Comparisons with the general population show a clear separation in RFM (body-fat levels). US-based female models cluster tightly around RFM of 18.4±3.14% with comparatively low variance. In contrast, US women aged 17 – 30 in the NHANES study cluster near 38.5±6.6% with a much broader spread (Fig. 2D and SI Appendix, section 2B). The two distributions exhibit minimal overlap, indicating that models remain concentrated at the low-RFM extreme (underweight range).

The annual gap, ΔRFM≡RFMNHANES−RFMModels is large (about 20 percentage points) and temporally stable, with only a negligible linear increase of ∼0.01 percentage points per year (Fig. 2E). Thus, the physiological distance between the models and age-matched women has not meaningfully narrowed or widened over the past two decades.

To account for multivariate body shape, we perform principal components analysis (PCA) using height, bust, waist, and hips (Fig. 2 F and G). In the PC1–PC2 plane, groups separate along a direction orthogonal to RFM isolines, indicating selection primarily on RFM (body-fat level). The decomposition shows that PC1 captures overall size (positive on height and circumferences), whereas PC2 indexes slenderness (positive on height, negative on circumferences) (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Together, these results show a strong selection; models exhibit an exceptionally lean, low-variance phenotype that differs significantly from that of age-matched women. This disparity persists despite the diversity gains documented in Fig. 1. Having established that diversity gains are concentrated in distributional tails rather than central tendencies, we next examine whether these patterns are uniform across the industry or stratified by institutional position.

Effects Are Heterogeneous Across the Industry.

To test whether diversity evolves uniformly across fashion and media, we stratify brands and magazines by prestige. We build a bipartite collaboration network linking models to brands or magazines and compute centrality-based influence scores to derive a data-driven hierarchy (33, 34). The distribution is partitioned into ordered tiers (Elite, High, Mid, Low; see Materials and Methods). This hierarchy captures sustained influence and aligns with expert assessments of brand and magazine status (SI Appendix, Fig. S4).

Using these tiers, we compare the prevalence of models at the extremes of RFM. The share of low-RFM appearances (Bottom decile) declines monotonically from Elite to Low, indicating stronger thinness selection at the top of the hierarchy (Fig. 3A). By contrast, high-RFM appearances (Top decile) follow a U-shaped pattern, overrepresented at both Elite and Low tiers, and least common in the middle tiers (Fig. 3B). Descriptive Patterns Following Regulatory Interventions.

Two regulatory interventions provide natural comparison points for body-size trends. In 2006, Milan Fashion Week introduced a ban on female models with a body mass index (BMI) below 18.5 (24). In 2017, France introduced a medical-certificate requirement for models, without specifying numeric cutoffs (25). The contrast between the two policies is stark. In Milan, mean RFM diverges sharply upward from control cities immediately following the 2006 intervention, with the gap persisting through 2010 (Fig. 3C). This shift is concentrated in the lower tail: the 25th percentile of RFM rises markedly after 2006, consistent with a reduction in the prevalence of the thinnest models (Fig. 3E). Preintervention trends show no such divergence, suggesting the break coincides with policy implementation rather than preexisting differences (SI Appendix, section 4H). Full distributions reveal a clear leftward truncation in Milan post-2006, with the lower tail effectively cut off relative to control cities (SI Appendix, Fig. S28). In Paris, no comparable discontinuity is visible. Trends in both the mean and lower tail remain stable, tracking broader industry patterns before and after the 2017 regulation (Fig. 3 D and F). These contrasting patterns are consistent with the hypothesis that explicit numeric thresholds may be more effective than flexible guidelines at reducing underweight representation (35).

Intersectional Minorities Carry Body Diversity.

We showed that body-size diversity has increased through the selective inclusion of plus-size outliers. In the meantime, diversity in phenotypes and national origins has also expanded. We now examine whether these dimensions of inclusion are distributed independently or intersect on the same bodies. Representation of non-White and plus-size models has increased substantially since the early 2010s. The share of non-White models rose from 13% in 2011 to 44% in 2024 (Fig. 4A). However, gains in size diversity are disproportionately carried by non-White models. Starting from similar levels in 2011, the gap in representation between White and non-White models has since widened (Fig. 4B). Since 2017, the odds ratio of being a plus-size model is significantly higher for non-White models. It averages 4.5 for the 2017–2024 period, meaning that non-White models are 4.5 times more likely to be plus-size models (Fig. 4 C and D and SI Appendix, Table S6). This intersectional concentration shows that much of the industry’s diversity is dis- proportionately borne by non-White models, reflecting showcase inclusion rather than systemic change (22, 30, 36).

This imbalance is not new. Slenderness and ethnicity have long been decisive criteria in model selection, both within fashion (2) and in the industry as a whole (37). Research on performative diversity argues that industries often favor intersectional representation that maximizes symbolic value while minimizing structural change (38, 39). In this light, body-size inclusion is disproportionately borne by racialized models, allowing brands to satisfy multiple representational pressures simultaneously by consolidating multiple markers of diversity within a single body.

Discussion

Our findings reveal a paradox in the cultural evolution of beauty standards. Although media and fashion representation has diversified in terms of visible traits and national origins, physique ideals remain stable. Variation in body size reflects the selective inclusion of outliers rather than a redefinition of the standard model body type. This symbolic inclusivity may accentuate, rather than diminish, the contrast between dominant thinness and marginal diversity (40, 41).

Diversity is also unevenly distributed. Increases in body-size representation are disproportionately carried by non-White individuals, indicating that the labor of inclusivity falls on groups already marginalized by ethnicity and region. This intersectional concentration provides quantitative evidence for critiques of superficial inclusivity and aesthetic labor in fashion (22, 30, 36). When benchmarked against the general population, this selective diversification reveals its limits: For two decades, the gap in body composition between US models and US women has remained close to 20 percentage points, with no sign of convergence. Symbolic diversification has not narrowed the divide between fashion’s ideals and real bodies, a disconnect long associated with body dissatisfaction and disordered eating (1, 6).

Descriptive trends following Milan’s BMI rule show an upward shift in model body-fat levels, whereas no comparable pattern followed France’s medical-certificate law. This is consistent with the view that explicit numerical thresholds may work better than flexible guidelines. Yet such measures address only the lowest end of representation. Rebalancing ideals requires attention to the center as well as the tails, encouraging broader casting, transparent reporting of body-measurement statistics, and independent monitoring of diversity commitments. Policies that reward gradual shifts in typical physiques, rather than occasional extremes, could move the industry toward more realistic and inclusive norms.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. For 23% of records where self-reported profile fields were unavailable, gender and ethnicity were imputed using probabilistic classifiers (face-based), which provide scalable but imperfect proxies for identity, the remaining 77% use labels self-reported by models or agencies. These methods necessarily impose simplified categories. Gender is reduced to a binary frame, and ethnicity is inferred from appearance rather than self-identification. Such simplifications risk excluding or misclassifying transgender, nonbinary, mixed-ethnicity, and other marginalized groups, and they collapse distinct identities into broad “White/non-White” categories. Although these tools enable systematic analysis across a large dataset, they should not be read as capturing lived identity in its complexity. Finally, the two regulatory interventions are separated by over 10 y and differ in their visibility and cultural context, so any comparison between them should be interpreted with caution.

While this dataset offers one of the most comprehensive records of model representation to date spanning anthropometric, phenotypic, and demographic attributes, it remains incomplete. Weight and age are disclosed only for a subset of models due to the industry’s historical reluctance to disclose health-related metrics (3). Lacking direct weight data, we use Relative Fat Mass (RFM) as a proxy derived from height, waist, and sex. Although RFM is generally more accurate than BMI for estimating body fat (27), it is not equivalent to a clinical measurement.

The narrow beauty standards documented here are precisely those encoded in generative AI training sets. As virtual and AI-generated models enter advertising and editorial contexts (42), these systems risk amplifying rather than correcting representational bias (43, 44). Mitigating algorithmic harm will require systematic auditing of training corpora, fairness benchmarks for generated outputs, and transparency standards governing synthetic media in commercial contexts (45).

Constraints on Generality.

Our findings characterize the evolution of beauty standards within the internationally oriented fashion and media industry between 2000 and 2024, as indexed by two professional aggregator platforms (models.com and fashionmodeldirectory.com). These platforms cover the worldwide fashion circuit—runways, advertising, editorials, and magazine covers—which is itself Western-centric in its centers of production and prestige. We expect our results to generalize to comparable industry segments during this period, but they may not extend to domestic fashion ecosystems outside this circuit, user-generated social media, or AI-generated media, which operate under different selection pressures. Our population benchmark (NHANES, ages 17 to 30) is US-specific, so the model–population gap we document is strictly a US comparison; analogous gaps in other countries may differ in magnitude. Our intersectional findings are bounded by the categorical simplifications required at this scale: Gender is treated as binary and ethnicity is collapsed into White versus non-White, which omits nonbinary and transgender identities and masks heterogeneity across specific racialized groups. We encourage future work to test these boundaries directly, using finer-grained identity categories and nonprofessional visual media.

Materials and Methods

Datasets.

We compiled a dataset from professional pages on (models.com and fashionmodeldirectory.com. These platforms aggregate portfolio data submitted by modeling agencies and verified by industry professionals; profile attributes are typically self-reported by models or provided by their representing agencies. After deduplication at the name-agency-URL level, the corpus comprises 793,199 records between the years 2000–2024. Prior to 2000, records are too sparse to support reliable statistical analysis (SI Appendix, Figs. S6–S8). Available attributes include anthropometrics (height, bust, waist, hips, dress size, shoe size), identity fields (name, nationality; when available, country of origin, age, weight, gender, ethnicity), Instagram handle, and profile photographs. All anthropometric measurements were standardized to SI units. The records are subdivided into the following categories: Advertisements (92,283 records), Magazine Covers (86,382), Fashion Runways (413,265), and Editorials (146,503). Each record includes the model identifier, job type, and at least one associated image. These images are used for downstream annotations. When self-reported profile fields are unavailable (23% of records), gender and ethnicity are estimated using probabilistic classifiers applied to profile photographs; these serve as scalable but imperfect proxies for identity (SI Appendix, Figs. S12 and S13) (46). Instagram follower counts were downloaded from instagram.com using model handles listed on the professional pages.

To compare body measurements of American models to a general population sample, we use data on demographics and body measurements from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) cycle (47) (2000–2023). We select only individuals between 17 and 30 to approximate the age range of the models. We use the information on gender, height, and waist measurements to calculate the Relative Fat Mass (RFM) metric.

Ethics.

This research constitutes a secondary analysis of publicly available data and does not meet the regulatory definition of human subjects research. Career records and anthropometric data were obtained from publicly accessible professional portfolios (models.com, fashionmodeldirectory.com). The NHANES comparison sample was collected by the US National Center for Health Statistics under an ethics protocol approved by the NCHS Ethics Review Board, with informed consent obtained from all participants at the time of data collection. No additional ethics approval was required for the present analysis.

Relative Fat Mass (RFM) is a body composition index that estimates an individual’s body fat percentage using height, waist circumference, and gender, which was found to better predict whole-body fat percentage among adult individuals than the body mass index (BMI) (27). Statistical Methods.

All error bars throughout this paper correspond to bootstrapped 95% CIs. When these bootstrapped 95% CIs do not include the null value (typically 0), they indicate a statistically significant difference at the α=0.05 level.

Network Analysis of Prestige Hierarchy.

The prestige hierarchy is derived entirely from the structure of the collaboration network, without relying on external rankings or expert labels. The approach rests on a recursive principle: A brand is prestigious if it books models who themselves work for other prestigious brands. This mutual reinforcement is captured by eigenvector centrality on the one-mode brand projection of the bipartite model–brand graph (34, 48). We prefer eigenvector centrality over degree centrality because degree conflates volume with influence: A brand booking many models who each work only for peripheral houses would score high on degree but low on eigenvector centrality, correctly reflecting its marginal position (48).

Odds Ratio and Intersectionality Analysis.

To quantify the association between ethnicity and plus-size status, we compute odds ratios comparing non-White to White models. We classify models as plus-size if their converted US dress size is ≥12, following industry standards (17). All non-White categories are collapsed into a single group due to sparse plus-size counts in individual categories. For each year t, let at, bt, ct, dt denote the counts of plus-size non-White, non-plus-size non-White, plus-size White, and non-plus-size White models, respectively. We apply a continuity correction of 0.5 to all cells to handle sparse counts. The odds ratio is[2]ORt=(at+0.5)(dt+0.5)(bt+0.5)(ct+0.5).
