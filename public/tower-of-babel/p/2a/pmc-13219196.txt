Caffeic acid restores neurogenesis and synaptic integrity under glucolipotoxic stress by suppressing inflammation and pyroptosis
Authors: I-Ning Tsai, Xiang-Ning Xie, Ping-En Ko, Yung-Che Tsai, Ching-Chun Chen, Li-Sung Hsu, Chau-Jong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diabetes mellitus is frequently associated with cognitive dysfunction, primarily attributed to impaired hippocampal neurogenesis, oxidative stress, inflammation, and pyroptosis. Caffeic acid (CA), a dietary polyphenol, has demonstrated antioxidant and neuroprotective effects. This study evaluated the protective role of CA under diabetic-like conditions using an in vitro glucolipotoxicity model in HT-22 hippocampal neurons exposed to high glucose and oleic acid (HG + OA). CA was administered at low (5 µM) and high (25 µM) concentrations prior to HG + OA treatment. CA significantly enhanced neuronal viability and restored the expression of neurogenesis markers (Nestin, DCX, NeuN) and synaptic proteins (PSD-95, Synaptophysin). Furthermore, CA elevated antioxidant enzyme levels (Nrf2, catalase, SOD-1), regulated apoptosis through increased Bcl-2 and decreased BAX expression, and attenuated inflammatory responses. Pyroptosis was also suppressed, as evidenced by reduced gasdermin D (GSDMD) expression. These findings suggest that CA confers multifactorial neuroprotection against glucolipotoxic injury, and may serve as a dietary modulator for mitigating diabetes-associated cognitive decline in vitro.Graphical abstractCA promotes adult hippocampal neurogenesis (AHN) through activation of the BDNF/TrkB/Akt/mTOR/CREB signaling pathway. In addition, CA reduces oxidative stress by activating the Nrf2-mediated antioxidant response and suppresses inflammation and pyroptosis through inhibition of the NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling pathway, thereby protecting neuronal function and improving cognitive performance. Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is increasingly recognized not only as a metabolic disorder but also as a key risk factor for cognitive dysfunction and neurodegenerative diseases (Moreno-Jimenez et al. 2021). Recent studies have demonstrated that patients with T2DM exhibit a significantly increased risk for Alzheimer’s disease and other forms of dementia, largely due to insulin resistance, oxidative stress, and chronic inflammation disrupting cerebral homeostasis (Kciuk et al. 2024). Among brain regions, the hippocampus—crucial for learning and memory—is particularly vulnerable to such metabolic insults (Davidson and Stevenson 2024).

The concept of glucolipotoxicity in T2DM was introduced based on the observation that elevated levels of glucose and free fatty acids (FFAs) act synergistically to impair cellular function (Weir 2020). Glucolipotoxic stress induces mitochondrial dysfunction, excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production (Zuo et al. 2019), and neuroinflammation (Ezkurdia et al. 2023), which collectively impair synaptic plasticity and adult hippocampal neurogenesis (AHN) (Wieckowska-Gacek et al. 2021). AHN, primarily occurring in the dentate gyrus, plays a crucial role in learning and memory by generating new neurons with enhanced plasticity and excitability (Liu et al. 2026). However, diabetes-associated hyperglycemia and insulin resistance significantly impair AHN, contributing to cognitive decline (Yu et al. 2025).

In addition to impaired neurogenesis, recent studies have identified pyroptosis as a key contributor to neuronal injury in metabolic disorders such as diabetes, given its proinflammatory nature and distinct mechanism from apoptosis (Oladapo et al. 2024). Pyroptosis is executed through the cleavage of gasdermin D (GSDMD) by inflammatory caspases, leading to membrane pore formation, cell swelling, membrane rupture, and the release of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18 (Yu et al. 2021). Activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in the central nervous system plays a pivotal role in both acute and chronic brain disorders, promoting neuroinflammation through caspase-1 activation and the subsequent release of IL-1β and IL-18, thereby contributing to neuronal dysfunction and disease progression (Chiarini et al. 2023). However, the interaction between pyroptosis and impaired AHN remains poorly understood, and approaches addressing both processes have yet to be fully explored.

Among natural compounds with neuroprotective potential, caffeic acid (CA) is a hydroxycinnamic acid that is abundantly found in coffee, fruits, and vegetables (Birková 2020). Caffeic acid (CA) has attracted considerable attention due to its strong antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects. Recent studies show that caffeic acid attenuates neuroinflammation by suppressing proinflammatory mediators and preserving mitochondrial function via activation of the SIRT1/PGC1α pathway (Zhang et al. 2024). Recent findings demonstrate that CA mitigates oxidative stress-induced neuronal injury by activating the Nrf2 pathway, promoting GSH synthesis, and inhibiting ROS accumulation and ferroptosis in ischemic brain models (Li et al. 2024). CA effectively suppresses both canonical and non-canonical inflammasome pathways, including NLRP3 and caspase-11 signaling, thus attenuating macrophage pyroptosis and systemic inflammatory responses (Liu et al. 2023). However, direct evidence under glucolipotoxic stress conditions remains sparse.

Given the high vulnerability of hippocampal neurons to oxidative and inflammatory insults, HT-22 hippocampal neuronal cells offer a suitable in vitro platform to dissect the molecular mechanisms underlying diabetes-induced neurotoxicity (Zhang et al. 2020). HT-22 cells provide a controlled environment to evaluate synaptic integrity, cell viability, and molecular pathways in response to metabolic stress.

We hypothesize that CA exerts neuroprotective effects under glucolipotoxic stress by promoting hippocampal neurogenesis and inhibiting pyroptosis. This study aims to evaluate whether CA restores synaptic integrity and supports neuronal survival, thereby improving cognitive outcomes under diabetic conditions.

Materials and methods

Preparation of caffeic acid

Caffeic acid, sourced from Sigma Aldrich (#C0625, St. Louis, MO, USA), had a purity of 98%. It was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide to create a stock solution at a concentration of 10 mM stock. This stock solution was then diluted according to the requirements of subsequent experiments.

Reagents and chemicals

Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Antibodies used in this study included Nestin (Abcam, #ab11306), DCX (Abcam, #ab207175), NeuN (Abcam, #ab177487), PSD-95 (Abcam, #ab18258), Synaptophysin (Santa Cruz, #sc-17750), BDNF (IReal, #IR14-38), p-TrkB (Abclonal, #AP0423), TrkB (Abclonal, #A2099), p-Akt (Cell Signaling, #4060), Akt (Santa Cruz, #sc-81434), p-mTOR (Santa Cruz, #sc-293133), mTOR (Santa Cruz, #H-266), p-CREB (Cell Signaling, #9198), Catalase (Enzo Life Sciences, #BML-SA115), SOD-1 (Santa Cruz, #A0274), Nrf2 (Santa Cruz, #sc-722), Bcl-2 (Santa Cruz, #sc-492), Bax (Santa Cruz, #sc-526), p-NF-κB (Abclonal, #AP123), NF-κB (Santa Cruz, #sc-8008), NLRP3 (Cell Signaling, #D4D8T), IL-1β (Abcam, #ab283818), and GAPDH (Novus, #NB300-221). All primary antibodies used in this study were commercially obtained and are listed with their corresponding Research Resource Identifiers (RRIDs) to ensure transparency and reproducibility. Detailed information, including antibody targets, vendors, catalog numbers, applications, and RRIDs, is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Cell culture and cell treatment

HT-22 murine hippocampal neuronal cells, a subclone derived from the HT4 neuronal cell line, were originally immortalized from primary mouse hippocampal neurons. The cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, United States). HT-22 cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 95% humidity and 5% CO₂. The culture medium was refreshed every two days to ensure optimal cell growth. For experiments, cells were preincubated with caffeic acid (CA) and subsequently treated with oleic acid and high glucose (OA + HG) at the indicated concentrations and durations. All treatments were prepared fresh immediately before application.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay. HT-22 cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 10^4 cells per well in a 24-well plate and cultured in an incubator for 24 h to allow attachment. Subsequently, cells were treated with the designated concentrations of the inducing and intervening agents for 24 h. After treatment, the culture medium was removed, and fresh medium containing 10% MTT reagent (5 mg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich) was added to each well. Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator for 3 h. Following the incubation, the medium was removed, and the plates were left uncovered to air-dry until completely dry. Next, 1 mL of isopropanol was added to each well to dissolve the purple formazan crystals. The solution from each well was transferred to Eppendorf tubes and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min. Subsequently, 200 µL of the supernatant was transferred to a 96-well plate, and absorbance at 563 nm was measured using a multifunctional microplate reader (Molecular Devices, SpectraMax M5). Cell viability percentages were calculated by comparing absorbance values to those of the DMSO-treated control group. All experiments were performed in triplicate to ensure statistical reliability, and results were analyzed to determine cell viability differences among the experimental groups.

Morphological analysis of neurite length

Neurite morphology was analyzed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Images of HT-22 cells were captured under a light microscope at 100× magnification. Prior to measurement, images were calibrated using the scale bar to convert pixel distances to micrometers (µm). Neurite length was determined using the segmented line tool in ImageJ by manually tracing the longest neurite extending from the cell body to the distal tip. A neurite was defined as a cellular projection longer than the diameter of the cell body. For each experimental group, images from randomly selected microscopic fields were analyzed. The longest neurite per cell was used for statistical analysis, and the results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Western blot assay

Following CA treatment, polyacrylamide gel (8–12%) was used to separate the entire cell lysates into equal proteins, which were then transferred to PVDF transfer membranes. After steeping in blocking buffer (PBS with 5% nonfat milk) for one to three hours, the blot membrane was incubated in primary antibody buffer at 4 °C for the entire night before being rinsed three times with PBS. Following three PBS washes, the blot was incubated for one hour at 4 °C in a secondary antibody buffer. Finally, the antigen-antibody complex was discovered using the ECL detection technique. A densitometer was used to measure the image’s relative band density.

Venn diagram analysis

To identify the common target genes associated with inflammation, pyroptosis, neurogenesis, and caffeic acid (CA), gene sets related to each condition were collected from publicly available databases. The overlapping genes among these datasets were analyzed using an online Venn diagram tool (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/). The intersecting gene sets were considered potential key targets involved in the multi-functional effects of CA.

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis

The overlapping target genes identified from the Venn analysis were imported into the STRING database (version 11.5; https://string-db.org/) to construct a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network. The organism was set to Homo sapiens, and a minimum required interaction score of 0.4 (medium confidence) was applied. The resulting interaction network was visualized to evaluate the relationships among target proteins, where nodes represent proteins and edges indicate predicted or known protein–protein associations.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis

To explore the biological functions and signaling pathways associated with the identified target genes, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the DAVID database (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) or similar enrichment platforms. Pathways with a p-value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. The top enriched pathways, particularly those related to inflammation and NF-κB signaling, were visualized and interpreted to elucidate the potential mechanisms underlying the effects of CA.

Molecular docking analysis

Molecular docking was performed to evaluate the potential interactions between caffeic acid (CA) and target proteins. The 2D chemical structure of CA was obtained from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) and saved in SDF format. The SDF file was then imported into ChemBio3D Ultra (v14.0) to generate the corresponding 3D structure, which was subsequently energy-minimized and saved in MOL2 format.

The protein structures of target genes were retrieved from the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org) to obtain the corresponding protein IDs. The three-dimensional crystal structures of target proteins were then downloaded from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) database (https://www.rcsb.org) in PDB format. Protein structures were preprocessed using PyMOL by removing water molecules and co-crystallized ligands, followed by saving the cleaned structures in PDB format.

AutoDockTools (v1.5.6) was used to prepare the receptor proteins by adding polar hydrogens and defining the active binding pockets, followed by conversion into PDBQT format. Molecular docking was conducted using AutoDock Vina. For each ligand–protein pair, docking simulations were performed 20 times, and the conformation with the lowest binding energy was selected as the optimal docking result.

The best docking models were visualized and analyzed using PyMOL to examine the interactions between ligands and amino acid residues within the binding sites. Three-dimensional interaction diagrams were generated to illustrate the binding modes and key residue interactions.

Immunofluorescence staining

Following a 5-minute acetone fixation, the slices were rinsed three times with PBS buffer, blocked for 30 min at 37 °C using 10% blocking buffer of goat serum (Solarbio), and then incubated with goat anti-rabbit GSDMD antibody (1:400; Abcam), Neutrophil Marker antibody (1:200; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), and the F4/80 antibody (1:100; Cell Signaling Technology) in that order. GSDMD, neutrophils, and macrophages were then labeled, and the process continued overnight at 4 °C. Following three gentle PBS rinses, the slices were stained with secondary antibody in the dark for one hour each. They were then left to stand at room temperature for ten more minutes using DAPI solution. The slices were then examined and photographed using a Zeiss Axiovert microscope at a magnification of × 40 after being immersed in an antifade solution and covered with a coverslip.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.2, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). For comparisons involving multiple groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied, followed by the Bonferroni post hoc test to correct for multiple comparisons. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), unless stated otherwise. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.(Ostertagová et al. 2014).

Results

Caffeic acid protects HT-22 cells against cytotoxicity induced by oleic acid + high glucose

To determine the optimal concentrations of CA for subsequent experiments, HT-22 cells were treated with varying doses of CA for 24 h, and cell viability was evaluated. The results revealed that 5, 10, and 25 µM CA maintained cell viability at > 95%, 50 µM reduced viability to < 90%, and 100 µM caused a significant decline in viability (Fig. 1A). To mimic a glucolipotoxic environment characteristic of T2DM, HT-22 cells were exposed to varying concentrations of oleic acid (OA) combined with a fixed high glucose (HG) concentration (25 mM) for 24 h. Cell viability decreased in a dose-dependent manner, with 250 µM OA + 25 mM HG reducing viability to approximately 60% (Fig. 1B). To evaluate the protective effects of CA under glucolipotoxic conditions, cells were divided into 3 groups: a control group (untreated), an OA + HG induction group, and intervention groups receiving CA treatment. The induction group was treated with 250 µM OA and 25 mM HG. Intervention groups were pretreated with 5, 10, or 25 µM CA for 30 min before OA + HG exposure for 24 h. The OA + HG group exhibited a significant decline in cell viability to approximately 65% (p < 0.05 vs. control). Treatment with 25 µM CA significantly improved cell viability, restoring it to > 75% (p < 0.05 vs. the induction group; Fig. 1C). Based on these findings, 5 µM (low dose) and 25 µM (high dose) CA were selected for further analysis.

Caffeic acid improves synaptic morphology and neurogenesis-related proteins through activation of the BDNF/TrkB pathway

Synapses play a crucial role in signal transmission in the brain, and alterations in synaptic morphology, particularly neurite length, are key indicators of neuronal health. Our results demonstrated that pretreatment with 5 or 25 µM CA for 30 min before OA + HG exposure markedly reduced neurite length in HT-22 cells. Treatment with CA significantly restored neurite length compared with the OA + HG group, demonstrating its protective effects on neuronal morphology (Fig. 2A). To further investigate neurogenesis-related molecular pathways underlying the protective effects of CA, we examined the expression of key neuronal markers. OA + HG exposure significantly reduced the expression of nestin (a neural stem cell marker), NeuN (a mature neuron marker), and doublecortin (a marker of immature neurons) compared with the control group. CA intervention effectively restored their expression, suggesting that CA promotes neurogenesis by counteracting the inhibitory effects of OA + HG on neuronal development (Fig. 2B). In addition, we examined the effects of CA on synaptic plasticity-related proteins. Several studies have demonstrated that PSD-95 expression is closely linked to cognitive function(Ugalde-Trivino and Diaz-Guerra 2021) and that SYP expression is significantly lower in individuals with neurodegenerative diseases compared with healthy controls (Lauretti et al. 2017). OA + HG treatment markedly reduced the expression of these proteins compared with the control group. CA treatment significantly increased the levels of Tuj1, PSD-95, and SYP (Fig. 2C). These findings confirmed that CA enhanced synaptic plasticity and protected neuronal health by mitigating synaptic damage caused by OA + HG-induced glucolipotoxicity.

These pathways promote neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity by increasing p-Akt and p-mTOR levels and enhancing CREB activity in the hippocampus. Protective effects of caffeic acid against oxidative stress and apoptosis in HT-22 cells

Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) is a key transcription factor that regulates antioxidant responses and cellular defense mechanisms. Under oxidative stress, Nrf2 is released from its inhibitor Keap1 and translocates to the nucleus, where it upregulates the expression of antioxidant enzymes such as Catalase and SOD-1 (McCord et al. 2023). These enzymes neutralize hydrogen peroxide and superoxide anions, thereby reducing oxidative damage within cells. Our findings revealed that the induction group exhibited a significant reduction in the expression levels of Nrf2, catalase, and SOD-1, indicating impaired oxidative defense mechanisms. However, CA intervention effectively restored the expression of these proteins, demonstrating its antioxidant properties in counteracting glucolipotoxicity-induced oxidative stress (Fig. 4). Furthermore, ROS play a dual role in cellular signaling and damage. Although physiological levels of ROS contribute to normal cell signaling, excessive ROS accumulation disrupts cellular homeostasis, leading to oxidative stress and apoptosis through the activation of various intracellular signaling cascades (Redza-Dutordoir and Averill-Bates 2016). ROS-induced apoptosis is closely linked to the Bcl-2/BAX pathway, a critical regulator of cell survival and death. BAX promotes apoptosis, whereas Bcl-2 counteracts it, and their balance ultimately determines cell fate (Villalpando-Rodriguez and Gibson 2021).

CA alleviates neuroinflammation and pyroptosis induced by glucolipotoxicity

Pyroptosis is a form of inflammatory cell death characterized by the release of proinflammatory cytokines and the disruption of cellular integrity(Vande Walle and Lamkanfi 2016). The nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) pathway plays a crucial role in mediating the inflammatory response. Upon phosphorylation, NF-κB translocates to the nucleus, where it promotes the expression of inflammation-related proteins such as NLRP3, leading to the release of IL-1β and the initiation of a robust inflammatory cascade (Liu et al. 2017). Pyroptosis is driven by the activation of gasdermin D (GSDMD), which forms transmembrane pores that release inflammatory cytokines and amplify the inflammatory response (Burdette et al. 2021).

To elucidate the potential molecular mechanisms underlying the anti-inflammatory and anti-pyroptotic effects of CA, an integrated bioinformatics analysis was performed. We first conducted an intersection analysis among CA-related targets, inflammation-associated genes, pyroptosis-related genes, and neurogenesis-related genes, which identified a subset of overlapping candidate proteins (Fig. 6A). A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was subsequently constructed to explore the functional relationships among these targets, revealing a highly interconnected network associated with inflammation and cell death processes (Fig. 6B). To further investigate the biological relevance of these targets, KEGG pathway analysis was performed with a specific focus on NF-κB-related signaling pathways, given their well-established role in inflammation and pyroptosis. The results showed that several candidate proteins were associated with NF-κB-related pathways (Fig. 6C), supporting the hypothesis that NF-κB serves as a central regulatory axis linking inflammation and pyroptosis. Based on these findings, key pyroptosis-related proteins downstream of NF-κB signaling, including NLRP3 and GSDMD, were selected for molecular docking analysis. CA exhibited favorable binding affinities toward NLRP3 and GSDMD, with lowest binding energies of − 6.6 kcal/mol and − 5.8 kcal/mol, respectively, and interacted with critical amino acid residues (Fig. 6D). These results suggest that CA may directly interact with pyroptosis-related proteins and modulate their activity.

Discussion

CA demonstrated significant neuroprotective effects against glucolipotoxicity-induced damage in HT-22 hippocampal cells. CA treatment improved neuronal viability, promoted adult hippocampal neurogenesis, and preserved synaptic structure under metabolic stress. These protective outcomes appear to involve three major regulatory processes: enhancement of neurogenic activity, reduction of oxidative and cellular stress, and suppression of inflammation and pyroptosis.

Our findings suggest that the anti-inflammatory and anti-pyroptotic effects of CA are closely associated with modulation of the NF-κB signaling pathway, a key regulator linking inflammation and pyroptosis. Beyond the observed downregulation of NF-κB, NLRP3, IL-1β, and GSDMD expression, our integrated analytical approach further provides mechanistic insight into these regulatory effects. Through target intersection analysis and PPI network construction, we identified candidate proteins connecting neurogenesis, inflammation, and pyroptosis, while KEGG pathway analysis highlighted NF-κB-related signaling as a central regulatory axis. Importantly, molecular docking analysis revealed that CA exhibits favorable binding affinities with key pyroptosis-related proteins, including NLRP3 and GSDMD, suggesting that CA may directly interact with these proteins and modulate their activity. This potential direct interaction provides a structural basis for the observed inhibition of inflammasome activation and pyroptotic signaling.

These findings support a mechanistic model in which CA not only suppresses upstream NF-κB activation but may also directly interfere with downstream pyroptosis execution, thereby effectively attenuating glucolipotoxicity-induced neuroinflammation. By using a defined cellular model, this study clarifies both upstream and downstream relationships among these biological responses, offering insight into how CA may support neuronal resilience in a diabetic-like environment.

A previous study demonstrated that CA enhanced memory and neurogenesis in aging rats by upregulating hippocampal BDNF and reducing oxidative damage (Saenno et al. 2022), which aligns with our findings that CA promotes BDNF/TrkB signaling and synaptic plasticity under glucolipotoxic conditions. Furthermore, caffeic acid phenethyl ester (CAPE), a derivative of CA, was shown to mitigate fibrinogen-induced oxidative stress, neuronal loss, and cognitive dysfunction via NF-κB pathway inhibition (Sulimai et al. 2025). Although that study addressed neuroinflammation in the context of traumatic brain injury, and ours focuses on metabolic stress, both support the neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory potential of CA and its derivatives in neurodegenerative disorders. AHN and synaptic plasticity are tightly regulated by BDNF, which is not only synthesized by neurons but also secreted by endothelial cells within the hippocampal vascular niche. Recent studies have shown that diminished BDNF signaling in diabetic and high-fat diet models leads to reduced dendritic complexity and spine density, contributing to cognitive impairment (Gomes et al. 2025). Importantly, BDNF acts via TrkB activation to initiate downstream pathways such as PI3K/Akt/mTOR and CREB, which are essential for neuronal differentiation and synaptic remodeling (Singh et al. 2025). In our study, CA significantly restored BDNF and TrkB expression, which led to the reactivation of this signaling axis and subsequent upregulation of downstream effectors such as p-CREB, a key regulator of dendritic spine maturation. These results imply that CA not only supports neuronal survival but may also enhance the neurovascular niche and facilitate BDNF-mediated synaptic remodeling under glucolipotoxic stress, providing a potentially restorative mechanism against diabetes-induced hippocampal dysfunction. In addition, we observed that both low (CA5) and high (CA25) doses of CA elicited comparable upregulation of these markers, suggesting a possible threshold or saturation effect, where even low concentrations are sufficient to achieve maximal biological efficacy. This is consistent with previous in vivo findings showing that both 10 mg/kg and 20 mg/kg CA enhanced BDNF expression and hippocampal neurogenesis to a similar extent in aging rats (Saenno et al. 2022). These results implied that CA robust neurogenic effects even at lower doses, highlighting its therapeutic potential with minimal dosage requirements.

Excessive levels of glucose and lipids induce glucolipotoxicity, a pathological condition characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction, elevated ROS production, and neuronal apoptosis. The hippocampus is especially vulnerable to oxidative damage due to its high metabolic demand and sensitivity to redox-dependent signaling pathways (McCord et al. 2023). In this study, treatment with CA significantly activated the Nrf2 signaling pathway, which governs the cellular antioxidant defense system. Activation of Nrf2 was accompanied by increased expression of antioxidant enzymes such as SOD-1 and catalase, contributing to the attenuation of oxidative stress and supporting neuronal survival under glucolipotoxic conditions. These findings are consistent with studies reporting that Nrf2 deficiency aggravates oxidative injury and promotes cognitive decline (Gray et al. 2022).

Recent findings have emphasized the role of pyroptosis in neurodegenerative progression, particularly within the hippocampus (Qiu et al. 2023). Complementing these studies, our results demonstrate that hippocampal neurons themselves undergo pyroptosis in response to glucolipotoxic stress. In line with recent findings highlighting the crosstalk between pyroptosis and apoptosis (Bertheloot et al. 2021), our results suggest that CA may shift the cellular death mode from inflammatory pyroptosis toward regulated apoptosis via suppression of GSDMD and modulation of the Bcl-2/BAX pathway.

Importantly, emerging evidence indicates that CA may also act directly on the pyroptotic machinery. Previous studies have demonstrated that CA can directly bind to gasdermin D (GSDMD) and inhibit its cleavage and pore-forming activity, thereby blocking the execution phase of pyroptosis independently of upstream signaling (Liu et al. 2023). This provides a plausible mechanistic explanation for our observation that CA markedly suppresses GSDMD expression and IL-1β release under glucolipotoxic conditions. Consistent with studies identifying NF-κB as a priming signal for pyroptosis via NLRP3 upregulation (Yu et al. 2021), CA treatment significantly attenuated NF-κB activation, thereby suppressing downstream inflammasome assembly and IL-1β release. These findings indicate that CA confers neuroprotection by concurrently modulating upstream inflammatory signaling and downstream cell death mechanisms, ultimately preserving hippocampal neuronal integrity under glucolipotoxic stress.

Nevertheless, several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the current investigation was conducted using an in vitro HT-22 hippocampal neuronal model to simulate glucolipotoxic conditions associated with diabetes (Prasansuklab et al. 2023; Pfeiffer et al. 2014). Although this model is widely used to study neuronal oxidative stress and neuroinflammatory mechanisms, it does not fully recapitulate the complex physiological and metabolic interactions present in the brain in vivo. Therefore, the neuroprotective mechanisms identified in this study require further validation in appropriate in vivo models of diabetes-associated cognitive dysfunction. Studies using diabetic animal models will be important to determine whether CA can improve hippocampal neurogenesis, synaptic plasticity, and cognitive performance under diabetic conditions.

Future studies should also investigate the pharmacological and translational potential of caffeic acid in metabolic and neurodegenerative disorders. Evaluating the bioavailability, optimal dosage, and long-term safety of CA will be essential for determining its therapeutic feasibility. Moreover, integrating behavioral assessments with molecular and histological analyses in diabetic animal models will help clarify whether CA mediated modulation of oxidative stress, inflammation, and pyroptosis can ultimately translate into improvements in learning and memory. These investigations will provide important insights into the potential application of CA as a dietary bioactive compound for preventing diabetes-associated cognitive decline.

Conclusion

CA is a naturally occurring dietary polyphenol widely found in coffee, fruits, whole grains, and vegetables. In this study, we demonstrate that CA exerts protective effects against glucolipotoxicity-induced neuronal damage in HT-22 hippocampal cells. CA improved neuronal viability and synaptic integrity and enhanced the expression of neurogenesis-related markers, potentially through modulation of the BDNF/TrkB/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway. In addition, CA alleviated oxidative stress by activating the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway and attenuated inflammasome-associated inflammatory signaling, including the NF-κB/NLRP3/GSDMD pathway.

These findings suggest that CA may function as a multifaceted neuroprotective modulator under glucolipotoxic conditions and highlight its potential as a candidate compound for preventing diabetes-associated cognitive dysfunction. Further studies, particularly in vivo investigations and mechanistic validation, are warranted to confirm these protective effects and elucidate the precise molecular targets of CA.
