IL33-ST2 axis is a predictive biomarker for anti-PD1 therapeutic efficacy in advanced gastric cancer
Authors: Chie Kudo-Saito, Hiroshi Imazeki, Kengo Nagashima, Hirokazu Shoji, Kai Tsugaru, Naoki Takahashi, Takeshi Kawakami, Yusuke Amanuma, Takeru Wakatsuki, Naohiro Okano, Yukiya Narita, Yoshiyuki Yamamoto, Rika Kizawa, Kei Muro, Hitoshi Ichikawa, Narikazu Boku
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe previously demonstrated that interleukin-33 (IL33) plays a key role in treatment resistance through tumor polyploidization and the consequent immune dysfunction by recruiting and expanding various immunosuppressive cells that express its receptor ST2, and that anti-IL33 blockade therapy induces potent anti-tumor effect in mouse tumor models resistant to anti-PD1 therapy. These suggest that IL33 and ST2 are potentially promising targets that could contribute to anti-PD1/PDL1 therapy. Recently, an increasing number of clinical studies have shown a significant correlation between IL33 expression in tumor tissues and poor prognosis in patients with gastric cancer (GC). However, the clinical significance of targeting the IL33-ST2 axis in anti-PD1/PDL1 therapy for GC remains unclear.MethodsTumor tissues and peripheral blood were collected from patients with advanced GC before and after nivolumab monotherapy in the WJOG10417GTR study. Gene expression of il33 in tumor tissues, IL33 in plasma, and ST2+ cell subsets in four cell populations (IL17RB+GATA3+ ILC2s, CD117+FceRIa+ mast cells, CD11b+HLA-DR−PDL1+ MDSCs, and CD3+CD4+FOXP3+ Tregs) in peripheral blood cells were analyzed by RNA-sequencing, IHC, ELISA, or flow cytometry, and the relationship between their levels and patient prognosis was statistically analyzed.ResultsThe levels of tumor il33 gene expression and peripheral IL33 and ST2+ cells were higher in patients who showed progressive disease (PD) than in non-PD patients. High levels of tumor il33 gene expression at post-treatment, and high levels of peripheral ST2+ cells at both pre- and post-treatment were significantly associated with shorter progression-free survival and overall survival. Patients with low post-treatment levels of both tumor il33 and peripheral ST2+ cells, especially the mast cell subset, had long-term survival without PD, indicating durable responders.ConclusionsThese data suggest high IL33/ST2 levels are significant poor prognostic factors for nivolumab therapy for advanced GC. The IL33/ST2 axis may be useful as a biomarker for predicting and pre-selecting possible responders/nonresponders to anti-PD1/PDL1 therapy for GC, and hopefully as a druggable target for developing new drugs for treating GC. Background

Gastric cancer (GC) is the third most common cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1]. Immunotherapy using monoclonal antibodies targeting immune brakes mediated by immune inhibitory checkpoint molecules (PD1, PDL1, LAG3, TIGIT, etc.) has recently attracted considerable attention, since the significant and long-lasting benefit of anti-PD1 nivolumab therapy in the first-line setting has been demonstrated by 3-year follow-up data from the CheckMate 649 [2] and ATTRACTION-4 [3] phase III trials for AGC. However, such cutting-edge immunotherapies as well as conventional therapy are insufficient to provide the significant benefit of long-term complete remission in many patients with unresectable advanced/recurrent GC or gastro-esophageal junction cancer (abbreviated as AGC) [4].

To pre-select patients who are likely to benefit from anti-PD1/PDL1 therapy, many biomarkers, including tumor mutation burden, microsatellite instability (MSI), combined positive score (CPS) of PDL1 expression in tumor tissues, or the composition ratio, number, and cellular function of immune cells in tumor tissues and peripheral blood, have been identified, and the former related to cancer cells are actually used in clinical practice [5]. However, these biomarkers are not necessarily correlated with clinical outcomes. Indeed, in the WJOG10417GTR study that we previously conducted, no correlation was seen between the baseline CPS-PDL1 and patient prognosis, although the relationship with MSI status was not determined because we did not perform MSI testing considering that it was not important due to the low positivity rate of MSI-high (6.3%) in Japanese, as described before [6]. Thus, there is still a need for establishment of biomarkers that can more accurately predict the therapeutic efficacy. Alternatively, there are many attempts to enhance the anti-PD1/PDL1 therapeutic efficacy by combining with other treatments, and to develop new immunotherapies that are more effective than anti-PD1/PDL1 monotherapeutic efficacy [7]. However, most have failed or are still being evaluated, necessitating the need for further breakthrough strategies for GC.

We previously found that interleukin-33 (IL33), which is an alarmin cytokine that is involved in the pathogenesis of various diseases, including allergies and cancer [8], plays a key role in treatment resistance via tumor polyploidization mechanisms [9]. In the study, we demonstrated that IL33 induction in the nuclei of cancer cells triggers polyploidization, in which giant cells are generated through mis-regulation of the canonical cell cycle without cell division, followed by rapid proliferation of a large number of its progeny cells in response to treatment stress showing hyperproliferation. Soluble IL33 is released from tumor cells, and exogenously promotes tumor progression and metastasis directly and indirectly via expansion of various immunosuppressive and/or pro-inflammatory cells expressing a IL33 receptor ST2, such as type 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s), mast cells (MCs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and regulatory T cells (Tregs). All of these events contribute to the development of intractable cancer. We also demonstrated that anti-IL33 blocking therapy can induce potent anti-tumor effect by abrogating such negative consequences in murine IL33+ tumor models, which are resistant to chemotherapy and anti-PD1 immunotherapy. Thereafter, several other studies have also demonstrated that IL33/ST2 blockade can enhance anti-PD1/PDL1 therapeutic efficacy [10, 11]. These raise the expectation that IL33 and ST2 are potentially promising targets that could contribute to anti-PD1/PDL1 therapy for GC in clinical settings.

Recently, there has been an increasing number of research reports showing a strong connection between IL33 and GC [12]. For example, in epithelial cells of gastric tissues infected with Helicobacter pylori, the expression of IL33 and ST2 are increased, and large amounts of leukocytes infiltrate, causing inflammation, which may contribute to GC development [13]. Indeed, many studies have reported that IL33 and ST2 are highly expressed in GC tissues, and that tumor IL33/ST2 plays an important role in acquisition and augmentation of malignant properties, such as high proliferation [14], invasiveness [15], chemoresistance [16], and cancer stemness [17], in GC cells. Robust progression and metastasis of GC requires external support from various ST2+ immune cells, including ILC2s [18, 19] and MCs [20, 21], which consequently expand Tregs and MDSCs. In addition to these basic research studies, there have also been many clinical reports. For example, serum IL33 concentrations in GC patients are correlated with depth of invasion, distant metastasis, advanced stage (stage III/IV), and patient survival [22, 23], and IL33 expression in GC tissues is significantly associated with poor prognosis [24]. However, it remains to be concluded that IL33 is a villain molecule in GC pathogenesis due to conflicting results [25]. Furthermore, the relationship between IL33/ST2 levels in GC patients and the outcome of anti-PD1/PDL1 therapy remains unclear in clinical practice.

In this study, to clarify whether IL33/ST2 has a high potential that can contribute to anti-PD1/PDL1 therapy for GC, we analyzed tumor tissues and peripheral blood from AGC patients before and after nivolumab monotherapy in the WJOG10417GTR study that we previously conducted [6] for IL33 and ST2+ cells utilizing RNA-sequencing, ELISA, and flow cytometry. Then, we statistically analyzed the relationship between their levels and patient prognosis.

Methods

Patients

The WJOG10417GTR study (UMIN000032686) was conducted in 10 hospitals according to the protocol (August 2018–November 2020) approved by the IRB of each participating hospital, including the National Cancer Center (No. 2017-473). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients before study enrollment. Ninety-six AGC patients (male × 67, female × 29; median age 69) received intravenous infusions of anti-PD1 monoclonal antibody nivolumab at 3 mg/kg or 240 mg/body every 2 weeks until disease progression or unacceptable toxicity. The details of the WJOG10417GTR study, including eligibility, tumor responses, and toxicities, were previously reported [6]. All activities were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the Japanese Clinical Research Ethics Guidelines.

Sample preparation

EDTA-added peripheral blood and endoscopic biopsy tumor tissues were collected from the patients before and after nivolumab monotherapy. The post-treatment timing corresponded to within approximately one month (after 21 days or more [22 – 43 days] from the first drug administration) when the immune status was considered to become stable after treatment initiation, as described before [6]. It was impossible to collect blood from 12 patients and tumors from 24 patients due to poor condition after treatment. After centrifugation of peripheral blood, whole cells in the pellet were used as peripheral blood cells (PBCs) for flow cytometric analysis following treatment with ACK Lysing Buffer (ThermoFisher), and plasma was used for ELISA. Tumor tissues were treated with RNAprotect Tissue Reagent (Qiagen) for RNA stabilization, and total RNA was extracted from the tumor tissues using a RNeasy kit (QIAGEN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions followed by quality check.

RNA-sequencing

Only high-quality RNA available at both time points (n = 39) was used to prepare the RNA libraries using a TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep (Illumina). The RNA libraries were sequenced using Illumina HiSeq2000 Sequencing System (RRID:SCR_020130; Illumina), generating standard 150 bp paired-end data. Mapping and expression level quantification were performed using a STAR-2.7.8a (RRID:SCR_005622; GNU Guix). We used a transcript per million (TPM) value for the clinical analysis as the gene expression level, since the sum of all TPM values is the same for all samples, allowing TPM values to be compared between samples in principle [26].

Immunostaining

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections of AGC patients (n = 84) were stained with immunofluorescence-conjugated anti-human IL33-AF488 mAb (R&D #IC36253G), anti-human ST2-PE mAb (R&D #FAB5231P), or the isotype controls according to standard methods. The immunofluorescence intensity observed at 200 × magnification was automatically measured as a pixel count using a TCS SP8 Laser Scanning Confocal Microscope (Leica), and the average count of three different fields was used as data. The mean pixel in the isotype control were 1.17 (median 1.30) for AF488, and 0.77 (0.81) for PE. The mean pixel count were 8.32 (4.41) for IL33-AF488, and 2.09 (1.60) for ST2-PE.

ELISA

IL33 in plasma (n = 84) was measured using an ELISA kit (BioLegend #435,907) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Flow cytometric analysis

After Fc blocking, PBCs were stained with the following immunofluorescence-conjugated antibodies: Anti-CD45-APC-Cy7 (BioLegend #304,014), anti-ST2-PE (eBioscience #12–9335-82), anti-IL17RB-APC (R&D #FAB1207A-100), anti-GATA3-BV711 (BD #565,449), anti-CD117-BV650 (BioLegend #313,222), anti-FceRIa-FITC (BioLegend #334,608), anti-CD11b-BV510 (BioLegend), anti-CD68-AF700 (Novus Biologicals #NB100-683AF700)anti-HLA-DR-BV605 (BioLegend #307,640), anti-PDL1-BV785 (BioLegend), anti-CD3-BUV496 (BD #612,940), anti-CD4-PerCP-Cy5.5 (BioLegend #317,428), anti-CTLA4-PE-Cy7 (BioLegend #369,614), anti-FOXP3-BV421 (BioLegend #320,124), and the appropriate isotype control. Data were acquired using a BD LSR Fortessa X-20 cytometer (BD), and were analyzed by FlowJo software (BD). Before defining specific molecular expressions, debris was firstly excluded by FSC/SSC followed by gating CD45+ leukocytes, and immunofluorescence intensity was compared to the isotype control (Supplementary Fig. S1). Data were shown as the number of specific cells per ml of peripheral blood. Patients with CD45+ leukocytes < 10% were considered ineligible for the integrated analysis, and were therefore excluded.

Statistical analysis

Significant differences (P value < 0.05) were statistically evaluated by the unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test or the Mann–Whitney test using GraphPad Prism 7 software (MDF), SAS software version 9.4, and R version 4.2.3. Progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. Hazard ratios (HRs) were obtained by univariable and multivariable models adjusting potential confounding factors (PS, prior gastrectomy, number of metastatic organs, and ALP), and 95% confidence intervals were calculated. Cutoff values were determined by change point of log HRs using the Cox regression models with penalized splines, and we used the cutoff values with the larger HRs calculated through careful analysis of each marker based on the method for modeling continuous-scale covariates as described elsewhere [27], while avoiding obtaining outlier cutoff values as described before [6].

Results

IL33 is significantly increased in tumor tissues and peripheral blood of AGC patients with progressive disease after nivolumab therapy

We analyzed il33 gene expression in tumor tissues by RNA-sequencing. The reason for choosing il33 gene expression for the main analysis is that post-treatment tumor tissue was not available for immunostaining (IHC), meaning that comparison of pre- and post-treatment data was not possible, and that quantification of IL33 protein expression levels in tumor tissues by IHC is difficult due to the diverse expression patterns of IL33, intracellularly, extracellularly, and in the nucleus [28]. Firstly, the levels were compared among three groups based on treatment responses, although there was a large imbalance in sample sizes because of very few responders: one showed progressive diseases (PD) within one month after treatment (early PD group, n = 11), one showed PD more than 1 month after treatment (late PD group, n = 23), and one did not show PD at least during this study (nonPD group, n = 4). The one-month period was chosen because it was considered an immunological cutoff point, at which the immune system agitated by treatment may subside in the patient’s body. Tumor il33 expression levels both before and after treatment tended to be higher in early/late PD groups compared with nonPD group, and increased further after treatment in approximately half of the patients in all groups, although no significant differences were seen between pre- and post-treatment levels (Fig. 1A).

We analyzed IL33 protein expression by IHC using the only pre-treatment tumor tissue sections available to us. We assessed the expression levels using an automated quantification method because it provides highly reproducible quantitative data by eliminating visual bias, which can be subjective and lead to variability and inconsistency in results, especially when analyzing large numbers of samples [29], although it does not provide detailed information about the localization of cellular expression that pathologists can visually assess. Although there was no correlation between the levels of IL33 gene expression and protein expression, the protein expression levels were also higher in early PD group (n = 34) and late PD group (n = 47) as compared to those in nonPD group (n = 3; Supplementary Fig. S2A). However, the significant difference was seen only between late PD group and nonPD group (P = 0.018; Supplementary Fig. S2A).

In contrast, no significant differences were seen among three groups (early PD group, n = 31; late PD group, n = 48; and nonPD group, n = 5) for plasma IL33 at either time point (Fig. 1B). There was no correlation between the levels of tumor il33 gene expression and plasma IL33 either before or after treatment (Supplementary Fig. S2A). These results suggest that IL33 in the local tumor site rather than in peripheral circulation appears to negatively affect anti-PD1 therapeutic efficacy in AGC. However, it should be noted that the small sample size of the nonPD group limits the statistical reliability and generalizability of the results to clinical settings even if statistical significance was obtained, and caution is needed in interpreting the results, considering potential biases and uncertainties.

High il33 gene expression levels in tumors at post-treatment are significantly associated with poor prognosis of AGC patients receiving nivolumab therapy

Indeed, statistical analysis of the relationship between these levels and patient prognosis revealed that patients with high il33 expression levels in tumors at post-treatment showed significantly shorter PFS (median PFS [mPFS] = 1.68 months [95% confidence interval (CI) = 0.95–NA] versus 3.01 months [95% CI = 1.97–5.42], hazard ratio [HR] = 3.539, P = 0.002) and shorter OS (mOS = 4.02 months [95% CI = 2.56–NA] versus 13.73 months [95% CI = 7.20–NA], HR = 2.659, P = 0.014), as compared to patients with low levels (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table S1). These suggest that high post-treatment il33 expression levels in tumors are a significant poor prognostic factor in nivolumab therapy for AGC. High il33 expression levels in tumors were also significantly associated with several patient characteristics, but not established biomarkers such as CPS-PDL1: high pre-treatment levels with HER2 positivity (P = 0.021), and high post-treatment levels with male sex (P = 0.022), liver metastasis (P = 0.040), and peritoneal metastasis (P = 0.012; Supplementary Table S2). As liver metastasis [30] and peritoneal metastasis [31] have been reported as significant risk factors associated with treatment resistance and poor prognosis in GC, it is expected that high post-treatment il33 expression levels in tumors may serve as a surrogate marker for these events.

In contrast, IHC data showed that patients with high baseline IL33 protein expression levels in tumors had significantly longer OS as compared to patients with low levels (7.16 months versus 3.81 months, HR = 0.461, P = 0.033; Supplementary Fig. S2B). As well, patients with high plasma IL33 levels at baseline showed a tendency to have longer OS (HR = 0.560, P = 0.051), although no significant differences were seen at either time point (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Table S1). The exact reasons for the opposing results are unclear. We will discuss it in more detail later.

ST2+ cells are significantly increased in peripheral blood of AGC patients with progressive disease after nivolumab therapy

Next, we examined ST2+ immune cells. In the RNA-sequencing of tumor tissues, immune-related signals, including ST2, were extremely low, and the CIBERSORT analysis, which enables immune profiling, showed significant differences only at baseline dendritic cell levels between nonPD and early/late PD groups (P < 0.05; Supplementary Fig. S3). We also analyzed ST2+ immune cells in tumor tissues by IHC using pre-treatment tumor sections, because flow cytometric analysis of tumor-infiltrating immune cells for ST2+ cells was impossible due to the low number of cells harvested. The IHC data showed a tendency for increased ST2 expression (corresponding to the number of ST2+ cells) in tumors of early/late PD groups as compared to nonPD group, and the ST2 expression was significantly correlated with IL33 expression, suggesting a possible causal relationship between them (P = 0.005; Supplementary Fig. S2A). Patients with high ST2 expression levels in tumors had significantly shorter PFS as compared to patients with low levels (1.64 months versus 2.55 months, HR = 1.758, P = 0.018; Supplementary Fig. S2B). This suggests that intratumoral ST2 is a risk factor for tumor progression in nivolumab therapy for AGC.

We then analyzed PBCs by flow cytometry for ST2+ cells focusing on four types of cell populations (IL17RB+GATA3+ ILC2s, CD117+FceRIa+ MCs, CD11b+HLA-DR−PDL1+ MDSCs, and CD3+CD4+FOXP3+ Tregs), which have been reported to promote cancer progression resulting in anti-PD1 resistance in mouse tumor models [9]. All ST2+ cell subsets both before and after treatment were lower in nonPD group (n = 3) as compared to those of early PD group (n = 31) and late PD groups (n = 46; Fig. 3). ST2+ ILC2s increased after treatment in more than half of patients in all groups, and the ST2+ ILC2 levels at both time points were significantly higher in early/late PD groups than those in nonPD group (P < 0.001; Fig. 3A). ST2+ MCs also increased after treatment in more than half of patients in all groups, and the post-treatment ST2+ MC levels (P ≤ 0.001) were significantly higher in early/late PD groups than those in nonPD group (Fig. 3B). ST2+ MDSCs were few in nonPD group at both time points, while the baseline ST2+ MDSC levels were higher in early/late PD groups, and increased further after treatment showing a significant difference from nonPD group (P = 0.006; Fig. 3C). ST2+ Tregs increased after treatment in more than half of patients in all groups, and the post-treatment levels were significantly higher in early/late PD groups than those in nonPD group (P ≤ 0.001; Fig. 3D). No significant differences were seen between pre- and post-treatment levels for any of the cell populations (P > 0.1). These results suggest that the ST2+ cells tested in peripheral circulation appear to negatively affect anti-PD1 therapeutic efficacy in AGC.

High ST2+ cell levels in peripheral blood are significantly associated with poor prognosis of AGC patients receiving nivolumab therapy

Indeed, ST2+ cell levels, excluding Treg subset levels, in peripheral blood both before and after treatment were significantly associated with poor prognosis (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table S1). Particularly, as compared to patients with low levels, patients with high baseline ST2+ ILC2 levels showed significantly shorter PFS (1.64 months [95% CI = 1.41–2.00] versus 3.10 months [95% CI = 1.38–5.32], HR = 2.002, P = 0.010) and shorter OS (5.49 months [95% CI = 4.60–8.28] versus 11.68 months [95% CI = 5.42–NA], HR = 1.891, P = 0.035). Patients with high baseline ST2+ MDSC levels also showed significantly shorter PFS (1.58 months [95% CI = 1.28–1.97] versus 3.25 months [95% CI = 2.00–5.52], HR = 1.977, P = 0.009) and shorter OS (5.59 months [95% CI = 4.60–8.28] versus 12.35 months [95% CI = 5.03–NA], HR = 2.206, P = 0.015), although the post-treatment levels also showed a significant impact on PFS (HR = 2.186, P = 0.016) and OS (HR = 2.453, P = 0.010). After treatment, ST2+ MC levels showed a much greater impact on patient prognosis than other ST2+ subset levels: patients with high post-treatment ST2+ MC levels showed significantly shorter PFS (1.97 months [95% CI = 1.64–3.02] versus 2.61 months [95% CI = 1.87–5.39], HR = 1.959, P = 0.008) and shorter OS (7.20 months [95% CI = 5.49–10.22] versus 15.31 months [95% CI = 5.42–NA], HR = 2.110, P = 0.019), and patients with low post-treatment ST2+ MC levels showed longest OS as compared to those of patients with low levels of other ST2+ subsets, although the baseline levels also showed a significant impact on PFS (HR = 1.638, P = 0.027) and OS (HR = 1.772, P = 0.024). This suggests that among these ST2+ subsets, the MC subset is the highest risk factor for nivolumab therapy for AGC. High baseline levels of two ST2+ subsets were significantly associated with several patient characteristics, but not established biomarkers such as CPS-PDL1: high ST2+ ILC2 levels with bone metastasis (P = 0.003; Supplementary Table S3), and high ST2+ Treg levels with male sex (P = 0.022) and peritoneal metastasis (P = 0.044; Supplementary Table S4).

AGC patients with low il33/ST2 levels are nivolumab-responders with long-term survival

Combining tumor il33 expression levels and ST2+ cell levels at post-treatment allowed us to more clearly define responders and nonresponders to nivolumab therapy. As compared to low levels of both, high post-treatment il33/ILC2 levels showed HR = 10.81 for PFS (P = 0.002) and HR = 6.95 for OS (P = 0.025), high post-treatment il33/Treg levels showed HR = 17.84 for PFS (P = 0.015) and HR = 10.36 for OS (P = 0.043), and high post-treatment il33/MC levels showed a greater impact on patient prognosis (HR = 31.47, P = 0.003, mPFS = 1.68 months versus NA, mOS = 6.44 months versus NA) as compared to patients with low levels of both, who were four nonPD patients with long-term survival (Fig. 5, Supplementary Fig. S4, Table S5). This suggests that high levels of both tumor IL33 and peripheral ST2+ MC subset after treatment are useful as a biomarker to define AGC responders/nonresponders to nivolumab therapy. Collectively, the IL33/ST2 axis may be useful as a biomarker to predict the anti-PD1 therapeutic efficacy in GC, and may be a promising target that can contribute to optimizing anti-PD1/PDL1 therapy for GC.

Discussion

This study revealed that high tumor il33 gene expression levels, but not tumor/peripheral IL33 protein levels, after treatment, and high levels of peripheral ST2+ ILC/MC/MDSC subsets at both time points significantly associated with poor prognosis in nivolumab monotherapy for AGC. Patients with low post-treatment levels of both tumor il33 gene expression and peripheral ST2+ MC subset were long-term survivors without PD. These suggest that high post-treatment il33/MC levels are a significant poor prognostic factor for nivolumab therapy for AGC. The IL33-ST2 axis may be useful as a biomarker to predict anti-PD1/PDL1 therapeutic effect and select possible anti-PD1/PDL1 responders/nonresponders in GC. Hopefully, it could be a promising target molecule for developing new anti-cancer drugs as an alternative or follow-up treatment to anti-PD1/PDL1 therapy for AGC. Strategies targeting the IL33-ST2 axis are expected to contribute greatly to improving clinical outcomes in the treatment of GC.

We previously identified IL33 expressed in tumor cells as a determinant of treatment resistance by showing that tumor-derived IL33 can induce not only tumor polyploidization but also abnormal immunity by expanding immunosuppressive and proinflammatory ST2+ cells, albeit in mouse melanoma models [9]. Indeed, in the present study, IL33+ giant tumor cells with multi-nuclei were frequently observed in tumor tissues from PD patients but not from non-PD patients (Supplementary Fig. S2A). In GC, IL33 in peripheral blood has been reported as a poor prognostic factor [22, 23]. In our study, however, high baseline levels of IL33 protein in plasma as well as tumor tissues were associated with favorable prognosis of AGC patients receiving nivolumab therapy, whereas high baseline levels of il33 gene expression in tumor tissues tended to be associated with poor prognosis, with high post-treatment levels showing significance. The precise reason for the opposing results is unclear; however, it is speculated that the main factors may be the difference in analytical methods, and the discrepancy between gene and protein expression. IL33 is expressed throughout tumor tissues, including in the nuclei, intracellularly, and extracellularly of tumor cells, making it extremely difficult to accurately quantify its staining levels by conventional IHC evaluated by visual inspection of a pathologist, particularly its nuclear expression. In this meaning, gene expression analysis, which can detect tumor-derived IL33 expression throughout the tumor tissue, may be a better method than IHC or ELISA, even though protein expression is more important than gene expression when considering physiological functions. On the other hand, in cancer cells, post-transcriptional regulation and various post-translational modifications, such as phosphorylation and methylation, are known to affect protein activity and function, and in some cases, despite high gene expression, the protein function is not activated after expression (32). IL33 is normally present in the nucleus within cells, and is released extracellularly upon cell damage or cell death. The IL33 is converted to its active form when cleaved by caspase-1, whereas it loses its biological activity and therefore immunological roles when cleaved by caspase-3 or caspase-7 during the apoptotic process [33]. This suggests that IL33 released from apoptotic cells is nonfunctional. Therefore, the significant association between high levels of IL33 protein expression analyzed by IHC and ELISA and favorable prognosis in patients may be due to the fact that the IL33 protein is an inactive form that is likely produced by cancer cells that have undergone apoptosis, possibly due to attack by anti-tumor immunity. It is not possible to determine which type of IL33 was observed in this study, because the ELISA kit we used detects all forms of IL33, including inactive and active forms, and the information of the antibody used for immunostaining is not disclosed. Therefore, to strengthen the conclusion that high IL33 gene expression level in tumors is a significant poor prognostic factor in anti-PD1 therapy for GC, further studies evaluating protein expression, its activation status, and/or caspases simultaneously are needed, thereby clarifying the reasons for the opposite results between gene and protein expression analyses. Another possible reason may be that AGC patients who experienced IL33-induced immunosuppression via immune checkpoint molecules [9] responded well to anti-PD1 therapy, even though the nuclear IL33 could not be interfered by this treatment.

We previously demonstrated that IL33+ tumor cells recruit and expand immunosuppressive and proinflammatory ST2+ cells, especially ILC2s, in mouse tumor models [9]. Indeed, in the present study, ILC2s were significantly associated with poor patient prognosis. ILC2s are known to play an important role in expulsion reactions of infected parasites and allergic diseases [34]. However, the role of ILC2s in cancer is still controversial [35]. In GC, ILC2s have been reported as tumor supporters: for example, Helicobacter pylori infection activates ILC2s through the induction of IDO+TSLP+ macrophages, causing gastric tissue damage that may lead to GC [36]. Another study also reported that administration of an inflammation inducer L635 induces accumulation of ILC2s at the damage site in the gastric tissues, but not seen in those of IL33-knockout mice, suggesting requirement of IL33 for the ILC2-induced gastric damage that may lead to GC [18]. Clinical studies also demonstrated that abundant ILC2s are observed in peripheral blood of GC patients [37], and that coexistence of ILC2s and tuft cells in tumor tissues from GC patients is significantly correlated with poor patient prognosis [38]. In our study, however, MCs rather than ILC2s had a greater impact on patient prognosis, at least in nivolumab monotherapy for AGC. This may be probably due to differences in cancer types among studies. MCs rather than other ST2+ cell populations may be more deeply involved in GC progression, since it has been reported that MCs promote GC progression and metastasis, and the high levels are significantly associated with poor prognosis of GC patients [12]. Combination of post-treatment levels of tumor il33 and peripheral MCs allowed us to define responders who survived long-term without PD, and non-responders who did not derive significant benefit, suggesting a useful biomarker for predicting anti-PD1 therapeutic efficacy in GC.

We previously demonstrated that anti-IL33 blocking therapy significantly induces potent anti-tumor effect in anti-PD1-resistant tumor models, and several other studies also demonstrated that IL33/ST2 blockade can enhance anti-PD1/PDL1 therapeutic efficacy [10, 11]. IL33 has been attracting attention recently as a therapeutic target not only for allergies but also for various cancers, including GC and other gastrointestinal cancers [39]. The present study also revealed that IL33 is a high-risk factor for nivolumab therapy for AGC, suggesting the clinical significance of targeting IL33 in anti-PD1/PDL1 therapy for GC. Therefore, the IL33-ST2 axis may have great potential not only as a biomarker, but also as a target for developing new anti-cancer drugs.

However, this study has several limitations. The usefulness of each factor for selecting the optimal treatment could not be evaluated due to the single-arm treatment setting. The lack of comparators precludes drawing precise conclusions, thereby making patient stratification difficult. The results of the nonPD group have inherent limitations, particularly in survival models, since they may contain the potential biases and uncertainties arising from the small sample size even if statistical significances were obtained. Therefore, larger studies with more patient samples are needed to draw more precise conclusions about the applicability of the results to clinical practice. Accumulating evidence of the significance of IL33/ST2 in anti-PD1 therapy for GC will contribute greatly to improving clinical outcomes in the treatment of GC. The cutoff values ​​determined by visual assessment of the continuous trends and change points of the log hazard ratios may have overestimated the effect of each factor. We did not examine the expression of IL33 and ST2 in tumor tissues at post-treatment, which may have had some effects on patient prognosis as those in peripheral blood. All these points suggest that validation cohorts and/or multicenter prospective studies are needed to solidify the predictive value of IL33/ST2 in the treatment of GC, although combination regimens with other drugs are necessary because nivolumab monotherapy is no longer possible in GC treatment.

Conclusions

This study provides significant clinical evidence suggesting that the IL33-ST2 axis is a significant risk factor for anti-PD1/PDL1 therapy for AGC. Targeting the IL33-ST2 axis may be useful in developing diagnostic biomarkers to select right patients who are likely to benefit from anti-PD1/PDL1 therapy. However, this is just one part of a broader landscape of biomarkers being investigated in GC. The integration of IL33-ST2 with the established biomarkers, including HER2, CPS-PDL1, and MSI [40], must be important to enhance the precision of prognostic assessments and treatment strategies in the anti-PD1/PDL1 therapy for GC. We believe that our study will make a significant contribution to improving the clinical outcomes of anti-PD1/PDL1 therapy for GC, and this may also have a positive impact on the treatment of other types of cancer.
