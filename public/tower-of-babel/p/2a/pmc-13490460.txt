Emergent piezoelectricity as the driving force for mechanoluminescence in crystals of atomically precise copper nanoclusters
Authors: Hao Li, Yuping Chen, Ying Xu, Yanming Liu, Qing Tang, Xi Kang, Manzhou Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMechanoluminescence (ML) is a phenomenon of force-response self-luminescence, which was observed long ago. However, the underlying mechanism of ML remains ambiguous due to the complexity of light-emitting processes. Atomically precise metal nanoclusters are expected to serve as ideal model systems for unraveling ML mechanisms in specific nano-systems. Herein, we report the observation of mechanoluminescent crystals of metal nanoclusters. Cu7 clusters, which crystallize in a non-centrosymmetric trigonal space group, exhibit bright green ML when subjected to mechanical stress. Through piezoelectric experiments and theoretical calculations, we demonstrate that piezoelectricity arising from mechanical deformation of the crystal leads to an asymmetric distribution of internal dipoles within the crystal. Furthermore, crystal fracture under external force induces charge separation. Such alterations modulate the transition dipole moments and induce localized electronic transitions through strain-induced charge asymmetry, thus triggering stress-dependent luminescence. The findings provide insights into the mechanism of ML in a nanocluster system at the atomic level.Subject terms: Structural properties, Structural properties

Introduction

It has been over 400 years since the 17th century when Francis Bacon first recorded the phenomenon of “mechanoluminescence”1. ML is a unique luminescent response that occurs when materials are subjected to mechanical actions2–5. Although several ML materials have been used as stress sensors6–11, the underlying mechanisms of ML remain unclear due to the complex structural changes and processes that materials undergo under external forces4. Various mechanisms have been proposed to explain ML7,12–14, with the most widely recognized one involving charge accumulation caused by the piezoelectric effect in crystals15. However, a uniform ML mechanism has yet to be established, mainly because of the imprecise structures, low luminescent intensities, and harsh conditions under which these ML materials operate4,16. To achieve an atomic-level understanding of ML in organic-inorganic luminescent compounds, it is essential to utilize precise molecular entities as models.

Metal nanoclusters, with their uniform sizes and well-characterized structures, serve as a platform for investigating the mechanisms of luminescence at the atomic level17–19. To date, photoluminescence (PL) and electrochemiluminescence (ECL) have been thoroughly studied due to their promising applications20–25. Numerous efforts have been made to clarify the mechanisms behind these two types of luminescence. Some research has identified PL changes in nanoclusters caused by mechanical stimulation, including mechanochromic properties and pressure-induced PL enhancement26–31. However, the direct induction of luminescence by mechanical force has remained uncharted territory in metal nanocluster research although the structural precision of nanoclusters could be conducive to understanding the ML mechanisms. It has been shown that crystalline materials with non-centrosymmetric space groups hold potential for exhibiting ML32,33. Unfortunately, most stable metal nanoclusters possess symmetric molecular structures and belong to centrosymmetric space groups, making the discovery of ML in cluster-based nanomaterials a challenge. To advance understanding of the ML mechanism, developing clusters with structures in non-centrosymmetric crystalline space groups may be beneficial and could enhance their applications in mechanically stimulated luminescence.

In this work, we report the observation of mechanoluminescent crystals in nanoclusters, using the Cu7(p-MBT)7(PPh3)4 (abbreviated as Cu7; p-MBT = 4-methylbenzenethiol and PPh3 = triphenylphosphine) cluster as a model (Fig. 1). A thermochromic luminescent Cu7 is synthesized, which crystallizes in a non-centrosymmetric trigonal R3c space group. Notably, the crystals of the Cu7 nanocluster exhibit ML when subjected to mechanical force. Both ML and PL are observed at wavelengths of 530 nm at 290 K and 560 nm at 77 K, indicating a unified electronic transition pathway for emission. Experiments and theoretical calculations are conducted to investigate the ML mechanism in the Cu7 cluster, which is elucidated in detail: crystal deformation under mechanical stress induces piezoelectricity, leading to an intrinsic dipole asymmetry. Lattice slippage of the cluster crystal further modulates transition dipole moments and triggers localized electronic transitions via strain-induced charge asymmetry, thereby triggering stress-dependent luminescence.

Results

Structure and optical property

The synthesis and crystallization of the Cu7 nanocluster are detailed in the Methods section. The complete structure of Cu7 was determined through single-crystal X-ray diffraction (Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Data 1 and 2). Structurally, the overall framework of Cu7 is non-centrosymmetric, exhibiting a C3 axis of symmetry (Fig. 2a–d)34,35. A trinuclear Cu3 with strong Cu-Cu interactions (average bond length: 2.67 Å, Supplementary Table 2) at the waist position could be regarded as the core of the cluster (Fig. 2a). The four Cu1P1 subunits (Fig. 2b) are connected to the Cu3 core via three μ2-, one μ3-, and three μ4-thiol ligands (Fig. 2c, d). Both the three-cornered Cu3 and the higher-coordinating sulfur atoms contribute to the overall rigidity of the cluster framework. Besides, the non-centrosymmetric nature of the Cu7 framework induces a similar non-centrosymmetric arrangement within the trigonal R3c crystallographic space group (Fig. 2e). The composition of Cu7 was confirmed by electrospray ionization mass spectrometry, with a mass signal of 2418.99 g mol−1 that matches well with the theoretical chemical formula of [Cu7(p-MBT)7(PPh3)4 + Cu+]+ (calculated: 2418.98 g mol−1; Supplementary Fig. 1).

The photophysical properties of the Cu7 cluster were investigated at room temperature. In a CH2Cl2 solution, Cu7 showed no emission (Supplementary Fig. 2). However, when in a crystalline or amorphous state, the cluster exhibited notable luminescence, indicating potential aggregation-induced emission behavior. Therefore, we focused primarily on investigating the photophysical properties of Cu7 in its crystalline state. The UV-vis spectrum of Cu7 crystal shows a broad absorption band at the wavelength of 250-420 nm (Supplementary Fig. 3). The PL spectrum revealed a maximum emission wavelength at 530 nm under the 440 nm excitation at room temperature (Fig. 2f). The absolute PL quantum yield (PL QY, ΦPL) for the Cu7 crystal was measured at 0.28 (Supplementary Fig. 4), with an average emission lifetime (τav) of 2.12 μs (Fig. 2h, Supplementary Fig. 5a, and Supplementary Table 3). The emission lifetime on the microsecond scale suggested that Cu7 exhibits phosphorescent emission behavior. The high PL QY of the Cu7 crystal can be attributed to its rigid molecular structure and the stable form it adopts in the crystalline state. This rigidity enhances the charge transfer of the excited-state electrons and facilitates energy release through radiative transitions, leading to high PL intensity. Additionally, the microsecond scale of the emission lifetime indicated that the PL nature of the Cu7 cluster is primarily phosphorescence. The radiative decay rate (kr = ΦPL/τav) and non-radiative decay rate (knr = 1/τav - kr) of Cu7 in the crystal state were identified: kr = 1.32 × 105 s−1, knr = 3.4 × 105 s−1.

The temperature-dependent PL of the Cu7 cluster crystal was recorded. As shown in Fig. 2g, the Cu7 crystal demonstrated distinct thermochromic luminescence, exhibiting green luminescence at 290 K while yellow luminescence at 80 K. As the temperature decreased from 290 to 80 K, the maximum PL intensity wavelength at 530 nm experienced a threefold enhancement and shifted to 560 nm (Fig. 2g). Additionally, the emission lifetime of the Cu7 crystal increased from 2.12 μs (green dots in Fig. 2h, Supplementary Fig. 5a) to 82.01 μs (blue dots in Fig. 2h, Supplementary Fig. 5b) as the temperature decreased. Time-dependent density-functional theory (TD-DFT) calculations reveal triplet charge-transfer (³CT) excited-state behaviors in Cu7 (Supplementary Fig. 6). The singlet (S1) and triplet (T1) charge-transfer excited states are degenerate, with an energy difference (ΔEST) of 0 eV, sharing identical excitation energies of ΔE(S1 − S0) = ΔE(T1 − S0) = 1.5914 eV. Given the microsecond-level PL lifetime, the PL of the Cu7 nanocluster was mainly attributed to the 3CT transitions.

ML property

Interestingly, the Cu7 cluster crystal not only exhibited noticeable PL but also displayed obvious ML (Fig. 3a). Specifically, when we applied pressure to the Cu7 crystals sandwiched between two glass plates at room temperature, a bright green emission—indicative of stress-responsive luminescence—was observed, confirming its ML characteristic (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Movie 1). Notably, the ML spectrum recorded at room temperature matched the PL spectrum in terms of the emission wavelength (Fig. 3c). Additionally, we recorded the thermochromic ML of the Cu7 crystals, which aligned well with their thermochromic PL characteristics (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Movie 2). Importantly, as evidenced by PL (Fig. 3e), laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM, Fig. 3f), and X-ray diffraction (XRD, Fig. 3g and Supplementary Fig. 7), the crystalline structure and the optical performance of Cu7 remained intact after mechanical treatment. The well-overlapping XRD patterns between the experimental and simulated results demonstrated the crystal and microcrystal states of Cu7 before and after mechanical force treatment. The attenuated XRD signal from the Cu7 microcrystals compared to its larger crystal is attributed to a decrease in crystallinity. The composition of the microcrystals remained consistent with that observed previously for their ML properties, as confirmed by ESI-MS (Supplementary Fig. 8), XPS (Supplementary Fig. 9), and unit cell parameters (Supplementary Fig. 10). These results confirmed the structural maintenance of Cu7 clusters after the ML process.

The microcrystals can be further broken by a glass rod, and the ML spectrum was successfully recorded, although the ML intensity is weaker than that obtained by the whole crystals (Supplementary Fig. 11). This might be attributed to the fact that, while glass-rod scratching can break microcrystals, they prove to be more difficult to break compared to the intact crystal. Furthermore, we incorporated Cu7 crystal powder into a PMMA film. However, no discernible ML was observed from the cluster-based PMMA film upon bending (Supplementary Fig. 12a). This might differ from the ML of other materials that arise from elastic deformation. In addition, we also tested the Cu7 crystal film samples under sustained loading under different conditions (duration of times: 10 s). When cyclic loads (10 N) are repeatedly applied to the same position on the film sample, the ML intensity gradually decreases with increasing test number (Supplementary Fig. 12b). This might be attributed to the reduction in the number of effectively fractured crystals under identical loading conditions at the same position. When loads are applied to different fresh film samples, the experimental results show that a small applied force (<≈ 5 N) cannot trigger fracture-induced ML of the crystals. When the applied force exceeds approximately 15 N, the ML intensity remains nearly unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 12c). This phenomenon may be attributed to the constant number of effective fracture-active crystals within the unit loading area. These results might be attributed to the ML of the Cu7 cluster being instantaneously excited by crystal fracture, rather than continuously by elastic deformation. These results highlight the ML nature of this nanocluster.

In addition, we collected the ML spectra of Cu7 cluster crystals immersed in dimethyl silicone oil to rule out gas discharge. The result demonstrates successful collection of ML spectra (Supplementary Fig. 13). To avoid tribo-induced thermoluminescence emission from Cu7 crystals, we also recorded temperature-dependent luminescence spectra of Cu7 cluster crystals at high temperatures in the absence of excitation light. The result indicated that the ML of Cu7 was not attributed to thermoluminescence induced by friction (Supplementary Fig. 14). As demonstrated by these ML/PL comparisons, the ML and PL of the Cu7 nanocluster likely follow a similar electron transition pathway from the excited state to the ground state due to the same emission wavelength. To further understand the ML mechanism of the Cu7 cluster, we examined its excitation process in detail.

ML mechanism

To date, several mechanisms have been proposed to interpret ML in specific cases. However, a thorough investigation of ML remains challenging since the excitation process of ML is of short duration, while complicated. Some studies ascribe that intermolecular reactions, such as C-H···π interactions, were influential for ML of organic molecule crystals13,36–38. Nevertheless, the C-H···π interactions are scarce in the crystal lattice, and the dominant intermolecular interactions are H-H interactions (Supplementary Fig. 15). The weak C-H···π intermolecular forces in Cu7 appear to be a disadvantage for ML, as the reduced rigidity of its molecular structure could enhance the non-radiative pathway. It is recognized that uneven distribution of electric charge can occur when materials are deformed under mechanical stress, exhibiting piezoelectricity in materials with non-centrosymmetric space groups. Here, the piezoelectric coefficient (d33) of the Cu7 cluster crystal was investigated to shed light on its ML mechanism. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 16 and detailed in Supplementary Table 4, the piezoelectric coefficient d33 of the Cu7 cluster crystals was determined to be ≈ 2.4 pC N−1. Although this value is relatively low compared to those of other piezoelectric materials39–41, it still supports the existence of piezoelectricity in Cu7 crystals42–44. The piezoelectric coefficient of Cu7 crystals was comparable to that of some other ML materials45,46. Nanoindentation tests revealed that the Cu7 crystals exhibit relatively low hardness. The load-displacement curve (Supplementary Fig. 17, Supplementary Table 5.) shows the creep behavior under external mechanical forces rather than the elastic deformation of the Cu7 crystal. Therefore, irreversible deformation occurs in Cu7 crystals under low loads, which triggers lattice slip. It increases the distance between molecular dipoles, thereby inducing charge separation47. In addition, we use single-crystal X-ray diffraction to establish the relationship between the macroscopic crystal morphology and the microscopic unit cell of the Cu7 cluster crystal. Compared with the state before the crystal undergoes fracture, the length along the direction of the crystallographic c-axis is markedly shortened (Supplementary Fig. 18). These results suggested that the fracture of the Cu7 cluster crystal mainly occurs via the truncation of the crystallographic c-axis.

The piezoelectricity in Cu7 crystals indicates a nonuniform distribution of electric charge under mechanical forces. To explore the relationship between ML and piezoelectricity in Cu7 crystals, we conducted TD-DFT calculations. The pristine Cu7 cluster exhibits a ground-state dipole moment of 17.24 Debye, predominantly oriented along the crystallographic c-axis (17.03 Debye, Fig. 4a, b), which aligns with its non-centrosymmetric lattice symmetry. Upon lattice slippage (Cu7-p), the dipole moment increases to 19.95 Debye (Fig. 4e, f), reflecting stress-induced charge asymmetry amplification via atomic displacement and electron density redistribution. Importantly, the c-axis direction polarization remains dominant (>90% contribution).

Furthermore, TD-DFT calculations reveal a four-fold enhancement in oscillator strength for Cu7-p (0.0322) compared to the pristine Cu7 cluster (0.0085; Fig. 4b, f), directly correlating with the experimentally observed intensification of ML at 540-550 nm. We performed a quantitative analysis of the transition dipole moment relative to the crystallographic c-axis based on TD-DFT results. For the pristine Cu7 cluster, the calculated transition dipole moment vector is μ = (0.18, 0.14, −0.32) Debye, which makes an angle of θ ≈ 35.5° with the c-axis. For the stressed Cu7-p, the transition dipole moment vector evolves to μ = (−0.03, 0.08, −0.76) Debye, with the angle sharply decreasing to θ ≈ 6.7°. This angular shift of ≈ 29° indicates that mechanical stress drives a pronounced reorientation of the transition dipole moment, resulting in an almost parallel alignment with the polar c-axis. Such stress-induced alignment optimizes the radiative transition probability within the non-centrosymmetric cluster framework. We carried out the piezo-response force microscopy (PFM) to verify the “reorientation of transition dipoles”. In the PFM test, the sample of Cu7 microcrystal simultaneously exhibited a piezoelectric response butterfly curve and a phase hysteresis loop (Supplementary Fig. 19), suggesting that the material has obvious piezoelectricity. The butterfly curve reflects the piezoelectric strain response driven by an external electric field. And the phase hysteresis supports the existence of spontaneous polarization reversal inside the material, which might correspond to the reorientation of molecules or dipoles at the microscopic scale under the action of an electric field. The above results collectively demonstrate the piezoelectricity of the Cu7 microcrystal, as evidenced by both macroscopic electrical response and microscopic structural evolution. Notably, the excitation energy remains nearly invariant (≈ 545 nm; Fig. 4d), indicating that piezoelectric effects modulate transition probabilities rather than bandgap energy. Specifically, Kohn–Sham orbital analysis further elucidates that pristine Cu7 supports delocalized multi-orbital transitions (e.g., H → L + 26/L + 24; Fig. 4c, g), characteristic of weak charge transfer. In contrast, Cu7-p undergoes a localized H-1 → L + 18 transition (49.5% contribution), indicative of directional donor-acceptor interactions. Electron localization function (ELF) maps confirm heightened charge confinement at these orbitals (Fig. 4h), a hallmark of piezoelectric strain-induced lattice distortion.

TD-DFT calculations reveal distinct triplet charge-transfer (³CT) excited-state behaviors in Cu7-p compared to pristine Cu7. Mechanical force breaks the degeneracy for the excited state in Cu7-p (Supplementary Fig. 20). The T1 (³CT) state stabilizes relative to S1 (¹CT), resulting in a positive singlet-triplet gap of ΔEST = 0.0025 eV. The emissive T1 (³CT) state exhibits a lower transition of energy (ΔE(T1 → S0) = 1.7137 eV vs. ΔE(S1 → S0) = 1.7162 eV). This stabilization of T1 (³CT) in Cu7-p establishes an efficient pathway for triplet-mediated ML. These results establish that mechanical stress amplifies the intrinsic dipole asymmetry of Cu7’s non-centrosymmetric lattice, generating localized electronic transitions derived by piezoelectric potential. The alignment of piezoelectric polarization with the optical field direction enhances radiative efficiency, thereby exciting stress-dependent luminescence.

Comparing the excitation processes of ML and PL is indeed crucial for understanding the underlying physics of the Cu7 nanocluster. While the indistinguishable emission spectra for both ML and PL confirm a shared radiative recombination center, the excitation dynamics diverge significantly. Unlike PL, which relies on extrinsic photon absorption, ML is excited by the charge transfer derived by an intrinsic piezoelectric potential between uneven distribution of dipoles. Our TD-DFT calculations reveal that structural slippage in Cu7-p amplifies the ground-state dipole moment, establishing a localized internal electric field essential for charge separation. Mechanical stress reconfigures the electronic landscape, increasing the oscillator strength four-fold by shifting delocalized multi-orbital transitions toward highly localized donor-acceptor interactions. Crucially, the force-induced symmetry breaking lifts the excited-state degeneracy observed in the pristine cluster. The resulting stabilization of the triplet charge-transfer state (T1) relative to the singlet state (S1) creates a positive gap, establishing a thermodynamically favored T1 → S0 pathway that is activated via piezoelectric modulation. Collectively, based on the atomically precise structure of the Cu7 nanocluster and theoretical calculations, we were able to clearly elucidate its ML mechanism. When influenced by external forces, the piezoelectricity and dipole-dipole interactions in the non-centrosymmetric crystal lattice of the Cu7 cluster lead to the uneven distribution of dipoles. The charge separation caused by crystal fracturing, in turn, prompted energy transfer within the Cu7 crystal lattice. Ultimately, the ML process of Cu7 was initiated by the electronic transition from the T1 to the S0 state, arising from an unevenly distributed electric field induced by piezoelectricity under mechanical force treatment (Fig. 5).

In contrast, the other similar-sized copper clusters with centrosymmetric space groups, such as Cu848, Cu1048, and Cu1149, show ML silence under the same conditions as the Cu7 cluster (Supplementary Fig. 21). These results indicate that the piezoelectric effect of the Cu7 crystal serves as an indispensable component that enables real-time energy conversion during ML generation. In the absence of piezoelectricity and the resultant stress-induced charge asymmetry, ML would fail to occur in Cu clusters. These results also imply the key role of non-centrosymmetric crystal systems in such ML-active cluster materials.

Discussion

The current core bottlenecks in traditional mechanoluminescence research include the difficulty of accurately analyzing the luminescence mechanisms of traditional materials and the lack of molecular design strategies grounded in clear structure-property relationships. Nanoclusters possess optical properties such as high photoluminescence quantum yield, tunable emission wavelengths, and well-defined structure-property relationships, making them promising for in-depth exploration of the intrinsic mechanisms of cluster-based ML materials. This can not only advance the development of fundamental ML theories but also deepen the understanding of the photophysical properties of nanocluster aggregates. This work reports the observation of mechanoluminescent crystals in atomically precise metal nanoclusters. Distinct from previous works focusing on pressure-induced changes in the photoluminescence of metal nanoclusters, mechanoluminescence is a real-time energy-conversion process that converts mechanical energy into light. We take advantage of the advantages of atomically precise nanoclusters to address the challenges in traditional mechanoluminescence studies, such as ambiguous structures and unclear mechanisms. The mechanoluminescence of the nanocluster crystal under mechanical force is triggered by the emergence of piezoelectricity, as demonstrated through experimental detection of the piezoelectric coefficient and theoretical analysis. Such piezoelectric potential further modulates transition dipole moments, and trigger localized electronic transitions via strain-induced charge asymmetry, thereby triggering stress-dependent luminescence.

In conclusion, this study establishes atomically precise metal nanoclusters as a platform for atomic-scale mechanoluminescence investigations, thereby addressing critical ambiguities in its mechanism. In addition to advancing fundamental understanding, the demonstrated stability and tunability of nanocluster-based mechanoluminescent systems present promising opportunities for applications. However, it should be acknowledged that the ML of Cu7 clusters is an irreversible process that depends on crystal fragmentation. This hinders most of the applications of similar materials compared to reversible elastico-ML materials. Conversely, this material with irreversible ML from Cu7 clusters still shows promise for specific single-use applications, such as disposable stress indicators, visualization of stress distribution, and anti-counterfeiting labels, in the future. Future research would be directed toward enhancing the piezoelectric properties of nanoclusters and exploring mechanoluminescent phenomena in a wider range of nanocluster compositions, further expanding the functional scope of these emerging materials.

Methods

Reagents

All reagents are purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used directly without further purification: cupric acetate monohydrate [(CH3COO)2Cu·H2O, 99.0%, metal basis], p-toluenethiol (C7H7S, p-MBT, 98%), triphenylphosphine (C18H15P, PPh3, 99%), dichloromethane (CH2Cl2, HPLC grade), methanol (CH3OH, HPLC grade).

Synthesis of the Cu7(p-MBT)7(PPh3)4 clusters

The preparation was carried out at room temperature. Specifically, (CH3COO)2Cu·H2O (0.2 mmol, 40 mg) was dissolved in 5 mL of methanol and 10 mL of CH2Cl2 and stirred at 1200 rpm. Then, PPh3 (0.2 mmol, 50 mg) was added. After 30 min, the solution turned colorless, indicating the formation of Cu-PPh3 complexes. Then, p-methylbenzenethiol (0.48 mmol, 60 mg) was added. After 1 h, the Cu7(p-MBT)7(PPh3)4 cluster was formed. Finally, 10 mL of methanol was added to the solution, and the yellow crystal of Cu7(p-MBT)7(PPh3)4 was obtained after the solvent evaporation.

Single-crystal X-ray diffraction

The data collection for single-crystal X-ray diffraction of Cu7 cluster was carried out on a Stoe Stadivari diffractometer under nitrogen flow using graphite-monochromatized Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54186 Å). Using Olex250, the structure was solved with the ShelXT51 structure solution program using Intrinsic Phasing and refined with the ShelXL52 refinement package using Least Squares minimization. All the non-hydrogen atoms were found directly. All the non-hydrogen atoms were refined anisotropically. All the hydrogen atoms were set in geometrically calculated positions and refined isotropically using a riding model.

ESI-MS spectrometry

Electrospray-ionization mass spectrometry was performed on a Waters XEVO G2-XS QTof mass spectrometer. The samples are dissolved in a mixture solution of CH2Cl2/CH3OH (v/v = 1:1), which is directly infused into the chamber at 10 µL min−1 in positive mode.

PL spectroscopy

PL spectra, absolute quantum yields, and emission lifetime were measured on a Horiba FluoroMax Plus spectrophotometer.

UV–vis spectroscopy

UV–vis absorption spectra in the solid state were carried out on a Shimadzu 3600-plus spectrophotometer with an integrating sphere.

ML spectroscopy

ML spectra were recorded with an Ocean Optics QE65 Pro high-sensitivity spectrometer. The optical signal was collected via an optical fiber signal transmission line equipped with a fiber-coupled lens. The integration time was set to 10 s. Before collecting ML signals, dark-field scanning was performed to subtract the ambient background signal. The contact force was measured on an ATI Nano17 device.

CLS microscopy

Confocal laser scanning microscopy images were recorded on a Leica TCS SP8 DIVE.

XRD

Powder XRD patterns were obtained on a SmartLab 9KW with Cu Kα radiation.

Piezoelectricity

The piezoelectric coefficient (d33) was measured multiple times on a quasi-static piezoelectric meter ZJ 3AN (Institute of Acoustics CAS, China). The Cu7 crystal powder samples were pressed into circular pellets with a diameter of ≈ 10 mm and a thickness of ≈ 1 mm, and both sides of the pellets were coated with conductive silver paste. The samples were treated using the direct current (DC) electric-field polarization method at room temperature, with a 3 kV voltage and a 60 min polarization duration. The final d33 value was determined as the average of repeated measurements.

Nanoindentation

Nanoindentation tests were performed on a KLA iNANO instrument equipped with a Berkovich indenter. The maximum load was 50 mN. The Oliver-Pharr method was used to calculate the hardness and elastic modulus of the samples. All data were automatically corrected for thermal drift and calibrated for the indenter area function.

TD-DFT calculations

For optical-absorption spectrum and orbital analyses, DFT calculations were performed using the CP2K package53. The pristine Cu7 cluster was modeled within a cubic supercell (35×35×35 Å3). To simulate the piezoelectric response under mechanical stress during crystal fracture, we constructed the slipped Cu7-p model by applying controlled displacements along the polar c-axis and a-axis to mimic inter-cluster distortion, and embedded the deformed cluster within an expanded 50 × 50 × 50 Å3 supercell, followed by full geometry optimization to ensure energetic stability. The electronic structure calculations were computed using the spin-polarized Perdew−Burke−Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within a mixed double-ζ Gaussian and plane-wave (GPW) basis sets54, with an energy cutoff of 450 Ry. Core electrons were described by Goedecker−Teter−Hutter (GTH) pseudopotentials with 11, 6, 5, 4 and 1 valence electrons for Cu, S, P, C and H, respectively. Subsequently, TD-DFT calculations for the optical absorption spectrum were carried out at the PBE level with the DZVP-MOLOPT-SR-GTH basis sets. Based on the above results, the Multiwfn program55 was employed to efficiently process and visualize the UV-Vis spectra, molecular orbital distributions, and ELF maps.

Piezoresponse force microscopy

PFM characterizations were carried out on a Bruker Dimension Icon atomic force microscope. A conductive SCM-PIC-V2 probe was used in the out-of-plane PFM mode. PFM images were recorded with a resolution of 256×256 pixels at a scanning rate of 0.5 Hz.

Statistics and reproducibility

The experiments for LSCM (Fig. 3f) and piezoelectric coefficient (Supplementary Fig. 16 and Supplementary Table 4) were repeated independently at least 3 times with similar results. No statistical method was used to pre-determine sample size. No data was excluded from the analysis.
