Liquid-Crystalline Photonic Sandwich: Electroresponsive Colloids of Clay Nanosheets Loading Photofunctional Dyes
Authors: Teruyuki Nakato, Tsuyoshi Watanabe, Takumi Harada, Mahito Shintaku, Emiko Mouri, Seiji Tani, Yasutaka Suzuki, Hirokatsu Miyata, Josef Breu, Jun Kawamata
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractColloidal clay nanosheets obtained by the delamination of layered crystals of smectite-type clay minerals in water form liquid crystals because of their shape anisotropy. Loading of organic dyes onto the liquid crystalline clay nanosheets will enable novel photonic materials, where photofunctions of the loaded dye are controlled by the liquid crystallinity of the clay nanosheets. However, adsorption of organic dyes onto the nanosheets renders the nanosheet surfaces hydrophobic, and consequently, colloidal stability of the nanosheets is lost. In this study, this drawback is overcome by sandwiching cationic stilbazolium dyes between a pair of synthetic fluorohectorite nanosheets. This is realized by the preparation of stilbazolium–clay second-stage intercalation compounds characterized by intercalation of dye cations into every other interlayer space of the hectorite clay, where nonintercalated interlayer spaces are occupied by Na+ ions. The second-stage intercalation compounds are obtained by partial ion exchange of mother clay mineral incorporating Na+ ions in all of the interlayer spaces and delaminated from the Na+-containing interlayer spaces to form clay nanosheets sandwiching the dye molecules. Aqueous colloids of the dye-sandwiching clay nanosheets form colloidal liquid crystals, and the dye-sandwiching liquid crystalline clay nanosheets respond to an applied AC electric field to be aligned parallel to the electric field. The assembled structure of the dye-sandwiching clay nanosheets under the electric field is characterized by aligned discrete clay platelets, which is somewhat different from that of a colloidal liquid crystal of clay nanosheets without dye loading characterized by macroscopic liquid crystalline domains up to submillimeters. The electric alignment of the clay nanosheets induces alteration of light absorption of the sandwiched stilbazolium molecules, which verifies a strategy of constructing stimuli-responsive photonic materials of clay–organic hybrids.

Introduction

Photonic soft matter that possesses specific photofunctions together with soft structures is an important category of novel advanced materials. This type of material allows on-site tuning of photofunctions by inducing structural changes. Typical natural examples are switchable structural colors of chameleon skins and squid bodies.1 As artificial materials, various photomechanical systems have been developed as photochromic, photomechanical, and photoactuating systems.2−4 Because light absorption and luminescence of organic dyes are sensitive to microenvironments of their matrixes, switching of photonic properties of dyes has been realized by employing stimuli-responsive matrixes.5−7 In these materials, organic moieties are employed as flexible parts to ensure their structural softness. However, organic components lack robustness, which limits their lifetime as well as their use under harsh conditions. Therefore, inorganic-based photofunctional soft matter has been awaited with the development of inorganic flexible components.

Inorganic-based soft matter using clay nanosheets is promising for constructing such novel photonic materials. Smectite-type clay minerals are layered aluminosilicates, which are earth-abundant environmentally friendly oxides.8 Their layered structure consists of the negatively charged aluminosilicate layers and the exchangeable interlayer cations (usually Na+ ions), leading to spontaneous delamination in water to form aqueous colloids of the delaminated clay nanosheets.9 Adsorption of photofunctional organic cations by the clay nanosheets through electrostatic attraction enables development of novel photonic materials.10,11 Thus, we can develop a variety of nanostructured photonic materials by using clay nanosheets adsorbed by photofunctional cations as building blocks. Because of the specific microenvironments provided by the clay nanosheet surfaces, the properties of the adsorbed guest cations are somewhat altered. Nevertheless, their photofunctions are basically preserved upon adsorption in most cases because the clay nanosheets are physicochemically stable and optically transparent. The metallic species involved in the aluminosilicate layers adopt stable oxidation states leading to bandgaps typically larger than 5 eV, and therefore they do not greatly deteriorate the structure and electronic state of the guest cations. Ordered films of clay nanosheets can realize anisotropy of photofunctions by the intercalated molecules.12−14

On the other hand, the delamination of clay mineral crystals yields colloidal liquid crystals due to the high shape anisotropy of the delaminated clay nanosheets. This enables the colloidal clay nanosheets to dynamically adopt ordered, yet flexible photonic soft matter structures. Nanosheet liquid crystals were found for clay colloids in the early 20th century15,16 and subsequently extended to various types of nanosheets such as layered metal phosphates,17,18 niobates and titanates,19−22 graphene and graphene oxide (GO),23−26 and MXene.27 In the liquid crystalline state, the colloidal nanosheets are organized into superstructures with the retention of their mobility in the colloids. Liquid crystalline nanosheets show collective motion due to the particle–particle interactions, which allows changing the alignment of the nanosheets with the application of external fields such as the electric field28−31 and optical pressure.32 Collective motion of the liquid crystalline nanosheets and their alignment under the external forces result in the formation of macroscopic (∼mm scale) colloidal structures in nanosheet liquid crystals.33,34 For example, hierarchical nanosheet organization up to macroscopic scales has been reported by us for the niobate nanosheet liquid crystals, which are prepared by exfoliation of potassium hexaniobate (K4Nb6O17) in an aquatic system.35,36 In this system, a variety of organized structures such as netlike, stuffed, and stripe structures are obtained by controlling both the liquid crystalline domain growth and conditions of the applied electric field.

The characteristic properties of clay minerals and liquid crystallinity of colloidal nanosheets prompted us to prepare colloidal liquid crystals of clay nanosheets that have been loaded with photofunctional molecules as a prototype of clay-based photofunctional soft matter. Photochemical and/or photophysical properties of the guest molecules matrixed by the colloidal clay nanosheets are expected to be switched with the alignment of nanosheets under an external field. So far, such an attempt has not been reported because the adsorption of organic molecules often changes the surface properties of the clay nanosheets to be hydrophobic, which in turn destabilizes their colloidally dispersed state. In the present study, this drawback was overcome by sandwiching the guest organic dye molecules between two clay nanosheets, which enables retention of the hydrophilicity of the clay surfaces exposed to the solution. Although a conventional method of loading a dye on clay is adsorption of the dye onto the clay nanosheets, delaminated prior to the loading results in adsorption of the dye molecules on both sides of the nanosheets. In contrast to this, employment of the second-stage intercalation compound incorporating the guest molecules in every other interlayer space enables the formation of bilayer-type clay nanosheets sandwiching dye molecules between a pair of clay silicate layers. We prepared the bilayer-type clay nanosheets of synthetic fluorohectorite clay (FHT) sandwiching cationic stilbazolium dye according to the method of Breu and co-workers.37 The dye-loaded clay nanosheets showed colloidal liquid crystallinity and electric alignment, which enabled switching of the light absorption by the alignment change of the nanosheets.

Experimental Section

Sample Preparation

Single-crystalline FHT was synthesized at the University of Bayreuth by melt synthesis followed by long-term annealing.38,39 Its expected composition is Na0.5[(Mg2.5Li0.5)Si4O10F2], and the cation exchange capacity (CEC) for this composition is 130 mequiv per 100 g of clay.38 Cationic stilbazolium dyes, 4-[3,4,5-(trimethoxy)styryl]-1-alkylpyridinium bromides (alkyl = tetradecyl, hexadecyl, and octadecyl), were synthesized according to a previous report.37 The dyes are written as CnSt+ (C14St+, C16St+, and C18St+) hereafter.

For the preparation of the bilayer-type FHT nanosheets sandwiching CnSt+ dyes, a second-stage intercalation compound, or ordered interstratified phase,37,40 of FHT with a CnSt+ dye was prepared by stirring roughly ground clay crystals in an ethanol–water mixed solvent at room temperature for 24 h. The amount of dye added to FHT was 50% CEC of the clay. The dye-intercalated sample was washed with the mixed solvent and dried under ambient conditions. Then, the second-stage intercalation compound was dispersed in water and stirred for 24 h at room temperature to obtain a colloid sample of dye-sandwiched clay nanosheets. We write these samples as “CnSt+-sandwiching FHT”.

For comparison, a colloid sample of FHT nanosheets without loading dyes was prepared by stirring roughly ground FHT single crystals in water. This sample is denoted as a “bare FHT” nanosheets. The average lateral diameter of the FHT nanosheets was 20 μm. Loading of C16St+ cations onto FHT nanosheets by a conventional procedure was also carried out by adding a dye solution to the colloid sample of bare FHT nanosheets followed by stirring at room temperature. By this procedure, the dye cations are adsorbed onto the external surfaces of the FHT nanosheets. We write this sample as “ordinary adsorbed C16St+–FHT”.

Characterizations

Powder XRD patterns of Na+-FHT and CnSt+-intercalated FHT were measured with monochromatic Cu Kα radiation using a Rigaku Ultima-IV diffractometer. Bare and CnSt+-sandwiching FHT nanosheets were directly observed by low-voltage high-resolution SEM (Zeiss GeminiSEM 560). Prior to the observation, a small portion of a clay colloid sample was drop-cast on a piece of silicon wafer and dried under ambient conditions. Then, the cast sample was observed with an accelerating voltage of 1–2 kV without coating.

Liquid crystallinity of the colloid samples was evaluated qualitatively by their birefringence. A small portion of a sample was poured into a flat capillary (Vitrocom) and placed upright between the crossed polarizers. Its side view was recorded with a conventional camera and a Keyence VHX-2000 optical microscope.

Electric response of the colloidal FHT nanosheets was examined by using a colloid sample placed in a thin layer cell.29,35 The cell was made of a pair of ITO-coated glass plates (∼1 mm thickness), between which a film spacer was slipped and sealed with epoxy resin.29 The cell thickness was equal to that of the spacer—100 μm for the electric alignment observations and 250 μm for the spectroscopic measurements—and the cell surface was about 1 cm2. Application of an AC electric field was further conducted by using an NF BP4610 bipolar generator. The applied AC voltage was 10 V (peak to peak), i.e., 1000 V cm–1 for the 100 μm cell and 50 kHz in the direction perpendicular to the cell surface. The cell was kept horizontally during the AC voltage application. Optical microscope images were recorded on an Olympus BX-51 microscope equipped with a high-resolution monochrome digital CMOS camera (ORCA-Flash 4.0 V3, Hamamatsu Photonics). Colloid samples in the thin-layer cell were subjected to the measurements of visible absorption spectra (Shimadzu UV-2450) under application of the AC voltage.

Results and Discussion

Formation of CnSt+-Sandwiching FHT Nanosheets

The CnSt+-sandwiching FHT nanosheets are obtained by delamination of the second-stage dye–clay intercalation compounds. The dye molecules are intercalated in every other interlayer space of the layered structure of the starting clay mineral, and the delamination occurs in the unintercalated (saturated with Na+ ions) interlayer space. Figure 1 shows the XRD patterns of the C14St+–, C16St+–, and C18St+–FHT intercalation compounds. Each sample exhibits an intense peak accompanied by higher-order peaks characteristic to a layered structure. Segregation to intercalated and nonintercalated domains41 is not observed, supporting the formation of the second-stage intercalation compounds.37 The basal spacings are determined to be 3.68, 3.84, and 3.84 nm for the C14St+–, C16St+–, and C18St+–FHT intercalation compounds. Since the basal spacing of bare FHT is 1.52 nm, corresponding to two layers of water molecules hydrated to the interlayer Na+ ions, the height of dye-intercalated interlayer spaces is estimated as 1.2–1.4 nm. Estimation details and schematic model of the structure are indicated in the Supporting Information. This estimation suggests that the alkyl chains of the intercalated CnSt+ cations are almost parallel to the layer surfaces.

Powder XRD patterns of (a) Na+-FHT and (b) C14St+–FHT, (c) C16St+–FHT, and (d) C14St+–FHT intercalation compounds.

Spectroscopic properties of the sandwiched CnSt+ cations are not deteriorated compared to those in a homogeneous solution. Figure S2 shows UV–visible absorption spectra of homogeneous solutions of CnSt+ cations and colloidal dispersions of CnSt+-sandwiching FHT nanosheets. Their spectral shapes are almost the same. The position of the CnSt+ solutions is 382 nm while that of the colloidal CnSt+-sandwiching FHT nanosheets is 380 nm, independent of the alkyl chain length. Thus, specific dye–dye intermolecular interactions are almost negligible in the interlayer spaces of FHT.

The sandwich structure is directly confirmed by SEM Figures 2 and S3 show SEM images of the bare and CnSt+-sandwich FHT nanosheets. The image of bare FHT nanosheets exhibits flat thin platy particle diameters of several micrometers, indicating delamination of the clay crystals into single nanosheets (Figure 2a and Figures S3a,b). In contrast, the images of CnSt+-sandwiching FHT nanosheets indicate the bilayer nature of platy particles (Figures 2b–d). This is typically evidenced by the sheet edges, where the edges of the upper and lower clay sheets are not perfectly aligned, but one sheet edge is partly chipped during the delamination (Figures S3c,d). Because the CnSt+-sandwiching nanosheets have a three-layer structure, where the edge structure is different from the dye-sandwiching parts (center) of the nanosheets due to peel-off of small parts of clay layers, electron scattering and secondary electron emission efficiency at the edges are different from those of center. Thus, the edges appear brighter in the secondary electron image. The uniform dark color of the sandwiched parts indicates that the homogeneous incorporation of the CnSt+ ions into the nanospaces between the pair of clay layers. The lateral lengths of the CnSt+-sandwiching FHT nanosheets are a hundred nanometers to micrometers.

SEM images of (a) bare FHT, (b) C14St+-sandwiching FHT, (c) C16St+-sandwiching FHT, and (d) C18St+-sandwiching FHT nanosheets.

Liquid Crystallinity of the CnSt+-Sandwiching FHT Colloidal Nanosheets

Both the bare and CnSt+-sandwiching colloidal FHT nanosheets show liquid crystallinity characteristic of the colloidal dispersions of anisotropic particles. Figure 3 shows photographs of the colloid samples in flat glass capillaries between crossed polarizers. The bare FHT sample (Figure 3a) indicates separation into lower birefringent (bright) and upper nonbirefringent (dark) parts due to biphasic coexistence of the liquid crystalline and isotropic phases in the sample.20,42 The C14St+-, C16St+-, and C18St+-sandwiching FHT nanosheets (Figures 3b–d) also show biphasic coexistense. All of the CnSt+-sandwiching FHT nanosheets settle faster than bare FHT nanosheets. The faster settlement suggests a less viscous nature of the colloids of CnSt+-sandwiching FHT nanosheets than that of bare FHT nanosheets. This would be indicative of reduced hydrophilicity due to the incorporation of the long-chain stilbazolium cations even though the external surfaces exposed to water are still hydrophilic. This leads to differences in the repulsive interactions between bare and CnSt+-sandwich FHT nanosheets.

Photographs of the colloids of (a) bare FHT, (b) C14St+-sandwiching FHT, (c) C16St+-sandwiching FHT, and (d) C18St+-sandwiching FHT nanosheets and (e) ordinary adsorbed C16St+–FHT between crossed polarizers.

In contrast, the ordinary adsorbed C16St+–FHT sample, where the dye cations are present on both external surfaces of the clay nanosheets, show aggregated particles recognizable by the naked eye (Figure 3e). The clay particles are no longer present as nanosheets in this colloid sample due to the surface hydrophobicity given by the C16St+ cations adsorbed on the external surfaces and thus do not show colloidal liquid crystallinity. This result demonstrates significance of the sandwiching strategy for preparing stable aqueous clay colloids loading hydrophobic organic cations.

Optical microscope observations from the horizontal direction of the colloids standing in flat glass capillaries confirm the liquid crystallinity and the biphasic coexistence. Figure 4 shows bright-field (BFM) and polarized (POM) optical microscopy images of the samples. The images of bare FHT nanosheet colloid show the isotropic upper and birefringent lower phases. The lower liquid crystalline phase does not show individual particles but large domains of up to several tens of micrometers with Schlieren texture in the POM image (Figure 4A, right). Such an optical texture is common to many liquid crystalline nanosheet colloids.20−22,25,43,44

BFM and POM images of the colloids of (A) bare FHT, (B) C14St+-sandwiching FHT, (C) C16St+-sandwiching FHT, and (D) C18St+-sandwiching FHT nanosheets. Each sample was in a standing flat glass capillary and observed upper and lower phases from the horizontal direction.

Although the colloid samples of CnSt+-sandwiching FHT nanosheets are also liquid crystalline, their optical texture is somewhat different from that of bare FHT nanosheets, as shown in BFM and POM images of Figures 4B–D. While the POM images of these samples show Schlieren textures due to the liquid crystalline domains, the BFM and POM images indicate many dark short lines, suggesting individual particles. The less viscous nature of the colloids of CnSt+-sandwiching FHT nanosheets (see Figure 3) can limit the growth of large liquid crystalline domains compared with the bare FHT nanosheets because of fast diffusion of the CnSt+-sandwiching FHT nanosheets in the colloids.

Electric Alignment of the Dye-Sandwiching Colloidal Clay Nanosheets

Electric alignment is a typical property of liquid crystalline colloidal nanosheets.29,43 Colloidal bare FHT and CnSt+-sandwiching FHT nanosheets both undergo electric alignment by applying an AC voltage. Figure 5 shows optical microscope images of a bare FHT colloid injected into an ITO thin-layer cell before and after the application of an AC voltage of 10 V (peak to peak) and 50 kHz. Before the application of the electric field, the BFM image (Figure 5A-i) shows platelike particles, and the POM image (Figure 5B-i) is almost dark. They indicate that the bare FHT nanosheets are oriented parallel to the cell–substrate because the sample was injected from the direction parallel to the substrate. In contrast, the sample placed under the electric field for 3 min shows many line-shaped objects in the BFM image (Figure 5A-ii), indicating alignment change of the nanosheets into the orientation perpendicular to the substrate surface. The multiply lined objects span more than 100 μm, indicating the formation of large liquid crystalline domains of aligned bare FHT nanosheets. The POM image (Figure 5B-ii) indicates birefringent objects corresponding to the lined objects in the BFM image, supporting the perpendicular alignment of nanosheets. The BFM and POM images of the sample after the AC voltage application for 60 min (Figures 5A-iii and 5B-iii) are almost the same as those of the application for 3 min, indicating the completion of the electric alignment within a few minutes.

(A) BFM and (B) POM images of a colloid of bare FHT nanosheets (i) before and after application of AC voltage (10 Vp-p, 50 kHz) for (ii) 3 and (iii) 60 min.

Although the CnSt+-sandwiching FHT nanosheets undergo electric alignment, the sample textures after the alignment are different from those of the bare FHT nanosheets. This is related to the difference in the liquid crystalline textures between these colloidal nanosheets. Figure 6 shows optical microscope images of the C16St+-sandwiching FHT nanosheets before and after application of an AC electric field. The BFM and POM images before the application of the electric field (Figures 6A-i and 6B-i) indicate both narrow (like short lines) and broad objects, which are assigned to the edges and faces, respectively, of platelike particles. The fluorescence optical microscope (FOM) image (Figure 6C-i) detects many broad platelike particles. From these images, the C16St+-sandwiching FHT nanosheets are present in a variety of directions, unlike bare FHT nanosheets that are oriented parallel to the substrate. This difference would also be related to the less viscous nature of the colloids of the CnSt+-sandwiching FHT nanosheets. The electric alignment of these nanosheets can be less cooperative than the bare FHT nanosheets because of faster diffusion of the former particles.

(A) BFM, (B) POM, and (C) FOM images of a colloid of C16St+-sandwiching FHT nanosheets (i) before and after application of AC voltage (10 Vp-p, 50 kHz) for (ii) 3 and (iii) 60 min.

The C16St+-sandwiching FHT nanosheets are aligned parallel to the electric field by the application of an AC voltage. The optical microscope images after the AC voltage application for 3 min show many linelike objects corresponding to the edges of the aligned nanosheets (Figures 6A–ii, 6B-ii, and 6C-ii), and the disappearance of broad objects observed in the images before the AC voltage application. Assemblage of the electrically aligned nanosheets forming macroscopic domains is not observed, which is different from the case for the bare FHT nanosheets forming macroscopic domains of electrically aligned nanosheets. The C14St+-sandwiching FHT and C18St+-sandwiching FHT nanosheets also indicate similar electric response, as shown in Figures S4 and S5. The alignment of the CnSt+-sandwiching FHT nanosheets occurs slower than the bare FHT nanosheets because the number of aligned particles in the microscope images increases by continuing the AC voltage application to 60 min (Figures 6A-iii, 6B-iii, and 6C-iii).

These observations demonstrate that the CnSt+-sandwiching FHT nanosheets tend to respond to an AC electric field as individual particles. Thus, the direction of the individually oriented nanosheets is not regulated in the in-plane direction of the substrate, and thus, the nanosheets are not assembled into macroscopic domains. This tendency is different from that of bare FHT nanosheets; they spontaneously form macroscopic liquid crystalline domains, the structure of which is reflected in the electrically aligned state.

Switchable Photofunctions of the CnSt+-Sandwiching FHT Nanosheet Colloids

The liquid crystallinity of CnSt+-sandwiching FHT nanosheet colloids enables electric alignment of the nanosheets, which realizes switching of photonic functions of the sandwiched dye molecules. A typical example is switchable light absorption, which is important for developing smart windows and displays. Therefore, we examined effects of electric alignment on the absorption spectra of the colloids of CnSt+-sandwiching FHT nanosheets. Figure 7A shows the absorption spectra of the CnSt+-sandwiching FHT nanosheets before and after application of an electric field. The colloids of C14St+-, C16St+-, and C18St+-sandwiching FHT nanosheets all exhibit a decrease in the absorbance by the CnSt+ dyes after the application of the electric field (dotted lines) compared with that before the application (solid lines). The absorbance after the electric alignment at the absorption normalized by that before applying the AC voltage is summarized in Figure 7B. The light absorption after the AC voltage application is reduced to 40–50% that without the AC voltage.

(A) Visible absorption spectra of the colloids of C14St+-, C16St+-, and C18St+-sandwiching FHT nanosheets before (solid lines) and after (dotted lines) the AC voltage application for 3 min (10 Vp-p, 50 kHz), and (B) peak intensity of the absorption band after the AC voltage application divided by that before the application.

This value is somewhat different from that obtained by an ideal geometry of the electric alignment, which is represented by the change of φ from 90° to 0°. This difference is rationalized by imperfect alignment of the FHT nanosheets. As seen in the microscope images (Figures 6, S4, and S5), some particles are inclined to the substrate surface before the AC voltage application, and some particles are not aligned perpendicular to the substrate surface after the electric field application. The spectroscopic result reflects the average alignment of the CnSt+-sandwiching FHT nanosheets.

Conclusions

In summary, we have prepared a prototype of inorganic photonic soft matter, where a colloidal liquid crystal of delaminated clay nanosheets works as a soft matrix responsive to external stimuli and organic dyes loaded by the clay nanosheets contribute as photofunctional moieties. Although conventional adsorption of organic dyes onto clay generally destabilizes their colloidal dispersibility, sandwiching the dye molecules by bilayers of clay nanosheets enables retention of their colloidal stability and liquid crystallinity in an aqueous medium. The sandwich structure is realized by the preparation of second-stage intercalation compounds by partial ion exchange of Na+ for the dye cations in mother clay mineral. Light absorption of the sandwiched dye molecules is remotely tuned by the alignment change of the clay bilayers upon the application of an AC electric field. While previously reported photonic soft matter employs organic components as the flexible or movable moieties, the use of an inorganic flexible moiety realized by a colloidal liquid crystal of clay nanosheets provides an important alternative because of robustness and durability of inorganic components. In addition, our strategy of sandwiching functional molecules within clay bilayers provides facile and effective methods of applying clay nanosheets to various advanced materials without deteriorating the surface properties of the clay nanosheets as well as the functions of the guest molecules.
