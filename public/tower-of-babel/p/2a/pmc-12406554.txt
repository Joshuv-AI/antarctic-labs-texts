Sarcopenia and sleep duration with the likelihood of successful aging among older adults in China: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Bo Liang, Dingchun Hou, Jiajia Li, Gong Chen, Lijun Pei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSarcopenia and sleep duration have emerged as crucial health-related factors in geriatric research. This study aimed to investigate the independent and joint associations of sarcopenia and sleep duration with successful aging and its components among older adults in China.MethodsA dynamic 10-year cohort of 6,410 adults aged ≥ 60 was constructed using the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) data (2011–2020). Sarcopenia was identified using the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) 2019 criteria. Sleep duration encompassed nighttime sleep and naps. Cox proportional hazards regression and multivariable logistic regression were used to examine the associations. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using alternative definitions of successful aging and applying generalized linear mixed models.ResultsThe mean age of the participants at baseline was 67.05 ± 6.30 years. Compared to non-sarcopenia adults, those with possible sarcopenia and sarcopenia had a 39% (HR = 0.61, 95%CI: 0.52–0.71) and 45% (HR = 0.55, 95%CI: 0.45–0.67) lower likelihood of successful aging, respectively, with a significant dose-response relationship (P < 0.001). Sleep durations of 6–8 h, 8–9 h and ≥ 9 h increased the likelihood of successful aging by 49% (HR = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.25–1.77), 46% (HR = 1.46, 95% CI: 1.19–1.78) and 48% (HR = 1.48, 95% CI: 1.21–1.91), respectively, compared to < 6 h sleep. Joint analysis revealed that individuals with sarcopenia and short sleep (< 6 h) had the lowest probability of successful aging (HR = 0.38, 95% CI: 0.28–0.52), suggesting a cumulative detrimental effect. Both possible sarcopenia and sarcopenia was associated with decreased likelihood of no major disease, no disability, high cognitive function, and no depressive symptoms in the successful aging. Subgroup analyses showed consistent effects of sarcopenia across demographic groups. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness and reliability of the primary findings.ConclusionsSarcopenia and short sleep duration independently and jointly hinder successful aging. Community sarcopenia screening integrated into primary care and advocating adequate sleep duration may served as a cost-effective successful aging strategy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06360-3.

Introduction

As the global population ages, ensuring quality of life in later years has become a pressing public health priority. Successful aging, a core concept in gerontology [1], is promoted to enhance life quality among older adults. Although there are various definitions and measures [2], a widely used one encompasses a low probability of diseases and disability, robust cognitive function, and active engagement with life [3]. However, recent studies have shown that the prevalence of successful aging in China remains low, typically below 15% [4, 5].

Adults aged 50 years above typically experience a loss of muscle mass at a rate of 1–2% annually [6]. Sarcopenia, an age-related disease affecting skeletal muscles, leads to a decline in both muscle mass and strength. It is closely linked to declines in physical performance, increased morbidity and mortality, and reduced independence in older adults [7, 8]. Furthermore, sarcopenia has been associated with mental health conditions such as depression [9], and cognitive function in older adults [10]. One study pointed that the coexistence of pain and sarcopenia was associated with a lower likelihood with successful aging. Although previous studies have suggested that sarcopenia could be linked to successful aging, there is a lack of evidence from cohort study to indicate the impact of sarcopenia on the likelihood of successful aging.

Sleep problems are another significant concern among older adults, who often experience age-related reductions in sleep duration and higher incidence of sleep disorders [11]. Poor sleep has been proven to be linked to reduced physical, psychological and social functioning [12–14]. Inadequate sleep also increases the risk of cognitive decline and dementia, and decreased social engagement among older adults [15]. A systematic review found that individuals with shorter sleep durations were less likely to age healthily [13]. However, longitudinal studies examining the association between sleep and successful aging remain limited and inconclusive.

Emerging evidence suggests a potential biological overlap between sarcopenia and poor sleep, such as shared pathways involving chronic inflammation and hormonal dysregulation, which may contribute to accelerated functional decline [16–18]. Nevertheless, few studies have examined their combined impact on successful aging using large-scale, longitudinal data. Therefore, this study used the data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to construct a 10-year study, aiming at investigating the independent and combined associations between sarcopenia and sleep duration with successful aging among older adults aged 60 years and above in China. The research aims to provide empirical evidence into how these factors influence the health and well-being of the aging population, therefore promoting successful aging.

Methods

Data sources

The study was based on the CHARLS, which employed a multi-stage stratified sampling technique, focusing on residents aged 45 and above across 28 provinces in China [19]. The survey covers demographic, socioeconomic and health information, including sex, age, marital status, residence, education, job status, smoke, drink, sleep and chronic diseases. Initiated in 2011, CHARLS has conducted follow-up surveys in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020. The research has received the review and approval of the Peking University Ethics Committee (IRB00001052-11015), and all participants have provided their written informed consent. The study selected tracking data from 2011, 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020 to establish cohort database encompassing successful aging, sarcopenia and sleep duration.

Study population

This study constructed a prospective cohort databases with specific inclusion criteria (1) older individuals aged ≥ 60 years who had biomarker data available at baseline in 2011; (2) participants at baseline who were not classified as successful aging; (3) individuals who were involved in the follow-up survey and responded to questions related to successful aging.

Establish a dynamic cohort database, with the adults aged ≥ 60 years who newly entered the cohort in 2013 and 2015 serving as the baseline populations. The baseline individuals were all classified as non-successful aging. The successful aging outcomes of the cohort populations were followed up in 2018 and 2020. Calculate the person-years of follow-up: from the time the respondent entered the cohort, until the occurrence of successful aging, death, loss to follow-up, or the last follow-up in 2020. Participants who had not achieved successful aging by the last follow-up or were lost to follow-up were marked as censored. A total of 6,684 individuals were selected based on the inclusion criteria. After excluding 274 individuals with missing variables, the final valid sample size for analysis was 6,410, accounting for 95.90% of the selected sample. The screening process is shown in Fig. 1.

Definition of successful aging

A universally accepted measurement of successful aging is absent, and the factors contributing to the concept varies in different cultural context. Relying on the definition of Rowe and Kahn [3], the assessment of successful aging in the study includes the following five components: (1) no major diseases; (2) no disability; (3) high cognitive function; (4) no depressive symptoms; and (5) active social engagement. Individuals who fulfill all the above criteria were deemed to be aging successfully. The operational definition for each indicator are as follows:① No major diseases: Previous research indicated that cancer, chronic lung diseases, heart disease, diabetes, and stroke account for the major disease burden among older adults [20]. Participants who reported not having been diagnosed with any of the above five chronic diseases by a doctor were classified as having no major diseases [4]. ② No disability: Disability was derived from the participants’ self-reported difficulties in the basic activities of living (BADL) scale [21]. The BADL scale comprises 6 items: dressing, bathing, eating, getting into/out of bed, using the toilet, and controlling urination and defecation. Each question has a 4-scale answer: “no difficulty”, “have difficulty but can still do it”, “have difficulty and need help”, and “cannot do it”. If a participant reports no difficulty or can perform any activity independently, the participant is considered to have no disability. ③ High cognitive function: The Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status (TICS-10) was used to assess participants’ cognitive function, which included naming the day of the week, month, day, year, and season correctly, performing serial subtraction of seven from 100 (up to five times), recalling ten words on a list, and drawing a picture. Participants who achieved a median or higher score were recognized as having high cognitive function [22]. ④ No depressive symptoms: The ten-item Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale (CES-D 10) was used to evaluate the symptoms of depression among middle-aged and older adults [23, 24]. The total depression score ranges from 0 to 30, with a cut-off score of ≥ 10 indicating the presence of depressive symptoms and a score < 10 suggesting no depressive symptoms. ⑤ Active social engagement: Participants were considered as having active social engagement if they participated in any of the following social activities last month: interacting with friends, voluntary or charity work; providing help to family, friends, or neighbors; going to a sport, social, or other kind of club; taking part in a community-related organizations, and playing Mahjong, chess, and cards, or going to community club, using the Internet, and other social activities [4, 25].

Definition of sarcopenia

The sarcopenia status was assessed using the diagnostic criteria recommended by Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) in 2019, including muscle strength, appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM), and physical performance [26]. Sarcopenia is defined as low muscle mass combined with low muscle strength or low physical performance. Possible sarcopenia is defined as low muscle strength or low physical performance assessed by the repeated chair stand test, but not meeting the criteria for sarcopenia. Those who do not meet the above conditions are classified as the non-sarcopenia group.

Handgrip strength of the dominant and non-dominant hands of the participants was measured using a hand dynamometer, and the maximum value was used in the analysis, with the cut-off points for low grip strength being < 28 kg for male and < 18 kg for female. Physical performance measurements included gait speed, chair stand test (CST), and the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB). Gait speed was measured over a 2.5-meter walk at usual pace; the average of two trials was used. CST involved performing five sit-to-stand movements as quickly as possible with arms crossed over the chest, and the time was recorded. The SPPB included balance tests (side-by-side, semi-tandem, and tandem), gait speed, and CST, with each component scored 0–4, yielding a total score from 0 to 12. Low physical performance is defined as gait speed < 1.0 m/s, time for 5 repetitions of CST ≥ 12s, or SPPB ≤ 9 [26]. Muscle mass was estimated using anthropometric formulas previously validated and applied for ASM [10], and studies have shown a high consistency between ASM obtained from this equation and ASM derived from dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) in the Chinese population [27]. Height was measured using the Seca TM213 stadiometer, and weight was measured using the Omron TM HN-286 scale. Adjusted muscle mass (ASM/height2) was calculated by dividing the ASM value by the square of height, and the criteria for low muscle mass were based on the 20th percentile of age-specific groups in the study population, consistent with previous study lacking DXA or BIA data [28].

Definition of sleep duration

Sleep duration was calculated by combining the duration of night sleep and daytime naps to determine the total daily sleep duration. The American National Sleep Foundation recommends that older adults aged 65 years and above should aim for 7–8 h of sleep per night, while sleep durations of less than 5 h or more than 9 h are not recommended [29]. A previous study has found that self-reported sleep duration is associated with race, with Asians tending to have shorter sleep durations compared with whites [30]. To accommodate population-specific sleep patterns and ensure adequate sample size and statistical power across categories, sleep duration was grouped into: <6 h, 6–8 h, 8–9 h, and 9 or more hours, following classifications used in previous Chinese cohorts.

Covariates

The assessment of covariates included demographic and social-economic characteristics, and lifestyle habits. Socioeconomic demographic characteristics included sex (male and female), age (60–69 years old, and ≥ 70 years old), marital status (single [separated, divorced, widowed, and never married], and married [included cohabitation]), residence (rural and urban), education level (illiterate, elementary school or below, and middle school or above), and job status (unemployed, and employed). Lifestyle habits included smoking currently (no and yes), drinking (no and yes), and body mass index (BMI = weight/height2 (kg/m2), BMI categories were defined according to Chinese standards [31]: underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal (18.5-<24 kg/m2), overweight (24.0-<28 kg/m2), and obesity (≥ 28.0 kg/m2)). Age, BMI, and sleep duration were categorized for analysis. Prior to categorization, their distributions were assessed using descriptive statistics, as shown in Text S1 and Table S1, to ensure interpretability.

Statistical analysis

Use Stata 16.0 software to perform statistical analysis. Participants characteristics of each exposure factor were summarized using descriptive statistics, including frequency counts, rates, and incidence density. The Log-rank test was utilized to examine the differences in exposure factors between the successful aging groups and control groups. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were constructed to visually assess the time to achieving successful aging across different categories of sleep duration and sarcopenia status. Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs) from an equivalent linear regression model. Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to estimate the hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for the independent and joint effects of sarcopenia and sleep duration on the likelihood of successful aging in older adults with adjustment for potential confounding factors. Moreover, multivariable logistic regression analysis was used to estimate the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI), assessing the association between sarcopenia, sleep duration and the five components of successful aging in older adults. Furthermore, subgroup analyses were conducted stratified by sex, age groups, residence and education level.

To evaluate the robustness of the findings, two sensitivity analyses were performed. First, successful aging was redefined as meeting at least four out of five components in the final wave. Although no universal standard exists, prior literature demonstrates considerable heterogeneity in defining successful aging [32, 33]. This more inclusive definition accounts for potential variability in measurements and to reduce the risk of underestimating successful aging due to a single domain falling short. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was employed using this alternative outcome definition. Second, a generalized linear mixed-effects model (GLMM) was employed to examine the longitudinal associations of sleep duration and sarcopenia with successful aging across the 2011 to 2015 waves, accounting for intra-individual correlations over time. These analyses aimed to confirm the consistency of the main results under different outcome definitions and statistical approaches. Statistical significance was set at a two-sided α level of 0.05.

Results

Incidence density of successful aging stratified by baseline exposure

Table 1 shows the distribution of exposure factors in the cohort and the incidence of successful aging. Among the 6,410 participants in the cohort, a total of 40,639 person-years of follow-up were observed. Among them, 960 adults were found to have achieved successful aging, corresponding to an incidence rate of 23.62 per 1,000 person-years. The mean age of the study cohort at baseline was 67.05 ± 6.30 years. Specifically, the mean age of participants exhibiting successful aging was 65.07 ± 4.76 years, while the mean age for those not achieving successful aging was 67.39 ± 6.47 years. The incidence of successful aging was higher among adult men aged 60–69 years, individuals who were married, lived in urban areas, attained higher levels of education, smoked, drank alcohol, had normal or overweight BMI, slept more than 6 h per day, and did not have sarcopenia. Specifically, for sleep duration, the incidence rates of successful aging were 15.47, 29.38, 26.93 and 24.55 per 1,000 person-years for older adults sleeping less than 6 h, 6–8 h, 8–9 h and at least 9 h per day, respectively. Moreover, the incidence rate of successful aging was 40.35 per 1,000 person-years for older adults without sarcopenia, 17.92 per 1,000 person-years for those with possible sarcopenia, and 13.68 per 1,000 person-years for those with sarcopenia.

The Log-rank test revealed statistically significant associations between the likelihood of successful aging and various factors, including sex, age, marital status, residence, education level, smoking, drinking, BMI, sleep duration, and sarcopenia.

The independent and combined effects of sarcopenia and sleep duration on the likelihood of successful aging

As shown in Fig. 2, the Kaplan-Meier curves indicate that older adults with sarcopenia and those with short sleep duration (< 6 h) had a lower cumulative probability of achieving successful aging over time. In contrast, participants without sarcopenia and those with sleep duration over 6 h showed higher probabilities of successful aging. Group differences were both visually evident and statistically significant (log-rank P < 0.001).

No evidence of multicollinearity was observed among covariates, with all VIF values below 2.0. Figure 3 exhibits the independent and combined association between sarcopenia and sleep duration with the likelihood of successful aging, adjusted for confounding factors. For sarcopenia status, compared to older adults aged 60 and above without sarcopenia, individuals with possible sarcopenia (HR = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.52–0.71) and sarcopenia (HR = 0.55, 95% CI: 0.45–0.67) had a lower likelihood of successful aging. There existed a significant dose-response relationship (P < 0.001). Optimal sleep durations of 6–8 h (HR = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.25–1.77), 8–9 h (HR = 1.46, 95% CI: 1.19–1.78), and ≥ 9 h (HR = 1.48, 95% CI: 1.21–1.91) were positively associated with successful aging compared to < 6 h.

The joint effects analysis demonstrated that sarcopenia status and short sleep duration (< 6 h) cumulatively predict reduced likelihood of successful aging. Compared to non-sarcopenia individuals sleeping ≥ 6 h, those with sarcopenia sleeping < 6 h showed the poorest outcomes (HR = 0.38, 95% CI: 0.28–0.52). Notably, the magnitude of sleep deprivation effects was amplified in sarcopenia populations, suggesting that muscle loss may exacerbate vulnerability to insufficient sleep’s detrimental effects. Notably, formal interaction testing between sarcopenia and sleep duration did not yield statistically significant results, and thus interaction terms were not retained in the final models. As such, the reported results reflect descriptive joint associations rather than a statistically confirmed interaction effect.

Association between sarcopenia and sleep duration with the likelihood of the components of successful aging

Table 2 presents the associations between baseline sarcopenia and sleep duration, and the likelihood of achieving each of the five components of successful aging at the final wave of follow-up. Adjusted for confounding factors, sarcopenia status and sleep duration were associated with no major diseases, no disability, high cognitive function and no depressive symptoms, and sarcopenia status was also associated with active social participation.

Compared to older adults without sarcopenia, those with possible sarcopenia had a lower likelihood of no major diseases (OR = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.76–0.98), no disability (OR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.33–0.49), high cognitive function (OR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.57–0.75), no depressive symptom (OR = 0.63, 95% CI: 0.56–0.72) and active social participation (OR = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.74–0.95). Those with sarcopenia also had a decreased likelihood of no disability (OR = 0.44, 95% CI: 0.35–0.56), high cognitive function (OR = 0.50, 95% CI: 0.42–0.59), no depressive symptom (OR = 0.63, 95% CI: 0.54–0.73) and active social participation (OR = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.67–0.90).

Compared to older adults sleeping less than 6 h per day, those sleeping 6-<8 h were associated with a 21% (OR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.06–1.37) higher likelihood of no major diseases, a 50% (OR = 1.50, 95% CI: 1.25–1.80) increased likelihood of no disability, a 21% (OR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.04–1.41) higher likelihood of high cognitive function and a 63% (OR = 1.63, 95% CI: 1.43–1.85) increased likelihood of no depressive symptom. Those sleeping 8–9 h had a 26% (OR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.08–1.46) higher likelihood of no major diseases, a 41% (OR = 1.41, 95% CI: 1.14–1.75) increased likelihood of no disability, and a 82% (OR = 1.82, 95% CI: 1.56–2.12) increased likelihood of no depressive symptom. Those sleeping at least 9 h had a 21% (OR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.04–1.40) higher likelihood of no major diseases, and a 93% (OR = 1.93, 95% CI: 1.67–2.24) increased likelihood of no depressive symptom.

Subgroup analyses of the association between sarcopenia and the likelihood of successful aging by sex, age group, education, and residence were shown in supplementary material.

Sensitivity analysis

When successful aging was redefined as meeting ≥ 4 out of 5 criteria, the associations remained largely consistent. Specifically, both sarcopenia and < 6 h sleep duration continued to be significantly associated with a lower likelihood of achieving successful aging (Figure S2). Furthermore, sarcopenia and short sleep duration exerted additive negative effects on successful aging. These findings suggest that the main results are robust to alternative definitions of the successful aging outcome.

Table S2 showed the longitudinal association between sleep duration, sarcopenia, and successful aging by GLMM. After adjusting for potential confounders, compared with sleep duration of less than 6 h, sleep durations of 6–8, 8–9, and ≥ 9 h were associated with 102% (OR = 2.02, 96% CI: 1.74–2.35), 116% (OR = 2.16, 96% CI: 1.82–2.56), and 47% (OR = 1.47, 96% CI: 1.24–1.74) higher odds of achieving successful aging, respectively. In contrast, compared to older adults without sarcopenia, those with possible sarcopenia and sarcopenia had significantly lower odds of achieving successful aging. The likelihood ratio test comparing the mixed-effects model with a standard logistic regression model indicated a significantly better fit for the mixed model (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\upchi\:}$$\end{document}2=379.89, P < 0.001), supporting the presence of substantial individual-level variability.

Discussion

This cohort study is the first to explore the longitudinal relationship between sarcopenia, sleep duration and the likelihood of successful aging among Chinese older adults aged 60 and above. Over a 10-year follow up, the incidence rate of successful aging was 23.62 per 1,000 person-years. Compared to non-sarcopenia older adults, those with possible sarcopenia and sarcopenia were 39% and 45% less likely to achieve successful aging, respectively, demonstrating a dose-response relationship. Additionally, older adults sleeping 6–7 h and 8–9 h had 49% and 46% higher likelihood, and those sleeping at least 9 h had a 48% higher likelihood of successful aging compared to those sleeping less than 6 h per day. Importantly, the joint effects analysis revealed that coexistence of sarcopenia and sleep duration had additive impacts on successful aging. These findings emphasize the need for integrated strategies addressing both muscle health and sleep hygiene in promoting successful aging. Regrading the five components of successful aging, both possible sarcopenia and sarcopenia were associated with a lower likelihood of no major diseases, no disability, high cognitive function, and no depressive symptoms. Conversely, sleep duration of 6–8 h, 8–9 h and ≥ 9 h were associated with a higher likelihood of these positive outcomes. Sarcopenia was also statistically associated with a decreased odds of active social participation. Subgroup analyses revealed similar impacts of sarcopenia on successful aging across different demographic groups. Sensitivity analysis suggested that the associations between sarcopenia, sleep duration, and successful aging are robust across different definitions and analytical strategies.

A conceptual framework (Figure S3) was developed to illustrate the potential pathways linking sarcopenia and sleep duration to successful aging. It proposes that both sarcopenia and inadequate sleep may contribute to functional decline via shared and distinct biological mechanisms, including systemic inflammation, neuroendocrine dysregulation, and mitochondrial dysfunction. This framework serves to guide interpretation of the findings and suggests plausible pathways through which these conditions may exert synergistic effects on successful aging.

Consistent with prior literature, both sarcopenia and sleep duration were related to the physical and mental functions and well-beings of older adults. Sarcopenia, SPPB performance and walking speed have been identified as predictive factors for the difficulties in daily activities and disability among older adults [34, 35]. A 2-year longitudinal study showed that sarcopenia were associated with a lower likelihood of successful aging, yet possible sarcopenia showed no significant association, potentially due to the short follow-up period [36]. As for the components of successful aging, previous research has indicated that sarcopenia raises the risk of chronic diseases, disability, cognitive decline, depressive symptoms and mortality [9, 10, 36, 37]. While sarcopenia is a risk factor for disability, it primarily reflects age-related loss of skeletal muscle mass and strength. In contrast, disability is defined by limitations in ADLs in the study. Thus, the two constructs are related but conceptually distinct. Furthermore, grip strength were related with chronic illnesses, cognitive performance and depression [38, 39]. Moreover, having conditions like osteoarthritis and sarcopenia does not automatically prevent a person from achieving a successful aging outcome [40]. By taking an active role in their health care and making necessary lifestyle adjustments, individuals can still achieve a successful aging outcome.

A systematic review showed that there was a significant positive cross-sectional association between sleep and healthy aging [13]. Both insufficient sleep duration and poor sleep quality were linked to a reduced probability of aging well. For example, a cross-sectional study among older adults in China found that both shorter and longer nighttime sleep were associated with lower odds of successful aging [22]. In this study, sleep duration of 6–8 h was most consistently associated with successful aging and its core components, particularly cognitive function. Although the association between ≥ 9 h of sleep and cognitive performance did not reach statistical significance, a downward trend was evident. This pattern is consistent with prior studies suggesting that prolonged sleep duration may reflect underlying health conditions and be associated with elevated inflammatory markers [41, 42]. One possible reason for the lack of statistical significance is the small number of participants with cognitive decline in these groups, which may have resulted in limited statistical power. Regarding individual components of successful aging, insufficient sleep less than 6 h was related with chronic diseases, cognitive and physical function, and disability, but no statistical association with active engagement with life was observed. This is consistent with findings from a previous study, and sleep quality rather than quantity may be more predictive of social engagement outcomes [43]. Additionally, the use of self-reported measures for both sleep duration and social participation may introduce recall and reporting biases that obscure true associations [44]. Another cross-sectional study among Australian older adults indicated that self-reported enough sleep was associated with a higher level of healthy aging [45]. However, longitudinal evidence on how sleep affects successful aging over time remains unclear. For instance, a study suggested that good sleep at baseline could lead to healthy aging in the future [46], while another study did not find any link between sleep and aging well [47].

Biologically, sarcopenia impairs mobility, reduces physiological functions, and is linked to metabolic dysregulation [48]. Muscle mass serves as an essential amino acid reservoir, and loss of muscle function undermines resilience to stressors, which is critical for aging well. Sleep deprivation, on the other hand, may activate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, elevate cortisol levels, increase systemic inflammation (e.g., CRP, IL-6), and promote neurodegenerative changes such as hippocampal atrophy and β-amyloid accumulation, all of which are detrimental to cognitive and emotional health [49, 50]. Additionally, poor sleep has been linked to sarcopenia, suggesting a potential bidirectional relationship that further compromises successful aging [51]. Notably, sarcopenia and poor sleep may exert synergistic effects on successful aging through shared biological pathways. Both conditions have been independently associated with systemic inflammation, hormonal dysregulation (e.g., reductions in growth hormone and testosterone), and mitochondrial dysfunction [16–18], which may jointly impair muscle regeneration, cognitive performance, and immune competence. Chronic low-grade inflammation, a hallmark of both sarcopenia and sleep disturbance, accelerates biological aging and contributes to physical and mental decline [16]. Moreover, poor sleep quality may exacerbate muscle catabolism via elevated cortisol levels, while sarcopenia-related fatigue may further disrupt sleep patterns, forming a vicious cycle [52, 53].

Given these interconnections, interventions that address both sarcopenia and sleep quality may be particularly effective. Tailored physical activity programs and behavioral sleep interventions have demonstrated benefits in improving muscle strength and sleep efficiency, respectively [54, 55], and may synergistically support successful aging in older adults.

Strengths and limitations

The study has several strengths. First, this study established a 10-year cohort to explore the independent and joint effects of sarcopenia and sleep duration on the likelihood of successful aging, addressing the limitations of cross-sectional data and providing opportunities for causal inference. Second, while previous studies typically investigated the relationship between sarcopenia, sleep, and specific health outcomes, this study adopted a comprehensive concept of successful aging as the outcome measure. Third, this study differentiates between sarcopenia and possible sarcopenia, enabling a more precise assessment of muscle status. This distinction helps to better comprehend the range of muscle health and its impact on successful aging. Fourth, the study utilized a large, nationally representative sample from China, enhancing the findings’ generalizability.

There are several limitations. First, sarcopenia was assessed using an anthropometric equation to estimate muscle mass, rather than the DXA or bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) recommended by AWGS 2019. The indexation method adopted (ASM/height2) may underestimate the prevalence of sarcopenia among overweight or obese individuals, as it does not account for excess adiposity [56]. Additionally, due to data limitations, low muscle mass was defined as the sex-specific lowest 20th percentile within the analytic sample, which may reduce comparability with studies using AWGS cutoffs. Nevertheless, this approach has been validated for estimating appendicular muscle mass and is widely recognized as a cost-effective alternative to DXA for diagnosing sarcopenia [57, 58]. Second, sleep duration was measured using self-reported questions, lacking objective assessment. While previous research suggested that self-reported sleep duration correlates moderately well with actigraphy measurements, it is acknowledged to be subject to overreporting bias [44, 59]. Third, since medical records were not available in the CHARLS data, the measurement of major diseases was based on self-reported physician diagnoses, which may introduce bias. Future studies could benefit from using objective data sources to mitigate this potential bias. Fourth, cognitive function was defined based on the sample median. While this method has been adopted in prior studies, it may misclassify individuals with lower educational levels. Future studies should consider education-adjusted thresholds or standardized cognitive scores to enhance validity. Fifth, sarcopenia and sleep duration were assessed only at baseline, which may not fully capture their dynamic nature over time. However, a sensitivity analysis using GLMM incorporating repeated outcome measurements was performed to account for intra-individual correlations. Future studies are encouraged to include repeated assessments of key exposures to better reflect their longitudinal changes. Finally, there is possibility that unmeasured factors could confound the relationship between sarcopenia, sleep duration and successful aging. For instance, due to incomplete coverage of physical activity data in the 2011–2015 CHARLS waves, adjustment for its potential influence on the associations between sarcopenia, sleep duration, and successful aging was not feasible. Future studies with more comprehensive physical activity assessments are needed to address this limitation.

Conclusion

This study provide critical longitudinal evidence on the independent and joint associations of sarcopenia and sleep duration with successful aging among older adults aged 60 years and above in China. Sarcopenia significantly reduced the likelihood of achieving successful aging and its key components. Adequate sleep duration (6–9 h/day) independently increased the likelihood of successful aging compared to < 6 h sleep. Moreover, sarcopenia and inadequate sleep had additive negative effects on successful aging. These findings underscore the importance of addressing both muscle health and sleep hygiene as modifiable targets in aging-related interventions. Public health strategies should incorporate routine community-based screening for sarcopenia, followed by timely interventions such as resistance training, nutritional support, and physical activity promotion. Simultaneously, nationwide health education campaigns should promote optimal sleep duration (6–9 h) as an accessible and cost-effective approach to support healthy longevity. Integrating musculoskeletal health and sleep management into comprehensive geriatric care pathways may synergistically reduce the burden of aging-related disability and enhance quality of life in rapidly aging populations.
