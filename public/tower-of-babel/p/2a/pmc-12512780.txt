The impact of zinc and testosterone co-treatment on tumourigenesis in prostate cancer: a novel model
Authors: Kofi Oduro Yeboah, Paul Atawuchugi, Fredrick Kwadwo Baah, Bernard Elikplim Petershie, Yolanda Ashie, Eric Boakye-Gyasi, Newman Osafo, Emmanuel Amankwah Ntim, George Ainooson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPre-clinical models play a key role in prostate cancer research, helping to understand the mechanism of the disease. The unavailability of suitable animal models in under-resourced areas, such as in sub-Saharan African (SSA) countries, presents a major barrier for them to contribute meaningfully to developing effective treatments and preventive strategies in this global effort against prostate cancer. This study investigated the use of a combination of high-dose zinc and testosterone to induce carcinogenesis in Wistar rats.MethodsThe histopathological assessment of the model prostate was carried out. In addition, the relative gene expression of common genes that are deregulated in prostate cancer such as Tmprss2, Akt1, Pdgfrβ and Tp53 were validated in the model.ResultsWe could show that, intramuscular administration of testosterone alone causes epithelial hyperplasia but did not result in abnormal glandular patterns or patterns associated with prostate carcinoma. However, administration of high dose zinc alone (10–100 mg/kg) resulted in epithelial dysplasia in all prostate lobes and was associated with papillary and tufting patterns of glandular architecture indicative of HGPIN. Moreover, administration of testosterone together with 100 mg/kg zinc resulted in tumour formation, characterized by glands with central necrosis, loss of glandular architecture and occasional presence of luminal cells in stroma. The induction significantly increased Tmprss2 expression by 789.8-fold and Akt1 expression by 2.93-fold compared to the naïve control. Combination treatment also led to a 15.89-fold rise in Pdgfrβ expression. Additionally, Tp53 expression increased 2.23-fold in the group treated with the combination.ConclusionIn summary, the zinc-testosterone model induced lesions characteristic of HGPIN with the possibility of early microinvasive adenocarcinoma, most importantly in the dorsolateral lobes, mimicking human disease characteristics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14893-4.Keywords: Prostate cancer, High-grade prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia, High-dose zinc, Testosterone, Preclinical model

Introduction

Pre-clinical models are a crucial part of prostate cancer (PCa) research and have been instrumental in understanding the mechanism of the disease. These models have provided essential information about the progression of the disease, from premalignant stage, through hormone-dependent stage to castration-resistant stage [1]. They have also been indispensable in the in vitro and in vivo screening of drugs for use in the management of PCa [2].

Numerous in vivo and in vitro models have been designed over the past few decades for the preclinical study of PCa. Although current PCa animal models offer several advantages, there is still a need for novel animal model systems, including those that are more accessible. These new models would enhance PCa research and contribute to the development of novel treatment and preventive strategies.

To clarify, cell line-derived xenograft models lack heterogeneity and do not accurately replicate the complexity of the tumour microenvironment [3, 4]. Also, the use of immunocompromised rats in these models limits the understanding of the role of the immune system in actual tumour response [5]. Genetically engineered mouse models are also limited by high cost of genotyping and breeding, thus making them unsustainable choice in resource-scarce laboratories [4]. Moreover, spontaneous models although cheaper, present extensive latency periods of about 3 years [5] and chemically-induced models, like the N-methyl-N-nitrosourea (MNU)-induced tumour model, generally require 13 to 15 months of experimentation. Additionally, MNU is a potent mutagen, and its toxicity can lead to unintended health issues in animals, including organ toxicity or systemic damage [5]. For these reasons, it is important that studies focus on developing novel, less complicated and less costly models that reflect human disease and are time-efficient.

Interestingly, there have been reports of high dietary zinc content inducing high-grade prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia (HGPIN), the main precursor lesion of prostate adenocarcinoma, in rats [6]. It has also been reported that orally administered high-dose zinc is associated with increased risk of PCa progression in humans [7, 8], and several other studies have unequivocally demonstrated an association between high-dose zinc intake and PCa risk [6, 9, 10]. A meta-analysis conducted by Mahmoud et al. [9] revealed a 10% rise in PCa risk with daily zinc intake. Additionally, in the Health Professionals Follow-up Study with follow-up until the year 2000, researchers identified an elevated risk of aggressive PCa associated with individuals taking zinc supplementation at doses of ≥ 100 mg/day or for ≥ 10 years [11]. Further follow-up spanning 16 years confirmed this correlation between high-dose zinc supplementation and the risk of advanced and lethal PCa [10] (Table 1).

The development of PCa has been shown to be heavily influenced by androgens, as it binds to androgen receptors (AR) in prostate cells, promoting cell proliferation and growth [12, 13]. Therefore, this study investigated possible combinations of high dose zinc and testosterone in the creation of malignant lesions in experimental animals which can be useful in screening for pharmacological agents needed for the treatment of PCa and prevention of disease progression. The current study therefore sought to model malignant lesions in the prostate of male Wistar rats using a combination of high doses of zinc sulphate and testosterone. Also, the study seeks to determine the validity of this model by comparing histopathological and molecular changes to that of the human disease.

Materials and methods

Animals

Male Wistar rats (10 weeks old) were obtained from Centre for Plant Medicine Research, Mampong-Akuapem, Ghana. The animals were maintained under standard laboratory conditions in a pathogen-free stainless-steel cage at standard temperature and humidity (temperature 25 ± 2 °C with a 12 h light-dark cycle), with dry wood shavings as bedding, at the Animal house facility of the Department of Pharmacology, Faculty of Pharmacy and Pharmaceutical Sciences, KNUST. Food and water were made available ad libitum. All protocols carried out in this study were conducted in compliance with Animal Welfare Regulations (USDA 1985; US Code, 42 USC § 289 d) and the Public Health Service Policy on Humane Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (PHS 2002). In addition, the department review board on the use of animals for experimental purposes, the Department of Pharmacology Animal Research Ethics Committee (DPAREC) of KNUST, Ghana approved (Approval No: PCOL/23/016) all the protocols used.

Chemicals

Zinc sulphate heptahydrate was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich, Steinheim am Albuch, Germany; Testosterone propionate was purchased from Organon, New Jersey, USA; sodium chloride from Merck, Darmstadt, Germany.

Induction of tumour

After animals were allowed to acclimatize for 1 week, 64 rats were randomly assigned to one of eight groups (n = 8). The rat dose of zinc sulphate heptahydrate equivalent to human dose of 100 mg/day (known to increase PCa incidence) was determined [10, 14]. Group 1 being the naïve control received 10 ml/kg Normal Saline orally followed by intramuscular (i.m.) injection of 1 ml/kg saline. Group 2 animals were treated with 5 mg/kg of a 50 mg/ml solution of testosterone (Organon, New Jersey, USA), i.m. Zinc sulphate heptahydrate was administered to groups 3 to 5 rats at doses of 10, 30 and 100 mg/kg respectively. Lastly, groups 6 to 8 rats were treated with 5 mg/kg testosterone i.m. in addition to 10, 30 and 100 mg/kg zinc sulphate respectively. All experimental animals were treated for 18 weeks, based on data obtained during initial optimization of the protocol.

Body weight changes

Body weight fluctuations were assessed every three weeks, prior to feeding, throughout the 18-week duration. Additionally, the general behavior of the rats was observed daily to assess their overall health status and detect any signs of moribundity. Relative organ weight

At the end of the 18th week of induction, rats from all groups were anaesthetized using combined ketamine (75 mg/kg)/xylazine (10 mg/kg) anaesthesia, and the prostate tissues were excised. Briefly, rats were placed in supine position after anaesthesia and a midline abdominal incision was made to expose the abdominal cavity. Within the pelvic region, the prostate was identified and carefully excised without damage to surrounding structures. Adhering connective tissue around the prostate was then removed. The bladder and seminal vesicles were removed and the prostate was weighed using an analytical balance (Fisher Scientific, USA). Histopathological analysis

At the end of the 18 weeks of induction, rats were euthanized under combined ketamine (75 mg/kg)/xylazine (10 mg/kg) anaesthesia to minimize pain and discomfort to the animals. Prostate tissues were excised, fixed in 10% formalin, and embedded in wax. Tissue sections, 5 μm thick, were cut using a microtome, collected, and dried on glass slides. The slides were then dewaxed with xylene, dehydrated with ethanol, stained with haematoxylin and eosin, and mounted with DPX mountant. Subsequently, two pathologists blindly and independently evaluated tissue sections to classify lesions. Any discrepancy that arose during interpretation were discussed and resolved. Sections were examined directly under the microscope (Leica DM750, Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) for presence of epithelial dysplasia in dorsal, lateral and ventral prostate glands. If any dysplasic tissue was identified, further examination was carried out under higher magnification. Subsequently, the presence of the four main patterns of HGPIN, i.e., tufting, flat, micropapillary and cribriform, were investigated. Finally, patterns of growth suggestive of malignancy were assessed in accordance with histological grading system by Gingrich et al. [15], with slight modifications.

Identification of genes that are differentially expressed in prostate adenocarcinoma

Microarray datasets

To identify genes that are differentially expressed in prostate cancer when compared to normal tissues, high-throughput gene expression profiles from numerous studies were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database hosted at the National Center for Biotechnology Information (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/, accessed on 31/12/2023). The use of the MeSH words “Prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia and Prostate cancer” led to the identification of two Geo Series, namely GSE210202 and GSE66633. GSE210202 involved 12 datasets (GSM423447–58) whereas GSE66633 contained 14 datasets (GSM1626707–20). By employing GEO2R for analysis, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) among normal samples and tumour samples were identified in both series using adjusted P-value (p < 0.05) and absolute value of the log fold change (|logFC| ≥ 1).

Functional analysis of common DEGs and exploration of interactions and relationships among the differentially expressed genes

Following the identification of DEGs, functional analysis involving Gene Ontology (GO) function enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis was conducted to gain insights into the biological roles of these genes. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses conducted in this study were performed using Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) bioinformatics (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/summary.jsp). To mitigate the risk of overcounting duplicated genes, Fisher Exact statistics were computed using corresponding DAVID gene IDs, thus, eliminating redundancies present in the original IDs. The analysis focused on the common DEGs identified in datasets of both GSE210202 and GSE66633 Geo series.

Subsequently, the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING) database (https://www.string-db.org/) was employed to predict potential interactions between proteins encoded by the identified DEGs. The interactions were then visualized using Cytoscape (www.cytoscape.org/) and the relevance of selected genes as biomarkers in human disease was assessed by evaluating tumour samples and their corresponding paired normal tissues using gene expression profile interaction analysis (GEPIA) platform (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/).

Gene expression analysis

Identification of differentially expressed genes

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in prostate cancer were identified from GEO datasets using GEO2R, with functional analysis and protein interaction visualization, while the GEPIA platform assessed their potential as cancer biomarkers. This resulted in the identification of Tmprss2, Akt1, Pdgfrβ and Tp53 as differentially expressed in prostate tumours. Sdha and Hprt1 expressions were measured and used as internal reference genes for normalizations.

RNA extraction and quantification

To obtain RNA for qRT-PCR gene expression analysis, RNA was extracted from the rat prostate tissues using RNAsimple Total RNA kit (Tiangen, China) according to manufacturer’s protocol. To measure the concentration of RNA, optical density (OD) of RNA specimens were measured at 230, 260, and 280 nm using the NanoDrop® ND-1000 spectrophotometer (PeqLab Biotechnologie GmbH, Germany). An OD260/OD280 ratio falling between 1.8 and 2.0 signifies a sample with satisfactory purity, indicating minimal protein contamination. Moreover, an OD260/OD230 ratio of 1.6 and higher indicates a preparation that is relatively devoid of organic chemicals and solvents. The purified RNA was stored at −80 °C until needed for cDNA conversion.

Complementary DNA synthesis

Prior to the synthesis of complementary DNA (cDNA) from RNA, 0.5 µg of RNA was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min with 1 unit of DNAse1 in a 10 µL total reaction volume using a GeneAmp® PCR System 2700 (Applied Biosystems), to eliminate genomic DNA contaminants. Following the DNase1 incubation, 1 µL of RQ1 DNase Stop Solution® (Promega, Mannheim) was introduced and incubated at 65 °C for 10 min in the thermocycler to deactivate DNase1. Subsequently, PCR tubes were prepared and labeled. A random primer mix intended to provide 5 µL of RNAse-free water were added to (+) RT tubes, and 1 µL of random primer was added to (+) RT. Subsequently, 6 µL of RNA were added to the PCR tubes to make a final volume of 12 µL. The PCR tubes were capped tightly and briefly vortexed to thoroughly mix contents of the tube. Afterwards, the tubes were spun down with a microcentrifuge (ONiLAB’s Scientific) and incubated for 5 min at 70 °C in the GeneAmp® PCR System 2700 thermocycler, for random primers to bind to RNA. Following this step, a (+) RT master mix containing 1 µL reverse transcriptase, 4 µL of 5x buffer, 2 µL of 10 nM dNTP Mix and 1 µL of RNAse-free water, was prepared. The (-)RT master mix contained 2 µL of 5x buffer, 1 µL of 10 nM dNTP Mix and 1 µL of RNAse-free water.

From the master mix, 8 µL were dispensed into the PCR tubes and then concurrently subjected a thermal cycling protocol. This included an initial incubation at 25 °C for 10 min to promote primer annealing, followed by reverse transcription at 42 °C for 60 min. The reaction was subsequently terminated by heating at 70 °C for 10 min to inactivate the reverse transcriptase. Finally, the tubes were held at 4 °C until further processing.

Following cDNA synthesis, the contents of PCR tubes were diluted with 80 µL of sterile RNase-free water. These samples were stored at −20 °C until ready for use.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) primer design

The qRT-PCR experiment was carried out with Rattus norvegicus (taxid:10116) gene-specific primers designed to span exon-exon junction with melting temperatures (Tm) ranging between 60 and 62 °C and amplicon size between 110 and 150 base pairs, using the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) primer designing tool PRIMER-BLAST® (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast). To carry out gene expression analysis using qRT-PCR, SYBR green-primer master mix was prepared by adding 10 µL SYBR Green qRT-PCR – Mix (TIANGEN, China), 1 µL forward primer, 1 µL reverse primer and 4 µL RNase-free water to a total volume of 16 µL. Results

Changes in body weight

In this study, changes in body weight of experimental animals were monitored to determine disease activity and moribundity. In the absence of disease induction, progressive increase in body weight was observed in the naïve control rats (Fig. 2A). Animals in this group reached a mean percentage change in body weight of 87.00 ± 8.27% in 18 weeks as compared to the beginning of the study. Similarly, intramuscular administration of 5 mg/kg of testosterone resulted in an increase in body weight, with a mean percentage change in body weight of 39.22 ± 9.76% on week 18. In contrast, treatment with a combination 5 mg/kg testosterone and 10 mg/kg zinc sulphate heptahydrate resulted in a significant (p < 0.05) reduction in body weight on week 18, with a percentage change in body weight of −4.76 ± 0.47% when compared to the naïve control. Compared to the naïve control, co-administration of 5 mg/kg testosterone and 30 mg/kg zinc or 100 mg/kg zinc resulted in significant (p < 0.05) reductions in body weight with percentage changes in body weight of −2.22 ± 3.17% and − 15.31 ± 1.05%, respectively on week 18 (Fig. 2A). On the other hand, the administration of the zinc at doses of 10 mg/kg and 30 mg/kg was associated with increase in body weight with percentage change in body weight of 48.42 ± 4.67% and 41.61 ± 4.87% respectively, on week 18. However, these changes in body weight were not significant (p > 0.05) when compared to the naïve control (Fig. 2A). Treatment with zinc at the highest dose of 100 mg/kg resulted in a significant (p < 0.01) reduction in body weight on week 18, with percentage change in body weight of −13.12 ± 5.42%, when compared to the naïve control (Fig. 2A). Over the experimental period, intramuscular administration of 5 mg/kg testosterone, resulted in a cumulative weight change of 417.3 ± 116.7%. Coadministration of testosterone with zinc in doses of 10 and 30 mg/kg was associated with a reduction in cumulative body weight, however not significant (p > 0.05) when compared to the naïve control (Fig. 2B). Treatment with 100 mg/kg zinc and 5 mg/kg testosterone resulted in a significant (p < 0.01) reduction in cumulative body weight (200.4 ± 55.89) when compared to the untreated control. The administration of 10 mg/kg zinc without testosterone resulted in a significant (p < 0.05) reduction in cumulative body weight with cumulative weight change of 257.3 ± 30.04%, when compared to the naïve control. Similarly, zinc at doses of 30 and 100 mg/kg were associated with significant (p < 0.01) reductions in overall body weight with cumulative weight change of 165.4 ± 12.63% and 140.0 ± 19.31% respectively (Fig. 2B). Thus, high doses of zinc-testosterone combination induce profound weight loss, probably due to increased disease activity.

Histopathology

Zinc induces epithelial luminal cell hyperplasia in the prostate

Histopathological examination of dorsal, lateral and ventral prostate glands of the rats revealed distinct morphological changes amongst the groups (Fig. 5). Specifically, the dorsolateral prostate of naïve control group displayed well-defined pseudostratified epithelial layer. Minimal secretions were identified in the glands and epithelial cell hyperplasia were not identified in any of the tissue sections of the naïve control rats (Fig. 5a). Conversely, glands with profound epithelial hyperplasia (red circle) and stromal hypertrophy were observed in rats treated with 5 mg/kg testosterone (Fig. 5b). Treatment with zinc sulphate heptahydrate at all doses, especially 100 mg/kg, resulted in profound hyperplasia of dorsal, lateral and ventral acini epithelia without noticeable stromal thickening (Fig. 5c). Moreover, coadministration of 5 mg/kg testosterone with 10, 30 or 100 mg/kg zinc resulted in stromal thickening and most importantly, widespread epithelial cell proliferation (red dotted circles) especially in the group treated with 100 mg/kg of zinc in combination with testosterone. In these groups, dorsal gland epithelial hyperplasia was observed in 100% of experimental animals (Fig. 5d).

Patterns of HGPIN and early malignancy

In this study, prostate glands of rats challenged with zinc only or a combination of zinc and testosterone were identified to express patterns of growth reminiscent of HGPIN and/or suspected adenocarcinoma. Glands with new stroma formation were observed in rats treated with the aforementioned treatments. With the exception of naïve control and testosterone only treated rats, acini inflammation characterized by polymorphous inflammatory infiltrates were observed in all rats (Fig. 6a). At least one major pattern of HGPIN was observed in groups treated with zinc alone (3, 10 and 100 mg/kg) or in combination with 5 mg/kg testosterone. However, tissue sections from rats treated with a combination of testosterone and 100 mg/kg zinc sulphate demonstrated multiple patterns of HGPIN.

Histological evaluation of the prostate tissue in this group demonstrated marked architectural distortion with focal loss of normal glandular organization (Fig. 6b). Identification of DEGs, Gene ontology function and kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses

To identify genes that are differentially expressed in normal and diseased prostates, the GEO database hosted by NCBI was employed as described earlier. A total of 20,782 and 16,996 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in GSE210202 and GSE66633 data series respectively. Cluster analysis conducted on these DEGs in both GEO series identified genes that were either upregulated or downregulated in cancerous samples when compared to samples belonging to normal controls. Among the identified DEGs, 25 genes and 201 genes were differentially expressed when normal control datasets were compared to only disease control datasets in GSE210202 and GSE66633 data series respectively.

Following the identification of DEGs, functional analysis was conducted on 25 of the common DEGs in GSE210202 and 201 of the common DEGs in GSE66633 through Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis. Functional annotation clustering revealed 96 annotation clusters with counts greater than 2. GO terms associated with the first annotation cluster with enrichment score of 1.93 is shown in Fig. 9A. As observed in all the annotation clusters, the DEGs exhibited significant enrichment in various biological processes, including protein autophosphorylation, ubiquitin-like DNA damage, DNA repair, etc. (Fig. 9A). In terms of cellular components, enrichment was observed in anchored components and plasma membrane, the cytoplasm, catenin complex, azonemes, etc. Molecular function analysis highlighted enrichment in ATP-binding, ubiquitin protein ligase and transferase activities, translation repressor activity, protein kinase activity, natural killer cell lectin-like receptor binding activity, etc. For KEGG enrichment, the analysis considered -log10(p-value), count value, and the number of genes enriched to the term. As shown in Fig. 9B, the DEGs were prominently enriched in pathways such as prostate cancer, the natural killer cell mediated cytotoxicity, HIF-1 signaling pathway, pancreatic cancer, etc.

Zinc-testosterone combination alters relative expression of Tmprss2, Akt1, Pdgfrβ and Tp53 in the Wistar rat prostate

As illustrated in Fig. 13, the genes Tmprss2, Akt1, Pdgfrβ, and P53 showed differential expression in the rat prostate following the induction of prostate adenocarcinoma using a combination of 100 mg/kg zinc and 5 mg/kg testosterone. Specifically, the induction resulted in a significant (p < 0.0001) increase in the relative mRNA expression of Tmprss2 (14.13 ± 0.75) when compared to the naïve control (0.01 ± 0.001) (Fig. 13A). This corresponds to a 789.8 ± 15.76-fold increase over the naïve control’s expression. Additionally, the induction led to a significant (p < 0.05) rise in Akt1 expression, with relative mRNA levels of 0.40 ± 0.04 in the induction group versus 0.21 ± 0.03 in the naïve control group (Fig. 13B), signifying a 2.93 ± 0.28- fold increase in expression. The combination treatment led to a significant (p < 0.05) increase in the expression of Pdgfrβ with relative mRNA expression of 0.44 ± 0.13 (Fig. 13C), with a corresponding 15.89 ± 0.94-fold increase when compared to the naïve control. Lastly, a significant (p < 0.05) elevation in the expression of Tp53 (0.22 ± 0.05) was measured in the group that received combination of 100 mg/kg zinc and 5 mg/kg testosterone (Fig. 13D). This corresponded to a 2.23 ± 0.26-fold increase in Tp53 mRNA expression in the tumour samples when compared to naïve control group with relative mRNA expression of 0.06 ± 0.008.

Discussion

Pre-clinical animal models of PCa have been indispensable in the screening of potential drugs for use in the management of PCa [2]. These in vivo models have provided significant advantages in PCa research over the years. Nonetheless, there is still the need to develop novel models with face and construct validities that can easily be used in resource-scarce regions such as sub-Saharan Africa, as alternative to currently available models. Thus, necessitating research into the development of novel and less costly in vivo models that reflect the human disease.

In this present study, disease induction with zinc in high doses, equivalent to or exceeding a 100 mg/day human dose, with or without testosterone resulted in weight loss and changes in relative prostate weight. Reduction in body weight is common in patients with cancer, and may be the foremost recognizable sign of the disease [16]. This may be as a result of disease progression and cancer cachexia, which is characterized by anorexia, anemia, tissue wasting, diminished physical performance, involuntary loss of weight, and ultimately, mortality [17]. This could explain the marked reduction in food intake and the significant weight loss observed in rats treated with high doses of zinc in combination with testosterone. Long has it been known that cachexia is strongly associated with PCa with prevalence reaching as high as 87% in these patients [17, 18]. Interestingly, a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials revealed a reduction in body mass index following a monthly supplementation with 100 mg of zinc daily [19]. However, authors of this study did not investigate for the presence of prostate-specific pathologies. In this study, the rat prostate was further screened to identify if the loss of body weight was an isolated finding or was associated with histopathological or molecular changes in the prostate disease.

Histopathological examination revealed that, administration of zinc in doses equivalent to or more than 100 mg/day human dose, with or without testosterone to 10-weeks old male Wistar rats resulted in epithelial hyperplasia of the prostate. Specifically, zinc with or without testosterone induced luminal hyperplasia, which has been associated with development of prostate adenocarcinoma [20]. Zinc and testosterone play important roles in the normal function of the prostate and have also been shown to accumulate in the prostate gland [21]. Normal levels of zinc have been reported to protect against carcinogenesis through the upregulation of ZIP1 transporter and reduction in the expression of insulin-like growth factor (IGF)−1 [22]. However, high doses such as those administered in this study are associated with significant prostate disease [10]. Increased intracellular zinc concentration and zinc transporter activity has been associated with cell proliferation and migration in other malignant conditions such as breast cancer [23], with tremendous biological similarities to PCa [24].

Moreover, high dose zinc in this study induced several histological changes in the dorsolateral glands suggestive of early neoplastic transformation. The glandular architecture was notably distorted, with crowded and irregularly shaped glands replacing the typical round-to-oval acini seen in normal tissue. There was increased epithelial cellularity in several areas, with evidence of multilayering, indicating epithelial hyperplasia. Nuclear crowding and loss of polarity are apparent in some glands. Additionally, the usual separation between glands and the surrounding stroma appeared diminished, potentially reflecting gland fusion. The combination of cellular and nuclear atypia, occasional luminal cells within the stroma, mitotic activity, and previously described features is consistent with early-stage lesions, such as high-grade prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia (HGPIN) or well-differentiated adenocarcinoma [25].

HGPIN, a premalignant lesion, represents a preinvasive stage in the spectrum of epithelial proliferations occurring within the lining of prostatic ducts and acini. Unlike other premalignant lesions, HGPIN is considered the main precursor of invasive prostate adenocarcinoma [25] and is linked to progressive phenotypic and genotypic abnormalities. Additionally, high dose zinc induces loss of senescence in prostate cells, which enhances tumourigenesis [6]. Subsequently, gradual impairment of cell differentiation and the suppression of regulatory control results, in part, to advancing of HGPIN to PCa. HGPIN when diagnosed indicates approximately 25% risk of detecting cancer in repeat biopsies [26]. This observation aligns with earlier findings by Trabzonlu et al. [27], who reported that prostate carcinomas may sometimes masquerade as HGPIN.

These neoplastic patterns were most prominent in dorsal and lateral acini, which are considered the most analogous to the peripheral zone of the human prostate — the commonest site for PCa development in humans [28]. For this reason, the face validity of prostate adenocarcinoma induced with high dose zinc, in combination with testosterone, is unquestionable as the presentation of prostate acini seems to relate to that of human PCa disease. It follows that, high dose zinc-hormone – induced prostate adenocarcinoma may be employed as a pharmacological model to screen agents for the management or treatment of PCa, more specifically, early stage PCa. Due to the role that genetic alterations play in the development and progression of PCa, it can safely be inferred that, zinc-hormone combination alters the expression of certain genes and disrupts cell signaling pathways, resulting in the growth of premalignant and malignant cells [29].

In this study, GEO2R analysis identified a significant association between changes in Tmprss2, Akt1, Pdgfrβ and Tp53 expressions and development of PCa. The differential expression of these genes in the rat prostate was subsequently investigated using qRT-PCR. It was determined that zinc-hormone combination alters the expression of Tmprss2, Akt1, Pdgfrβ and Tp53. In PCa, the most predominant genetic rearrangement is the fusion of Tmprss2 with ETS transcription factor ERG [30]. The increased expression of ETS factors regulated by the androgen receptor promoter, Tmprss2, induces the expression of matrix metalloproteinases which are relevant in degrading extracellular matrix and basal membrane, contributing to the invasiveness of PCa [30–32]. Moreover, elevated levels of phospho-AKT have unsurprisingly been associated with higher Gleason scores in PCa. Transient knockdown of Akt1 using small interfering RNA suppresses the proliferation of PCa cells both in vitro and in vivo, and high level phosphorylated AKT1 serves as a robust predictor for PCa recurrence [33]. Likewise, the deletion of phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN), a negative regulator for the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)-AKT signaling pathway, is associated with poor prognosis, metastasis, and progression towards hormone-independent disease [34]. Pdgfrβ, on the other hand, plays a significant role in angiogenesis, a process in which mutational activation or upregulation of this factor may lead to uncontrolled blood vessel formation, contributing to cancer progression [31]. This suggests the significance of Pdgfrβ gene expression in the progression of PCa disease. Thus, by enhancing Akt1 and Pdgfrβ expression, high dose zinc-testosterone activates PI3K-AKT signaling pathway and angiogenesis respectively, resulting in the survival and metastatic growth of PCa cells.

Lastly, tumour induction with high dose zinc-testosterone combination altered the expression of Tp53 in the rat prostate. Inhibition of p53 pathway through the suppression of Tp53 expression reduces tumour immunogenicity to natural killer cells, resulting in enhanced tumourigenesis [35]. It is worth noting that, in the absence of cellular stress, p53 protein maintains low levels in the normal cell [36]. However, following tumour induction, p53 response is prompted, and higher levels of expression is observed secondary to stabilization of p53 due to the disruption of the p53/MDM2/MDM4 complex and the sequestration of MDM2 in the nucleolus in response to oncogene-induced stress [37]. This implies that, the higher relative mRNA level of Tp53 observed in the disease control group is as a result of stabilization of mutant p53 which are more stable and tends to accumulate within the cell. This results in impaired G1 and G2 arrest, and reduced apoptosis [38]. Given this, construct validity of this novel animal model is satisfied, as molecular mechanisms of the animal model are similar or the same to those indicated to be involved in human PCa disease [39].

In summary, oral administration of high dose zinc together with testosterone administered intramuscularly induces prostate adenocarcinoma in rat prostate lobes that are homologous with the human prostate. This induction protocol resulted in the development of prostate lesions within a relatively short period of 18 weeks, making it more time-efficient than many established models, which often exhibit long latency periods before tumor emergence. In contrast to transgenic and other chemically induced models that are costly and technically demanding, this model is relatively low-cost, straightforward to implement, and does not require complex breeding or genetic manipulation. Importantly, the observed lesions were anatomically restricted to the prostate, enhancing the specificity of the model for prostate cancer research. Given these, the model may serve as a pharmacological model for screening novel agents for the treatment of PCa.

Conclusion

This study has, for the first time, showed that oral zinc at very high doses in combination with intramuscular testosterone induces proliferative epithelial lesion arising in a background of chronic inflammation, most suggestive of HGPIN with the possibility of early microinvasive adenocarcinoma, histologically and molecularly similar to the human disease. Thus, this model may serve as a potential alternative model to currently available models for screening novel agents for the treatment of PCa, especially in resource-scarce regions. Nonetheless, a major limitation of the present study is the absence of immunohistochemical analysis. While H&E staining provided valuable insights into the architectural changes within the prostate tissue, it lacks the molecular specificity required to confirm cellular identity and distinguish between morphologically similar lesions. We recommend that, future studies incorporate immunohistochemistry, and other assays such as biochemical tests, to enhance the diagnostic precision, molecular depth, and translational relevance of the models. While the androgen dependency of the lesions remains to be fully elucidated, future studies must assess their hormone sensitivity and potential for castration resistance.
