Acute effects of swimming goggle wearing on intraocular pressure, anterior chamber biometrics, and optic nerve head morphology
Authors: Serdar Bilici, Aydın Toprak, Mine Koru-Toprak, Suat Hayri Ugurbas
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Swimming goggles (SG) are widely used in water sports, and this study aimed to evaluate the acute effects of wearing SG on intraocular pressure (IOP), anterior chamber biometrics, axial length (AL), and optic nerve head (ONH) morphology. Twenty-eight healthy young adults participated in this cross-sectional study, with assessed parameters including IOP, central corneal thickness (CCT), anterior chamber depth (ACD), anterior chamber angle (ACA), AL, and optical coherence tomography (OCT) imaging of the ONH, specifically Bruch membrane opening (BMO), Bruch membrane opening-minimum rim width (BMO-MRW), lamina cribrosa depth (LCD), and prelaminar tissue (PLT). Measurements were taken at four time points: before wearing SG, at the 1st and 10th minutes of wearing, and immediately after removal. The results showed a significant increase in IOP at the 1st and 10th minutes of SG wear compared to pre-wear and post-removal values. Additionally, decreases in CCT, ACD, and ACA, along with an increase in AL, were observed while wearing SG. However, these changes reverted to baseline after the goggles were removed. No significant alterations were detected in ONH parameters during the study. The findings suggest that wearing SG induces an acute rise in IOP and changes in anterior segment parameters, likely due to oculopression, but does not appear to affect ONH morphology in the short term. Further studies are needed to investigate any potential long-term effects.

Introduction

Swimming goggles (SG) are widely used in water sports to improve underwater vision and provide protection. Recent studies have shown that, depending on the model, there are transient variations in anterior chamber biometrics and intraocular pressure (IOP) with the usage of SG, which returns to baseline levels once they are removed1–3.

Fluctuations in IOP and higher baseline IOP are regarded as critical risk factors for the development and progression of glaucoma4,5. The effects of transient alterations in IOP on optic nerve head morphology have been demonstrated in the acute phase as well as in the chronic phase6. Notably, wearing SG has been shown to lead to a discernible increase in IOP. However, to date, it is unknown how this elevation may affect the optic nerve head morphology. Thus, the aim of this study is to evaluate whether wearing SG affects the optic nerve head (ONH) morphology in addition to IOP, and anterior chamber biometrics.

Materials and methods

Participants and ethics committee

The current study was approved by the Zonguldak Bülent Ecevit University Ethics Committee (Ethics Committee approval: 2023-04-03) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Before taking part in the study, each participant gave written, informed consent. Using the GPower 3.1 program, we conducted an a priori power analysis to determine the sample size. We took into account a power of 0.80 and an alpha of 0.05, and we based our calculations on an estimated low effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.20). Based on this computation, it was found that 26 individuals were needed to reach the specified accuracy level. Participants were selected from medical school students who did not swim regularly. None of the participants had history of ocular or systemic diseases or were using any medication. Additionally, participants were instructed to abstain from alcohol and caffeine-based beverages 24 h before the study.

Experimental design

After participants read and signed the consent form and their demographic information was recorded, basic measurements were taken sequentially, including IOP, anterior chamber biometrics, axial length (AL) measurements, and ONH evaluations with optical coherence tomography (OCT). Then, the partipicants put on the SG (Eye socket type SG - Nabaiji, Decathlon Group Inc., Villeneuve d’Ascq, France) in which a portion of the plastic of the right lens was drilled (Fig. 1) before allowing for measurements of corneal topography, biometry, and OCT while wearing the goggles1–3. Measurements were repeated for the right eye at the 1st minute and 10th minute. The same measurements were taken again just after the participant removed the SG.

Intraocular pressure measurements

IOP measurements with the Icare Pro rebound tonometer (Icare, Helsinki, Finland) were taken on the right eye using the manufacturer’s recommendations. Six measurements were taken from each subject’s right eye, and the average value (in mmHg) was recorded. To control for the factor of corneal thickness, all IOP measurements were corrected using the following equation: corrected IOP = IOP value + 0.02 (545 - CCT)7.

Anterior chamber biometrics

Anterior segment biometrics were measured with Sirius Corneal Topography (Costruzione Strumenti Ophthalmici, Florence, Italy) which had a Scheimpflug camera system. The following parameters were obtained with this device: (a) central corneal thickness (CCT - centralized at the corneal apex), (b) anterior chamber depth (ACD - distance from the corneal endothelium to the anterior surface of the lens), (c) anterior chamber angle (ACA - average of angles taken from all meridians).

Axial length measurements

AL measurements were obtained using the NIDEK AL-Scan biometry device (Nidek CO, Aichi, Japan).

Optic nerve head evaluations

Evaluations of ONH morphology were performed using the Heidelberg Spectralis SD-OCT device (Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany). Six high-resolution radial B-scans of the ONH were obtained using a radial scanning pattern by the same person (AT) (Fig. 2). To obtain images from the same sections in repeated measurements “Set reference” property of the device was used. The following parameters were measured manually with the internal caliper of the device: (a) Bruch’s membrane opening (BMO - termination of the retinal pigment epithelium), (b) Bruch’s membrane opening - Minimum rim width (BMO - MRW, shortest distance drawn from the BMO to internal limiting membrane [ILM]), (c) Mean cup depth (MCD), (d) Lamina cribrosa depth (LCD), (e) Prelaminar tissue (PLT). All parameters were measured by two independent observers (SB and AT), three times by each observer and the mean values of 6 results for each parameter were used in analysis. Intra- and inter-observer variability were assessed using intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) from randomly selected 10 measurements. The inra- and inter-observer ICCs were 0.92 and 0.91 for BMO, 0.89 and 0. 87 for BMO-MRW, 0.88 and 0.84 for MCD, 0.85 and 0.82 for LCD, and 0.89 and 0.83 for PLT, respectively.

Image acquisition of optic nerve head. Infrared image showing six radial B-scan pattern overlay.

The termination of the retinal pigment epithelium layer is known as BMO. The BMO points in OCT reflect the actual location of the optic disc as described previously8. The shortest distance between the BMO and the ILM was designated as the BMO-MRW. A horizontal reference line was drawn between the two Bruch’s membrane terminations in each ONH image. A vertical reference line was constructed using the cup’s deepest point as a point of reference. At distances of 100 and 200 μm from the vertical reference line, two additional vertical measurements were taken. At the three locations mentioned above, the lengths between the anterior surface of the prelaminar tissue (PLT) and the horizontal reference line were measured. MCD was defined as the average of the three measures. Measurements were also made of the distances between the anterior surface of the lamina cribrosa and the same three reference line points. LCD was defined as the mean of these measurements. The difference between the LCD and the MCD was thought to be the thickness of the PLT. (Fig. 3)

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed with SPSS 20.0 software (IBM SPSS, IBM, New York, USA). The normality of data was determined with the Shapiro–Wilk test. The changes in IOP, AL, anterior segment parameters (CCT, ACD, and ACA), and OCT parameters of ONH (BMO, BMO -MRW, MCD, LCD, and PLT) before, after, and during the SG wearing were analyzed by using the one-way analyses of variance (ANOVAs) or Friedman tests. Bonferroni correction was adopted for multiple comparisons and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Intraocular pressure

The mean IOP was 16.95 ± 2.51 mmHg at baseline, 17.72 ± 2.36 mmHg at 1 min, 17.92 ± 2.31 mmHg at 10 min after wearing SG, and 16.55 ± 1.66 mmHg just after the SG removal. Compared to the baseline, a significant increase in intraocular pressure was observed at the 1st and 10th minute after wearing swim goggles (p = 0.017, and p = 0.015, respectively). However, after removing the goggles, a significant decrease in IOP was observed compared to both the 1st and 10th minutes after wearing SG (p < 0.001, p < 0.001).

Axial length

The axial length was measured as 24.12 (± 1.09) mm at baseline, 24.12 (± 1.08) mm at 1 min, 24.14 (± 1.08) mm at 10 min after wearing SG, and 24.13 (± 1.08) mm at 10 min after removal. A significant increase in axial length was observed at the 5th and 10th minutes compared to baseline, and a significant increase was also noted at the 10th minute compared to the 5th minute (p = 0.01, p < 0.001, p = 0.012). Additionally, a significant decrease was observed when comparing the 10th minute after wearing SG to after their removal (p < 0.001).

Anterior segment biometrics

Central corneal thickness

The means of CCT was 534.81 ± 26.41 μm at baseline, 525.89 ± 32.03, and 528.54 ± 27.15 μm at the 1st and 10th minute of SG wearing, and 536.37 (± 25.81) µm after the removal of SG. Significantly lower CCT values were observed at both the 1st and the 10th minute of SG wearing in comparison to before wearing SG (p = 0.006, and p = 0.045, respectively), and after removing SG (p = 0.011, and p = 0.044, respectively).

Anterior chamber depth

The means of ACD were 3.16 ± 0.37 mm at baseline, 2.98 ± 0.45 mm at the 1st minute, 2.92 ± 0.47 mm at the 10th minute of SG wearing, and 3.16 ± 0.36 mm after the SG removal, respectively. Significantly decreased ACD values were observed during SG wearing in comparison to both before and after SG wearing (p < 0.001, p < 0.001, respectively).

Anterior chamber angle

The means of ACA were 41.44 ± 7.01 at baseline, 39.72 ± 7.32 at the 1st minute of SG wearing, 37.57 ± 10.19 at the 10th minute of SG wearing, and 40.44 ± 6.71 after the removal of SG, respectively. ACA significantly decreased at the 10th minute of SG wearing compared to baseline (p = 0.034).

Optic nerve head evaluations

BMO, BMO-MRW, MCD, LCD, and PLT values are presented in Table 2. No significant changes were observed in these parameters at 5 and 10 min after wearing goggles compared to baseline. Similarly, after the removal of goggles, no significant changes were observed compared to the baseline.

Discussion

Our study evaluated the acute effects of wearing SG on the IOP fluctuation, anterior chamber biometrics, and the morphological properties of the ONH in a healthy young population. Previously, a few studies have reported changes in the IOP and anterior chamber biometrics with the SG wearing1–3. However, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that evaluated the OCT parameters of ONH while wearing SG. Our results revealed significant elevations in the IOP, reduction in the CCT, ACD, and ACA, and elongation of AL. Nevertheless, no alteration was observed in the OCT parameters of ONH morphology.

Prior research has also documented increased IOP associated with SG wear, which returns to baseline levels immediately following SG removal1–3. Our findings are also consistent with existing observations. We observed a quick return to baseline values upon SG removal and an average IOP rise of about 1 mmHg during SG wear. The probable explanation for this reversible increase in IOP is that the SG’s mechanical pressure on the orbital tissue compresses the globe, causing a little but statistically significant increase in IOP1–3.

It has also been shown that wearing SG causes rapid alterations in anterior chamber biometrics: a reduction in CCT, ACD, and ACA1–3. Comparable findings were observed in the present study regarding anterior chamber biometrics. It is also attributed to the compression of the globe by SG which results in corneal thinning and reduction of the iridocorneal angle may restrict aqueous outflow and lead to ocular hypertension2,3,9. Additionally, previous research has demonstrated that wearing SG causes reversible increases in axial length by about 15 microns along with the IOP changes10. Furthermore, a tiny but noteworthy reversible AL elongation of approximately 16 microns was demonstrated by our analysis. This small but consistent elongation in AL was likely due to the sclera’s elastic reaction to temporary fluctuations in intraocular pressure.

There are some reports about the change in ONH morphology depending on the IOP levels6,11,12. Pardon et al. demonstrated that when IOP is raised and does not decrease back to baseline, monkeys’ BMO-MRW gradually thins over an extended period. They explained this by claiming that there’s a likelihood that retinal ganglion cell axons are being compressed or stretched in the presence of high IOP6. On the other hand, Sanchez et al. revealed that the decrease in IOP following trabeculectomy provides a significant improvement in OCT-based ONH rim parameters: increased BMO-MRW, decreased LCD, and MCD11. In our study wearing SG had no statistically significant effect on ONH morphology although there was a slight decrease in BMO-MRW. The main difference from the previously mentioned studies is the amount of change in IOP which was around 10 mmHg in other situations while it was around 1 mmHg in SG wearing. Hence, this little amount of fluctuation in IOP did not affect the morphological state of ONH in the acute phase. However, there is a need for long-term follow for evaluation of the cumulative effect of this little fluctuation on ONH morphology.

It has been noted that the BMO-MRW reflects the changes in optic nerve head morphology more accurately, which may mean that it’s anatomically more vulnerable to deformational changes due to IOP fluctuations8,12,13. Sharma and coworkers demonstrated that acute IOP spikes lead to a decrease of BMO-MRW in glaucomatous eyes, while non-glaucomatous eyes did not exhibit this similar change14. The cohort of the present study consists of non-glaucomatous young participants, which may also have a protective effect on the stability of ONH morphology. Therefore, there is a need for evaluation in the glaucoma cohort besides the amount of IOP change.

The current study focused on the short-term effects of SG wear on ONH morphology. Although there were significant reversible changes in anterior chamber parameters and IOP, no significant alterations were observed in ONH morphology. These findings suggest that while transient changes in IOP and anterior chamber biometrics may not immediately pose a threat to ONH morphology, the potential for prolonged exposure to swimming goggles still warrants caution. Given the limitations and biases noted in the study by Franchina et al. regarding the long-term effects of goggle wear on glaucoma risk, it is crucial to discuss the implications of sustained goggle use, especially in individuals with pre-existing ocular conditions15. Paula et al. have recommended using goggles with larger, smoother edges and proper positioning to minimize the elastic force and impact on the orbital rim area, which could otherwise contribute to IOP elevation and subsequent ocular perfusion disturbances1. Thus, it is essential to consider the potential risks of prolonged goggle wear in clinical practice, especially for swimmers with a predisposition to glaucoma or other ocular diseases. Future longitudinal studies are necessary to explore the long-term relationship between swimming goggle use and ocular health, potentially leading to more precise anatomical models and the development of customized goggles to mitigate these risks.

The current study has some limitations. First of all, although it has very low coefficient of variance (0.052), iCare Pro has a measurement variability and there was no objective standardization of SG tightness which could affect IOP measurements1,16. It should be noted that the mean difference of IOP was within the measurement variability of the Icare device, which may affect the interpretation of our results. Secondly, our experimental setup deviated from real-world conditions due to the obligation of removing the front part of the SG to become available to evaluate IOP and anterior segment biometrics and ONH morphology during wear. This modification of SG results in compromising airtightness and negating the suction effect. In realistic scenarios, optimal SG suction ranges from 0 to 25 mmHg, akin to the sensation induced by a 60-meter elevation, and is not anticipated to impact eye metrics, including IOP. Notably, our measurements did not account for the pressure increase of 0.74 mmHg per centimeter of water depth affecting external water pressure on the goggles17. Consequently, unaltered SG, coupled with external water pressure effects, might contribute to a more substantial IOP rise. Additionally, our assessments occurred without physical activity, emphasizing the importance of recognizing potential variations in IOP and anterior segment parameters linked to exercise conditions, considering factors like exercise intensity, participant fitness, and measurement timing18,19. A prospective resolution involves adopting Triggerfish (Sensimed), a novel eye pressure-sensing contact lens, for evaluating IOP changes during swimming20. Lastly, our study was conducted on a relatively small sample size of healthy participants, lacks direct applicability to patients with conditions like glaucoma, keratoconus, or those who underwent corneal refractive surgery. Therefore, further research involving larger and more diverse populations, including those with glaucoma or other ocular conditions, is necessary to provide a more comprehensive understanding.

In conclusion, our study indicated that wearing SG results in an acute IOP rise, decrease in CCT, ACA, and ACD, and elongation of AL which all return to baseline immediately after removal. However, wearing SG had no significant effect on the OCT parameters of ONH in the acute phase. The pressure induced by SG on orbital tissues is the plausible reason, and long-term results are needed to enlighten the potential effect on ONH morphology.
