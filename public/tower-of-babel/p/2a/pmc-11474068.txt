LAMTOR1 ablation impedes cGAS degradation caused by chemotherapy and promotes antitumor immunity
Authors: Juntao Bie, Yutong Li, Chen Song, Qiaoyou Weng, Long Zhao, Li Su, Zhongwei Zhao, Yingjiang Ye, Zhanlong Shen, Jiansong Ji, Jianyuan Luo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceIn this manuscript, we present a mechanism for regulating the cGAS–STING pathway during chemotherapy, shedding light on the mechanism of chemotherapy resistance. We find that DNA stimulus induced by chemotherapeutic agents leads to elevated expression of LAMTOR1 and promotes LAMTOR1 interacting with cGAS, facilitating the degradation of cGAS in lysosomes with the help of p62. Importantly, our study demonstrates that the absence of LAMTOR1 enhances the efficacy of both chemotherapy and immunotherapy by reactivating cGAS activity. These findings open up a promising strategy for enhancing chemotherapy efficacy via targeting LAMTOR1.

Since the first use of nitrogen mustards to treat cancer in 1940s, chemotherapy has become one of the most common strategies against cancer and developed spectacularly over the past decades. Although the discovery of Bcr-Abl kinase inhibitors led chemotherapy into the era of targeted therapy, the exclusive or combined application of classic chemotherapy, which causes robust DNA damage and apoptosis is still widely used to treat the hematologic malignancies as well as a wide range of solid tumors. In addition, neoadjuvant chemotherapy is now commonly used for breast, ovarian, and rectal cancers to improve surgical outcome. However, drug resistance poses the most pressing dilemmas in chemotherapy, limiting its long-time benefits. Despite well-studied mechanisms such as tumor burden and tumor heterogeneity (1), whether cancer cells could manipulate the tumor microenvironment to impair the efficacy of chemotherapy remains elusive.

cyclic-GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) was initially identified as a pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP), responsible for sensing dsDNA from pathogens and catalyzes the synthesis of cyclic guanosine monophosphate–adenosine monophosphate (cGAMP) from adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and guanosine triphosphate (GTP) (2, 3). As a second messenger, cGAMP binds to and activates the stimulator of interferon genes (STING), promoting the translocation of STING from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) to the ER-Golgi intermedia compartments, where STING sequentially activates TANK binding kinase 1 (TBK1) and interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) (4). Phosphorylated IRF3 subsequently translocates into the nucleus and activates the transcription of type I Interferons (IFNs) and IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs) (5), pivotal in activating innate immunity to combat pathogens (6). In particular, an increasing body of evidence suggests that cGAS plays an indispensable role in regulating antitumor immunity through recognizing the cytosolic dsDNA that results from the intrinsic chromosomal instability of cancer cells or the severe genomic stress caused by chemotherapeutic agents or radiation (7). Activated cGAS promotes the production of type I IFNs and ISGs, which function in maintaining natural killer (NK) cells killing of tumor cells (8), activating Dendritic cells (DCs) (8, 9), and facilitating CD8+ T lymphocyte infiltration in tumors (10). Moreover, the cGAS–STING pathway can induce senescence, serving as an obstacle to tumor progression (11, 12).

LAMTOR1, named for its involvement in MAPK and mTORC1 pathway regulation, primarily localized at the late endosome/lysosome membrane (13). It was well studied as a hinge protein of the pentameric regulator complex, functioning as a guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF) for the rag GTPases, thereby signaling amino acid level to the mTORC1 complex (14). Besides, LAMTOR1 deficiency leads to aberrant intracellular distribution and morphology of late endosomes and lysosomes (15, 16). Recently, increasing evidence indicated the potential role for LAMTOR1 in immune response modulation. LAMTOR1-deficient CD4+ T cells exhibited significant reduction in proliferation and IL-2 production, while Th17 cell polarization and the suppressive effects of Treg cells were also diminished upon LAMTOR1 loss (17). Furthermore, the amino acid-stimulated LAMTOR1/v-ATPase/mTORC1 axis was critically required in liver X receptor (LXR)-mediated M2 macrophage polarization (18). LAMTOR1 could also suppress the transcription factor EB (TFEB) activation via mTORC1, thereby inhibiting the transcription of various proinflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-6 (19).

In this study, we found that LAMTOR1 mediates cGAS degradation via the p62–lysosome axis in response to chemotherapy. LAMTOR1 ablation rescued the decreased protein levels of cGAS triggered by chemotherapeutic agents-induced DNA fragments, which significantly enhanced antitumor efficiency of chemotherapy in colorectal and breast cancer, and immunotherapy in melanoma, by promoting the infiltration of effective T lymphocytes. Our findings unveil a mechanism governing cGAS abundance during chemotherapy and suggest a potential strategy for overcoming chemotherapy resistance by modulating LAMTOR1 amount or LAMTOR1–cGAS interaction.

Results

cGAS Is Down-Regulated Upon Chemotherapy.

We investigated the protein levels of cGAS in rectal cancer and breast cancer samples grouped by whether the patients received chemotherapy treatment using Immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis. The results showed that cGAS staining intensity was significantly decreased after chemotherapy treatment both in rectal cancer samples (Fig. 1 A and B) and in breast cancer samples (Fig. 1 C and D), which indicated the decline of cGAS protein levels. In addition, cGAS protein levels were also found to be down-regulated in rectal and breast tumors from chemotherapy-treated patients by western blot (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and B). cGAMP content was also found to remain unchanged or down-regulated in the tumors treated with chemotherapy (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C), which indicated the loss of cGAS activation upon chemotherapy. However, cGAS mRNA levels were up-regulated in chemotherapy-treated rectal or breast tumors (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D). To verify the results recapitulated from clinical tumor tissues, mice were inoculated with 4T1 or MC38 tumor cells and given 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) and the tumor volumes and weights were in line with the treatments (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 E–H). The harvested tumors were ground in liquid nitrogen to evaluate protein and mRNA levels of cGAS (Fig. 1E). While cGAS protein levels were lower in the tumors exposed to 5-FU (Fig. 1F), the mRNA levels were higher in the 5-FU administrated groups (SI Appendix, Fig. S1I). To further verify the findings, we examined the cGAS levels in various cancer cell lines incubated with chemotherapeutic agents for 24 h and then released in fresh medium for 0 to 3 d (SI Appendix, Fig. S1J). As expected, cGAS protein levels gradually decreased during the release time (Fig. 1G and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 K–N). However, the mRNA levels of cGAS remained up-regulated during the treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 O and P), indicating that there could be a degradation process for cGAS triggered by chemotherapeutic agents. Since the exogenous DNA damage caused by chemotherapy is able to activate cGAS (20), we investigated whether DNA stimulation could directly induce cGAS degradation. Thus, we transfected HeLa cells with exogenous Herring testis DNA (HT-DNA) and found that the protein levels of cGAS decreased in a time-dependent manner after transfection (Fig. 1H), whereas the mRNA levels of cGAS increased (SI Appendix, Fig. S1Q). To explore whether lysosome or proteasome is involved in the degradation of cGAS, cancer cells were treated with 5-FU combined with MG132 or Bafilomycin A1 (Baf A1), which respectively inhibit the proteasome or lysosome-mediated degradation. The results showed that only Baf A1 dampened the degradation of cGAS induced by chemotherapeutic agents (Fig. 1I and SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). The immunofluorescent staining results also showed that cGAS gradually entered into lysosome following 5-FU, etoposide, and dsDNA stimuli (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 B–G). To confirm these findings, we constructed a lysosome immunoprecipitation system (21) to detect the amount of cGAS protein in lysosomes. Upon 5-FU treatment or HT-DNA transfection, cGAS was increasingly enriched in the lysosome in a time-dependent manner (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 H and I). However, the accumulation of cGAS into lysosome was not observed after cGAMP treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S2J). Also, 5-FU or etoposide was unable to increase the total amount of lysosomes or canonical autophagy capacity (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A–C). In conclusion, our results demonstrated that chemotherapeutic agents-induced cytoplasmic DNA-triggered cGAS degradation via a selective lysosome pathway.

DNA Fragments Enhance cGAS–LAMTOR1 Interaction.

Since the lysosomal degradation of cGAS induced by chemotherapeutic agents has not been reported, we applied the immunoprecipitation/mass spectrometry analysis of cGAS interaction partners to dig out the lysosomal proteins which could bring cGAS into lysosome upon 5-FU treatment (Fig. 2A), and the gene ontology analysis of interacting proteins was performed (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A and B). Overlapping those proteins whose interaction with cGAS was enhanced after 5-FU treatment with defined lysosomal proteins revealed four candidates, which were AP5M1, ARL8A, ATP6V0A2, LAMTOR1 respectively (Fig. 2B). Since LAMTOR1 has been reported involving in lysosome autophagic function (22), we speculated that LAMTOR1 might count for chemotherapeutic agents induced cGAS degradation via lysosome. We first validated the direct interaction between LAMTOR1 and cGAS through endogenous and exogenous coimmunoprecipitation (Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Fig. S4C) and GST pull-down assays (SI Appendix, Fig. S4D). We further constructed different truncations of cGAS and LAMTOR1 respectively, and through GST pull-down assay, we validated that only the C-terminal of LAMTOR1 was able to bind with cGAS, whereas every domain of cGAS could interact with LAMTOR1 (Fig. 2 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S4E). To understand how cGAS and LAMTOR1 fit together, we further performed the molecular docking between LAMTOR1 and cGAS, which indicated that the L174, R339, and Q473 residues of cGAS could interact with W102, Y140, Q145, and R147 residues of LAMTOR1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 F and G). Mutation of any one of these amino acid residues to alanine weakened the interaction of cGAS and LAMTOR1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 H and I). To evaluate the binding affinity between cGAS and LAMTOR1, microscale thermophoresis (MST) assay was performed, which revealed that the dissociation constant (Kd) between cGAS and LAMTOR1 is 15.6 nM (SI Appendix, Fig. S4J). Notably, we found that both chemotherapeutic agents and dsDNA stimulation could promote LAMTOR1–cGAS interaction (Fig. 2 F–J and SI Appendix, Fig. S4 K–O), while cGAMP did not exhibit such effect (SI Appendix, Fig. S4P). Next, we generated K279E/K282E and K427E/K428E mutated cGAS constructs which were previously reported to diminish the cGAS–DNA complex formation (23). The results showed that the association of LAMTOR1 with mutated cGAS or enrichment of mutant cGAS into lysosomes could not be promoted by DNA fragments (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A–D). In addition, chemotherapeutic agents or HT-DNA no longer induced degradation of the two cGAS mutants whether LAMTOR1 was absence or not (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 E and F). In collection, these data revealed that the lysosomal protein LAMTOR1 was an interactor of cGAS, and the interaction was positively mediated by DNA fragments.

LAMTOR1 Mediates DNA Fragments-Induced cGAS Degradation.

Since LAMTOR1 was located on the surface of lysosome, we speculated that LAMTOR1 was able to induce the lysosomal degradation of cGAS. We compared the wild-type and LAMTOR1 knockdown 4T1 tumors from BALB/c mice and found that LAMTOR1 deficient tumors exhibited impaired cGAS degradation triggered by 5-FU treatment (Fig. 3A). Consistently, in B16F10 and HeLa cells, knocking down LAMTOR1 was able to dampen cGAS degradation due to 5-FU treatment (Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). Besides, silencing LAMTOR1 in various cancer cells led to higher cGAS protein levels (Fig. 3C and SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). Reversely, cGAS abundance decreased in response to LAMTOR1 overexpression in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3D). To determine the protein dynamic alteration of cGAS, the cycloheximide (CHX) chase assay was performed, which demonstrated that loss of LAMTOR1 prolonged half-life of cGAS (Fig. 3 E and F, and SI Appendix, Fig. S6C), whereas overexpression of LAMTOR1 shortened half-life of cGAS (SI Appendix, Fig. S6D). Unexpectedly, besides the decrease of cGAS protein level, we found that both protein and mRNA levels of LAMTOR1 were markedly up-regulated in chemotherapeutic agents stimulated tumors (Fig. 3 G–J and SI Appendix, Fig. S6 E-G) and cancer cells (Fig. 3K and SI Appendix, Fig. S6 H–K). In addition, dsDNA transfection was able to induce LAMTOR1 mRNA and protein levels (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 L and M). Additionally, IHC and immunoblot analysis of human colorectal cancer samples reveled a strongly negative correlation of cGAS and LAMTOR1 expression (Fig. 3 L–N). According to the prediction of GeneCards website, IRF2 might be the transcription factor of LAMTOR1. By real-time PCR assay, we found that IRF2 mRNA level was also up-regulated by 5-FU or HT-DNA treatment. And silencing IRF2 by siRNA reduced not only basal but also 5-FU or HT-DNA-induced LAMTOR1 transcription (SI Appendix, Fig. S6N). The result suggested that IRF2 might account for DNA stimulus-induced LAMTOR1 transcription. Moreover, LAMTOR1 knock-down or 5-FU treatment had little influence on canonical autophagy capacity or mTORC1 and TFEB activities (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 O–Q). Taken together, DNA fragments not only strengthened cGAS–LAMTOR1 interaction but also up-regulated LAMTOR1 expression to promote selective degradation of cGAS by lysosomes.

LAMTOR1 Targets cGAS for Selective Autophagy Through Interacting with p62.

To further investigate the mechanisms of cGAS degradation through LAMTOR1, we first confirmed that LAMTOR1-mediated degradation of cGAS was dependent on the lysosome pathway. We treated cells with lysosome inhibitors chloroquine (CQ) and Bafilomycin A1 (Baf A1) or proteasome inhibitor MG132 and found that only Baf A1 and CQ could dampen the degradation of cGAS induced by LAMTOR1 overexpression (Fig. 4A and SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A and B), while MG132 could not (SI Appendix, Fig. S7C). Overexpressed LAMTOR1 brought more cGAS into lysosome (Fig. 4 B–D), while knocking down LAMTOR1 prevented cGAS from entering into lysosome upon 5-FU or HT-DNA stimulation (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 D and E). Considering that cGAS degradation by lysosomes is also regulated by its ubiquitination (24, 25), we evaluated the influence of LAMTOR1 or chemotherapeutic agents on ubiquitination level of cGAS. The result revealed that both had almost no effect on ubiquitination of cGAS (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 F and G). However, as a lysosome-localized protein, LAMTOR1 had not been reported to participate in the selective autophagy pathway of lysosomes. Therefore, we inferred that LAMTOR1 might function as a scaffold protein linking cGAS and its receptor for selective autophagy. Through immunoprecipitation/mass spectrometry analysis, we overlapped LAMTOR1 interacting proteins (SI Appendix, Fig. S7H) and cGAS interacting proteins (Fig. 2A). Notably, SQSTM1 (p62), a recently reported as a selective degradation receptor of cGAS (25) was identified (Fig. 4E). We first validated the interaction between LAMTOR1 and p62 via coimmunoprecipitation assay (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 I and J). Further, the interaction between cGAS and p62 could be significantly strengthened by LAMTOR1 overexpression in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4F). In line with it, the immunofluorescent staining assay showed augmented colocalization of cGAS and p62 upon LAMTOR1 overexpression (Fig. 4 G and H). On the contrary, loss of LAMTOR1 weakened cGAS and p62 interaction (Fig. 4I). Accordingly, knocking down p62 blocked cGAS degradation induced by LAMTOR1 (Fig. 4J) or chemotherapeutic agents in vivo (Fig. 4K). Also, p62 deficiency dampened the chemotherapeutic agent-induced enrichment of cGAS in lysosomes (SI Appendix, Fig. S7K). As the first three amino acids of LAMTOR1 sequence, glycine–cysteine–cysteine (GCC) has been reported to be vital for the lysosomal localization of LAMTOR1 (26, 27). LAMTOR1 with GCC deletion was dispersive in cells and showed less merge with cGAS (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 A and B). Moreover, the GCC deletion construct of LAMTOR1 could no longer promote cGAS degradation (SI Appendix, Fig. S8C), which suggested that lysosome localization of LAMTOR1 was indispensable for its role in cGAS degradation. Moreover, overexpression of LAMTOR1-Flag WT or the GCC deletion construct had little influence on mTORC1 activities (SI Appendix, Fig. S8D). Also, we found that FIP200 or ATG5 knockdown abrogated cGAS degradation induced by LAMTOR1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 E and F). These results indicated that lysosome-localized LAMTOR1 bridged cGAS and p62, and facilitated degradation of cGAS through selective autophagy.

Loss of LAMTOR1 Enhances the cGAS–STING-IFN-β Pathway.

Herring testis DNA (HT-DNA) is a potent and common activator of the cGAS–STING-IRF3-IFN-β pathway. As LAMTOR1 could regulate cGAS abundance, we examined whether LAMTOR1 also participated in the activation of the cGAS–STING pathway. We transfected HT-DNA into HeLa cells with LAMTOR1 overexpression, and found that LAMTOR1 suppressed HT-DNA induced IFN-β production (Fig. 5A) and reduced the phosphorylation levels of TBK1 and IRF3 (Fig. 5B). Also, the luciferase assay revealed that LAMTOR1 overexpression reduced cGAS-dependent transcription of IFN-β promoter (SI Appendix, Fig. S9A). Conversely, loss of LAMTOR1 enhanced HT-DNA induced IFN-β and CXCL10 production in MC38, B16F10, and HeLa cells (Fig. 5C and SI Appendix, Fig. S9 B and C). LAMTOR1 deficiency also promoted HT-DNA-triggered phosphorylation of TBK1 and IRF3 in HeLa and B16F10 cells (Fig. 5 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S9D). Besides, LAMTOR1 knock-down enhanced HT-DNA induced luciferase reporter activity of Interferon-Stimulated Response Element (ISRE) (SI Appendix, Fig. S9E). Classic chemotherapeutic agents, such as 5-FU, etoposide, and cisplatin (cDDP), have been reported to activate the cGAS–STING pathway in cancer cells via cytosolic leakage of nuclear DNA (28, 29). We found that loss of LAMTOR1 boosted the activation of the cGAS–STING pathway stimulated by 5-FU (Fig. 5 F and G) and etoposide (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 F and G), as well as the production of IFN-β (Fig. 5 H and I) and CXCL10 (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 H and I) induced by 5-FU, etoposide, and cDDP treatments. By immunofluorescent imaging, we found that 5-FU or etoposide could induce the formation of cGAS puncta comprising dsDNA (SI Appendix, Fig. S9J). The immunofluorescent staining of cGAS showed that ISD or chemotherapeutic agents could induce more puncta of cGAS when LAMTOR1 was knocked down (Fig. 5 J and K and SI Appendix, Fig. S9 K and L), while the intensity of dsDNA staining remained unchanged upon LAMTOR1 knock-down (SI Appendix, Fig. S9M), which directly explained why loss of LAMTOR1 was able to enhance cGAS activities upon DNA fragments stimulation. RNA-sequencing results of Scramble and shLAMTOR1 B16F10 cells also indicated that LAMTOR1 deficiency promoted type I interferon-mediated signaling pathway (SI Appendix, Fig. S10A). In addition, cGAS knock-out reversed the hyperphosphorylation of TBK1 and IRF3, as well as the increased mRNA levels of IFN-β and CXCL10, which were caused by the loss of LAMTOR1 (Fig. 5L and SI Appendix, Fig. S10B). Considering the involvement of lysosomes in cGAS degradation, we evaluated the influence of lysosome inhibitor on cGAS–STING pathway activation. The results showed that lysosomes inhibitor CQ and Baf A1 could boost cGAS–STING pathway activation and up-regulate IFN-β transcription (Fig. 5M and SI Appendix, Fig. S10C), which showed little correlation with STING protein level (SI Appendix, Fig. S10D). To figure out LAMTOR1 function alone or as a member of Ragulator complex in mediating cGAS degradation, we knocked down LAMTOR1, LAMTOR2, or LAMTOR3 and demonstrated that only silencing of LAMTOR1 but not the other components of Ragulator complex could up-regulate cGAS abundance and boost cGAS-STING pathway activation (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 E–G). In order to examine whether cGAS synthase activity is impaired upon interaction with LAMTOR1, we performed an in vitro cGAMP synthesis assay, to compare the cGAS enzymatical activity between cGAS alone and cGAS/LAMTOR1 coincubation groups, or compare enzymatical activity of cGAS from whole cell lysate and lysosomes. The results indicated that cGAS synthase activity remains unchanged upon interaction with LAMTOR1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 H and I). In summary, LAMTOR1 but not Ragulator complex negatively regulated the activation of the cGAS signaling pathway and IFN-β production induced by cytosolic DNA.

LAMTOR1 Promotes Tumor Growth via Impeding Antitumor Immunity.

Since cGAS has been reported to play a negative role in tumor progression (30, 31), we examined whether LAMTOR1 could also function in tumor development by regulating cGAS abundance. Although knocking down LAMTOR1 had little influence on the growth of cancer cells in vitro (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 A–D), the B16F10, MC38, or 4T1 cells derived tumors in mice demonstrated that LAMTOR1 ablation significantly impaired tumor volume (Fig. 6A and SI Appendix, Fig. S11 E and G) and tumor weight (Fig. 6B and SI Appendix, Fig. S11 F and H). However, the tumor suppressive effects of LAMTOR1 deficiency were almost abrogated when these cancer cells were inoculated into immunodeficient mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 I and J), suggesting that LAMTOR1 regulating the tumor growth depended on the immune system of the hosts. Moreover, LAMTOR1 knocking down in B16F10 and 4T1 cells significantly extended survival time of the tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 6C and SI Appendix, Fig. S11K). Also, cGAMP content was higher in the LAMTOR1-deficient B16F10 tumors, which indicated stronger activation of the cGAS–STING pathway (Fig. 6D). However, there was no significant difference in tumor growth between control and LAMTOR1 knock-down cells when cGAS was also knocked out (Fig. 6 E and F). Besides, anti-IFNAR1 antibodies could significantly rescue growth of LAMTOR1-deficient tumors (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 L and M). We also evaluated mTORC1 activation in LAMTOR1 knock-down tumors, which showed no significant difference with the control group (SI Appendix, Fig. S11N). These results demonstrated that the antitumor effects of LAMTOR1 deficiency depended on the cGAS–STING pathway but not mTORC1 pathway. We further explored The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) cohorts for the correlation between LAMTOR1 expression levels and the survival time of patients. Consistently, lower expression of LAMTOR1 is associated with better outcomes in colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) and rectum adenocarcinoma (READ) (SI Appendix, Fig. S12A). Also, we found that the tumors with higher expression of LAMTOR1 were more likely to exhibit worse response to chemotherapy by analyzing the medical record and IHC results of LAMTOR1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S12B). Analysis of TCGA database also showed that LAMTOR1 expression is relatively higher in tumor tissue of various types of cancers, including COAD and READ (Fig. 6G and SI Appendix, S12 C and D). In consistence, the IHC analysis of human colorectal cancer samples also demonstrated that LAMTOR1 expression was higher in tumor tissues compared to the adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 6 H and I and SI Appendix, Fig. S12E). Taken together, all these results demonstrated that LAMTOR1 promoted tumor growth in vivo.

cGAS has been demonstrated to play an important role in regulating adaptive immunity, such as T lymphocytes recruitment and differentiation (31–33). As we have shown that LAMTOR1 negatively regulated cGAS protein levels and activation, we therefore further examined the role of LAMTOR1 in antitumor immunity, such as the regulation of effective T cell infiltration. We performed immunofluorescent staining and flow cytometry assay of the harvested tumor, and found that the percentages of the tumor-infiltrating CD3+, CD4+, and CD8+ T cells all increased when LAMTOR1 was knocked down in B16F10 or MC38 tumor cells (Fig. 6 J–L and SI Appendix, Figs. S12 F–H and S13 A and B). Integrative analysis of TCGA database revealed that LAMTOR1 expression positively correlated with protumor Treg cells enrichment while negatively correlated with antitumor CD8+ T cells, T helper cells, and other immune cells across various types of cancers (SI Appendix, Fig. S13 C–E). In conclusion, loss of LAMTOR1 enhanced antitumor immunity at least by increasing the effective T cell infiltration in tumors.

LAMTOR1 Deficiency Synergizes with Immunotherapy and Chemotherapy.

As LAMTOR1 knocking down exhibited a potential ability to convert cold tumors into hot tumors, we speculated that deficiency in LAMTOR1 could synergize with the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy. Indeed, LAMTOR1 knock-down B16F10 tumors treated with anti-PD-1 antibodies exhibited the slowest tumor growth (Fig. 7A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S14 A and B) along with the highest percentage of effective T cell infiltration (Fig. 7C and SI Appendix, Fig. S14 C–E). According to the established algorithm (34), we found that lower expression of LAMTOR1 is associated with better outcomes in tumors treated with anti-PD-1 antibodies (SI Appendix, Fig. S14F).

cGAS has been reported to be essential to the chemotherapy-activated immune response (28, 31). As we found that the cGAS-IRF3-IFN-β pathway was hyperactive in LAMTOR1-deficient tumor cells exposed to chemotherapeutic agents (Fig. 5), we logically assumed that tumors with lower protein levels of LAMTOR1 should benefit more from chemotherapy. The results showed that 5-FU exhibited an enhanced antitumor efficacy in the mice bearing the LAMTOR1 deficient MC38 or 4T1 tumors (Fig. 7 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S14 G and H). In addition, the mice inoculated with MC38 cells with LAMTOR1 deficiency and received 5-FU administration had the longest survival time (Fig. 7F). Through the flow cytometry and immunofluorescent staining, we found that 5-FU also triggered markedly higher infiltration of CD3+, CD4+, CD8+ T cells in LAMTOR1 deficient MC38 tumors (SI Appendix, Fig. S15 A–C). These results demonstrated that combining immunotherapy or chemotherapy with LAMTOR1 knocking down in cancer cells could prevent immune evasion due to cGAS degradation and provided a strategy to overcome the immunotherapy and chemotherapy failure.

Discussion

Although targeted therapy and immunotherapy have been developed, chemotherapy is still widely used in clinical practice. However, chemotherapy resistance remains a major challenge in the treatment of cancers, highlighting the need to investigate how cancer cells develop resistance to chemotherapy and to devise novel strategies to overcome it. In this study, we demonstrated that LAMTOR1 expression was up-regulated following chemotherapy, and its interaction with cGAS was also strengthened, which in turn promoted p62-mediated degradation of cGAS via selective autophagy in cancer cells. More importantly, our findings provided a potential strategy for overcoming chemotherapy resistance by impeding cGAS degradation mediated by LAMTOR1– p62–lysosome.

The pieces of evidence provided in this study demonstrate that LAMTOR1 negatively regulated cGAS abundance and suppresses the activation of the cGAS–STING pathway. Therefore, either down-regulating the expression levels of LAMTOR1 or disrupting the interaction between cGAS and LAMTOR1 could offer a novel and potent strategy to enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy and chemotherapy in treating cancers. In recent years, proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) have become a universal strategy to target protein for proteasomal degradation. Therefore, the specific PROTAC system could be designed for LAMTOR1 destruction especially in cancer cells, potentially offering greater benefits and fewer side-effects. Alternatively, screening for the small molecules based on the interaction features of cGAS and LAMTOR1 to disrupt their interaction could prevent cGAS degradation via the LAMTOR1-lysosome pathway. In the current study, deletion of the first three amino acids (GCC) of LAMTOR1 led to its scattered distribution in cells and disabled LAMTOR1 from mediating cGAS degradation by lysosomes, which manifested the necessity of LAMTOR1’s lysosomal localization in the degradative process (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 A–C). Indeed, N-myristoyltransferase-1 (NMT1) catalyzes N-terminal myristoylation of LAMTOR1 at the glycine residue (Gly 2), which is indispensable for the lysosomal membrane localization of LAMTOR1 (13, 35). Moreover, NMT1 inhibitors not only reduce lysosome localization of LAMTOR1 but also promote its ubiquitination-mediated degradation (35, 36). Considering the dual roles of NMT1 inhibitors in modulating LAMTOR1, they might regulate lysosomal degradation of cGAS through influencing the localization and protein abundance of LAMTOR1. Further studies are required to evaluate the efficacy of NMT1 inhibitors in the cGAS–STING–IFN-β pathway and antitumor immunity.

The results of this study showed that LAMTOR1 knockdown cancer cells recruited more effective T cells in the tumor immune niche (Fig. 6). In addition to T lymphocytes, type I interferons also impact NK cells, DCs, and other immune cells (8, 9). Therefore, comprehensive investigations such as single-cell sequencing or multiplexed immunohistochemistry are necessary to understand the integrated immune network remodeling due to LAMTOR1 deficiency.

Our study clearly demonstrates that LAMTOR1 facilitates degradation of cGAS in lysosome with the assistance of p62 and DNA fragments. Besides chemotherapeutic agents, radiation and PARP inhibitors have also been reported to induce DNA damage and cytosolic micronuclei formation (37, 38). Therefore, whether radiotherapy and PARP inhibitors have similar effects as chemotherapy remains to be elucidated.

Using the lysosome immunoprecipitation system mentioned above, we conducted lysosomal proteomics of cancer cells exposed to chemotherapeutic agents. Intriguingly, not only cGAS but also other regulators and effectors of innate immunity such as STING, DDX3X, and ISG15 were enriched in lysosome upon the treatment with 5-FU, which shows that lysosomal degradation of proteins involved in innate immunity during chemotherapy might be a general phenomenon. Consistently, Zhao et al. showed that lysosome inhibitor chloroquine (CQ) could enhance antitumor immunity (39). By inhibiting the degradation of cGAS and other type-I-IFN-related proteins, lysosome inhibitors may rescue the tumor immune microenvironments impaired by chemotherapeutic agents, indicating possible synergistic effects of lysosome inhibitors and chemotherapy in cancer treatment.

Taken together, our results demonstrated that LAMTOR1–p62–cGAS interaction leads to cGAS degradation via selective autophagy triggered by chemotherapeutic stimulation in cancer cells, significantly weakening the efficacy of chemotherapy. By manipulating this axis, new drugs or strategies could be developed to overcome chemotherapy resistance and treat cancers more efficiently.

Methods

Treatment to Cultured Cells with Chemotherapeutic Agents.

Cells were seeded in dishes and cultured until reaching proper confluence. Then cells were treated with 5-FU (100 μM), etoposide (20 μM), paclitaxel (25 nM), or cDDP (4 μM) for 24 h and released for indicated days before harvesting.

Luciferase Assay and type I IFN Bioassay.

HEK 293T were seeded in a six-well plate and cultured until the confluence reached 70%. Then, the cells were transfected with 500 ng of the IFNβ-luci reporter plasmid, 500 ng pRL-TK, and equal amount of various expression plasmids or empty control plasmids. After 24 h, the reporter gene activity was measured via the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) and normalized by Renilla luciferase activity.

Relative amount of type I IFNs in HeLa culture medium was measured with the 2fTGH-ISRE cell line stably expressing an ISRE-Luci reporter. After transfection with HT-DNA for 24h, 2mL HeLa culture medium was incubated with 2fTGH-ISRE-Luci cells which were seeded in a 6-well plate in advance for 8 h. Later, cells were lysed with passive lysis buffer and subjected to luciferase quantification kit (Promega).

Lysosome Immunoprecipitation.

The lysosome-localized protein TMEM192-3HA was stably overexpressed in HeLa or MC38 cells. These cells were treated cells with 5-FU (Sigma-Aldrich) or HT-DNA for different days and the cells were harvested and homogenized to collect the mixture containing intact lysosomes by centrifuging at 1,000×g. Anti-HA Magnetic Beads (Pierce) were incubated with the mixture and rotated for 50 min at 4 °C. The beads were washed with KPBS (136 mM KCl and 10 mM KH2PO4 pH 7.25) using DynaMag™ (Invitrogen) and eluted with RIPA.

Mass Spectrometry Analysis.

HEK 293T cells were transfected with cGAS-Flag or LAMTOR1-Flag plasmids and treated as indicated. The cells were harvested and subjected to immunoprecipitation using anti-Flag M2 beads. Then the interacting partners of cGAS or LAMTOR1 were identified by mass spectrometry.

GST Pull-Down Assay.

cGAS–GST protein was induced by Isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG, 1 mg/mL) and purified using GST·Bind Resin (Novagen). The beads with cGAS–GST binding were incubated with LAMTOR1-Flag protein solution and rotated overnight at 4°C. Then beads were washed for several times with lysis buffer and eluted by boiling the beads in 1X loading buffer.

In Vitro cGAMP Synthesis Assay.

His-tagged cGAS was purchased from MedChemExpress (HY-P72337) and His-tagged LAMTOR1 was purchased from CUSABIO (CSB-EP757083HU). Purified recombinant human cGAS protein (0.05 μg) was incubated with HT-DNA (1 μg) in a reaction buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5; 5 mM MgCl2; 2 mM ATP and 2 mM GTP) at 37 °C for 1.5 h, with or without purified recombinant human LAMTOR1 protein (0.25 μg). Then, the cGAMP contents in the samples were determined using 2'3'-cGAMP ELISA Kit (Cayman) according to the manufacturer’s instruction.

As for enzymatic activity measurement of cGAS from whole lysate or lysosomes, the same amount cGAS protein was taken according to the results of western blot followed by intro vitro cGAMP synthesis assay.

Measurement of cGAMP Abundance.

The tumors were ground in nitrogen and lysed in M-PER™ Mammalian Protein Extraction Reagent (Thermo Scientific) to obtain cell lysate followed by protein concentration measurement. According to the lowest concentration of the samples, all the samples were adjusted to the same protein concentration followed by measurement of cGAMP abundance using the 2'3'-cGAMP ELISA Kit (Cayman) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. The results were then normalized according to the protein concentration of these samples.
