Charge-dependent localization of Toll-like receptor 5 at the plasma membrane
Authors: Ji-Won Huh, Kwangcheon Park, Hyun-Sup Song, Seongryong Kim, You-Me Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractProper subcellular localization of Toll-like receptors (TLRs) is essential for initiating appropriate innate immune responses against pathogens while avoiding self-reactivity. Unc-93 homolog B1 (UNC93B1) is known to mediate the intracellular trafficking of nucleotide-sensing TLRs such as TLR9 which undergoes rapid internalization into endolysosomes upon reaching the cell surface. We previously demonstrated that UNC93B1 also facilitates the plasma membrane localization of TLR5, a sensor for bacterial flagellin. Unlike TLR9, TLR5 remained stably at the cell surface under steady-state conditions, suggesting the involvement of distinct sorting mechanisms. Using mutagenesis-based approaches, we found that the cytoplasmic domain of TLR5 is required for its surface retention, whereas the cytoplasmic domain of TLR9 is dispensable for internalization. Notably, TLR5 contains polybasic residues in its C-terminal region, absent in other TLRs. Deletion or alanine substitution of these residues led to constitutive endocytosis of TLR5. Conversely, appending the TLR5 C-terminal region to the C-terminus of TLR9 promoted its surface accumulation. Moreover, when the TLR5 C-terminal sequence was fused to a cytosolic protein along with a myristoylation motif, it mediated membrane association of the cytosolic protein in a charge-dependent manner. We further found that this region can directly interact with phosphatidic acid, an anionic phospholipid enriched in the plasma membrane. These findings reveal an electrostatic mechanism by which TLR5 is selectively retained at the plasma membrane, providing new insight into receptor-specific localization of TLRs.Keywords: Plasma membrane localization, Polybasic residues, Toll-like receptor 5, Toll-like receptor 9, UNC93B1

INTRODUCTION

Toll-like receptors (TLRs) recognize conserved pathogen–derived molecular patterns and rapidly initiate defense mechanisms such as proinflammatory cytokine production, costimulatory molecule upregulation, and immune cell proliferation (Fitzgerald and Kagan, 2020, Kawasaki and Kawai, 2014). They can also be activated by host molecules released during stress, injury, or infection (Piccinini and Midwood, 2010). Based on subcellular localization, TLRs are classified into cell surface or endolysosomal receptors (Barton and Kagan, 2009). Nucleotide-sensing TLRs, including TLR3, TLR7, and TLR9, are typically sequestered in endolysosomes to sense nucleic acids from engulfed pathogens while avoiding self-reactivity (Barton et al., 2006, Lind et al., 2022, Miyake et al., 2018). The importance of proper localization is underscored by a bone marrow transplantation experiment, where ectopic TLR9 expression at the cell surface caused fatal autoinflammation (Mouchess et al., 2011).

UNC93B1, a 12-pass transmembrane protein, escorts nucleotide-sensing TLRs from the endoplasmic reticulum to endolysosomes (Kim et al., 2008, Pelka et al., 2018). Although it binds all client TLRs via transmembrane and juxtamembrane regions, evidence suggests receptor-specific modulation of localization and signaling of individual TLRs (Brinkmann et al., 2007, Ishida et al., 2021, Kim et al., 2013, Lind et al., 2022, Miyake et al., 2018). For example, an adaptor protein 2 (AP-2) complex–binding motif in the UNC93B1 mediates the constitutive internalization of TLR9 from the cell surface into endosomes without affecting other endolysosomal TLRs (Lee et al., 2013). Moreover, a single point mutation in the UNC93B1 alters its binding preference to TLR7 and TLR9, disrupting the balance between their signaling and promoting autoimmunity (Fukui et al., 2011, Fukui et al., 2009).

We previously showed that TLR5, a cell surface receptor for bacterial flagellin, also requires UNC93B1 for endoplasmic reticulum exit and MyD88-dependent signaling (Huh et al., 2014). Unlike endosomal TLRs, which constitutively internalize, TLR5 remains at the plasma membrane until ligand engagement (Kang et al., 2020). These findings suggest that TLR5 and TLR9 are subject to distinct sorting mechanisms at the plasma membrane, despite their shared requirement for UNC93B1-mediated localization.

Here, we demonstrate that the cytoplasmic domain of TLR5 is critical for its plasma membrane retention, whereas the TLR9 cytoplasmic domain is dispensable for internalization. Notably, TLR5 contains conserved polybasic residues in cytoplasmic tail functioning as a retention signal—a feature absent in other UNC93B1-interacting TLRs. These findings reveal a previously unrecognized mechanism underlying differential TLR localization.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Mice, Cell Lines, and Reagents

C57BL/6 wild-type (WT) mice and TLR9 knockout mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratories and Oriental Bio Service, respectively. All mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions at the animal facility of Pohang University of Science and Technology with approval from the institutional ethics committee. HEK293T and RAW 264.7 cells were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS). TLR agonists, brefeldin A, and antibodies for epitope tags, tumor necrosis factor (TNF), and TLR4 were purchased from the indicated suppliers (see Supplementary Materials and Methods).

DNA Constructs

N-terminal 3xFlag and C-terminal green fluorescent protein (GFP)–tagged mouse TLR9 was generated by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) stitching. Chimeric TLRs combining ectodomains and transmembrane/cytoplasmic domains from TLR9, TLR4, and TLR5 (eg, TLR955, TLR995, TLR959, TLR499, and TLR449) were constructed using conserved cysteine residues and defined transmembrane sequences as junction points. Site-directed mutagenesis substituted lysines with arginine or alanine (K-to-R/A) in TLR9+5 and TLR5. Additionally, TLR5–derived polybasic peptides fused to an N-terminal myristoylation consensus sequence and C-terminal mCherry were generated from WT and mutant templates. All constructs were verified by sequencing.

Further information is available in the Supplementary Information.

RESULTS

The Cytoplasmic Domain of TLR5 Mediates Receptor Localization at the Plasma Membrane

To identify the domain–mediating TLR5 localization at the plasma membrane, we generated chimeric TLRs by exchanging the transmembrane and cytoplasmic domains of TLR5 and TLR9 (Supplementary Fig. S1A). As reported, TLR5 was localized at the cell surface, whereas TLR9 colocalized with the endolysosomal marker CD63 (Fig. 1A). The TLR955 chimera, comprising the TLR9 ectodomain with the transmembrane and cytoplasmic domain of TLR5, was localized at the plasma membrane, similar to TLR5. In contrast, TLR959, carrying the cytoplasmic domain of TLR9, no longer localized to the cell surface whereas TLR995 remained at the surface.

To validate these observations, a tandem Flag tag was introduced at the N-terminus of each TLR to allow quantification of receptor surface expression via flow cytometry. TLRs were coexpressed with either WT or a Y539A mutant form of UNC93B1 in human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T cells, which express minimal endogenous UNC93B1. The Y539A mutation disrupts AP-2 binding, thereby blocking constitutive internalization of the TLR9-UNC93B1 complex into endolysosomes and resulting in increased surface localization of TLR9 (Lee et al., 2013). Consistently, TLR9 and TLR959 both exhibited increased surface localization, when expressed with Y539A UNC93B1 (Fig. 1B). In contrast, surface levels of TLR955 and TLR995 remained unchanged, confirming their constitutive localization at the plasma membrane.

Unlike surface-localized TLRs, endolysosomal TLRs require an acidic environment for signaling (Barton et al., 2006). As expected, bafilomycin A, an inhibitor of endosomal acidification, significantly reduced TLR9–driven TNF-α production but not TLR5 response (Fig. 1C). The inhibitory effect of bafilomycin A was substantially attenuated in cells expressing TLR955 and completely absent in TLR995-expressing cells (Fig. 1C), indicating that TLR9 chimeras harboring the TLR5 cytoplasmic domain can signal independently of endosomal acidification, consistent with their surface localization.

The Carboxyl Tail of TLR5 Contains a Cell Surface Retention Signal

These findings suggested 2 possible mechanisms: (1) the TLR9 cytoplasmic domain carries an internalization signal, or (2) the TLR5 cytoplasmic domain contains a retention signal that prevents spontaneous internalization. To test the former, we generated TLR449 by replacing the cytoplasmic domain of TLR4 with that of TLR9 (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Like TLR4, which does not bind UNC93B1 (Brinkmann et al., 2007), TLR449 was localized at cell surface, indicating that the TLR9 cytoplasmic domain alone does not drive receptor internalization (Supplementary Fig. S1C and D). By contrast, TLR499, which retains UNC93B1 binding, was barely detected at the plasma membrane, but regained surface localization when coexpressed with Y539A UNC93B1, confirming UNC93B1-AP-2–dependent internalization. Although the TLR9 cytoplasmic domain contains putative AP-2 biding motifs (YXXΦ and [D/E]X3–4L[L/I/V]) (Ohno et al., 1995, Traub and Bonifacino, 2013), neither TLR9∆870, lacking all AP-2-binding motifs, and TLR9∆837, lacking the entire cytoplasmic domain, escaped constitutive internalization—yet both was stabilized at the surface by Y539A UNC93B1 (Supplementary Fig. S1E and F). Thus, TLR9 internalization is dependent on UNC93B1-AP2 interaction but does not require the cytoplasmic domain.

Surface localization of TLR995, which differs from TLR9∆837 only by the addition of the TLR5 cytoplasmic domain, supports the second hypothesis that the TLR5 cytoplasmic domain contains a surface retention signal and prompted us to map the retention signal. C-terminal truncation mutant TLR5∆849 and TLR995∆1027, lacking the last 10 amino acids, still localized to the cell surface. In contrast, TLR5∆830 and TLR995∆1008, which lack the last 29 residues, failed to remain at the plasma membrane (Fig. 1D and E; Supplementary Fig. S2). These findings indicate that residues 830 to 849 of TLR5 harbor the surface retention signal.

Polybasic Residues in the Carboxyl Tail of TLR5 Mediate the Receptor Retention at the Cell Surface

Alignment of UNC93B1-binding TLRs revealed variation in their carboxyl-terminal regions following the Toll/interleukin-1 receptor signature box 3 motif. Notably, human TLR5 contains 6 lysine residues, 5 of which are highly conserved across species (Supplementary Fig. S3A and B). To test whether these conserved polybasic residues act as a surface retention signal, we substituted all lysines with alanines in TLR5 and TLR995. Both lysine-to-alanine mutants exhibited markedly reduced surface expression (Fig. 1F).

We next examined whether the polybasic residues of TLR5 could prevent constitutive internalization of TLR9. To this end, the C-terminal 21 amino acids of TLR5 were fused to full-length TLR9 to generate TLR9+5 chimera, which showed strong accumulation at the cell surface (Fig. 1G and Supplementary Fig. S3C). Substitution of the lysine residues with alanines (K-to-A) in TLR9+5 restored UNC93B1-dependent internalization, which was again prevented by Y539A UNC93B1. Together, these findings establish that the conserved polybasic residues in the TLR5 cytoplasmic tail act as a charge–dependent surface retention signal that counteracts UNC93B1-mediated internalization.

Polybasic Residues in the Carboxyl Tail of TLR5 Interact With the Plasma Membrane

Polybasic residues in cytoplasmic tails often anchor membrane proteins to the plasma membrane through direct electrostatic interaction with anionic membrane lipids (Hancock et al., 1990, Heo et al., 2006, McLaughlin and Murray, 2005, Shi et al., 2025, Sigal et al., 1994, Xu et al., 2008). Structural modeling by AlphaFold predicts that the lysine–rich C-terminal extension of TLR5 projects outward from the globular Toll/interleukin-1 receptor structure as a flexible, unstructured segment (Supplementary Fig. S3D), optimally positioned to engage membrane lipids or other proteins (Jumper et al., 2021).

To directly assess the membrane-binding capacity of the TLR5 carboxyl tail, we fused it to the N-terminus of a cytosolic protein mCherry (Supplementary Fig. S3E). Because polybasic residues alone are generally insufficient to confer membrane association on cytosolic proteins, we further incorporated an N-terminal myristoylation motif from Src kinase, which relies on both myristoylation and polybasic residues for membrane targeting (Sigal et al., 1994). The TLR5 tail-mCherry fusion protein localized to the plasma membrane in more than half of cells, with additional puncta suggestive of endosomal membrane association (Fig. 1H and Supplementary Fig. S3F). Importantly, substitution of lysines with arginines largely preserved the membrane localization, whereas lysine-to-alanine substitutions abolished it, confirming the importance of electrostatic interactions.

We next synthesized a peptide corresponding to the C-terminal tail sequence, fused to a net-neutral V5 tag (Supplementary Fig. S3G). In a protein–lipid overlay assay, this peptide bound strongly to phosphatidic acid (PA) and, to a lesser extent, other anionic lipids (Fig. 1I). In contrast, lysine-to-alanine substitution abolished lipid binding, demonstrating that the polybasic residues engage anionic lipids and thereby mediate the steady–state surface retention of TLR5.

DISCUSSION

The subcellular localization of TLRs determines the types of ligands they encounter and the signaling pathways they initiate, ultimately shaping distinct immunological outcomes. Here, we demonstrate that polybasic residues in the C-terminal tail of TLR5 act as a surface retention motif that mediates charge-dependent interactions with membrane lipids and prevents constitutive internalization observed in other UNC93B1-dependent TLRs lacking such a motif (Fig. 1J). Appending the TLR5 tail to TLR9 blocked its constitutive internalization, whereas K-to-A mutation abolished, and K-to-R substitution preserved, receptor surface retention, supporting a charge-dependent mechanism. Nevertheless, precise molecular basis by which these electrostatic interactions interfere with constitutive TLR endocytosis remains to be elucidated.

Our observation that a TLR5–derived polybasic peptide drives membrane association of a cytosolic protein strongly supports its direct binding to anionic membrane lipids via electrostatic interactions. In lipid overlay assays, the TLR5-derived peptide displayed the strongest binding to PA, while it also interacted with other anionic lipids such as phosphatidylserine (PS) and phosphatidylinositol. Given that PS constitutes approximately 10% to 15% of total phospholipids in the plasma membrane inner leaflet, compared with only ∼0.25% to 1% for PA, it is plausible that PS and other abundant anionic species collectively contribute to the electrostatic association with the TLR5 polybasic motif (Kooijman and Burger, 2009, van Meer et al., 2008). Nonetheless, PA may exert a disproportionate physiological influence relative to its low abundance, owing to its capacity for localized enrichment in signaling microdomains (Wang et al., 2006).

An alternative, though not mutually exclusive, possible mechanism for TLR5 surface retention is that TLR5 polybasic residues impair recruitment of AP-2 complex to the TLR-UNC93B1 complex, thereby blocking receptor internalization. Because both the α and μ2 subunits of AP-2 interact with anionic phospholipids (Motley et al., 2006), a high local density of positive charges near the cytoplasmic interface of the TLR5-UNC93B1 complex could electrostatically hinder AP-2 engagement with either the plasma membrane or UNC93B1. Other possibilities, including tethering to unknown membrane-anchored proteins, also cannot be ruled out.

Importantly, TLR5 undergoes rapid internalization upon flagellin engagement, indicating that polybasic residue–mediated surface retention must be actively disengaged upon receptor activation. Several mechanisms may account for this transition. One possibility is lipid remodeling at the plasma membrane. Similar to TLR4, ligand stimulation of TLR5 may activate PA phosphohydrolase 1, thereby reducing plasma membrane PA levels and weakening polybasic residue–mediated membrane association (Grkovich et al., 2009). Alternatively, activation-induced phosphorylation near the polybasic residues may diminish electrostatic interactions with membrane lipids—a common mechanism regulating membrane association of polybasic residue–containing proteins (Kim et al., 1994, Shi et al., 2025). In addition, the conserved lysine residues in TLR5 may serve as potential ubiquitination sites upon flagellin stimulation. Ubiquitination is a critical determinant of receptor internalization and endosomal trafficking. Triad3A, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, has been shown to interact with the cytosolic domains of multiple TLRs and to promote degradation of TLR5, thereby attenuating flagellin-induced signaling (Chuang and Ulevitch, 2004). Thus, ubiquitination may both initiate internalization and limit signaling output by targeting receptors for degradation.

Among human TLRs, TLR5 uniquely recognizes a protein ligand—flagellin—displayed on the surface of many invasive bacteria. TLR5 is prominently expressed at the frontline barrier tissues against microbial invasion, particularly at mucosal surfaces. It is abundantly expressed in intestinal lamina propria dendritic cells, which extend processes into the gut lumen to sample motile bacteria, and on the basolateral surface of intestinal epithelial cells, where it detects pathogens breaching cell-cell junctions (Gewirtz et al., 2001, Uematsu et al., 2008). Upon flagellin detection, TLR5 not only initiates innate signaling from the plasma membrane but also mediates internalization of flagellin into endolysosomes, enabling proteolytic degradation and antigen presentation via major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II molecules, thereby linking innate and adaptive immunity (Lee et al., 2023, Letran et al., 2011). Consequently, TLR5 deficiency impairs humoral responses to flagellated bacteria, increases bacterial translocation across the intestinal barrier, and predisposes mice to colitis (Kang et al., 2020, Uematsu et al., 2008, Vijay-Kumar et al., 2007). This dual function—as a sentinel for extracellular bacteria and a carrier for antigen delivery—necessitates tightly controlled receptor trafficking. Our study identifies a unique cell surface retention mechanism for TLR5 and provides mechanistic insights into TLR intracellular trafficking that advances our understanding of innate immune recognition.
