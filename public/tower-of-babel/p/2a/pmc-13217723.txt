Financial toxicity and psychosocial distress of breast and cervical cancer patients at a tertiary healthcare facility in south-western Nigeria
Authors: Damilola A. Busari, Atara Ntekim, Yetunde O. John-Akinola, Prisca Olabisi Adejumo, Dezheng Huo, Olufunmilayo I Olopade, Mojisola M. Oluwasanu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreast and cervical cancers are leading causes of morbidity and mortality in women worldwide. The treatment and management of these illnesses are accompanied with financial toxicity and psychological distress to the patients. This study investigated financial toxicity and psychosocial distress of breast and cervical cancer patients at the University College Hospital, Ibadan, Nigeria.MethodsThis was a descriptive cross-sectional study that employed a convergent parallel, mixed-methods design. The study was conducted among 105 breast and cervical cancer patients receiving treatment between June and August, 2021. Data were obtained using a semi-structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire and in-depth interview guide. The quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive and inferential statistics, and qualitative data were analyzed using thematic analysis.ResultsThe mean age of the respondents was 48.8 ± 10.0 years, and almost half of the participants (45%) were aged between 51 and 60 years. Most of the respondents (67.6%) were breast cancer patients, while (32.4%) were cervical cancer patients. This study found that (96.2%) of the respondents experienced high financial toxicity, and (73.3%) of the respondents had a high level of psychosocial distress. Financial toxicity was the strongest predictor of psychosocial distress (Wald χ² = 87.28, p < 0.001), indicating that higher financial toxicity was associated with high psychosocial distress. Factors that contributed to the financial toxicity and psychosocial distress as deduced from the qualitative interviews were high cost of treatment, loss of income, and negative body image.ConclusionBreast and cervical cancer patients experience a high level of financial toxicity and psychosocial distress. Multi-component interventions are needed to defray costs, improve psychological well-being, reduce disparities in treatment outcomes, and improve the quality of cancer care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-026-04436-9.

Introduction

Cancer is one of the leading contributors to the global burden of diseases [1, 2]. The diagnosis of cancer and its treatment result in drastic changes in the patient’s life, such as their daily activities, work, family roles, and relationships, which results in several financial, psychological, psychosomatic, and physical problems for cancer patients [3]. The long duration of the cancer treatment, side effects of the treatment, and repeated hospitalization are some of the factors responsible for the high level of psychological stress in cancer patients, which shows up as anxiety and depression [4]. The high costs of cancer treatment, coupled with the fear of being unable to cope with the bills for treatment, fear of reoccurrence, and low chances of survival, among others, negatively impact the psychological well-being of cancer patients [5].

In Nigeria, the quality of cancer care services is affected by several challenges, such as the absence of a well-structured tumor board at the comprehensive cancer care centers and the fact that the country has only 8 public and 1 private comprehensive cancer care center and an estimated number of over 102,000 new cancer cases annually [6]. In addition, most times, these centers are nonfunctional because the radiotherapy machines are down, which further worsens the timely access to treatment in Nigeria [6].

Some other challenges in the cancer health system in Nigeria increase the psychological and financial burden of the cancer patients, and they include limited oncological facilities and radiotherapy treatment centers (when compared with the population size), limited workforce such as specialists in cancer management, difficulties in accessing oncology drugs as most pharmaceutical companies have their manufacturing plants outside Nigeria, and no active government participation in ensuring cancer screening, diagnosis, and treatment as part of citizens’ rights [7].

It is pertinent to understand the financial experiences of diverse patients’ sub-groups and quantify the burden of financial toxicity (this is a term used to describe problems a patient has related to the cost of medical care [8]) and its association with psychological responses. This is critical due to its adverse effects on treatment outcomes, recovery, post-treatment survivorship, and quality of life. This study, therefore, assessed the financial toxicity and psychosocial distress of breast and cervical cancer patients in a tertiary hospital in the southwestern region of Nigeria.

Methods

This study was a descriptive cross-sectional study that employed a convergent parallel mixed-method approach where quantitative and qualitative data were collected simultaneously, analyzed, and results integrated. The study was conducted among breast and cervical cancer patients receiving treatment from the clinic of the Radiation Oncology Department of the University College Hospital, Ibadan. The University College Hospital is a tertiary health facility with a 1,000-bed capacity. The hospital provides healthcare services for many patients, with an average of 6,500 patients annually in the Emergency department and about 150,000 new patients in the various outpatient clinics. One hundred and five breast and cervical cancer patients were recruited for this study using a consecutive patient series. The data collection process took place from the18th of June to the 13th of August 2021. All consenting breast and cervical cancer patients aged 25 years and above who have been receiving treatment for three months or more were recruited into the study. Patients that were critically ill were excluded.

A semi-structured, pre-tested, interviewer-administered questionnaire, which was adapted from the Comprehensive Score for Financial Toxicity (COST) [9, 10], the Profile of Mood States (POMS) [11], and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) [12], was used to obtain data on their socio-demographic characteristics, level of financial toxicity, psychosocial distress, associated factors, and the coping behaviors [9]. Questions on factors contributing to financial and psychosocial distress were developed from past studies findings [5, 13–16].

The scoring of the COST was adapted from the original COST scale. According to the scoring scale, the lower the score, the more severe the financial toxicity, and the higher the score, the better the financial well-being. For each item, 0 is the lowest, 4 is the highest, and 7 of the items in the scale were also reverse scored. Items 2, 3, 4, 5, 8, 9 and 10 of the COST scale were reverse scored. Financial toxicity was assessed on a 44-point scale with 0 and 44 being the highest and lowest financial toxicity, respectively. Financial toxicity was grade 0 if ≥ 26, grade 1 = (14–25), grade 2 = (1–13), and grade 3 = 0.

Grade 3 was described as the most severe financial toxicity, grade 2 as severe financial toxicity and grade 1 as less severe financial toxicity. The grading was adapted from a study conducted among radiation oncology patients using the validated COST tool to assess the financial toxicity of cancer patients [13].

The HADS is a self-report rating scale of 14 items on a 4-point Likert scale. It is designed to measure anxiety and depression (7 items for each subscale). The total score is the sum of the 14 items, and for each subscale the score is the sum of the respective seven items. The POMS originally consists of 65 items and was designed to evaluate individuals within seven different mood domains: fatigue-inertia, anger-hostility, vigor-activity, confusion-bewilderment, depression-dejection, tension-anxiety, and friendliness. The scale has been recommended for evaluating affective changes over the course of a brief treatment or assessment period. The revised abbreviated version consists of 40 items on a 4-point Likert scale.

5 items from HADs questionnaire and 5 items from the POMS questionnaire focused on evaluating anxiety & depression, as well as findings from past studies, were used to develop questions to assess the psychosocial distress of the participants in this study. The questions were modified and tailored to answer the objectives of the study. Each item was scored on a 4-point scale. The higher the score, the higher the psychosocial distress of the patient.

Psychosocial distress was assessed on a 56-point scale: ≤18, 19–36 and ≥ 37 were categorized as low, medium and high distress levels, respectively. The instrument was pretested in Lagos State University Teaching Hospital (LASUTH), which is a secondary healthcare institution that offers treatment to cancer patients like the University College Hospital, Ibadan, to assess the validity and reliability of the research instruments. The Cronbach alpha score for the psychosocial and financial distress sections were 0.848 and 0.910, respectively.

Data was analyzed on SPSS version 25, using descriptive and inferential statistics. The main outcome variable was psychosocial distress (continuous), measured using the total psychosocial distress score. Descriptive statistics were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for approximately normally distributed continuous variables, and median with interquartile range (IQR) for skewed variables. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Financial distress was highly skewed and therefore summarized using median (IQR). Psychosocial distress, which was less skewed, was summarized using mean ± SD. The association between financial toxicity (continuous) and psychosocial distress (continuous) was examined using Spearman’s rank correlation.

Differences in financial toxicity (continuous) across psychosocial distress categories (categorical: low, medium, high) were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis H test, with mean ranks reported. A generalized linear model (GLM) with a normal distribution and identity link function was used to examine factors associated with psychosocial distress, treating psychosocial distress as a continuous dependent variable. The identity link specifies a direct linear relationship between predictors and the psychosocial distress score, allowing coefficients to be interpreted on the original scale. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ten in-depth interviews were conducted using a guide to obtain different perspectives on the factors contributing to the financial toxicity and psychosocial distress of cancer patients. The interviews were conducted face-to-face in the Radiation Oncology clinic using English or Yoruba languages based on the preference of the interviewee. The interview time spanned 20–30 min. The interviews were audio-recorded and later transcribed verbatim to Word files. A deductive thematic approach was used for data analysis. A coding guide was jointly developed by the research team and used to code the qualitative data using NVIVO 12 software. A narrative approach of integration was used to present the findings.

The research was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Ibadan and University College Hospital, Nigeria (UI/EC/21/0120). The study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines and regulations of the National Health Research Committee, Nigeria. All participants provided written informed consent to participate in the study.

Factors contributing to the financial and psychosocial distresses of respondents

Some of the self-reported factors contributing to the distresses of the respondents include lack of health insurance 29 (27.8%), travelling long distances to get their cancer treatment 49 (46.7%), lower sex drive 42 (40.0%), and negative body image 49 (46.7%), details in supplementary Table 1.

All the participants were of the opinion that the financial toxicity experienced by cancer patients aggravated their psychosocial distresses. One participant said, “The high costs of treatment make most people think [become psychologically distressed] if they can get help with that, it will help relieve some of the stress.

Majority, 104 (99.0%), seek social support from their family, while over a third, 39 (37.1%), seek social support from friends. All the participants admitted to having the support of their families, and some said they also had the support of their friends. One breast cancer patient said; “My relatives have been supporting me by always being around me.” Another said; “My husband has been standing by me since I was diagnosed till now (…) he cheers me up when I am down and doesn’t let me feel sad.”

While some of the participants said they had their friends’ support, some kept the news of the illness away from their friends. One participant said; “I didn’t tell any of my friends because friends will just be spreading [sharing] the news of my illness.” Another stated that; “(…) telling friends will just make them gossip about it, so nobody aside from my family knows about it.”

All the participants admitted that their spirituality helped them cope with their psychosocial distress and stated that they didn’t seek professional support for their psychological distress. “I put all my worries and trust in God almighty to take control,” breast cancer patient said “(…) if not for God and prayers, I don’t think I would have the strength to fight this illness.

One of the participants expressed that there should be a cancer support group to help new patients; “I think it’ll be good if we can develop a cancer support group in every state; it will help those that have just been diagnosed.”

All the participants also emphasized the importance of family support on the patients’ psychosocial health.

Association between financial toxicity and psychosocial distress

Spearman’s rank correlation revealed a correlation between financial toxicity and psychosocial distress (ρ = -0.520; p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Differences in financial toxicity across levels of psychosocial distress

Financial toxicity differed significantly across psychosocial distress categories (Kruskal-Wallis H = 21.68, df = 2, p < 0.001). The mean ranks of financial toxicity scores were 102.67, 70.58, and 45.36 for participants with low, medium, and high psychosocial distress, respectively (Table 5). Since lower financial toxicity scores indicate higher financial toxicity, participants with high psychosocial distress had the highest financial toxicity, while those with low psychosocial distress had the lowest.

Discussion

The majority of the respondents experienced high financial toxicity and a correspondingly high level of psychosocial distress. This study’s financial toxicity level was higher than the findings from a similar survey in Tunisia, which was 80.4% [17]. The slight difference could be attributed to variances in study location as well as the socioeconomic and health system factors. Various factors contributed to financial toxicity; all the respondents paid for their treatment out of pocket, and most of them had no medical insurance, primarily because the National Health Insurance Scheme did not cover cancer treatment and palliative care services. Furthermore, most Nigerians, especially those in the private and informal work sectors, have not enrolled in the medical health insurance program. Recently, a dedicated Cancer Health Fund was established [18]. This, however, is a pilot study limited to 100 indigent prostate/breast/cervical cancer patients annually in each of the six teaching hospitals in Nigeria. Among the very few (27.6%) with medical insurance, they were enrolled in the National Health Insurance Scheme, which does not provide adequate treatment coverage for their illness and care-related expenses. Challenges with the cost of treatment are not limited to Nigerian patients; in America, patients with health insurance still provide some counterpart contributions to meet the cost of treatment, as stated by Carrera et al. (2018) [19]. Furthermore, a good number (46.7%) of the respondents had to travel long distances to get their cancer treatments due to limited oncological facilities and radiotherapy treatment centres in Nigeria when compared with the population size. All these factors have a negative impact on the financial burden, psychosocial problems, compliance to treatment, follow-up care, treatment outcomes, and quality of life.

The study highlighted that the lower the age of the participants, the more financially distressed they were, as shown in past studies [13, 20]. This could be because of the fact that respondents that were older in age had grown-up children that assisted them with the cost of their treatment. This study also showed that the severity of financial toxicity of those newly diagnosed was more than those diagnosed for a longer period [14]. This could be attributed to the higher frequency of treatment among those newly diagnosed. As expected, the study also indicated that respondents with lower monthly incomes were more financially distressed than those with higher monthly incomes, which is consistent with findings from past studies [21, 22].

Unmarried respondents had a higher level of psychosocial distress compared to the married. This may be associated with the lack of spousal support which is essential in improving psychological health and well-being. Respondents with a lower income had a higher level of financial toxicity and were more psychosocially distressed [23]. This can largely be attributed to the higher financial burden, which worsens their psychological stress [24, 25]. The level of severity of psychosocial distress of the respondents that were newly diagnosed was more than those that had been diagnosed for a longer period of time, and this could be attributed to the fact that those that had been diagnosed for a longer period of time received less frequent treatment [26] and have adapted coping strategies to help with their psychosocial distress.

Several coping behaviours were adopted by the respondents to manage their financial and psychosocial distresses, such as selling properties, cutting back on basic expenses, and relying on family and friends, as highlighted in a study in Ghana [27]. Almost all the respondents sought emotional support from their families, as shown in a similar study in Kenya [5]. This is because emotional support from family members is essential for the patient’s psychological adaptation to the effects of the treatment. Only a third sought emotional support from their friends, and this is due to the non-disclosure of their diagnosis due to fear of stigmatization [28]. Unfortunately, most of the respondents did not seek emotional support from medical personnel, and this can be attributed to the fact that most cancer care centres do not have functioning psycho-oncology care, and hence they are not motivated to seek emotional support from health personnel.

Financial toxicity faced by cancer patients needs to be addressed by establishing national initiatives and programs to manage financial distress and assist cancer patients with drug costs, payment plans, and charity care, among other things [29]. According to Khera et al. 2020 [30], providing patients with upfront financial information about financial issues early in treatment will help them, and their caregivers cope and plan better for costs throughout the care continuum.

Strength and limitation of the study

This study focused on breast and cervical cancer patients in Nigeria, which are the leading types of cancers affecting women in Nigeria. It also explored the financial and psychosocial distresses of these patients, which are significant issues associated with cancer treatment in Nigeria.

The limitation of this study is that it focused on a particular region in Nigeria, which will limit generalization of results obtained. Also, the results of this study may have been influenced by gender bias, as only women were involved in the study.

Conclusion

Breast and cervical cancer patients experienced a high level of financial toxicity, which worsened their psychosocial distress. Due to the current realities of cancer patients in Nigeria, coupled with the findings from this study, there is the need for improved financial protection in healthcare to reduce hardship associated with treatment. This should be supported by the government by ensuring health facilities providing treatment services to cancer patients have functioning psycho-oncology services to address the psychosocial distress of the patients. Addressing financial toxicity and psychological distress by relevant governmental health organizations such as the Federal Ministry of Health and the Federal Ministry of Women Affairs is critical to bridging the disparities in cancer treatment outcomes.
