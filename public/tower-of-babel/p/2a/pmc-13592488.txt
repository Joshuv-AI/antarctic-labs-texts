Systematic review and meta‐analysis of lifestyle modification interventions and their impact on seizure reduction and quality of life
Authors: Elizabeth Spurgeon, Alina Ivanuik, Shivanee Sodani, Elizabeth Butler, David Martinez, Mary Schleicher, Spence Sylvester, Robert Saper, Imad Najm, Dennis Lal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study was undertaken to provide an overview of the research landscape and evaluate the impact of lifestyle modifications on seizure reduction and quality of life in people with epilepsy receiving standard medical treatment.MethodsFollowing PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses) guidelines, we conducted a systematic review of major databases (Medline, Embase, American Psychological Association PsycInfo, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science) through November 2025. After screening 5151 articles, 118 studies met inclusion criteria: prospective trials reporting seizure frequency in adults or mixed populations with epilepsy. Four epilepsy‐trained reviewers independently assessed studies. A random‐effects meta‐analysis was performed on studies reporting ≥50% seizure reduction or data allowing its calculation. Short‐term response was defined as the earliest postintervention time point, and long‐term response as the final reported time point.ResultsThe 118 studies covered diet (n = 33), supplements (n = 20), biofeedback (n = 14), psychosocial/behavioral therapy (n = 13), yoga/meditation/mindfulness (n = 8), music therapy (n = 7), and others. Fifty‐two (44%) included control groups, whereas 91 (77%) reported statistical analysis of seizure frequency. Among those, 68% showed significant seizure reduction. Quality of life was assessed in 31 studies, 68% reporting improvement.Seventy‐one studies (60%) were included in the meta‐analysis. Overall, the ≥50% seizure reduction response rate was 44% for short‐term outcomes (95% confidence interval [CI] = 38%–50%) and 50% for long‐term outcomes (95% CI = 44%–56%). Dietary interventions had the largest evidence base (n = 33), with short‐ and long‐term response rates of 40% and 51%, respectively. Meditation/yoga/mindfulness had the highest response rate (62% short term, 79% long term), whereas exercise and supplements had the lowest (30%–35%).SignificanceLifestyle modifications may offer beneficial, adjunctive effects on seizure control. However, the current evidence is limited by variability in study design and quality. Larger, well‐designed trials with standardized outcomes are needed to clarify how these interventions can complement epilepsy treatment. This systematic review and meta‐analysis serves as a foundation for future research to better define the role of lifestyle interventions in epilepsy care.

INTRODUCTION

Management of epilepsy has advanced significantly since the first electroencephalogram (EEG) in 1924, 1 with the development of advanced imaging, stereotactic techniques, neurosurgical interventions, neuromodulation (e.g., vagus nerve stimulation, responsive neurostimulation, deep brain stimulation), and numerous antiseizure medications. 2 Despite these options, nearly one third of individuals continue to have seizures despite multiple medication trials. 3 Moreover, seizure reduction does not always improve quality of life (QOL), suggesting that well‐being is influenced by more than seizure control alone. Although epilepsy surgery and neuromodulation may benefit select individuals, only approximately 50%–55% of those with drug‐resistant epilepsy are eligible for resective surgery after comprehensive evaluation. 4 This prompts an important question: can nonpharmacological, nonsurgical approaches be used alongside medication to improve seizure control or QOL?

Stress is one of the most commonly reported seizure triggers. 5 , 6 Beyond everyday stressors, people with epilepsy may face additional burdens, including medication side effects, driving restrictions, workplace limitations, financial strain, relationship challenges, and stigma. 7 , 8 Recently, there has been growing interest in lifestyle modifications and their potential impact on seizure frequency, QOL, cognition, and mental health. 9 , 10 , 11 These interventions encompass a wide range of strategies, including dietary approaches, physical activity, mind–body practices, and behavioral therapies, and likely act through multiple mechanisms. Whereas stress reduction may contribute to the effects of some interventions, others may influence seizure susceptibility through biological, physiological, or neurochemical pathways, highlighting the importance of evaluating these approaches collectively and systematically.

Lifestyle medicine (LM)—a nonpharmacological specialty using interventions such as nutrition, exercise, stress reduction, and sleep to prevent or treat chronic diseases 12 , 13 —has shown benefits for general health and in reducing anxiety, depression, and stress. 14 , 15 Although its application in epilepsy has been limited to small studies and case reports, early findings suggest LM may help reduce seizures when used alongside standard treatments, likely through its stress‐reducing and health‐promoting effects.

Several systematic reviews have examined LM interventions in epilepsy, including yoga, 16 mindfulness‐based therapies, 17 music interventions, 18 , 19 dietary therapies such as ketogenic and modified Atkins diets, 20 , 21 psychosocial and behavioral therapies, 10 , 22 and exercise. 23 Although these reviews suggest potential benefits of each individual type of intervention, their findings are difficult to compare because of nonuniform outcomes and heterogeneous study designs. Comparing the effectiveness of different LMs is crucial from a practical standpoint; although nonpharmacological interventions are generally appealing as a whole, there is a lack of an evidence‐based framework for prioritizing LM selection and introduction based on patient preference and accessibility.

To address this gap, we conducted the first comprehensive systematic review and meta‐analysis to quantitatively compare the effectiveness of diverse LM interventions for seizure reduction. By integrating prospective and randomized trials across modalities, our study provides a unified estimate of efficacy, identifies understudied approaches, and outlines priorities for future LM research. These results offer a practical reference for clinicians seeking to individualize LM recommendations in epilepsy care.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Protocol

A protocol was developed in concordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and was registered with the PROSPERO International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (registration number CRD42022313342).

Search strategy

The search was conducted in collaboration with a medical librarian (M.S.) with previous experience in conducting systematic reviews. A comprehensive search was created by using indexing and keyword terms for epilepsy or seizure and combining that with a large group of concepts representing nonpharmacological interventions such as yoga, diet, exercise, or herbal medicine. This was further added to a list of terms intended to yield publication types such as systematic reviews, randomized trials, or other primary research. The following databases were searched on March 2, 2022 and updated on June 6, 2024 and November 25, 2025: Medline ALL, Embase, and American Psychological Association PsycInfo (all via Ovid); Cochrane Library from Wiley; and Web of Science from Clarivate‐searched Science Citation Index Expanded and BIOSIS Citation Index platforms. There were no language or publication year limits imposed on the search. Complete search strategies can be found in Appendix S1.

Although the initial search included clinical trial registries (e.g., ClinicalTrials.gov), conference abstracts, and other gray literature to ensure broad coverage, these records were later excluded from the final analysis if they did not meet inclusion criteria as stated in Section 2.3. This approach was taken to ensure consistency with established standards of scientific rigor and to prioritize peer‐reviewed data for synthesis.

Before the formal search, the authors conducted a literature review to obtain an overview of the existing literature and to refine search terms for the detailed search further. The search was not restricted to language or publication date and included clinical trials, prospective studies, randomized controlled trials, systematic reviews, conferences, and abstracts for initial screening.

Study eligibility and selection

Four epilepsy‐trained authors (one neurologist in fellowship training, two board‐certified epileptologists, and one epilepsy physician assistant) independently reviewed the studies using Covidence web‐based software. Two authors independently screened titles and abstracts and selected the included studies (E.S., E.B.). The third and fourth authors (D.M. and S.So.) resolved conflicting decisions after initial screening. Full‐text articles were reviewed and selected for inclusion based on the criteria specified below. The reasons for study exclusion were documented.

We included studies satisfying the following criteria: (1) prospective interventional study design, which encompassed randomized and nonrandomized controlled trials and prospective single‐arm interventional studies; (2) participants were adults (age ≥ 18 years) or were mixed adult and pediatric populations; (3) the intervention concerned any lifestyle or nonpharmacological intervention, defined as approaches involving behavioral, physical, or complementary therapies (e.g., dietary interventions, supplements, psychosocial/behavioral therapies, exercise, and mind–body interventions), excluding epilepsy surgery and neuromodulation techniques; although the ketogenic diet is often classified as a medical therapy, it was included given its reliance on sustained dietary and behavioral modification; (4) baseline and postintervention seizure frequency was included as a primary or secondary outcome; and (5) language of publication was English at the full‐text review stage. Inclusion was not limited by publication year, country, or sex of participants.

The primary outcome of our meta‐analysis was seizure frequency. Due to heterogeneity in how seizure outcomes were reported, we included only studies that provided the number of participants with a ≥50% reduction in seizure frequency, or those for whom this number could be calculated from raw data. This threshold is commonly used in randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating antiseizure medications and allows for comparability across interventions. 24 Seizure reduction was based on the study‐defined reporting intervals. In most cases, studies used consistent baseline and postintervention time windows (e.g., 4 or 12 weeks), and a ≥50% reduction was defined relative to the baseline period within each study. Therefore, no additional normalization of seizure counts across studies was applied. In 26 studies (23%), individual participant data on pre‐ and postintervention monthly seizure frequency were available, and the ≥50% responder rate was calculated accordingly.

Secondary outcomes included QOL. All validated QOL instruments were eligible for inclusion. Across the included studies, a variety of tools were used, most commonly epilepsy‐specific instruments, such as Quality of Life in Epilepsy Inventory (QOLIE)‐31, QOLIE‐31‐P, QOLIE‐89, and QOLIE‐10. A smaller number of studies used other validated general health‐related measures, including the World Health Organization Quality of Life‐BREF (WHOQOL‐BREF), 36‐Item Short Form Health Survey (SF‐36), and 12‐Item Short Form Health Survey, Version 2 (SF‐12v2). One study utilized the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL), reflecting inclusion of a cohort spanning both pediatric and young adult participants. 25 QOL was not included in the meta‐analysis because studies were only eligible if they assessed seizure frequency. Due to the variability in QOL instruments and reporting methods, QOL outcomes were qualitatively summarized based on whether studies reported a statistically significant improvement in overall QOL as defined by total scores or author‐identified primary QOL endpoints.

Data extraction

The following variables were extracted from each study: first author, year, PMID, study type, epilepsy intractability, age group, LM intervention, control intervention (if applicable), number of participants at baseline, during the study, and at study end, seizure reduction (percent or calculated from the individual participant data provided), and number of individuals with ≥50% seizure reduction. Articles were categorized based on prior systematic reviews 19 , 26 , 27 or shared characteristics (e.g., yoga/meditation/mindfulness, psychosocial/behavioral therapy). 28 Due to heterogeneity and limited numbers in some areas, interventions were grouped broadly (e.g., vitamin B12, omega‐3, and quercetin under “supplements”). Seizure frequency measures (e.g., mean, median, percent seizure reduction, raw data) and QOL outcomes were recorded. To ensure accuracy, the primary reviewer verified all extracted data through multiple independent rounds of review.

When duplicate reports from the same cohort were identified, the most comprehensive or recent dataset was included to avoid duplication. One study published both immediate 29 and 10‐year follow‐up results 30 ; we included the initial publication to maintain consistency in follow‐up duration and reduce heterogeneity introduced by long‐term data. The meta‐analysis focused on dichotomous seizure outcomes (≥50% responder rate); studies were included if they reported or allowed calculation of responder rates. Variance data were not required. For crossover trials, seizure reductions were analyzed as reported, typically comparing participant's baseline seizure frequency to both intervention and control phases.

Risk of bias assessment

As this review focused on a qualitative synthesis of heterogeneous studies with variable methodological reporting, and given the heterogeneity in study designs, a formal risk of bias assessment using standardized tools was not performed. However, study limitations were considered through narrative appraisal, including factors such as study size, outcome reporting, and potential sources of bias (e.g., lack of blinding, incomplete follow‐up, and selective reporting). Overall, methodological rigor varied across studies, which should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Data synthesis and analysis

Analyses were done using R version 4.2.0 (libraries meta and metafor). We summarized the included studies using descriptive statistics and visualizations. We conducted a random‐effects meta‐analysis of studies that reported data on rates of seizure‐related response, defined as achieving ≥50% seizure reduction, or raw numbers of seizure reduction that would allow calculating response rate. The participant numbers and response rates for studies investigating several interventions were considered as separate data points. These studies included an RCT by Ibrahim et al., 31 which investigated two supplements (eicosapentaenoic acid and docosahexaenoic acid), and an RCT by DeGiorgio et al. 32 that investigated low and high doses of fish oil. Kishk et al. 33 investigated two different ratios of ketogenic diet. Coppola et al. 34 reported response rates in two different Mozart music exposure protocols. Of note, we excluded the study by Betts, 35 which investigated aromatherapy in conjunction with massage or hypnosis, because the group allocation was unclear and raised concerns about significant confounding factors.

Given that we expected a high degree of heterogeneity across the studies on different LM modalities, we used a random‐effects model for meta‐analysis. We used the I 2 statistic to analyze heterogeneity between studies. For controlled studies, we estimated between‐group treatment effects using risk ratios (RRs) comparing the proportion of responders in intervention and control arms. Random‐effects meta‐analyses were performed using restricted maximum likelihood estimation, with Hartung–Knapp adjustment to account for between‐study heterogeneity. Continuity corrections were applied where required. We summarized outcomes as pooled proportions of responders, using logit‐transformed proportions for the total pool of studies, including controlled, uncontrolled, and observational studies. These analyses were intended to provide clinical context regarding response frequency rather than to estimate comparative treatment effects.

We assessed publication bias with visual inspection of funnel plots and the Egger method. 36 If bias were to be found, we intended to perform correction using the Duval and Tweedie 37 trim and fill procedure.

To account for variation in study duration, we conducted two separate analyses for short‐ and long‐term seizure reduction responses. Short‐term response was defined as the earliest postintervention time point reported in each study, and long‐term response as the final reported time point. If a study reported results at only a single time point, that outcome was included in both analyses to allow consistent comparison across studies with differing designs and durations. Due to variability in study design, follow‐up durations were not standardized across studies. Visual inspection of the funnel plots for both analyses showed that no correction for publication bias was required.

To assess the influence of small studies on pooled estimates, we performed a sensitivity analysis restricted to studies with ≥30 total participants. The same random‐effects model was applied, and the results were compared with the main analysis.

Outcome measures are summarized in Table 1. Seizure frequency was the primary outcome in the vast majority of studies (80%), whereas only a smaller portion (20%) used it as a secondary outcome. Just less than half of the studies (44%) reported responder rates (defined as the number of participants achieving ≥50% seizure reduction), whereas the rest reported pre‐ and postintervention seizure frequencies as group averages or individual‐level data. Only approximately one quarter of studies (26%) included a QOL measure.

Most studies (77%) conducted a statistical analysis of seizure frequency, with a p‐value reported for seizure frequency. Among those, 68% reported a statistically significant reduction. Some intervention categories stood out with more consistent positive findings (Figure 2B); all studies on biofeedback (8/8) and nearly all studies on meditation/yoga/mindfulness (7/8) reported significant seizure reduction. Similarly, most studies on dietary interventions (15/18), music therapy (5/6), and psychosocial/behavioral therapy (8/13) also showed significant effects and were supported by five or more studies with significant seizure reduction.

Thirty‐one (26%) of the 118 articles included QOL as an outcome measure. Of these, nine (29%) lacked a control or comparison group, whereas 22 (71%) included one, of which 20 (91%) were randomized. As displayed in Figure 2C, 21 of these studies reported a significant improvement in QOL. QOL was measured most frequently in studies evaluating psychosocial/behavioral therapies, of which seven of seven (100%) found statistical improvement in QOL.

Meta‐analysis of seizure‐related response rates across lifestyle interventions

Of 118 studies, 71 (60.0%) could be included in the meta‐analysis, with 81 instances of LM investigations included, as some studies investigated several modalities or LM paradigms. Further, we report number of such instances as n. The random‐effects meta‐analysis for short‐term outcomes across all studies (Figure 3) showed a 44% seizure frequency response rate across all treatment modalities (95% confidence interval [CI] = 38%–50%), with moderate‐to‐high heterogeneity (I 2 = 74%). Psychological/cognitive interventions demonstrated the highest short‐term response rate at 62% (95% CI = 38%–82%, n = 4), with moderate heterogeneity (I 2 = 49%). Music therapy followed with a 50% response rate (95% CI = 35%–65%, n = 5) and low observed heterogeneity (I 2 = 19.1%). Dietary interventions, with the greatest number of studies, had a 40% response rate (95% CI = 33%–48%, n = 32) with moderate heterogeneity (I 2 = 72%). Supplements and exercise yielded the lowest short‐term responses at 36% (95% CI = 23%–53%, n = 16, I 2 = 83.8%) and 30% (95% CI = 7%–70%, n = 3, I 2 = 64%), respectively. For long‐term outcomes (Figure 4), the pooled seizure reduction response rate increased to 50% (95% CI = 44%–57%), again with moderate‐to‐high heterogeneity (I 2 = 75%). Meditation/yoga/mindfulness was the most effective, with a 78% response rate (95% CI = 51%–92%, n = 5, I 2 = 73%), followed by psychosocial/cognitive interventions at 62% (95% CI = 38%–82%, n = 4, I 2 = 49%). Music therapy had a 50% response (95% CI = 35%–65%, n = 5) with low heterogeneity (I 2 = 19.1%). Dietary interventions showed an improved response of 51% (95% CI = 41%–61%, n = 32), although heterogeneity remained high (I 2 = 75%). The lowest responses were again observed in exercise (30%, 95% CI = 7%–70%, n = 3, I 2 = 64%) and supplements (32%, 95% CI = 18%–50%, n = 14, I 2 = 84%).

Subgroup analysis of controlled studies (Figures S1 and S2) and sensitivity analyses excluding studies with fewer than 30 participants (Figures S3 and S4) are presented in Appendix S3. Both pooled proportions and effect sizes showed results similar to the primary analysis. At the first reported time point, dietary interventions (RR = 3.79, 95% CI = .71–20.22) and supplements (RR = 1.17, 95% CI = .99–1.38) had higher response likelihood compared with controls. Progressive muscle relaxation had RR = 2.07 (95% CI = 1.48–2.91). At the later time point, dietary interventions (RR = 3.79, 95% CI = .71–20.22) and supplements (RR = 1.17, 95% CI = .93–1.46) showed similar estimates.

CIs were wide across several analyses, and some intervention categories included a limited number of studies. High heterogeneity was observed across the vast majority of the categories in the sensitivity analyses. These patterns reinforce the importance of interpreting results in the context of both study quantity and consistency, highlighting the need for more robust, replicated research to validate early findings.

Funnel plots were constructed and Egger test conducted for each pooled outcome to assess potential publication bias in both controlled and noncontrolled studies reporting short‐ and long‐term outcomes (Appendix S5, Figures S5–S8). Visual inspection of funnel plots suggested approximate symmetry, indicating no strong evidence of publication bias, supported by the results of Egger test.

DISCUSSION

This systematic review and meta‐analysis is the first, to our knowledge, to provide a comprehensive landscape analysis of lifestyle modifications (LM) in epilepsy, integrating data across study designs and intervention types. It is also the first to conduct individual systematic reviews and meta‐analyses for specific LM categories and compare their relative efficacy and heterogeneity. By analyzing and comparing individual LM categories, we outline their relative efficacy, variability, and evidentiary strength. These findings offer a direct reference for clinicians seeking to match LM options with patient goals and feasibility, while also delineating methodological gaps that future studies should address.

Although seizure frequency was the primary outcome due to its central role in evaluating treatment efficacy, we also examined QOL, because it may improve independently of seizure reduction. We limited our outcomes to these two domains to maintain a feasible scope for analysis given the large number of eligible studies and variability in reported measures. Among 118 studies and a meta‐analysis of 71, our findings revealed mixed evidence regarding the effectiveness of LM interventions alongside standard epilepsy treatment. Although 62 of the 91 studies with statistical analyses (68%) showed significant seizure reduction, the substantial variability in study design, intervention protocols, and outcome measures underscores the need for more standardized, high‐quality research. To address one of the universal deficiencies—the wide variability in sample size across included studies—we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding studies with <30 participants. The pooled proportions of responders for both short‐ and long‐term outcomes remained comparable to the primary analysis, suggesting that the overall estimates were not driven primarily by very small studies. Importantly, the relative performance of individual intervention modalities was also largely preserved. However, substantial heterogeneity persisted across most intervention categories. This suggests that variability in treatment effects reflects genuine clinical and methodological differences rather than small‐study effects alone.

Diet was the most frequently studied intervention, likely due to established evidence for the ketogenic diet in pediatric epilepsy. 26 There was a notable temporal trend in the LM modalities research, with dietary interventions and supplements receiving more research attention since the beginning of 2000s. Meditation/yoga/mindfulness showed the highest short‐term and long‐term response rates, consistent with prior findings on stress reduction benefits, 113 , 149 although heterogeneity remained moderate to high, reflecting variations in these findings. In contrast, exercise showed the lowest response. Two of the three exercise studies reported seizure frequency as a secondary outcome, and none focused on drug‐resistant epilepsy, limiting their interpretability and ability to detect meaningful changes in seizure burden. A 2022 meta‐analysis of 14 studies found no significant effect on seizure frequency 23 ; only 10 of those studies measured seizure frequency, with just four reporting significant improvements. Our stricter inclusion criteria—requiring >50% responder data—reduced the eligible studies to three, likely explaining the difference in findings and highlighting the need for more targeted research.

Across all studies, 52 (44%) included a control group, 48 (41%) lacked a control or comparison group, and the remainder used either an intervention‐versus‐intervention (n = 9, 8%) or crossover design (n = 9, 8%). The absence of control groups in many studies may reflect limitations such as small expected sample sizes or reliance on baseline seizure frequency as a comparator, particularly in diet studies. Most studies (n = 75, 64%) enrolled participants with refractory epilepsy, likely due to the ease of tracking seizure frequency and detecting a response.

Seizure frequency reduction

Our meta‐analysis demonstrated that some lifestyle modifications, such as diet, psychological interventions, and biofeedback, show some promising effects in reducing seizure frequency, with significant improvements in many studies. The overall proportion of participants with more than 50% reduction in seizure frequency varied across interventions. For instance, interventions like biofeedback, music, yoga, and dietary changes were associated with notable improvements, although with considerable variability in effectiveness (I 2 ranging from 64% to 87%). An expanded discussion of dietary, mind–body, and music‐based lifestyle interventions is provided in Appendix S4.

QOL improvements

QOL was an outcome measure in 26% 32 of the studies, with 21 of the 31 studies (68%) reporting significant improvements. Notably, interventions involving psychological/behavioral therapies (7/7, 100%) and meditation/yoga/mindfulness (4/4, 100%) demonstrated the highest levels of improvement. These findings suggest the importance of integrating outcome measures other than seizure control in prospective epilepsy study designs.

QOL is a crucial disease‐related metric across different disorders, including epilepsy. 150 Although there is a correlation between seizure frequency and QOL in people with epilepsy, 151 other components of physical and mental health, such as stress levels, anxiety, fatigue, and social factors play an important role in QOL level. 150 , 152 , 153 Some studies have indicated that there may not be a direct correlation between seizure reduction and QOL, which suggests that overall well‐being in epilepsy may be more complex than solely seizure control. Although antiseizure medications can offer seizure control, managing other components of QOL with pharmacological interventions alone presents a challenge and opens opportunities for employing other modalities such as lifestyle interventions. 154 , 155 , 156

Challenges in studying lifestyle modifications

Marked variability within each intervention category, such as differences in pranayama‐ versus asana‐dominant yoga regimens, ketogenic diets with diverse fat‐to‐carbohydrate ratios, or types of music therapy (e.g., Mozart K. 448 vs. medical resonance therapy), makes it difficult to isolate the “active ingredients” that may contribute to seizure reduction. This heterogeneity complicates the interpretation of individual study results and limits cross‐study comparisons. To improve reproducibility and future meta‐analyses, interventions should be developed using existing literature and expert consensus, described in sufficient detail (e.g., dose, frequency, and duration), and include implementation fidelity checks.

Another key challenge is determining the optimal duration needed to achieve seizure reduction, which likely varies by intervention. Some modalities may have early effects, whereas others require sustained engagement. Studies should align outcome assessments with expected onset of benefit. Much of this remains unknown, highlighting the need for longer follow‐up to better understand timing effects.

Although RCTs are generally considered the gold standard in clinical research, 157 they pose feasibility challenges for studying lifestyle changes, which depend on personal motivation, preferences, background experiences, access to resources, and cognitive or physical capacity. 158 Alternative designs, such as adaptive trials, observational studies, or randomized encouragement trials, may better capture real‐world effectiveness. Until such harmonization is achieved, existing positive signals should be viewed as hypothesis‐generating rather than definitive, and guide the next wave of targeted, standardized, and patient‐centered trials aimed at reproducible seizure control.

Limitations

Despite encouraging signals, the evidence base is limited by methodological heterogeneity and low statistical power. Fewer than half of the studies (52/118, 44%) reported the ≥50% responder rate commonly used in antiseizure medication trials; others provided only group means or individual‐level data, limiting meta‐analytic inclusion and cross‐study comparability. Seizure and QOL outcomes varied widely, and small, single‐center cohorts with nonstandardized protocols and short follow‐up (<6 months in most) reduced confidence in pooled estimates. Additionally, reporting of baseline patient characteristics was inconsistent across studies. Although 64% of studies included participants with drug‐resistant epilepsy, details regarding antiseizure medication regimens, epilepsy type, prior epilepsy surgery, and adherence to the lifestyle interventions were often limited or missing, limiting the generalizability of the findings.

We deliberately included a broad range of interventional study designs, including both controlled and uncontrolled studies, to capture the full scope of available evidence and maximize the available data for synthesis. Although this approach provided a richer and more comprehensive overview of lifestyle interventions, it also introduced substantial heterogeneity due to differences in study design, intervention protocols, outcome definitions, and clinical settings. To address these limitations, we conducted a subanalysis restricted to controlled studies, reporting pooled RRs to estimate between‐group effects. Nonetheless, the results of the primary meta‐analysis should be interpreted with appropriate caution, recognizing the methodological diversity of the included studies.

Several interpretative gaps warrant attention. Most trials did not adjust for epilepsy syndrome, comorbidities, or medication changes, which further limits generalizability, raising concerns that unmeasured confounders may explain observed improvements. Mechanistic insight remains limited, as few studies included biomarkers or neurophysiological measures to investigate how biological pathways influence seizure thresholds. Likewise, the interpretation of statistical heterogeneity in our review warrants caution. The I 2 estimates were low in some intervention categories with very small numbers of studies and participants, conditions under which I 2 is known to be unstable and potentially misleading. Consequently, heterogeneity in this review is best understood qualitatively rather than inferred from I 2 estimates alone and is still driven by a substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity, including differences in intervention protocols, outcome definitions, follow‐up duration, and participant characteristics.

Future aims

Future research should prioritize adequately powered, multicenter RCTs and adaptive or pragmatic designs tailored to lifestyle interventions. Standardized, manualized protocols should specify intervention components, dose, duration, fidelity, and adherence. Common endpoints—including ≥50% responder rate, seizure‐free days, and validated QOL metrics—are essential to enable synthesis. Longer follow‐up (at least 12 months) is needed to assess sustainability, safety, and adherence. Finally, incorporating cost‐effectiveness analyses, equity factors (e.g., access to yoga, dietitians, wearable technologies), and mechanistic substudies (e.g., EEG biomarkers, cortisol, heart rate variability) will help advance LM from promising adjunct to scalable, evidence‐based epilepsy care.

CONCLUSIONS

Our findings suggest that lifestyle modifications may offer adjunctive benefits for seizure management in epilepsy, although more rigorous research is necessary. Future studies should prioritize large‐scale, well‐designed randomized controlled trials with standardized interventions and outcome measures, longer follow‐up durations, and consistent reporting frameworks. Clinicians and patients alike would benefit from clearer guidance on how to incorporate lifestyle changes alongside standard medical management in the treatment of epilepsy. This systematic review and meta‐analysis lays the groundwork for future research that can provide more definitive answers on the role of lifestyle interventions in epilepsy care.
