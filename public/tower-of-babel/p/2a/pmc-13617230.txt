Idiopathic Orbital Inflammation Masquerading as Relapse of Seronegative Juvenile Myasthenia Gravis: A Case Report
Authors: Wenhao Duan, Yanqing Zhang, Wensha Nie, Hongchao Jiang, Huiping Wang
Published: 2026

Introduction

Juvenile myasthenia gravis (JMG) most commonly presents with fluctuating ptosis, diplopia and fatigable extraocular muscle weakness, typically with diurnal variation (worse later in the day) [1, 2]. Uncomplicated JMG is usually not accompanied by prominent orbital pain, conjunctival injection, tearing or eyelid oedema. Idiopathic orbital inflammation (IOI) is an uncommon non‐infectious, non‐neoplastic inflammatory disorder of the orbit in children and remains a diagnosis of exclusion. It typically manifests with acute or subacute orbital pain, eyelid swelling, conjunctival injection, tearing and proptosis. Orbital magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) often demonstrates inflammatory changes in the orbital soft tissues, and symptoms frequently respond rapidly to corticosteroids [3, 4, 5]. The coexistence of myasthenia gravis (MG) and IOI has rarely been reported [6, 7].

We describe a 13‐year‐old boy previously diagnosed with seronegative JMG whose recurrent ptosis was initially interpreted as a relapse of MG. However, a characteristic ‘dissociation phenomenon’, serial imaging evolution and repeated rapid responses to corticosteroids ultimately supported a dual diagnosis of JMG with concomitant IOI.

This case report was prepared in accordance with the CARE guidelines.

Case Report

A 13‐year‐old boy had been diagnosed with seronegative JMG, corresponding to Myasthenia Gravis Foundation of America (MGFA) Class I, approximately 1 year before the index admission. At the initial presentation, he developed left ptosis and diplopia accompanied by mild left periorbital pain, conjunctival injection and tearing. A neostigmine test was positive, and low‐frequency repetitive nerve stimulation demonstrated a > 15% decrement in compound muscle action potential amplitude recorded from the left facial nerve. Serum antibodies to acetylcholine receptor (AChR), muscle‐specific tyrosine kinase (MuSK), LRP4, titin and ryanodine receptor (RyR) were all negative. Chest and mediastinal computed tomography and brain/paranasal sinus MRI were unremarkable. Oral prednisone (55 mg/day) plus pyridostigmine led to complete resolution within several days. He subsequently remained clinically asymptomatic, with an MG‐Activities of Daily Living (MG‐ADL) score of 0 for nearly 11 months. During this period, prednisone was gradually tapered to 7.5 mg/day, which had been maintained for more than 6 weeks before the index admission and pyridostigmine had been discontinued for 12 weeks.

Approximately 1 week before the index admission, he developed recurrent left orbital pain, conjunctival injection, tearing, eyelid oedema and ptosis, along with fatigue, dyspnoea, choking on water and hypophonia. A transient febrile illness had occurred 3 weeks earlier. His family increased prednisone to 10 mg/day without clear benefit, and he was admitted. The day of admission was defined as Day 1. On examination, he had bilateral ptosis with a positive fatigability test; marked swelling of the left eyelid with conjunctival injection and tearing; limb strength of approximately 4/5; and an MG‐ADL score of 11, comprising bulbar symptoms 4, respiratory symptoms 2, limb weakness 2 and ocular symptoms 3. His presentation was classified as MGFA Class IVb. The combination of dyspnoea at rest, bulbar symptoms and generalised fatigability was considered consistent with a high risk of progression to myasthenic crisis; therefore, multidisciplinary consultation supported the use of efgartigimod. Off‐label treatment was approved with the informed consent of his guardian. He was treated for JMG relapse with intravenous efgartigimod and oral pyridostigmine. Within hours, dyspnoea, limb fatigability and ptosis improved markedly.

However, while systemic symptoms improved rapidly, local orbital inflammatory signs did not improve in parallel. Orbital MRI on Day 4 showed mild left proptosis, T2 hyperintensity within the intraconal and retrobulbar spaces, inflammatory change around the optic nerve, enlargement of the lacrimal gland and eyelid soft‐tissue oedema. Contrast‐enhanced MRI on Day 6 demonstrated heterogeneous enhancement around the optic nerve and multifocal enhancement in the intraconal and retrobulbar spaces with involvement of extraocular muscles, the lacrimal apparatus and eyelid soft tissues. Laboratory evaluation showed normal C‐reactive protein (CRP) and procalcitonin (PCT) levels; normal thyroid function and serum IgG4 level; and negative antinuclear antibody (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) tests.

Given the need to differentiate from orbital cellulitis, empiric vancomycin plus ceftriaxone was administered from Day 6 to Day 18, with limited clinical improvement. On Day 9, orbital pain, tearing and eyelid swelling worsened, whereas MG‐related symptoms were no longer prominent. Repeat MRI demonstrated progression of the left‐sided lesion and new involvement of the right retrobulbar/optic nerve sheath region, extraocular muscles and lacrimal gland. Intravenous methylprednisolone 40 mg/day was given on Days 9–10, leading to rapid relief of pain, swelling and tearing within 48 h. During admission, he experienced a brief febrile episode and respiratory pathogen nucleic acid testing was positive for respiratory syncytial virus, but subsequent imaging continued to improve. Repeat orbital MRI on Day 18 showed marked interval improvement compared with Day 9. After multidisciplinary discussion involving neurology, ophthalmology and radiology, IOI was diagnosed. Antibiotics were discontinued and oral prednisone was adjusted to 25 mg/day. Follow‐up orbital MRI on Day 24 showed further reduction in lesion extent.

During follow‐up, prednisone was tapered to 10 mg/day on Day 41. On Day 64, without an obvious trigger, he again developed left orbital pain, conjunctival injection and tearing, but had no MG‐related symptoms. Orbital MRI showed mild recrudescence of left periorbital, intraconal and retrobulbar inflammation, while prior right‐sided abnormalities remained stable. A second course of intravenous methylprednisolone 40 mg/day for 3 days (Days 64–66) resulted in prompt symptom resolution, and prednisone was increased to 25 mg/day on Day 67. At follow‐up on Day 93, he had no MG‐related symptoms and no orbital pain, eyelid swelling, conjunctival injection or tearing. Discussion

This case highlights that, in children with established JMG, recurrent ptosis does not necessarily indicate relapse of MG itself. Orbital inflammatory signs were the most important atypical‐for‐MG signals in this patient. A more decisive diagnostic clue was the ‘dissociation phenomenon’: rescue treatment directed at MG led to rapid improvement of dyspnoea, limb fatigability, choking on water and ptosis, whereas orbital inflammatory signs did not improve concurrently and instead progressed. When systemic neuromuscular junction symptoms and local orbital inflammatory manifestations follow discordant trajectories, early repeat orbital imaging and renewed differential diagnosis are warranted to avoid diagnostic anchoring based solely on past history.

The diagnosis of seronegative autoimmune JMG was supported by a compatible clinical syndrome, a positive neostigmine test, abnormal repetitive nerve stimulation with a > 15% decrement recorded from the facial nerve, and a rapid response to prednisone combined with pyridostigmine. At the initial presentation, weakness was confined to ptosis and diplopia, corresponding to ocular MG and MGFA Class I. At the index admission, dyspnoea at rest, choking on water, hypophonia and limb fatigability indicated conversion to generalised MG, with MGFA Class IVb disease; relative to the previous post‐treatment status, this episode was consistent with exacerbation according to the MGFA postintervention status. Seronegative JMG can be diagnostically challenging, particularly in ocular presentations, because antibody negativity is more common in paediatric ocular MG, delayed seroconversion may occur and routine assays may miss low‐affinity antibodies detectable by cell‐based assays [1, 2]. Single‐fibre electromyography may provide additional support but is technically demanding in children and was not performed in this patient.

The initial MG‐directed management should also be interpreted in the context of current treatment principles for JMG and adult MG. In JMG, pyridostigmine is commonly used for symptomatic treatment, whereas corticosteroids are generally used as first‐line immunosuppressive therapy when symptomatic treatment is insufficient; steroid‐sparing immunosuppressive agents may be considered when corticosteroids are ineffective, cannot be tapered to an acceptable maintenance dose or cause intolerable adverse effects [1, 8]. For severe acute exacerbation or myasthenic crisis, IVIg and plasma exchange remain conventional rescue therapies [1, 8]. In the present case, the child had MGFA Class IVb disease on admission, with dyspnoea at rest, choking on water, hypophonia, generalised fatigability and an MG‐ADL score of 11, indicating a high risk of progression to myasthenic crisis. However, he did not fulfil the usual definition of established myasthenic crisis because intubation, mechanical ventilation or non‐invasive ventilatory support was not required. Efgartigimod was selected after multidisciplinary discussion because rapid stabilisation of bulbar and respiratory symptoms was needed, whereas immediate high‐dose corticosteroid escalation alone was considered less suitable before orbital infection had been reasonably excluded and because corticosteroids may occasionally cause early transient worsening of MG symptoms. IVIg and plasma exchange were considered conventional alternatives; however, emerging adult evidence suggests that efgartigimod may have a rapid clinical effect in manifest or impending myasthenic crisis [9]. Therefore, efgartigimod was chosen as an individualised targeted rescue option rather than as standard paediatric crisis therapy. This use was off‐label because paediatric safety and efficacy data remain limited; therefore, treatment was undertaken only after multidisciplinary review, approval according to the institutional off‐label medication process, guardian informed consent and close monitoring for infection, infusion‐related reactions and subsequent clinical response.

In this patient, the diagnosis of IOI was primarily supported by clinical evolution, serial MRI findings, exclusionary investigations and repeated rapid responses to corticosteroids. Serial MRI demonstrated inflammatory involvement of the intraconal and retrobulbar spaces, optic nerve sheath region, lacrimal gland, extraocular muscles and eyelid soft tissue, with contralateral new lesions emerging despite poor response to antibiotics. In contrast, symptoms and imaging improved rapidly after intravenous methylprednisolone and recurred after steroid tapering. These features are consistent with the current diagnostic consensus and paediatric case series for IOI [3, 4, 5, 10]. Several alternative diagnoses were considered. Orbital cellulitis was initially difficult to exclude because of orbital pain, eyelid oedema, conjunctival injection and tearing; however, normal CRP and PCT levels, limited response to broad‐spectrum antibiotics, bilateral/asynchronous imaging evolution and rapid corticosteroid responsiveness made bacterial infection less likely. Thyroid‐associated orbitopathy was unlikely given normal thyroid function and the acute/subacute inflammatory course. IgG4‐related ophthalmic disease was also less likely because serum IgG4 was normal and there were no typical chronic tumefactive or systemic features. Although MOG‐IgG testing was not performed, MOG antibody‐associated disease was considered because of optic nerve sheath‐region involvement; however, the absence of visual loss, optic neuritis‐like presentation or other central demyelinating features, together with predominant orbital soft‐tissue, lacrimal gland and extraocular muscle involvement, made this diagnosis less likely. Orbital neoplasia was considered radiologically, but the migratory inflammatory evolution and marked steroid‐responsive regression argued against a tumour, although this could not be definitively excluded without histopathology. Other systemic autoimmune orbital diseases were less likely because ANA and ANCA were negative and there was no clinical evidence of systemic vasculitis or connective‐tissue disease.

Notably, mild periorbital pain, conjunctival injection and tearing were already present at the initial diagnosis of ocular MG. Therefore, an unrecognised orbital inflammatory component may have coexisted from disease onset rather than representing only a later comorbidity. Nevertheless, the longitudinal pattern during this relapse, the imaging evolution and two rapid responses to intravenous steroids strongly support a dual diagnosis of seronegative JMG with concomitant IOI. Prior reports of MG coexisting with IOI have largely involved adult cases [6, 7], and paediatric cases appear particularly rare. A transient viral infection may have triggered or amplified inflammation in this patient, but it does not fully explain the subsequent relapsing, highly steroid‐responsive orbital inflammatory course.

Orbital biopsy was considered because of the bilateral/asynchronous progression and involvement of the optic nerve sheath region, which raised the need to exclude neoplastic, granulomatous, IgG4‐related or other systemic inflammatory diseases. However, biopsy was not performed because the predominant lesions were located deep within the intraconal and retrobulbar spaces and were adjacent to the optic nerve sheath, making tissue sampling potentially higher risk. In addition, there was no progressive visual loss or other features requiring urgent histopathological confirmation. The highly steroid‐responsive clinical course, with rapid symptomatic improvement after intravenous methylprednisolone and clear radiological regression on serial MRI, further supported an inflammatory rather than neoplastic process. Nevertheless, the absence of histopathological confirmation remains a limitation of this case, and biopsy would be reconsidered if the lesion becomes steroid‐resistant, shows persistent or progressive mass‐like enlargement, recurs atypically or is associated with visual deterioration. In addition, because multiple therapies partially overlapped in time, the effect of any single intervention cannot be definitively determined. From a practical standpoint, for children with JMG in whom recurrent ptosis is accompanied by orbital pain, tearing, conjunctival injection or eyelid oedema, early orbital MRI and multidisciplinary assessment are recommended. Timely recognition of concomitant IOI may reduce unnecessary antibiotic exposure and help guide steroid tapering and follow‐up.

Learning Points

In children with JMG, recurrent ptosis does not always indicate relapse of MG itself.

When ptosis is accompanied by orbital pain, tearing, conjunctival injection or eyelid oedema, concomitant orbital inflammatory disease (such as IOI) should be considered, and orbital imaging should be obtained promptly.

A ‘dissociation phenomenon’, in which systemic MG symptoms improve rapidly while orbital inflammatory signs persist or recur, is an important clue to comorbidity.

Serial imaging and multidisciplinary assessment may reduce unnecessary antibiotic exposure and support relapse monitoring during steroid tapering.
