Progress and recommendations of developing occupational exposure limits for noise–A systematic review
Authors: Anke Zeng, Yuwen Huang, Jiarui Xin, Jinzhe Li, Wei Qiu, Meibian Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveNoise exposure limit is one of the critical measures to prevent noise-induced hearing loss (NIHL). This review aimed to review the progress and recommendations for developing occupational exposure limits (OELs) for workplace noise.MethodsA systematic review was used. Thirty-eight national or international organizations' noise exposure standards (including OEL) and laws, regulations, and guidelines for noise exposure control were analyzed. Articles on recommendations for revising noise OEL standards between 2000 and 2023 were selected.ResultsThe definition of different noise types (especially for non-steady and impulsive noise) varied worldwide, and the used 8-h OEL varied from 80 to 90 dB(A). Maximum sound pressure level (Lmax) and noise dose for industrial noise and peak sound pressure level (Lpeak) for impulsive noise have been incorporated into the OELs. Countries developed noise risk management measures based on OELs, action levels (ALs), and exposure risk ratio or classification. The risk of co-exposure to noise and ototoxic organic substances and the effects of noise on susceptible populations were concerns in EU country standards. Scholars suggested revising the existing noise exposure standards based on noise's temporal structure (expressed by kurtosis), effective noise level, impulsive noise OEL, action level, and key factors of risk assessment.ConclusionsIndicators such as Lmax, noise dose, Lpeak, and action level can be incorporated into noise OELs. Developing noise OEL standards should consider the co-exposure of noise and ototoxic substances, HPD's noise attenuation, susceptible groups, and noise's temporal structure.Keywords: Noise, Occupational exposure limit, Standard, Exposure control, Review

Introduction

Noise is one of the most common occupational hazards in the workplace. Long-term exposure to occupational noise can cause hearing loss and hearing defects (e.g., tinnitus and auditory allergy); it can also result in stress, cardiovascular diseases, and cognitive decline [1]. Noise-induced hearing loss (NIHL) has been a worldwide public health problem. In 2019, excessive noise caused 26.61 % (25.04–28.25 %) of hearing loss in workers aged 15–49 worldwide [2]. In the United States, approximately 22 million workers were exposed to hazardous occupational noise with 16 % experiencing significant hearing impairment and about 33 % of working-age adults with a history of occupational noise exposure showed audiometric evidence of NIHL [3]. In China, occupational NIHL was the second largest occupational disease after pneumoconiosis, with a prevalence of 21.3 % [4].

International Labor Organization (ILO) stated that occupational exposure limits (OELs) for noise should be developed to prevent hearing impairment, interference with information exchange for safety, and eliminate neurological fatigue due to occupational noise [5]. Currently, developing noise OELs aims to prevent the risk of NIHL [1,6].

Based on measurement time, noise OELs can be categorized into time-weighted average permissible limits (PEL-TWA) and ceiling limits (PEL-C). PEL-TWA is determined using the energy-averaged metric, i.e., equivalent sound pressure level (Leq, including metrics LEX,8h and LEX,40h) [[7], [8], [9]]. Leq is an energy metric for noise level based on the "equal energy hypothesis (EEH)." The EEH assumes that the hearing loss caused by noise is proportional to the noise energy without considering the temporal structure (energy distribution over time) [[10], [11], [12], [13]].

According to the difference in temporal structure, industrial noise is usually categorized as steady-state noise and non-steady noise (i.e., complex noise that includes impulsive components in noise exposure). Non-steady noise, as a primary noise type in the workplace, contains high-energy impulse components with a complex temporal structure [[9], [10], [11]]. Kurtosis, defined as the ratio of the fourth-order central moment to the squared second-order central moment of a distribution, describes the tendency for a sound to have high amplitude events that depart substantially from the underlying, continuous, steady-state noise. Kurtosis can quantify the impulsiveness of complex noise and is much more practical as a specific metric for the temporal structure of complex noise [[9], [10], [11]]. Evidence from animal experiments and epidemiological studies suggested that kurtosis was an essential risk factor for NIHL in addition to noise energy, and applying existing noise exposure standards based on the EEH without considering temporal structure might lead to a significant underestimation of hearing loss caused by non-steady noise [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15]]. The kurtosis-adjusted LAeq metric could effectively assess occupational hearing loss associated with non-steady noise and significantly improve the underestimation of hearing loss predicted by the ISO 1999 model [9]. Therefore, kurtosis and its adjustment for LAeq could be used to modify the EEH and relevant noise exposure standards, including noise OELs.

A comparison of noise levels at workplaces with noise OELs is critical to identify noise hazards, characterize the risk of NIHL, and conduct risk management. By assessing whether the noise level exceeds the OEL, workers with high noise exposure levels are identified, and subsequent hearing protection measures can be taken. In addition, countries such as the United States and European countries have developed noise risk management measures based on action levels (ALs) to encourage increased employer motivation [16]. In recent years, the combined exposure to noise and ototoxic compounds (e.g., organic solvents, heavy metals, and asphyxiants) have been a concern due to their potential to increase the risk of NIHL [17,18]. Compared with the control group, co-exposure to noise and mixed solvents could increase the risk of hearing loss by 2.95 times [19]. Up to a six-fold increase in hearing loss risk of co-exposure to noise and chemicals (e.g., fuel, solvent, and paint) among farmers and ranchers was reported compared with a three-fold increase in risk of noise exposure alone [20]. Despite theinsufficient evidence for a dose-response relationship between hearing loss and the combined effects of ototoxic substances and noise, it is clear that co-exposure can aggravate workers' hearing loss.

Regulations, legislation, and standards are usually acknowledged measures to control noise at workplaces developed by official or authoritative institutes [21]. Currently, there have been few literature reviews on the progress of noise OELs. In 2011 and 2014, Suter et al. compared noise OELs, exchange rates (ERs), and the noise levels for noise engineering controls and audiometric testing among 22 countries or international organizations [21,22]. However, there is still a research gap that comparisons of these elements in different noise OEL standards or guidelines are limited. The comparisons among different countries or organizations need to be further expanded regarding the definition of noise types, exposure assessment indicators, risk factors (e.g., co-exposure of ototoxic substances, susceptible groups, and noise's temporal structure), and noise control strategies.

In this study, a systematic literature review was used to analyze noise OEL standards, regulations, and legislation among different countries and organizations regarding noise types, OELs, risk factors, and risk management measures, and summarize the proposals for revising the noise OELs retrieved from literature, providing a basis for further revising and improving the noise OEL standards.

Methods

A systematic review method was used. The literature was retrieved from two sources. One source was the official websites of each country's governing bodies (including the official websites of governments and occupational safety and health administrative bodies) and international organizations. The other source involved academic literature retrieval databases.

Search for regulations, legislation, and standards on the official websites

This paper selected three international organizations (including ILO, ISO, and EU) and 35 countries spanning 6 continents (including Asia, Africa, Europe, North America, South America, and Oceania) for comparison. Referring to Suter's research methodology [21,22], regulations or standards related to OELs for noise were retrieved from the official websites of respective countries and organizations.

Information on definitions of different noise types, OELs, noise levels for certain risk management measures, and risk factors for NIHL will be extracted from the documents searched. If some regulations, legislation, or standards do not specify the definitions of different types of noise, relevant guidelines or technical guides will be considered and searched on the official websites of occupational safety and health administrative bodies, such as KOSHA in South Korea and DOSH in Malaysia. In addition, ISO 1999, an authoritative standard for NIHL estimation, was also considered despite its inexplicit stipulation of noise OELs.

Steady noise, also known as "stationary noise" or "stable noise” , was generally defined as noise with negligible or less than 3 or 5 dB(A) in SPL fluctuation during an observation period. The extent of fluctuation in noise levels was the major consideration in distinguishing steady noise from non-steady noise in most countries [26].

There was no consensus on the definition of non-steady noise (also known as "variable noise", "fluctuating noise", "changing noise", "non-stationary noise", "unstable noise" or "time-varying noise") in different standards. ILO defined non-steady noise as noise whose level shifts significantly during the observation period, and it was classified as fluctuating noise, intermittent noise, and impulsive noise. Many countries considered a change in noise level. For example, China, Malaysia, and Thailand used 3 dB(A) to distinguish between steady and non-steady noise. At the same time, ISO, Russia, Spain, Mexico, and Chile classified the two noise types by 5 dB(A).

The definition of impulse noise was qualitatively or quantitatively developed based on three elements (i.e. duration, interval, and amplitude). In terms of the duration of each impulse, ILO, Brazil, NIOSH, ACGIH, and Russia stipulated that the duration of an impulse was less than 1 s; China stipulated it less than 0.5 s; other countries only qualitatively characterized it by a rapid sound pressure for a very short time. Regarding the interval between impulses, China, the Philippines, Chile, Spain, and Brazil stipulated that the interval between two impulses was >1 s, and Portugal stipulated it > 0.2 s. As far as the amplitude of the impulse was concerned, Australia, Malaysia, the USA, and South Africa qualitatively defined the amplitude of the impulsive noise as higher than that of the background noise or the normal noise; China and Thailand quantitatively stipulated the change of rms of SPL ≥40 dB(A).

In addition, Spain and Russia stipulated that the difference between SPLs measured in the "Impulse" response and the "Slow" response was >3 dB, 5 dB(A) and 7 dB(A), respectively.

The PEL-C for noise included the maximum allowable limit [expressed by maximum sound pressure level (Lmax)] for continuous noise and the peak noise limit [expressed by peak sound pressure level (Lpeak)] for impulsive noise. For PEL-C of continuous noise, 39.4 % of the included countries or organizations used the Lmax indicator with the time and frequency weights of "S "and "A," ranging from 105 to 140 dB. For PEL-C of impulsive noise, most countries mainly used the "Impulse" response as time weighting and A, C, or unweighting as frequency weighting. 47.4 % of the included countries [e.g., Australia, Malaysia, and EU countries (e.g. Greece, Portugal)] set peak OEL at 140 dB(C); 13.2 % of the included countries, such as the OSHA in the USA, Thailand, and the Philippines, set 140 dB as peak OEL; Several countries like Russia, Denmark, and Germany set it at 137 dB(C); Poland and Switzerland set it at 135 dB(C); Brazil used both C-weighted and unweighted Lpeak, which were 120 dB(C) and 130 dB, respectively. Only a few countries, such as China, JSOH in Japan, India, and South Korea, set OEL for impulsive noise by peak level and impulse number per day.

Comparisons of noise exposure risk management among countries

Comparing noise measurement results with noise OELs or action levels (AL) was a basis for noise exposure risk management. Table 2 shows that LEX,8h was the primary evaluation index of noise control in various countries. Many countries or organizations, such as Australia, Malaysia, and the European Union, also considered the Lpeak value of impulsive noise. Only a few countries, such as Malaysia and Thailand, considered the Lmax as the indicator for noise control measures.

When a worker's noise exposure level exceeds the OEL or AL, the employer shall take feasible risk management measures to reduce the worker's noise exposure risk. Noise risk management measures include elimination, replacement, engineering and administrative controls, personal hearing protection device (HPD), medical monitoring (including baseline, periodic, hearing test confirmation), risk communication (risk notification and warning signs), and employee training.

Noise exposure risk management based on OELs

Table 2 shows 22 countries or organizations that have initiated noise risk management measures based on 8-h OEL. Eleven countries or organizations including NIOSH in the United States, Australia, Argentina, India, Korea, Thailand, Peru, Philippines, Russia, Singapore, and South Africa initiate various risk management measures at the same level as the 8-h OEL value. Six countries (including China, Mexico, Czech Republic, Malaysia, Japan, and Canada) used multiple noise levels, including OEL and other noise levels not defined as action levels, to initiate various noise management measures. For example, in Mexico, the noise level for engineering and administrative controls was 90 dB(A); the levels for medical monitoring (or wearing HPDs or conducting hazard communication) were 85 dB(A); the level for training was 80 dB(A). The rest of the countries or organizations do not provide information about various noise risk management measures or only a single measure.

Noise exposure risk management based on ALs

Table 3 shows noise risk management measures based on ALs among countries or organizations. The single AL values adopted by Brazil, Chile, and OSHA are 80, 82, and 85 dB(A) respectively. According to the Directive of the European Union (Directive 2003/10/EC. 2003), the United Kingdom, Turkey, and most EU member states have formulated lower and upper ALs in LEX,8h, i.e., the lower value was 80 dB(A), and the upper value was 85 dB(A). When noise exposure exceeds the lower AL, employers must inform of the noise risk, train employees, and provide HPD and preventive hearing examinations. When noise exposure exceeds the upper AL, employers must implement engineering and administrative measures, set up warning signs, and ensure that employees wear hearing HPD and have the right to a hearing examination. Belgium and Portugal also stipulated the hearing test duration for workers according to the upper and lower ALs.

The 8-h OEL and ER specified in Brazil and Singapore's technical guidelines or standards were 85 dB(A) and 3 dB(A), respectively. Italy divided the noise risk level using the OEL and AL based on the EU directive and stipulated different risk management measures at the corresponding noise levels. Compared with the countries mentioned above, China's standard for noise hazardous classification was too lenient. For example, in many countries, the noise level for initiating management actions or engineering measures was 85 dB(A). Factors of concern in noise exposure risk assessment

Developing noise OELs should comprehensively assess the health risks. Some countries considered the risk of combined exposure to noise and ototoxic substances (Table 5) or vibration. In the directive issued by the European Union, in the risk assessment part, attention should be paid to the risks caused by the combined exposure of noise and ototoxic organic substances or vibration. Australia, Brazil, ACGIH, Germany, Switzerland, South Korea, and Argentina listed the ototoxic substances (including organic solvents, heavy metals and their compounds, and carbon monoxide) that should be considered in the noise risk assessment. The common ototoxic chemicals listed by different countries included toluene, carbon disulfide, lead, and carbon monoxide. In addition, EU member states (e.g., Sweden and Germany) have considered the impact of noise on individuals at specific physiological stages. In their noise-related regulations, special attention should be paid to the impact of noise on vulnerable groups (pregnancy, breastfeeding, and minors).

Discussion

Tables 1–1 and Tables 1 and 2 demonstrate that in the noise criteria of different countries, industrial noise was classified into steady noise and non-steady noise. It also can be classified into continuous noise and intermittent noise. Furthermore, industrial noise could be classified based on the noise's temporal structure (expressed by kurtosis). In recent years, animal experiments and human investigation have shown that noise kurtosis is a sensitive indicator reflecting noise structure, which can be used to classify the noise type, including low-kurtosis, medium-kurtosis, and high-kurtosis noise [98]. Tables 1–1 and Tables 1 and 2 show that countries did not reach a consensus on the definition of non-steady noise. Most countries or organizations defined non-steady noise as noise whose level shifted significantly during the observation period, with fluctuations >3 or 5 dB(A). The definition reflected the change in noise energy within a period. However, it cannot reflect the noise's temporal structure, i.e., the temporal distribution of noise energy that contains three elements of an impulse, i.e., a peak value of sound pressure, duration time of the peak, and interval between two peaks [6,98]. Scholars proposed that non-steady complex noise was composed of transient high-energy impulsive noise superimposed on Gaussian background noise, with a non-normal and time-varying energy distribution [98,99]. It could be medium-kurtosis or high-kurtosis noise. The definition considered the noise's temporal structure, aligns with the characteristics of non-steady noise in real-world occupational environments. Therefore, the definition and classifications of non-steady noise based on kurtosis can be applied in modifying noise exposure standards or guidelines.

Table 2, Table 3 demonstrate there was no consensus on noise OEL indicators. For instance, ILO, India, and Poland applied the Lmax indicator as the maximum permissible limit for continuous noise; the European Union, China, and Austria did not formulate the Lmax limit. In addition, the USA OSHA and NIOSH recommended the noise dose based on 8-h OEL and ER for noise exposure assessment, considering the noise level during different exposure durations. Adopting the Lmax and noise dose as noise OEL indicators was necessary to thoroughly assess workers' noise exposure risk for those countries improving noise OEL standards.

LEX,8h was the primary indicator for occupational noise exposure assessment and noise risk management. However, it was suitable to qualify the steady noise based on the EEH, but not appropriate for non-steady complex noise in the workplace, leading to underestimating the risk of NIHL. Kurtosis, as an additional metric for noise energy, might correct the limitation of EEH. Zhang M. et al. proposed a new indicator of LEX,8h adjusted by kurtosis. The adjustment formula was: LEX,8h-K = LEX,8h + 6.5 × lg (βN/3). Compared with the noise energy indicator, the recommended indicator (LEX,8h-K) could significantly decrease the underestimation of NIPTS predicted by the ISO 1999 model and effectively evaluate the hearing loss associated with non-steady complex noise. The authors recommended that the kurtosis metric and kurtosis-adjusted LEX,8h, be included in modifying noise exposure standards [95].

Effective assessment of the risk of impulsive noise remains a complicated problem. Table 2, Table 3 illustrate that the OEL of impulsive noise was based on two methods: (1) C-weighted or unweighted peak SPL (e.g., in the USA OHSA, ACGIH, EU member countries, and Thailand); (2) noise energy composed of peak SPL and impulse numbers (e.g., in China, India, and Korea), e.g., a tenfold increase in the impulse numbers, peak SPL was adjusted by 10 dB(A) [[100], [101], [102]]. Neither method effectively quantifies the risk of impulsive or impact noise. Some important factors influencing the noise exposure risk of impulsive noise included noise energy, spectrum, peak, interval, and duration [102,103]. A single peak SPL failed to take critical factors other than noise intensity (or spectrum) into account. Human and animal evidence indicated that impulsive noise or complex noise with impulse/impact components was more hazardous to hearing than continuous steady (Gaussian) noise at similar noise exposure levels [[104], [105], [106], [107], [108]]. In addition, the impulsive noise criteria were mainly based on military research. The exposure patterns of military impulsive noise (peaks ranging from 150 to 180 dB, impulse duration on the order of microseconds) quite differed from those of industrial impulsive noise (high background noise, peaks often below 140 dB, impulse duration on the order of milliseconds) [109]. Since industrial impulsive noise was also a specific non-steady noise due to its particular temporal structure, it could be effectively measured and evaluated using the kurtosis metric and its adjustment for noise level.

Wearing HPD can reduce the effective dose of noise on the human ear. Some countries have considered the noise attenuation effect of HPD in noise OEL standards. For example, the European Union Directive issued in 2003 stated that when using noise OELs, the determination of workers' exposure should consider the attenuation provided by the individual HPD, while AL did not need to consider the role of any HPD [69]. The OEL for those considering HPD is based on the calculation of the effective sound pressure level while wearing the HPD. This requires that the attenuation effect of HPDs on noise at various frequencies and the actual wear of the worker (e.g., HPD type and wearing method) be consistent with that in an experimental setting [[110], [111], [112]]. However, many studies have shown that the attenuation of HPD measured in an experimental environment was significantly higher than that for workers. The underestimation of the effectiveness of wearing HPD would put workers at risk of increasing noise exposure. Future research is needed to evaluate the use of HPDs by workers and the noise attenuation effects of HPDs to facilitate consideration of the noise attenuation effects of HPDs in the development of OELs and to improve the assessment of ear noise levels while wearing HPDs.

Noise exposure control strategies include elimination, substitution, engineering control, administrative control, and individual protection. Table 2, Table 3 show that OELs and AL are essential bases for noise hierarchy control. AL refers to the level of hazardous and toxic substances that lead to a health risk to workers and require preventive measures [113]. AL is usually set at half the exposure limit dose. However, the actual set level varies from standard to standard to determine that most random sampling results are below the level of OEL [113]. Some countries like Brazil, Chile, and the USA (OSHA) took half of the 8-h OEL noise dose as the AL for noise control [e.g., LEX,8h OEL of OSHA was 90 dB(A), ER was 5 dB(A), the half of LEX,8h OEL dose was 85 dB(A)]; The EU set upper and lower ALs. Since noise OELs alone may discourage employers from implementing noise management because of the difficulty in reducing workplace noise levels to the noise OEL, setting ALs below OELs can encourage employers to adopt preventive noise risk control measures. In addition, as shown in Table 4, Singapore, Brazil, China, and Italy have adopted noise risk classification management to inform employers to take noise control measures when workers are exposed to a particular noise level, also protecting noise-susceptible populations. These measures could save costs and reduce noise emissions [114,115]. Therefore, many scholars suggested that noise OELs should be combined with the action level, and employers should be encouraged to take preventive measures to protect workers' hearing health before noise levels exceed the OEL [86].

Table 5 shows that a few countries, such as EU member states and ACGIH of the United States, require consideration of the impact of ototoxic organic substances on hearing loss in noise risk assessments. The United Kingdom, Poland, and Slovakia proposed protective measures for high-risk populations, not just those exposed to over-OEL noise levels. Currently, many countries do not consider the risk of hearing loss caused by co-exposure to noise OELs standards, and few procedures or methods for co-exposure risk assessment in current noise standards. Studies have shown that workers exposed to noise and ototoxic organics could develop hearing loss at levels lower than OELs of noise and ototoxic chemicals [90,113,116]. Future research must focus on the effects of combined exposure to ototoxic chemicals and industrial noise on hearing loss, including the mechanism of action, mode of action (additive or synergistic effect), and dose-response relationship between combined exposure and hearing loss. In addition, a few studies have also shown a synergistic effect between noise and vibration, but sufficient experimental evidence is still needed [117].

People with high susceptibility may be more impacted by noise and suffer more severe hearing loss. EU member states (e.g., Sweden and Germany) stated that special attention should be paid to the impact of noise on highly susceptible populations (e.g., pregnant women and fetuses) when conducting noise risk assessments. Animal and human studies have shown an association between noise exposure (especially low-frequency and high-level noise) during pregnancy and hearing loss in offspring [[118], [119], [120]]. A prospective cohort study of pregnant women showed a 1.05-fold and 1.27-fold increased risk of childhood hearing impairment in the 75–84 dB(A) and ≥85 dB(A) groups, respectively, compared with the control group (<75 dBA) (Selander et al., 2016). In addition, studies reported that people with cochlear dysplasia or increased fragility were a high noise-susceptibility population.

Conclusions

Based on the above findings, the conclusions were as follows: Lmax, noise dose, Lpeak indicators, and action level can be incorporated into noise OELs. Developing noise OEL standards should consider the co-exposure between noise and risk factors (e.g., ototoxic substances), HPD's noise attenuation, susceptible groups, and noise's temporal structure.
