In vitro bioactive metabolite production and plant regeneration of medicinal plant Ligusticum chuanxiong Hort
Authors: Shunlu Chen, Xiaoke Wei, Xiang Chen, Qian Mao, Ruoshi Li, Yun Zhen, Yanghuan Xing, Mengmeng Wu, Chi Song, Guihua Jiang, Xianmei Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ligusticum chuanxiong Hort. is a renowned genuine medicinal material from Sichuan with significant medicinal value. However, the lack of an efficient tissue culture system and quality deterioration in conventional cultivation—caused by pests, diseases, environmental pollution, and continuous cropping obstacles—have hindered the sustainable utilization and industrial development of its superior germplasm resources. This study successfully developed an efficient regeneration system via suspension cell cultures of L. chuanxiong. Callus induction from petioles on MS medium supplemented with 1.5 mg/L 2,4-D, 1.5 mg/L 6-BA, and 30 g/L sucrose achieved an induction rate of 86.15%. The callus was then transferred to liquid MS medium containing 1 mg/L 2,4-D and 1 mg/L 6-BA at an inoculation density of 40 g/L, yielding embryogenic suspension cells capable of sustained growth and division. When cultured on solid MS medium with 1 mg/L 6-BA and 0.50 mg/L NAA, the suspension cell aggregates achieved a shoot regeneration rate of 93.64%. The highest rooting rate (76.67%) was obtained on MS medium containing 1 mg/L IBA and 0.5 mg/L NAA. Rooted plantlets transplanted into pots with a peat: perlite ratio (2:1 v/v) showed a survival rate of 90.91% after 3–4 weeks. Genetic fidelity analysis using four ISSR primers revealed no somaclonal variation. Treatment with 100 μmol/L salicylic acid increased senkyunolide A and senkyunolide I contents by 10.10-fold and 25.54-fold, respectively, while 150 μmol/L salicylic acid enhanced butylidenephthalide, ligustilide, caffeic acid, and biomass by 10.29-fold, 3.73-fold, 2.84-fold, and 1.27-fold, respectively. This study accomplished rapid, large-scale plant regeneration through L. chuanxiong suspension cells, offering essential support for industrial propagation, an ideal cell source for genetic transformation, and key solutions to germplasm degradation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08506-w.

Introduction

Ligusticum chuanxiong Hort. (Apiaceae) is a medicinal and edible plant widely recognized for its therapeutic and culinary value. In several Asian countries, its rhizomes have traditionally been used as dietary supplements and herbal remedies for the prevention and treatment of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases. Bioactive compounds isolated from L. chuanxiong, including ligustrazine, ferulic acid, and ligustilide, which demonstrated antioxidative, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and hepatoprotective properties [1, 2]. Clinically, it has been widely used in treating cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases such as atherosclerosis, angina pectoris, and hypertension [3, 4].

No wild populations of L. chuanxiong remained, and the plants did not flower or set seeds, relying primarily on an asexual propagation method described as "Mountain Breeding, Dam Cultivation " for cultivation [5]. This propagation mode led to reduced genetic diversity and weakened adaptability and stress resistance in progeny [6]. L. chuanxiong was relatively sensitive to soil conditions, with both its growth and medicinal quality being influenced by soil quality. Studies have indicated that soils in traditional planting areas of L. chuanxiong contained elevated levels of heavy metals such as cadmium [7, 8]. Resulting in frequent exceedances of heavy metal limits in the medicinal material and seriously compromising its safety. Excessive application of pesticides, plant growth regulators, and chemical fertilizers during cultivation further caused environmental pollution and residual exceedances in the herb, thereby reducing quality and undermining market competitiveness. Frequent disease and pest outbreaks remained key constraints on yield and quality. Together with concerns over the safety and efficacy of the medicinal material, L. chuanxiong repeatedly faced export restrictions [9], posing severe challenges to the sustainable utilization and industrial development of its resources. Therefore, promoting facility-based, soilless, and standardized cultivation techniques became an important approach for improving the quality and yield of L. chuanxiong and achieving sustainable industrial development.

Rapid plant tissue propagation allowed for year-round seedling production indoors, offering advantages such as short cycles, fast reproduction, and preservation of desirable plant traits [10]. In vitro culture technology has emerged as a vital tool for efficient plant regeneration and large-scale propagation. As an important medicinal species, research on tissue culture techniques for L. chuanxiong dates back to the 1980 s [11]. And primarily focused on callus induction, adventitious bud differentiation, rooting culture, and accumulation of secondary metabolites. In recent years, some researchers have successfully established a rapid propagation system for L. chuanxiong by optimizing medium composition and hormone combinations and using stem tips as explants, thereby shortening the tissue culture cycle and achieving rapid plant multiplication [12]. Although preliminary progress had been made in the tissue culture of L. chuanxiong, it still faced multiple challenges, including long culture cycles, low regeneration efficiency, and high contamination rates.

Compared with solid culture, liquid suspension culture demonstrated advantages including faster growth rate, higher proliferation capacity, greater biological activity, and more stable growth, significantly improving cell proliferation efficiency and shortening the culture cycle [13]. Plant regeneration or genetic transformation from uniform suspension cultures effectively alleviated the inhibition of differentiation caused by callus browning and shortened the culture period. Key parameters identified during suspension culture—such as inoculation density, cell doubling time, and subculture interval—could be effectively used to determine the suitable stage of suspension cells for genetic transformation [14]. Zhan et al. established a suspension cell culture system for L. chuanxiong using MS liquid medium supplemented with 0.5 mg/L 6-BA, 2.0 mg/L 2,4-D, and 30 g/L sucrose. Using GC–MS, dozens of compounds were identified in the suspension cells, including isopropyl stearate, dodecyl acrylate, and 1,2-benzenedicarboxylic acid [15]. The use of in vitro culture techniques to produce medicinal plants has become an important means of achieving efficient plant regeneration and large-scale propagation. Compared with traditional cultivation, suspension cell culture for secondary metabolite production offered advantages such as environmental independence and product homogeneity [16]. And plant cell culture was recognized as a promising alternative method for obtaining valuable secondary metabolites [17]. The application of elicitors—such as methyl jasmonate and salicylic acid—in suspension cultures enhances secondary metabolite accumulation by activating plant defense mechanisms. This approach represents a major biotechnological strategy for large-scale production and is widely adopted in plant tissue culture [18].

Although preliminary progress had been made in both tissue culture research and the chemical analysis of suspension cell cultures of L. chuanxiong, a well-established micropropagation system remained unavailable, and no successful plant regeneration from suspension cultures had been reported. In this study, we developed a renewable cell suspension system for L. chuanxiong and established a more efficient micropropagation protocol, and regenerated plants were first successfully obtained from L. chuanxiong suspension cells. This work provided ideal material for genetic transformation research and offered a reference for factory-scale seedling production, further in-depth research, and sustainable utilization of L. chuanxiong.

Materials and methods

Plant material and sterilization methods

Plant materials were collected from the " High-Quality Chuanxiong Ecological Planting Scientific Research Demonstration Base " in Dujiangyan City, Sichuan Province. After collection, the plants were cultivated in the L. chuanxiong Hort. nursery of the Medicinal Plant Garden at Chengdu University of Traditional Chinese Medicine. Younger petioles, leaves, stem segments, and stem nodes were selected as explants. The explants were rinsed under running water for over one hour and then transferred to a laminar flow hood. They were sterilized by immersion in 5% NaClO for 3 min, followed by washing with sterile water. This was followed by disinfection with 75% ethanol for 2 min and washing with sterile water, a step which was repeated once. Finally, the explants were rinsed twice with sterile water, and the leaves were placed on sterilized filter paper to remove excess moisture.

Induction of callus and initiation of suspension cultures

The sterilized explants were cut into 1–2 cm segments and inoculated onto MS solid media supplemented with various combinations of 6-BA, 2,4-D, NAA, KT, and IAA at different concentrations to induce callus formation. After callus formation, the calli were subcultured on the induction medium for approximately 15 days. Calli exhibiting good viability, a loose and friable texture, and no browning were selected and inoculated into MS liquid media containing different concentrations of 2,4-D, 6-BA, and NAA. MS medium without any plant growth regulators was used as the control. Suspension cultures were established in 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks, each containing 100 mL of medium. The suspensions were incubated on a gyratory shaker at 120 RPM and 25 ℃ in the dark. The medium was replaced every 15 days by removing 90% of the old medium and adding fresh medium. After one growth cycle, the suspension cells were subcultured 2–3 times repeatedly.

Optimization of suspension cell culture conditions

The effects of culture conditions on growth rate and bioactive metabolite content were investigated using MS liquid medium (pH 5.8) supplemented with sucrose (30 g/L), 1 mg/L 6-BA, and 0.5 mg/L 2,4-D, in 150 mL flasks on a shaker at 100 rpm. The photoperiod for the cell suspensions was optimized at four levels: 0/24, 8/16, 12/12, and 24/0 (light/dark hours), and cultures were maintained for 15 days. The culture temperature was optimized at three levels: 20℃, 25℃, and 30℃, for 15 days. The initial inoculation density was optimized at five levels: 20 g/L, 40 g/L, 60 g/L, 80 g/L, and 100 g/L, the volume of the culture medium was set at 100 mL, for 15 days. Based on the optimal conditions determined from the above studies, the relative growth of suspension cells at different culture times (3, 6, 9, 12, 15 days) was investigated to plot the growth curve and infer the logarithmic and exponential growth phases of the suspension cells. For each level of every culture condition, three replicates were set up. After reaching the target days, the cells were collected by vacuum filtration, and the cells were collected on filter paper. The collected suspension cells were blotted to remove residual surface liquid medium, weighed, and transferred to 50 mL centrifuge tubes. The fresh weight of cells from all five inoculum densities was recorded at the end of the growth cycle. Identification of chemical constituents in the suspension cells of Ligusticum chuanxiong

Preparation of suspension cell extracts and internal standard

To elucidate the metabolite profile of the suspension cells, three replicate samples were selected. The suspension cells were subjected to vacuum freeze-drying for 63 h and subsequently pulverized. A 30 mg aliquot of the resulting powder was weighed, and 1.5 mL of a −20℃ pre-cooled 70% aqueous methanol internal standard extraction solution was added. The internal standard extraction solution was prepared by first dissolving 1 mg of the standard compound in 1 mL of 70% aqueous methanol to obtain a 1 mg/mL stock solution. This 1 mg/mL stock solution was then further diluted with 70% aqueous methanol to prepare a 0.25 mg/mL internal standard solution. The mixture was vortexed every 30 min for 30 s each time, repeating this process six times in total. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was collected. The samples were filtered through a microporous membrane (0.22 μm pore size) and stored in injection vials for subsequent UPLC-MS/MS analysis.

UPLC-MS conditions

Chromatographic separation was performed using an Agilent SB-C18 column (1.8 µm, 2.1 mm 100 mm; Agilent Technologies, Germany). The mobile phase consisted of ultrapure water with 0.1% formic acid (A) and acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid (B). The elution gradient was programmed as follows: 5% B at 0.00 min, linearly increased to 95% B over 9.00 min, maintained at 95% B for 1 min, decreased to 5% B from 10.00 to 11.10 min, and then equilibrated at 5% B until 14.00 min. The flow rate was set at 0.35 mL/min, the column temperature was maintained at 40 ℃, and the injection volume was 2 µL.

An electrospray ionization (ESI) source was operated at a temperature of 500 ℃. The ion spray voltage (IS) was set at 5500 V for positive ion mode and −4500 V for negative ion mode. The ion source gas I (GSI), gas II (GSII), and curtain gas (CUR) were set at 50, 60, and 25 psi, respectively, and the collision-induced ionization parameter was set to high. Multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) scans were used with the QQQ, and the collision gas (nitrogen) was set to medium. The declustering potential (DP) and collision energy (CE) for each MRM ion pair were optimized individually. Specific sets of MRM ion pairs were monitored according to the metabolites eluting within each period.

Data analysis was conducted at Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, Hubei, China). Compounds were identified by querying the company's in-house database (MetWare Database, MWDB) and matching the secondary mass spectra. Redundant signals from irrelevant fragment ions were excluded during the analysis.

Effect of Salicylic Acid (SA) on metabolites and biomass in suspension cells

SA treatment method

On day 7 of the suspension cell culture with an initial inoculation density of 40 g/L, filter-sterilized (SA) solution, prepared using a 0.22 μm sterile membrane filter, was added to the cultures. The final SA concentrations in each flask were adjusted to 0, 50, 100, 150, and 200 μmol/L, respectively. Each concentration treatment was performed in triplicate. On day 14 of the suspension culture process, cell growth was observed, the suspension cells were collected, and the final fresh weight was measured to calculate the relative growth increase.

Preparation of SA-treated suspension cell extracts and internal standard

For the SA-treated suspension cells, 0.5 g of the freeze-dried powder (lyophilized for 63 h) was weighed and extracted with 10 mL of methanol via ultrasonication for 2 h. The mixture was then refluxed until nearly dry. The residue was dissolved in 0.5 mL of methanol and filtered through a 0.22 μm microporous membrane.

The internal standard extraction solution was prepared by separately weighing 0.1 mg each of the following reference standards: Senkyunolide-I, Senkyunolide-A, Butylidenephthalide, Ligustilide, and Caffeate. Each standard was dissolved in 1 mL of methanol to prepare 0.1 mg/mL stock solutions. These 1000 μg/mL stock solutions were then serially diluted with methanol to obtain internal standard solutions at concentrations of 500, 250, 125, 62.5, 37.25, and 18.625 μg/mL. During preparation, the mixtures were vortexed every 30 min for 30 s each time, with this process repeated six times in total. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected, filtered through a 0.22 μm microporous membrane, and stored in injection vials for UPLC-MS analysis.

UPLC chromatographic conditions

A WondaSil C18-WR column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm; Agilent Technologies, Germany) was used for the analysis. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and methanol (B). The elution gradient was programmed as follows: 10% B at 0.00 min, linearly increasing to 100% B over 8.00 min, maintained at 100% B for 25 min, then decreased to 10% B from 25.00 to 27.10 min, and finally equilibrated at 10% B until 28.00 min. The flow rate was set at 0.5 mL/min, the detection wavelength was set at 280 nm, the column temperature was maintained at 30 °C, and the injection volume was 5 μL.

Investigation of suspension cell differentiation

The suspension cells that had been subcultured twice were blotted with filter paper to remove residual liquid medium, and different concentrations of 6-BA, NAA, and IBA were tested for adventitious bud induction. Approximately ten suspension cell clumps were inoculated per petri dish, with three petri dishes for each hormone combination. The culture conditions were set at 25 °C with a 16-h photoperiod. When the adventitious buds grew to approximately 1 cm, they were transferred to a strengthening medium to obtain more robust adventitious buds.

Study on developmental morphology of suspension cells

To better understand the dynamics of suspension cell morphogenesis, samples were collected at four stages: the friable callus stage, the suspension cell aggregate stage, the initial bud differentiation stage, and the leaf formation stage. Sample sections were prepared according to the paraffin section method described by Li [3, 19]. Briefly, samples from each stage were fixed in FAA solution (ethanol: glacial acetic acid, 3:1 v/v) at 4℃ overnight for at least 12 h. After fixation, the samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (70%, 80%, 90%, and 100%) at 4℃ for 2 h each. The dehydrated samples were immersed in a gradient of an environmentally friendly dewaxing solution and ethanol ratio (1:1 v/v) for 30 min, followed by washing with tap water. The plant tissue sections were then stained with toluidine blue for about 2 min, rinsed with water, examined under a microscope, rinsed again with tap water, and dried in an oven at 60℃. The sections were cleared in clean xylene for 5 min and mounted with neutral balsam. The sections were examined under a microscope, and images were acquired and analyzed.

Effect of different conditions on root induction and soils on seedling acclimatization and transplantation

Zhu and Wang showed that IBA and NAA combinations promoted rooting in L. chuanxiong [20, 21]. As IBA is the most reliable auxin for adventitious rooting and NAA rapidly stimulates root proliferation, both are standard in medicinal plant research. Therefore, we focused on their effects on root induction in L. chuanxiong.Adventitious shoots longer than 2 cm were transferred to MS and ½MS media containing different concentrations of IBA and NAA for root induction. The number of rooted adventitious shoots and their growth and development were recorded. Seedlings with roots longer than 5 cm had their culture bottle caps opened for acclimatization in a greenhouse for 2 days. They were then washed with distilled water to remove residual medium and transplanted into pots containing different ratios of peat soil, perlite, and vermiculite. They were initially kept in darkness for 24 h, then cultured normally in the greenhouse, supplied with sufficient water and nutrients every 2–3 days. The survival rate of regenerated plantlets in each group was counted after one month. After successful acclimatization in the greenhouse for one month, the potted plants were transferred to open ground for further adaptation.

Analysis of regenerated plant fidelity

Leaf samples were randomly collected from 12 regenerated plants and the mother plant. Genomic DNA was extracted from the leaves using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method, and the DNA concentration was measured using a NANODrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). Data analysis

Data on callus formation were recorded every 15 days, and the induction rate was calculated. The fresh weight and relative growth of suspension cells were measured and recorded every 15 days. The number of calli forming buds after suspension cells were inoculated onto solid medium was recorded every 20 days, and the bud formation rate was calculated. Root growth in the rooting medium was observed, and data were collected every 15 days. All experiments were repeated three times. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 21 software. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed with a significance level of p ≤ 0.05. Duncan's multiple range test (DMRT) was used to determine significant differences among means for the same variable (p ≤ 0.05).

Results

Effects of different explants and exogenous hormones on callus induction in L. chuanxiong

Explant browning constituted a major obstacle in plant tissue culture. In this study, the browning rate of petioles was significantly lower than that of leaf blades, stem nodes, and stem segments. When petioles were used as explants on MS basal medium supplemented with 2,4-D 1.5 mg/L and 6-BA 1.5 mg/L, a substantial amount of callus was obtained within 10 days, with the highest induction rate reaching (86.15 ± 13.48)%. With the callus maintaining high viability and showing almost no browning. For leaf blades cultured on the same medium (MS with 2,4-D 1.5 mg/Land 6-BA 1.5 mg/L), the maximum callus induction rate achieved was (74.00 ± 8.19)%. Due to more severe browning (Fig. 1B), the callus induction capacity of leaf blades was generally weaker than that of petioles across most hormone combinations. When stems and stem nodes served as explants, severe browning was observed. Effects of different culture conditions on the growth of suspension cells

To enhance callus biomass and secondary metabolite accumulation during suspension cell culture, the culture conditions were optimized to obtain homogeneous and stable suspended callus. In this study, significant differences in the relative growth rate of suspension cells were observed under different conditions (Fig. 2). When the inoculation density was 40 g/L, the relative growth of the suspension cells was 1.23, 1.33, 1.31, and 1.36 times higher than that at inoculation densities of 20, 60, 80, and 100 g/L, respectively (Fig. 2A). The relative growth at a culture temperature of 25 ℃ was 1.26 and 1.36 times higher than that at 20℃ and 30℃, respectively (Fig. 2B). Under a photoperiod of 0/24 h (light/dark), the relative growth of the suspension cells was 1.18, 1.14, and 1.01 times that under 16/8 h, 12/12 h (light/dark), and continuous light, respectively (Fig. 2C). Biomass accumulation was nearly identical between the dark and continuous light groups. On the 6th day of culture, the relative growth of the suspension cells was 1.76, 1.41, 1.42, and 2.37 times that on the 3rd, 9th, 12th, and 15th days, respectively (Fig. 2D-E). Therefore, the logarithmic growth phase of the suspension cells was inferred to occur between days 6 and 9, closer to day 6, with a growth cycle of 15 days. The suitable culture conditions were preliminarily determined as follows: an inoculation density of 40 g/L, a temperature of 25℃, a photoperiod of either complete darkness or light, a logarithmic growth phase around day 7, and a culture cycle of approximately 15 days.Fig. 2Effects of different culture conditions on the suspension cell culture of L. chuanxiong. Investigation of chemical constituents in suspension cells

Broad-targeted metabolomic analysis of the L. chuanxiong suspension cells detected 3,005 metabolites, which were categorized into 13 distinct classes (Fig. 3A). Classes containing over 100 metabolites included: amino acids and their derivatives (482), phenolic acids (415), flavonoids (325), alkaloids (332), terpenoids (374), lipids (279), organic acids (120), nucleotides and their derivatives (127), lignins and coumarins (130), and other components (386). The suspension cell cultures of L. chuanxiong were found to contain various active constituents, including ferulic acid, caffeic acid, ligustilide, butylphthalide, butylidenephthalide, senkyunolide A, and senkyunolide I. Furthermore, terpenoids, flavonoids, and polysaccharides accounted for a relatively high proportion in the cultures (Appendix 1). After treating the suspension cells with different concentrations of salicylic acid (SA), UPLC analysis revealed that the contents of senkyunolide A and senkyunolide I reached their maximum levels following treatment with 100 μmol/L SA, which were 25.54 and 10.10 times higher, respectively, than those in the 0 μmol/L SA treatment. Treatment with 150 μmol/L SA resulted in the maximum levels of butylidenephthalide, ligustilide, and caffeic acid, which were 10.29, 3.73, and 2.84 times higher, respectively, than those in the 0 μmol/L SA treatment. The biomass also reached its maximum under the 150 μmol/L SA treatment, being 1.27 times that of the 0 μmol/L SA treatment (Fig. 3B). These findings indicated that SA treatment at appropriate concentrations promoted the accumulation of both biomass and secondary metabolites.

Adventitious shoot induction

Various plant growth regulators were applied in different combinations to optimize somatic embryogenesis and shoot development from cell suspension cultures; however, shoot formation was not successfully achieved in all medium combinations. When subcultured granular suspension cell aggregates were transferred to solid medium for shoot induction, under the hormonal condition of 1 mg/L 6-BA and 0.50 mg/L IBA, the shoot formation rate reached up to 93.64% (Fig. 4A). These aggregates maintained good viability during growth with almost no browning observed. Around 20 days of culture, continuously differentiating shoot buds became visible, and by day 30, true leaves had developed (Fig. 4B). In contrast, when callus was transferred to solid medium for shoot induction under the same hormonal combination (1 mg/L 6-BA and 0.50 mg/L IBA), growth was relatively slow, browning occurred, and no shoot buds had formed even after 40 days of culture (Fig. 4C). After three subcultures of suspension cells (equivalent to 45 days in liquid culture) under suitable conditions, the biomass increased approximately fivefold. Callus cultured on solid medium for 45 days failed to achieve comparable growth. For adventitious shoot induction, using suspension cell aggregates enabled more rapid acquisition of abundant induction material with minimal browning and a shortened cycle. Somatic differentiation and developmental stages

Somatic embryo development was observed under the microscope starting from the callus stage (Fig. 4D-G). The callus appeared light yellow with a loose texture, exhibiting large, irregularly shaped cells containing small nuclei and displaying disorganized arrangement (Fig. 4D). In contrast, suspension cell aggregates showed a light yellow coloration with a compact texture, composed of regularly shaped cells tightly arranged with large nuclei (Fig. 4E). During initial differentiation, the suspension cells turned yellowish-green with a dense texture, and wart-like protrusions became visible. These tissue centers exhibited strong mitotic activity, leading to cell differentiation. At early differentiation stages, meristematic centers along with shoot primordia and leaf primordia were observable (Fig. 4F). In the organogenesis differentiation phase of suspension cells, leaf epidermal cells and differentiated spiral vessel structures were observed. The cells had developed formed.

organs, with differentiated suspension cells possessing shaped leaves, while white protrusions indicated the origin of shoots (Fig. 4G). The investigation of various somatic differentiation and developmental stages further demonstrated that suspension cell aggregates differentiated more readily and served as a more ideal material source for obtaining regenerated plants of L. chuanxiong.

Root induction and plant transplantation

Root initiation was observed after 20 days of culture, and rooting results were quantified after 30 days (Fig. 5A). The rooting rate reached 73.33% on medium containing 1 mg/L IBA and 0.5 mg/L NAA. When the IBA concentration exceeded that of NAA in the combinations, a greater number of roots were produced (Fig. 5B-C); whereas higher NAA concentrations relative to IBA resulted in thicker root systems (Fig. 5D-E). The rooting rate decreased when the IBA concentration reached 1.5 mg/L. The lowest rooting rate was observed when both IBA and NAA concentrations were 1.5 mg/L. Under identical hormonal concentrations, MS basal medium yielded the best rooting results. Therefore, MS medium supplemented with 1 mg/L IBA and 0.5 mg/L NAA was selected as the most suitable rooting medium for L. chuanxiong, although the rooting rate did not exceed 80% in this study. Thus, more optimal rooting hormones and concentrations require further investigation. In this study, under the condition of peat soil: perlite ratio (2:1 v/v), the survival rate of tissue-cultured plantlets reached 90.91%. After one month of growth in the greenhouse, the number of roots multiplied, and rhizome structures appeared at the shoot base (Fig. 5F-H), indicating enhanced robustness of the regenerated plantlets for outdoor transplantation. Successful acclimatization and survival in open-field conditions were achieved (Fig. 5I). Discussion

Conventional cultivation practices are constrained by issues such as environmental pollution, biotic stresses, and soil degradation, often leading to germplasm decline and quality instability in medicinal plants [22]. Thus, deploying modern biotechnological approaches to establish optimized tissue culture platforms could help alleviate bottlenecks in medicinal plant conservation.

In plant tissue culture, different explant types exhibited varying regenerative capacities under identical culture conditions [13]. In this study, petioles showed the best callus induction efficiency when used as explants. Although stem nodes and segments were also tested for callus induction, they showed higher contamination rates, which was inconsistent with previous research [23]. This discrepancy could be attributed to differences in explant sterilization protocols, seasonal variations in the physiological status of donor plants, or genotypic differences in the L. chuanxiong materials used. Callus formation capacity might be related to the growth status of explants at the time of collection.

Multiple studies confirmed that 6-BA and 2,4-D exhibited synergistic effects at specific ratios and showed excellent efficacy in callus proliferation culture [24]. In this study, under hormonal conditions of 1 mg/L 2,4-D and 1 mg/L 6-BA, callus was subjected to shake flask culture, rapidly yielding large quantities of suspension cells in the optimal growth state. Compared with proliferation culture on solid medium, this method obtained substantial regeneration materials in a shorter time while maintaining good viability with almost no browning.

Inoculum density, temperature, light and culture duration affected plant cell growth. This study determined 40 g/L as the optimal density for L. chuanxiong suspension cells, with significantly higher relative growth than 20, 60, 80 and 100 g/L groups. Low density caused insufficient intercellular signaling and slow proliferation, while high density intensified nutrient competition and waste accumulation to inhibit growth, consistent with Salvia miltiorrhiza and Astragalus membranaceus membranaceus studies [25, 26]. Maximum biomass was achieved at 25 °C (vs. 20/30 °C), matching most medicinal plants [27, 28])Suspension cells relied on medium sucrose; light conferred limited benefits and was capable of inducing oxidative damage. so dark conditions favored growth, consistent with Panax ginseng and Eleutherococcus senticosus findings [29, 30].Growth peaked on day 6 (log phase: days 6–9; 15-day cycle), declining after day 15 due to nutrient depletion and waste accumulation. Uniform log-phase cells provided an ideal period for subsequent experiments, consistent with typical plant cell culture dynamics [31].

Suspension cell factories provided a promising alternative for producing plant secondary metabolites, potentially alleviating supply pressures on traditional Chinese medicine resources [32]. In this study, broad-target metabolomic analysis of L. chuanxiong suspension cells confirmed a diverse metabolite profile rich in phenolic acids and various characteristic phthalides, with high proportions of terpenoids, flavonoids, and polysaccharides, demonstrating significant potential for secondary metabolite production. This culture method also enabled stable synthesis of target compounds in a controlled environment while avoiding issues related to heavy metal contamination and pests, establishing an ideal platform for scalable production of active ingredients.

Through optimized culture conditions, elicitor treatment, and genetic engineering, suspension cells were shown to accumulate target secondary metabolites at levels surpassing those in intact plants [33]. It was demonstrated that an appropriate concentration of salicylic acid enhanced biomass accumulation and gypenoside biosynthesis in Gynostemma pentaphyllum [34]; and significantly increased lignan and neolignan biosynthesis in Linum usitatissimum cell cultures [35].

In this work, optimal salicylic acid concentration promoted L. chuanxiong cell growth and boosted the accumulation of ligustilide, senkyunolide A, senkyunolide I, butylphthalide, and caffeic acid, demonstrating the potential of suspension cells as an industrial-scale alternative to cultivated plants for producing these metabolites. Senkyunolide A acted as a direct or indirect precursor of ligustilide, while senkyunolide I was downstream in the ligustilide biosynthetic pathway [36]. This study showed that these three phthalides did not accumulate maximally under the same SA concentration in L.chuanxiong suspension cells: 100 μmol/L SA was optimal for senkyunolide I and senkyunolide A, whereas 150 μmol/L SA was required to maximize ligustilide, caffeic acid, and biomass. This differential response was likely attributable to the varying SA sensitivity of biosynthetic genes. Some genes were more responsive to low-concentration SA; thus, under 100 μmol/L SA, cells may have preferentially activated upstream synthases or hydroxylases, redirecting precursors toward senkyunolide biosynthesis. Conversely, other genes required stronger elicitation for full induction. Under 150 μmol/L SA, cells likely entered a heightened defense state, initiating downstream branches and broader defense metabolism (e.g., phenolic acids, ligustilide), while also promoting growth-related metabolism, ultimately enhancing ligustilide, caffeic acid, and biomass accumulation.

Plant regeneration was successfully achieved with enhanced efficiency and speed through suspension cell cultures of L. chuanxiong in this study. Embryogenic cells displayed dense cytoplasm and large nuclei, exhibiting distinct polar organization and stage-dependent morphological dynamics [37]. Compared with conventional callus, L. chuanxiong suspension cell aggregates showed more defined embryonic features, including more compact nuclear arrangement, higher cytoplasmic density, and closer structural similarity to embryogenic callus. The high-frequency recovery of regenerated plants underscored the superior regenerative potential of suspension cells in this system.

Zhu et al. reported that MS medium with 2.0 mg/L KT and 0.5 mg/L IAA was optimal for shoot regeneration in L. chuanxiong, achieving a 44.4% differentiation rate [21], while Wang et al. found MS medium with 0.5 mg/L 6-BA and 0.5 mg/L IAA most suitable for adventitious bud formation, reaching 25% [20]. In this study, suspension cell clusters of L. chuanxiong showed a markedly higher adventitious bud differentiation rate of 93.63% on MS medium supplemented with 1 mg/L IBA and 0.50 mg/L 6-BA, with bud formation shortened to approximately 20 days, indicating stronger regeneration potential.

For root induction, the highest rooting rate reached 74.00% on medium containing 1 mg/L IBA and 0.5 mg/L NAA. In contrast, although the condition reported by previous researchers [21], (1/2MS with 0.5 mg/L NAA and 0.5 mg/L IBA) was applied, the rooting rate was only 48.20%, suggesting that rooting may depend on material sources and requires further optimization.

Throughout the regeneration stages of L. chuanxiong, the MS medium was selected as the basal medium for shoot regeneration following a preliminary comparison with the 1/2 MS medium, as it consistently yielded superior results. This choice was justified not only by its empirical performance but also by its optimal ammonium-to-nitrate ratio and balanced ionic composition. Which rely on elevated nitrate, the comprehensive nutrient profile of MS medium provided a solid physiological foundation that robustly supported cell division, meristematic activity, and organogenesis in plants [38], and its reliability was confirmed during shoot induction and growth in this study.

Under the substrate condition of peat soil: perlite ratio (2:1 v/v), the survival rate of tissue-cultured plantlets reached 90.91%. After one month of greenhouse growth, root systems multiplied vigorously, and successful acclimatization was achieved in open-field conditions.

Somaclonal variation refers to genetic or epigenetic changes that occur during prolonged in vitro culture and can compromise the genetic stability of clonal progeny. Therefore, four ISSR primers were used to test 12 L. chuanxiong regenerated plants. Somaclonal variation was detected in none of the plants. The results demonstrated that the established embryogenic callus suspension culture system for L. chuanxiong reliably enabled the stable and efficient production of genetically uniform regenerated plants, confirming the safety and reliability of this technical system.

This study established an efficient in vitro regeneration system, however, some limitations should be noted. First, only ISSR molecular marker analysis was conducted on the regenerated plants, thus a comprehensive assessment of potential genetic stability issues arising from somaclonal variation was not achieved. Second, the work concluded with the acclimatization and transplantation of tube plantlets, without evaluating the long-term field performance, stress resistance, or fruiting capacity of these transplanted plants. To fully assess the applied potential of this technique, it will be necessary in future studies to evaluate the agronomic stability, yield potential, and long-term adaptability of these regenerants under field conditions.

Conclusions

In this study, callus was successfully induced from petiole explants of L. chuanxiong Hort. through optimization of plant growth regulator ratios (2,4-D 1.0 mg/L + 6-BA 0.5 mg/L), achieving an induction rate of 89.67%. The callus was transferred to liquid MS medium supplemented with 2,4-D 1.0 mg/L and 6-BA 1.0 mg/L for shake-flask culture (25 ± 1℃, 110 rpm, dark conditions). After three subcultures, an embryogenic suspension cell line with good dispersibility was established, exhibiting a biomass doubling time of 7 days and a growth cycle of 15 days. When suspension cell aggregates were inoculated onto differentiation medium MS + 1.0 mg/L IBA and 0.50 mg/L KT, a bud formation rate of 93.63% was achieved. The highest rooting rate of 73.3% was obtained on MS medium containing 1.0 mg/L IBA and 0.5 mg/L NAA. Under the condition of peat: perlite = 2:1, the survival rate of tissue-cultured plantlets reached 90.91%. Through optimization of culture conditions, the suspension cell aggregates of L. chuanxiong enabled rapid and large-scale production of regenerated plants without environmental constraints, providing crucial support for germplasm conservation and biotechnological research (Fig. 7A). This study also employed UPLC-MS technology to analyze metabolites in L. chuanxiong suspension cultured cells, confirming the presence of active components including ferulic acid and ligustilide. Following treatment with appropriate concentrations of salicylic acid, the contents of these compounds increased multiplicatively, providing references for utilizing this suspension culture system to elucidate high-yield mechanisms of active substances such as phenolic acids and phthalides, and for further production regulation.
