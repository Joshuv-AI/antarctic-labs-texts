Syphilis Diagnosis and Interest in Doxycycline Postexposure Prophylaxis Among Young Black Sexual and Gender Minorities
Authors: E Remund Wiger, E Almirol, M C Castro, J Knox, Y T Chen, A Hazra, D T Duncan, J A Schneider, I Matthews, J Pagkas-Bather
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDoxycycline postexposure prophylaxis (doxy-PEP) is a low-cost, effective strategy for preventing syphilis and other bacterial sexually transmitted infections among men who have sex with men and transgender women, yet doxy-PEP remains underused by young black sexual and gender minorities (YBSGM). This analysis examined sociodemographic, sexual health, and substance use factors associated with interest in doxy-PEP among a human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) status–neutral cohort of YBSGM.MethodsThe Neighborhoods and Networks Phase 2 (N2P2) cohort included YBSGM, and our primary outcome was interest in doxy-PEP. Generalized linear models were fit to calculate the unadjusted and adjusted relative risks (RRs) between interest in doxy-PEP and sociodemographic and health factors.ResultsA total of 538 participants were included in the analysis, with 59.9% reporting interest in doxy-PEP. Participants interested in doxy-PEP were older (RR, 1.02 [95% confidence interval (CI), 1.00–1.04]; P = .04), more likely to be living with HIV (1.25 [1.08–1.44]; P < .01), and more likely to have a history of syphilis (1.45 [1.23–1.70]; P < .01). Self-report stimulant use (RR, 1.28 [95% CI, 1.15–1.53]; P < .01) and group sex (1.32 [1.15–1.53]; P < .01) were greater among those interested in doxy-PEP. When adjusted for age, HIV status, and stimulant use, history of syphilis (adjusted RR, 1.17 [95% CI, 1.05–1.31]; P = .01) and group sex (1.14 [1.02–1.28]; P = .02) remained associated with interest in doxy-PEP.ConclusionsYBSGM who were interested in doxy-PEP were more likely to have had a syphilis diagnosis and participate in group sex. YBSGM with these risk factors represent an important demographic for the scale-up of doxy-PEP use.Keywords: HIV, substance use, syphilis, YBSGM

Over the past decade, there has been an increase in reported cases of bacterial sexually transmitted infections (STIs) in the United States. Since 2014, there has been a 32.5% increase in syphilis, gonorrhea, and chlamydia. Specifically, the rate of reported cases of all stage and congenital syphilis in the United States has increased yearly from 2011 to 2023 (14.8–62.5 per 100 000 population) [1]. Several factors have shown to affect the risk of acquiring a bacterial STI, including sociodemographic factors (such as age, employment, and income) and substance use behaviors, such as illicit drug use [2].

Multiply marginalized communities, including young black sexual and gender minorities (YBSGM), continue to be disproportionately affected by STIs [3]. In 2021, 15–34-year-olds accounted for 62.6% of primary and secondary syphilis among black men [4], while in 2018 34.7% of all primary and secondary syphilis in the United States occurred among black individuals [5]. Men who have sex with men (MSM) account for 53% of male primary and secondary syphilis cases [6]. These statistics indicate the importance of studying syphilis in YBSGM despite this subgroup making up <1% of the US population [7]. In addition, Hall et al. [6] found that young sexual and gender minorities and black and transgender populations have a higher lifetime prevalence of syphilis than those of other races and gender identities. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention also report that young black men have a higher rate of gonorrhea and chlamydia than their white counterparts [5].

In 2023, the use of doxycycline as postexposure prophylaxis (doxy-PEP) was shown to be a low-cost and clinically effective method to reduce STI incidence in people assigned male sex at birth: a dose of 200 mg of doxycycline administered up to 72 hours after condomless sex reduced the risk of acquiring syphilis, chlamydia, and gonorrhea [8, 9]. Based on the evidence, in 2024 the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention published clinical guidance to counsel MSM and transgender women (TGW) with a history of bacterial STIs in the past 12 months or who expect to participate in behaviors associated with an increased risk of STIs about the benefits and adverse effects of doxy-PEP [10].

Studies on doxy-PEP efficacy, such as the substudy of the ANRS IPERGAY trial by Molina et al [9] have predominantly included white and cisgender populations with limited studies looking at the impact of doxy-PEP in black sexual and gender minority populations thus far. In the aforementioned trial demonstrating the efficacy of doxy-PEP, which was conducted among MSM and TGW, only 7% of participants were black, and the majority were white (67%) [8]. Elevated rates of syphilis among black people in the in the United States illustrate the need for doxy-PEP research and interventions that prioritize black individuals, particularly YBSGM [5].

Individuals who have previously acquired an STI, are receiving preexposure prophylaxis (PrEP) for the prevention for human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) transmission, or are in talks with a healthcare provider about PrEP, are significantly more likely to be aware of novel methods to manage bacterial STIs, such as doxy-PEP [11]. Because doxy-PEP is a biomedical intervention for STI prevention, it is valid to consider barriers to PrEP uptake when thinking about potential barriers to doxy-PEP [12]. Barriers to health-promoting activities in combination with historical mistrust in the healthcare system continue to affect black sexual minority men and TGW populations with regard to access and uptake of HIV prevention [12–14]. Thus, it is important to understand how these factors may affect doxy-PEP uptake within particularly affected populations, such as YBSGM.

The aim of the current study was to describe the characteristics associated with an interest in using doxy-PEP to prevent syphilis acquisition among an HIV status–neutral [14–16] cohort of YBSGM (N = 538). We examined factors related to doxy-PEP use and interest, including sociodemographic characteristics, sexual behaviors, such as group sex [17], and laboratory-reported bacterial STIs, as current guidelines recommend doxy-PEP for MSM and TGW with a history of STIs in the past 12 months [10]. We also explore potential associations with substance use (ie, cannabis, alcohol, and stimulant use), given their association with increased risk of syphilis acquisition [2].

METHODS

Study Design

The Neighborhoods and Networks Phase 2 (N2P2) study is a prospective, longitudinal cohort study conducted at a research and community service center affiliated with an academic medical center in Chicago. It aims to address issues relevant to health outcomes across the HIV prevention and care continuum in a cohort of YBSGM individuals [18], aged 16–35 years. This age range was chosen to be inclusive of the population with the highest rate of HIV acquisition [4, 19]. Baseline survey data was collected from October 2022 to May 2024 (N = 617). After completion of the survey, blood samples were collected by trained study staff, and their analysis included testing for HIV and syphilis. All study procedures were approved by the University of Chicago Institutional Review Board (IRB), and all participants provided written consent before participating in research activities, using an IRB-approved informed consent form. All identifiers were removed from the manuscript.

Study Eligibility

Eligibility criteria were as follows: age 16–35 years, identification as black or African American, male sex assigned at birth, ≥1 self-reported sexual encounter with another man or transgender woman in the past 12 months, and currently residence in the Chicago metropolitan statistical area, with no plan to relocate during the proposed study period. Participants with a history of doxy-PEP use to prevent syphilis were excluded from analysis because they were not asked about interest in doxy-PEP use.

Study Measures

Sociodemographic Characteristics and Behavior

We collected age, gender identity (categorized as cisgender man, transgender woman, and nonbinary/other), and sexual identity (categorized as gay or homosexual, bisexual, straight or heterosexual, and other). Educational attainment (less than high school education vs high school graduate or greater education), annual income (<$25 000 vs ≥$25 000), health insurance status, housing stability, and history of incarceration were included as sociodemographic characteristics. In addition to sociodemographic characteristics, we also included group sex behavior and awareness of doxy-PEP to prevent syphilis. Group sex was defined as sexual activity with >1 other person during a single sexual encounter within the past 3 months. Awareness of doxy-PEP was determined by asking, “Have you heard of any medicines that can be taken to prevent syphilis infection?”

HIV and STIs

Each participant was tested for HIV via a point-of-care third-generation HIV antibody screen, with confirmatory testing after positive test results. Participants with a known history of syphilis underwent repeated testing with a rapid plasma reagin (RPR) test. For those without a known history of syphilis, screening was conducted using the reverse syphilis testing algorithm, beginning with syphilis enzyme immunoassay/immunoglobulin G testing followed by an RPR test and, if needed, a confirmatory Treponema pallidum particle agglutination assay. Syphilis data was supplemented by health department and clinic data, and results closest to the study within a 90-day window before or after the survey were included. This time frame was selected based on the incubation period of early primary syphilis and treatment follow-up recommendations [20]. Given limited access to clinical history to assess active syphilis, and no access to treatment records, we defined presumed active infection as an RPR titer ≥1:8. While a single titer alone cannot confirm active infection, and a cutoff of ≥1:8 may produce an overcount of active syphilis [21], this cutoff has been used in previous studies [22] and is less likely to represent a serofast state than lower titers [21]. Gonorrhea and chlamydia test results 14 days before or after the study visit were included. This time frame was based on test of cure and partner treatment guidelines for gonorrhea and chlamydia [23, 24].

Substance Use

The Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT) was administered to participants to assess alcohol use. The responses were then scored and categorized as follows: abstainers (score of 0), low-risk consumption (score of 1–7), hazardous or harmful alcohol consumption (score of 8–14), and likelihood of alcohol dependence (score of ≥15) [25]. The Cannabis Use Disorders Identification Test—Revised (CUDIT-R) [26] was administered to participants who self-reported cannabis use in the past 12 months. The responses were scored and categorized as follows: nonhazardous use (score of <8), hazardous use (score of 8–11), and possible cannabis use disorder (score of ≥12) [26]. Self-report stimulant use was indicated as a “yes” if a participant self-reported use of cocaine and/or methamphetamine in the past 12 months. Laboratory-based stimulant use was a positive methamphetamine or cocaine urine toxicity screen result obtained within 5 days of the survey study visit [27–29].

Primary Outcome

Our primary outcome was interest in doxy-PEP among participants who had never used it. Participants were initially asked whether they had ever used doxy-PEP to prevent syphilis. If they had a history of doxy-PEP use, they were not asked about their future interest, while participants who did not report previous use of doxy-PEP were asked, “Would you be interested in taking doxy to prevent syphilis?” to which they could answer, “Yes,” “No,” “Don’t know,” or “Refuse.”

Statistical Analysis

Participants were eligible for this analysis if they answered the question, “Would you be interested in taking doxy to prevent syphilis?”; thus, individuals with a history of doxy-PEP use were not asked about doxy-PEP interest and were excluded from the analysis (n = 47 [7.6%]). We compared baseline differences between participants interested in and those not interested in doxy-PEP, using a t test for continuous variables (eg, age) and χ2 or Fisher exact tests for categorical variables (eg, sexual or gender identity).

We performed generalized linear models to calculate the relative risk (RR) between associated exposures and interest in doxy-PEP. In the adjusted models, we controlled for covariates significant in the univariate analyses (ie, age, HIV status, active syphilis, and self-reported stimulant use). Due to missingness of >10%, we did not include laboratory-based stimulant use in the models, regardless of significance. Differences were considered statistically significant at P ≤ .05. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute).

RESULTS

A total of 538 participants were included in this analysis, with 59.9% reporting an interest in doxy-PEP (Table 1). Their mean age (SD) was 27.8 (3.8) years. Most participants self-identified as cisgender men (83.1%) and as gay (59.1%), most had completed high school or a higher level of education (91.6%), and most reported an annual income <$25 000 (56.1%). The majority reported having medical insurance (88.3%). More than half of participants (51.2%) were living with HIV and had a history of syphilis (59.0%). Of those with a history of syphilis, 44.3% had presumed active infection (31.7% of the total participants).

Participants with an interest in doxy-PEP were older than those not interested in doxy-PEP (mean age [SD], 28.1, [3.8] vs 27.4 [3.8] years, respectively; P = .04) and reported more participation in group sex in the past 3 months (20.8% vs 10.2%; P < .01). Of participants with HIV, 56.7% were interested in doxy-PEP to prevent syphilis, compared with 43.0% of those without HIV (P < .01). A history of syphilis was more common among those who were interested in doxy-PEP than in those who were not (67.6% vs 45.6%, respectively; P < .01), as was presumed active syphilis infection (38.9% vs 20.6%; P < .01) (Table 1). A higher proportion of those with an interest in doxy-PEP self-reported stimulant use in the past 12 months (31.1% vs 18.5% in those not interested; P < .01), but there was no difference in laboratory-based stimulant use between the 2 groups (P = .33). There was no difference in interest in doxy-PEP based on other sociodemographic factors or use of other substances.

We observed significant differences between doxy-PEP knowledge, age, HIV status, syphilis diagnosis, group sex, and self-reported stimulant use with respect to doxy-PEP interest (Figure 1). In the unadjusted models, interest in doxy-PEP was positively associated with knowledge of doxy-PEP (RR, 1.20 [95% CI, 1.03–1.40]; P = .02) and 1-year increase in age (1.02 [1.00–1.04]; P = .03), and people living with HIV were more likely to have an interest in doxy-PEP than those not living with HIV (1.25 [1.08–1.44]; P = .01). Higher interest in doxy-PEP was found among those with a history of syphilis than in those without such history (RR, 1.45 [95% CI, 1.23–1.70]; P < .01) and with presumed active infection (1.37 [1.18–1.59]; P < .01). Increased interest in doxy-PEP was also found among participants who engaged in group sex in the past 3 months (RR, 1.32 [95% CI, 1.15–1.53]; P < .01) or self-reported stimulant use in the past 12 months (1.28 [1.12–1.47]; P < .01), compared with those who did not. In the adjusted models, significant associations with interest in doxy-PEP remained for history of syphilis (adjusted RR, 1.17 [95% CI, 1.05–1.31]; P = .01) and group sex (1.14 [1.02–1.28]; P = .02).

DISCUSSION

In an initial analysis to understand awareness and interest in doxy-PEP among YBSGM, a population heavily affected by STIs, we found that a majority (59.9%) of YBSGM without a history of doxy-PEP use expressed interest in it to prevent syphilis, with higher levels of interest among group sex participants, a behavior associated with increased risk of syphilis acquisition [17].

YBSGM with a history of syphilis also expressed increased interest in doxy-PEP, which aligns with clinical guidance to discuss doxy-PEP with MSM and TGW with a history of STIs [10] and is consistent with the findings of Samuel et al [11] that diagnosis of a bacterial STI in the past year was positively associated with doxy-PEP awareness, suggesting that people with recently diagnosed STIs may be more receptive to prevention strategies. The observed patterns suggest that individuals engaging in behaviors more likely to lead to STI acquisition may have had prior STI diagnoses, greater awareness of their STI risk, and increased motivation to adopt protective interventions. These findings also emphasize the need for improved outreach and education among YBSGM who participate in group sex and have a history of bacterial STIs.

The high level of interest observed among our study population underscores a critical opportunity to expand access to STI prevention strategies, such as doxy-PEP, in communities most affected by syphilis. These findings are especially notable given the disproportionate burden of syphilis among black sexual minority men [4] and TGW and the underrepresentation of these populations in prior doxy-PEP research [9]. However, despite high interest in doxy-PEP, a striking finding was the low awareness of doxy-PEP among participants (only 18.6% of study participants had previously heard of it) despite its known efficacy. This level of awareness is lower than has been found in other studies. Traeger et al [30] found that almost half of respondents to a US-based online survey had heard of antibiotics to prevent STIs, while Leocadio et al [31] found that 28.9% of a New York City sample had heard of doxy-PEP to prevent STIs, and Samuel et al [11] found that 26% of respondents of an online snapshot cross-sectional study reported having heard of doxy-PEP to prevent bacterial STIs. The discrepancy between the findings of previous studies and those in our cohort and the association between awareness of and interest in doxy-PEP may indicate a failure of current dissemination efforts to effectively reach YBSGM in Chicago. This gap highlights an urgent need for culturally relevant educational strategies that engage both healthcare providers and YBSGM populations.

These results highlight the importance of integrating doxy-PEP education, counseling, and access into STI testing and treatment in care settings, where individuals may be especially motivated to adopt preventive strategies, in line with current guidance. One potential way to do this is through bundling, a set of multiple evidence-based interventions designed to improve care outcomes [32]. Bundling positions multiple facets of healthcare into single visits. For example, someone who came for a general health screen might be offered STI screening during that visit, despite it not being the primary reason for their visit. Kerkerian et al [33] found that implementing a bundle of interventions increased screening for sexually transmitted and blood-borne infections at an addiction clinic, an important outcome given that main point of contact with healthcare for many people who inject drugs is though addiction treatment. Similarly, bundling STI prevention methods into general healthcare may be an effective method of integrating doxy-PEP into the care settings where YBSGM already interact with the healthcare system, rather than requiring a population with barriers to care [12–14] to make separate visits for STI care.

In addition, extending these efforts to settings that effectively engage younger populations could enhance early adoption of STI prevention strategies. Person-centered [14, 34] interventions are another way healthcare providers can integrate doxy-PEP into care. They should be delivered through trusted sources, including community healthcare providers and integration into PrEP and HIV care services. Peer navigation has been found to be acceptable for PrEP [35], while shared decision making may reduce stigma related to PrEP care [36]. As such, these strategies could also improve awareness, leading to interest and ultimately uptake of doxy-PEP among individuals most at risk, particularly those who are often overlooked and least likely to be offered this intervention.

Given the literature showing the association between STI acquisition and structural factors, such as education [6], income [37, 38], and insurance status [38], an association between these factors and interest in doxy-PEP was expected. This was not found, however, which may be due to the relatively homogeneous socioeconomic status of the sample population. We evaluated income, education, insurance status, and unstable housing as proxies for socioeconomic status in our adjusted model but found no meaningful change in the adjusted risk ratios or P values. Instead, clinical and behavioral risk factors, particularly history of a syphilis diagnosis and group sex, were most strongly associated with doxy-PEP interest. This finding suggests that framing doxy-PEP as a tool tailored to individual health risks may be more effective to increase doxy-PEP uptake than approaches driven primarily by socioeconomic status.

In addition, given the association between STI acquisition and substance use [2], it was also expected that substance use would be significantly associated with doxy-PEP interest. While self-report stimulant use lost statistical significance in the adjusted model (adjusted RR, 1.10 [95% CI, 0.99–1.21]; P = .09), it may still indicate clinical significance. In addition, the CUDIT-R approached a statistically significant association with interest in doxy-PEP (P = .08), and it is possible that a significant association would have been found in a larger cohort. Given this finding, it may be worth including substance use behaviors in conversations between care providers and patients about doxy-PEP.

Finally, our findings are particularly relevant given the major gap and persistent underrepresentation of YBSGM populations in the current body of doxy-PEP research [9], which parallels gaps in other areas of research and knowledge, particularly relating to PrEP awareness, uptake, and adherence [39]. In doxy-PEP, we have an opportunity to learn from the failures of PrEP scale-up and implementation among populations most at risk for HIV [39] so that opportunities to prevent syphilis and concomitant bacterial STI are not missed among YBSGM. Many trials have focused on white and cisgender populations [8, 9, 40], limiting our understanding of how doxy-PEP might be received, adopted, or implemented in populations with the highest burden of STIs. To ensure equitable public health responses, future doxy-PEP research must focus on black SGM, investigate the barriers they face, and explore culturally grounded approaches for increasing doxy-PEP uptake.

One limitation of the current study is that we did not have extensive clinical histories or treatment records for participants. Thus, we could not identify active syphilis and instead used a titer of ≥1:8 as a proxy. While this has been previously used as a metric when clinical data is missing [22], Seña et al [21] found that among HIV-negative patients with syphilis, 13.0% still had a titer of ≥1:16 12 months after treatment. This suggests that some N2P2 participants were miscategorized as having active syphilis despite being serofast. In addition, physical examinations were not performed, and the stage of syphilis could not be determined. Future research should include clinical histories and physical examinations for active syphilis.

The quantity of missing data for laboratory-based stimulant use (17%) did not allow us to examine the relationship between laboratory-based stimulant use and our primary outcome. In particular, the missingness was not at random because participants enrolled early in the study did not receive a toxicology screen (n = 74). Thus, we would not be able to interpret the data reliably. Future research should include toxicology screening at the beginning of data collection so that it can be included in analysis as substance use is an important covariate.

In addition, our sample population is limited to the Chicago metropolitan statistical area. Therefore, findings are not generalizable to YBSGM in other regions with different healthcare infrastructures, socioeconomic status, or community and cultural norms. We also did not include participants with a history of doxy-PEP use, so we were unable to determine whether these participants would want to use it again or whether their experiences made them uninterested in future use. Finally, interest in doxy-PEP may not translate to actual doxy-PEP uptake, especially given the known barriers to accessing care for YBSGM [13]. Future research should focus on identifying methods for increasing awareness and addressing barriers to uptake of doxy-PEP. It should also include clinical histories and acceptability of doxy-PEP among those with a history of use. Despite these limitations, our findings provide important insight into interest in doxy-PEP use by those disproportionately affected by the growing rates of STI acquisition in the United States.

In conclusion, while doxy-PEP has proven to be a safe and effective medication for the prevention of syphilis and incident bacterial STIs [8], it is underused by groups that might benefit from prioritization of doxy-PEP as a sexual health intervention [10]. Our findings suggest that YBSGM with a history of syphilis or who participate in group sex are an appropriate population for doxy-PEP implementation. Given that STIs are predictive of each other and of HIV [41], efforts should be made to screen YBSGM for STIs and doxy-PEP interest to increase shared decision making among YBSGM and their providers. Given the relative cost of treating syphilis and other STIs compared with that of syphilis prevention, it would be prudent for public health departments across the United States to share in prioritizing syphilis prevention among YBSGM. We have an opportunity to prevent syphilis among a highly affected population, and it is our responsibility as healthcare practitioners and public health experts to ensure that we prevent as many syphilis diagnoses as possible, given the breadth, depth, and concentration of the syphilis epidemic within the United States.
