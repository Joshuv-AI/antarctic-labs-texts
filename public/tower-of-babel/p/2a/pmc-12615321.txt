Examining the reversal hypothesis on socioeconomic status and noncommunicable diseases, and gender disparities in China: a nationwide longitudinal analysis between 1991 and 2020
Authors: Xuemei Zhang, Tianxin Pan, Barbara McPake
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe term “reversal hypothesis” has been attached to the observation that the socio-economic status (SES) gradient of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) tends to reverse over time as country's economic and social development progresses. We examined this hypothesis in China between 1991 and 2020, to test whether a divergence in the experiences of men and women can be identified and to explore the possible contributors to any disparity.MethodsUsing nine waves of China Health and Nutrition Survey (CHNS, 1991–2015) and six waves of the China Family Panel Studies (CFPS, 2010–2020), we investigated the SES-NCD gradient among participants aged 18 years and older. We examined the relationship between self-reported doctor diagnosed NCDs and SES proxied by formal educational years by gender using multivariable logistic regression and Blinder-Oaxaca decomposition models.FindingsPrevalence of a list of NCDs in the overall population increased from 3.52 % (1991) to 13.72 % (2020). The association between SES and NCDs has shifted from positive to negative over time, shifting earlier among women compared to men. For both men and women, the difference in prevalence between high and low SES groups was mostly explained by the socio-demographic and risk behaviour variables included in the models: age, being overweight/obesity, employment status, residency, and region. However, the contribution size of these factors and consistency was different by gender and changed over time.ConclusionsChina has experienced a reversal in the SES-NCD gradient which occurred first among women. The explanations of SES differences are complex and differ by gender.Keywords: Reversal hypothesis, Non-communicable diseases, Socioeconomic status, Gender disparities, China, Panel data

Introduction

Existing literature has established the association between socioeconomic status (SES) and the development of negative health outcomes such as non-communicable diseases (NCDs) (Wu et al., 2019). While NCDs are concentrated in low-SES populations in high income countries (HICs) (Pampel et al., 2012; Porhcisaliyan et al., 2021), patterns are much more mixed in low and middle-income countries (LMICs) (Corsi & Subramanian, 2019; Subramanian et al., 2011). For example, the prevalence of diabetes among individuals with low and high SES is 19.2 % and 25.0 % (no schooling vs. higher secondary) in India (Corsi & Subramanian, 2019), 5.47 % and 6.97 % (no formal education vs. college or above) in China (Wu et al., 2019) and 35.9 % and 29.2 % (primary school and below vs secondary and above) in Singapore (Porhcisaliyan et al., 2021), separately. The “reversal hypothesis” proposes that the burden of negative health outcomes shifts from the rich to the poor as a country's economic and social development progresses and with the process of epidemiological transition (Jones-Smith et al., 2012; McPake, 1982; Pampel et al., 2012; Zhou, 2021). However, the evidence for this hypothesis is not fully explored and the understanding of its underlying processes at earlier stages of development is limited.

NCDs pose a substantial health burden all over the world. The World Health Organisation (WHO) estimates that NCDs account for about 74 % of all deaths globally each year and 77 % of all NCD deaths are in LMICs. (World Health Organization) If NCDs become increasingly a problem for the poor, the reversal hypothesis has substantial policy implications for the reduction of poverty and health impacts. Existing studies of the reversal hypothesis mostly focus on the association between body weight and SES (Pérez-Ferrer et al., 2018) and draw from cross-sectional data (Goryakin & Suhrcke, 2014; Kinge et al., 2015). The evidence from transitioning economies is limited and findings of associations between SES and different types of NCDs are inconsistent with evidence related to metabolic syndrome related conditions more clearcut than for example for cancers, mental health conditions or chronic respiratory conditions (Corsi and Subramanian, 2012, 2019; Emamian et al., 2017; Jung et al., 2019; Monteiro et al., 2001; Nikparvar et al., 2021; Siddiquee et al., 2015; Subramanian et al., 2013; Veisani et al., 2019). A recent cross-sectional data analysis on the reversal hypothesis in China found that NCDs and risk factors were concentrated among the richer population but it did not explore trends in the relationship between SES and NCDs over time (Zhang et al., 2023).

Better understanding the processes underpinning an increasing concentration of NCDs in poorer populations as economic development and epidemiological transition progress might offer new insights into how to attenuate the process and protect poorest populations from future health burdens. Conversely, neglect of this process suggests acceptance of its inevitability. Gender differences are important because health promotion and behavioural change programs that might build on new understandings of the ‘reversal’ process have to be tailored to their target populations. Previous studies have found gender differences in the SES gradient in negative health outcomes, (Han et al., 2014) (Sánchez-Vaznaugh et al., 2009) (Yang et al., 2020), (Jiwani et al., 2019) (Zhao et al., 2018) but they have been dominated by analyses of body weight and provided limited evidence on the contributing factors or underlying explanations.

This study examines the “reversal hypothesis” in China between 1991 and 2020 and aims to clarify gender differences and to investigate the factors shaping those differences. China provides a promising setting to explore these questions. China has been undergoing rapid epidemiological and socio-demographic transition over the past 30 years and a transformation in gender roles reflected in employment and education participation. (National Bureau of Statistics of China) The experience of dramatic changes in a relatively short period allows insight into large changes within periods covered by longer-running panel data sets. Such data sets covering multiple decades as exist in China may be sufficiently long to provide new insight into the dynamic inter-relationships taking shape.

Methods

Data and population

This study used two longitudinal datasets: nine waves of the China Health and Nutrition Survey (CHNS, 1991–2015) and six waves of China Family Panel Studies (CFPS, 2010–2020). The two datasets allow us to examine trends over the 30-year period 1991–2020. CHNS is a panel study that collects families' and individuals’ information including nutrition intake and health status. The baseline survey was launched in nine Chinese provinces in 1989, and nine follow-up waves to date (1991, 1993, 1997, 2000, 2004, 2006, 2009, 2011 and 2015). (The University of North Carolinaa) We did not used the 1989 survey as the NCD information was not collected in this wave.

CFPS is an ongoing nationally representative longitudinal survey that collects data at individual, household and community levels in 25 provinces of China on a range of topics including socio-demographic characteristics and health status. (Institute of Social Science Survey and Peking Universitya) The baseline survey was launched in 2010, with five follow-up surveys to date (2012, 2014, 2016, 2018 and 2020). Detailed information describing the CHNS (The University of North Carolinaa; The University of North Carolinab) and CFPS (Institute of Social Science Survey and Peking Universitya; Institute of Social Science Survey and Peking Universityb) has been reported elsewhere.

To investigate how SES-NCD gradients have evolved over time, a single, long-running longitudinal dataset covering multiple decades would be ideal. In the absence of such data, we used CFPS and CHNS.

We excluded participants under 18, and those with missing information for NCDs and SES (years of formal education) and key socio-demographic variables (i.e., age and gender, after filling missing values based on information from other waves).

Measurements

Our health outcome was self-reported NCDs. In the CHNS, participants were asked about their doctor-diagnosed history of high blood pressure, diabetes, myocardial infarction, stroke or ischemic attack, cancer, and asthma. A code of 1 is given if the individuals reported any of these conditions. In the CFPS, participants were asked: “During the past six months, have you had any doctor-diagnosed chronic disease”. Each respondent who answered “Yes” to this question was asked to select the type of disease from the Disease Codebook, and the two most serious diseases were recorded if there was more than one. We categorized individuals as having NCD if they answered “Yes” to any of the NCDs on the list. The CFPS therefore identifies a more comprehensive set of NCDs and the measures from the two surveys are therefore not directly comparable. Although the NCD definitions differ slightly between those two surveys, both datasets include the most common NCDs and allow us to explore longer term trends in SES-NCD gradients over time in China.

We used participants completed years of formal education to represent SES at the individual level. Participants were categorized into two low or high SES groups based on the median years of education. There has been substantial growth and a significantly gendered pattern of educated year over the past 30 years, particularly due to the implementation of nine-year compulsory education since 1986. Consequently, we generated the quantiles of educated year by age groups and gender separately for each wave.

The following variables were included as covariates in our regression models: age (18–30, 31–40, 41–50, 51–60, 61+), gender (men, women), marital status (never married, currently married, divorced/widowed/separated), residency (urban, rural), per capita household income (Q1 to Q5, Q1 being the poorest), geographical region (western, eastern, central), employment status (no, yes), smoking (never smoked, ever smoke) and drinking (drink less than three times a week, more), overweight/obesity (if BMI ≥24 kg/m2 or not) (Fang & Liang, 2017), health insurance (no, yes).

Statistical analyses

We reported descriptive sample characteristics for baseline waves of the two datasets (i.e., 1991 in CHNS and 2010 in CFPS). We reported the prevalence of NCDs between 1991 and 2020 for the whole sample and by gender respectively.

Multivariate logistic regression models were fitted to estimate adjusted odds ratios (AOR) and 95 % confidence intervals (CI) for the associations between SES and NCDs among whole sample and by gender for each wave respectively. The regressions were adjusted by covariates. We included cluster robust standard errors at the community level to account for the observed and unobserved homogeneity across communities.

Blinder-Oaxaca (BO) decomposition models were fitted to explain the difference in the prevalence of NCDs between low and high SES groups in women and men, respectively. Based on the BO model, the NCDs prevalence gap between two groups can be decomposed into the ‘explained’ component (endowment effect), which refers to the gaps explained by differences in independent variables in the model, and the ‘unexplained’ component (coefficients effect), which captures all the differential effects of independent and unobserved variables (Okamoto et al., 2021; Rahimi & Hashemi Nazari, 2021). We divided the sample into low and high SES groups for men and women separately, then calculated the relative percentage of contribution of each variable to the difference between two SES groups. Low SES groups were treated as the reference group and covariates were controlled as independent variables. Analysis was run separately in CFPS and CHNS.

We conducted sensitivity analysis by repeating the multivariate logistic regression models in individuals under and above 50 years of age respectively. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA/MP 14.1. P-values less than 0.05 were considered to be statistically significant.

Sensitivity analyses

Given that age is closely linked with education years in China and the strong association between age and NCD prevalence, we performed a sensitivity analysis by repeating our main analysis in individuals under and above 50 years of age respectively, to investigate if the results at the population level may mask differences by age.

Results

We reported the results from the two datasets separately to account for any inconsistencies, given the differences in the measurement of NCD and age distribution in population between the two surveys.

Descriptive characteristics

We finally included participants from the year 1991 (n = 9936), 1993 (n = 9262), 1997 (n = 9639), 2000 (n = 10,185), 2004 (n = 10,565), 2006 (n = 10,527), 2009 (n = 10,716), 2010 (n = 32,522), 2011 (n = 13,840), 2012 (n = 30,626), 2014 (n = 30,771), 2015 (n = 14,157), 2016 (n = 28,760), 2018 (n = 27,615), and 2020 (n = 20,126). In CHNS 1991, the mean age of the study population was 40.5 years old (standard deviation [SD], 16.03 years), 49.9 % were women, the median year of formal education is 5.9 years (SD, 4.2) and 64.6 % had received 5.9 or fewer years, 75.4 % were married, 68.5 % resided in rural areas. In CFPS 2010, the mean age of the study population was 46.4 years old (SD, 15.8 years), 51.6 % were women, the median year of formal education is 6.9 years (SD, 4.8) and 64.3 % had received 6.9 or fewer years, 82.3 % were married, 53.4 % resided in rural areas. The descriptive sample characteristics of CHNS 1991 and CFPS 2010 are presented in Appendix 1 (p1-3).

The overall crude prevalence trends of NCDs among Chinese adults between 1991 and 2020 are displayed in Fig. 1a and b (detailed prevalence rates are reported in Appendix 2 p4). Overall, there were large increases in the prevalence of NCDs in the overall study population between 1991 (3.52 %) and 2015 (19.51 %) and between 2010 (11.28 %) and 2020 (13.72 %). Women consistently have had higher NCD prevalence than men in both datasets but the gap between women and men has varied over the period studied.

NCD prevalence by SES group

Fig. 1c–f shows the prevalence of NCDs by SES group for the full sample and by gender over time respectively. High SES men and women consistently have had higher NCD prevalence than their low SES counterparts in both datasets. However, the gap changed overtime. Among women, the gap widened from approximately 2.06 % in 1993 to 7.74 % in 2011, then narrowed down to almost 0 % since 2011. The gap in NCD prevalence by SES group had similar pattern in men, widening from 2.97 % in 1993 to 7.87 % in 2011, then narrowing and stabilising after 2018.

The association between SES and NCD by gender

Fig. 2 shows the adjusted associations between NCD prevalence and SES obtained from multivariate logistic regression. Generally, the plots show a positive association between NCDs and SES in the whole population, but the association shifts towards to negative over time, although the association was not statistically significant in most survey years with an exception in 1997 (AOR = 1.40 [95 % CI 1.05–1.87], (Fig. 2, Overall). By gender, the pattern was more pronounced in women. There was a significant positive association in 1997 (AOR = 1.70 [95 % CI 1.13–2.56]) and becoming negative in 2016 (AOR = 0.82 [95 % CI 0.72–0.94]). For men, we did not find statistically significant associations during 1991 and 2020, except a positive relationship in 2010 (AOR = 1.16 [95 % CI 1.00–1.35]).

Sensitivity analysis

Our sensitivity analysis showed that a more significant reversal in the SES-NCD gradient happened in 2016 among those who were under 50 years compared to those who were aged 50 years and above. This implies that transition occurs following the differential experience of the next population cohort (rather than because of behaviour change in the older cohort). The association was in general positive. Similar to our main results, transition among younger women occurred earlier (results are provided in Appendix 4 p20-24).

Discussion

This study investigated the reversal hypothesis across the SES-NCD gradient among Chinese men and women by using two nationally representative datasets covering 1991–2020. We identified a significant increase in NCD prevalence in both genders during the period and women consistently have had higher NCD prevalence than men in both datasets. Over the past 30 years, the prevalence of NCDs was always higher in high SES women and men than in their low SES counterparts in both datasets. However, we found that the association between SES and NCDs was shifting from positive towards the negative over time, occurring first in women. As might be expected, the results from younger age groups support the reversal hypothesis more clearly.

We found a significant increase in the prevalence of NCDs in the past 30 years in China. Rapid economic growth and urbanization have accompanied growing risk factors such as unhealthy diet and lack of physical activity (Li et al., 2012) and have contributed to the increase of NCDs and their predisposing factors including obesity (Pang et al., 2022) and hypertension (Miao & Wu, 2016). We found that women have a higher prevalence of NCDs than men, and this difference persists over time, in line with previous studies on obesity (Han et al., 2014). This may be partially explained by the fact that women have larger hormonal changes throughout their lifetimes and generally longer lifespan than men (Okamoto et al., 2021), and experience higher rates of mental health pressures like anxiety (Fang & Liang, 2017), increasing their risk of developing NCDs.

We found that high SES participants had higher NCD prevalence than low SES participants in both datasets and in both genders. However, the gap between low and high SES groups in men and women narrowed. The association between SES and NCDs shifted from positive to negative over time, with an earlier transition in women, in line with the evidence on education and obesity from other countries (Pérez-Ferrer et al., 2018). In our study, we also found a reverse of the SES-overweight gradient among women but not in men (Appendix 5a p25), which is likely to contribute to the earlier transition of burden of NCDs in women in China. Nevertheless, this association between NCD and SES was only significant in a few survey years, which may indicate that China is in relatively early stages of reversal.

The observed NCD differences between low and high SES groups in men and women can be mostly explained by certain independent variables in our Blinder-Oaxaca decomposition models. However, we identified different contributing factors and varying magnitudes of their contributions to the SES-NCD gradients in men and women. Specifically, age contributed most, followed by overweight/obesity, employment, residency, and region to the SES-NCD difference in men, whereas age contributed most, followed by residency, region, overweight/obesity and employment status to the SES-NCD difference in women. We found that there are certain factors only contributing to the NCD difference in one gender. For example, living in the urban area consistently contributed to the SES-NCD gradient in women between 2004 and 2015 whereas only this was only the case in 1993 and 2011 for men. Per capita household income has consistent impact for men while not for women.

Our further analysis partially explains these differences. For example, compared to men, the difference between low and high SES the proportion of the population with overweight/obesity is smaller in men and almost disappeared after 2005 (Appendix 5a p25). It is noteworthy that higher employment rates among low SES participants accounts for a lower prevalence of NCDs among low SES participants compared with high SES participants in both women and men. We found that low SES participants had a higher employment rate than high SES participants (Appendix 5b p26), especially among participants aged 51 and above in men and aged 41 and above in women (Appendix 6a p34). This might imply higher rates of physical activity in older age groups among participants of low SES compared to their higher SES counterparts. We found a strong negative association between employment and NCDs (Appendix 6b p35).

Counter-intuitively, we found a contribution of current or previous smoking and frequent drinking contributing to the NCD difference in men in 2012 and 2016 (CFPS survey). This may be due to the economic and epidemiological shifts over time during which the factors affecting NCD prevalence have become more complex and our results may be related to omitted variable bias (Pan & Palmer, 2018). Another possible explanation is that participants may change their behaviour after NCDs have been diagnosed (Pan & Palmer, 2018), but our repeated cross-sectional design cannot infer causality. The above results may indicate that economic development and urbanization have more consistent and/or more significant impact on women's health than men's, whereas individual/household income has more impact on men's health.

Our sensitivity analysis showed that the reversal pattern was more pronounced among younger participants, indicating that general population effects may obscure important age and gender differences and that transition is a cohort more than whole population effect. Younger participants have a number of differences from older ones besides age. They have received more years of education (Appendix Table 6c p36), they may have a higher health literacy than older participants (Pérez-Ferrer et al., 2018), and they've also benefited from technical improvements and better understandings of the risk factors for NCDs at younger ages. Another possible explanation of earlier reversal of NCD prevalence in younger groups is that different generations of the Chinese population have experienced stages of the life cycle under markedly different conditions. Older participants may have experienced more disadvantaged situations such as low parental SES, food shortages, or bad health during their infancy, childhood, or adulthood than younger generations, all factors that affect NCD prevalence (Ding et al., 2021; Ogunsina et al., 2018) and may then influence the NCD-SES relationship.

Limitations

We present the first study that assesses the reversal hypothesis in SES-NCDs gradient using population-based longitudinal datasets in a country that has progressed from low to upper middle-income status over 30 years. However, there are several caveats when interpreting the results. However, there are several caveats when interpreting the results. Firstly, the use of self-reported information including the presence of NCDs, smoking and drinking might be subject to recall or social desirability bias. Secondly, causation between independent variables and NCDs cannot be conclusively attributed from repeated cross-sectional data. Limitations also arise from the use of two datasets that are different in some respects (e.g., different questions on self-reported NCDs and list of included NCDs, age distribution of population, questions to measure drinking behaviour). Those differences may limit the consistency of our results between the two datasets.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we found that the burden of NCDs is shifting towards low SES groups, and this process is occurring earlier in women. Therefore, special attention needs to be paid to disadvantaged groups in the development of prevention policies for NCDs. Our study also highlighted that the most important common factors affecting the difference of NCD prevalence between high and low SES groups were different in women and men. This implies the importance of developing gender-specific dynamic interventions to address the emerging socioeconomic disparities in NCDs.
