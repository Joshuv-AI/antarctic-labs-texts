Conceptual Methodological Framework for Incorporating Antimicrobial Resistance Considerations in Economic Models for Pneumococcal Conjugate Vaccines
Authors: Mark H Rozenbaum, Maria J Tort, Ruth Chapman, Katherine K Perez, Desmond Dillon-Murphy, Benjamin M Althouse, Raymond Farkouh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a substantial global health threat and economic burden. Vaccines reduce antibiotic use and prevent resistant infections, combating AMR. However, their economic and health benefits are often underestimated because economic analyses do not consider vaccines’ broader impacts, such as effects on AMR. We conceptualize a framework for estimating the impacts of vaccination on AMR using pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCVs) as an example.MethodsThe proposed framework includes three pathways: population and pathogen, care, and health outcomes. Operationalizing this framework requires extensive detailed data, such as serotype distribution, disease incidence, resistance profile, antibiotic use, and treatment failure, derived from multiple sources, such as national surveillance systems, epidemiological studies, and hospital records, which are often unavailable. However, considering vaccines’ impact on AMR is crucial because of potential future health and cost issues. Therefore, we adopted a simplified framework leveraging all available data related to antibiotic prescriptions and resistance to estimate the impact on critical outcomes.ResultsRoutine PCV20 vaccination was estimated to prevent up to 23,509,406 antibiotic prescriptions and 14,050,115 antibiotic-resistant infections over 25 years compared to PCV13 and 12,087,128 antibiotic prescriptions and 7,245,908 antibiotic-resistant infections compared to PCV15, demonstrating the potential impact of PCV infant immunization on AMR cases and antibiotic prescriptions.ConclusionsA simplified model can effectively incorporate critical AMR parameters for a more comprehensive evaluation of PCVs. Our framework also identifies key data gaps that should be addressed for future modeling efforts.Keywords: Antibiotic prescriptions, Antimicrobial resistance, Pneumococcal conjugate vaccines, Pneumococcal disease

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a global health threat, causing millions of deaths annually and a substantial economic burden. Vaccines, such as pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCVs), can reduce antibiotic use and prevent resistant infections, but their broader impacts on AMR are often underestimated in economic analyses.This study presents a framework for evaluating the impact of PCVs on AMR, addressing the gap in most modeling studies that do not include AMR dynamics. As a result of data availability limitations, a simplified version of the conceptual framework was applied to explore the impact of PCV20 on antibiotic prescriptions and antibiotic-resistant infections compared to PCV13 and PCV15.What was learned in study?Routine PCV20 vaccination could prevent up to 23.5 million antibiotic prescriptions and 14 million antibiotic-resistant infections over 25 years compared to PCV13 and 12 million antibiotic prescriptions and 7.2 million antibiotic-resistant infections compared to PCV15.The study highlights the broader benefits of vaccination in combating AMR, which are often overlooked in economic models, and emphasizes the importance of incorporating AMR considerations into vaccine evaluations, leading to more informed decision-making and improved public health outcomes.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) occurs when pathogens evolve to evade antimicrobials, including antibiotics, antivirals, and antifungals, thereby hindering the effectiveness of available treatments [1]. AMR is among the most significant threats to public health worldwide, contributing to an estimated 4.95 million deaths each year [2]. Additionally, AMR poses an excessive economic burden and is projected to lead to additional healthcare costs of US $1 trillion by 2050 and an annual loss of US $1–3.4 trillion in gross domestic product by 2030 [3]. The rapid evolution of AMR necessitates a thorough, sustainable, integrated strategy beyond the development of new antibiotics.

Vaccination to minimize AMR is a powerful, but often underappreciated, strategy that warrants additional development. A recent report found that vaccination could save up to 2.5 billion antibiotic doses or nearly a quarter of antibiotic use globally [4]. Vaccines directly decrease the prevalence and transmission of infections due to targeted pathogens, both drug susceptible and resistant [4]. In this manner, vaccines decrease antibiotic and antiviral use, associated adverse events and complications, and prevent secondary bacterial superinfections [5]. Additionally, vaccination effectively reduce a pathogen’s replication rate at subclinical levels, decreasing the likelihood for selecting AMR mutations [6]. In syndromes associated with multiple potentially causative pathogens, such as pneumonia, removing a causative pathogen through vaccination can reduce the need for the use of broad-spectrum antibiotics [7]. Therefore, vaccines reduce antibiotic consumption and the circulation of resistant strains and facilitate the development of indirect effects, ultimately contributing to reducing AMR [8, 9].

Among vaccine-preventable infections, vaccination against Streptococcus pneumoniae stands out as a proof of concept for vaccine impact on AMR. S. pneumoniae diseases include pneumonia, meningitis, and otitis media (OM) [10], which are commonly treated with antibiotics [11]. The emergence of antibiotic resistance to S. pneumoniae complicates treatment strategies, further heightening the challenge of combating pneumonia’s impact. In 2024, the World Health Organization (WHO) prioritized macrolide-resistant S. pneumoniae to their list of priority bacterial phenotypes [12]. Since the introduction of pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCVs), multiple studies have shown an association between PCV introduction and reductions in antibiotic use, second-line antibiotic use, and incidence of resistant infections [9, 13–17].

The seven-valent PCV (PCV7), first introduced in 2000, offered protection against seven serotypes (4, 6B, 9V, 14, 18C, 19F, and 23F) [18, 19]. In the USA, the infant PCV7 program was associated with a 64% and 45% decrease in penicillin-resistant invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) among children aged < 5 years and older adults aged > 65 years (due to indirect effects), respectively [20]. Subsequently, the 13-valent PCV (PCV13), introduced in 2010, covered six additional serotypes (1, 3, 5, 6A, 7F, and 19A) [18, 19]. Following the introduction of PCV13, a decrease in antimicrobial-resistant pneumococcal isolates was observed in children with IPD and OM and adults with IPD in the USA and Europe [21]. PCV13 was associated with a significant reduction in visits for OM, which is the most common infection for which children are prescribed antibiotics [22], particularly among children aged < 2 years [23]. Altogether, since their introduction in 2000, PCV7 and PCV13 have been associated with a 97% and 60% decrease in antibiotic-resistant invasive pneumococcal infections caused by the covered strains in the USA in children aged < 5 years and adults, respectively [23, 24].

Two additional PCVs were subsequently introduced after PCV13 for pediatric vaccination: the 15-valent PCV (PCV15), introduced in 2022 covering two additional serotypes (22F and 33F), and the 20-valent PCV (PCV20), introduced in 2023 covering the serotypes included in PCV15 and five additional serotypes (8, 10A, 11A, 12F, and 15B) [25, 26]. Although data concerning the impact of PCV15 and PCV20 on AMR are not yet available, vaccination with these PCVs is expected to further prevent antibiotic-resistant infections [27]. From 2016 to 2019, the additional serotypes of S. pneumoniae bacteria covered by PCV15 and PCV20 were responsible for 0.4% and 2.1%, respectively, of all antibiotic prescriptions for children in the USA, with PCV20’s additional serotypes responsible for more than five times the number of prescriptions compared to those covered by PCV15 [28, 29].

The prevalence of antibiotic resistance is concerning as nearly half of pneumococcal isolates collected from patients hospitalized in the USA between 2018 and 2021 exhibited resistance to multiple antibiotics, with non-invasive isolates exhibiting higher resistance [30]. Altogether, these data underscore the potential impact of PCVs in reducing the incidence of antibiotic use and excess healthcare resource use as well as combating AMR.

Cost-effectiveness analyses (CEAs) have shown that PCVs are cost-effective, yielding substantial reductions in disease incidence, mortality, and healthcare costs globally [31, 32], in Asia [33] and in various countries, such as China [34], Germany [35], the USA [36], Norway [37], and Canada [38]. However, notably, the current application of CEAs of vaccination generally fails to capture all socioeconomic benefits associated with introducing a vaccination program, notably the prevention of primary and downstream antibiotic-resistant disease [39, 40].

The objective of this study was to establish a conceptual framework for a model that quantifies the impact of pediatric vaccination on AMR using newer, higher valent PCVs (PCV15 and 20) as a case study. As the focus of the study was pediatric vaccination, PPSV23 and PCV21 have not been considered in the analysis.

Conceptual Framework

Building upon the framework proposed by Atkins et al. [41], our conceptual framework (Fig. 1) involves three pathways: (i) a population and pathogen pathway, (ii) a care pathway, and (iii) a health outcomes pathway, to fully account for the impact of vaccines on AMR. The mechanisms within each pathway capture the diverse interaction between vaccination, antibiotic resistance, and reduction in the overall disease burden.

Conceptual framework. OM otitis media, CAP community-acquired pneumonia, IPD invasive pneumococcal disease, SoC standard of care

The population and pathogen pathway describes disease epidemiology at the population level. For pneumococcal disease, this pathway would include the population-level distribution of S. pneumoniae serotypes, incidence of each pneumococcal disease manifestation, including IPD, community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), and OM, the proportion of OM and CAP episodes attributable to S. pneumoniae, the proportion of specific serotypes resistant to antibiotic treatment, and the likelihood of those susceptible developing and/or acquiring resistance mechanisms.

The care pathway describes the clinical course of case management. Treatment entails antibiotic access and other protocol-driven country-, age-, risk-factor-, and disease manifestation-specific healthcare interventions as determined by national guidelines, hospital protocols, society guidelines, available healthcare resources, and antibiotic stewardship practices. As an example, a variety of diagnostic and treatment approaches exist for pneumococcal disease. For IPD, a blood culture may be conducted to identify the causative pathogen. For CAP and OM, clinical management is based on standard protocols, best practices, and/or clinical judgement [42]. Subsequent treatment is required in the case of treatment failure due to resistance to first-line antibiotics and is further complicated by the amount of time patients are left untreated [43]. Patients on inadequate empiric therapy are at a significantly greater risk of adverse events and complications [44, 45].

The health outcomes pathway characterizes AMR-attributable pneumococcal disease clinical and economic outcomes. The direct outcomes relevant to health economic assessments impacted by AMR include a longer hospital length of stay [46, 47], additional office visits or rehospitalization [48], increased costs [47, 49], higher mortality rates and serotype-specific fatality rates [48, 50], additional antibiotic prescriptions [51], adverse events and decreased health-related quality of life [50]. Furthermore, the indirect outcomes of AMR have significant implications. For example, AMR infections can lead to the spread of resistant strains, undermining the effectiveness of public health initiatives, such as surgical prophylaxis effectiveness or rises in nosocomial infections [52]. AMR can also limit the ability to safely perform certain medical procedures, such as organ transplants, due to the increased risk of untreatable infections [2]. Additionally, AMR infections can lead to outbreaks, and the misuse and overuse of antibiotics further exacerbate AMR [53]. Antibiotic use can select for drug-resistant strains, creating a conflict between individual treatment and community welfare [6, 54].

However, despite the important roles of these pathways in AMR, published data that could inform these are lacking as demonstrated in a recent meta-analysis of Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Escherichia coli [55]. Overall, modeling the impact of vaccines on infections and AMR is both structurally and parametrically complex, particularly as a result of the considerable data needed to develop such models, which are often unavailable. To operationalize the model, it is necessary to include data specific to each disease syndrome, such as incidence, serotype distribution, and serotype-specific resistance profiles. Although such data are not required for the modeling of all vaccines, they are particularly crucial for modelling the impact of PCVs on S. pneumoniae. To appropriately model the population and pathogen level of the framework, data from national surveillance systems, prospective epidemiological studies, and upper respiratory specimen testing with high sensitivity and specificity are needed. IPD serotype-specific data are generally available in countries with robust surveillance systems. However, such data are typically lacking for CAP because of the lack of reliable detection methods and for OM as a result of the invasive nature of the sampling procedure (tympanocentesis). Parameterizing the care pathway requires model inputs related to first-line, second-line, and even third-line antibiotic treatment pathways, which could be derived from country-, age-, risk-factor-, and disease manifestation-specific treatment protocols. Parameterizing the health outcomes pathway requires age- and serotype-specific hospital discharge and cost data or claims data to inform the length of hospital stay, cost of first-line and subsequent treatments in the event of treatment failure, and healthcare resource utilization. Utility decrements should be informed by patient or caregiver surveys, and case fatality rates should be informed by data related to serotype-specific incremental mortality due to resistant cases. While these data sources are optimal for the implementation of the framework, it is recognized that they are idealistic, and many are unavailable. Further work is needed to generate these data sources and address gaps.

Methods

Model Structure

As certain data required to fully parameterize the pathways in the proposed framework are currently unavailable, we adopted a simplified framework levering all available PCV data, including age- and disease-type-specific data related to antibiotic prescriptions and resistance, to estimate the impact on critical outcomes. This approach allowed us to provide initial insights into the impact of vaccination on AMR while highlighting the critical need for more extensive data collection and research.

To operationalize this novel framework, we leveraged a previously published multi-cohort, population-based decision-analytic Markov model to assess the impact of routine vaccination with PCV20 compared to routine vaccination with PCV13 and PCV15 [36]. Static models have been widely used for the assessment of PCVs in the USA and globally [56–59]. Although pneumococcal carriage and transmission are not directly modeled, indirect effects can be captured. Additionally, our model incorporated age- and disease-type-specific data, including incidence data for all age groups to reflect the age specific pattern of incidence (i.e., high incidence in younger children, followed by a decline in older children and adults, with another increase in incidence later in life). Briefly, the model assumed that the PCV20 vaccine was delivered in a 3 + 1 dosing schedule, with three primary doses administered at 2, 4, and 6 months, followed by a booster dose administered at 12–15 months. At the beginning of each 1-year cycle, a new cohort was vaccinated. Vaccine strategy- and age-specific transition probabilities governed individuals’ transitions across the health states within each cycle. The only difference in the model presented in this analysis is that a 25-year time horizon was considered instead of the 10-year time horizon used in the original publication; a 25-year time horizon was selected for this updated analysis to ensure stability of results over time. This analysis, therefore, considers 25 birth cohorts entering the model. The full details of the model structure can be found in Rozenbaum et al. [36].

We added the conceptual framework described above to the existing PCV health economic model to derive health outcomes related to susceptible and resistant pneumococcal disease cases. Next, we describe the available data and assumptions required to operationalize the framework.

Model Inputs

The full details of the model inputs and assumptions (except for those related to AMR) are published elsewhere [36].

Antibiotic Prescriptions

Antibiotic prescriptions were assumed to be given to 100% of individuals with IPD and hospitalized pneumonia. The antibiotic prescription rate for non-hospitalized pneumonia (78%) and OM (85%) in the pediatric population (aged < 18 years) was calculated by dividing the annual number of antibiotic prescriptions for non-hospitalized pneumonia and OM due to the PCV20-non13 serotypes by the number of pediatric outpatient visits for outpatient pneumonia and OM due to the PCV20-13 serotypes, respectively, based on data reported by King [11]. The antibiotic prescription rate for non-hospitalized pneumonia in the adult population (aged ≥ 18 years) was assumed to be the same as that in the pediatric population. The antibiotic prescription rate is assumed to remain unchanged over the time horizon.

Antibiotic Resistance

The calculation of the proportion of cases caused by antibiotic-resistant S. pneumoniae was based on US Centers for Disease Control data on antimicrobial susceptibility testing from 2018 to 2021 [30]. This study identified cases in which S. pneumoniae was non-susceptible to at least one of 27 antibiotics clinically used to treat pneumococcal infections as specified in the National Healthcare Safety Network (NHSN) Antimicrobial Use and Resistance (AUR) reporting module. The reported data were collected by either invasive sampling methods (i.e., blood or cerebrospinal fluid) or non-invasive sampling methods (i.e., urine or lower respiratory tract) among hospitalized patients. The data were also reported by age (< 18 and ≥ 18 years). The values are shown in Table 1. The antibiotic resistance rate is assumed to remain unchanged over the time horizon. The simplified framework currently does not assign a cost or utility decrement associated with antibiotic-resistant cases and prescriptions and, therefore, does not impact the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER).

Scenario Analysis

To investigate the impact of potential parameter uncertainty, additional scenario analyses were performed. Rates of antibiotic resistance and prescriptions were decreased and increased by 25% (scenarios 1 and 2), and vaccine coverage rates were decreased and increased by 25% (scenarios 3 and 4). For the scenarios where the rates are increased by 25%, there are multiple cases where the rates would exceed 100% and therefore the values were fixed to a maximum of 100% for plausibility.

Ethical Approval

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Results

Compared with PCV13, PCV20 could prevent an additional 23,509,406 antibiotic prescriptions, including prescriptions for IPD (0.7% of the total antibiotic prescriptions), hospitalized pneumonia (5.6%), non-hospitalized pneumonia (23.9%), and OM (69.7%), and avert 14,036,616 antibiotic-resistant infections over 25 years (Table 2; Fig. 2). Compared with PCV15, PCV20 could prevent an additional 12,087,128 antibiotic prescriptions, including prescriptions for IPD (0.7%), hospitalized pneumonia (5.9%), non-hospitalized pneumonia (25.8%), and OM (67.6%), and avert 7,245,908 antibiotic-resistant infections (Table 2; Fig. 2). The majority of averted antibiotic-resistant infections and antibiotic prescriptions (Table 3) were due to prevented cases among those aged 0–17 years. It should be noted that a proportion of adults were assumed to be excluded from indirect effects (5.7% for 18- to 49-year-olds, 12.3% for 50- to 64-year-olds, and 49.3% for those aged ≥ 65 years) as a result of having direct protection from vaccination; therefore, the number of antibiotic-resistant cases averted in adults may be an underestimate. Scenario analysis demonstrated that lowering and increasing antibiotic resistant and prescription rates by 25% could result in a substantial change from base case in antibiotic-resistant cases and antibiotic prescriptions averted compared to PCV13 and PCV15 respectively (Table 4). Scenarios investigating increase and decrease in vaccine coverage resulted in differences in total cases averted; however, they had less impact on the incremental cases of antibiotic resistance and prescriptions than from changing the resistance and prescriptions rates.

Discussion

This study presents a conceptual framework for assessing the impact of PCVs on AMR. Our framework describes a complex process model capturing the dynamic nature of resistance in bacterial pathogens and the changing probabilities of contracting resistant strains over time. The framework incorporates three key pathways (i.e., population and pathogen, care, and health outcomes) to comprehensively account for the impact of vaccines on AMR.

The population and pathogen pathway captures the interactions between the pathogen and individuals ideally using a dynamic transmission model. These models are particularly useful for simulating the spread of S. pneumoniae-caused diseases and estimating the impact of interventions such as PCVs. However, the complexity of these interactions requires detailed data on various factors, including age-specific contact patterns, serotype distribution of S. pneumoniae in pre- and post-PCV eras, serotype-specific clearance rates, and resistance data. Additionally, models must consider the efficacy of different PCVs (PCV7, PCV13, and PCV20) against both vaccine type and non-vaccine serotypes, changes in strain prevalence over time, AMR ratios, antibiotic utilization, and the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of antibiotics to predict AMR burden [60]. It is also crucial to account for serotype competition and the progression from carriage to disease [60, 61].

Several studies have used transmission models to evaluate the effect of PCVs on morbidity and mortality due to S. pneumoniae diseases; however, these studies have struggled with missing data and were forced to make simplifying assumptions. For instance, Oidtman et al. [62] struggled with obtaining data for certain serotypes and relied on limited historical data. Choi et al. [61] faced challenges such as accounting for secular changes, dynamic transmission complexities, and serotype interactions. Their models considered potential replacement disease and relied on static models to estimate forces of infection, which could distort estimates due to insufficient data. Other studies by Malik et al. [63] and Rinta-Kokko et al. [64] also attempted to simulate these interactions but often lacked comprehensive data on certain parameters and had to be calibrated using existing historical data. Løchen et al. [65] reviewed existing literature, identifying several challenges in modeling and data availability for evaluating PCVs, including the scarcity of data on the natural history of pneumococcal infection, serotype-specific transmission dynamics, and vaccine-induced immunity duration. Assumptions about herd immunity and serotype replacement further introduce significant uncertainty.

Although vaccines are increasingly recognized for their role in reducing AMR, most modelling studies did not include AMR dynamics, as integrating these elements adds further complexity to already challenging modelling [60, 66–68]. Studies that explored vaccination’s impact on antibiotic resistance typically focused on specific questions, populations, or regions [69, 70]. A comprehensive model capturing AMR dynamics required detailed data on serotype-specific resistance rates, serotype prevalence shifts, resistant S. pneumoniae strains, antibiotic treatment impacts, and treatment failures [7, 60, 63, 64, 66, 69]. However, obtaining the data needed to accurately parameterize these models is challenging because of the lack of surveillance and longitudinal data tracking changes in strain prevalence, serotype distribution, and resistance patterns over time [60, 61, 66, 71]. Variability in data collection across studies and regions can lead to inconsistencies and gaps [62, 70, 72]. The framework and associated data gaps presented were focussed on US data; however, these data gaps are likely to be more pronounced in lower-income countries where estimates show the burden of AMR is substantially higher than in the USA [73]. For lower-income countries a simplified approach such as the one used in this study is likely the best approach for demonstrating vaccine impact on AMR. The complex interactions between serotypes, vaccine impacts, and AMR dynamics require detailed data that are not always available [61, 74]. Reyburn et al. [70] highlighted significant variability in AMR rates across different settings, emphasizing the need for caution in considering AMR drivers.

These data limitations pose significant challenges to developing comprehensive transmission models and incorporating AMR concepts, increasing uncertainty. Studies often address data gaps by making assumptions and calibrating models with existing data, which sometimes is imperfect or originates from different settings that do not accurately describe the exact setting of interest. This can add significant uncertainty to the results. Therefore, in the pathogen and population pathway, we chose a simplified but robust model to analyze available data effectively within these constraints while still providing meaningful insights.

In the care pathway, for treatment outcomes, the probability of experiencing treatment failure is influenced by the level of AMR. Patients with treatment failure often face prolonged treatment durations, additional diagnostic tests, potentially costlier second-line drugs, and productivity losses [69]. However, data on these aspects, as well as on treatment follow-up and the distribution of inpatients across different health facilities, are limited [66, 69, 75].

In the health outcomes pathway, healthcare utilization, including inpatient and outpatient services, the proportion of inpatient care, consultations with experts, and the need for more diagnostics, also depend on the availability of data [7, 71]. However, the costs and resource use associated with and attributable to AMR are not available in many cases [55].

Modeling studies estimating the economic value of pneumococcal vaccines in reducing AMR are scarce and, so far, only assess PCV13. Breton et al. [76] examined the impact of PCV13 vaccination on antibiotic use for OM and AMR in Canada using a forecasting model and reported that PCV13 use reduced the number of cases and prevented approximately 3.7 million antibiotic prescriptions over 11 years. Likewise, Lu et al. [60] utilized an agent-based microsimulation model that simulated in detail the relationship among PCV vaccination, infection, treatment pathways, and bacterial resistance. There are similarities between the approach used by Lu et al. [60] and that proposed in the conceptualized framework, the latter separating the analysis into human and bacterial components. Consistent with our results, Lu et al. reported that the introduction of PCV13 vaccination would substantially reduce antibiotic use and treatment failure caused by AMR. In a recent analysis by Rozenbaum et al. [36], the theoretical introduction of a PCV20 catch-up program in the USA was estimated to prevent even additional cases, antimicrobial-resistant infections, and antibiotic prescriptions for pneumococcal infections.

The dearth of analyses incorporating AMR could be attributed to difficulties in modeling the impact of vaccination on AMR, particularly a lack of data informing model inputs, leading to reliance on assumptions and extrapolations across the population and pathogen, care, and health outcomes pathways. In this study, as a result of these complexities, requirements, and data limitations, a simplified approach was adopted. Despite extensive research on S. pneumoniae, the specific data required for these analyses remain scarce. Consequently, we utilized the available data to efficiently predict the vaccine impact and cost-effectiveness.

While offering a more practical approach that enables valuable conclusions on pneumococcal vaccines’ impact on AMR despite data limitations, this analysis demonstrates that PCV20 could prevent substantially higher numbers of antibiotic prescriptions and resistance cases compared to PCV13 and PCV15. Specifically, PCV20 could prevent an additional 14 million and 7 million AMR cases, and 23 million and 12 million antibiotic prescriptions compared to PCV13 and PCV15, respectively. Scenario analysis demonstrated the predicted antibiotic-resistant cases and prescriptions are relatively sensitive to higher and lower resistance and prescription rates and less sensitive to changed in vaccine coverage. However, even with a 25% reduction in resistance and prescription rates, PCV20 could still prevent an additional 10 million and 5.5 million AMR cases, and 17 million and 9 million prescriptions compared to PCV13 and PCV15, respectively. However, the impact of PCV20 on AMR cannot be fully captured, potentially resulting in an underestimation of the overall impact of vaccination. The simplified approach does not account for nuanced interactions between the vaccine and resistance by serotype, the impact of serotype competition, nor does it capture potential changes in resistance and prescriptions over time. Consequently, these limitations may not fully reflect PCV20’s potential benefits in reducing AMR, underscoring the need for more comprehensive data and advanced modeling techniques to accurately assess the vaccine’s broader implications.

Additionally, in our study, the impact of AMR on the ICER was not assessed because of very limited data on the impact of AMR on costs or health outcomes. More data are needed; in particular, age-specific data on the impact of AMR on the length of stay, mortality rates, office visits or rehospitalization, which could result in an impact on the ICER, are lacking.

Surveillance data undoubtedly show the impact of PCVs in reducing the burden of AMR in the pneumococcal population. Translating this into public health and economic impact is challenging and further complicated by the lack of standardized evaluation approaches and data limitations [13]. The impact of preventing AMR on incremental cost and health outcomes (i.e., improvement in the ICER of PCVs) is typically not accounted for. CEAs typically focus on immediate outcomes, while the impact of PCVs on AMR extends beyond these immediate considerations and encompasses broader societal and economic implications that are challenging to capture and accurately quantify within the framework of a CEA. This further contributes to the underestimation of the true value of PCVs at the population level and undervaluation by national immunization technical advisory groups (NITAGs)/health technology assessments (HTAs). In the future, attempts should be made to consider AMR, and the impact on costs and QALYS should be included in CEAs.

Conclusion

The global challenge of AMR requires innovative solutions that extend beyond traditional antimicrobial development. Our analysis demonstrates that vaccines, particularly PCVs, represent a powerful yet underutilized tool in combating AMR. This broader benefit to society is not widely recognized or accounted for when vaccines are evaluated by NITAGs/HTAs, partially because AMR is often overlooked in economic models. Developing a systematic approach to include AMR considerations in economic models could improve the vaccine evaluation process and capture the complex, long-term societal benefits of preventing AMR. The framework we have proposed opens new pathways for evaluating vaccines’ role in AMR prevention, and should be implemented, but its full implementation faces substantial challenges. Looking forward, several critical steps are needed: development of standardized surveillance systems to track disease incidence as well as AMR patterns and vaccine impacts, integration of AMR considerations into economic models and HTAs, investment in research to better understand the relationship between vaccination and resistance dynamics, and creation of new metrics that capture broader societal benefits of AMR prevention. As we face an era where the risks of AMR loom, reconceptualizing how we value and implement vaccination programs becomes increasingly urgent. Our framework presented here represents a step towards better understanding the complete role of vaccination in preserving the future effectiveness of antimicrobials.
