Comparative study on the uropygial glands of three goose breeds: a histomorphological and transcriptomic perspective
Authors: Shenqiang Hu, Zhaoyan Chen, Fuli Huang, Qingliang Chen, Xiaopeng Li, Jiasen Chen, Yang Song, Jiwei Hu, Liang Li, Wanxia Wang, Jiwen Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe uropygial gland is a gland peculiar to birds, and it is widely recognized that its secretion confers water-repellent properties on the feather coat and maintains the suppleness of the feathers. The aim of this study was to reveal differences in the developmental patterns and the regulatory mechanisms of the uropygial glands among different goose breeds (Sichuan White goose, SW; Landes goose, LD; and Gang goose, GE) through comparative analysis of their uropygial gland histomorphological parameters and transcriptomic data during the period from week 0 to week 30 post-hatching.ResultsMorphologically, our results showed that the uropygial gland weight of LD was significantly lower than that of SW (P < 0.01) and GE (P < 0.05) at week 10 but was significantly higher than that of SW and GE at week 30 (P < 0.01). Histologically, the tubular area of zone I, the interlobular septum, luminal area, and epithelial thickness of zone II, and the luminal area of zone III increased with age in the uropygial glands among almost all three goose breeds. Moreover, these histological parameters differed most significantly at week 30, when the luminal area and epithelial thickness of zone II were significantly greater in SW than in LD (P < 0.05) but the luminal area of zone III was significantly greater in LD than in SW (P < 0.05). Comparative analysis of the uropygial gland transcriptomes of LD and SW at week 10 and 30 further identified several key genes, signaling pathways, and interactions networks that are involved in regulating the breed- and age-related goose uropygial gland development.ConclusionsThe uropygial glands of LD might have a stronger capacity of lipid synthesis and secretion when approaching the age of sexual maturity. GPAT3, LPIN2, EP300, and MGMT involves breed-related uropygial gland development. LCAT, PLPP3, TIMP2, and MFAP5A involves age-related uropygial gland development. Besides, the glycerophospholipid metabolism pathway may be the main regulatory pathway for lipid metabolism in the uropygial gland of LD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12143-2.Keywords: Goose, Uropygial gland, Histomorphological characteristic, Transcriptomic analysis, Regulatory mechanism, Background

The uropygial gland, a holocrine gland enclosed in a connective tissue capsule, is located dorsally between the fourth caudal vertebrae and the pygostile in most birds [1]. This gland usually consists of two lobes and one ductal system [2]. Each lobe consists of tubules and cavities of various sizes, which are arranged radially from the periphery to the center and lead to a common channel known as the central cavity. The central cavity collects lipid secretions from those tubules arranged radially around the cavity, and the lipids are eventually transported to the surface through ducts opening at the top of the papilla [3]. Structurally, three functional zones (namely zone I, IIand III) have been previously defined based on the length of the tubules, which corresponds to the tubular peripheral, intermediate, and internal regions, respectively [4]. Among them, zone I has a thicker epithelial layer, indistinct lumen, elongated interlobular septum, and more cells in the middle layer, with the main functions of lipid synthesis and secretion [2, 5, 6]. Compared to zone I, zone II has a thinner epithelial layer, small lumen of the secretory tubule, and slightly fewer cells in the middle layer, with the main functions of lipid synthesis, secretion, and storage [2, 5, 6]. Zone III is the innermost portion of the tubule and is mainly characterized by the confluence of collecting tubules or secondary tubules, which in turn form the secondary lumen and finally lead to the central lumen, with the main functions of lipid transport and storage [2, 5, 6].

The main functions of the avian uropygial gland are to synthesize and secrete the lipids, most of which are used for waterproofing, lubricating, and protecting feathers [3]. Specifically, the birds apply these lipid secretions to their feathers through the beaks to prevent the feather drying and breaking and enhance water repellency and suppleness, thus improving the integrity and quality of feathers and down [7]. It has been shown that the lipid contents in the avian feathers, which mainly come from the secretions of the uropygial glands, increase during their morphological changes from filamentous to tufted down to normal ones [8], supporting the crucial roles of the uropygial glands in determining the quality of feathers and down. The lipid synthetic, secretory, and circulatory capacities of tubular cells in the uropygial glands have been reported to be significantly influenced by a variety of factors, including the age [9], sex [10], season [11], habitat [5], steroids and hormones [12], diet [13], vitamin C deficiency [14], and depopulation [15]; and meanwhile, the genetic factors such as species and breeds were supposed to play a determinant role [13, 16].

Although the feather traits are of great economic importance to the goose industry, little is known about the developmental dynamics of goose uropygial glands. Landes goose (LD) is one of European domestic goose breeds that originate from graylag geese (Anser anser), while Sichuan White goose (SW) and Gang goose (GE) are two of Chinese domestic goose breeds that originate from swan geese (Anser cygnoides). These three goose breeds possess distinguished production traits, such as egg-laying performance in SW, foie gras production performance in LD, and meat production in GE, hence playing crucial roles in China's agricultural economy, particularly in the Southwestern regions. Considering that all three goose breeds are widely used in the current commercial goose production and show breed-related differences in the feather and down traits, the present study aimed to compare the age-dependent changes in the uropygial gland histomorphological and transcriptomic characteristics among these three goose breeds and to reveal the underlying regulatory mechanisms. These data are expected to provide novel insights into the developmental dynamics of goose uropygial glands and lay a theoretical basis for the genetic improvement of goose feather and down traits.

Methods

Experimental birds and sample collection

The experimental geese from three breeds were provided by the Waterfowl Breeding Experimental Farm of Sichuan Agricultural University (Ya’an, Sichuan, China). A total of 100 healthy 0-day-old male geese per breed, which were hatched at the same batch, were fed under similar environmental conditions. At 0, 6, 10, and 30 weeks of age, ten geese per breed were randomly selected for sampling, respectively. After recording the live body weight following a 12-h fasting, they were euthanized by CO2 inhalation followed by cervical dislocation. The uropygial glands were weighed, measured, and photographed post-slaughter to document morphological characteristics. At each sampling time point, the left uropygial glands from 5 geese per breed were sampled for histological examination, while the right uropygial glands were stored at −80°C after a rapid frozen in liquid nitrogen until RNA isolation.

Measurement of the uropygial gland morphological parameters

The weight of goose uropygial glands was weighed using an electronic balance, and the uropygial gland organ index (%) was subsequently calculated as the uropygial gland weight (g) / live body weight (g) × 100%. The uropygial gland length (defined as the distance from the top of the secretory papilla to the base of both lobes) and width (defined as the distance across the left and right lobe) were measured using a straightedge and a digital vernier caliper with an accuracy of 0.01 mm, respectively.

Determination of the uropygial gland histological parameters

The freshly-collected goose left uropygial glands were fixed in 4% formaldehyde at room temperature for 72 h, dehydrated through graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and paraffin-embedded. Sections (5 μm thick) were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), and imaged using a BA410-Digital digital microscope (Motic China Group Co., Ltd., Xiamen, China). The uropygial gland histological parameters were analyzed using the Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA), including the tubular area of zone I, the luminal area of zone II, the epithelial thickness of zone II, the luminal area of zone III, and the epithelial thickness of zone III. Each histological parameter was calculated as the average of the observations of all examined geese per sampling time point per breed.

RNA preparation and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from the uropygial glands of SW and LD at 10 and 30 weeks age (n = 3 per breed per week) using the RNAiso Plus reagent (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instruction, respectively. The RNA concentration and integrity were determined using both a NanoDrop 2000 Micro-ultraviolet Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The RNA-seq libraries were prepared using the Illumina TruSeq mRNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions, and were subsequently sequenced on the Illumina Hiseq X-Ten platform.

Transcriptomic bioinformatics analysis

Quality control analysis of transcriptome sequencing data was performed using FastQC (version 0.11.9) and fastp (version 0.22.0) [17]. Clean reads were obtained after removing adapter-containing, poly-N, and low-quality reads, and aligned to our recently assembled high-quality genomes of SW and LD using Hisat2 (version 2.0.5). The sequencing alignment/mapping (SAM) files were sorted and converted to the binary alignment/mapping (BAM) files using SAMtools software (version 1.6.0) [18]. The relative expression of each transcript was analysed using featureCounts v1.6.0 [19]. Subsequently, the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between different experimental groups were identified using DESeq2 (version 1.40.2) package [20], with the screening criteria of |log2Foldchange|> 1 and adjusted P value < 0.05. In the weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), the top 5000 genes ranked by median absolute deviation (MAD) were selected to construct the co-expression network [21]. Hub genes were filtered with module membership (MM) > 0.8 and gene significance (GS) > 0.4. The hub genes were overlapped with DEGs, and the overlapping genes were identified as overlapped DEG clusters. Functional enrichment analysis of Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways was performed using KOBAS [22]. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks among overlapped DEG clusters were retrieved from STRING 10 database and visualized with Cytoscape (version 3.7.1) [23]. The sequencing data of this study have been archived in the GSA database with the accession number PRJCA036490 (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa).

Quantitative real-time reverse transcription PCR validation

Total RNA was extracted from tissues using TRIzol reagent, followed by DNase I digestion to remove genomic DNA. cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg RNA using PrimeScript RT reagent kit. The reactions of quantitative real-time reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) were carried out on a Bio-Rad CFX96 real-time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The RT-qPCR amplification conditions were listed as follows: 10 μL of 2 × ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), 0.4 μL each of forward/reverse primers, 2 μL cDNA template, and 7.2 μL ddH2O. Thermal cycling conditions: 95°C for 30 s; 40 cycles of 95°C for 5 s and 60°C for 30 s. GAPDH/β-ACTIN served as reference genes, and relative gene expression was calculated by the 2−ΔΔCT method [24]. Statistical analysis

The results from morphological and histological analysis were expressed as the mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). A two-way ANOVA was conducted with age and goose breed as fixed effects. When a significant effect was seen, the post-hoc Duncan’s multiple range test was employed to evaluate the significance of differences between different weeks of age or breeds. Differences were considered statistically significant at a probability (P) value < 0.05 and highly significant at P < 0.01. Finally, both GraphPad Prism (version 8.0) software (San Diego, CA, USA) and R (version 4.2.1) software were applied to draw the figures.

Results

Comparison of age-dependent changes in the uropygial gland morphology among three goose breeds

As shown in Fig. 1A, the uropygial gland weight of SW and GE increased significantly (P < 0.05) with age, whereas that of LD increased significantly (P < 0.05) from week 10 to 30. Meanwhile, the uropygial gland organ index of LD showed a significant increase (P < 0.05) from week 10 to 30, but that of SW and GE showed no significant change (P > 0.05; Fig. 1B). Besides, the uropygial gland length of SW and LD decreased significantly (P < 0.05) with age, but that of GE increased significantly from week 10 to 30 (P < 0.05; Fig. 1C). The uropygial gland width of all three goose breeds showed a significant increase during post-hatch development (P < 0.05; Fig. 1D).

As for interbreed comparisons, the uropygial gland weight of LD was significantly lower than that of SW (P < 0.01) and GE (P < 0.05) at week 10 but was significantly higher than that of SW and GE at week 30 (P < 0.01; Fig. 1A). Meanwhile, the uropygial gland width of LD was significantly greater than that of SW at week 6 (P < 0.05; Fig. 1D).

Comparison of age-dependent changes in the uropygial gland histology among three goose breeds

As shown in Fig. 2A-C, in general, the uropygial glands of all three goose breeds were surrounded by a dense capsule, and the extent of zone I and zone III progressively became larger with age. It was noteworthy that both zone I and zone II were clearly seen in the uropygial glands of all three goose breeds at week 0, when they did not have a distinct zone III structure. Moreover, the interlobular septum changed significantly with age. Specifically, the interlobular septum of zone III was more developed than that of zone I, and the interlobular septum of zone III became thicker with age (Fig. 2D-G). Also, the interlobular septum of zone I were elongated during post-hatch development, accompanied by a progressively thicker epithelial layer (Fig. 2H). In addition, compared to zone I, the epithelial thickness of zone II was much thinner, the lumen of zone II was much smaller and was sometimes filled with secretions, and the interlobular septum of zone II were more obvious (Fig. 2I). Compared to that of both zone I and zone II, the epithelial thickness of zone III was extremely thin and much keratinized, with a larger lumen containing scattered cortical secretions (Fig. 2J).

Quality control and read mapping of RNA-seq data

In order to reveal the regulatory mechanisms underlying the observed interbreed differences in the goose uropygial gland development, the genome-wide transcriptomes of the uropygial glands collected from SW and LD at week 10 and 30 (n = 3 per breed per week) were sequenced and analyzed. As shown in Additional file 1: Table S1, a total of 292,646,849 raw reads were obtained from these 12 samples, and after a strict quality control, an average of 24,387,237 clean reads were obtained from each sample, with an average Q30 score of 94.62%. Meanwhile, the read mapping rates of all samples were more than 91.87%. Besides, the results of principal component analysis and hierarchical clustering analysis showed that these samples clustered together based on the goose breed and the developmental stage (Additional file 2: Figure S1). Altogether, these data demonstrated the high quality and good biological reproducibility of our transcriptomic sequencing data, which could be used for subsequent bioinformatics analysis.

Identification of differentially expressed genes

By comparing the uropygial gland transcriptomes of the two goose breeds (LD and SW) at different weeks of age, a number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in multiple pairwise comparisons. As shown in Fig. 4A, a total of 1,369 DEGs, including 900 up-regulated DEGs and 469 down-regulated DEGs, were identified in the uropygial glands of LD between week 10 and 30. A total of 618 DEGs, including 154 up-regulated DEGs and 464 down-regulated DEGs, were identified in the uropygial glands of SW between week 10 and 30 (Fig. 4B). The number of age-related DEGs identified in the uropygial glands of LD was nearly twice more than that of SW. As for interbreed comparisons, a total of 1,312 DEGs, including 768 up-regulated and 544 down-regulated DEGs, were identified in the uropygial glands between LD and SW at week 10, (Fig. 4C), while a total of 1,598 DEGs, including 586 up-regulated and 1,012 down-regulated DEGs, were identified in the uropygial glands between LD and SW at week 30 (Fig. 4D).

Identification of co-expressed gene modules through WGCNA analysis

To further identify the key genes regulating the goose uropygial gland development, the genes with the top 5,000 median absolute deviation (MAD) from these 12 sequenced goose uropygial gland transcriptomes were screened to construct a weighed gene co-expression network through WGCNA analysis. As shown in Fig. 5A-B, a total of 14 co-expressed gene modules were identified to show correlations with different experimental group, e.g., the goose breed or the developmental stage. Further correlation analysis between each identified gene module and each experimental group was performed, and the obtained results are shown in Fig. 5C. It was seen that the MEblue gene module was significantly positively correlated with the uropygial gland development of LD at week 10 (P < 0.05) but was significantly negatively correlated with that of SW at week 10 (P < 0.05), which indicated that the genes clustered in the MEblue module could be responsible for the observed differences in the uropygial gland development between SW and LD during the first 10 weeks post-hatching. The MEbrown module showed a significant positive correlation with LD at week 30 (P < 0.01) but no significant correlations with other experimental groups (P > 0.05), suggesting that the genes clustered in this module could have a role in regulating in both the age- and breed-related uropygial gland development of LD. The MEyellow module was significantly positively correlated with SW at week 30 (P < 0.05) but was not significantly correlated with SW at week 10 (P > 0.05), suggesting that the genes in this module could have a role in regulating the uropygial gland development of SW from week 10 to 30. To identify the key genes responsible for either the age-related or breed-related differences in the goose uropygial gland development, the genes clustered in the MEbrown module were overlapped with the DEGs in the uropygial glands of LD between week 10 and 30 and the DEGs in the uropygial glands between LD and SW at week 30, respectively, and the resultant 142 up-regulated DEGs and 17 down-regulated DEGs (namely the brown1 DEG cluster) as well as 5 up-regulated DEGs and 44 down-regulated DEGs (namely the brown2 DEG cluster) were respectively identified. Meanwhile, the genes clustered in the MEyellow module were overlapped with the DEGs in the uropygial glands of SW between week 10 and 30, and the resultant 4 up-regulated DEGs and 9 down-regulated DEGs (namely the yellow DEG cluster) were identified. The genes clustered in the MEblue module were overlapped with the DEGs in the uropygial glands between LD and SW at week 10, and the resultant 94 up-regulated DEGs and 194 down-regulated DEGs (namely the blue DEG cluster) were identified.

We annotated the GO biological process (BP) terms significantly enriched by each of the 4 above-overlapped DEG clusters. As shown in Fig. 6A, the genes in the blue DEG cluster were significantly enriched in the fatty acid metabolism-related terms, including triglyceride biosynthetic process, fatty acid catabolic process, fatty acid catabolic process, and fatty acid catabolic process. The genes in the brown1 DEG cluster were significantly enriched in the cellular activity-related terms, including cell differentiation, cell differentiation, positive regulation of mesenchymal cell proliferation, and negative regulation of endothelial cell proliferation, and negative regulation of endothelial cell proliferation. The genes in the brown2 DEG cluster were significantly enriched in the cell differentiation and organ development-related terms, including bleb assembly, cell differentiation, skeletal muscle tissue development, and roof of mouth development. The genes in the yellow DEG cluster were significantly enriched in the terms such as kinetochore assembly, resolution of meiotic recombination intermediates, epithelial cell differentiation, and cell division. Taken together, the GO-BP terms significantly enriched by the genes in the blue DEG cluster were highly relevant to lipid metabolism, while those significantly enriched by the other three DEG cluster groups were more relevant to growth and development.

KEGG enrichment analysis

We also annotated the KEGG pathways significantly enriched by each of the 4 above-overlapped DEG clusters. As shown in Fig. 6B, the genes in the blue DEG cluster were significantly enriched in the lipid metabolism-related pathways such as glycerolipid metabolism, glycerophospholipid metabolism, and PPAR signaling pathway. Among them, Glycerol-3-Phosphate Acyltransferase (GPAT3) and Lipin 2 (LPIN2) were simultaneously significantly enriched in both the glycerolipid metabolism and glycerophospholipid metabolism pathways, while Phospholipid Transfer Protein (PLTP) and Retinoid X Receptor Gamma (RXRG) were significantly enriched in the PPAR signaling pathway. The genes in the brown1 DEG cluster were significantly enriched in the ECM-receptor interaction, glycerophospholipid metabolism, and MAPK signaling pathways, and Lecithin-Cholesterol Acyltransferase (LCAT) and Phospholipid Phosphatase 3 (PLPP3) were significantly enriched in the glycerophospholipid metabolism pathway. The genes in the brown2 DEG cluster were significantly enriched in the focal adhesion, apelin signaling, and calcium signaling pathways. Among them, Myosin Light Chain Kinase (MYLK) and Myocyte Enhancer Factor 2C (MEF2C) were significantly enriched in the sapelin signaling pathway. Besides, the genes in the yellow DEG cluster were significantly enriched in the cytochrome P450 and Glutathione metabolism, peroxisome, and Fanconi anemia pathways. Among them, Glutathione S-Transferase Kappa 1 (GSTK1) was significantly enriched in the pathways of drug metabolism-cytochrome P450, metabolism of xenobiotics by cytochrome P450, and glutathione metabolism.

Identification of hub genes through PPI analysis

To further identify the hub genes regulating the age- and breed-related differences in the goose uropygial gland development, the PPI networks were constructed using the genes in each of the four above-overlapped DEG clusters by the STRING database. As shown in Fig. 6C, in the blue DEG cluster, Dedicator of Cytokinesis 8 (DOCK8), Ceramide Kinase Like (CERKL), Retinoid Isomerohydrolase RPE65 (RPE65), Retinaldehyde Binding Protein 1 (RLBP1), Ras Homologous Family Member G (RHOG), Phosphatidylinositol Glycan Anchor Chain Biosynthesis Class G (PIGG), E1A Binding Protein P300 (EP300), Glycerol-3-Phosphate Acyltranslate 3 (GPAT3), O-6-Methylguanine-DNA Methyltransferase (MGMT), and Kinesin Family Member 25 (KIF25) were identified as the hub genes responsible for the interbreed differences in the early uropygial gland development between SW and LD. As shown in Fig. 6D, in the brown1 DEG cluster, Von Willebrand Factor (VWF), Fibroblast Growth Factor Receptor 1 (FGFR1), Cartilage Oligomeric Matrix Protein (COMP), Vestigial Like Family Member 3 (VGLL3), Myocilin (MYOC), TIMP Metallopeptidase Inhibitor 2 (TIMP2), Microfibril Associated Protein 5 (MFAP5), Neuropilin 1 (NRP1), Chordin (CHRD), and LCAT were identified as the hub genes regulating the age-dependent uropygial gland development of LD. However, no PPI networks were constructed using the genes in either the brown2 or the yellow DEG cluster.

RT-qPCR verification

To ensure the reliability and accuracy of our RNA-seq data, five DEGs were randomly selected for RT-qPCR validation. As shown in Fig. 7, the expression profiles of each selected DEG determined by RT-qPCR were consistent with the RNA-seq results, which unequivocally supported the reliability of our RNA-seq data.

Discussion

The main functions of the avian uropygial gland are to synthesize and secrete the lipids that are essential for the feathers to obtain waterproofing and flexibility [5]. Given that goose feather and down is of great economic values to the light industry because of its peculiar quality properties, it is important to reveal the developmental dynamics of the goose uropygial gland as well as the underlying regulatory mechanisms [25]. In this study, we systematically analyzed and compared the developmental changes in the uropygial gland histomorphological and transcriptomic characteristics during the period from hatch (week 0) to sexual maturity (week 30) among three economically important goose breeds. Morphologically, similar to the observations in other birds [5] the uropygial glands of all three goose breeds are located dorsally subcutaneous on the tail, in the midline of the trunk, and above the caudal vertebrae and caudal raphe, and they show a cardioid appearance as well as extensive and complex branches for glandular aggregates. Our results also showed that the uropygial gland weight of almost all three goose breeds increased significantly (P < 0.05) with age, which was consistent with the findings in a previous study, where the hen uropygial gland weight increased with age and body weight [9]. Meanwhile, these three goose breeds differed significantly in the uropygial gland length, weight, and organ index during the developmental period from week 10 to 30, especially between Chinese (SW and GE) and European (LD) goose breeds. Compared to SW and GE, the uropygial gland weight of LD showed a more rapid development when approaching to the age of sexual maturity, possibly implying a stronger capacity of lipid synthesis and secretion.

At the histological level, the uropygial glands of all three goose breeds were observed to be surrounded by dense connective tissues and a capsule membrane formed by many blood vessels, and the glandular parenchyma in each lobe consisted of secretory tubules that were straight at the periphery, curled near the center of the lobe, and slightly branched and rejoined, which was similarly reported in gull [26]. Our results also showed that the tubular area of zone I, the interlobular septum, luminal area, and epithelial thickness of zone II, and the luminal area of zone III increased with age in the uropygial glands of almost all three goose breeds. Moreover, these histological parameters differed most significantly at week 30, when the luminal area and epithelial thickness of zone II were significantly greater in SW than in LD (P < 0.05) but the luminal area of zone III was significantly greater in LD than in SW (P < 0.05). Several previous studies in the avian uropygial glands have reported that the zone I is mainly involved in lipid synthesis and secretion, while the zone III is mainly responsible for lipid storage and transportation [5, 6], it was thus inferred that as the age increased, the goose uropygial gland (especially LD) could synthesize and secrete more lipids to ensure the feathers to obtain waterproofing and flexibility and to resist microorganisms and parasites. Nevertheless, other methodologies such as Oil Red O staining or fatty acid composition analysis may be further implemented in future studies to quantitatively assess the lipid secretion capacity of the goose uropygial gland.

To explore the molecular mechanisms responsible for the age- and breed-related variations in the goose uropygial gland development, the uropygial gland transcriptomes of LD and SW at week 10 and 30 were analyzed. Through pairwise comparisons, 1,369 DEGs were identified in the uropygial gland of LD between week 10 and 30, while 618 DEGs were identified in that of SW between week 10 and 30, implying that the uropygial gland development of LD during this period might be more greatly affected by age compared to SW. These breed-specific DEGs may be partially responsible for the more pronounced increases in the uropygial gland organ index, width, luminal area of Zone III, and epithelial thickness of Zone III observed in LD compared to SW during the post hatch period from week 10 to 30. GO analysis showed that central regulators were enriched in 105 biological processes in the blue DEG cluster. Among them, GPAT3 and LPIN2 play important roles in the triglyceride biosynthetic process, which is mainly related to lipid metabolism. Glycerol-3-phosphate acyltransferase catalyzes the first step in the synthesis of glycerolipids and glycerophospholipids, and it has been demonstrated to be the major GPAT in regulation of energy, glucose, and lipid homeostasis in white adipose tissue [27]. LPIN encodes Lipin protein, which has phosphate phosphatase activity and is involved in the regulation of triglyceride and phospholipid biosynthesis, and plays a role in lipid metabolism and disease [28, 29]. Besides, GO analysis showed that central regulators were enriched in 246 biological processes in the brown1 DEG cluster. Among them, Caveolin 1 (CAV1) and Atonal BHLH Transcription Factor 8 (ATOH8) were found to be involved in cell differentiation and negative regulation of endothelial cell proliferation, which are mainly related to growth metabolism. KEGG analysis showed that the central regulators of the blue DEG cluster were significantly enriched in the Glycerolipid metabolism, Glycerophospholipid metabolism and PPAR signaling pathway, which are closely related to lipid metabolism. Both Glycerolipid metabolism and Glycerophospholipid metabolism were enriched to GPAT3 and LPIN2. Through the PPI network, we obtained the top ten hub genes, among them, GPAT3, EP300 and MGMT play important roles in the regulation of lipid metabolism. EP300 is a major acyltransferase, and its mediated histone butyrylation is essential for lipogenesis [30]. O-6-Methylguanine- DNA Methyltransferase was found to significantly reduce lipid droplets in MGMT unmethylated glioblastomas, suggesting that the expression of unmethylated MGMT was negatively correlated with lipid droplet content [31]. MGMT was down-regulated in the Blue group, and the expression of MGMT in 10-week-old SW was significantly lower than that in 10-week-old LD, suggesting that 10-week-old SW may have more lipid droplet content than 10-week-old LD, which was consistent with the results of uropygial gland morphology. KEGG analysis showed that the central regulators in the brown1 DEG cluster were significantly enriched in the ECM-receptor interaction, MAPK signaling pathway, and Glycerophospholipid metabolism pathways, which are closely related to lipid metabolism. The Glycerophospholipid metabolism pathway was enriched in the uropygial glands of both the blue and brown1 DEG clusters. In the blue DEG cluster, the genes enriched in this pathway were up-regulated and down-regulated except for the LPIN2 gene, whereas in the brown1 DEG cluster, all the genes enriched in this pathway were up-regulated, suggesting that the Glycerophospholipid metabolism pathway may be the main regulatory pathway for lipid metabolism in the uropygial gland of LD, in which LCAT and PLPP3 are significantly enriched. Lecithin-Cholesterol Acyltransferase has phospholipase A2 and acyltransferase activities [32] and plays an important role in adipogenesis [33]. Through the PPI network, we obtained the top ten hub genes that, among them, TIMP2, MFAP5 and LCAT play important roles with the regulation of lipid metabolism. TIMP2 plays an important role in lipid deposition in chickens, especially in pectoral muscle intramuscular fat deposition, promoting intramuscular fat deposition [34]. MFAP5 protein is highly expressed in human subcutaneous adipose tissue and its important role in lipid metabolism [35]. Thus, it is postulated that differential expression of these hub DEGs (GPAT3, LPIN2, EP300, and MGMT) involved in lipid metabolism may lead to the observed histomorphological differences in the uropygial gland between SW and LD at the same age, and it is also hypothesized that differential expression of these hub DEGs involved in cellular proliferation and differentiation (CAV1 and ATOH8) and lipid metabolism (LCAT, PLPP3, TIMP2, and MFAP5) may lead to the observed histomorphological differences in the uropygial gland of LD at different ages, which can be considered as potential target genes for the molecular breeding of goose feather traits. Meantime, the Glycerophospholipid metabolism pathway may be the main regulatory pathway for lipid metabolism in the uropygial gland of LD.

Conclusions

In summary, both age and breed were key factors affecting the goose uropygial gland development. Compared with the two Chinese goose breeds (SW and GE), the uropygial glands of the European goose breed (LD) increased significantly from week 10 to 30 through morphological and histological analysis, implying that the uropygial glands of LD might have a stronger capacity of lipid synthesis and secretion when approaching the age of sexual maturity. Comparative transcriptomic analysis further identified several key genes, signaling pathways, and interactions networks that are involved in regulating the age- and breed-related goose uropygial gland development. These data not only provide new insights into the physiological and molecular basis of the varied output and quality of feathers and down among different goose breeds but would also be helpful for the genetic improvement of goose feather traits.
