Transition from pediatric to adult care in thoracic transplantation—Challenges and opportunities
Authors: Nicole Gaffney, Melissa K. Cousino, Miranda A. Paraskeva
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdvances in medical care and improved transplant outcomes have led to a increasing number of adolescent and young adult solid organ transplant recipients entering adult-orientated services. Adolescence is a period associated with rapid physical, cognitive and psychosocial change. For thoracic transplant recipients it is a period associated with higher rates of acute and chronic rejecyion and mortality. These factors make transition and transfer particularly high-risk periods.MethodsThis paper explores the challenges encountered in the transition and transfer of heart and lung transplant recipients from pediatric to adult care. Examining the structural, psychosocial, developmental and systemic barriers that influence outcomes and appraises best-practice statements and emerging models of care to identify actionable opportunities.ResultsKey barriers include fragmented service structure, gaps in continuity, developmental vulnerabilities (executive functioning, autonomy, risk-taking) and system-level constraints (policies, funding, access). Evidence and consensus highligh the importance of a planned, graduated, patient-centered transition process. Opportunities to improve outcomes included dedicated transition programs, intentional pediatric-adult interdisciplinary collaboration, provision of targeted health information and structured self-management skills training for adolescents and young adults and their carers.ConclusionsSuccessful transition and management of adolescents and young adults with a heart and/or lung transplant requires coordinated efforts among pediatric and adult providers, patients, their caregivers and health care systems. Implementing structured, youth-appropriate, patient-centred transition pathways, grounded in collaboration, continuity of care and skills building, offers a clear route to improving long-term outcomes for this vulnerable population.

Background

Advances in surgical techniques and immunosuppression strategies, as well as improved transplant outcomes, have led to an increasing number of adolescent and young adult (AYA) solid organ transplant recipients entering adult-oriented transplant services. A growing body of evidence, however, has demonstrated that this population faces disproportionately poorer outcomes, including higher rates of allograft failure, mortality, and need for retransplant.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 These findings have led to an increased focus on the processes of transition, the planned, developmentally appropriate preparation for adult-oriented care, and transfer, the formal handover of care from pediatric and adult services. Together, these processes aim to support young people during this critical period and improve long-term outcomes.

Despite the absence of thoracic transplant-specific transition guidelines, several expert consensus statements have been developed to support transition planning and optimize outcomes for AYA transplant recipients.7, 8., 9. Although improving long-term outcomes and in particular graft survival remains the primary objective, effective post-transplant care during transition must also address other important patient-centered outcomes—namely, quality of life, emotional well-being, and psychosocial development. The success or failure of transition is shaped by a complex interplay of individual and systemic factors. These include individual factors such as cognitive development, health literacy, ability to advocate for oneself, carer support, and financial capacity. Alongside broader institutional and systemic challenges such as fragmented electronic medical records, changes in insurance coverage, limited access to age-appropriate services, and the absence of formalized transition policies. A well-executed transition plan prepares young people for independent health management, facilitates adaptation to adult care models, and ensures a coordinated, uninterrupted transfer to adult health care providers without loss of treatment continuity.

This paper explores the complex and multifaceted process of transitioning AYA thoracic transplant recipients from pediatric to adult health care. It aims to synthesize current evidence on developmental, psychosocial, and systemic factors influencing transition outcomes, and to advocate for structured, youth-appropriate models of care that promote long-term graft survival, adherence, and holistic well-being.

The adolescent transplant recipient in context

Adolescence marks the developmental stage between childhood and adulthood. It is characterized by a shift from dependence on caregivers to the growing autonomy and independence of adulthood. It is a period of rapid physical, psychological, and social transformation.

Traditional definitions of adolescence are based on chronological age and sociocultural norms, while the medical literature has shown inconsistency of definition, often attributing varying age ranges to this stage of life. Contemporary frameworks increasingly integrate both neurocognitive development research and changing social expectations, recognizing that the full autonomy and independent functioning that defines adulthood often develops beyond age 18. As such, the accepted definition of adolescence and young adulthood now more broadly encompasses individuals aged 10 to 24 years.10

This developmental period is associated with increased vulnerability. Adolescents face higher rates of trauma, suicide, and substance use,11., 12., 13. with mortality rates beginning to rise from around age 11. Mortality peaks in young adults aged 18 to 24 years, and is 6 times greater than that of children aged 5 to 11 years, and twice that of 12- to 17-year-olds.11 These elevated risks are further amplified in AYAs with chronic health conditions. Poorly coordinated AYA care and inadequate transition support have been associated with worse clinical outcomes, including worsening physical and mental health, social and health care disengagement, a higher burden of disease, increased hospitalization rates, and increased health service utilization.14, 15 Youth from indigenous and marginalized communities face additional systemic and cultural barriers, compounding these risks and contributing to persistent health inequities.16

AYA transplant recipients represent less than 10% of the total transplant population; however, they experience disproportionately poor outcomes, including higher rates of rejection, graft loss, and mortality compared to both younger children and older adult recipients.1, 2, 3, 4, 17 Foster et al first identified a heightened risk of graft loss during late adolescence among kidney transplant recipients, identifying the period between 17 and 21 years as the high-risk window.2, 3 Similar results have been observed in thoracic transplantation. Heart transplant recipients aged 17 to 24 years have demonstrated the highest risk of graft loss,1 while lung transplant recipients transplanted between the ages of 10 and 24 years show worse survival compared to younger children and older adults, with the lowest survival seen in those aged 15 to 19 years.4

The drivers of these adverse outcomes are likely to be multifactorial. High rates of nonadherence and the inherent challenges of transitioning to adult care are key contributors. These are further exacerbated by psychosocial stressors intrinsic to the AYA age group and compounded by systemic issues, such as nonintegrated electronic health records, changes in service structure, and discontinuities in care. With adult health services often lacking the holistic, developmentally appropriate support offered in pediatric settings, leaving AYAs vulnerable during this critical transition.

Nonadherence considerations

Nonadherence is particularly prevalent among AYA transplant recipients and is widely recognized as a major contributor to poor outcomes in this population. Nonadherence encompasses a number of behaviors, including failure to take immunosuppressive medications, attend clinics, and engage in recommended health-promoting activities. Adolescence is a developmental period marked by increased risk for disengagement from health-related behaviors, and this is reflected in adherence patterns observed across various chronic illnesses, including diabetes, rheumatological conditions, and asthma, where declines in medication adherence, clinic attendance, and routine care (e.g., blood draws) are well documented.6, 18, 19 Within the context of pediatric and AYA solid organ transplantation, rates of nonadherence have been reported to range from 20% to 80%, depending on the age group studied, the definitions employed, and the methodologies used to assess adherence.20 These findings highlight both the prevalence and complexity of adherence challenges in this high-risk group, underscoring the need for tailored, developmentally appropriate interventions. Nonadherence has been consistently associated with worse transplant outcomes, in particular with graft loss and the development of de novo donor-specific antibodies.17, 21 A study of renal transplant recipients in Australia and New Zealand17 demonstrated a modest association between age and all-cause graft loss, but a strong relationship between age and graft loss due to late acute rejection attributable to nonadherence. Notably, this risk increased sharply from age 16 and persisted into the mid-20s, closely aligning with the period during which transfer of care typically occurs.

Importantly, nonadherence is often multifactorial.20 Contributing factors include limited health literacy, poor understanding of the treatment regimen, treatment fatigue, intolerable medication side effects, and the financial burden of lifelong immunosuppression. In liver transplantation, Shemesh et al22 demonstrated a dose-dependent association between nonadherence and biopsy-proven rejection, reinforcing the critical role adherence plays in outcomes. Moreover, they highlighted that adherence behavior is dynamic and can fluctuate over time. In that study, almost 20% of participants who were adherent in the first year became nonadherent by the second year, highlighting the dynamic nature of adherence and the need for long-term, adaptable interventions to support continued engagement with care.

Taken together, these findings underscore the importance of proactively identifying at-risk individuals, understanding the multifaceted drivers of nonadherence, and implementing sustained, individualized support strategies. Transition programs must be equipped to detect and address lapses in adherence early and offer continuous reinforcement of medication routines and clinic engagement throughout the transfer to adult care.

Developmental considerations

A number of adolescent-specific risk factors may impact the development of effective self-management skills in AYA transplant recipients and undermine successful transition to adult care. Adolescence is marked by substantial physical, cognitive, and emotional changes, including the onset of puberty, the development of advanced thinking skills, and heightened emotional reactivity. Neurocognitively, brain and cognitive maturation is seen well into the third decade. Adolescence is thereby characterized by early maturation of the limbic system (which is responsible for reward seeking and emotional sensitivity) and slower maturation of the prefrontal cortex (which is responsible for impulse control and executive functions). This temporal mismatch in development predisposes AYAs to higher risk behaviors, sensation-seeking, and prioritization of immediate rewards over long-term consequences.23, 24 As a result, AYAs may struggle with the sustained planning and risk aversion needed to adhere to post-transplant regimens.

For AYAs with a thoracic transplantation, these normative developmental challenges are compounded by transplant-specific complexities. Many have congenital conditions or have undergone interventions (such as cardiopulmonary bypass, intensive care unit length of stay, and pretransplant hospital stay) that predispose them to neurodevelopmental and neurocognitive deficits.23, 25 These deficits can directly affect core executive functions, such as planning and problem solving, and are associated with poorer adherence,26 further complicating the transition process.

Identity formation is another salient factor. AYA recipients often grapple with the impact of transplant on their wider identity, describing the duality between being “healthy” and still feeling defined by their illness.27, 28 Some articulate a sense of resilience and growth,29, 30 while others report emotional experiences such as survivor's guilt or sadness linked to their awareness of their donor organ.30 This desire for “normalcy” can lead adolescents to resist health-related restrictions or hide their transplant history from peers, potentially diminishing engagement with care. This developmental drive to “fit in” and appear “normal” can create tension with the behavioral expectations of transplant care, particularly in visible or socially stigmatizing aspects such as taking medication in public or disclosing one’s transplant status. These competing priorities may result in intentional or unintentional nonadherence.

Biological considerations

Sex and gender have been purported to play a role in treatment adherence and disease outcome.31 While findings in adolescent solid organ transplant (SOT) recipients (liver, heart, and kidney) have been inconsistent, most studies using self-report and/or electronic monitoring suggest that males tend to show poorer adherence than females.32, 33, 34 A more recent study by Vaisbourd et al,35 however, highlights important nuances in adherence assessment. This study found that although young women following SOT reported higher adherence, they demonstrated greater variability tacrolimus trough levels (a marker associated with nonadherence). The underlying cause of this remains unclear, but several contributing factors have been proposed. These include socioenvironmental influences such as gender-related social desirability bias, where females may be more inclined, provide responses perceived as favorable to the health care provider,36 and biological factors, including the potential influence of sex hormones on immunosuppressant metabolism.35 Similar trends have also been observed in the cystic fibrosis population, where young women tend to experience poorer outcomes during adolescence and young adulthood.37 These findings underscore the importance of both biological sex and sociocultural influences when assessing adherence and tailoring interventions for AYA transplant recipients.

Mental health considerations

Internationally, 10% to 20% of young people live with a mental health condition, most commonly anxiety and/or depression.38 Mental health is an under-recognized but critical component of successful transition practices with high rates of mental health conditions reported among AYAs with a thoracic transplant. Up to a third of pediatric heart transplant recipients report clinically significant psychological distress, including generalized anxiety, transplant-related worries, and severe needle phobia39 and post-traumatic stress symptoms have been documented across both pediatric heart and lung transplant recipients.40 The presence of a mental health comorbidity both before and after transplant is linked to poorer adherence and adverse transition outcomes, including increased mortality.41, 42 These factors underscore the critical need for early identification, integrated care, and access to mental health support in providing comprehensive post-transplant care.

Caregiver and family considerations

The transition to greater independence that is the core of adolescent development may itself present barriers to the transfer of care. The readiness of both AYAs and their caregivers plays a pivotal role in shaping health care engagement, adherence, and overall transition success. An adolescent’s ability to take on increasing responsibility for their health management is influenced by developmental maturity, health literacy, and prior opportunities to build self-management skills. However, caregivers of children with critical and/or chronic illnesses experience difficulty relinquishing control and allowing the young person greater autonomy in medical decision-making. This is further compounded by evidence of a discordance between the preferences of AYAs and their caregivers. While young people generally desire more autonomy and independent decision-making, caregivers prefer shared decision-making models that maintain their involvement.43 Discrepancies in transition readiness between adolescents and their caregivers can generate tension, hinder effective communication, and impede the development of essential competencies, ultimately compromising the transition process.44 Overinvolvement or reluctance on the part of caregivers to relinquish control may undermine the adolescent’s confidence and ability to engage independently in health-related behaviors. Conversely, insufficient parental support in the context of low adolescent readiness may heighten the risk of nonadherence and disengagement from care. Moreover, the strong relational bonds formed between families and pediatric providers can further complicate the transition, as the disruption of these trusted relationships may provoke anxiety, diminish trust in adult care settings, and reduce engagement with new providers. Accordingly, systematic assessment of transition readiness in both adolescents and their caregiver coupled with strategies to maintain continuity and support emotional adjustment is essential to fostering adherence and achieving successful transition outcomes.

Young people with disabilities

The transition from pediatric to adult care presents distinct challenges for young people with disabilities. This group often requires complex, multidisciplinary management and may face additional barriers related to neurodevelopmental or cognitive impairments, many of which are associated with underlying conditions or complications of transplant care, such as prolonged intensive care stays or cardiopulmonary bypass.25 These challenges can impair self-management capacity, hinder communication with health care providers, and increase reliance on caregivers during a period when autonomy is expected to grow. Adult transplant services, however, may lack the developmental expertise or infrastructure necessary to support these needs, leading to fragmented care and increased risk of nonadherence, graft loss, or psychosocial distress.45 Furthermore, rigid age-based transfer policies often fail to account for developmental readiness, placing young people with disabilities at heightened risk for poor transition outcomes. Tailored, individualized transition planning, incorporating cognitive assessments, caregiver involvement, and supported decision-making, is essential to ensuring continuity of care and optimizing long-term outcomes in this vulnerable subgroup.

Financial considerations

In addition to the developmental and psychosocial challenges faced during transition, structural and financial factors can significantly impact health outcomes. The shift toward independence often coincides with the loss of parental or family-based health insurance coverage, as well as the discontinuation of age-limited governmental support programs. These changes can result in reduced access to essential post-transplant care, including immunosuppressive medications, laboratory monitoring, and follow-up with transplant specialists, due to financial constraints. Such barriers have been associated with increased nonadherence and poorer clinical outcomes in the post-transition period.46 Recognizing and addressing these systemic vulnerabilities is critical to improving continuity of care and long-term graft survival in this population.

In sum, although the challenges of adolescence are shared across populations, transplant recipients face additional complex barriers. These include cognitive vulnerabilities, identity conflicts, psychological distress, shifting social roles, and evolving family dynamics, all of which require thoughtful, individualized strategies to support successful transition.

Transition vs transfer

Transition and transfer are distinct yet interconnected processes in the continuum of care for AYA transplant recipients. Transition refers to the purposeful, planned process that prepares AYAs to move from pediatric, family-centered care to an adult health care system, where the emphasis shifts to individual autonomy and self-management.47 This process ideally begins in early adolescence and continues into early adulthood. In contrast, transfer describes the single, logistical event of moving a patient’s care, administratively and clinically, from pediatric to adult services. Optimal transfer occurs at the culmination of a well-supported transition process.48

The goal of transition is not merely to relocate care, but to empower AYAs with the competencies required to independently manage their health. A developmentally appropriate, patient-centered transition aims to promote self-determination, strengthen communication between the patient and provider, and build capacity for health-related decision-making.8., 9., 49

Transition should be youth-centered, strengths-based, and developmentally appropriate. Its core principles include early assessment of transition readiness of both young person and their caregiver, individualized transition planning, provision of support to facilitate stepwise acquisition of self-management skills, and structured support during and after transfer to an adult center.8., 9.

The timing of transition varies between jurisdictions but usually occurs at a vulnerable time in an individual’s life, between the ages of 17 and 25 years. The fact that this high-risk age window corresponds with the timing of transfer of care may not be purely coincidental. The act of transferring care, physically moving to a new setting and establishing relationships with new providers, can itself disrupt continuity of care and contribute to adverse outcomes. Empirical data confirm the risks associated with poorly executed transfers.50 In renal transplantation, more than one-third of AYA recipients lose a functioning graft within 36 months of transfer to adult services.51 In a cohort of heart and lung transplant recipients, 10% died within 1 year of transfer.52 Transfer before the age of 21 was also associated with a 57% higher risk of graft failure than those transferring at 21 years or older.53 These findings, similar to those in other chronic disease groups,54 suggest that adult care models may not fully meet the developmental needs of young recipients, and that early transfer may compound the inherent risks of this life stage.

In this context, transition is not a static checklist, but a dynamic, responsive process—one that must be personalized to the unique needs, capacities, and circumstances of each young person. Adult services must be prepared to receive and support these patients with the same developmental sensitivity that underpins pediatric care. Without this continuity and investment, transfer risks become a point of attrition, rather than a gateway to continued success.

Providing youth-appropriate care

A wide range of transition models has been developed to support AYAs in their progression from pediatric to adult health care systems. These models vary in scope and design, from joint pediatric-adult transition clinics and designated transition coordinators to skill-building programs focused on fostering independence and health care self-management.51, 55, 56 While the structures differ, a shared foundation across these models is early, developmentally appropriate planning that supports AYAs in acquiring the skills needed to transfer to adult care.

High-quality, youth-appropriate care must be developmentally appropriate, holistic, and responsive to the evolving physical, emotional, and social needs of AYAs as they transition into adulthood. This requires a nuanced understanding of AYA development and the complex interplay of biological, cognitive, and social changes occurring during this formative period. Structural and societal factors, such as implicit biases toward youth, limitations in service accessibility, and abrupt care model shifts must also be acknowledged and addressed.

Geographic access to care may further complicate the transition process, particularly for lung transplant recipients, as the number of adult transplant centers is limited and often regionally concentrated. For AYAs relocating for education or employment, this can result in logistical challenges for follow-up care, including difficulty accessing transplant expertise or coordinating care across locations.

Although transition occurs within a defined chronological window, the developmental trajectory of each young person is unique. Therefore, care models should be flexible and personalized, adapting to the individual’s stage of maturity, rather than rigidly adhering to age-based timelines.57

Key components of youth-appropriate care include:

Clear, inclusive, and developmentally tailored communication: Health information should be delivered using age-appropriate language with sensitivity to the young person’s cognitive and emotional maturity. The pace and depth of discussion should be adaptable and trusted sources of supplementary information should be provided to discourage reliance on unverified and less credible platforms.58

Continuity of care: Characterized by ongoing relationships with familiar providers, it helps foster trust, engagement, and consistent communication.59 Though continuity is important in all therapeutic relationships, it is particularly important in the AYA population, where long-term provider relationships support not only medical management but also recognition of evolving psychological and social needs that may otherwise exacerbate clinical risk.

Compassionate, nonjudgmental approaches: An approach that acknowledges the unique challenges of adolescence, incorporates compassionate, nonjudgmental communication, and allows for flexibility and shared negotiation around some aspects of care may also promote more meaningful and effective partnerships with young people.16

Balancing autonomy and family involvement: Effective transition includes supporting the young person’s emerging independence while appropriately involving caregivers. Clinicians must balance potentially beneficial family involvement with fostering individual autonomy and encourage the young person’s active participation in shared decision-making.60, 61, 62

Importantly, youth-appropriate care must reflect generational characteristics. Today’s AYA population belongs to Generation Z,63 a cohort deeply engaged with digital technology. Nearly all own smartphones,64 the majority spend more than 5 hours a day on their devices, often using digital platforms to track and manage aspects of their health.65 While many still prefer face-to-face medical interactions, social media and digital platforms play a prominent role in shaping health behaviors and telemedicine platforms may provide options to improve clinic attendance by providing an alternative to face-to-face medical interactions. Over 60% of AYAs report that social media influencers shape their health decisions.65 Programs that integrate digital health interventions, such as mobile reminders, secure messaging, and app-based health tracking as well as increased access to telehealth, may show promise and improve adherence in this group.

Finally, youth-appropriate care must be prepared to address sensitive and often underexplored topics, including mental health, sexual and reproductive health, body image, identity, and peer relationships. AYAs with chronic health conditions, including transplant recipients, face increased risks of anxiety, depression, and social isolation. These psychosocial factors can negatively affect adherence and engagement, which may in turn impact health behaviors such as medication adherence and clinic attendance. Tailored screening and timely referral pathways for support services are essential components of holistic care.

Assessment of transition readiness

Assessing transition readiness is a vital component of preparing AYAs for the successful transfer from pediatric to adult care. Transition readiness encompasses not only knowledge of one’s medical condition and treatment plan but also the acquisition of skills necessary for independent health management, such as appointment scheduling, prescription refills, and effective communication with health care providers. Recognizing that adolescents vary widely in their developmental trajectories, several standardized tools have been developed to assess readiness in a structured, developmentally sensitive manner. These tools facilitate tailored transition planning, help identify areas requiring support, and foster meaningful conversations between health care teams, AYAs, and their caregivers. Transition interventions

Studies evaluating transition interventions have yielded mixed results. While these programs are generally considered feasible and acceptable by adolescent recipients and their families,68, 69, 70, 71 their impact on clinical outcomes has been inconsistent. Some studies have demonstrated no significant effect on key health outcomes70 while others have shown improvements in transplant-related knowledge and medication adherence without corresponding gains in long-term outcomes such as graft survival or reduced hospitalization rates.68 Promising results have emerged from trials of mobile app-based health interventions aimed at improving adherence in adult transplant recipients.72, 73 Given the high uptake and familiarity with mobile technology among AYAs, such digital interventions have the potential to be both acceptable and effective in this population.

The role of the adult provider in transition

While pediatric providers have traditionally led transition planning, it is increasingly recognized that transition cannot be successful without active and sustained engagement from adult health care providers. A collaborative, cross-sector approach is essential—not only to maintain continuity of care, but to ensure that the developmental, psychosocial, and clinical needs of AYA transplant recipients are met throughout and beyond the transfer process.74, 75 Successful transition requires adult providers to adopt a developmentally sensitive model of care that extends beyond standard medical management. This includes acknowledging and addressing the behavioral, psychological, and social dimensions of adolescent health, which are often central to long-term transplant success.

Despite general acceptance of the importance of transition efforts, adult providers cite barriers including lack of training in adolescent medicine, family expectations, time constraints and cost, low patient volumes of AYAs relative to overall adult caseload, and lack of resources often available in pediatric settings, including case management and mental health support.76 To overcome these challenges and promote successful transition, system-level interventions are likely to be required. Health professionals involved in transition should receive training in youth-appropriate care principles. Designating consistent adult providers, where possible, enables continuity of care and is key to the success of transition programs.77 The implementation of dedicated transition coordinators has been proposed to assist young people in navigating the complexities of adult care and orientating them to adult health systems.78

Structured transition programs have demonstrated success in various transplant populations. In renal and liver transplant populations, such programs have been associated with improved adherence, reduced hospitalizations, and lower rates of graft loss51, 75 and while relatively, expert consensus supports their adoption.7, 8., 9., 77 While readiness for transition can be assessed in a standardized manner, there are no standardized measures for transition success beyond graft and patient survival. Broader metrics, such as adherence, health-related quality of life, health care utilization, and patient-reported experiences, should be adopted to better capture the full impact of transition.

Every center involved in the care of AYA transplant recipients should prioritize the development of a formal transition policy. These policies must extend into the adult setting, ensuring that skills developed in pediatric care are not only preserved but strengthened, and that outcomes are tracked and evaluated in a standardized manner.

Conclusion

The transition from pediatric to adult care is a critical point in the lifelong care of AYA thoracic transplant recipients. It is a period marked by heightened risk for nonadherence, graft loss, health care disengagement, and psychosocial distress that occurs at a time when young people are simultaneously navigating the complex developmental demands of emerging adulthood. Successful transition is more than the effective administrative transfer of care. It is an organized, structured, developmentally appropriate process that prepares young people for independent health management in an adult health care setting. It is therefore essential that transition is a coordinated process beginning in early adolescence in the pediatric setting and continues through early adulthood and past the point of transfer into the adult setting and includes both the young person and their carer. This requires active collaboration between pediatric and adult providers, integration of mental health and psychosocial supports, and system-level redesign to ensure continuity of care. For transition to succeed, adult clinicians must be engaged and supported to offer youth-oriented health care services, with transition policies in place that address the evolving needs of this population.

Importantly, this period also offers unique opportunities. Adolescence and young adulthood are times of growth, identity formation, and increasing autonomy, and are therefore fertile ground for empowerment, education, and the development of lifelong self-management skills. When supported effectively, transition can serve as a launchpad for improved health literacy, patient engagement, and graft survival. Furthermore, the integration of digital health technologies, peer support models, and strengths-based practices offers novel pathways to improve care delivery and engagement with this highly connected, digitally literate generation.

As formal transition programs gain traction, instruments such as Transition Readiness Assessment Questionnaire, Self-Management and Transition Readiness Questionnaire, and Got Transition offer frameworks to evaluate self-management competencies, self-advocacy, and caregiver preparedness and can help guide individualized transition planning. However, there remains a need to establish standardized outcome measures that go beyond survival metrics and include adherence, health-related quality of life, and patient experience as part of their evaluation to evaluate such programs and determine their effectiveness. Investment in research, policy, and workforce development will be critical to ensuring that every child undergoing thoracic transplantation is equipped and supported to thrive in adult care settings and beyond.
