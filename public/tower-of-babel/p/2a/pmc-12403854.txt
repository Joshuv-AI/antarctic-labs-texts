Comparison of the incidence of reperfusion syndrome and outcomes of liver transplantation among pediatric recipients of partial vs. whole graft: report of six-year retrospective study
Authors: Fatemeh Khalili, Mohammad Bagher Khosravi, Mohammad Ali Sahmeddini, Mohammad Hossein Eghbal, Fatemeh Kanaani Nejad, Mahsa Banifatemi, Naeimehossadat Asmarian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdvancements in graft selection, surgical and anesthetic techniques have improved the outcomes of pediatric liver transplantation over the last few decades. In this study, we evaluate preoperative factors, the incidence of post-reperfusion syndrome, rejection, and survival among 523 pediatric patients who received whole vs. partial liver transplantation.MethodsIn a six-year retrospective observational study on 523 pediatric liver transplantation recipients (age < 18 years old) in Abu-Ali Sina Organ Transplantation Hospital, we collected recipients’ demographic data, warm and cold ischemic time, post reperfusion syndrome, and postoperative outcomes, including mechanical ventilation time, ICU length of stay, acute renal failure, acute cellular rejection, re-transplantation, and patients’ survival. We compared these preoperative, operative, and postoperative outcomes among whole and partial graft recipients using SPSS software for Windows.Results259 cases received partial and 264 cases received whole organ transplantation. We compared the donor factors, preoperative, intraoperative recipient factors, and postoperative outcomes between the two groups. The donors’ age and percentage of macrovesicular steatotic changes of the partial donor graft were significantly higher than the whole donor graft, respectively (33.5 vs. 6,p < 0.001) (31% vs. 17%,p < 0.001). The recipients of partial graft had younger age (3.5 (1.5-8) vs. 9 (5–14) years, P < 0.001) than recipients of whole organ graft. Although partial organ recipients had shorter cold ischemic time (25 (15–30) vs. 480 (390–570) minutes, P < 0.001), they significantly developed post-reperfusion syndrome more than whole organ recipients (14% vs. 7.5%, P = 0.01). The outcome of partial organ recipients also seemed to be poorer compared with whole organ recipients; they had longer ICU stays (18 (11–29) vs. 14 (9–20) days, P = 0.004) and lower survival (61.4% vs. 81.8%, P < 0.001).ConclusionAlthough partial organ transplantation provides more chances for pediatric liver transplant candidates to survive, we observed that physicians have faced many challenges, and children had more complications and lower survival after partial organ transplantation.

Introduction

The only curative treatment for pediatric patients with end-stage liver disease is orthotopic Liver transplantation (OLT). Both living and deceased donor liver grafts are now utilized, increasing the pediatric organ pool, reducing the mortality rates of candidates on the waiting list, and reducing the wait time for transplantation. Advancements in surgical and anesthetic techniques have improved the outcomes of pediatric liver transplantation over the last few decades [1, 2].

The most critical intraoperative challenge for anesthesiologists during orthotopic liver transplantation (OLT) is the post-reperfusion syndrome (PRS), which is defined as a decrease in mean arterial pressure of more than 30% of baseline for more than one minute, asystole, or significant cardiac arrhythmias during the first five minutes following graft reperfusion [3, 4]. It can lead to mortality and morbidity, such as postoperative kidney injury (AKI), early allograft dysfunction, and prolonged ICU or hospital stay [3–6]. The incidence of PRS was reported to be 12–77%. Graft reperfusion is influenced by variables related to the donor, recipient, and surgical technique [3–6]. To date, many studies have found some donor risk factors that contribute to PRS, but the effect of graft type in the occurrence of PRS and its related outcomes in pediatric liver transplantation are less known [3–9].

This study aimed to compare the incidence of PRS and postoperative clinical outcomes of OLT among those who received partial vs. whole grafts in Abu-Ali Sina Hospital, the largest referral center for pediatric liver transplantation in the Middle East.

Methods

This is an observational retrospective study. The study protocol conforms to the ethical guidelines of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki, approved by the Ethics Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (IR.SUMS.REC.1401.574).

Study design

We reviewed the medical records of all pediatric patients (age < 18 years old) who underwent LT at Abu-Ali Sina Organ Transplantation Hospital over six years (April 2016 to March 2022). The exclusion criteria were the following: (1) split and reduced graft, (2) combined transplantation, (3) re-transplantation, (4) patients with missing preoperative, operative, or postoperative data. A total of 523 pediatric patients were included. As there were no split or reduced grafts from deceased donors, the included participants were divided into two groups related to graft types: partial graft and whole graft.

Anesthesia protocol

All pediatric patients had undergone general anesthesia for liver transplantation with midazolam (0.1 mg/Kg) and fentanyl (3–5 µ g/Kg) as premedication. Anesthesia induction was achieved using propofol (2–3 mg/kg) and cisatracurium (0.2–0.3 mg/kg), followed by maintenance with isoflurane and a 50:50 air–oxygen mixture. Ventilation was managed using pressure-controlled ventilation (PCV) for patients weighing less than 20 kg or volume-controlled ventilation (VCV) for those over 20 kg, ensuring end-tidal CO2 levels remained between 30 and 35 mmHg. After the induction of anesthesia, an arterial line with a suitable catheter through the radial artery and a central venous line with a triple lumen catheter (French No: 5–7) through the right internal jugular vein were inserted under ultra-sonography guidance. The other intraoperative monitoring included electrocardiography, pulse oximetry, capnograph, urine measuring device, and core body temperature. Forced-air warming blanket was used for all of them. After the unclumping of vasculature, upon diagnosis of PRS, epinephrine (10–50 µg IV bolus) was used to treat hypotension or new-onset arrhythmias, and then norepinephrine and vasopressin were infused if persistent hypotension or vasoplegia occurred.

Surgical technique

We used the piggyback technique without venovenous bypass during surgery. Both grafts were procured and preserved with a University of Wisconsin solution and flushed with a balanced crystalloid solution (normal saline 0.9%) with a volume of 1 cc per g of graft.

Data collection

Demographic data related to the recipient (age, sex, weight, body mass index (BMI)), the model for end-stage liver disease (MELD) score, pediatric end-stage liver disease (PELD), and the cause of liver failure were recorded. We also evaluated intraoperative factors such as cold ischemic time (CIT), warm ischemic time (WIT), graft weight to recipient weight ratio (GRWR), and post-reperfusion syndrome (PRS). The postoperative outcomes, including mechanical ventilation time, ICU length of stay, acute renal failure, acute cellular rejection, re-transplantation, and survival rate, were compared between 2 groups.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 22, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). We provided descriptive statistics as median (Q1-Q3) for continuous variables, and categorical variables were summarized using numbers and percentages. Also, we used the Chi-square test, the Fisher exact test for categorical variables, and the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables (because normality was violated) for statistical analysis. The Kaplan- Meier curve was used for survival analysis. The accepted level of significance was 0.05 or less.

Results

Within 6 years of the study, 550 consecutive pediatric patients underwent liver transplantation, of which 27 patients were excluded due to incomplete data (n = 20), split graft (n = 4), and combined liver-kidney transplantation (n = 3). Among 523 pediatric patients, 264 (50.5%) received whole grafts, and 259 (49.5%) had partial grafts. We found no significant difference between the two groups in the demographic data of recipients except for age and weight (p < 0.001, p < 0.001, respectively) (Table 1). The whole graft recipients had a higher mean age and weight than the partial graft recipients (p < 0.001). The most common causes of liver failure in both partial and whole graft groups were cholestatic disease and metabolic disease (especially Wilson) (Table 1). It should be noted that the PELD/MELD scores were also similar in both groups (p = 0.96). The donors’ age and percentage of macrovesicular steatotic changes of the partial donor graft were significantly higher than the whole donor graft, respectively ((33.5(28.75-38) vs. 6 (3–12), p < 0.001) and (31% vs. 17%, p < 0.001)) (Table 1).

The most common cause of death among whole graft recipients was multi-organ failure (14.6%), followed by sepsis (12.5%) and PNF (10.4%). Also, the most common causes of death among partial graft recipients were sepsis at 20%, multi-organ failure at 11%, and PNF at 11%.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that the incidence of PRS among children who received partial grafts was significantly higher than those who received whole grafts. Despite the higher incidence of PRS in patients with partial grafts, there was no difference in the incidence of postoperative renal failure among the studied groups. Recipients of partial grafts also had a longer duration of mechanical ventilation and ICU stay and a lower overall survival rate. Despite extensive research, there is a lot yet to be explored, especially in the donor- and recipient-related factors in pediatric liver transplants [10, 11]. We found that the donors’ age and percentage of macrovesicular steatotic changes of the partial donor graft were significantly higher than the whole donor graft. Siniscalchi et al. [3] and Chung et al. [2] support our findings that the donor’s age and macrovesicular steatosis were the most donor-related factors associated with the occurrence of PRS. We must note that donor age difference is largely attributable to the nature of pediatric living donor liver transplantation (LDLT), where donors are commonly parents or first-degree relatives of the recipient child, naturally resulting in older donor age compared to deceased donor whole grafts. This demographic reality makes the donor age difference between groups an inherent and unavoidable aspect of pediatric liver transplantation practice [12].

In a study by Ayanoglu et al.in 2003, with 185 participants, there was no difference in the incidence of PRS among adults with LDLT and DDLT, and it be may related to the age of recipients. In contrast to our findings, in another study in 2010, with 164 participants [13], the incidence of PRS in DDLT was higher than LDLT among children. Consequently, the most important cause of this difference may be related to considerable disparities in PRS definition or prophylaxis treatment before the occurrence of reperfusion [10, 11]. Some studies did not consider arrhythmia and asystole in the definition of PRS, and they used 20% mannitol and calcium gluconate as prophylaxis for PRS [10, 11]. The results of the studies on the impact of ischemic time on PRS are heterogeneous and inconclusive. Most studies mentioned that prolonged CIT is one of the strongest predictors of PRS [14–16], but our study showed that prolonged CIT in whole graft did not lead to PRS. Probably, the other most important factors than CIT influenced the occurrence of PRS. A study by Hilmi et al. mentioned that managing the WIT below 90 min prevented serious hepatic cellular injury during vascular clumping and did not lead to severe PRS [17]. We found that the duration of WIT in the partial graft was significantly longer than in the other group, but the duration of WIT in both groups was consistent with the above limitation time for inducing hepatic cell necrosis (32 vs. 30 min, p = 0.027).

Regarding the survival rate of pediatric patients following LDLT or DLLT, a meta-analysis with no hint of PRS revealed that children who had living donors have relatively improved post-liver transplantation survival [2, 8, 9, 18], which was in contrast with the result of the current study. In the current study, the patients who underwent DDLT (whole graft) had improved survival rates during the median follow-up time of 3.9 (2.2–4.2) years. (81.8% survival of those with whole grafts compared with 61.4% survival in those with partial grafts). It should be noted that when it comes to PRS, the results on the survival of patients will change. This discrepancy in existing survival in pediatric LT literature may be related to the surgical technique of partial hepatectomy in living donors, which may lead to a marginal liver graft. Siniscalchi et al. [3] support our findings that the risk of PRS and affected outcomes increased with marginal grafts related to donor age and graft steatosis. Notably, steatotic changes impair hepatic microcirculation due to increased cellular volume and obstruction of the sinusoidal space. Therefore, marginal liver grafts may lead to poor graft function [14–16].

Previous studies found similar results that PRS can increase graft failure and post-transplantation morbidity and mortality [3, 6, 19–21]. However, we considered that the PRS was the crucial factor that affected postoperative outcomes and survival. We found that the children who received partial grafts not only experienced PRS significantly more than those who received whole grafts, but they also had longer intubation times, longer ICU stays, and lower survival rates.

We observed that 52 cases out of 264 recipients of whole grafts (20%) and 56 cases out of 259 recipients of partial grafts (22%) had acute cellular rejection, which was not a statistically significant difference. In contrast to our results, in another study in 2018 on 177 DDLTs and 64 LDLT pediatric recipients, it was reported that DDLT was associated with a higher rate of acute cellular rejection [22]. Regarding that multiple factors play a role in the rejection of transplanted organ [23], the results of this study on the association between the type of graft and the rejection rate still cannot be conclusive.

According to previous studies, the PRS is one of the most important intraoperative risk factors that induce acute kidney injury (AKI) and renal failure. It was considered an important post-liver transplantation complication affecting graft and patient survival [24]. In our study, the incidence of postoperative renal failure is exactly the same among recipients of whole and partial grafts. This can be explained by other probable renal failure risk factors other than PRS [25].

Limitation

The limitations of our study include retrospective design and some missing information in our database. Future cohort studies with more detailed data, including more information about the preoperative condition of patients, and surgical techniques are recommended to clarify different aspects of partial and whole liver grafting among children.

Conclusion

In this large retrospective cohort of pediatric liver transplant recipients, those receiving partial grafts experienced a significantly higher incidence of post-reperfusion syndrome, longer mechanical ventilation and ICU stays, and lower overall survival compared to recipients of whole grafts. However, these findings must be interpreted in the context of significant differences in recipient age and donor characteristics, particularly the older age and higher macrovesicular steatosis of donors in the partial graft group. This donor age difference is an inherent feature of living donor transplantation, as donors are often parents or first-degree relatives of the recipient child.

Although a partial graft (living donor liver transplantation, LDLT) provides more chances for pediatric liver transplant candidates, physicians have faced many challenges in surgical techniques, and patients have more risk of complications with this type of liver graft. While partial graft transplantation remains a critical option to expand the donor pool and provide life-saving treatment for pediatric patients, the observed poorer outcomes may be influenced by these confounding factors rather than graft type alone. Our initial attempt at propensity score matching was limited by data loss; therefore, further prospective studies with rigorous adjustment for recipient and donor variables are essential to clarify the independent impact of graft type on outcomes.
