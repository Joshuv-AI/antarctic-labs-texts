Organ-on-chip technologies: Novel tools with the potential to revolutionize osteoarthritis research and clinical development
Authors: Carlo Alberto Paggi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo critically evaluate the evolution of in vitro models for osteoarthritis (OA) research, with a specific focus on organ-on-chip (OOC) technologies, and to discuss their potential to overcome the limitations of traditional 2D, 3D, and explant models in disease modelling, drug discovery, and preclinical toxicology.MethodsThis review synthesizes findings from recent literature regarding historical and current in vitro platforms used in OA research. It categorizes models based on biological complexity and physiological relevance, with particular emphasis on biomechanical and biochemical stimulation in OOC systems. Key examples from the literature are discussed to illustrate the utility and scalability of emerging platforms.ResultsTraditional 2D cultures, while widely used, lack the spatial and environmental complexity necessary to replicate native joint physiology. 3D cultures and explant models provide improved architecture and cellular context, but face challenges related to standardization, longevity, and reproducibility. OOC systems offer a dynamic and tunable microenvironment that supports real-time monitoring and integration of mechanical cues such as shear stress and compression. These platforms have been successfully employed to investigate immune cell migration, drug permeability across barriers, and cytokine-induced matrix degradation. Furthermore, their application in preclinical modelling for efficacy and safety studies highlights the potential for more predictive, human-relevant data compared to conventional methods.ConclusionOOCs technology represents a transformative advance in OA modelling, offering increased physiological relevance and experimental precision. With continued refinement and validation, OOC platforms could significantly improve the translational success of disease-modifying OA therapies.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a joint degenerative disease affecting predominantly hands, hips and knees [1]. OA is the most common form of arthritis affecting around 528 million people worldwide in 2019 according to the World Health Organization [2]. From 1990, this number has progressively increased up to 113 ​% more in 2020([3]). Moreover, as the disease is predominantly present in the elderly, this number is expected to increase due to the aging of the population [4]. The most common characteristics of OA are joint swelling, stiffness and pain followed by a progressive joint-dysfunction which leads, in the last stages of the disease, to surgery [5]. The disease has been widely described with primarily loss of cartilage tissue which initiates a series of mechanisms that bring to a progressive inflammatory cycle [6].

Despite extensive investigation, OA remains challenging to treat, largely due to an incomplete understanding of the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying its onset and progression [7]. This knowledge gap hampers the development of effective therapeutic interventions by pharmaceutical companies aimed at treating or modifying the course of the disease [8]. As of today, no disease-modifying osteoarthritic drug has been able to reach the market due to the complexity of the disease [9]. Hence, the nonsteroidal anti-Inflammatory Drugs (ibuprofen (Motrin, Advil) naproxen (Aleve) and diclofenac (Voltaren, others)) are the most common treatments focused on only relieving the symptoms associated with OA [10].

This is partially due to the type of models that are currently adopted in the context of the pre-clinical phase. Here, typically researchers focus on high throughput 2D assays followed by animal models [11]. Selection of preclinical animal models is dictated by the OA phenotype and research objective, spanning large species (e.g., horse, pig) to small species (e.g., mouse, guinea pig). [12]. The model makes use of genetic modification, spontaneous aging or surgical intervention among others. Typically, the most common model is based on mice as they are the fastest and cheaper, speeding the transition from IND filing to clinical phase.

Although animal models have served as indispensable tools in the study and treatment of various diseases, including cancer [13], their translational reliability from animal to human remains limited in the context of osteoarthritis. [14]. Example of large OA clinical trials like the NGF inhibitors (tanezumab) of Eli Lilly and Pfizer [15] or UBX0101 of Unity Biotechnology [16], which showed promising response in animals, had poor translatability and both trials ceased after proving low efficacy in patient. These results can be accounted to multiple variables like appropriate selection of cohort of patients and dosing among others [17].

Overall, the current pre-clinical models, whether in vitro or in vivo have limitations in accurately representing the disease's complexity and heterogeneity, hindering the development of effective treatments [18]. These models often focus on aspects of OA, like cartilage degradation, while neglecting other factors such as inflammation pain and, for example, subchondral bone changes. In addition, variability in OA presentation among individuals and lack of standardized endpoint for research studies makes it challenging to accurately translate findings from pre-clinical models, such as animals, to human patients [19].

Hence, it is clear that new innovative technologies, able to recapitulate the human tissue function and behavior, may be the key to a successful clinical trial and access to a market that accounts for more than 10 ​% of the world population. In this context, in the last two decades, scientists have been identifying new potential strategies to create appropriate tissues in vitro. One of the main technologies that was developed are organ-on-chips (OOCs) [20]. In this review we will provide the definition of OOCs, the manufacturing processes and material currently adopted which have also previously been assessed by other reviews in the field. Next, we highlight, for the first time, an overview of the current regulatory initiative landscape for these technologies. Lastly, an update with respect to current literature will be provided on available OOC technologies, in the context of OA, will be showcased to conclude with the current challenges and opportunities.

Animal models in drug assessment

In the last decades animal models have been the gold standard for drug evaluation [21]. They have provided important insights into toxicity as well as the potency of the variety of compounds that are currently commercially available [22]. Despite their widespread use, animal models present significant limitations in the assessment of OA. A major drawback is their poor translational relevance to human physiology, particularly in complex multifactorial diseases like OA. Anatomical, biomechanical and immunological difference between animals and humans resulted in discrepancies in disease onset as well as drug response [23]. While animal models can exhibit cartilage degradation or joint inflammation, they rarely capture the chronicity, heterogeneity and pain phenotype shown in human OA patients. NGF inhibitors [24] or senolytic compounds that have shown efficacy in animals have repeatedly failed in clinical trials due to poor human predictability. This mismatch delays drug development and leads to substantial financial losses combined with ethical concerns regarding animal welfare. Lastly, OA is often induced artificially through surgical or genetic modification which does not accurately mirror the natural course of the disease seen in humans [12]. These limitations underscore the urgent need for alternative technologies that could potentially better capture OA pathophysiology in humans.

From 2D culture to OOC technologies

The development of the first known 2D in vitro assay date more than 100 years ago with, among the others, Ross Harrison which cultured tissue fragments on a cover slip [25]. These major finding opened the possibility for scientist to explore culturing different tissues and cells outside the human body. The era of in vitro 2D culture had major findings from iPSCs cell differentiation [26] to inflammatory stimulation and pharmaceutical screening. To date, this is still one of the technique most adopted by scientist and starting point for exploratory research. However, as previously discussed, the disadvantages such as the inability of mimic the appropriate physiological environment and the mimicry of a more fibrotic-like phenotype led researchers to look for alternatives.

In the late 20th century, growing recognition of the limitations of existing models prompted the development of three-dimensional culture systems in microplate formats [27]. These 3D models were consisting of cell aggregates or cells emended into a 3D hydrogel matrix resembling the human tissue structure [28]. Among one of the major discoveries of the current days is the possibility to recreate organoids [29].

An additional approach involves the use of tissue explants, particularly in OA research. Explant models—typically harvested from human cartilage or synovial tissue—retain much of the native extracellular matrix, cellular diversity, and structural organization of the original tissue [30]. This makes them particularly valuable for studying disease-relevant pathways, testing pharmacological agents, and examining patient-specific responses in a more physiologically relevant context. However, explants also present several disadvantages, including limited availability, donor variability, a relatively short viable culture period, and the difficulty of maintaining precise control over environmental conditions such as oxygen tension, nutrient gradients, and mechanical forces. Moreover, the lack of standardization in explant preparation and analysis can contribute to experimental variability, limiting their scalability and utility for high-throughput applications [31].

Although key models that helped the research society to assess OA pathophysiology, 2D and 3D models are still far from mimicking the physiological and diseased conditions present in the human body Fig. 1. The biochemical and biomechanical cues influence cells and shift their phenotype and maturity leading to potential biases in the obtained results. In contrast, tissue explants offer a faithful representation of native tissue architecture; however, their limited availability and donor-dependent variability constrain the generation of robust datasets necessary for comprehensive biological evaluation.Fig. 1From 2D to organ-on-chip cultures. In 2010, Donald Ingber and his team designed what was defined as the first Lung-on-chip model [32]. The model had the capabilities of a standard in vitro model while allowing for controlled active stretching and perfusing mechanisms. This first paper introduced, for the first time, the concept of tissue or organ-on-a-chip. From that day, the field of this “advanced” in vitro models or OOCs is becoming the new gold standard in the in vitro assay assessment [33].

What are OOCs models?

OOCs or micro-physiological systems are devices presenting miniaturized engineered tissues residing in a transparent micro-fluidic chip. In essence, it is a micro-device presenting engineered features that are meant to recapitulate tissue or organ characteristics in vitro [34]. In these devices different cell types and hydrogels can be added in a highly controlled fashion [35]. In essence, they combined the current 3D structure complexity given by bioengineered construct with a fully controlled surrounding environment. The main objective of OOCs is to mimic key aspects of human physiology with full control on multiple variables like structural organization, biomechanical [36] and/or biochemical stimuli. Moreover, as the technology is in a miniaturized device it allows for parallelization using a significantly lower amount of biological material making it ideal when human material is rare.

The typical material that is currently adopted is polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) which is one of the most widely used silicone polymers in a range of industrial applications. The selection of PDMS was based on the ease-of-use, gas permeability, transparency, elasticity and inexpensive cost of production [37]. However, the material absorbs small hydrophobic molecules which make it challenging to assess the specific types of pharmaceutical compounds. Moreover, as the technology was developed in university laboratory settings, no immediate attention was given to the large-scale manufacturability of the devices [38]. Hence, in the last years, different types of materials more “standard” in the context of in vitro cells culture were adopted like polyester or thermoplastic elastomers [39]. These materials can be easily used in large scale industrial machining, such as injection molding, making production cheap and fast [38].

The typical fabrication process of OOCs, independently of the material used, requires the creation of a mold presenting engineered features designed to recapitulate human tissue physiology [40]. The mold can be fabricated with 3D printing technologies, micro-milling machining or more standardly with photolithography. The technique of production varies based on the features requirement of the device [40]. With small feature size, in the order of micro-meters, the higher the complexity and accuracy required for the fabrication. Next, the material is cast or placed on the mold and based on the different manufacturing processes gets the negative shape of the mold. The newly formed device is then bonded to a second surface of the same material or a material with different characteristics and the OOC device is finally complete. Fig. 2 shows a step-to-step process for the development of an OOC model using photo-lithography for the mold production followed by soft-lithography for the device fabrication using PDMS as material [41]. Organ-on-chip technologies: Bridging biological complexity and experimental control

One of the principal advantages contributing to the emergence of OOCs as the next generation of in vitro models lies in their modularity, adaptability and control to specific experimental parameters. These advanced in vitro models enable precise manipulation of the cellular microenvironment through controlled biochemical and biomechanical variables which are known to critically influence cellular phenotype and function. For example, Delannoy et al. engineered a micro-plate with 48 discrete OOC devices where blood vessels were created and inflammatory stimulation could be added to assess blood permeability [42]. This platform is a great example of parallelization and biomechanical assessment of endothelial cells under the influences of continuous shear stress. Wevers et al. created a perfusable blood-brain barrier with which they could assess barrier function and antibody transport [43]. Additional platforms have been developed to investigate directed cell migration through chemical gradients. Os et al. developed an extravasation model that enabled immune extravasation and migration into a 3D hydrogel structure via the use of chemoattractant. This technology can be a valuable tool for studying immune cell behavior in inflammatory diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis [44]. Liu et al. introduced a model that allowed generation of a gradient series for the assessment of Neuroleukin on chondrocytes proliferation. The platform allowed, by the simple geometric configuration, to have different gradient ratios on the culture chambers [45]. Lastly, has the technology derives from the micro-electromechanical systems, several technologies have already integrated biosensors capable of capturing electrical signals [46] or biochemical outputs in real time [47].

Collectively, OOC technologies significantly enhance biological model complexity and fidelity, allowing for the recapitulation of key human physiological and pathological features while maintaining experimental control over multiple microenvironmental parameters. This positions OOCs as powerful analytical tools for dissecting mechanistic cellular responses to defined stimuli in vitro.

Societal and regulatory adoption of OOC technology: current Perspectives

Over the past two decades, scientific paradigms have undergone significant transformation in both conceptual frameworks and methodological approaches. 50 years of animal models with poor translatability have opened the road to potential new solutions that could better mimic the human physiological environment. With the introduction of the project “human on chip” by the US research agency in 2011 the development of this advanced human-based OOC has become a major objective in the development of reliable human-like biological assays for drug development, toxicity and safety [48].

Hence, over the years, the community around this technology has expanded leading to the development of two main and most recognized conferences. The European OOC Society (https://euroocs.eu/) which is a not-for-profit organization established to encourage adoption of OOC devices comprising audience including general public, government institutions, charities, research groups, universities, industry, pharmaceutical industries, OOC manufacturers and biotech. The Micro-physiological Systems World Summit (MPS world summit) (https://mpsworldsummit.com/about-us/) which is a conference aimed at establishing and implementing OOCs and harmonizing the approach to implement it in the regulatory process of drug development.

Although extremely useful in the assessment of biological response, these models require appropriate standardization and validation.

By standardization we mean the use of standardized ISO processes to perform the manufacturing of the devices. Hence, the European union has established a pool of experts within NEN (https://www.nen.nl/) that is currently drafting a roadmap meant to standardize these models (https://www.cencenelec.eu/areas-of-work/cen-cenelec-topics/OOC/). The international group led by Andries van de Meer, highlighted all the aspects within the OOC that should be considered when looking at standardization such as material to use for the manufacturing, geometry, type of cells to be used. Although just at its infancy, different working groups have been created around the world to establish ISO standards.

As OOC technologies expand in complexity and adoption, EMA and FDA are starting to address the need to a validation process to integrate these analytical tools in the regulatory processes for drug development. In Europe, the Innovation Task Force (https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/human-regulatory-overview/research-development/supporting-innovation) is meant to support in the assessment of where can animal be reduced using advanced in vitro technologies. The FDA has established funding specific for the OOC devices and is currently establishing collaboration with different OOC manufacturers based on the technology they have (https://www.fda.gov/emergency-preparedness-and-response/mcm-regulatory-science/human-organ-chips-radiation-countermeasure-development). These initiatives are meant to phase out animal testing and introduce OOC technologies as alternatives in the context of pre-clinical IND filing.

OOC can be seen as analytical tools that can be used to assess the potency or the toxicity of a drug. Hence, validation of these assays is fundamental to have regulatory acceptance. The EMA defines validation of analytical methods as the process of demonstrating that a method is suitable for its intended purpose, particularly in the context of testing the quality of drug substances and drug products. Some of the key parameters that should be accounted for are accuracy, precision, specificity, linearity among others. More information on the processes can be found here: https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/ich-q2r2-validation-analytical-procedures-scientific-guideline.

As the flexibility of this technology offers the possibility to potentially assess both the safety and efficacy of different compounds, additional regulatory bodies in Europe are interested in the implementation of these technologies. One of the main bodies established is the EU Reference Laboratory for alternatives to animal testing (EURL ECVAM) (https://joint-research-centre.ec.europa.eu/eu-reference-laboratory-alternatives-animal-testing-eurl-ecvam_en). This body, which is an integral part of the Joint Research Centre, is used by the European Commission to validate these models in the context of safety.

It is important to understand how this change may affect biotechnological companies and how this technology will progressively be implemented and used as alternatives Fig. 3. In fact, both the EU with their directive of 2010 (https://eur-lex.europa.eu/LexUriServ/LexUriServ.do?uri=OJ:L:2010:276:0033:0079:en:PDF) and the USA with their modernization act 2.0([49]) and now 3.0 (https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/house-bill/7248/text) are stressing how the use of animals in the preclinical validation is “not necessary if an alternative is possible”. Joint tissues and anatomical structures: Building blocks for in vitro modelling

The human joint is a complex anatomical system comprised of multiple highly specialized tissues that work together to enable load-bearing, mobility and homeostasis. In vitro modeling of joint physiology and pathophysiology requires therefore a detailed understanding of the key structural, biochemical and biomechanical components of each of the tissue present in the joint.

At the core of the synovial joints lies the articular cartilage, an avascular loadbearing tissue that provides low-friction, wear-resistant surface for the articulation. Composed predominantly of chondrocytes embedded in an extracellular matrix rich of collagen type II and proteoglycans it functions as shock absorber [50].

Beneath the articular cartilage lies the subchondral bone, a thin layer of mineralized tissue that further supports the mechanical loading exerted on the cartilage. This tissue is highly responsive to mechanical forces and undergoes major changes in response to disease processes such as osteoarthritis. The key cell types present in this tissue are osteoblasts, osteoclasts, osteocytes and bone lining cells which receive nutrients via vascular network [51].

Encasing the joint is the synovial membrane. This tissue presents a synovial lining and sublining layer. It presents a combination of synovial fibroblasts and macrophages in the lining layer which faces the inner part of the joint and has direct contact with the synovial fluid. The sublinng layer present vascularization and innervation. This tissue plays an essential role in producing the synovial fluid which facilitates lubrication and nutrient exchange [52].

Tendons serve a distinct biomechanical role by transmitting force from muscle to bone to enable joint movement. They are composed of aligned collagen fibers, tenocytes and a small population of progenitor cells embedded within a hierarchical matrix [53].

Stabilizing the joint are ligaments, fibrous connective tissues that play a crucial role in limiting the excessive movement of the joint. Ligaments consist of collagen fibers that are regularly arranged and sparsely distributed fibroblast with limited vascularization [54].

Another critical yet often overlooked structure is the infrapatellar fat pad or Hoffa's fat pad. It is a highly vascularized and innervated adipose tissue that in recent years has shown playing an important role in joint homeostasis and disease initiation and progression. It presents adipocytes, mesenchymal stem cells and immune cells [55].

Lastly the meniscus is a fibrocartilaginous structure playing a critical role in load distribution and shock absorption. The outer red zone is vascularized and populated predominantly by fibroblast-like cells, while the inner zone is avascular and contains chondrocyte-like cells embedded in a dense extracellular matrix rich of collagen type I and II as well as proteoglycans [56].

What is currently being done in OA using these technologies

OOC technologies are progressively changing the way in vitro studies are performed. In the context of OA and rheumatoid arthritis these tools are becoming more prominent with also OOC manufacturers providing technologies to researchers that want to reduce the use of animals.

The synovial joint is an “organ” presenting a series of different tissues that are communicating via the synovial fluid [57]. Hence, replicating the entire joint spaces is currently still challenging and requires a highly complex system. In this context, scientists have decided to focus on specific tissues that have been considered main game changers in the development and progression of the disease. Note that all the joint of the human body have differences in characteristics and tissue types [58]. In this review we primarily focus on the knee joint which is currently mostly investigated in the context of OA.

Currently the main body of work is the development of cartilage and synovial membrane (Table 1). However, multiple other tissues like ligament, Hoffa's fat pad, meniscus and bone may be necessary to recreate an appropriate joint homeostasis. Cartilage-on-chip

The hyaline articular cartilage is the tissue covering the surface of the bone in synovial joints. It is an avascularized tissue able to sustain the cyclic motion of the joint. It presents one cell type, the chondrocyte, and has a very peculiar architecture which helps in its shock-adsorption function [64]. Cartilage is one of the most investigated tissues in respect to OA also when looking at OOC technologies (Table 2). The first known work on a “cartilage-on-chip” model was of Donghee Lee et al., in 2018. Here, the technology allows cyclic mechanical stimulation on 3D hydrogels containing chondrocytes. The model allowed different degrees of stimulation using elastic membrane that could be deflected using a pressure pump. The interesting aspect was the size of the system, making the single units smaller than the size of a coin. Current overview of cartilage-on-chip technologies.

The second well-known example is from Occhetta et al. The cartilage-on-chip model applied mechanical stimulation forces in the same fashion as Lee et al. In their study they managed to apply both physiological and hyper-physiological forces to cells and recreating inflammatory stimuli similar to what could be seen in nature [66]. One of the major drawbacks of this technology was the number of cells seeded in the model which does not represent the cell density of a physiological knee but rather a pre-natal chondrogenic condition. Nonetheless, the technology showed the clear potential of OOC technologies in the context of OA.

Cartilage in the joint experiences both compression and shear stress. Hence, Rosser et al. focused on the shear stress exerted on chondrocytes on a 3D structure. Here, to validate their model, they used equine chondrocytes and could influence the expression of key cartilaginous markers like Sox9, aggrecan and collagen II [67]. Interestingly, they focused on parallelization of the technology and the potential user friendliness of the system. As compressive forces are missing in the model it is difficult to assess whether the technology could fully replicate human physiology. However, this technology is one of the first examples of parallelization and user-friendliness present in literature. Moreover, its complexity is more representative than the standard 2D or 3D models currently adopted.

Paggi et al. developed a cartilage-on-chip technology presenting a mechanical actuation model able to recapitulate both compression and shear strain [68,69]. The model allowed real time visualization of the 3D construct containing human chondrocytes. The model is the first of its kind in terms of multi-directional stimulation and it has shown clear advantages in combining both compression and shear strain with higher extracellular matrix production both for aggrecan and collagen II. The largest drawback of the technology is the complexity and the parallelization. This makes the advanced in vitro device difficult to translate in standard laboratory settings. Nonetheless, the model is the closest in terms of biomechanical stimulation and provides a clear explanation on the importance of forces applied onto human chondrocytes.

Hao et al. developed a magneto-mechanical stimulation hydrogel for a cartilage-on-chip model. Here, the authors investigated the use of magneto-responsive (NdFeB)/Poly-GelMA-HAMA hydrogel films to modulate strains onto C28/I2 cells [70]. Here, they successfully generated different strain levels and frequency replicating both physiological and hyper-physiological stimulation. By modulating increasing strain level between 20 and 30 ​% they showed up-regulations of characteristic osteoarthritic markers COL I, -X, MMP-13, ADAMTS-5. This work provides a good example of how combination of different areas, hydrogel development and microfluidics, are providing to investigators additional tools to better represent human physiology in vitro. The model is still in its infancy has no human primary cells were used to assess whether similar responses could be reached.

Another exquisite example of mechanically stimulated cartilage-on-chip platform is the platform from Qin et al. [71]. This study presents the first integration of flexible, stretchable electrochemical sensor within a cartilage-on-chip platform. This sensor allows for real time monitoring of Nitric oxide released by chondrocytes. This technology advancement provides insights that cannot be modeled with current in vitro or in vivo models providing a completely new way of assessing biological response. Nonetheless, this technology is difficult to produce and scalability for potential large-scale manufacturing may be challenging.

Zhao et al. developed a cartilage-on-chip technology focused on rapid drug assessment using perfusion systems showing drug potency in alleviation of OA [72]. Here, mouse chondrocytes loaded with GelMA on a micro-well adjacent to a medium channel connected to a peristaltic pump. The interesting aspect, aside the possibility to do pharmaco-response, it the possibility to fully automate the process, reducing the manual operator steps. Disadvantages of the model are the lack of active mechanical stimulation which was reported being a key aspect in the matrix or disease progression as well as the use of mouse instead of human cells. Nonetheless, the technology is an exquisite example of automation and how parallelization can be re-created for drug-screening assessment.

Upadhyay et al. used a bioink and composite hydrogel together with a microfluidic device to induce chondrogenesis in vitro combining the effect of the matrix with shear pressure forces [73]. The combination of MSCs with dECM-enriched hydrogels under microfluidic shear stress led to upregulated expression of cartilage-specific markers such as collagen type II, aggrecan, and laminin suggesting chondrogenesis. This tool allows again automation and possibility to assess therapeutic efficacy. However, the model is quite complex to fabricate and requires highly trained personnel due to the specialized hydrogel.

Overall, the results obtained by the different groups across the world showed how OOC technologies could be the game changers in replicating specific aspects of cartilage physiology. Moreover, these models are progressively leading towards an advanced in vitro automation which could lead to more accurate and reproducible results.

Synovial membrane-on-chip

The synovial membrane is one of the most complex tissues within the joint. It retains synovial fluid while providing immune-modulatory capacity to the joint. It comprises of two different layers, a synovial lining layer and a deeper subintimal layer [74]. As it is one of the key regulators in joint homeostasis, synovial membrane-on-chip technologies have been developed to better assess the function of this tissue (Table 3). Thompson et al. created a vascularized and mechanically stimulated synovium-of-chip system to investigate synovial inflammation and monocyte recruitment [75]. The work is intriguing as it shows how the impact of different strains on fibroblasts-like synoviocytes affects their structural organization. Moreover, by adding inflammatory cytokines (e.g. IL-1β) it was possible to increase inflammatory markers such as IL-6 or MMP-1. The model was additionally used to assess monocyte migration although the technology was not designed in an accurate way to appropriately mimic a vasculature layer. Hence, although monocytes migration was detected it is questionable whether this could effectively represent the in vivo behavior where monocytes extravasate from a tubular blood vessel. Rothbauer et al. designed a model to mimic inflammatory arthritis using a 3D synovium-on-chip with biosensing. TNF-α addition in the system impacted several prominent cytokines in inflammatory conditions (e.g. Il-6, IL-8, MMP-1, MMP-3) [76]. The 3D environment allowed remodeling of synovial fibroblasts and assessment of their migratory and proliferative nature during inflammatory conditions. Potential follow-up work would require the addition of vasculature and immune system in the model to reach even further complexity allowing proper assessment of disease response. Liu et al. developed a model to assess fibroblasts synoviocites migration in a bone-synovial model. Here, although the model used SW982 cancer cell line it showed that synovial fibroblast migratory behavior was increased when in proximity with osteoclastic RAW264.7 ​cells. Lastly, Lee et al. worked on a synovium-on-a-chip model where they investigated fibroblasts-macrophages crosstalk [77]. The team focused on fibroblast-like synoviocytes and M2c macrophage-like synoviocyte embedded in a custom collagen/alginate hybrid hydrogel to mimic synovial extracellular matrix stiffness. The device was able to replicate the synovial linin layer with specific expressions of key markers like Zo-1, CLDN5, TREM2 and VSI4. Interestingly, the team was also the first to simulate gout on an OOC platform. The platform has the capability of assessing immune-crosstalk, however, it lacks fluid flow, and it showed results from a single macrophage donor which potentially limits the claim performed. Osteochondral-on-chip

The osteochondral tissue consists of articular cartilage and subchondral bone. This composite structure is fundamental in shock adsorption. During OA, this structure is highly impacted and is typically used via histology to assess the stage of the disease in animal models. Due to its relevance, the osteochondral structure has been investigated by several groups around the world (Table 4). Lin et al., in 2014 developed the first stem cell-based microphysiological osteochondral system [79]. The model consisted of a bioreactor presenting two chambers connect by two independent medium stream to provide specific nutrients to the cartilage and the bone compartment respectively. The team successfully differentiated human bone marrow cells with clear tissue-specific gene expression and matrix production. In addition, the study showed that IL-1β treatment of the osseous compartment led to strong catabolic activity. The same team developed a human-induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)- derived osteochondral tissue chip for the assessment of OA [80]. The model comprises iPSC differentiated into mesenchymal progenitor cells with the potential to become chondrocytes or osteoblasts. The cells were encapsulated in photo-crosslinkable gelatin scaffold cultured in a dual flow bioreactor allowing supplement of chondrogenic or osteogenic medium depending on the compartment. The model was used to study inflammation and look at the response of celecoxib, a selective COX-2 inhibitor, to determine therapeutic effects on OA. This was the first model able to recapitulate cartilage and bone in a micro-physiological system. Tuerlings et al. developed a human osteochondral unit-on-a-chip model using human mesenchymal stem cells differentiated in chondrogenic and osteogenic lineages [81]. The microfluidic platform integrated a polycaprolactone matrix where cells were seeded and bone a cartilage tissue formed while nutrients were provided using dynamic flow. Although only a proof-of-concept, the models was able to resemble some of the characteristics of OA-like conditions using a thyroid hormone treatment on the platform. As for other models, the main limitations were the complexity of the system due to the use of highly specific hydrogel and the relatively short culture conditions. Nonetheless, the technology showed high potential for future drug efficacy studies.

Another interesting osteochondral-on-chip model is the one of Mainardi et al. [82]. The team already developed a cartilage-on-chip model and in 2023 published a work where, with a similar geometry as their previous technology, they created cartilage and mineralized bone tissue. The bi-compartment structure was stimulated by tissue-specific compression to mimic the physiological and hyper-physiological behaviour present in vivo. Here, up-regulation of inflammatory markers (e.g. IL-8, MMP-13) was observed when hyper-physiological loading was applied to the device followed by increased calcium deposition providing additional insights in OA-disease mechanisms. Further steps of this platform would require the addition of vasculature and immune cells to further capture the complexity of OA. Ong et al. Developed a microfluidic co-culture platform with spatially patterned granular hydrogels containing oxygen scavenger to create localized hypoxia using a combination of primary human chondrocytes and osteoblasts [83]. Chondrocytes in the hypoxic environment showed elevated expression of HIF-1α and increased collagen type 2. The platform is simple allowing for scalability as well as modularity which provides a great advantage in terms of increase in complexity. Moreover, the bone-cartilage interface can be used to mimic disease-relevant crosstalk mechanisms observed in OA. Nonetheless, the platform lacks dynamic loading which, as previously discussed, is key for extracellular matrix production of chondrocytes.

Lastly, Conceição et al. established a sex-stratified osteochondral-on-chip platform to assess gender specific responses towards inflammatory stimuli [84]. The microfluidic system presented male and female OA cartilage donors together with non-OA osteoblasts into a GelMA hydrogel. Continuous flow was applied to the system which allowed nutrient refreshment as well as triggering of an inflammatory stimulation pattern using IL-1β and TNF-α. The team discovered that female chondrocytes are prone to generate more collagen I respect to male under inflammation. Interestingly, they also assess matrix stiffness using nano-indentation technologies which enabled assessment of the 3D construct before and after stimulation. Although having some limitations, such as lack of compressive or shear strain forces, the model bring the scientific community closer to capturing this tissue-tissue interaction. Overall, the technologies that are being developed in the osteochondral space may lead to finally capture key aspects of OA that could not be studied with previous in vivo models. Conceição et al. work showed how important is to consider gender to make better assessment of therapeutic responses.

Tendon-on-chip

Tendons are fibrous cords of tissue connecting the muscles to bones facilitating the movement of joints. They are force-transmitting structures subject to tensile, compressive and shear forces particularly in proximity to the bone insertions. In the fibrous structure tenocytes, tendon stem cells and immune cells balance repair and inflammation. The first development of a human-based tendon-on-chip model was from a team of University of Rochester with James McGrath as lead scientist [85] (Table 5). The tendon-on-chip was developed to assess complex cellular interactions leading to tendon fibrosis. The model presented a vascular and a tissue compartment and it was designed for cellular crosstalk and paracrine signaling. Interestingly, the technology allowed for mimicry of specific features of tendon fibrosis such as myofibroblast differentiation, tissue contraction, fibrotic extracellular matrix deposition and inflammation. However, the model currently operates in static condition while tendons are in a highly dynamic environment. Moreover, the limited complexity may hamper the appropriate data interpretation which is further affected by potential donor variability. Nonetheless, Award et al. are the first to introduce a first tendon-on-chip model paving the way to further OOC developments on this important tissue. Bakht t al. developed an organotypic 3D tendon-on-chip to study bioactive hydrogel matrix with biophysical magnetic fibers to have precise fiber alignment in the microfluidic device [86]. Here, tendon-derived cells, in the core compartment, were co-cultured with endothelial cells. The objective was to investigate spatiotemporal dynamics between the tendon-resident cells in combination with immune cells and assess their interaction upon inflammation. The model showed clear cell-cell interaction with T cell recruitment and upregulation of typical inflammatory markers when the IL-1β is added to the system. Major drawback of the system is the length of the study and the lack of active mechanical stimuli. Ligament-on-chip

The knee joint comprises four primary ligaments with the primary role of stabilizing and helping in motion guidance. Among the four, the anterior cruciate ligament is the most investigated as is the one the most ruptured and can cause, in decades, OA. This tissue comprises a dense regular connective tissue rich in collagen I fibers with ligament fibroblasts, endothelial and sensory nerve cells. The only paper currently investigating the ligament is focused on mimicking the periodontal ligament present on the tooth. Svanberg et al. established a ligament-on-chip model integrating patient-derived periodontal ligament cells and endothelial cells in a fibrin hydrogel scaffold [87] (Table 5). The OOC mimicked vascularization as well as simulating the inflammatory conditions by introducing lipopolysaccharides. As for other technologies, it allows for flow, providing assessment of cell-cell interaction in dynamic conditions. While this ligament-on-chip provides a first of its kind platform it still suffers from long-term culture and scalability, but it can be used as a feasibility assessment tool for potentially developing an OOC mimicking the anterior cruciate ligament. Initial attempts were made by University of Twente where fluid flow shear stress was assessed on the alignment of PDL-hTERT a cell line (https://essay.utwente.nl/96400/). For the mimicry of an accurate ligament-on-chip model particular focus should be considered in mimicking the 3D structure, with the fibre alignment, the cells used, and the type of stimulation applied.

Hoffa fat pad-on-chip

The Hoffa's fat pad, also known as the infrapatellar fat pad, is a soft adipose tissue in the joint having an important role in joint homeostasis and recently acquiring major interest due to its immunological characteristics [88]. It is a highly vascularized and innervated tissue presenting among others, adipocytes, fibroblasts, macrophages, endothelial cells and nerve fibers. Currently, there is a team of the university of Twente working on a Hoffa's fat pad model (https://www.utwente.nl/en/tnw/dbe/education/assignments_2024-1/development-of-a-fat-pad-on-chip.pdf) while others may be in the development phase. Adipose-based OOC devices have been already investigated and could be a starting point for researchers wanting to develop their own fat pad-on-chip models (Table 5). These models are primarily focused on assessing obesity and immune-inflammation [89]. Liu et al. Investigated the interaction of human adipocytes and immune cells (specifically U937 monocytes) in the context of obesity-induced inflammation. The advantages of the platform were as previously reported, the dynamic flow which provided continuous nutrient supply to the system and the immune-metabolic interaction where adipose exhibited signs of insulin resistance when co-cultured. The drawbacks are similar to previously discussed technologies such as the choice of a cell-line, the scalability and the still oversimplification of the tissue [89]. Rogal et al. developed a white Adipose Tissue-on-Chip integrating all major cellular components including mature adipocytes, organotypic endothelial barriers, stromovascular cells including adipose tissue macrophages [90]. The platform used autologous cells and replicated key adipose tissue functions such as lipid storage and endocrine activities like adipokine secretion. The platform had high complexity level which typically hamper scalability, but it provides a good representation of how complex an infrapatellar model could be developed. Huff et al. Established a hyaluronic acid (HA)-based hydrogel to encapsulate human adipose-derived stem cells [91]. The team successfully differentiated the stem cells into quasi-like native adipose tissue showing lipid accumulation and expression of adipogenic markers. The model was developed for high reproducibility and non-invasive monitoring as a tool for drug screening platform. Also, this model includes limitations such as donor variability, over-simplified architecture and potentially long-term stability but it provides an idea on the possibility of scalable solutions. These example in the literature can function as base for developing a model that could recapitulate the key characteristics of the infrapatellar fat pad.

Meniscus-on-chip

The meniscus is a crescent-shaped fibrocartilaginous structure located in the knee joint. The knee has two menisci, the medial and lateral which function as stabilizer, shock and load absorber [92]. The structure comprises two zones. A vascular zone, defined as red zone and an avascular zone (white zone). There are different cell populations where fibroblast-like and chondrocyte-like are the most prominent [56]. To our knowledge there are currently no meniscus-on-chip models. Nonetheless, the technologies previously described could function as steppingstone for the establishment of a model considering parameters such as vascularization, hydrogel stiffness and mechanical stimuli.

Multi-tissue models

One of the first models integrating multi-tissue function was developed by Mondadori et al. The model includes a series of 3D chambers each presenting different cell types: synovial fibroblasts, endothelial cells, articular chondrocytes and monocytes. The model was able to successfully capture the extravasation of monocytes upon inflammatory stimulation or OA fluid. Moreover, dual blockades of CCR2 and CCR5 (using Cenicriviroc) more effectively suppressed monocyte extravasation in response to OA synovial fluid, compared with CCR2-only blockade (RS-504393). This study showed the direct implication in OA synovial fluid in promoting extravasation. Although simplistic, as no active mechanical stimulation was applied directly to the synovial membrane and cartilage compartments, it provided a first overview of how immune cells respond to a complex environment [93]. A second model from Rothbauer et al. combined cartilage and a synovial membrane cell-hydrogels communicating via a perfusion channel. The model allows for assessment of inflammatory response of the two tissues in the presence of shear stress [94]. The model is simple but effective although lacking an immune response as well as other biomechanical stimulation aspects that are key for the mimicry of physiological and hyper-physiological stimuli. Here, coculture with synovial organoids significantly enhanced cartilage phenotype: chondral organoids displayed more mature architecture and elevated production of cartilage markers compared to chondral monocultures. Moreover, cytokine profiling revealed a distinct differential secretory response between co-culture and monocultures underscoring the importance of bidirectional signaling of synovial and cartilage tissues. Lastly the group of Lin Hang has developed a model able to recapitulate different tissues of the joint using also cell differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells. The technology is unique as it allows tissue-tissue communication with two different mediums, one specific for the tissue and the second generic for all the units. Here, tissue-specific perfusion streams allowed maintenance of phenotype for each tissue while a shared “synovial fluid” channel enabled tissue-tissue communication. Upon il-1β stimulation, the model recapitulated inflammation-driven changes observed in human osteoarthritis. Lastly, the platform successfully evaluated therapeutic responses to naproxen and four other disease-modifying osteoarthritic drug candidates showing attenuation of inflammation and cartilage breakdown. The major drawback of the technology is the use of stem cells making it challenging to assess the maturity of the constructs. Moreover, the platform does not present application of mechanical forces which limit partially the reliability of the results [95,96]. Nonetheless, this is one of the most elegant technologies which offer multi-tissue communication.

Challenges in the OOC devices

The major challenge of OOC technologies is the capability of fully developing a mature tissue that presents all the characteristics within the human tissue (Table 6). This is mostly due to the lack of proper understanding of human physiological parameters and the immature engineering and biological tools currently being used. The reduction in the number of cells and nutrients may affect the potential readouts. As the number of cells is low it could lead to a lack of sufficient proteins or molecules to be detected. One of the approaches would be as per Occhetta et al. to increase by 20 folds the number of cells which could lead to an improper characterization of the biological response [66]. Hence, identifying the appropriate tradeoff between the accuracy of cell percentage in the model in respect to human physiology is key for the success of OOC technologies.

The standard material (PDMS) that is currently adopted allows for visualization and gas permeability, but it also absorbs hydrophobic molecules making it less ideal for these types of substances [98]. Nonetheless, current surface treatments are being implemented which reduce these aspects [99] and other materials are being studied as alternative [37].

One of the major challenges of multi-tissue or multi-organ systems is to have a medium composition that allows maintenance of the appropriate phenotypic behaviors of each of the tissues currently addressed. Growth factors that could be used to enhance vasculature growth in the scaffold could potentially trigger an immune reaction and a shift to M1 state for macrophages. In the ideal scenario, a synthetic synovial fluid should be created able to address the needs of each of the tissues studied. Moreover, it has been shown that other organs, like the gut, can affect joint homeostasis suggesting the importance of organ-to-organ communication [100]. Hence, once fully established, the supposed joint-on-chip should be combined with other organs to mimic a full body-on-chip. Studies in these aspects are currently being performed for other tissues [101].

Lastly, some of these models, developed from academia, are still quite complex to use, which brings challenges for broader adoption. However, since the establishment of companies and the development of standardized guidelines, the OOC commercially available are becoming more user-friendly [102].

Opportunities – what can we expect?

OOC technologies are poised to become the next-generation standard for in vitro platforms, owing to their precise control over the cellular microenvironment and their tunable complexity. As previously presented, these platforms offer a unique approach to tackle specific research questions and have the potential to be the replacement of animals in efficacy and potency assessment.

One of the major advantages of these devices is the possibility to adopt only human samples and assess how these respond to a series of different biological stimuli. In the context of clinical validation this is a unique opportunity as it offers the pharmaceutical organizations a tool for potential pre-screening of patient cohorts [103]. The identification of responders and non-responders could provide a unique advantage for companies wanting to bring therapies rapidly to the market. Moreover, it is widely known that the current standard mouse models are primarily male and this create a huge gap in the appropriate assessment of pharmaco-response. Interestingly, OA presents higher number of cases in the female population making the current available pre-clinical models inappropriate. OOCs can be used for both genders, and it has been shown to identify differences in patient response to therapy providing better insights on the disease heterogeneity [104].

These miniaturized devices use a limited number of cells which allow for parallelization and evaluation of different parameters for the same patient giving a clearer overview of the biological response. Moreover, due to their size, it is possible to run cohorts of patients simultaneously and obtain initial results in a few weeks from the initiation of the assay allowing rapid assessment of drug response potentially reducing costs. Identification of inefficacy of drugs in the early pre-clinical stages can be key in preventing the initiation of expensive clinical trials. An extensive pre-clinical validation with OOCs could provide the resources for potential optimization of the drug and the dosing to be provided to patients [20].

As organ-on-chip technologies advance in complexity and scalability, companies in the field increasingly envision their use in personalized medicine. Current treatments are provided with no specific discrimination which often leads to adverse events or lack of response. Here, a pre-screening of the potential treatments could be performed on personalized organ-on-chip device to assess the optimal therapy and drug concentration a the single patient level [105].

Many biological processes presumed to occur in the human body or in animal models are inferred from assumptions regarding underlying molecular mechanisms and the intricacies of cell–cell and tissue–tissue interactions. Fundamentally, current in vivo models function as biological black boxes, wherein experimental inputs are applied and outcomes are observed, often without full insight into the intermediate mechanistic processes. OOCs open the “black box” allowing for clear step by step process of understanding. This gives a better overview of the biological and molecular mechanisms.

Lastly, these devices are animal-free allowing for better representation of human physiology while providing an ethical alternative to the use of animals that will be sacrificed. Although potentially not a major concern for some readers, in Europe alone, the number of certified animals’ experiment in 2019 was more than 10.5 million (https://en.3rcenter.dk/fileadmin/user_upload/Editor/images/Om_Danmarks_3R-Center/Symposium/Symposium_2022/CFE_Danish_3Rs_abstract_Sep_2022.pdf). This is a staggering number which is aggravated by the lack of appropriate translatability of these models.

Conclusion

OA remains one of the most prevalent and debilitating disease worldwide yet progress in developing effective therapies has been hindered by limitations in current pre-clinical models. Traditional in vitro and in vivo systems fail to capture the multifactorial complexity of OA, contributing to the translational gap between promising preclinical findings and clinical outcomes. OOC technologies offer a compelling alternative by enabling the recreation of human tissue microenvironments under controlled, physiologically relevant conditions. As demonstrated by the expanding body of cartilage-on-chip, synovial membrane-on-chip, and osteochondral models, OOCs are uniquely positioned to probe specific disease mechanisms, assess therapeutic responses, and reduce reliance on animal testing. Additionally, the technology offers patient cohort screening for a specific compound and are the first step towards personalized medicine.

Despite their promise, OOC platforms still face challenges related to standardization, scalability, biological relevance, and regulatory integration. However, efforts across academia, industry, and regulatory bodies are converging to address these limitations and foster widespread adoption. With continued innovation in materials, microfabrication, and integrated sensing, OOCs have the potential to transform preclinical research, enhance drug discovery pipelines, and enable personalized approaches to OA treatment. As the field progresses, the implementation of OOC technologies may become not only a scientific imperative but also a regulatory and ethical one—shaping the future of disease modelling and therapeutic development.
