Evaluation of the productivity of maize (ATP) under different sowing dates in the western highlands of Cameroon
Authors: Boris Junior Tsafack, Honoré Beyegué Njonko, Lucas Kengni, Simplice CHE. Neba, Brice Tene Fongang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study aims to assess the effects of three sowing dates on the growth and yield of maize. The trial was conducted at the Teaching and Research Farm of the University of Dschang in Bansoa (Penka-Michel) over two growth cycles: 2020 and 2021. The experiment was arranged in a randomized complete block design. Treatments, replicated three times, included a control (T) and three levels of fertilizer input: mineral fertilizer (E), organic fertilizer (F), and organo-mineral fertilizer (EF). The maize variety used was the Acid Tolerable Population (ATP). Sowing occurred sequentially on March 30, April 10, and April 20. Daily rainfall data were collected using a graduated cylinder rain gauge. Observations focused on growth and yield parameters at different sowing dates. The results obtained indicate that maize responded significantly to different sowing dates (p < 0.05). Fertilizer application also positively influenced growth compared to the control. The tallest plants were observed in the F treatment sown during the first date (300 cm). Similarly, the collar diameter followed the same trend, with the F treatment measuring 3.43 cm compared to 2.14 cm for the control, indicating significant differences. Yield results showed the first sowing date > second date > third date. The highest yield was achieved with the EF treatment during the first period (6985 kg/ha), compared to the control during the third period (2009 kg/ha). Within each period, the yield results followed the order: EF > E > F > T. Organic fertilizers alone also produced favorable results compared to the control. Given the current climatic conditions and the results obtained, maize sowing conducted in the first sowing period (March 30) with organo-mineral fertilizer would likely result in optimal grain yield.

Introduction

In sub-Saharan Africa, agriculture plays a leading social and economic role [1]. For this reason, the need to increase agricultural production to supply the growing demand of its population is one of its greatest challenges [2]. This part of the world is a sector where the demographic transition is long overdue by 2050, such as the global one where between 8 and 11 billion inhabitants are projected to be fed [3]. In this zone, soils undergo the degradation of the physico-chemical properties because of the aggressive character of the climate of the region. According to Ref. [4], these soils lose more of their qualities and become infertile. In such soils, agricultural production is low enough, especially for cereals such as maize, to meet with the food needs of an ever-growing population [5]. Corn (zea mays L.) is an annual plant of the Poaceae family. It is native to Central America [6] (GNIS, 2012). This plant is widely cultivated today in temperate and tropical zones for its seeds with high nutritional potential [7]. It is the main food crop as well as the main cereal in Cameroon [8]. With a high cultivation frequency in Western Cameroon, it is cultivated by 42.7 % of Cameroonian farming households [9]. According to Ref. [10], maize yield in Cameroon is around 2.5 t/ha in semi-intensive cultivation and 4.5 t/ha in large-scale farming. In order to compensate for low yields and increase the productivity of maize farms, improved seed varieties are offered by the Institute of Agronomic Research for Development (IRAD). For this purpose, it also offers technical production sheets adapted to each agro-ecological zone. In Cameroon, maize is the most consumed product by a large part of the population [11,12]. According to the [9], the West Cameroon region alone accounts for nearly 60 % of national production. In addition, the district of Penka-Michel, occupies the first rank with an annual production, around 33 % of the production of the Menoua Division [13]. However, obtaining an optimal yield requires the control of several factors: the favorable sowing period, the nature of the soil, and the state of soil fertility.

The climatic disturbances observed in recent years have prompted a revision of the agricultural calendar and adaptation to new environmental conditions. This is essential for profitable and sustainable agriculture. Over 38 correlated years in the West region of Cameroon, the evolution of maize grain yields is contradictory to the general evolution of rainfall [14]. Studies conducted in the Penka-Michel district show that the start and end dates of the agronomic season are variable [15]. Indeed, according to the same author, Penka-Michel, like other localities in Cameroon, has a very variable climate. This is observed by alternating years of low, average and high rainfall. However, like other cereals, maize cultivation is very sensitive to variations in rainfall and temperature [16]. It should be noted that corn is a plant that requires a certain amount of water for its growth. Indeed, its water needs increase with the length of the cycle and the type of climate. According to Ref. [17], the yield of maize depends on the availability of water resources during the critical phase of its development. However, regular dry spells occurring during the crop development cycle cause the main food crops to decline [18]. These anomalies can result in declines in maize grain yield of more than 60 % [19]. Penka-Michel is one of the major maize producing areas in the western highlands of Cameroon. Farmers there practice maize cultivation without taking into account the effect of sowing date on grain yield. Hence this study conducted in this area during 2020 and 2021 (March to September) to evaluate the productivity of the Acid Tolerable Population (ATP) variety of maize when sown on different dates in the Western Highlands of Cameroon. Specifically, the study aims to determine how varying sowing dates influence the growth parameters and yield outcomes of maize, with the ultimate goal of identifying the optimal sowing period for maximizing maize productivity in this region.

Materials and methods

Study site

The study was conducted at the Teaching and Research Farm of the University of Dschang in Bansoa during two growth cycle: 2020 and 2021 (March to July), with geographical coordinates: 05°26′39.4″ N, 10°16′02.1″ W and 1415 m. The soils of this locality are well differentiated and moderately desaturated on volcanic rocks. They are low in humus on the surface, with a very thick B oxic horizon, most often indurated [20]. The climate is humid tropical, western mountain type [21,22]. The average annual rainfall is 1796 mm, with an average annual temperature of 21.4 °C.

Plant material

The maize variety used, usually cultivated in the region is Acid Tolerable Population (ATP). It comes from the Institute of Agronomic Research for Development (IRAD) of Foumbot. This variety has a crop cycle varying between 110 and 130 days with a grain yield varying from 5 to 7 t/ha. The organic manure used was fowl droppings from a poultry farm and the mineral fertilizer was NPK (20-10-10).

Setting up the trial

The experimental design comprised randomized complete blocks with three replicates and four treatments. Each experimental unit consisted of an area of 25 m2, separated by a distance of 1 m. The treatments were as follows: E: mineral manure, F: organic manure, E F: organo-mineral manure, T: control (without manure). This system was reiterated according to the different sowing dates (March 30, April 10, and April 20) for the years 2020 and 2021. The unit plots consisted of 6 rows, each 5 m in length and spaced 0.8 m apart. The seeds of each accession were sown at a depth of 3 cm with a spacing of 0.5 m between the hills. The effective plots were represented by the 8 central plants of the 2 central rows. Two weeks after sowing, the maize plants were thinned to 2 plants per hill to achieve a density of 48.000 plants per hectare.

The first dose of organic manure was incorporated into the soil for plots F and E F at sowing on March 30 (2.5 t/ha). The second dose of organic manure (2.5 t/ha) was applied only to the F plots 45 days after sowing. The dose of mineral manure was applied 45 days after sowing (300 kg/ha) to plot E as well, and 200 kg/ha to plots E F. Rainfall data were collected daily using a graduated cylinder rain gauge (direct reading) installed on the experimental site.

The percentage on the NPK fertilizer bag indicates the quantity of N, P, and K in 100 kg, thus in 2 bags of 50 kg each. For a bag marked NPK 20-10-10, there are 20 kg of nitrogen, 10 kg of phosphorus, and 10 kg of potassium in 100 kg of this fertilizer, along with 60 kg of inert matter. For a dose of 300 kg/ha of mineral manure (NPK 20-10-10), there is a quantity of 60 kg of nitrogen, 30 kg of phosphorus, and 30 kg of potassium, with 140 kg of inert material. This equates to 0.15 kg of nitrogen, 0.075 kg of phosphorus, and 0.075 kg of potassium for each experimental plot of 25 m2. For a dose of 200 kg/ha of mineral manure (NPK 20-10-10), there is a quantity of 40 kg of nitrogen, 20 kg of phosphorus, and 20 kg of potassium, with 120 kg of inert material. This equates to 0.1 kg of nitrogen, 0.05 kg of phosphorus, and 0.05 kg of potassium for each experimental plot of 25 m2.

Measurement of growth and yield parameters

From the fifth week after sowing, growth variables were measured on the eight central plants of each experimental unit every seven days until complete female flowering. These are the plant size, the number of sheets and the collar diameter. At maturity, samples of eight plants from each experimental unit were collected. They were then deseeded and weighed, then dried until a moisture content of less than 20 % was obtained. The grain yield of each treatment was obtained in ton/hectare according to Ref. [23].

Statistical analysis

The data collected were entered and processed using Microsoft Office Excel 2013 software. They were then subjected to analysis of variance at the 5 % probability threshold using R version 4.0.3 software. Means were compared a posteriori using the least significant difference test.

Plant size

It appears that the tendency of the curves observed for the number of leaves is confirmed with regard to the sizes produced during vegetative growth (Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6). The application of fertilizing materials in the plots demonstrated a gain in growth size throughout the duration of the experiment compared to the control group. Indeed, the E, F, and EF fertilizers similarly increased the size of maize during growth, and significant differences were observed compared to the control (p < 0.05) for all time periods. On the 30th day of growth, treatments E, F, and EF presented plants of approximately 64 cm, while the lowest value was recorded with the control treatment (approximately 37 cm). On the 90th day, treatments E, F, and EF presented plants of around 300, 280, and 250 cm for the first, second and third sowing period respectively. Collar diameter

From Fig. 7, Fig. 8, Fig. 9, which display the collar diameters of maize plants from 30 to 90 days of growth, it can be observed that the trend noted for maize height is reaffirmed. On the 30th day of growth, the control yielded a collar diameter of 1.33 cm compared to 1.73 cm for treatment F. Treatments E F and F did not show a significant difference at the 5 % probability threshold regarding their diameters. On the 90th day of growth, across all periodss, treatments E F and F demonstrated a significant difference compared to E, and a very significant difference compared to the control. The control recorded collar diameters of 2.35, 2.14, and 2.41 cm for the first, second and third sowing period, respectively, while treatment F achieved the highest root collar diameter in the first sowing period (3.43 cm). It is noted that the organic treatment (F), organo-mineral treatment (E F), and mineral treatment (E), each applied individually in this study, revealed significant differences (p < 0.05) for this parameter from the 60th day until the end of the observations. Yield parameters

The net maize yield under the influence of the applied fertilizers is illustrated in Fig. 10. It is evident that the application of the E F treatment resulted in a higher yield (6985 kg/ha) compared to the other treatments. Conversely, the control yielded the lowest (2009 kg/ha). However, all treatments were significantly (p < 0.05) superior to the control. The application of organic and mineral amendments, as well as the organo-mineral combination, appears to enhance nutrient availability in the soil more effectively than untreated soil. The mineral supplement played a crucial role in enhancing the productivity of soil enriched with organic matter. The yields from the first sowing period were significantly (p < 0.05) higher than those from the second sowing period, which in turn were significantly higher than those from the third sowing period.Fig. 10Three-sowing date yield.E = mineral fertilizer, F = organic fertilizer, E F = organo-mineral, T = control.Fig. 10

Discussion

The results of daily rainfall data indicate the occurrence of 65 rainy days between March and June. This number of days is adequate to satisfy the water requirements of maize, as [24] indicates that the average water needs of maize in the sub-Saharan zone range between 60 and 90 days per crop cycle. The analysis of variance regarding the influence of sowing dates on maize grain yield revealed a significant difference (p < 0.05) among the three sowing dates. These findings are consistent with those of [25]. Maize cultivation in Penka-Michel occurs in two cycles [15]: the first, wetter cycle from March to June with an average of 593.3 mm of rainfall; and the second, less wet cycle from September to December with an average of 509.48 mm of rainfall. In tropical and intertropical zones, maize's water requirements range from 500 to 800 mm [26,27]. Thus, the recorded precipitation volumes for both cycles are sufficient to meet the water needs of maize in Penka-Michel [15] safely. These water volumes significantly increase soil water content through infiltration. The entry of rainfall into the soil surface is governed by the intensity and nature of the precipitation and surface conditions [28]. The role of rain energy in infiltration is evident during the impact of raindrops on the ground. This impact energy pulverizes soil clods, creating a surface layer of very fine soil particles with low permeability (battance). The soil crust indices of all the plots are below 1.4. Therefore, the soil surfaces in the study area have a high capacity to allow water passage during rainfall, as these values correspond to non-crusting soils [29]. After penetrating the soil surface, water transmission into the soil layers is governed by Darcy's law, considering the thickness of the layers and their hydraulic conductivity. These parameters are controlled by soil structure and its structural stability. The soils in this study exhibit a granular structure, imparting a fairly permeable nature that facilitates easy water movement. The structural stability index exceeds 9 for all soils, corresponding to a stable structure according to the scale of [29]. This good structure and stability promote better hydraulic conductivity and, consequently, better infiltration capacity.

The period of high sensitivity for maize is between 20 and 30 days before flowering and ends around the 10th to 15th day after flowering. A water deficit during this stage can lead to a yield reduction of over 60 % [17]. The most detrimental rainfall risks for maize cultivation are identified by characterizing the variability of rainfall and the number of rainy days on a fine (daily) scale. All experimental units across the different sowing period and treatments received identical manure doses. The dry spells recorded during the flowering of plants in the second sowing period (a 5-day dry spell) and the third sowing period (a 13-day dry spell) likely contributed to the observed decline in maize grain yields from the first to the third sowing period [[30], [31], [32], [33]]. explain that the low yield for late sowing is attributed to a cooler climate, the aggressiveness of rain, and the timing of harvest. Additionally, late sowing is another cause of reduced yield due to the high probability of water stress during the critical grain-filling phase. Similarly, late sowing often results in stunted and underdeveloped plants, thereby affecting their productivity. These late sowings, as also demonstrated in studies by Refs. [34,35], typically coincide with periods of high transpiration by the plant, which negatively impacts grain production. This study also found that maize grains harvested in the first sowing period were of very high quality. This finding aligns with the work of [36,37], which showed that, in most cases, cereals produce higher yields with early sowing.

Conclusion

The objective of this study was to determine the effect of sowing date on the growth and grain yield of maize in Penka-Michel. To achieve this, rainfall data were collected using a graduated cylinder rain gauge installed on site. Analysis of daily rainfall data and agricultural production indicates that the year experiences several dry phases as well as periodic occurrences of maximum daily rainfall. It was found that sowing performed during the third ten days of March results in the highest number of leaves, plant height, collar diameter, and grain yield. Consequently, delayed sowing reduces maize yield in the Penka-Michel region. Overall, the water requirements of maize are satisfactorily met. Most climatic risks adversely affecting maize cultivation are related to excessive rainfall, water deficits, and the instability of key agricultural season parameters. These findings can serve as a basis for developing strategies for the prevention and management of climatic hazards.
