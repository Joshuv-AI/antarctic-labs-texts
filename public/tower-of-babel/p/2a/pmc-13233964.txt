Reduced catalytic activity of KPC β-lactamase can increase ceftazidime resistance
Authors: André Birgy, Dignite Fabrice Ngango, Murugesan Palaniappan, Shahlo O Solieva, Devin G Kelly, Zhizeng Sun, Gregory Bowman, Timothy Palzkill
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstractβ-Lactamases confer bacterial resistance by hydrolyzing β-lactam antibiotics. Klebsiella pneumoniae carbapenemases (KPCs) are a major cause of resistance to carbapenems, but combinations of β-lactams with β-lactamase inhibitors, such as ceftazidime/avibactam, initially restored activity against these enzymes. However, treatment has driven the emergence of KPC variants that evade inhibition. Here we investigate the evolutionary and mechanistic consequences of three clinically observed substitutions -D179Y, T243M and H274Y- that together convert the broad-spectrum KPC-2 enzyme into a variant specialized for ceftazidime/avibactam resistance. Using steady-state and pre-steady-state kinetic analyses with ceftazidime, cephalothin and imipenem, we show that T243M enhances cephalosporin hydrolysis, whereas H274Y modulates this effect. Introduction of D179Y decreases both kcat and KM and shifts the rate-limiting step of catalysis to deacylation. Together, these mutations act cooperatively, reducing catalytic efficiency while promoting resistance to ceftazidime/avibactam. These findings reveal how epistatic interactions shape the evolution of clinically relevant β-lactamase variants and may help anticipate future resistance trajectories and guide inhibitor design.Subject terms: Antibiotics, Enzyme mechanisms

Introduction

β-lactam antibiotics are among the most widely used agents for the treatment of bacterial infections. Unfortunately, because of their widespread use, bacterial resistance to these drugs now represents a threat to antimicrobial therapy. The major mechanism of resistance to β-lactam antibiotics is the bacterial production of β-lactamases, which hydrolyze the amide bond of the β-lactam ring of these drugs to render them ineffective. In order to counteract β-lactamase-mediated antibiotic resistance, β-lactamase inhibitors have been introduced1.

Klebsiella pneumoniae carbapenemases are class A serine β-lactamases. They have become a common mechanism of carbapenem resistance among Enterobacterales with worldwide diffusion2. The most frequently encountered variants are KPC-2 and KPC-3 (which differs from KPC-2 due to the H274Y mutation)3. Their broad spectrum activity can confer high bacterial resistance to most β-lactams. Since 2015, new inhibitors have been introduced, including avibactam, vaborbactam, and relebactam, that inhibit KPC β-lactamases and show good clinical efficacy when combined with a β-lactam, such as ceftazidime-avibactam1,4. Avibactam is a non-β-lactam, diazabicyclooctane inhibitor of serine β-lactamases. It forms a covalent, but reversible, carbamoyl bond with the catalytic serine5. Subsequent to the introduction of CAZ-AVI, a rapid evolution of the Klebsiella pneumoniae carbapenemase (KPC) has been observed3.

To date, 264 clinical KPC variants have been reported6. Among these, there are 246 KPC variants for which nucleotide sequences are available, and many of them are associated with ceftazidime-avibactam resistance, with some also cross-resistant to cefiderocol3. Substitutions at residue D179, alone or in combination with other substitutions, are the most frequently observed mutations implicated in ceftazidime-avibactam resistance and account for 18% of KPC clinical variants (n = 43). Many substitutions at D179 have been studied in vitro7, but D179Y is the most important clinically and is found in 28 alleles—representing 11% of KPC variants with sequences available. Of these variants, KPC-33 and KPC-31, which are derived from the KPC-2 and KPC-3 β-lactamases by the addition of the D179Y substitution, are among the most frequent and have been extensively studied3,8–11. The T243 residue is also frequently mutated, and these mutations are found in 17 KPC variants (7%), with T243M being found in 4 different variants. Little is known of its impact on enzyme activity. The T243M mutation associated with H274Y (KPC-89) has been reported to confer resistance to ceftazidime-avibactam12,13. Interestingly, the combination of T243M and D179Y (forming KPC-128) and also with the addition of the H274Y mutation (forming KPC-32) have been found in clinical KPC variants and potentiates ceftazidime-avibactam resistance while also decreasing carbapenemase activity12,14.

Globally, mutations involved in ceftazidime-avibactam resistance are distributed along 3 hot-spots represented by loops surrounding the active site of KPC β-lactamase: the Ω-loop (residues 164–179), the 240-loop from residues 237 and 243, and the 270-loop from residues 267–275. The KPC-32 variant combines mutations from these 3 loops (D179Y/T243M/H274Y). Thus, the study of this mutant and of the evolutionary paths from KPC-2 to KPC-32 enables an investigation of the impact of these mutations on the enzyme activity and interactions between these residues (Fig. 1).

Ceftazidime is a 3rd generation cephalosporin and is a bulky substrate that is poorly accommodated by the active site of KPC-215. The addition of H274Y to form KPC-3 has been shown to increase ceftazidime hydrolysis while maintaining good carbapenemase activity and remaining susceptible to inhibition by avibactam15,16.

The resistance to ceftazidime-avibactam exhibited by KPC-33 (D179Y) and KPC-31 (D179Y/H274Y) has been examined through biochemical and structural investigations8,14,17,18. It has been observed that the D179Y mutation in KPC-2 or KPC-3 results in a reduction in kcat and KM with minimal change in the kcat/KM ratio for ceftazidime hydrolysis. The kinetic parameter kcat/KM, called catalytic efficiency, reflects the hydrolysis of substrate under conditions where the substrate concentration is low compared to the KM, a situation that may reflect the specificity of the enzyme in vivo. Using pre-steady state kinetics, we and others showed recently that the hydrolysis mechanism of ceftazidime by the D179Y variants involves the formation of a long-lived covalent intermediate—indicating that the rate-limiting step in the reaction is deacylation, which could be associated with resistance to this drug3,8–11. The apparent increase in ceftazidime affinity (decrease of KM) could be linked to a diminished inhibition effectiveness for avibactam in the KPC-2 background, aligning with the observed resistance to ceftazidime-avibactam17. However, this decrease in inhibition is not observed for the KPC-3 background, suggesting that different mechanisms are at play and that this question is not yet solved14.

Both kcat and KM are macroscopic parameters that are influenced by microscopic rate constants. Additionally, KM is commonly interpreted as a direct measure of the enzyme-substrate affinity, but it is affected by microscopic rate constants, particularly the deacylation rate19,20.

In this study, we investigated how, upon acquiring ceftazidime/avibactam resistance, the enzyme (KPC-2, KPC-3) evolves from a good carbapenemase to one highly specialized in ceftazidime resistance, to the detriment of carbapenemase activity. By performing steady-state, pre-steady state kinetics and study of inhibition potency, we determined the rate constants of β-lactam hydrolysis by β-lactamases, focusing particularly on enzymes with the T243M mutation (KPC-32, KPC-89, KPC-100, and KPC-128) with ceftazidime, cephalothin, and imipenem as substrates. We show that the T243M mutation increases ceftazidime and cephalothin hydrolysis in KPC-2, but its impact is concealed by the presence of H274Y and/or D179Y mutations, suggesting finely tuned interactions between mutations.

Results

Impact of T243M in KPC-2 and KPC-3 (H274Y) backgrounds

We sought to elucidate the mechanism by which the T243M substitution influences enzyme activity and alters antibiotic susceptibility. To this end, we analyzed the catalytic effects of the T243M mutation on KPC-2 and KPC-3 (H274Y) enzyme backgrounds using the substrates ceftazidime, imipenem, and cephalothin. Steady state and pre-steady state enzyme kinetics results for KPC variants

As previously demonstrated by our group and others, accurately determining kcat for ceftazidime with KPC-2 or KPC-3 is challenging due to the high KM value (>400 µM). However, the catalytic efficiencies (kcat/KM), determined by fitting progress curves, were 0.001 µM−1 s−1 for KPC-2 and 0.007 µM−1 s−1 for KPC-311,15,22 (Table 1). The addition of the T243M substitution to KPC-2 (to form KPC-100) resulted in a 15-fold increase in catalytic efficiency for ceftazidime. No significant change was observed when T243M was introduced into KPC-3 (to form KPC-89) (Fig. 1 and Table 1). An increase was also observed with cephalothin as a substrate, where the T243M mutation in KPC-2 (KPC-100) led to a 3-fold increase in catalytic efficiency21. Introducing the T243M mutation in the KPC-3 (H274Y) background (KPC-89) did not significantly affect catalytic efficiency with cephalothin, as observed with ceftazidime. The T243M mutation reduced both kcat and KM for imipenem hydrolysis in KPC-2 and KPC-3 (H274Y). Nevertheless, the catalytic efficiency remained within the same range. Thus, the T243M mutation enhances hydrolysis of ceftazidime and cephalothin in KPC-2 (KPC-100) but not in the KPC-3 (H274Y) background (KPC-89), while having no significant impact on the catalytic efficiency for imipenem hydrolysis.

We next employed pre-steady state kinetic analysis to examine the rates of various steps in the reaction mechanism. If deacylation is the rate-limiting step, we would expect to observe burst kinetics, characterized by two phases: an initial rapid phase corresponding to the formation of the covalent intermediate (acylation), followed by a slower phase associated with the deacylation of the ester intermediate, which corresponds to the steady-state rate23. However, when analyzing KPC-2 T243M (KPC-100) and KPC-3 (H274Y) T243M (KPC-89) mutants with the three substrates—ceftazidime, cephalothin, and imipenem—the progress curves were best described by a single exponential equation. The absence of burst kinetics suggests that deacylation is not the rate-limiting step, implying that either acylation is the rate-limiting or both acylation and deacylation rates are similar (k2 ≤ k3) (Table 1, Figs. 2, S4, S5). The kobs versus substrate concentration plot of KPC-2 T243M (KPC-100) and KPC-3 (H274Y) T243M (KPC-89) with the substrates did not reach a maximum plateau. Consequently, it was not possible to determine either the acylation rate or the KD for substrate binding. Nonetheless, the results indicate the T243M substitution does not alter the rate-limiting step compared to the ancestral alleles (KPC-2) and KPC-3 (H274Y) for the hydrolysis of ceftazidime, cephalothin, and imipenem11.

To assess the resistance levels conferred by the KPC enzymes, we evaluated the minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) for E. coli strains harboring plasmids encoding mutations associated with KPC-2, KPC-3 (H274Y), KPC-89 (T243M/H274Y), and KPC-100 (T243M). MICs were determined for ampicillin, cephalothin, ceftazidime, imipenem, ampicillin/clavulanate, and ceftazidime/avibactam.

The T243M mutation led to an increase in ceftazidime MICs in both the KPC-2 and KPC-3 backgrounds, by 5-fold and 3-fold, respectively, while simultaneously reducing imipenem MICs by 4-fold and 2-fold, respectively. Additionally, T243M reduced the ampicillin/clavulanate MIC by 2-fold in both KPC-2 and KPC-3 (H274Y) and decreased cephalothin MICs specifically in the KPC-3 (H274Y) background. Furthermore, in the KPC-2 background (KPC-100), the T243M mutation had no effect on resistance to ceftazidime-avibactam, whereas in the KPC-3 background (KPC-89), an 8-fold increase in MIC was observed (Table 2).

The rate constant for the carbamylation reaction (k2/Ki) was evaluated based on the inhibition of nitrocefin hydrolysis (200 µM) by avibactam using a two-step reaction model, as previously described5. The reaction was initiated by mixing the enzymes with a buffer containing nitrocefin and varying concentrations of avibactam. Progress curves of nitrocefin hydrolysis were fitted to obtain kobs values at different avibactam concentrations (Eq. 1). Subsequent fitting of these kobs values to Eq. 2 yielded k2/Ki values of 2150 M−1 s−1 for KPC-2 T243M (KPC-100) and 3600 M−1 s−1 for KPC-3 (H274Y) T243M (KPC-89). These values are approximately 2-fold lower than those for KPC-2 and KPC-3, indicating the T243M substitution reduces carbamylation efficiency somewhat (Tables 1, 3).

Using the same experimental setup, we estimated the rates of avibactam decarbamylation (k−2), which corresponds to the reversibility of avibactam carbamylation of serine β-lactamases, leading to the recyclization of the DBO ring in avibactam and the release of the free enzyme (24). The decarbamylation rate of avibactam was approximately 3-fold slower for T243M (KPC-100) compared to KPC-2 and 15-fold faster for T243M H274Y (KPC-89) versus KPC-3 (Table 3), likely contributing to the observed ceftazidime-avibactam MIC.

We also determined the IC50 for clavulanate inhibition and found it to be similar for both KPC-100 and KPC-89 enzyme variants, although approximately 10-fold lower than the values for KPC-2 and KPC-3. These findings are consistent with the MIC for ampicillin-clavulanate for E. coli containing the variants that is lower than that for KPC-2 and KPC-3 (Table 2).

Clavulanate is a covalent inhibitor that forms a bond with the active site serine residue of β-lactamases. We determined k2/Ki for clavulanate using the same experimental approach as for avibactam. The k2/Ki values were similar for KPC-2 T243M (KPC-100) and T243M in the KPC-3 (H274Y) background (KPC-89), indicating comparable clavulanate efficiency between these variants but 50-100 times higher than the values for KPC-2 and KPC-3, indicating the T243M substitution increases the inhibition rate, consistent with the IC50 and MIC results (Tables 2, 3). Additionally, the estimated rates of clavulanate release (k−2), were similar for KPC-2, KPC-3, T243M (KPC-100), and T243M/H274Y (KPC-89) (Table 3).

In summary, these results suggest that the T243M mutation enhances the catalytic efficiency for ceftazidime and cephalothin hydrolysis in KPC-2 and modestly decreases the inhibition potency of avibactam and increases the potency of clavulanate (KPC-100). Conversely, in the KPC-3 (H274Y) background (KPC-89), the same mutation does not alter catalytic efficiency for ceftazidime or cephalothin hydrolysis and modestly decreases the inhibition potency of avibactam while increasing the potency of clavulanate.

Impact of T243M in D179Y and D179Y-H274Y backgrounds

Given that D179Y is another common variant among KPC β-lactamases, we investigated the impact of the T243M mutation on D179Y in both KPC backgrounds (KPC-128 and KPC-32). As previously shown, the addition of the D179Y mutation to KPC-2 (KPC-33) and KPC-3 (KPC-31) greatly reduced both kcat and KM for ceftazidime hydrolysis with little impact on the catalytic efficiency11. Similarly, the addition of the D179Y mutation to T243M (KPC-128) or T243M/H274Y (KPC-32) resulted in a significant decrease in both kcat and KM but the catalytic efficiency remained in the same order of magnitude, albeit somewhat lower for T243M-D179Y (KPC-128) compared to D179Y (KPC-33) (8-fold) and higher for T243M-D179Y-H274Y (KPC-32) compared to D179Y-H274Y (KPC-31) (5-fold) (Figs. 1, S1–S3, Table 1).

The D179Y mutation alone in KPC-2 (KPC-33) or with KPC-3 (H274Y) (KPC-31) had also a major impact on kcat and KM for cephalothin hydrolysis as well as kcat/KM, which decreased 142-fold and 112-fold, respectively. The addition of the D179Y mutation to T243M (KPC-128) or T243M/H274Y (KPC-32) also resulted in a large decrease in kcat and a decrease in KM, which resulted in a large decrease (188- and 325-fold) in the kcat/KM ratio compared to the parent T243M and T243M/H274Y enzymes. Of note, these ratio are in the same order of magnitude as when only the D179Y mutation was present in KPC-2 (KPC-33) or KPC-3 (KPC-31) (Figs. S1–3, Table 1), indicating that the kinetic parameters are governed mainly by the D179Y mutation.

The addition of the D179Y mutation had a large impact on the kcat for imipenem hydrolysis in the D179Y (KPC-33), D179Y/H274Y (KPC-31), D179Y/T243M (KPC-128), and D179Y/T243M/H274Y (KPC-32) mutants, compared to the same alleles without the D179Y mutation. The decrease of kcat was 16,000, 11,000, 5900, and 550-fold, respectively. Whereas, the KM decreased only slightly by 2.7, 2.4-fold for the D179Y (KPC-33) and D179Y/H274Y (KPC-31) and increased by 3-fold in D179Y/T243M (KPC-128) and D179Y/T243M/H274Y (KPC-32) compared to the same alleles without the D179Y mutation. These changes in kcat and KM lead to a drastic reduction in the catalytic efficiency, which was decreased by 5900, 4700, 15,600-fold, and 2200-fold, respectively (Figs. 1, S1-S3, Table 1). Notably, these decreases in catalytic efficiency are attributed to the D179Y mutation, as the presence of T243M does not significantly impact the degree of reduction.

Therefore, the D179Y mutation, regardless of the genetic background in which it is incorporated, greatly decreases kcat, leading to a decrease in catalytic efficiency for all tested substrates and especially for imipenem. The KM was also greatly decreased for ceftazidime and cephalothin, but less for imipenem, explaining the large decrease in kcat/KM for imipenem. In the kinetic model for β-lactamases, a simultaneous decrease in both kcat and KM is consistent with a reduced and rate-limiting deacylation step19. We previously showed using pre-steady state kinetics analysis that the addition of the D179Y substitution to KPC-2 (KPC-33) and KPC-3 (H274Y) (KPC-31), changes the rate-limiting step for the hydrolysis of ceftazidime and cefiderocol from the formation of the covalent intermediate (acylation) to deacylation of the ester intermediate, and that this change impacts resistance levels to ceftazidime and cefiderocol11. Here, we focused on variants containing the T243M mutation and used pre-steady state kinetics with ceftazidime, cephalothin, and imipenem in order to determine the impact of the mutation on the microscopic rate constants contributing to the steady-state kinetic parameters.

The reaction of D179Y/T243M (KPC-128) and D179Y/T243M/H274Y (KPC-32) with ceftazidime, cephalothin, or imipenem as a substrate revealed a burst of acyl-enzyme formation, followed by a slower steady-state rate (Figs. S6–S11). These findings show that deacylation constitutes the rate-limiting step, and thus, k2 > k3 for the 3 substrates. This indicates that the D179Y substitution alters the rate-limiting step from acylation to deacylation when added to the T243M substitution in the wt (KPC-2) and H274Y (KPC-3) backgrounds.

The progress curves could be fitted with the burst equation, giving two important parameters: The deacylation rate (kss = k3) and the kobs. Plotting kobs values obtained with different substrate concentrations and fitting to the equation for a hyperbola provided an estimate of the acylation rate (k2) and the substrate binding constant KD. These analyses performed on D179Y/T243M (KPC-128) and D179Y/T243M/H274Y (KPC-32) reacting with ceftazidime gave similar deacylation rates kss (=k3) of 0.01 s−1, which is close to the kcat values obtained by steady-state kinetics, as expected if deacylation is the rate-limiting step. The estimated acylation rates (k2) were broadly similar for the two enzymes and approximately 1000 to 2000-fold faster than the deacylation rates, consistent with the deacylation reaction being strongly rate-limiting (Table 1). The binding constants KD for ceftazidime were also broadly similar for the D179Y/T243M (KPC-128) and D179Y/T243M/H274Y (KPC-32) enzymes with values of ~500 μM, although the error is high for these estimates (Figs. S6, S9, Table 1). When comparing these parameters with those obtained with D179Y alone in KPC-2 or KPC-3, we observe that the T243M mutation increases the acylation rate for both the D179Y-T243M (KPC-128) and the D179Y-T243M-H274Y (KPC-32) enzymes, does not impact the deacylation rate, and has a modest impact on the KD.

When reacting the D179Y/T243M (KPC-128) and D179Y/T243M/H274Y (KPC-32) enzymes with cephalothin, we obtained deacylation rates (kss) in the same range as the kcat values obtained by steady-state kinetics, as expected for rate-limiting deacylation (Table 1). Plotting kobs values obtained with different cephalothin concentrations against a hyperbola indicated a similar acylation rate s−1 and the KD for both enzymes was approximately 30 µM (Figs. S7, S10, Table 1).

Finally, when reacting imipenem with the D179Y/T243M (KPC-128) and D179Y/T243M/H274Y (KPC-32) enzymes, we obtained similar deacylation rates kss of ~0.005 s−1, which is in the same range as the kcat value obtained by steady-state kinetics (Table 1). The acylation rate and binding constant could not be determined due to the inability to reach saturating imipenem concentrations, although k2 must be greater than 1 s−1 and 1.5 s−1, and KD greater than 250 µM and 300 µM, respectively, for the D179Y/T243M (KPC-128) and D179Y/T243M/H274Y (KPC-32) enzymes (Figs. S8, S11, Table 1).

Taken together, these results suggest that in the T243M backgrounds, the D179Y mutation governs the change in pre-steady state kinetics parameters, with deacylation becoming the rate-limiting step of the reaction. The T243M mutation exhibits little difference on the kinetics between the D179Y and D179Y/H274Y backgrounds.

We then tested the impact of the D179Y mutation on antibiotic resistance levels. As previously shown, it increased the ceftazidime and ceftazidime-avibactam MICs in both the wild-type KPC-2 and KPC-3 backgrounds (respectively 16- and 4-fold for ceftazidime and 5- and 10-fold for ceftazidime-avibactam) and decreased imipenem MICs (16- and 8-fold, respectively) but also ampicillin and cephalothin MICs (Table 2)11. The addition of the T243M substitution to D179Y in the KPC-2 and KPC-3 backgrounds (respectively KPC-128 and KPC-32) did not affect resistance levels for ceftazidime, ampicillin, cephalothin, and imipenem, and moderately increased resistance to ceftazidime-avibactam (Table 2).

Overall, these results suggest that positions 243, 179, and 274, alone or combined, play a role in resistance to ceftazidime and ceftazidime-avibactam. Substitutions at these positions also increase susceptibility to ampicillin, cephalothin, ampicillin/clavulanate, and imipenem, suggesting a trade-off between ceftazidime or ceftazidime/avibactam resistance and resistance to other β-lactams, as already described3,15. We then determined avibactam and clavulanate inhibition potency for these variants to examine the impact of the D179Y mutation. As previously, we used nitrocefin as the reporter substrate. The addition of the D179Y mutation to the T243M (KPC-128) and T243M-H274Y (KPC-32) variants decreased the catalytic efficiency for nitrocefin hydrolysis by 225 and 1560-fold, respectively (Table 1). Although the catalytic efficiencies for nitrocefin are low, they are still higher than for ceftazidime, which justified the choice of this colorimetric substrate as a reporter.

Surprisingly, the IC50 value of avibactam for D179Y-T243M (KPC-128) was 7.8 µM corresponding to a 130-fold increase compared to T243M alone (KPC-100), while it was 0.08 µM for D179Y-T243M-H274Y (KPC-32) (Table 3). Thus, inhibition by avibactam seems to be less potent when the T243M and D179Y mutation are combined but the H274Y mutation increases inhibition potency. The rate constant for the carbamylation reaction (k2/Ki) was also evaluated based on the inhibition of nitrocefin hydrolysis by avibactam5. Fitting of the progress curves of nitrocefin hydrolysis resulted in kobs values at different avibactam concentrations (Eq. 1). Subsequent fitting of these kobs values to Eq. 2 led to k2/Ki values for avibactam of 0.34 and 2600 M−1 s−1 for D179Y-T243M (KPC-128) and D179Y-T243M-H274Y (KPC-32), respectively, showing a reduction in the avibactam carbamylation efficiency for D179Y-T243M (KPC-128), which is consistent with the high IC50 value (Fig. 1, Table 3).

We also determined the rates of avibactam decarbamylation (k-2)5, which were in the same range for T243M (KPC-100) and D179Y-T243M (KPC-128) but were lower than for T243M-H274Y (KPC-89) and D179Y-T243M-H274Y (KPC-32), showing that the H274Y mutation increases the decarbamylation rate (Table 3).

We then determined the IC50 for clavulanate inhibition, revealing similar values for the 4 enzymes, suggesting that the T243M and D179Y mutations have only a moderate impact on the inhibition concentration of clavulanate, although these values are approximately one order of magnitude lower than those observed for KPC-2 and KPC-3. We then determined the (k2/Ki) based on the same experiments as for avibactam. Similar k2/Ki were obtained for T243M-H274Y (KPC-89) and D179Y-T243M-H274Y (KPC-32), suggesting similar clavulanate acylation efficiency. Whereas the k2/Ki of D179Y-T243M (KPC-128) was 66-fold lower compared to T243M (KPC-100) (Table 3), showing D179Y reduces clavulanate acylation efficiency in the KPC-2 T243M background but not the KPC-3 T243M background, similar to the observation with avibactam. An estimate of the rates of clavulanate release (k-2) corresponding to the reversibility of the clavulanate covalent bond to serine β-lactamases gave similar values for T243M-H274Y (KPC-89) and D179Y-T243M-H274Y (KPC-32). Whereas, the k-2 of D179Y-T243M (KPC-128) was around 10-times lower than the other enzymes, suggesting the H274Y mutation increases the off rate of clavulanate. Thus, the H274Y substitution is associated with faster off-rates for both avibactam and clavulanate.

Interactions between mutations

Our kinetics data suggest interactions between mutations, which can be calculated as interaction free energies (ΔΔG). When two substitutions are simultaneously introduced into the enzyme, their collective impact on catalysis can exhibit either an additive or cooperative effect24. In the absence of any cooperativity, the free energy difference of the double mutant is expected to be the sum of the free energy changes of the single mutants. Conversely, if the two substitutions interact, either directly or indirectly, the free energy changes in the double mutant may deviate significantly from what is expected based on the additive effects of the single substitutions. To assess whether the interactions involving D179Y, T243M, and H274Y are additive or cooperative in the context of ceftazidime, cephalothin, and imipenem hydrolysis, the free energies (ΔΔG) associated with the kcat/KM values of the single, double, and triple mutants were calculated.

If the interactions between the single mutations are purely additive, then the coupling free energy is zero, ΔG(I) = 0; however, if the substitutions are non-additive (positive or negative cooperativity), then ΔG(I) ≠ 0, with a negative ΔG(I) indicating the double mutant has higher activity than expected based on the single mutants and a positive ΔG(I) indicating antagonism and a lower than expected activity of the double mutant. The resulting ΔΔG values and ΔG(I) values are summarized in Table 4. Briefly, the ΔΔG values were negative or neutral for all mutations compared to KPC-2 with ceftazidime. The ΔΔG values were negative or neutral for the H274Y mutation and the T243M mutation combined for both cephalothin and imipenem. When the D179Y substitution was added, whatever the genetic background, the ΔΔG values was positive for these 2 substrates.

Simulations reveal mechanistic insights into catalytic efficiency differences among KPC variants

The variants KPC-2 and KPC-3 have highly similar crystal structures but vary in their catalytic efficiencies, with the T243M substitution increasing catalytic efficiency for ceftazidime and cephalothin hydrolysis in the KPC-2 but not in the KPC-3 background. These differences could be modulated through protein dynamics. To uncover the dynamics that may be responsible for the catalytic efficiency differences, we ran molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of KPC-2, KPC-2-T243M (KPC-100), KPC-3, and KPC-3-T243M (KPC-89). We used an adaptive sampling method, fluctuation amplification of specific traits (FAST)25, to maximize the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of residues within the active site in order to rapidly explore the conformational landscape of the catalytic region in these variants. Using cluster centers from a constructed Markov state model, we calculated minimum distances between all non-neighboring residue pairs and used the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence test to uncover differences between the variants. We found two regions of interest that are different between the variants. Both regions are within an active-site loop, the 270-loop. It is known that mutations within the 270-loop result in changes in substrate specificity26, making it reasonable that the dynamics of this loop could alter substrate specificity.

The first region of interest is at K270. In a substrate-bound crystal structure of KPC-2 (PDB ID 5UJ3), K270 reaches in towards the catalytic site and forms a hydrogen bond with one of the hydrolyzed products27. Our simulations of KPC-2 show that K270 adopts two main conformations (Fig. 4A, B). We used the minimum atomic distances between K270 and C238 as a measure of whether K270 is pointing towards the catalytic site (small distance) or away from the catalytic site (large distance). The lower probability conformation is very similar to the KPC-2 crystal structure, where K270 is pointing towards the catalytic site (Fig. 4A, gray structure). In the higher probability conformation, K270 is pointing away from the active site (Fig. 4A, blue structure). However, compared to KPC-2, all other variants (KPC-2-T243M (KPC-100), KPC-3, and KPC-3-T243M (KPC-89)) more strongly favor K270 pointing towards the catalytic site with very low probability of K270 pointing away (Fig. 4B). This stabilization of the K270 conformation pointing towards the catalytic site in all variants more catalytically active than KPC-2 suggests that this could be one of the mechanisms responsible for increasing catalytic efficiency.

The second region of interest is at residue 274, which differentiates KPC-2 (H274) and KPC-3 (H274Y). In the KPC-2 crystal structure, H274 is located on the 270-loop, just before the helix that leads into the loop. However, our simulations of KPC-2 show that H274 becomes part of that helix, thus going from pointing towards the catalytic site to pointing away from it (Fig. 4C). We used minimum atomic distances between residue 274 and V278 as a measure of whether residue 274 gets incorporated into the helix; this distance should be very small when the helix includes residue 274. Figure 4D shows that KPC-2 favors H274 being part of the helix, pointing away from the active site and reducing activity. Adding T243M to the KPC-2 background (KPC-100) allosterically disrupts this helical turn, keeping His274 pointed to the active site and increasing activity. Interestingly, residue 274 is pointed into the active site in KPC-3, so addition of T243M has little impact on the orientation of this residue or on activity.

In summary, the simulations show that residues K270 and H274 point away from the active site in the KPC-2 background, thereby reducing activity. In contrast, these residues point into the active site and accelerate catalysis in KPC-2-T243M (KPC-100), KPC-3, and KPC-3-T243M (KPC-89).

Discussion

The rapid emergence of new KPC variants from clinical isolates demonstrates the enzyme’s adaptability and evolutionary potential. It is worrisome that some of these variants confer resistance to last-resort antibiotics like ceftazidime-avibactam or cefiderocol11,28–31. Understanding the enzymatic mechanisms behind this resistance is essential to anticipate the development of future resistance and thus adapt the development of future antibiotics.

In this study, we sought to better understand the impact of the T243M mutation and the evolutionary paths from KPC-2 to D179Y-T243M-H274Y (KPC-32). The T243 residue is frequently mutated in KPC beta-lactamases, with T243M being the most common variant. It is found both in KPC-2 and KPC-3 (H274Y) backgrounds, both in the presence and absence of D179Y, a well-studied mutation impacting the ceftazidime-avibactam resistance (Fig. 1).

Taken together, our results indicate that the T243M mutation in KPC-2 increases the catalytic efficiency for ceftazidime and cephalothin hydrolysis and decreases the catalytic efficiency for imipenem. The same mutation in KPC-3 has little impact on the catalytic efficiency for these substrates. Finally, its introduction in the KPC-2 or KPC-3 backgrounds does not alter the rate-limiting step of β-lactam hydrolysis. This mutation does not alter ampicillin, cephalothin, and ampicillin/clavulanate resistance but increases resistance to ceftazidime at the expense of carbapenemase activity. The mutation has been found in clinical isolates, likely selected for by ceftazidime or ceftazidime-avibactam treatment12,13.

The addition of the D179Y mutation, irrespective of the initial genetic background (KPC-2 or KPC-3+/− T243M), leads to a similar effect for the 3 tested substrates (ceftazidime, cephalothin, imipenem): a very sharp decrease in kcat, a more moderate and enzyme-dependent decrease in KM, and a decrease in kcat/KM.

Pre-steady-state kinetics analysis showed that the decreases in kcat associated with the D179Y mutation are due to a change in the rate-limiting step from acylation to deacylation for ceftazidime, cephalothin, and imipenem. The rate-limiting deacylation step results in trapping of molecules in the active site of the β-lactamase with a long-lived covalent intermediate as described previously7,11,22. The change in the rate-limiting step is due to the D179Y mutation, while the T243M mutation does not alter the limiting step.

These changes in enzyme kinetics have very different repercussions on β-lactam resistance. Overall, there is an increase in resistance to ceftazidime, a moderate decrease in resistance to cephalothin, and a sharp decrease in resistance to imipenem. The enzymes harboring T243M and D179Y mutations are becoming increasingly “specialized” for ceftazidime resistance. Note that MICs are low overall due to the low expression of the chosen vector—but should be compared with the MICs of the ancestral alleles (KPC-2 or KPC-3)15. It is counter-intuitive to see a different impact of decreasing catalytic efficiency depending on the antibiotic substrate (i.e., decreasing catalytic efficiency for imipenem and cephalothin confers greater sensitivity, while decreasing catalytic efficiency for ceftazidime confers greater resistance). To explain these results, we need to consider β-lactamase in its natural environment—i.e., the bacterial periplasm - taking into account multiple factors such as: the different affinity of the antibiotic (IC50) to specific penicillin binding protein (PBP) impacting the physiology of the cell (i.e., filamentation when PBP3 is inhibited first with ceftazidime vs spheroplast formation when PBP2 is inhibited with imipenem), cell penetration rate of the antibiotic (slower for ceftazidime), expression level of the beta-lactamase and affinity of the antibiotic to the enzyme (KD). All these parameters determine the amount of free beta-lactam in the cell and thus the MIC as discussed in previous works8,14.

Using the catalytic efficiency data, we were able to study the interactions between residues by comparing the free energy ΔΔG values of single, double, and triple mutants. This makes it possible to study the interactions between residues that lead to changes in the catalytic properties of the enzyme. For ceftazidime and cephalothin, when combining D179Y-T243M + /- H274Y mutations (KPC-128 or KPC-32), these values are positive and higher than the sum of the ΔΔG values of the individual mutants. This apparent antagonism acts to reduce the catalytic efficiency of the multiple mutants more than expected based on adding the effects of the single mutants. This shows that the sites interact with each other to alter enzymatic activity. Although counterintuitive, a decrease in catalytic activity leads to an increase in resistance to ceftazidime, likely due to the formation of the long-lived covalent intermediate. So, overall, there is negative cooperativity with reference to catalytic efficiency, but positive cooperativity with reference to ceftazidime resistance. The ΔG(I) values are close to 0 for imipenem, suggesting simple additivity between mutations.

Resistance to ceftazidime is also associated with resistance to ceftazidime-avibactam. This resistance increases when the T243M, D179Y, and H274Y mutations are combined. A number of studies have investigated the carbamylation rate and avibactam inhibition capacities of the KPC-2, KPC-3 (H274Y), KPC-33 (D179Y), and KPC-31 (D179Y-H274Y). These show very different results. The k2/Ki, i.e., the second-order rate constant for enzyme inactivation by avibactam, was found to be between 23,000 and 29,000 M−1 s−1 for KPC-217,18 and 7000 M−1 s−1 for KPC-314. Our data shows a value of 6900 M−1 s−1 for KPC-2, which is similar to our previously determined value of 6800 M-1s-1 (Sun et al.). In addition, KPC-3 yielded a value of 6000 M−1 s−1, similar to the value reported by Shaprio et al. (7100 M−1 s−1). These results suggest KPC-2 and KPC-3 are inactivated at a similar rate by avibactam. Here, the 4 proteins having the T243M mutation (Fig. 1) were studied using the same experimental conditions. Our results are consistent with previous studies and gave similar carbamylation rates for the T243M (KPC-100), T243M/H274Y (KPC-89), and D179Y/T243M/H274Y (KPC-32) mutants—between 2000 and 3000 M−1 s−1, but also a very low carbamylation rate for the D179Y/T243M (KPC-128)—0.34 M−1 s−1. With regard to clavulanate inhibition, our results indicate the inactivation rate for the T243M (KPC-100), T243M/H274Y (KPC-89) and D179Y/T243M/H274Y (KPC-32) mutants is ~40-fold faster than that for KPC-2 and KPC-3, while the rate for D179Y/T243M (KPC-128) is slower and similar to that of KPC-2 and KPC-3. These results indicate that the inhibition of the D179Y-T243M enzyme (KPC-128) by avibactam or clavulanate is much slower than the other mutants, and the IC50 determined experimentally for avibactam is higher. These results are consistent with the k2/Ki found for KPC-33 in previous studies17,18,32.

An interesting finding in this study is that KPC-2 and KPC-3 have highly similar crystal structures but vary in their catalytic efficiencies, with the T243M substitution increasing catalytic efficiency for ceftazidime and cephalothin hydrolysis in the KPC-2 but not in the KPC-3 background. This suggests that the H274Y mutation antagonizes the effect of T243M. Molecular dynamics simulations point to the 270-loop as the structural basis of this effect. The simulations show that the T243M substitution may increase the probability of the K270 side chain pointing towards the active site and interacting with cephalosporin substrates. Further, the H274Y substitution on the 270-loop differentiates KPC-3 from KPC-2. Simulations show two possible conformations of residue 274, where in one conformation it is part of an alpha-helix, where the side chain points away from the active site, and in the other it is not in a helix and points towards the active site. Thus, the T243M mutation may prevent the incorporation of H274 into the helix, thereby explaining the increase in catalytic efficiency from KPC-2 to KPC-2-T243M (KPC-100) by keeping H274 oriented towards the active site.

Enzyme evolution involves a compromise between multiple parameters such as thermostability, protein aggregation, degradation, and catalytic activity, which must ultimately be considered in the broader context of their impact on the organism’s overall fitness33. The T243M mutation reduces the Tm of KPC-2 and KPC-3, reflecting a reduction in stability. However, this mutation retains good hydrolytic activity on ceftazidime (increased or similar to KPC-2 and KPC-3). When the D179Y mutation is added alone in KPC-2 or KPC-3, it reduces stability, but when it is added to the alleles harboring the T243M mutation, the protein Tm increases, suggesting that the D179Y mutation has a stabilizing effect when combined with the T243M mutation. This indicates that D179Y can mitigate the destabilizing impact of other substitutions, maintaining a functional balance between resistance and folding stability. From an evolutionary perspective, this compensatory property likely explains the recurrent emergence of D179Y, which is present in approximately 11% of all reported KPC variants. Its ability to increase stability when combined with destabilizing mutations such as T243M may mitigate the structural costs usually associated with resistance-conferring substitutions. This balance between inhibitor resistance and structural robustness likely provides a fitness advantage, facilitating persistence within bacterial populations and transmission under antibiotic pressure.

From a structural perspective, the reduced stability conferred by the introduction of the D179Y, T243M, and H274Y mutations can be explained by the interactions between the loops surrounding the active site, which contribute to the maintenance of the protein structure and its stability. Briefly, the introduction of the D179Y mutation breaks the salt bridge interaction between R164 and D179, resulting in the destabilization and disorder of the omega-loop. In particular, the E166/N170 residues, which are essential for the deacylation reaction, are repositioned, and as a consequence, the catalytic turnover is greatly decreased8,22,34. These structural modifications are reflected in loop 237-243, which interacts with the omega-loop. Although there appear to be no direct interactions between this loop and ceftazidime22, its disruption could contribute to the overall destabilization of loop interactions and thus reducing protein stability. The combination of the D179Y-T243M mutations increases the molecule’s stability compared with that of single mutants (either D179Y or T243M). This suggests that the reorganization of the omega and 237–243 loops is at the origin of new interactions between the latter, probably affecting the protein structure. Changes in loop 267–275, such as those suggested by the molecular dynamics simulations, could facilitate the reorganization of loop 237–243 and reduce the energy load associated with the reorganization of the other loops3,7,8,22.

Taken together, these results show that the T243M, D179Y+/− H274Y mutations modify protein structure through a complex and finely regulated set of interactions within loops surrounding the active site. The mutations reduce protein stability compared with ancestral alleles. Thus, the appearance of these mutations in nature has little reason to exist in the absence of antibiotic selection pressure, suggesting that ceftazidime +/− avibactam is very likely to have selected for these mutants. However, as KPC beta-lactamase has a higher stability than most other class A beta-lactamases, their evolutionary potential remains high15. Whether these mutants are able to spread without selective pressure remains to be elucidated.

Beyond their molecular significance, these findings have important biological and clinical implications. The emergence of KPC variants illustrates how minimal structural changes can profoundly affect β-lactam and inhibitor efficacy, leading to potential diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. Understanding these adaptive mechanisms is critical to anticipate resistance evolution and to guide the development of next-generation β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations less prone to escape. Clinically, the characterization of such variants supports the need for continuous surveillance of carbapenemase producers and reinforces the importance of integrating structural data into resistance monitoring and drug design strategies.

Methods

Bacterial strains and plasmids

The plasmids KPC-100-pET28a-TEV (T243M), KPC-128-pET28a-TEV (D179Y/T243M), KPC-89-pET28a-TEV (T243M/H274Y) and KPC-32-pET28a-TEV (D179Y/T243M/H274Y) were constructed by site-directed mutagenesis using either pET28a-TEV-KPC-2 or pET28a-TEV-KPC-3 (H274Y) as the template DNA as previously described35,36. The KPC-33-pET28a-TEV (D179Y) and the KPC-31-pET28a-TEV (D179Y-H274Y) were available in the lab. pET28a vector is a modified plasmid with a thrombin recognition sequence between 6×His tag and the protein of interest, replaced by Tobacco Etch Virus (TEV) protease recognition sequence. E. coli SHuffle cells were used for overexpression experiments.

The same KPC variants were also constructed using the pTP123-KPC-2 plasmid as template by site-directed mutagenesis37.

Expression and purification of KPC proteins

The overexpression of KPC-2, H274Y (KPC-3), T243M (KPC-100), T243M-H274Y (KPC-89), D179Y (KPC-33), D179Y-H274Y (KPC-31), D179Y-T243M (KPC-128), and D179Y-T243M-H274Y (KPC-32) proteins was performed by transformation of the corresponding plasmid into E. coli SHuffle cells26.

Few well-isolated colonies were introduced into LB broth supplemented with 25 μg ml⁻¹ kanamycin and cultured overnight at 37 °C with vigorous agitation. The resulting overnight cultures were diluted 1:100 in 1.5 L of LB broth containing 25 μg ml⁻¹ kanamycin and incubated at 37 °C until the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) reached 0.8. At this point, isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) was added to achieve a final concentration of 0.5 mM. The incubation with shaking continued at 23 °C for 16 h, after which cells were harvested by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 15 min.

For the purification of 6xHis-tagged KPC proteins, the cell pellet was re-suspended in Lysis Buffer (20 mM sodium phosphate, pH 7.4, 500 mM NaCl, 20 mM imidazole). Sonication was employed for cell lysis, and the resulting lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 12,000 g for 30 min. The soluble fraction was then filtered using a 0.45 µm filter unit and applied to a 5 ml HisTrapTM FF column (GE Healthcare). Following column washing with lysis buffer, the 6×His-tagged recombinant KPC protein was eluted using a 20–500 mM imidazole gradient in the Lysis Buffer. Fractions containing the recombinant proteins were consolidated, concentrated, and subjected to buffer exchange using a 10-kDa cut-off Amicon concentrator unit (EMD Millipore). Subsequently, the 6×His tag was cleaved through overnight incubation at 4 °C with TEV protease at a 1:50 (TEV: His-KPC protein) ratio, and the TEV protease was removed by incubation with Ni Sepharose Fast Flow beads (GE Healthcare). Further purification of KPC proteins involved gel-filtration chromatography with a Superdex 75 Increase (10/300) column (GE Healthcare), using (20 mM sodium phosphate, pH 7.4, 200 mM NaCl) as the running buffer. Fractions containing the recombinant proteins were combined, concentrated using a 10-kDa cut-off Amicon concentrator unit (EMD Millipore). The purity of each protein exceeded 95%, as determined by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) analysis11.

Steady-state enzyme kinetic measurements

Figure 2 illustrates the kinetics of β-lactamases catalyzing the hydrolysis of β-lactam substrates through a two-step reaction. The Michaelis-Menten kinetic parameters for the T243M (KPC-100), T243M-H274Y (KPC-89), D179Y (KPC-33), D179Y-H274Y (KPC-31), D179Y-T243M (KPC-128), and D179Y-T243M-H274Y (KPC-32) enzyme-substrate pairs were determined at 25 °C in a 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer at pH 7.0, containing 0.1 mg/mL BSA. Variable amounts of enzyme were used depending on the enzyme-substrate pair. The initial velocities of β-lactam hydrolysis were measured at 260 nm for ceftazidime, using a Beckman-Colter spectrophotometer model DU-800 (Fullerton, CA). Extinction coefficients were employed for the calculations: for ceftazidime at 260 nM (Δε260 = −8660 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹), nitrocefin at 482 nm (Δε482nm = 15,000 M−1 cm−1), imipenem at 300 nm (Δε300nm = −9000 M−1 cm−1), and cephalothin at 262 nm (Δε262nm = −7660 M−1 cm−1) (antibiotics were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich)11.

GraphPad Prism 8.4.2 was utilized to derive the steady-state parameters through a non-linear least squares fit of the data to the Michaelis-Menten equation v = kcat[S]/(KM + [S]). In instances where substrate hydrolysis velocity could not be saturated due to a high KM, the second-order rate constant at steady-state kcat/KM was determined by fitting progress curves to the equation v= kcat/KM [E][S], where [S] << KM. Each set of measurements was replicated in at least two independent experiments.

The error associated with kcat and KM represents the standard error of the fit of initial velocity determinations to the Michaelis-Menten equation. The error on kcat/KM was calculated by propagating the errors of both kcat and KM, involving the square root of the sum of the squared fractional errors of kcat and KM23.

Pre steady-state kinetics

Pre-steady state kinetics were carried out on a Kintek SF-300X stopped-flow spectrophotometer. For the T243M (KPC-100), T243M-H274Y (KPC-89), D179Y-T243M (KPC-128), and D179Y-T243M-H274Y (KPC-32) proteins, we used 10 µM of enzyme and variable concentrations of substrate (ceftazidime, cephalothin, or imipenem). These reactions were performed at 25 °C in 50 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7.0, containing 0.1 mg/mL BSA. For each kinetic experiment, 2000 data points were collected, and at least five kinetic traces were collected to ensure high-quality data for times ranging from 1 to 60 s on two different protein extracts. The dead time of the instrument is of 0.85 ms. The observed data were fit to either a one-phase decay (exponential) equation or to the Burst equation using GraphPad Prism 8.4.211.

Differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF) to determine protein stability

The thermal shift assay was employed to measure the melting temperature (Tm) of KPC mutants compared with WT data. Protein denaturation was assessed by monitoring the increase in fluorescence of SYPRO Orange dye (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA), which selectively binds to hydrophobic residues exposed during protein unfolding. Purified proteins were suitably diluted in a buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, and 150 mM NaCl. The experiment was performed with KPC variants proteins at a concentration of 2 μg and SYPRO Orange dye at 5× in a 10-μL reaction volume. Thermal scanning (20–95 °C) was conducted using a Roche Lightcycler 480 real-time PCR instrument, and fluorescence intensity was recorded at 10-s intervals. Data analysis was performed on both the Roche Lightcycler 480 real-time PCR instrument and the LightCycler thermal shift analysis software. The results were averaged from three technical replicates.

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) determinations

Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC’s) for E. coli strain XL1-Blue containing the following plasmids: pTP123 with KPC mutants was determined for ceftazidime, cephalothin, imipenem, ampicillin, ampicillin/clavulanate, and ceftazidime/avibactam using the broth microdilution method as recommended by the CLSI. Briefly, overnight cultures of the KPC-2, KPC-3, and their variants were diluted into wells containing two-fold dilutions of the antibiotic in a total volume of 200 μl Mueller-Hinton cation adjusted broth. The plate was allowed to incubate overnight at 37 °C with vigorous shaking and scored for visible growth to determine the MIC. Antibiotics were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

Determination of avibactam and clavulanate inhibition potency

The susceptibility of KPC enzymes to inhibition by avibactam and clavulanate was assessed using purified KPC-2, H274Y (KPC-3), T243M (KPC-100), T243M-H274Y (KPC-89), D179Y-T243M (KPC-128), and D179Y-T243M-H274Y (KPC-32) enzymes. The enzymes were combined with inhibitors at different concentrations in 50 mM sodium phosphate (pH 7.0), supplemented with 0.02% Tween-20 and 1 µg/ml BSA, followed by an incubation at room temperature for 10 min (clavulanate) or 20 min (avibactam). The reaction was initiated by introducing an equal volume of nitrocefin prepared in the same buffer (final concentration of 45 µM). The initial velocity (Vo) of nitrocefin hydrolysis in assays with inhibitors at 28 °C was expressed as a percentage of the Vo observed in reactions without inhibitors, which was set as 100%. The half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50) of inhibitors were determined by plotting Vo against the logarithms of inhibitor concentrations (Log[I]) and fitting the data log(inhibitor) versus normalized response using GraphPad 8.4.2.

Molecular dynamics simulations

Simulation setup

We ran simulations for 4 variants: KPC2, KPC2-T243M (KPC-100), KPC3, and KPC3-T243M (KPC-89). We used UCSF Chimera38 to add the T243M mutation to the KPC2 (PDB ID 5UJ3)27 and KPC3 (PDB ID 6QWD)39 structures. The simulations were run in GROMACS 2023.240 using the Amber03 force field41 with TIP3P waters, in a dodecahedron box with 0.1 M NaCl at 310 K. For equilibration, we first used the steepest descent algorithm for energy minimization, followed by NVT equilibration for 200 ps, and NPT equilibration for 1000 ps. Our production runs were each 50 ns. We used the adaptive sampling algorithm FAST25 to maximize the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of the residues near or within the active site region (non-hydrogen atoms from residues 70, 73, 104, 105, 130, 132, 166, 167, 170, 234, 237, 238, and 239). We ran 10 FAST generations with 10 “kids” (replicates) in each generation, resulting in an aggregate of 5000 ns of simulation time per variant. Scripts for analysis have been deposited42.

Simulation analysis

For dimensionality reduction and elucidation of the conformational landscapes explored, we built Markov state models (MSMs) for each variant using Enspara43. We then analyzed the equilibrium probability weighted cluster centers from the MSM. To analyze the cluster centers, we calculated the minimum distance between heavy atoms in every non-neighbor residue—residue pair in each variant using the contacts function in MDTraj44. This resulted in 33,670 residue—residue pairs for the KPC2 and KPC3 variants. The Kullback-Leibler divergence test was conducted between KPC2 and KPC2-T243M (KPC-100), KPC2 and KPC3, and KPC3 and KPC3-T243M (KPC-89) to find which residue pairs had the largest divergences in their distributions between variants.

Determination of avibactam carbamylation efficiency (k2/Ki) and decarbamylation rate (k-2)

Experiments to investigate avibactam carbamylation and clavulanate inhibition were conducted at 28 °C in a solution comprising 50 mM sodium phosphate (pH 7.0), 0.02% Tween-20, and 1 µg/ml BSA. A Tecan Infinite MPlex microplate reader, with 200 µM nitrocefin as the substrate, was employed. The enzyme concentrations used were 1 nM for KPC-2 and KPC-3, 2 nM for T243M (KPC-100) and T243M-H274Y (KPC-89) and 1 µM for D179Y-T243M (KPC-128) and D179Y-T243M-H274Y (KPC-32), respectively. Avibactam concentrations ranging from 0.01 to 1000 µM were tested, depending on the enzyme. A minimum of two replicates were performed.
