Using of artificial intelligence to evaluate image quality for interproximal bone level assessment on bitewing radiographs
Authors: Carolina Tiemi Kuteken, Thiago de Oliveira Gamba, Rafael de Mattos Hahn, André Luis Martinotto, Mariana Boessio Vizzotto, Michael Marc Bornstein, Heraldo Luís Dias da Silveira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo develop an artificial intelligence (AI) model capable of certifying whether the quality of the bitewing image is adequate or compromised, as an intermediate tool for the technical analysis prior to the final evaluation of the alveolar bone crest.Materials and MethodsA total of 372 bitewing radiographs from a radiographic database were classified by three evaluators into control 186 (standard vertical angulation) and test 186 (offset vertical angulation) groups, forming a consensus gold standard for accuracy with the algorithm classification. The region of interest was defined as the non-overlapping area between the buccal and palatal/lingual cusps for data labeling, using the YOLOv8n-Seg architecture for training, validation, and testing. The model's performance was evaluated by comparing the average areas among groups, computing the metrics of precision, sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, recall and F1 score.ResultsThe AI model achieved a classification accuracy of 96.7%, with 100% sensitivity and 93.8% specificity (ICC = 0.95). The precision was 0.93, the recall 1.0 and the F1-score 0.96. Across both training and validation stages, significant differences were observed between the test and control groups (P < 0.05), with consistently larger regions of interest areas in the test group. A direct relationship was observed between bitewing radiographs classified as having offset vertical angulation and the regions of interest area. The model achieved excellent accuracy, comparable to that of expert evaluators.ConclusionThe AI model accurately segmented and classified whether or not vertical angulation was compromising the quality of bitewing radiographs, thereby minimizing errors in the diagnostic interpretation of the alveolar bone crest.Keywords: Alveolar Process; Artificial Intelligence; Bitewing Radiography; Radiography, Dental, Digital; Machine Learning

Introduction

Intraoral radiographs remain a gold standard for the assessment of periodontal disease and are the preferred imaging modality for evaluating alveolar bone status.1 The use of receptor positioning holders plays a crucial role in ensuring consistency and standardization, key factors for accurate radiographic assessment of the alveolar bone crest (ABC) in both periapical and bitewing (BW) views. Additionally, these devices help minimize patient exposure by reducing the need to repeat radiographs.2

Within the current classification of periodontal diseases, radiographs are emphasized as a key diagnostic tool for staging and grading periodontitis, based on the extent of bone loss.3,4 Bitewing radiographs, in particular, are considered reliable for measuring alveolar bone height in the posterior region.5 However, the accuracy of these measurements depends not only on image quality but also on the expertise of the interpreter.6

The presence and severity of alveolar bone loss are typically assessed by measuring the distance from the cementoenamel junction to the ABC, in conjunction with clinical probing and digital radiography.1 Although radiographic positioners are used to standardize image acquisition, vertical angulation errors remain a common limitation.

Inaccurate angulation can distort the perceived height of the ABC, potentially leading to underestimation or overestimation of bone loss, causing the alveolar bone level to appear artificially elevated or reduced relative to its true anatomical position and the alveolar crest is a three-dimensional anatomical structure that is represented on radiographs as a two-dimensional projection. For example, if the X-ray beam is angled too steeply, it can cause foreshortening, which makes the bone crest appear more apical relative to surrounding landmarks than it really is. Clinically, this can be misinterpreted as greater bone loss. When the central X-ray beam deviates from the ideal projection, geometric distortion alters the apparent positions of the cementoenamel junction and the ABC on the radiograph.7,8

Optimal vertical angulation, approximately 0° to the ABC plateau, can be verified radiographically by the overlap of buccal and lingual cusp tips, resulting in more reliable and reproducible measurements.9 BW radiographs are most suitable for detecting mild to moderate bone loss, while periapical radiographs are preferred for cases of more advanced bone loss. Importantly, the use of one technique does not exclude the other. Nevertheless, variations in vertical angulation can compromise ABC assessment in both methods, i.e, an under or overestimation of bone loss.2,3

Over the past three decades, maxillofacial radiology has been a key driver of innovation in dental medicine. This progress began with the advent of digital radiography in the 1990s, followed by the introduction of cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) in the early 2000s,10 and more recently, the integration of artificial intelligence (AI). Currently AI tools related to oral radiology offer potential applications for refined diagnostics and assisting treatment planning across various fields of dentistry.11 For dental imaging, studies have shown the effectiveness of AI in detecting caries,12 impacted teeth,13,14 supernumerary teeth,15 and alveolar bone loss on panoramic radiographs.16 While these studies highlight the diagnostic potential of AI in dentistry, they often overlook a critical factor: the quality of input images, which plays a fundamental role in determining diagnostic accuracy.

Given that variations in vertical angulation can compromise ABC evaluation,9 there remains a need for AI systems to not only analyze and assess radiographs for potential pathology, but also assess their technical adequacy (e.g. image quality or lack thereof).

Therefore, the objective of this study was to develop an AI model capable of certifying whether the quality of the interproximal image is adequate-standard vertical angulation (SA) or compromised-offset vertical angulation (OA), serving as an intermediate tool for technical analysis prior to the final ABC evaluation. Specifically, to report a problem related to the technical quality of the image, i.e, an intermediate tool and not an AI tool capable of detecting changes in the ABC level.

Materials and Methods

The study was conducted after approval by the Research Ethics Committee of the Federal University of Rio Grande do Sul under protocol number 5.133.756.

Images selection

This investigation was designed as a cross-sectional observational study, and was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Federal University of Rio Grande do Sul (UFRGS), Porto Alegre, Brazil (Process No. 5.133.756). All procedures were conducted at the Digital Image Processing Laboratory (LAPID), Oral Radiology Division, School of Dentistry of the Federal University of Rio Grande do Sul.

The study sample comprised 372 BW radiographs of the premolar and molar regions, equally divided between SA (control group) and OA (test group) groups (i.e. 86 each), selected from the institutional radiographic archive of a total of 6,010 patients (Fig. 1). All images were acquired using the Vista Scan imaging system with DBSWIN software (Dürr Dental, Bietigheim-Bissingen, Germany). The inclusion criteria were as follows; 1) BW radiographs obtained from patients with permanent dentition in the right and left premolar and molar regions, 2) adequate horizontal angulation, 3) clear visualization of the ABC, with or without evidence of bone loss, 4) BW radiographs with standard vertical angulation, and 5) BW radiographs acquired with offset vertical angulation (OA) in cases where the heights of the buccal and lingual cusp tips were discrepant. The exclusion criteria were as follows; 1) presence of mixed dentition, 2) complete edentulism, 3) impacted or angulated third molars, 4) radiographic artifacts that interfered with image interpretation, including extensive restorations, orthodontic appliances (e.g., brackets), dental implants, or fixed prostheses.

Flowchart of study sample selection at each stage was subdivided into standard vertical angulation (SA) and offset vertical angulation (OA) groups. BW: bitewing, ROI: region of interest

All images were anonymized, encoded, and stored in JPEG format prior to model training. Preprocessing included resizing and normalization. The image width in pixels was proportionally converted to millimeters based on the physical width of No. 2 radiographic film (41 mm).

A consensus gold standard (i.e. ground truth) was established by three calibrated oral radiologists, each with extensive experience in diagnostic imaging, for the classification of the total sample included (372 BW radiographs). All images were randomly ordered prior to classification and subsequently assigned to the control SA group and test OA group. This consensus served as the reference standard for the images used in the training, validation, and testing phases of the artificial intelligence (AI) model developed in this study.

YOLOv8 architecture and training

The initial dataset consisted of 372 interproximal radiographs (186 SA and 186 OA) with a total of 3,131 regions of interest (ROIs). These ROIs were defined as the non-overlapping area between the buccal and palatal/lingual cusps, expressed in pixels per square millimeter (px/mm2) (Fig. 2). The YOLOv8 architecture and training consisted of training (training and validation) and testing phases, following the transfer learning approach using the YOLOv8n-Seg version.

The general method of the training phases (training and validation stage) follows a transfer learning approach, using the YOLOv8n-Seg architecture. In the model training phase, in sequential order: image resizing and standardization; image preprocessing; segmentation of regions of interests in green-standard vertical angulation (SA) and red-offset vertical angulation (OA) for mask annotation; application of data augmentation; validation of the trained model with the configured hyperparameters.

Prior to data labeling, two calibrated evaluators underwent a calibration process involving consensus-based training on a representative subset of images to standardize segmentation criteria. Thus, manual segmentation of these ROIs was performed independently to ensure consistency and minimize annotation bias. This procedure aimed to harmonize the interpretation of anatomical boundaries and reduce inter- and intra-observer variability, thereby enhancing the reliability and reproducibility of the ground-truth annotations used for model training.

The calibration phase of these 2 evaluators included 12 randomly selected images (6 SA, 6 OA; 212 ROIs total) (Fig. 1). Segmentation reproducibility was assessed in ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) via a two-way mixed-effects model.17 These 12 images were excluded from subsequent analyses. Following calibration, all radiographs 360 BW (Fig. 1) radiographs were manually segmented using the Computer Vision Annotation Tool (CVAT; CVAT.ia, Palo Alto, CA, USA).18 Bounding box masks were generated for each category SA and OA groups, based on the expert consensus gold standard (Fig. 3).

Masks were exported and converted to YOLO text format with a custom Python script. ROI areas (mm2) were quantified using the pandas library in Python,19 and the data recorded (Fig. 2). These 360 BW radiographs were randomly split into: 1) training set (80%), 2) validation set (10%), and 3) testing set (10%). Each subset contained equal sample numbers of SA and OA images (Fig. 1).

The detection and segmentation task was addressed using the YOLOv8n-Seg architecture, a nano-scale variant optimized for real-time object localization and segmentation.20 The model was initialized with COCO-pretrained weights to leverage transfer learning and subsequently fine-tuned on the current dataset.

Model training was conducted using the PyTorch framework in Python (v3.x) on a dedicated workstation equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 2080 GPU (16 GB VRAM). The training configuration was as follows: 300 epochs, input image size of 1,280 pixels, batch size of 6, and early stopping with a patience value of 20 (Fig. 2).

To increase dataset variability and improve model generalization, data augmentation techniques were applied prior to training. The Albumentations library21 was used to simulate variations commonly encountered in clinical practice, such as changes in brightness, contrast, orientation, and scale, thus improving the model's robustness to real-world imaging conditions. Data augmentation has been shown to be a critical step in improving cross-domain generalization in image analysis, as it helps mitigate overfitting and ensures consistent model performance across different imaging devices and acquisition protocols.22

Data augmentation included brightness and gamma adjustments applied using the Albumentations library. In addition, during training, horizontal flipping (probability = 0.5) and small rotations (±10°) were applied as on-the-fly transformations to further improve robustness. The model was optimized using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001, momentum parameters β1=0.9 and β2=0.999, ε=1×10−8, and a weight decay=0.0001. This configuration ensured stable training and allowed for robust convergence of the network.

The implemented transformations included brightness adjustment (factor = 0.5, probability = 1) and gamma correction (range = 50–200, probability = 1) (Fig. 2). Data augmentation was performed only on the training and validation image groups, while the test group was not augmented. Augmentation tripled the dataset size, yielding 900 training images and 90 validation images (Fig. 1). Final dataset distribution: 1) training: 900 BW (450 SA = 3,267 ROIs; 450 OA = 3,918 ROIs); 2) validation: 90 BW (45 SA = 357 ROIs; 45 OA = 372 ROIs); 3) testing: 30 BW (15 SA = 128 ROIs; 15 OA = 153 ROIs).

Model performance analysis

The model was evaluated for its ability to segment and classify BW radiographs into SA and OA groups, focusing on image quality assessment as an intermediate analytical tool rather than as a definitive diagnostic instrument.

For segmentation, the mean ROI area (mm2) and standard deviation were calculated in the training, validation, and testing phases. This analysis assessed the relationship between the area of non-overlapping cusps in interproximal regions (i.e., the mean sum of ROIs per image) and compromised image quality due to vertical angulation variations (SA vs. OA).9

This study evaluated the performance of the AI model in classifying images based on the consensus gold standard established by expert evaluators for the OA and SA groups. A rule-based classification strategy was adopted: if the number of ROI segments of OA in a radiograph was greater than or equal to the number of ROI segments of SA present in a BW radiographic image, the image was classified as OA; otherwise, it was classified as SA. Since the relationship between the area of non-overlapping vestibular and lingual/palatal cusps and the applied vertical angulation is direct, the greater the variation in vertical angulation, which compromises the quality of the BW radiographic image, the larger the area/ROI of non-overlapping cusps will be. Therefore, this classification rule was consistently applied across all phases (training, validation, and testing) of the AI model, ensuring standardization and accommodating minor variations in dental positioning. This ensured whole-image classification, considering potential tooth malpositions (e.g., rotation, distoversion, mesioversion, mesioangulation, distoangulation, palatinization, or lingualization).

The performance of the AI model was evaluated using standard classification metrics: precision, recall, F1-score, and accuracy (%). These metrics were calculated based on the number of true positives (TP = correctly classified as OA), true negatives (TN = correctly classified as SA), false positives (FP = classified as OA but actually SA), and false negatives (FN = classified as SA but actually OA). Additionally, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed, with corresponding area under the curve (AUC) values and 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed in JASP software (v0.19.3; JASP Team, University of Amsterdam, Amsterdam, The Netherlands). Normality and variance homogeneity were assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Levene’s tests during training, while the Shapiro–Wilk test was applied in the validation and testing phases. Comparative analyses were performed as follows; 1) Welch’s t-test during training (to account for unequal variances), 2) independent student’s t-test to compare training and validation results, 3) paired student’s t-test during testing.

Results

A direct relationship was observed between BW radiographs classified as OA and ROI area. Images classified by the AI model when there was an offset in the vertical angulation positioning showed a larger area with the sum of ROIs per image, compared to the control group-SA, which had a smaller ROI area (Fig. 4). The AI model slightly over-segmented the actual ROI area (as defined manually in CVAT) in both the OA and SA groups. However, during the test phase, this did not affect classification performance, as the model achieved excellent accuracy comparable to that of expert evaluators (Fig. 5). Manual segmentation of ROIs using CVAT demonstrated excellent consistency, with an ICC of 0.95 (95% CI, α = 0.05).

Bar graph representing the results (px/mm2) of regions of interest (ROIs) area and standard deviation (SD) of standard vertical angulation (SA) and offset vertical angulation (OA) groups-training, validation and testing stages of the algorithm execution.

Receiver operating characteristic curve generated in Python using sensitivity and specificity values of the classification data from the algorithm’s testing phase, accurate with the previously established consensus gold standard.

Across all stages of model application (training, validation, and testing), the OA group consistently presented larger ROI areas compared to the SA group (Fig. 4). In the training stage (n = 300; 80% of the dataset sample), the SA group obtained a mean ROI area of 5.06 ± 1.70 px/mm2, while the OA group achieved 13.53 ± 2.65 px/mm2. Welch’s t-test confirmed a highly significant group difference, P < 0.001. In the validation stage (n = 30; 10% of the dataset sample), mean ROI areas were 4.75 ± 1.57 px/mm2 SA group and 10.94 ± 1.78 px/mm2 OA group. Student’s t-test again showed a significant difference, P < 0.001. In the testing stage (n = 30; 10% of the dataset sample), the SA group reached 5.96 ± 2.26 px/mm2 compared with 14.12 ± 3.81 px/mm2 in the OA group. Paired comparisons of manual CVAT and AI segmentations demonstrated consistent oversegmentation by the AI model: SA P = 0.045 and OA P = 0.003. Descriptive values confirmed this trend, with AI-derived ROI areas exceeding gold-standard manual segmentation in both groups (Fig. 4).

Classification of images into SA and OA groups yielded 96.7% accuracy (95% CI) with 100% sensitivity and 96.8% specificity of the AI model compared with the examiners' consensus gold standard (Fig. 5). Only one false positive was observed, and no false negatives occurred (Fig. 6).

Confusion matrix (algorithm vs. consensus gold standard). OA: offset vertical angulation, SA: standard vertical angulation.

The performance metrics demonstrated high values across all evaluations. Precision was 0.93, recall was 1.00, F1-score was 0.96, and overall accuracy was 0.96. These results indicate robust classification performance, with excellent sensitivity and balanced predictive ability.

Discussion

Most studies in oral radiology involving AI have aimed to demonstrate its potential applications in diagnostics, treatment planning or even monitoring outcomes.11 In contrast, the present study highlights the importance of evaluating the model’s performance in identifying bitewing (BW) radiographs with acquisition errors, particularly those related to OA. In this context, our approach was designed as an intermediate AI tool for diagnostic support, similar to the framework proposed by Barayan et al.23 Thus, the objective was not to develop a final diagnostic system, but rather to focus on image quality assessment as an intermediate analytical step that can enhance subsequent diagnostic accuracy.

This contrasts with studies such as those by Cassiano et al.24 and Butnaru et al,25 which aimed to develop definitive diagnostic instruments. Although the use of AI has proven to be a promising tool for final diagnosis, the importance of technical image analysis must be remembered, since good diagnoses are made from good images.26 For example, if the X-ray beam is angled too sharply, it can cause shortening, which makes the bone crest appear more apical in relation to the surrounding anatomical landmarks than it actually is. Clinically, this can be misinterpreted as greater bone loss. Therefore, the possibility of a more accurate diagnosis consequently leads to better treatment planning and greater success,7,8 with the application in digital radiography systems.

The correct application of vertical angulation assessment during bitewing acquisition is fundamental for accurate evaluation of the ABC,2 which is a critical diagnostic criterion in the detection and staging of periodontal diseases.3 Despite the increasing use of artificial intelligence (AI) as a diagnostic aid,24,25,27,28 the influence of radiographic acquisition parameters—particularly vertical angulation—remains underexplored. Our findings confirm that radiographs classified as having incorrect vertical angulation exhibit a larger ROI area at the non-overlapping area between the buccal and palatal/lingual cusps of the molar and premolar region. This may be indicative of a tendency to underestimate or overestimate alveolar bone loss,9 since image quality is compromised by incorrect application-OA of the angulation of the X-ray beam (Fig. 7). Thus, this developed algorithm is useful as an intermediate tool for the diagnosis of imaging evaluation of ABC, that is, its clinical application is to avoid and minimize the diagnostic error of bone loss due to acquisition-related errors.

Across all stages of model application, the mean ROI area was significantly larger in the group with OA than in the group with SA. This relationship is consistent with cusp overlap phenomenon, where reduced overlap corresponds to greater angulation variation.9 Although the AI model systematically oversegmented ROIs compared with manual segmentation, classification accuracy remained excellent (96.7%), with 100% sensitivity and 96.8% specificity. This performance indicates that the model is capable of reliably distinguishing between images with and without angulation variation, even in the presence of segmentation bias.

Clinically, these findings highlight the risk of misdiagnosis when angulation variation is not accounted for. Radiography acquisition with excessive vertical angulation may underestimate bone loss, since the rays project the ABC image in the direction of the cementoenamel junction.2,3,9 The proposed AI model offers a practical solution by automatically detecting angulation variation before diagnostic interpretation, thereby supporting more reliable assessment of periodontal bone levels. This model could be integrated into available digital radiography systems so that, during scanning of phosphor plate photostimulable, the software would automatically detect and report angulation errors, alerting the user that measurement accuracy may be compromised. Therefore, the developed algorithm serves as a supportive tool that assists in achieving a more accurate final diagnosis.

The use of a YOLOv8n-Seg architecture with transfer learning contributed to the high accuracy achieved, outperforming traditional two-stage detection networks such as Faster R-CNN.29,30 The use of hyperparameters and data augmentation strategies (brightness and gamma) further increased the model's robustness. This ensures the model's applicability across various acquired radiograph conditions, such as film position, kilovoltage (kV), milliamperage (mA), and others.

A key strength of this study is the use of a gold-standard segmentation protocol with high inter-rater reliability (ICC = 0.95), ensuring robust and reliable reference data for model training and evaluation. By adopting a validated ground truth approach, as recommended in prior studies,24,25 our methodology helps mitigate potential algorithmic bias and reinforces the critical role of standardized reference data in developing accurate and clinically applicable AI tools for dental radiology.

However, some limitations should be acknowledged. The dataset was limited to bitewing radiographs, although the algorithm’s underlying identification principle could potentially be extended to periapical images. Moreover, while the model effectively detected variations in vertical angulation, it did not quantify the exact degree of deviation, a feature that could be valuable for future refinement. Additionally, larger and multicenter datasets would enhance the model’s generalizability across diverse imaging systems and patient populations, ultimately improving image quality assessment prior to final ABC diagnosis.

The AI model developed in this study demonstrated high accuracy in detecting BW radiographs with offset vertical angulation of the ABC area. By reliably identifying acquisition errors that might compromise the evaluation of the ABC, the algorithm represents a promising clinical support tool for identifying radiographic images with compromised technical quality (OA group), which may help reduce the risk of misinterpretation, such as errors in ABC height assessment, i.e. over- or underestimation of alveolar bone loss. Integrating such models into digital radiography systems could support clinicians in assessing image quality prior to diagnostic interpretation, thereby enhancing the accuracy of periodontal evaluation and improving treatment planning.
