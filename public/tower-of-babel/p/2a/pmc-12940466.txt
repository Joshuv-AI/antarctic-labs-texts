Spider taxonomy: A historical and global perspective
Authors: Xiao-Qing Zhang, Xin-Yu Li, Yuri M Marusik, Mei-Chen Yan, Rui-Han Yang, Ya-Xuan Zhang, Fan-Yu Bu, Yu Ding, Ming-Yue Wang, Zhi-Yuan Yao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUsing comprehensive records from the World Spider Catalog, this study presents an in-depth, data-driven analysis of global trends in spider taxonomy, revealing an exceptional acceleration during the first quarter of the 21st century. Of the more than 53 000 extant spider species described since 1757, 19 159—approximately 36%—were described during the last 25 years. In 59 families, the number of new species described during this period exceeded the total accumulated over the entire 20th century. Asia emerged as the leading continental hotspot for spider discovery, with China at the forefront. The number of species described in China during the 25-year window was 1.5 times greater than the combined total documented over the preceding 243 years, with Chinese arachnologists responsible for 23.9% of all new species described in the last 25 years. Three taxonomists—Eugène Simon, Norman Platnick, and Shu-Qiang Li—rank as the most prolific contributors to spider systematics in history. These findings reflect a shift in the global center of arachnological research and underscore the growing scientific output from China. Patterns were examined from three complementary angles: temporal acceleration in species descriptions, individual contributions of leading taxonomists, and the emergence of Chinese arachnology as a dominant force in global taxonomy. Our analysis also emphasizes the critical role of sustained international collaboration in advancing biodiversity discovery.Keywords: Araneae, Arachnologist, Species, Taxon, Biodiversity, China

INTRODUCTION

The history of spider taxonomy began a year before Carl Linnaeus formally applied the binomial system for animals in his 10th edition of Systema Naturae in 1758. Clerck (1757) became the first naturalist to describe spiders under the binomial system, thus establishing himself formally as the earliest pioneer in arachnological taxonomy. With texts and illustrations, Clerck recorded the morphology and distribution of 68 species of spiders from Sweden. Since that landmark contribution, the field has expanded dramatically, with more than 50 000 species formally described over the subsequent 268 years.

Early efforts to systematize this expanding body of knowledge emerged in the 20th century, initiated by the French arachnologist Pierre Bonnet (1897–1990) and German arachnologist Carl Friedrich Roewer (1881–1963). Bonnet (1959, 1961) compiled taxonomic records and bibliographic references through 1939, while Roewer (1942, 1955) documented spider species and their associated literature up to 1940 and, for some families, as late as 1954. The Italian arachnologist Paolo Marcello Brignoli (1942–1986) expanded these efforts with the publication of the Catalogue of the Araneae described between 1940 and 1981. This was later refined and expanded by Norman Platnick (1951–2020) at the American Museum of Natural History, who issued three supplemental volumes spanning 1981 to 1995 and retrospectively standardizing taxonomic revisions, synonymies, and nomenclatural transfers from 1940 to 1980 (Platnick, 1989, 1993, 1997).

The rapid increase in taxonomic output toward the end of the 20th century, combined with growing demands for accessible, up-to-date records, led Platnick to launch the first online version of the World Spider Catalog (WSC) in 2000. Maintained through 2014 with biannual updates—totaling 30 versions—the catalog was initially organized as family-level HTML files and hosted by the American Museum of Natural History. After retirement, responsibility for the WSC shifted to the Natural History Museum in Bern, where the resource was restructured into a relational database with integrated search functionality, greatly enhancing its utility for large-scale taxonomic analysis.

The development of a searchable WSC database has enabled systematic extraction of taxonomic records and facilitated large-scale analyses of global trends in spider classification, both across centuries (Jäger et al., 2021; Li, 2020; Platnick & Raven, 2013) and at annual resolution (Li et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2023; Yao & Li, 2021; Zhang et al., 2022). Building on these foundations, the present study offers a more granular, analytical, and up-to-date assessment of global taxonomic activity from 1757 to 2024, with a focus on identifying and interpreting major shifts in research methodologies and author-level productivity that have characterized the modern era of spider systematics.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The dataset analyzed in this study was sourced from the WSC (2025), with 31 December 2024 set as the cut-off date. It encompassed 52 617 currently recognized species spanning 4 412 genera and 136 families (Supplementary Table S1). Only species with valid names were included; all synonyms were excluded to prevent duplication in species counts. For each entry, information on authorship, year of publication, and recorded distribution range was retained. Data were compiled in Microsoft Excel, and all summary statistics and visualizations were generated using base packages in R, executed within R-Studio.

Continental- and country-level spider distribution patterns were derived primarily from geographic records listed in the WSC. For European taxa, additional verification and supplementation were conducted using Spiders of Europe (Nentwig et al., 2025). Although most species had clearly defined distributions, some were listed under broad geographic descriptors (e.g., “Europe to Central Asia”, “Libya to India”, “West Africa”, “South America”, “Africa”, “Asia”), which compromised spatial resolution at the national level. To minimize errors, country-level occurrence data for such species were manually curated from original taxonomic descriptions and other relevant literature. While these refinements may introduce minor deviations from the unadjusted WSC dataset, the overall continental and national trends remain consistent with published distribution patterns across historical periods.

To quantify individual contributions to species descriptions, only those names included in the authority citation provided by the WSC were counted. If a species was described in a multi-authored publication but the authority citation included only a single individual, the others were not credited with the description. In cases where two names appeared in the authority citation of a three-author publication, only those two authors were credited, while the third—absent from the citation—was excluded.

RESULTS

Global trends in species descriptions

Only 137 spider species were described during the 18th century (Figure 1A, B), including the initial 68 species documented by Clerck in 1757. These species were distributed across 33 families, including Araneidae (37 species), Lycosidae (20 species), Salticidae (13 species), Theridiidae (10 species), Thomisidae (six species), and Linyphiidae (five species). The 19th century saw a substantial increase, with 8 945 new species described (Figure 1A, B). Six families accounted for over 500 species each, including Salticidae (1 287), Araneidae (970), Linyphiidae (792), Thomisidae (665), Theridiidae (649), and Lycosidae (560) (Supplementary Table S2). The yearly rate of description during this period peaked in 1895, with 462 species recorded (Figure 1A).

In the first half of the 20th century, annual species descriptions declined, likely influenced by the disruptions of the two World Wars. However, from 1950 onward, rates rose sharply (Figure 1A). A total of 24 376 species were described throughout the 20th century (Figure 1B). Notably, 10 families accounted for more than 500 species each, including Salticidae (3 071), Linyphiidae (3 050), Araneidae (1 530), Lycosidae (1 439), Theridiidae (1 431), Gnaphosidae (1 330), Thomisidae (1 137), Agelenidae (557), Tetragnathidae (511), and Pholcidae (504). The highest annual count for the century occurred in 1995, with 925 species described.

In the first quarter of the 21st century, 19 159 new species were described (Figure 1B). Nine families contributed over 500 species each, including Salticidae (2 433), Oonopidae (1 581), Pholcidae (1 437), Linyphiidae (1 018), Agelenidae (761), Zodariidae (757), Sparassidae (753), Gnaphosidae (738), and Araneidae (605) (Supplementary Table S2). A total of 1 236 spider species were formally described in 2023, setting a new benchmark for annual taxonomic output and eclipsing all previous yearly counts recorded over more than two and a half centuries of systematic study (Figure 1A).

The accumulation of described spider species has accelerated continuously since the mid-18th century. It required 146 years, from 1757 to 1902, for the number of described species to reach 10 000. The global total then doubled to 20 000 after another 51 years (1902–1953), increased to 30 000 within 38 years (1953–1991), and rose to 40 000 in only 19 years (1991–2010). The 50 000th species, the South American jumping spider (Guriurius minuano Marta, Bustamante, Ruiz & Rodrigues, 2022), was described in 2022, marking the completion of the most recent 10 000 species interval in 12 years (2010–2022) (Supplementary Table S1). Average annual description rates across these five intervals were 68, 196, 263, 526, and 833 species, respectively (Zhang et al., 2022). Remarkably, the most recent five-year period (2020–2024) yielded an annual average of 1 019 new species.

Species descriptions across historical periods

A total of 137 spider species were formally described between 1757 and 1799. This figure rose to 8 945 during the 19th century. The 20th century marked a major expansion in taxonomic activity, with 24 376 species recorded, approximately 2.7 times the 19th-century total. Within just the first 25 years of the 21st century (2000–2024), 19 159 additional species have been described, already comprising 78.6% of the 20th-century output (Figure 1B).

Taxonomic distribution among the most diverse families

The 10 most species-rich spider families were Salticidae (6 804), Linyphiidae (4 865), Araneidae (3 142), Theridiidae (2 584), Lycosidae (2 491), Gnaphosidae (2 478), Thomisidae (2 169), Pholcidae (2 024), Oonopidae (1 962), and Sparassidae (1 516) (Figure 1C; Supplementary Figure S1), collectively accounting for 57.1% of all described species to date. Taxonomic effort across historical periods closely reflected this concentration of diversity. For example, between 2000 and 2024, species newly described within these 10 families represented 69.4% of the total recorded for the same families during the entire 20th century.

Expanding the scope to the 20 most speciose families (each containing >500 species), these lineages comprised 73.8% of all known spider species. New descriptions within these families during 2000–2024 reached 75.2% of the corresponding 20th-century total. Among the 136 families currently recognized in the WSC, 59 recorded higher numbers of species descriptions between 2000 and 2024 than throughout the entire 20th century. The most pronounced increases occurred in Oonopidae and Pholcidae, which expanded by 1 279 and 933 species, respectively, relative to their 20th-century total. Furthermore, in 45 families, the number of species described during the first quarter of the 21st century exceeded the cumulative total documented over the preceding 243 years (Supplementary Table S2).

Species descriptions by geographic region

Over the past 268 years, Asia yielded the highest number of described spider species, followed by South America, Africa, North America, Oceania, and Europe, with no species recorded from Antarctica (Figure 2A–G; Supplementary Table S3). Across most regions, taxonomic activity remained limited through the 18th century and the first half of the 19th century, followed by a gradual acceleration beginning around 1870. A general slowdown occurred during the first half of the 20th century, after which the rate of species descriptions increased exponentially, particularly after 1950. This post-1950 increase was most pronounced in Asia, where species discovery outpaced that of all other continents. When normalized by land area, Oceania exhibited the highest density of described species, with 7.7 species per km2, followed by Europe (5.7), South America (5.1), Asia (4.0), North America (3.5), Africa (3.0), and Antarctica (0).

At the national level, the 10 countries with the highest numbers of validly described spider species were China, Australia, Brazil, USA, Mexico, Russia, South Africa, India, Indonesia, and Japan (Figure 2H–Q; Supplementary Table S4), collectively accounting for 59.9% of the global total. From 2000 to 2024, the number of species newly described from China doubled relative to its 20th-century total, representing the highest national increase. Australia ranked second with a 1.5-fold increase. During the 20th century, the United States led in both absolute number of new species described and rate of taxonomic growth.

Most prolific arachnologists

Eugène Simon (France) ranked as the most productive arachnologist, followed by Norman Platnick (USA), Shu-Qiang Li (China), Herbert Levi (USA), Tamerlan Thorell (Sweden), Willis Gertsch (USA), Bernhard Huber (Austria, based in Germany), Ralph Chamberlin (USA), Octavius Pickard-Cambridge (United Kingdom), and Cândido de Mello-Leitão (Brazil) (Figure 3; Supplementary Table S5). Among these researchers, Simon, Thorell, and Pickard-Cambridge were the leading contributors in the 19th century, describing 2 109, 1 120, and 872 species, respectively. In the 20th century, six of the 10—Simon (1 554), Levi (1 072), Gertsch (980), Chamberlin (918), Mello-Leitão (894), and Platnick (850)—each described more than 850 species. During the first quarter of the 21st century, Shu-Qiang Li emerged as the foremost contributor, describing 2 034 species, representing 10.6% of all new species documented between 2000 and 2024. Platnick (1 205) and Huber (921) were the next most prolific during this period.

Contributions of Chinese arachnologists

No spider species were described by Chinese researchers before the 20th century. During that century, they described 966 species, accounting for approximately 4.0% of all species described globally. Since 2000, their output has increased dramatically to 4 575 species—equivalent to 23.9% of the global total between 2000 and 2024—a 4.7-fold increase relative to the 20th century baseline (Figure 1B; Supplementary Tables S6). This expansion reflects the rapid growth of the Chinese arachnologist community in the 21st century. Of the 344 arachnologists who described new taxa worldwide in 2023, 94 were based in China, representing the largest national contingent and accounting for 27.3% of global descriptions that year, compared with 22.1% in 2022 and 23.4% in 2021 (Li et al., 2024). Chinese researchers also produced 540 of the 1 311 new taxa published globally in 2023, or 41.2% of the total, exceeding 37.0% in 2022 and 33.8% in 2021.

Collaboration between Chinese arachnologists and international partners has also expanded. Between 2022 and 2024, 17%–22% of publications by Chinese authors were co-authored with collaborators based outside China (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

Progress in global spider taxonomy research

In interpreting long-term trends in spider taxonomy since 1757, three qualitative perspectives warrant consideration alongside quantitative metrics. First, the emergence of arachnological taxonomy in the 18th century was closely tied to the expanding colonial and scientific frontiers in Europe, where the rise of natural history intersected with imperial exploration. Over time, taxonomic research has become globally distributed, though its development has remained uneven across regions. This expansion was largely driven by sustained institutional support—principally from governments funding taxonomists in museums, universities, and research centers—to document biodiversity. While contributions from non-institutional researchers have been documented, their role in species-level descriptions has remained comparatively minor. This longstanding reliance on public-sector funding now poses a structural vulnerability: as taxonomy is increasingly marginalized within national research agendas and dismissed as obsolete by bureaucratic institutions, the continuity of species discovery efforts may be at risk (Hong et al., 2022). The potential erosion of taxonomic capacity is especially paradoxical amid rising global imperatives for biodiversity assessment, ecological monitoring, and conservation policy—all of which require a robust and operational taxonomic infrastructure.

Second, the exponential rise in taxonomic productivity in the 21st century may be attributed to two transformative developments: the launch of the online, fully searchable WSC database and the widespread adoption of high-resolution photomicroscopy. The WSC, maintained by a six-member Editorial Board and an eight-member Expert Board, has greatly extended the foundational contributions of Platnick by creating a comprehensive, open-access, continuously updated repository that now serves as the central infrastructure for global spider taxonomy. This platform has effectively democratized access to taxonomic data, enabling both professional researchers and citizen scientists to retrieve critical literature instantaneously, thereby narrowing the gap in research capacity. In parallel, advances in photomicroscopy have streamlined the production of diagnostic images, allowing taxonomists to generate detailed visual documentation of species with unprecedented speed and precision. This shift has accelerated the pace of publication, facilitating higher throughput in species description. Despite relying on hand-drawn illustrations rather than photomicroscopy, Bernhard Huber achieved exceptionally high species output and ranks among the most prolific arachnologists. This example underscores the continued value of traditional illustration techniques and supports retaining them alongside photo-based methods, rather than abandoning them entirely in favor of phototaxonomy (Jäger, 2016).

Third, spider taxonomy has undergone a methodological transformation since its origins, when species were primarily characterized by brief textual descriptions of external somatic morphology, often with minimal attention to illustrations or microscopic details. Contemporary studies increasingly include high-resolution photomicroscopy, detailed anatomical drawings, and full-color images of copulatory structures, whole-body habitus, habitat context, and natural history. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images are now routinely used to resolve fine-scale morphological features, particularly of genitalia and surface textures. These developments reflect broader technological shifts. For instance, from 2008 to 2018, there was a marked rise in the use of digital and SEM images in published figures (Bond et al., 2022). By 2023, 96.2% of the 315 taxonomic papers published that year included digital imaging, and 18.7% incorporated SEM images (Li et al., 2024). In parallel, the field has shifted from predominantly faunistic checklists toward more integrated revisions and phylogenetic analyses, driven by the widespread adoption of cladistics and DNA sequencing (Kuntner, 2022). For example, while only 6% of spider taxonomic papers from 2008 to 2018 incorporated molecular data (Bond et al., 2022), this proportion rose to 16.8% in 2023 (Li et al., 2024). Additional methodological innovations continue to emerge. For example, Ding et al. (2022) introduced an artificial intelligence-driven deep neural network capable of recognizing sexual dimorphism, offering a new computational tool for trait-based classification in spider taxonomy.

Most prolific arachnologists

Eugène Simon remains the most influential and productive figure in the history of spider systematics (Platnick & Raven, 2013), credited with the description of 3 663 valid species—the highest number recorded by any arachnologist to date (Figure 3). He led global species descriptions in both the 19th and 20th centuries and played a pivotal role in shaping modern arachnology. Simon established 46 of the 136 spider families currently recognized in the WSC, accounting for nearly one-third of all valid families, and described approximately 20% of extant genera. His magnum opus, the four-volume Histoire Naturelle des Araignées (1864–1903), remains a cornerstone of systematic arachnology and continues to serve as an authoritative reference more than a century after publication (Platnick & Raven, 2013). Despite the brevity and limited illustration in many of his original species accounts—which often require reexamination of type material for accurate diagnosis—his contributions provided a coherent global framework for spider classification. Moreover, his extensive and globally sourced type collection constitutes a critical taxonomic repository and forms an irreplaceable component of the world’s spider biodiversity legacy.

Norman Platnick ranks as the second most productive arachnologist in recorded history, having described 2 055 spider species (Figure 3). His influence, however, extended far beyond taxonomic output. Platnick established the online WSC in 2000 and maintained biannual updates for two decades, transforming it into the definitive global reference for spider taxonomy. This enduring digital framework fundamentally reshaped the accessibility, standardization, and transparency of arachnological data, securing his legacy as a central architect of modern systematic research (Jäger et al., 2021; Prendini, 2020b).

As of 31 December 2024, Shu-Qiang Li had described 2 054 valid species, placing him just behind Platnick in cumulative output since 1757 (Figure 3). By early 2025, his total reached 2 063 species (WSC, 2025), establishing him as the second most prolific arachnologist in history and the foremost taxonomist of the 21st century. Beyond numerical achievement, Li has played a transformative role in advancing methodological innovation. He pioneered the integration of artificial-intelligence-assisted DNA barcoding for species identification (Zhang et al., 2008), helping to redefine the interface between molecular and morphological taxonomy. His leadership also extends to conservation science. As Co-Editor-in-Chief of Zoological Research: Diversity and Conservation, Li has promoted biodiversity documentation and species protection across Asia. He directed the first China Spider Red List assessment—identifying 41 threatened species—and championed the inclusion of Cyriopagopus hainanus (Liang, Peng, Huang & Chen, 1999) as a nationally protected species under China’s second-class protection framework.

The present analysis confirmed the leading roles of Eugène Simon and Norman Platnick as the most prolific contributors to spider taxonomy, consistent with earlier assessments by Platnick and Raven (2013). Two additional taxonomists, Shu-Qiang Li and Bernhard Huber, have now joined the global top ranks, occupying third and seventh positions, respectively (Figure 3A), with 2 034 and 921 valid species described since 2000. Several figures included in the earlier ranking by Platnick and Raven (2013) have been displaced due to subsequent taxonomic revisions. For example, 335 species described by Embrik Strand and 162 by Cândido de Mello-Leitão were synonymized or deemed nomina dubia between 2013 and 2024. Although Mello-Leitão still ranks tenth (down from sixth), these adjustments underscore the importance of rigorous taxonomic standards. Inadequate descriptions, insufficient comparison with type material, and missing or inaccessible type specimens were major factors contributing to invalidation. These findings reinforce the critical need for detailed morphological documentation, high-resolution illustrations, careful literature review, and strategic deposition of paratypes across multiple institutions to ensure long-term reproducibility and reliability in spider systematics.

China’s contributions of global spider taxonomy

As of 31 December 2024, China has documented 6 526 spider species, surpassing all other nations in known araneofaunal richness (Figure 2H). This exceptional species count may be attributed to various biogeographic and sociopolitical factors, including its vast territories; diverse climate zones; complex geographical formations—such as the mountain ranges of Southwest (Yao & Li, 2025; Zhang et al., 2023) and Northeast China (Lu et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2021)—that promote speciation; deep reservoir of trained taxonomic personnel, bolstered by the sustained output of a large, highly educated population; and scale and intensity of field collection efforts, which have further accelerated species discovery rates.

Four broad interrelated conditions have enabled the sustained growth of spider taxonomy in China. First, long-term public investment in higher education has expanded access to advanced instrumentation, laboratory infrastructure, and postgraduate training. Second, the national scientific research and development (R&D) strategy allocates increasing resources to basic science, with funding for fundamental research rising from 6% of total R&D expenditure in 2021 to 8% in 2025 under the current Five-Year Plan (Kennedy, 2025). Third, the environmental governance framework in China embeds biodiversity research within broader ecological conservation priorities, incentivizing foundational taxonomic work. Fourth, prolonged economic expansion has secured stable funding mechanisms through major grant agencies such as the National Natural Science Foundation of China (NSFC), provincial talent-development schemes, and biodiversity initiatives aligned with the Framework for Building an Ecological Civilization. Nonetheless, this favorable landscape may face attrition. A growing emphasis on publication metrics and impact-factor-driven funding criteria threatens to marginalize traditional taxonomic outputs, especially those centered on morphological species descriptions, thereby weakening future talent pipelines. As a result, research that does not integrate genomic tools or broader ecological narratives may receive limited institutional support.

Despite this shift, China remains positioned for significant near-term gains in species discovery. A RMB 10 million (approximately US$1.4 million) national project sponsored by the Ministry of Science and Technology aims to inventory canopy-dwelling spiders and other invertebrates in tropical and subtropical forests, mobilizing expert taxonomists for systematic fogging and high-throughput sampling. Preliminary evidence from Xishuangbanna Tropical Botanical Garden (XTBG) illustrates the potential magnitude of such efforts: species records of the XTBG region increased from fewer than 50 before 2006 to 936 by the end of 2024 (Mi et al., 2024). If research momentum is maintained and infrastructure scaled through the national canopy project, China’s recorded spider diversity is poised to expand rapidly beyond the current 6 526 species threshold.

CONCLUSION

The global trajectory of spider taxonomy has been profoundly shaped by the advent of electronic communication and the expansion of in-person scientific exchange at regional and international meetings. These developments have facilitated the circulation of ideas and catalyzed collaborative networks across continents. A pivotal milestone in this evolution was the 1960 gathering of pioneer arachnologists in Bonn, Germany, which laid the groundwork for the establishment of the Centre International de Documentation Arachnologique (CIDA) and its eventual successor, the International Society of Arachnology (ISA). Since then, 22 meetings have been convened under their coordination, fostering sustained international engagement in arachnological research. The 23rd International Congress of Arachnology, scheduled for 2026 in Kochi, India, marks the continuation of this tradition and reflects the growing prominence of Asian contributions to the field.
