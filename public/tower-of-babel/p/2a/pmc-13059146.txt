Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio is associated with short-term mortality in patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome: a meta-analysis
Authors: Danzhou Liu, Dansi Zhang, Anning Wang, Long Chen, Chengbo Xiang, Yuan Tian, Wu Zu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the association between neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and short-term mortality in patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) through a comprehensive meta-analysis of cohort studies.MethodsWe performed a comprehensive literature search across Pubmed, Web of Science, and Embase from inception of the databases to July 31, 2025. To quantify the relationship between the NLR and short-term mortality in patients with ARDS, Hazard Ratios (HRs) were pooled using random or fixed-effects models based on heterogeneity (I2 statistic). Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and the trim-and-fill method. Sensitivity analyses and subgroup analyses were conducted to assess the stability and potential influences of study characteristics.ResultsEight cohort studies involving 3396 participants were included. In studies treating NLR as a continuous variable, per-unit or standard deviation increase in NLR was associated with the higher risk of short-term mortality (HR = 2.25, 95% CI[confidence interval] 1.28–3.96, I2 = 92.1%). In studies categorizing NLR, higher NLR categories were associated with higher mortality (HR = 1.59, 95% CI 1.28–1.97, I2 = 60.0%). Funnel plot asymmetry suggested minor publication bias, which was corrected using the trim-and-fill method, yielding an adjusted HR of 1.91 (95% CI 1.01–3.60). Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these findings. Subgroup analyses showed that sample size, NOS score, and country of study significantly influenced the results. Meta-regression indicated that these factors collectively explained a substantial portion of the observed heterogeneity.ConclusionThis meta-analysis demonstrates that elevated NLR is associated with increased short-term mortality in ARDS patients. NLR may serve as a reliable prognostic marker. Future research should focus on validating these findings in diverse populations and exploring the underlying mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-12972-w.Keywords: Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, Short-term mortality, Acute respiratory distress syndrome, Meta-analysis, Cohort study

Introduction

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a severe and life-threatening condition characterized by acute onset, bilateral pulmonary infiltrates, and hypoxemia that is not fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload [1, 2]. Despite advances in critical care medicine, ARDS remains associated with high mortality rates, significant morbidity, and substantial healthcare costs [3, 4]. Early identification of patients at high risk of poor outcomes is crucial for guiding clinical management and improving patient outcomes. In recent years, there has been growing interest in the potential use of easily accessible and cost-effective biomarkers to predict prognosis in patients with ARDS. One such biomarker that has garnered attention is the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR). The NLR is a readily available and inexpensive marker that reflects the balance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory immune responses [5, 6]. Elevated NLR has been associated with adverse outcomes in various inflammatory and infectious conditions, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [7], acute cholecystitis [8], sepsis [9], community-acquired pneumonia [10], and cardiovascular diseases [11–13]. However, the relationship between NLR and short-term mortality in patients with ARDS remains unclear and inconsistent across different studies.

To address this gap in knowledge, we conducted a comprehensive meta-analysis to synthesize the available evidence on the association between NLR and short-term mortality in patients with ARDS. This meta-analysis aims to provide a more robust and definitive answer to the question of whether NLR can serve as a reliable prognostic marker for short-term mortality in this patient population. By pooling data from multiple studies, we aim to increase statistical power and provide a more accurate estimate of the effect size, thereby informing clinical practice and future research directions.

Materials and methods

Literature search strategy

To identify relevant studies on adult patients with ARDS from inception of the databases to July 31, 2025, we performed an extensive literature search across Pubmed, Web of Science, and Embase. We employed the following MeSH terms: (“acute lung injury” OR “ARDS” OR “acute respiratory failure” OR “acute respiratory distress syndrome”) AND (“NLR”OR “neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio”) AND (“mortality” OR “prognosis”). In order to broaden our search, we also made use of Pubmed’s “related articles” feature and reviewed the reference lists of the articles we found. The studies we retrieved encompassed both prospective and retrospective research designs. Detailed description of database search strategy in Supplementary material Table 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The criteria for including studies in this meta-analysis were as follows: (1) Research involving adult inpatients diagnosed with ARDS and short-term (28–30 day) mortality; (2) Clear definition of ARDS and its management in line with international guidelines [14–16]; (3) Utilization of NLR as a prognostic indicator for ARDS; (4) Presence of Hazard Ratio (HR) and the corresponding 95% Confidence Interval (95% CI) that could be extracted from the study using Cox regression. The following exclusion criteria were applied: (1) Research focusing on neonatal or pediatric ARDS; (2) Studies involving animal experiments, systematic reviews, case reports, or correspondence; (3) Studies from which data could not be extracted. The process of applying inclusion and exclusion criteria for this meta-analysis adhered strictly to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [17].

Data extraction and quality assessment

For each study that was included, data extraction included first author, publication year, study design, country of the population, overall count of participants, mean age of population, male proportion of overall count (%), cut-off NLR value, time for mortality observation, time of sampling NLR analysis, HR, and the 95% CI for prognostic prediction and variables adjusted. Two separate researchers conducted data extraction, and any discrepancies were settled through dialogue and mutual agreement. In cases where disagreements persisted, senior authors were consulted to reach a resolution. The quality of the studies included was evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), which assessed the exposed cohort, comparability, outcome of interest, outcome assessment, and follow-up of the cohort. In this evaluation system, the highest possible score is 9 points, and studies with scores exceeding 6 points are deemed high quality [18].

Statistical method

To quantify the relationship between the NLR and short-term mortality in patients with ARDS, the HR and its corresponding 95% CI were employed as a comprehensive metric. In studies where the NLR was treated as a continuous variable, the HR for short-term mortality in ARDS patients was extracted for per-unit or standard deviation increase in NLR. For studies that classified the NLR into categories, the HR comparing short-term mortality in ARDS patients with the highest NLR levels to those with the lowest levels was extracted. The I2 statistic was used to assess heterogeneity among the included cohort studies. If the I2 value surpassed 50%, it indicated substantial heterogeneity, prompting the use of a random effects model to synthesize the HR data; otherwise, a fixed-effects model was applied [19]. The stability of the findings was evaluated through sensitivity analyses, which involved sequentially excluding each study one at a time [20]. Predefined subgroup analyses were carried out to explore how factors such as the total number of participants, the NOS score, and country of study might affect the association between the NLR and short-term mortality in ARDS patients. Subgroup analysis by study design was not performed due to insufficient prospective studies (n = 1). Meta-regression analysis was conducted to investigate the origins of heterogeneity. The potential for publication bias was examined by visually inspecting the symmetry of funnel plots [21]. When potential publication bias was present, the effect size was further assessed by using the trim-and-fill method for correction [22]. The meta-analysis and statistical analyses were conducted using R (4.2.2) software. A p-value below 0.05 or a 95% CI that did not include 1 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Literature search

As shown in Fig. 1, the database search was carried out in an organized manner. A total of 532 articles were initially obtained from PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase databases after removing duplicate records. During the initial screening of titles and abstracts, 456 articles were identified as irrelevant and excluded. This resulted in 76 articles being selected for full-text assessment. Following this, 68 additional articles were excluded due to inappropriate exposure conditions (n = 32), inappropriate outcome settings (n = 25), inappropriate study design (n = 6), and insufficient data (n = 5). Ultimately, eight cohort studies [23–30] were included in the meta-analysis.

Flowchart of the database search and study identification

Study characteristics and quality evaluation

Table 1 presents the characteristics of the eight cohort studies. Collectively, these studies involved 3396 participants. The studies were conducted in America and China. One study was prospective, while seven were retrospective, with all being published between 2017 and 2024. Five studies had more than 60% male participants, while the rest had less than 60%. Moreover, two studies had sample sizes over 500, while six studies had sample sizes under 500. The quality of these studies was appraised using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale, which has a maximum score of 9. The assessment results indicated that two studies received a score of 7, two achieved a perfect score of 8, and the remaining studies all scored 6. Therefore, four cohort studies were classified as high-quality (Table 1).

Main results

In the meta-analysis of seven studies where NLR was treated as a continuous variable, the combined findings revealed that for per-unit or standard deviation increase in NLR, there was a 125% increase in short-term mortality in ARDS patients (HR = 2.25; 95% CI 1.28–3.96; I2 = 92.1%) (Fig. 2A). This is consistent with the findings from three studies where NLR was categorized. In these studies, higher NLR categories were associated with higher short-term mortality in ARDS patients, with an HR of 1.59 (95% CI 1.28–1.97; I2 = 60.0%) when comparing the highest and lowest categories (Fig. 2B).

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

The sensitivity analysis, which involved sequentially removing each study and reassessing the meta-analysis, revealed that when the NLR was continuous variable, the pooled risk estimate for short-term mortality in ARDS patients fluctuated between 1.83 (95% CI 1.14 to 2.92) and 2.62 (95% CI 1.43 to 4.78) (Fig. 3). This indicates that the overall effect is still reliable even if individual studies are omitted.

Sensitivity analysis for the meta-analysis of the association between neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (continuous variable) and short-term mortality in patients with ARDS

The funnel plot exhibited a slight asymmetry upon visual inspection when NLR was evaluated as a continuous variable, suggesting the potential presence of minor publication bias (Fig. 4A). To assess the reliability of our findings, we employed the trim-and-fill method, with the results depicted in Fig. 4B. This method yielded an adjusted effect size of 1.91 (95% CI 1.01–3.60) following the addition of 1 studies Fig. 4C. The analysis indicated that publication bias exerted minimal impact, thereby affirming the robustness of our results.

Subgroup analysis

Stratification was carried out to explore how factors such as the total number of participants, the NOS score, and country of study. The analysis shows that studies with a sample size of 500 or more patients have a pooled HR of 1.04 (95% CI: 1.00, 1.08), indicating a 4% increase in hazard, while those with fewer than 500 patients have a pooled HR of 3.17 (95% CI: 1.81, 5.55) (Fig. 5A). Studies with a NOS score ≤ 6 and＞6 also demonstrate a significant association, with a pooled HR of 1.04 (95% CI: 1.00,1.09) and 3.73 (95% CI: 2.37,5.87) (Fig. 5B). Geographically, studies conducted in America have a pooled HR of 1.04 (95% CI: 1.00, 1.08), whereas studies in China show a significantly higher pooled HR of 3.17 (95% CI: 1.81, 5.55) (Fig. 5C).

Meta-regression

Throughout the meta-analysis, addressing confounding factors was essential to enhance the interpretability of the observed heterogeneity among studies. As our data synthesis via the random-effects model revealed significant heterogeneity, we employed meta-regression techniques. These methods allowed us to adjust for potential confounders and investigate the underlying causes of the heterogeneity. In our meta-regression analysis, which included variables, such as the number of participants, NOS score, and country of study, we found heterogeneity values (Tau2) of 0.2370, 0.1933, and 0.1648, respectively (Table 2). These values are considerably lower than the heterogeneity value of τ2 = 0.5006 obtained from previous data synthesis (Fig. 2A), with the country of study showing the most notable reduction. Thus, it can be deduced that the number of participants, NOS score, and country of study collectively explained approximately 52.66%, 61.39%, and 67.07% of the observed heterogeneity in this meta-analysis, respectively (Table 2). This suggests that these factors are significant contributors to the variability observed across the studies.

Discussion

This meta-analysis of cohort studies involving 3396 patients with ARDS demonstrates that elevated NLR is significantly associated with increased short-term mortality. Specifically, per-unit or standard deviation increase in NLR as a continuous variable was associated with a 125% increase in short-term mortality (HR = 2.25, 95% CI 1.28–3.96, I2 = 92.1%), while higher NLR categories were associated with higher mortality (HR = 1.59, 95% CI 1.28–1.97, I2 = 60.0%). These findings remained robust after sensitivity analyses and correction for potential publication bias using the trim-and-fill method. The results suggest that NLR may serve as a reliable prognostic marker for short-term mortality in ARDS patients. Future research should focus on validating these findings in diverse populations and exploring the underlying mechanisms.

The biological plausibility of our findings is rooted in the pathophysiological role of neutrophils and lymphocytes in ARDS. ARDS is characterized by a dysregulated inflammatory response in the lungs, with neutrophils being the primary effector cells. They infiltrate the alveolar spaces, releasing proteolytic enzymes, reactive oxygen species, and pro-inflammatory cytokines that directly contribute to epithelial and endothelial damage, increased vascular permeability, and diffuse alveolar damage [31–33]. Lymphocytes, particularly T-cells, play a more modulatory role; their relative depletion (lymphopenia) is a well-documented phenomenon in critical illness and is associated with a state of immune suppression or exhaustion [26, 34]. Therefore, a high NLR primarily reflects an intense pro-inflammatory state driven by neutrophilia, which may be further amplified over time if lymphopenia develops during the disease course [26]. As a baseline measurement, NLR may predominantly capture the initial neutrophilic inflammation, whereas longitudinal trends in NLR might better reflect the subsequent lymphopenic component. Nevertheless, even as a single time-point assessment, NLR serves as a robust integrated marker of inflammatory dysregulation, which appears to be a key determinant of survival in ARDS.

The substantial heterogeneity observed in our primary analysis (I2 = 92.1%) warrants careful interpretation. Our pre-specified subgroup analyses and meta-regression provided crucial insights into the sources of this heterogeneity. The most striking moderators were sample size and geographical location (country). Studies with larger sample sizes (n ≥ 500) and those conducted in America reported more conservative, albeit still significant, hazard ratios (HR ~ 1.04). In contrast, smaller studies and those from China demonstrated markedly higher effect estimates (HR = 3.17). This nearly threefold discrepancy raises critical concerns that the overall pooled effect (HR = 2.25) may be driven by smaller, potentially biased studies. Small studies are particularly vulnerable to publication bias and methodological limitations, and the observed funnel plot asymmetry further supports this hypothesis. Consequently, the conservative estimate from large studies (HR = 1.04) may more accurately reflect the true prognostic value of NLR. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in patient populations, healthcare systems, ARDS etiologies or variations in baseline characteristics and co-morbidities [23, 25–30]. Furthermore, the NOS score was a major contributor to heterogeneity, explaining approximately 61.39% of the variance in the meta-regression. This suggests that methodological quality, particularly in terms of cohort selection, comparability, and outcome assessment, profoundly influences the observed effect size [18]. The NLR, being derived from a routine, inexpensive, and rapidly available complete blood count, possesses significant clinical utility. It can be easily calculated at the bedside upon ICU admission, potentially aiding in early risk stratification. Identifying patients with a high NLR could help clinicians flag those at greatest risk for deterioration, prompting more aggressive monitoring or consideration for novel therapies. Furthermore, as suggested by one of the included studies [29], NLR might help identify ARDS patients who could benefit from corticosteroid therapy, aligning with the growing interest in personalized medicine in critical care.

Despite the robust findings, there are several limitations to this meta-analysis. First, the included studies were primarily from America and China, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Future research should focus on validating these findings in diverse populations, including those from other continents and ethnic groups. Second, there was significant heterogeneity among the studies, which was partly explained by factors such as sample size, NOS score, and country of study. This suggests that the predictive power of NLR may vary depending on these factors, and further research is needed to explore the reasons for this variability. Third, the studies included in this meta-analysis were mostly retrospective, which may introduce bias and limit the strength of the causal inferences that can be drawn. Prospective studies are needed to further investigate the dynamic changes in NLR over time and their prognostic value. Additionally, future research should explore the underlying mechanisms that link elevated NLR to increased mortality in ARDS patients, such as the role of specific inflammatory pathways or the impact of comorbid conditions [32, 35].

Conclusion

This meta-analysis demonstrates that elevated NLR is associated with increased short-term mortality in ARDS patients. NLR may serve as a reliable prognostic marker. Future research should focus on validating these findings in diverse populations and exploring the underlying mechanisms. Additionally, prospective studies are needed to further investigate the dynamic changes in NLR over time and their prognostic value. The findings of this meta-analysis have significant clinical implications and may help guide clinical management and improve patient outcomes in ARDS.
