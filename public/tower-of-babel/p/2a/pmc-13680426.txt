Clinical benefits of tirzepatide in patients with steatotic liver disease and cardiometabolic dysfunction
Authors: Bo-Wen Shiau, Wan-Hsuan Hsu, Chia-Chih Kuo, Po-Yu Huang, Ya-Wen Tsai, Jheng-Yan Wu, Ting-Hui Liu, Min-Hsiang Chuang, Chih-Cheng Lai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMetabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease affects more than 30% of adults globally, yet effective treatment options remain limited. Tirzepatide has shown promise in early clinical trials, but its real-world effectiveness with liver-related outcomes remains uncertain.MethodsUsing TriNetX Global Collaborative Network, adults with steatotic liver disease (SLD) and cardiometabolic dysfunction were identified between June 1, 2022, and April 25, 2025. Individuals newly prescribed tirzepatide were propensity score–matched 1:1 to controls not receiving tirzepatide. The primary outcome was major adverse liver outcomes (MALO), defined as decompensated liver events, hepatocellular carcinoma, or liver transplantation.ResultsAmong 54,882 matched individuals, tirzepatide was associated with a lower incidence of MALO compared to the control group (HR, 0.32; 95% CI, 0.28-0.37). Tirzepatide use was associated with reductions in composite decompensated liver events (HR, 0.31; 95% CI, 0.26-0.36), esophageal variceal bleeding (HR, 0.39; 95% CI, 0.26-0.58), hepatic encephalopathy (HR, 0.27; 95% CI, 0.21-0.34), ascites-related complications (HR, 0.28; 95% CI, 0.23-0.33), hepatocellular carcinoma (HR, 0.36; 95% CI, 0.25-0.53), and liver transplantation (HR, 0.16; 95% CI, 0.08-0.33). Additionally, tirzepatide was associated with lower risks of all-cause mortality (HR, 0.22; 95% CI, 0.18-0.28), major adverse cardiac events (HR, 0.46; 95% CI, 0.40-0.52), and major adverse kidney events (HR, 0.26; 95% CI, 0.22-0.32).ConclusionsIn this retrospective study, tirzepatide use was associated with substantially lower risks of liver-related complications among patients with SLD and cardiometabolic dysfunction, supporting the need for prospective validation of its potential hepatic benefits.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), formerly known as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), is characterized by hepatic steatosis in the presence of one or more cardiometabolic risk factors1. It has emerged as the most prevalent chronic liver condition worldwide, affecting over 30% of adults and imposing significant clinical and economic burdens1–3. The global age-standardized disability-adjusted life years attributable to MASLD increased by 81.21% between 1990 and 2021, with mortality rates more than tripling during this period2. This progressive disorder leads to cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, and liver transplantation while concurrently elevating cardiovascular and metabolic risks4. Despite its substantial health impact, current management remains limited to lifestyle modifications, with limited pharmacotherapies specifically targeting MASLD pathogenesis in most regions1,5. While emerging therapies such as resmetirom primarily target liver-specific pathways6, MASLD is increasingly recognized as a systemic metabolic disorder. Metabolic therapies with established cardiometabolic benefits may therefore offer broader therapeutic potential. Tirzepatide, a dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist with pleiotropic metabolic effects beyond glycemic control, has recently emerged as a promising candidate7; however, its clinical impact in individuals with MASLD remains incompletely characterized in real-world populations.

In addition to its efficacy in improving blood glucose levels and promoting weight loss, tirzepatide has shown promise in addressing a range of conditions, including cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, and obstructive sleep apnea8–15. Emerging evidence also suggests its potential benefit in patients with MASLD. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that tirzepatide reduces hepatic fatty acid uptake by downregulating CD36/FABP pathways, enhances cholesterol excretion through upregulation of ABCG5/8, and improves mitochondrial-lysosomal function16. Furthermore, a phase 2 clinical trial involving individuals with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) and moderate-to-severe fibrosis reported a dose-dependent therapeutic effect: 44–62% of patients receiving tirzepatide achieved MASH resolution without fibrosis worsening at 52 weeks, compared to 13% in the placebo group17. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of tirzepatide in liver-related metabolic disorders and warrant further investigation in broader clinical settings.

However, despite these encouraging results, the clinical impact of tirzepatide in patients with MASLD has not been fully characterized in real-world settings. Most of the current evidence is derived from preclinical experiments or early-phase clinical trials16,17, and real-world data on its effectiveness and safety in MASLD populations are still scarce. Therefore, we conducted a multi-institutional retrospective study using propensity score matching (PSM) to evaluate the association between tirzepatide use and clinical outcomes in individuals with MASLD.

In this study, tirzepatide use is associated with substantially lower risks of major adverse liver outcomes, all-cause mortality, major adverse cardiac events, and major adverse kidney events among patients with steatotic liver disease and cardiometabolic dysfunction.

Methods

Data source

This retrospective cohort study utilized the TriNetX Research Network (Cambridge, MA, USA), a federated, multi-institutional platform that offers real-time access to anonymised electronic health records (EHRs) from participating healthcare organizations (HCOs). The TriNetX database consolidates de-identified clinical data from over 100 million patients globally, including academic medical centers, regional hospitals, and outpatient care facilities. The database includes detailed information on demographics, diagnoses (based on International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification [ICD-10-CM] codes), procedures (classified using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision Procedure Coding System [ICD-PCS] or Current Procedural Terminology [CPT] codes), medications (documented by Veterans Affairs Drug Classification System and RxNorm codes), laboratory tests (standardized with Logical Observation Identifiers Names and Codes codes), and health care utilization data. Data analyses were conducted using statistical tools embedded within the TriNetX platform. Additional details on the structure and validation of the TriNetX network have been described previously 18.

Ethical statement

Ethical approval for using the TriNetX database in this study was granted by Chi Mei Hospital’s institutional review board (approval number: 11402-E02). Informed consent was waived because this study was conducted using only aggregated statistical summaries of deidentified information from TriNetX Research network. In conducting and reporting this research, we adhered to Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for observational studies.

Study population

We identified adult patients (aged ≥18 years) diagnosed with steatotic liver disease (SLD) and cardiometabolic dysfunction between June 1, 2022, and April 25, 2025. SLD with cardiometabolic dysfunction was defined as the presence of hepatic steatosis, in combination with at least one cardiometabolic risk factor. These risk factors included insulin resistance (determined by a diagnosis of type 2 diabetes (T2D), hemoglobin A1c [HbA1c] level ≥5.7%, use of insulin or glucose-lowering agents, or a fasting plasma glucose level ≥100 mg/dL), visceral obesity (defined as a body mass index [BMI] ≥25 kg/m² or a waist circumference ≥94 cm), hypertension (defined as a systolic blood pressure ≥130 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥85 mmHg, or the use of antihypertensive medication), low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C; defined as a level <40 mg/dL or the use of lipid-lowering therapy), and hypertriglyceridemia (defined as triglyceride levels ≥150 mg/dL)19,20. Specific diagnostic coding used to operationalize cohort identification is provided in Supplementary Data 1. To improve cohort specificity and align the study population with contemporary MASLD definitions, patients with other chronic liver diseases, including alcoholic liver cirrhosis, viral hepatitis, and autoimmune hepatitis, were excluded. The study period was selected to coincide with the approval of tirzepatide by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) on May 13, 2022.

Patients were excluded if they had major adverse liver outcomes (MALO), including hepatic decompensation (such as gastroesophageal variceal bleeding, hepatic encephalopathy, ascites, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, or hepatorenal syndrome), hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), or liver transplantation before the index date. To avoid confounding by pre-existing cardiovascular conditions, patients with a documented history of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), including acute myocardial infarction, ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), non–ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), cerebral infarction, or nontraumatic intracranial hemorrhage, were also excluded. Furthermore, to minimize misclassification of renal outcomes, individuals with stage 5 chronic kidney disease (CKD), end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), or those receiving dialysis at baseline were excluded from the analysis of major adverse kidney events (MAKE). To establish an incident user design, patients with any record of tirzepatide use prior to the index date were also excluded.

Patients in the tirzepatide group were defined as those who initiated tirzepatide during the study period. The control group included patients with SLD and cardiometabolic dysfunction who had no history of tirzepatide use at any time. For the tirzepatide group, the index date was defined as the date of first tirzepatide prescription. For the control group, index date was the first date received the diagnosis of SLD with cardiometabolic dysfunction. Coding used for patient selection was listed in Supplementary Data 1.

Propensity score matching

To account for potential confounders, we applied 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) using a greedy nearest-neighbor algorithm with a caliper width of 0.1 pooled standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score. The propensity score was estimated using logistic regression based on baseline demographics (age, sex, race, ethnicity), comorbid conditions (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, cirrhosis, chronic kidney disease), laboratory values (e.g., BMI, HbA1c, LDL, total cholesterol, Hb, blood pressure, eGFR), and medication use (e.g., antihypertensives, antidiabetic agents including GLP-1 RAs, SGLT2 inhibitors, insulin, and lipid-lowering drugs). Baseline characteristics, including medication use, were ascertained from records documented prior to the index date and were modeled as historical exposures to minimize misclassification of concurrent therapy. All covariates were obtained within the one-year period preceding the index date, and covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with values < 0.1 considered indicative of adequate balance. Coding details are in Supplementary Data 2.

Prespecified outcomes

The primary outcome was the incidence of MALO, defined as a composite of decompensated liver events (esophageal variceal bleeding, hepatic encephalopathy, ascites-related complications), HCC, and liver transplantation.

Secondary outcomes included the individual components of MALO. Other outcomes included all-cause mortality, MACEs, and MAKEs. Cardiovascular and kidney outcomes were prespecified to contextualize liver-related findings within the broader systemic effects of metabolic dysfunction and to allow comparison with established clinical outcomes previously reported in tirzepatide studies15,21,22 These events were identified using validated ICD-10 codes and CPT procedure codes. Coding used for outcomes identification is provided in Supplementary Data 3. To assess the potential impact of unmeasured confounding, we included sensorineural hearing loss and skin cancer as negative control outcomes, which are not biologically expected to be associated with the exposure. This approach helps establish a baseline for comparison and strengthens the validity of our findings. Coding used for negative control outcomes is provided in Supplementary Data 5.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses were conducted based on sex (female vs. male), age (18–64 years vs. ≥ 65 years), BMI (30–34.9 vs. 35–39.9 vs. 40–49.9 vs. 50–59.9, and ≥60 kg/m2), presence of coexisting comorbid conditions (type 2 diabetes, heart failure, cirrhosis, and chronic kidney disease). Coding used for subgroup identification is provided in Supplementary Data 4.

Statistics and reproducibility

Baseline characteristics of the tirzepatide and control groups were presented as means with standard deviations for continuous variables and as counts with corresponding percentages for categorical variables. Between-group comparisons were conducted using the χ² test for categorical variables and the independent two-sample t-test for continuous variables. To reduce confounding and achieve balance between cohorts, one-to-one propensity score matching (PSM) was performed using a greedy nearest-neighbor algorithm with a caliper width of 0.1 pooled standard deviations. Covariate balance after matching was assessed using standardized mean differences, with values < 0.1 indicating adequate balance, as evaluated through TriNetX’s built-in analytic functions.

After PSM, incidence rates were reported per 1000 person-years. Survival probabilities were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and comparisons between groups were made using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each outcome of interest. In addition, reverse Kaplan–Meier analysis was conducted in Stata version 18.0 using survival data derived from the primary Kaplan–Meier analysis to evaluate cumulative event probabilities. Stratified analyses were also performed in Stata version 18.0, based on preliminary matched cohort data exported from the TriNetX statistical module. Sensitivity analyses using the United States Collaborative Network and subgroup analyses were performed to assess the robustness and reproducibility of the findings across different populations and analytical settings.

Results

Patients’ selection

A total of 1,072,387 adults with SLD and cardiometabolic dysfunction were identified from 146 HCOs within the Global Collaborative Network of the TriNetX platform on April 25, 2025. After applying exclusion criteria, 763,181 individuals were included. Of these, 27,584 were newly prescribed tirzepatide, while 735,597 did not receive tirzepatide. Demographic features of included patients

Before PSM, individuals in the tirzepatide group were younger than those in the control group (52.1 ± 12.6 years vs. 53.4 ± 16.4 years); however, the age difference was small (SMD = 0.094). In contrast, several baseline characteristics demonstrated meaningful imbalance between groups, including higher prevalences of hypertensive disease (64.0% vs. 44.1%), diabetes mellitus (56.3% vs. 24.4%), and related complications, cirrhosis (8.1% vs. 5.3%), chronic lower respiratory disease (25.1% vs. 16.9%), and neoplasms (35.3% vs. 25.9%) in the tirzepatide group. Use of lipid-lowering agents, antihypertensive medications, and antidiabetic therapies was also more frequent, and cardiometabolic risk profiles were generally higher in the tirzepatide group. After PSM, baseline characteristics were well balanced between the tirzepatide and control groups, with standardized differences <0.1 for all variables (Tables 1–3). Secondary outcomes

For specific individual outcomes of MALO, compared to the control group, the tirzepatide group exhibited significantly lower risks of composite decompensated liver events (HR, 0.31; 95% CI, 0.26–0.36), including EV bleeding (HR, 0.39; 95% CI, 0.26–0.58), hepatic encephalopathy (HR, 0.27; 95% CI, 0.21–0.34), ascites-related complications (HR, 0.28; 95% CI, 0.23–0.33), HCC (HR, 0.36; 95% CI, 0.25–0.53), and liver transplant (HR, 0.16; 95% CI, 0.08–0.33) (Table 2).

Other outcomes

The use of tirzepatide was associated with a significantly reduced risk of all-cause mortality (HR, 0.22; 95% CI, 0.18–0.28), MACEs (HR, 0.46; 95% CI, 0.40–0.52), and MAKEs (HR, 0.26; 95% CI, 0.22–0.32), as shown in Table 4.

To assess for potential residual confounding, two negative control outcomes were examined. There was no significant difference between groups in the incidence of skin cancer (HR, 0.80; 95% CI, 0.61–1.04; log-rank p = 0.090) or sensorineural hearing loss (HR, 0.89; 95% CI, 0.77–1.03; log-rank p = 0.105), as presented in Supplementary Data 5.

Discussion

This retrospective study, which included over 54,000 individuals, provides large-scale real-world evidence regarding the clinical outcomes associated with tirzepatide use tirzepatide uses in adults with SLD and cardiometabolic dysfunction. Our findings suggest that tirzepatide is associated with improved clinical outcomes in this population. Overall, tirzepatide was associated with a 68% reduction in the rate of MALO compared to the control group. Specifically, it was associated with reductions of 69% in composite decompensated liver events, 61% in EV bleeding, 73% in hepatic encephalopathy, 72% in ascites-related complications, 64% in HCC, and 84% in liver transplant. These associations remained consistent across most subgroups, including age, sex, BMI categories, and various comorbidities. These findings suggest that tirzepatide may have clinical relevance in metabolically enriched SLD populations aligned with contemporary MASLD concepts, particularly given its association with lower risks of clinically significant hepatic complications.

Beyond liver-specific benefits, we found that tirzepatide was also associated with significantly lower rates of all-cause mortality, MACE, and MAKE, with relative reductions of 78, 54, and 74%, respectively, compared with the control group. For clinical interpretability, we examined absolute incidence differences for all-cause mortality. The observed rates (3.6 vs. 17.6 per 1000 person-years) correspond to an absolute rate difference of 14.0 fewer deaths per 1000 person-years, yielding an estimated number needed to treat of approximately 72 patients per year. These results align with prior studies demonstrating the clinical benefits of tirzepatide in individuals with various diseases or conditions15,22,23. In individuals with T2D, treatment with tirzepatide was associated with lower risks of all-cause mortality (HR, 0.58; 95% CI, 0.45–0.75), MACE (HR, 0.80; 95% CI, 0.71–0.91), and MAKE (HR, 0.54; 95% CI, 0.44–0.67), compared to GLP-1RA15. Among individuals with obesity, tirzepatide was associated with a significantly lower risk of all-cause mortality (HR, 0.31; 95% CI, 0.26–0.38), MACE (HR, 0.74; 95% CI, 0.67–0.82), and MAKE (HR, 0.38; 95% CI, 0.34–0.42), when compared with bariatric metabolic surgery23. In patients with obstructive sleep apnea, tirzepatide was similarly associated with reduced risks of all-cause mortality (HR, 0.44; 95% CI, 0.34–0.58), MACE (HR, 0.73; 95% CI, 0.62–0.86), and MAKE (HR, 0.43; 95% CI, 0.34–0.53)22. Our study extends these findings by demonstrating that tirzepatide may offer similar systemic benefits in individuals with MASLD, reinforcing its potential as a multifaceted agent for high-risk populations.

Although the retrospective nature of our study precludes mechanistic conclusions, several prior studies17,24,25 provide insights into the potential pathways underlying tirzepatide’s liver benefits. Preclinical study in diabetic mice have shown that tirzepatide attenuates hepatic steatosis through multiple mechanisms, including modulation of gut microbiota and bile acid metabolism24. Clinical trials have corroborated these findings. In a pivotal phase 2 trial involving participants with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) and moderate or severe fibrosis, tirzepatide demonstrated superior efficacy compared to placebo17. After 52 weeks of treatment, the proportion of participants achieving MASH resolution without worsening of fibrosis increased in a dose-dependent manner, with approximately 51–55% of tirzepatide-treated patients showing improvement of at least one fibrosis stage without MASH progression, compared to 30% in the placebo group17. Similarly, a substudy of the phase 3 SURPASS-3 trial showed that tirzepatide significantly reduced liver fat content, visceral adipose tissue volume, and abdominal subcutaneous fat volume compared to insulin degludec in patients with T2D25. Beyond improvements in liver histology, tirzepatide offers broader metabolic benefits that support liver health indirectly. It has been shown to induce substantial weight loss, enhance insulin sensitivity, and improve glycemic control8–10,12,23,26–28. Together, these findings suggest that tirzepatide’s benefits in MASLD may be driven by both direct hepatic mechanisms and broader metabolic improvements.

This study has several strengths. By leveraging the TriNetX network database, we analyzed data from a large and diverse patient population, enhancing the statistical power and generalizability of our findings. The use of real-world clinical data increases the translational relevance of our results. PSM ensured well-balanced baseline characteristics between the tirzepatide and control groups, minimizing confounding. This comprehensive and methodologically rigorous approach supports the robustness and clinical applicability of our findings.

However, this study has several limitations. First, contemporary MASLD could not be fully operationalized using the available EHR data. Although we required evidence of hepatic steatosis in the presence of cardiometabolic risk factors and excluded other well-defined chronic liver diseases, detailed information on alcohol consumption was not uniformly available across the TriNetX network. Therefore, the study population may not strictly represent consensus-defined MASLD and should instead be interpreted as a metabolically enriched population within the broader spectrum of SLD. This limitation may have permitted residual etiologic overlap, particularly with metabolic and alcohol-related liver disease (MetALD), and could affect the interpretability and generalizability of the findings. Second, although propensity score matching (PSM) balanced observed baseline characteristics between groups, residual confounding from unmeasured variables cannot be excluded, and the retrospective observational design precludes definitive causal inference. Third, the dataset lacked granular information on disease severity, which may have resulted in misclassification or unmeasured heterogeneity. To partially address this, subgroup analyses among individuals with cirrhosis were performed and yielded findings consistent with the primary analysis. Several additional limitations are inherent to the use of large-scale EHR data. Disease and outcome identification relied on diagnostic and procedural coding within a federated EHR network, which may introduce misclassification associated with administrative data while enabling analyses with greater population scale, statistical power, and external validity at the expense of detailed clinical phenotyping. In addition, completeness of clinical variables varied across participating healthcare organizations, reflecting differences in routine clinical documentation. Cohort identification, therefore, relied on predefined operational criteria that permitted alternative clinical pathways to satisfy inclusion definitions. The index date differed between treatment groups, with tirzepatide users indexed at treatment initiation and controls indexed at diagnosis of SLD. This indexing strategy was adopted to emulate an incident-user design, a widely accepted approach to reduce biases related to prior treatment exposure in observational studies; however, residual time-related bias, including immortal time bias, cannot be entirely excluded. The primary analysis utilized the Global Collaborative Network to maximize sample size; however, geographic-specific analyses were not feasible because of the federated structure of the TriNetX platform, precluding evaluation of regional differences in prescribing patterns and outcomes. A sensitivity analysis using the US Collaborative Network yielded results consistent with the primary analysis, supporting the robustness of the findings. Nonetheless, prospective studies incorporating detailed clinical, geographic, and histological data are needed to further validate and refine these observations.

Conclusion

Our real-world data indicate that tirzepatide is associated with substantially lower risks of major liver-related, cardiovascular, renal, and all-cause mortality in a large metabolically enriched SLD population aligned with contemporary MASLD concepts. These findings extend the growing body of evidence supporting the systemic associations observed with tirzepatide and provide clinically relevant real-world insights within the broader MASLD framework, a field with increasing global burden and limited treatment options. Given the observational nature of this study, prospective randomized studies are needed to confirm these findings and further evaluate the long-term efficacy and safety of tirzepatide in this population.
