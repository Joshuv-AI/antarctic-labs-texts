Impact of inflammatory bowel diseases on the intimate lives of youths: Creating a brief screening questionnaire
Authors: Alexandre Mancheron, Agnès Dumas, Christine Martinez‐Vinson, Aurélie Bourmaud
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe objectives were to describe the challenges faced by youths with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) in their intimate lives before the onset of sexual activity and develop a screening tool to detect early difficulties.MethodsWe conducted a cross‐sectional qualitative study via semi‐structured interviews with 15–19‐year‐olds diagnosed with IBD. To be eligible for inclusion, these adolescents had to respond having not yet started their sexual life. This led to a collaborative development of a detection tool with a multidisciplinary team to evaluate adolescents' intimate life stage and to early screen difficulties.ResultsFifteen subjects were recruited. Among them eight were males, 10 were followed for Crohn's disease while five were followed for ulcerative colitis. The median age was 16 years. Nine out of 15 teenagers felt unprepared and reluctant for intimate relationship. Teenagers who have started their romantic journey, expressed satisfaction with their intimate lives, but the possibility of experiencing diarrhea or abdominal pain during intimate moments may be a concern.ConclusionThe study highlights a general reluctance among adolescents with IBD to engage in intimate relationships, and IBD symptoms may be a concern for intimate life. It resulted in the creation of a brief detection tool allowing clinicians to early screen and address intimate life issues, fostering better support for this vulnerable group's challenges.

INTRODUCTION

Inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD) are becoming increasingly common in developed countries such as France. 1 These diseases frequently occur in children, with an estimated 25% of new cases occurring before the age of 20. 2 The impact of IBD on the quality of life of adolescents and young adults can be significant. 2 , 3 Youth with IBD reports more depressive disorders and poorer social functioning than healthy youths. 3 , 4

The discovery of intimacy takes place in adolescence. This period, between the teenager's discovery that they are a sexual being and the start of their active sexual life with one or more partners, is a pivotal time in the process of sexual activity learning. Disturbances in adolescence, such as chronic illnesses, can result in long‐term sexual and relational challenges. IBD may prevent some young people from pursuing an intimate relationship. 5 However, while some studies have already looked at the impact of IBD on sexual relationships, such as vulvovaginal comfort 6 , 7 and erectile dysfunction, 8 , 9 the literature is poor about the impact of the disease on the process of discovery of intimacy in young people, not sexually active yet. Moreover, addressing sexuality during a medical consultation with an adolescent or young adult can be challenging. 10

(1) To identify the effect of the disease on patients' first stages of intimacy and sexuality, and its effect on their expectations about their future sex life and (2) to develop a screening tool for possible sexual and intimate difficulties, we conducted an exploratory qualitative study. The objective of this study was more precisely to analyze perceptions, expectations and difficulties anticipated by adolescents and young adults with IBD in their sexual and intimate lives, before the onset of sexual activity. The secondary objective was to develop a screening tool to detect early sexual and intimate difficulties among these patients, to be used in clinical practice, based on interviews results.

METHODS

We conducted an observational cross‐sectional qualitative study among a sample of teenagers followed for IBD, consisting of semi‐structured interviews, between May 2023 and September 2023. In accordance with French Law, this study was approved by the local ethics committee of the Robert Debré Hospital, Paris (Number 2022‐636bis, accorded the 2023/01/03). This manuscript follows the checklist “Consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research.”

Pediatric patients and settings

Patients were recruited in the pediatric gastroenterology department of a pediatric university hospital.

Inclusion criteria consisted in the following: (1) Being aged 15–19 years; (2) being followed‐up for an IBD in our center; and (3) no prior sexual relations. We defined a sexual relationship as a sexual act performed with at least one partner, whether penetration had taken place, and, above all, whether the act was significant for the young person.

Exclusion criteria were refusal to participate or bad French understanding.

A prescreening phase took place where a pediatric gastroenterologist would inquire during a day hospital visit if the adolescent was willing to discuss sexuality, and if they had already engaged in sexual relations. This request was made verbally, in the absence of parents and with tact. If not, the adolescent was then informed about the study and asked if they wished to participate. Eligible and willing adolescents were subsequently included based on the logistical possibilities of both the adolescents and the study's sexologist.

Interview grid

An interview grid was created by investigators, including: two physicians, one sexologist, and one sociologist. It investigated following themes: (1) Discovery of IBD and impact of the diagnosis and initial symptoms on oneself; (2) impact of IBD on one's family and friend; (3) personal romantic and sexual (with oneself or others) history; and (4) impact felt or projected on one's romantic and sexual future life. Even though the study did not focus on the impact of IBD on daily life, it seemed important to mention it first to put the subject at ease and better understand the context of their romantic life. At the end of the interview, adolescents were asked if they thought that the disease could impact their future sexual life in different areas. Those areas were elicited from questionnaires evaluating sexual function in adults (International Index of Erectile Function and the Sexual Function Questionnaire 28 11 , 12 and adapted to the teenage population and the interview format. The adolescents were asked if they thought that, when they would become sexually active, they would be concerned by ability to experience pleasure or orgasm, to experience sexual desire, to achieve an erection or accept penetration, to provide pleasure to the partner, to undress in front of partner, to feel tiredness, and to fear about experiencing sexual pain. Considering that diarrhea, gas, and abdominal pain can manifest even in patients deemed to be in a state of equilibrium and given that these symptoms are recognized to impact the quality of life of patients, two more topics were probed: Apprehension of experiencing abdominal pain and fear of encountering diarrhea or gas emissions during sexual intercourse.

The semi‐directive interview guide was tested and modified after the first interview, with a reversal of questions order, to ensure a greater fluidity in the open exchange between the interviewer and the patients.

Individual interviews

Individual interviews were conducted by an experienced physician specialized in sexology. These interviews took place in a quiet, intimate setting in a consulting room, with no parents. The interviews were conducted using the interview grid and were voice‐recorded so that they could be transcribed. The interviews were based on a semi‐structured open‐ended question format to address all aspects of daily life and intimacy that may be affected by the disease. Content analysis of transcribed interviews was made until saturation of themes, which is believed to have been reached when repeating interviews did not provide any new information in relation to the data already acquired. A number of 12 interviews is considered as permitting to reach data saturation. 13

Analysis and creation of a brief first‐contact detection tool on sex life inquiries

Data analysis was done by two members of the research team (A. M. and A. D.). The coders read each transcribed interview separately and then gathered to discuss their note, reflections, and identify emerging themes. The two coders then gathered to establish a preliminary codebook to start coding. The analysis was inductive and larger themes were identified from a preliminary coding of the data. Defining the final themes was an iterative process. Saturation is believed to have been reached as all themes identified were present both in early and later interviews.

Based on the qualitative results, the same multidisciplinary team assembled to construct a rapid questionnaire. To do this, the team used the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer questionnaire construction methodology (Guidelines for Developing Questionnaire Modules, 2011 Fourth edition). Four questions were drafted and validated by consensus, making it possible to construct the first brief first‐contact detection tool on sex life enquiries. These four questions were translated in English in a backward–forward process by two native English speakers who were also bilingual in French.

RESULTS

Description of the population

During the recruitment phase, 54 teenagers receiving treatment for IBD were queried about their willingness to discuss aspects of their sexual life. Out of them, 51 (94.4%) consented to engage in such discussion, with 10 (19.6%) revealing they had already engaged in sexual activity. Forty‐one (80.4%) had not yet initiated sexual activity. Thirty‐one were open to speaking with the sexologist. Ultimately, 15 teenagers participated, achieving data saturation (Supporting Information: Data 1). Among these 15 subjects, eight (53.3%) were males, 10 (66.7%) were followed for Crohn's disease, while five (33.3%) were followed for ulcerative colitis. Practically two‐thirds of those young individuals did not feel prepared to embark on their relationship journey, regardless of their disease (n = 9/15) (Table 2, Verbatims 1A–1B).

Main theme 2Impact of IBD on intimate, romantic, and sexual life

Impact of IBD on intimate, romantic, and sexual life

For 3/15 youths, the disease could affect body image and make seduction more challenging, especially when there were scars due to surgery (Table 2, Verbatim 2A). The restoration of a pleasing body image appeared significant to some young individuals (n = 1/15) (Table 2, Verbatim 2B). Weight loss and thinness, particularly during the initial flare‐up, significantly bother many youths (n = 12/15) (Table 2, Verbatim 2C). The decrease in sport performance can be bothersome for adolescents (n = 5/15) (Table 2, Verbatim 2D).

They figured out how symptoms could affect their intimate life

Some teenagers said they were embarrassed or afraid of being embarrassed by certain symptoms of the illness. They may be afraid of experiencing abdominal pain, especially during a sexual activity (n = 4/15) (Table 2, Verbatim 2E–2F). Taking analgesia before an intimate moment may be a solution for some teenagers to counter the impact of IBD (n = 3/15) (Table 2, Verbatim 2G). Avoiding intimate moments or sexual relationships out of fear of a disease flare‐up can also be a coping mechanism (Table 2, Verbatim 2H). Diarrhea was also a cause for concern (n = 4/15) (Table 2, Verbatim 2I). They thought that having easy access to toilets and taking antidiarrheal medication could help them (Table 2, Verbatim 2J). The possibility of diarrhea could prevent some of them from engaging in certain sexual positions (n = 1/15) (Table 2, Verbatim 2K).

They imagined how to cope with the impact of illness on romantic life

Practically two‐third of adolescents believed that the illness may have a very limited impact on their intimate life (n = 9/15) (Table 2, Verbatim 2L). However, it could be more complicated for other subjects (n = 4/15) (Table 2, Verbatim 2M). Communication and closeness seemed important to cope with the impact of the illness on intimacy for many young people (n = 10/15) (Table 2, Verbatim 2N). However, discussing their disease, especially at the beginning of a relationship, could be challenging (n = 2/15) (Table 2, Verbatim 2O–2P).

They anticipated the impact of the disease on their first sexual experience

Concerning their anticipations of their first sexual experience, twothird of the teenagers believed that the illness would not hinder their ability to experience pleasure and fully engage in this initial encounter (n = 10/15) (Table 2, Verbatim 2Q). Those who already had a nonsignificant sexual experience reported finding pleasure (n = 2/15) (Table 2, Verbatim 2R). Nevertheless, some young people thought that the illness might still be a hindrance (Table 2, Verbatim 2S).

For most of these teenagers, their imagination about their first time was very limited, and they did not discuss how they envisioned experiencing their first time with their future partner.

They had questions about sexuality and reproductive functions

Several young individuals had concerns about their intimate life, sexuality, and how the illness may affect their reproductive functions (n = 4/15) (Table 2, Verbatim 2T). Gaining knowledge helped ease the worries (Table 2, Verbatim 2U). Sex education classes in school may serve as an information source (n = 2/15) (Table 2, Verbatim 2V). Parents and friends were not mentioned as a main source of information (Table 2, Verbatim 2W).

Creation of brief first‐contact detection tool on sex life inquiries

Figure 1 presents an English version of the brief first‐contact detection tool designed to assess the level of maturity in intimate and sexual life and detect if the youth may need counseling or consultation about their intimate and sex life. Given the substantial number of adolescents who had not yet embarked on their sexual journeys and appeared disinterested, our approach prioritized identifying those who had questions and potentially difficulties to cope with their disease in their romantic and sex life. This early screening will avoid provoking unaware and distressing questions in adolescents who have not yet made the transition to the stage of emotional, psychological and sexual development where these questions may arise. Second, it was deemed essential to inquire about the initiation of sexual activity and evaluate distress levels surrounding sexual encounters, to address the topic if needed, as soon as possible.

DISCUSSION

This qualitative study was the first to explore challenges teenagers with IBD can encounter in their intimate lives. The initial noteworthy observation is that a significant portion of the surveyed youths, aged 16 on average, seemed to display limited interest in matters of sexuality. A third of them (5/15) have never engaged in an intimate relationship and exhibited no inclination to do so, whereas n = 6/15 never experienced an intimate relationship. In the general population, in their 15th year, 80% of boys and 79% of girls report having previously dated someone 14 and the median age at first sexual intercourse was 17.6 for women aged 18–19 and 17.2 for 18–19‐year‐old men. 15 This sample therefore reflects a later onset of preoccupation, intimacy and sexual activity than that observed in the general adolescent population. Yet, it is widely acknowledged that it's typically short‐term intimate relationships that pave the way for the initiation of intimate experiences, ultimately culminating in more committed partnerships. 16 When it comes to sexuality, as in other areas, teenagers need to compare themselves to the norm (even if the notion of norm is far removed from sexual models): they repeatedly express the need to be “like the others.” The fact that IBD patients do not have the same sexual characteristics and behavior as their contemporaries is a factor of vulnerability in their development as adults. Thus, commencing one's sexual activity later in life could have consequences for them, including lower self‐esteem, poorer mental health, and less proficient social skills concerning intimate relationships in adulthood. 17 The only reasons identified for these adolescents appearing disinterested in having an intimate life are those they shared with us during the interviews. As mentioned in the article, some adolescents reported not being interested in having an intimate life at the time of the interview (for reasons that may not be related to the disease) or fearing the impact of IBD symptoms on their future intimate and sexual life.

The disease's symptoms can also have an impact on intimate relationships. A European study estimated that 35% of patients suffering from IBD were unable to continue an intimate relationship because of the disease, and 17% experienced an intimate breakup due to the disease. 5 Diarrhea and toilet accessibility, fatigue, impairment of body image, and abdominal pain appear to be the most challenging elements in the daily lives of patients with IBD. 5 , 18 In dealing with these challenges, most of interviewed adolescents appeared to place importance on complicity and communication with their partner.

All adolescents appreciated the participation in the study and the discussion with a sexologist. For most of them, it was the first time they discussed sexuality with a healthcare professional. Healthcare professionals often infrequently address the topic of sexuality with adolescents suffering from chronic illness, even though these adolescents may have questions or have already begun their sexual lives. 10 , 19 Despite their inquiries, adolescents rarely initiate discussions about sexuality during a medical appointment. 20 , 21 However, healthcare professionals may find it challenging to broach the topic of sexuality, fearing it is a taboo subject or feeling unprepared to answer adolescents’ questions. 10 , 19 , 21 It also seems necessary to prepare the adolescent for discussing sexuality during a medical consultation, especially when it is not led by a sexuality expert. 21

To aid clinicians in more systematically addressing the issue of intimacy and sexuality in this population at a higher risk of facing challenges in their sexual lives and to prepare the adolescent to discuss this matter, it is possible to use questionnaires, but adequate and tailored to the population's needs ones. 22 The questionnaire we created can allow the clinician to assess the adolescent's current sexual status, gather quick insights into the adolescent's difficulties with their present intimate and sexual life and either provide counseling by themselves or refer the adolescent to a professional trained in sexual health care. This questionnaire also serves to avoid posing questions that could be perceived as intrusive or shocking for an adolescent who may be less knowledgeable in their intimate and sexual life, especially in a population where many adolescents seem to commence their sexual lives later than the general population. 22 However, our proposed questionnaire requires validation to ensure psychometric accuracy. The significance of this study lies in being, to our knowledge, the first examination of the intimate and sexual lives of adolescents with IBD at the dawn of their sexual lives. The findings indicate that adolescents seem disinterested in the idea of pursuing intimate or sexual relationships at an age when such concerns typically arise. However, it's important to note that this study has a small sample size due to rapid data saturation and was conducted at a single center. According to Guest et al., 13 data saturation can be achieved after including around a dozen subjects when the study population is homogenous. In our case, most adolescents were not very interested in sexuality and had a limited imagination regarding their future sexual life, so data saturation was reached quickly. Despite the fact that this was a single‐center study, we believe we have ensured a moderatum generalization, 23 with patients having characteristics similar to the same age patients studied in the literature. These patients also came from a wide geographical area of France, to access the University IBD expert center, with different socio‐demographic backgrounds. However, this was not a quantitative study, so we do not claim statistical external validity. Therefore, it seems important to conduct quantitative studies on the intimate lives of adolescents with IBD in other settings to ensure the generalizability of our results. One of our inclusion criteria was believing they had not yet started their sexual life. This criterion made it easier to address the topic with young individuals, but it also resulted in recruiting participants who had, in fact, already engaged in sexual acts (oral sex and touching). Indeed, many adolescents seem to believe that oral sex is less risky, more common among their age group, and socially less involved than vaginal sex. 24 This led to greater diversity within our population, with youths at different stages of experience and potentially reflecting on sexuality differently. This diversity is also one of the strengths of this study: it reflects the diversity of beliefs, attitudes and experiences of today's teenagers, emphasizing that their thresholds are fluid and nonnormative, as in the models of modern sexology.

CONCLUSION

This qualitative descriptive study suggests that adolescents aged 15–18 and suffering from IBD seemed mostly uninterested in the prospect of engaging in intimate and sexual relationships. They expressed the possibility of experiencing diarrhea or abdominal pain if they engage in an intimate moment and felt concerned about it. We used this information to develop a brief first‐contact detection tool to assess these adolescents' interest in their intimate and sexual lives, aiming to facilitate a nonintrusive discussion for healthcare providers involved in their care.
