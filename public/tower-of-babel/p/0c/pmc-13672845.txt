ABSTRACT
Key Points•Hi-C sequencing for 159 patients with DLBCL reveals genome-wide rearrangements and fusion genes, and shows high sensitivity and specificity.•Hi-C sequencing detects all gene rearrangements for precise diagnosis, prognostication, and optimal treatment of large B-cell lymphomas.

Introduction

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most common lymphoma. It is an aggressive malignancy with heterogeneous pathogenesis and variable prognosis. Approximately 60% of patients can be effectively managed with standard therapies (including first-line R-CHOP [rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone]),1 whereas other patients have refractory or relapsed disease with poor clinical outcomes.

DLBCL diagnosis relies on histological examination, immunophenotyping, cytogenetics, and molecular analyses to discriminate it from other large B-cell lymphoma subtypes with overlapping morphological features. The World Health Organization (WHO) classification2 includes 18 subtypes of large B-cell lymphoma, many of which are defined by rearrangements involving specific genes, including DLBCL/high-grade B-cell lymphoma (HGBCL) with MYC and BCL2 rearrangements, ALK-positive large B-cell lymphoma, large B-cell lymphoma with IRF4 rearrangement, and HGBCL with 11q aberrations.3 Genetic testing methods for lymphoma diagnosis include fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and, sometimes, next-generation sequencing (NGS). FISH is the current gold standard for detecting specific genetic rearrangements such as MYC, BCL2, and BCL6 translocations, which are critical for diagnosing DLBCL/HGBCL with double-hit or triple-hit features.2 However, FISH can only detect the rearrangements that it targets, which is a significant limitation as the diagnostic biomarkers increase. Moreover, FISH does not detect rearrangements when the breakpoints are outside the FISH break-apart probes (Figure 1A) and when nonreciprocal translocations leave break-apart probes intact. In a recent study,4 ∼19% of HGBCL with MYC and BCL2 rearrangements were cryptic to MYC and/or BCL2 FISH probes. Unbalanced break-apart FISH is also common; in 1 study, 13% of HGBCL with MYC and BCL2 rearrangements5 showed loss of 1 probe, which is difficult to interpret as deletion, complex translocation, or other alterations. Furthermore, the inability of break-apart FISH to determine the partner gene of a translocation, corearranged genes proximal to the breakpoints,6 and the fine structure of gene fusions7 lead to uncertainty in the clinical relevance of detected translocations. Conventional NGS assays for DNA samples have been used to identify mutations across targeted gene panels.13,14 Although comprehensive genomic profiling, which integrates both DNA and RNA sequencing, can identify structural variants, its sensitivity and reliability are insufficient. Importantly, 67% to 89% of non-IG heavy locus (IGH) MYC fusions detected by FISH are missed by comprehensive genomic profiling.15,16

High-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) sequencing is a novel DNA-based NGS assay that can detect chromosomal structural variations in formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue samples, based on sequencing linked pairs of reads that occur near one another in 3-dimensional space and linear genomic sequences (schematic illustration in Figure 1; supplemental Figure 1). Hi-C sequencing has been applied to diverse cancer types and has shown promise in detecting previously unidentified gene fusions and rearrangements, as well as important variants missed by other clinical testing modalities.17, 18, 19, 20 Here, the FFPE-based Hi-C method was applied to an unselected case series of DLBCLs to determine its sensitivity and specificity for detecting clinically relevant fusions and rearrangements.

Methods

Patients

From a consecutive case series of de novo DLBCL assembled by the International DLBCL R-CHOP Consortium Program, 159 patients with FISH data for MYC, BCL2, and BCL6 rearrangements21 and uniformly treated with R-CHOP after diagnosis were selected. All FFPE specimens were collected between April 1999 and October 2009 as part of the routine diagnostic workflow. FISH, immunohistochemical staining (IHC), and multiplex fluorescent IHC (mfIHC) were performed on serial 4-μm FFPE tissue microarrays constructed for the study cohort using experimental techniques and scoring processes as described previously.21, 22, 23, 24, 25

Arima Hi-C sequencing

Each specimen was sectioned to produce unstained 3- to 10-μm tissue curls for Hi-C library preparation and sequencing. FFPE sections were dewaxed, rehydrated, and subjected to Hi-C sample preparation using the Arima Hi-C FFPE kit (material part number A311038). Cross-linked chromatin in FFPE tissues was digested, and spatially proximal DNA ends were ligated to capture the sequence and structure of the genome. Proximally ligated DNA was purified, fragmented, and size selected using solid-phase reversible immobilization beads. DNA fragments containing biotinylated ligation junctions were then enriched using enrichment beads and converted into short-read sequencing libraries using the Arima Library Prep kit (material part number A303011) or Swift Accel-NGS 2S Plus kit (Swift Biosciences, Ann Arbor, MI; material part number 21024). After adapter ligation, DNA was amplified by polymerase chain reaction and purified using solid-phase reversible immobilization beads. The purified DNA was subjected to standard quality control (quantitative polymerase chain reaction and Bioanalyzer) and paired-end sequencing using NovaSeq 6000 or NovaSeq X (Illumina, San Diego, CA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Sequencing data analysis

Cases that generated >10 million paired-end sequence reads and >10 million single-end reads (13 693 814-1 388 168279) aligned to the genome with a mapping quality score of ≥Q30 were further analyzed. 15 cases with <10 million (548 808-9 910 813) unique valid pairs (UVPs), a cutoff set for quality control process in clinical testing to ensure sensitivity, were not excluded in this retrospective study. Genomic rearrangements were identified using Arima-SV v1.3 pipeline.26 Raw read pairs were aligned to the human reference genome (hg38) and deduplicated using Hi-C user pipeline (HiCUP).27 Genomic rearrangements were called using HiC_Breakfinder,28 which outputs rearrangement calls in which the breakpoints are localized to a genomic bin, such as a 1-kb or 10-kb bin. Deduplicated alignments from HiCUP were converted into Hi-C matrices using Juicer software (version 1.19.02)29 and visualized alongside genomic rearrangement calls using Juicebox.30

The Hi-C sequencing workflow and structural variant calling were blinded to clinical and FISH results.

Prognostic analysis

Overall survival (OS) duration was calculated from diagnosis to the last follow-up or death from any cause. Progression-free survival (PFS) was calculated from diagnosis to disease progression, relapse, or death, whichever occurred first; patients without an event were censored at the last follow-up. Univariate survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan-Meier estimator and log-rank test. Genome-wide analysis and machine learning methods are detailed in the supplemental Methods, supplemental Tables 2-8, and supplemental Figure 2 supplemental Material.

Ethics approval statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Material transfer agreements and data collection protocols were approved as minimal-to-no risk or exempted by the institutional review board of each participating institution.

Results

Study overview

From a consecutive R-CHOP case series of de novo DLBCL,31 159 patients were included in the Hi-C sequencing study based on the availability of FISH and IHC data for MYC, BCL2, and BCL6 rearrangements and expression.21, 22, 23 CCND1 break-apart FISH data were available only for 5 patients showing cyclin D1+ IHC.21,32 The FISH-positivity cutoffs for MYC, BCL2, BCL6, and CCND1 rearrangements were >6.5% for IGH/MYC fusions, >4% for MYC breaks, >3% for BCL2 breaks, >1.5% for BCL6 breaks, and >7.5% for CCND1 breaks.21,32 The cohort had a median age of 62 years, and slightly more than half the participants were male (56.6%). The clinical characteristics of the cohort are presented in supplemental Table 1.

Hi-C sequencing, which differs from traditional NGS in sequencing linked DNAs in 3-dimensional space (supplemental Figure 1), was used to detect all gene rearrangements in FFPE samples by numerous linked pairs of sequence tags in sequence bins using HiC_Breakfinder.28 All except 3 patients in the study cohort harbored at least 1 gene rearrangement or fusion, totaling 1903 rearrangements in the study cohort (supplemental Figure 2). A total of 923 genes, including 746 cancer-relevant genes (supplemental Table 2), were identified in the proximity (<1 Mbp) of detected gene breakpoints, in which IGH was the most frequent gene. Among the 159 patients studied, 77 (48.4%) harbored IGH rearrangements, and 7 (4.4%) harbored IG kappa or lambda locus (IGK/L) rearrangements, suggesting that errors in the germinal center (GC) reaction–associated DNA remodeling played an important role in DLBCL pathogenesis.33 BCL6, BCL2, and MYC rearrangements accounted for 82% of the IGH gene rearrangements. Other IGH gene rearrangement partners included IRF4/DUSP22, BACH2/MAP3K7, MEF2B, CIITA, GSK3B, ITPKB, BCOR (supplemental Figure 3A), POLD1, REL/BCL11A, GSE1, CPEB3, CCND1, CCND3, PD-L1(CD274)/PD-L2(PDCD1LG2)/JAK2, SP140, etc. The frequency of IGH rearrangements in the GC B-cell–like (GCB) subtype was nonsignificantly higher than that in the activated B-cell–like (ABC) subtype of DLBCL (56% vs 43%; P = .14). IGH rearrangements were associated with a nonsignificant trend toward poorer PFS only in the GCB subtype (supplemental Figure 3B).

Concordance between Hi-C– and FISH-detected MYC, BCL2, and BCL6 rearrangements

In this study, we focused on identifying WHO guideline–based gene rearrangements (supplemental Table 3). Structural variant calls by HiC_Breakfinder were validated by visual inspection of Hi-C sequence heat maps, blinded to clinical and FISH results. After excluding 3 Hi-C–positive cases with low protein expression as suspicions of false variant calls (1 MYC, 1 BCL2, and 1 BCL6 rearrangement), 103 of the 159 patients (65%) had at least 1 Hi-C rearrangement related to WHO guidelines, as illustrated in Figure 2A. Compared with break-apart FISH, which detected MYC, BCL2, BCL6, and/or CCND1 rearrangements in 76 of 159 patients (48%; Figure 2A),21,32 Hi-C sequencing detected 45 additional gene rearrangements or fusions in 42 of 159 patients (26.4%; Table 1). MYC, BCL2, and BCL6 rearrangements, routinely tested by FISH to differentiate double-hit lymphoma from DLBCL and relevant for selecting chemotherapy regimens, were successfully tested by both FISH and Hi-C sequencing in the entire cohort, allowing for a matched comparison of their sensitivity and specificity. Seven MYC, 6 BCL2, and 12 BCL6 rearrangements were identified by Hi-C but not FISH (cases marked in cyan in Figure 2A). The 6 BCL2-rearranged cases showed trends toward associations with high somatic hypermutations in IGH variable region (IGHV) and BCL6’s first intron (supplemental Figure 3C).34,35 Importantly, in addition to the 2 MYC/BCL2 double-hit (Figure 2A; supplemental Figure 4A-B) and 2 MYC/BCL6 double-hit HGBCL cases detected by both FISH and Hi-C methods in this cohort (supplemental Figure 4C), Hi-C sequencing detected a MYC/BCL6 double hit in a patient with primary testicular lymphoma (Figure 1C).36,37

To resolve the FISH/Hi-C discordance in these 25 patients, we reexamined the break-apart FISH images and the sequence heat maps and probability significance of Hi-C structural variant calls, as well as MYC, BCL2, and BCL6 protein expression levels. Various causes for FISH rearrangement–negative results were identified (Table 1). For example, in Figure 1B-C, the breakpoints were distal to MYC, so that the breaks and rearrangements did not disrupt the orange and green MYC break-apart FISH probe signals; hence, no split signals were observed, as in MYC-normal cases (Figure 1B). In supplemental Figure 5A, the red probe signal was too faint to be interpreted; however, FISH break-apart signals were detectable in some cells, supporting Hi-C results. In supplemental Figure 5B, Hi-C sequencing identified a breakpoint between BCL6 and the chromosome 3 centromere, but FISH observed only red signals (unbalanced break-apart FISH). However, for 1 BCL2-rearranged case and 1 BCL6-rearranged case detected by Hi-C, the causes for the FISH rearrangement–negative results were not identified. BCL2/BCL6 protein expression was high in these 2 cases, at 60% and 100%, respectively.

Rearranged gene partners, which were not available in the break-apart FISH results, were revealed by Hi-C sequencing. All BCL2 rearrangement partners were IGH. In contrast, 63% of BCL6 rearrangements’ partners were not IGH. IGH::MYC rearrangements were significantly more frequent in FISH-detected MYC rearrangements than in those additionally detected by Hi-C (P = .015). Of the 10 MYC rearrangements detected by both FISH and Hi-C, 8 were partnered with IGH (the other 2 were partnered with immunoglobulin lambda locus (IGL) and CIITA, respectively; supplemental Table 3). Of the 7 MYC rearrangements missed by FISH (supplemental Figure 6), only 1 was partnered with IGH, and 1 was with IGL (both were GCB-DLBCL), whereas 5 were rearranged with non-IG partner genes (4 were ABC-DLBCL, and 1 was unclassifiable). The only case with non-IG (CIITA)–MYC rearrangement detected by FISH (and Hi-C) was a MYC/BCL2 double-hit lymphoma with poor clinical outcome (Figure 2A). MYC was overexpressed, likely due to enhancers from CIITA (supplemental Figure 4A). Both MYC/BCL2 double-hit cases had high MYC/BCL2 expression and lost immunoglobulin M (IgM)/IgG/IgA expression on IHC,38 resembling the B-cell receptor silencing phenomenon.39,40 In contrast, 3 MYC/BCL6 double-hit cases showed IgM or IgG expression. There were no significant prognostic differences between IG and non-IG MYC-rearranged patients, although non-IG MYC-rearranged patients showed a nonsignificant trend toward poorer OS (supplemental Figure 5C).

Hilton et al41 showed that a double-hit gene signature (DHITsig; currently referred to as the GC dark-zone signature [DZsig]) can be used to identify HGBCL with cryptic BCL2 and MYC translocations in GCB-DLBCL.4 We classified our GCB-DLBCL cases based on DHITsig or molecular high-grade (MHG) signature expression levels (GSE31312)31 using unsupervised clustering by the DHITsig (supplemental Figure 7A) and the MHG signature, sharing 5 genes with DZsig (Figure 3A-B42,43). Consistent with previous results by Hilton et al41, Hi-C–additionally detected MYC/BCL2 rearranged GCB-DLBCL cases all had high MHG/DZsig expression except 1 case in our cohort, whereas FISH-detected MYC/BCL2 rearrangements were not significantly associated with high MHG/DZsig expression. In contrast, 2 MYC rearrangements, 1 BCL2 rearrangement, and 6 BCL6 rearrangements were detected by previous break-apart FISH but not by Hi-C sequencing. Five of these 9 cases were retested by FISH independently in this study and showed negative break-apart FISH results (cases marked in magenta in Figure 2A), although 1 MYC FISH-positive case had an abnormal deletion in MYC of the 3′ probe region, and 2 BCL6 FISH-positive cases had BCL6 gene copy number gains but not translocations. Other 4 rearrangements were counted as FISH-positive Hi-C–missed rearrangements (1 MYC, 1 BCL2, and 2 BCL6; marked in red in Figure 2A). The Hi-C–missed BCL2-rearranged case had the lowest UVP percentage of mapped reads (1.6%) in the study cohort and <10 million UVPs. In addition, among cases with concordant rearrangement-negative results of MYC break-apart FISH and Hi-C sequencing, 2 cases had 10% of cells positive for IGH::MYC fusion FISH (cases marked in pale pink in Figure 2A), which could be subclonal fusions. MYC IHC levels in these 2 cases were 20% and 50%, respectively. Both patients had long-term survival.

After orthogonal validation, the total occurrences of MYC/BCL2/BCL6 rearrangements were 106 times in the study cohort; FISH detected 81 of 106 occurrences (76%) in 72 of 159 patients (45%), whereas Hi-C detected 102 of 106 occurrences (96%) in 92 of 159 patients (58%; Table 1; Figure 2C). The specificity was 94% (81/86) by break-apart FISH and 97% (102/105) by Hi-C calling (Table 1).

Detection of CCND1 rearrangements by Hi-C and FISH in the DLBCL cohort

Unlike the gene specificity of FISH probing, Hi-C sequencing identifies gene fusions in an unbiased manner. Relevant for DLBCL vs mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) diagnosis, Hi-C identified CCND1 gene rearrangements in 4 patients in the DLBCL cohort. Cyclin D1 IHC was positive in 3 of the 4 cases (Figure 4),32 and their CCND1 rearrangement partners were IGH (supplemental Table 3; Table 2; supplemental Figure 8A). A total of 5 patients were cyclin D1+ on IHC in the study cohort, and previous CCND1 break-apart FISH for cyclin D1+ cases identified 2 CCND1-rearranged patients (DLBCL91 and DLBCL108; Table 2; Figure 4A-B).32 In the other cyclin D1+ patient with Hi-C-detected CCND1 rearrangement (DLBCL 3 in Table 1), only 4% of cells displayed CCND1 break-apart FISH signals, which was below the positivity cutoff of 7.5% set for FISH-defined rearrangement32 (Figure 4C). A concept is evolving that CCND1-rearranged DLBCL also exists,44 and the pathogenesis pattern is that CCND1 rearrangements in DLBCL are mainly mediated by aberrant class switch recombination (CSR) or somatic hypermutation, whereas CCND1 rearrangements in blastoid MCL are mainly caused by aberrant variable (V) diversity (D) joining (J) segment gene rearrangements45 (however, also present in CCND1-rearranged DLBCL44). Hi-C sequencing revealed IGH as the CCND1 rearrangement partner in 3 cyclin D1+ cases (Figure 4). In DLBCL-91 and DLBCL-108 cases, the IGH breakpoints were within the IGH-C and close to the IGH-C (switch) regions, respectively, consistent with the CSR-mediated pathogenesis in DLBCL.45 In DLBCL-3 case, the IGH breakpoint was within the IGH-J region, which could reflect either a CSR/somatic hypermutation– or VDJ rearrangement–mediated mechanism. In addition, these 3 CCND1::IGH cases all had BCL6 rearrangements, and the DLBCL-3 case additionally had IRF4 rearrangement (Table 2; supplemental Figure 8A), favoring a DLBCL classification.

To gain further insight into whether the 4 CCND1-rearranged cases were pleomorphic MCL or DLBCL with CCND1 rearrangement, we examined their genomic mutation profiles and IHC results, as summarized in Table 2. DLBCL-91 and DLBCL-108 cases, the 2 Hi-C/FISH–concordant IGH::CCND1 cases, had multiple CCND1 mutations and predominant (tumor) IGH DJ–only segment genes identified.25,34,46 IHC showed SOX11− and CD5− staining47 and low IgM expression.38 In contrast, DLBCL-3 had a mutated productive VDJ sequence, no gene mutations in the targeted NGS panel, a low percentage of weak SOX11+ cells (approximately 10%), a low percentage of cyclin D1+, CD5−, high CD10+, CD38+, and high IgM expression. DLBCL-132 case had a C1-like mutation profile,48 SOX11− and CD5− staining, and isotype-switched IgG expression.

Hi-C detection of other rearrangements associated with other lymphoma subtypes

Beyond MYC/BCL2/BCL6/CCND1, 18 rearrangements related to the WHO guidelines were identified by Hi-C sequencing in 17 patients (Figure 2A).

In patients without CCND1 rearrangement, 5 CCND3 rearrangements were detected (supplemental Figure 8B). In only 1 CCND3-rearranged patient, the CCND3 rearrangement partner was IGH, and cyclin D3 IHC was positive (Table 2), associated with significantly poorer survival (supplemental Figure 5C).

Important for the diagnosis of large B-cell lymphoma with IRF4 rearrangement (LBCL-IRF4), IRF4 rearrangements were detected in 7 of 159 patients (4%; 1 IRF4 rearrangement, IRF4::SELL, was also detected by targeted RNA sequencing49) without a significant prognostic effect, 5 of which showed 100% MUM1/IRF4 expression31 (the other 2 cases showed 5% [DLBCL-2 case] and 0% [DLBCL-3 case] expression). Of the 7 patients with LBCL-IRF4, 2 were aged <60 years (a 39-year-old patient and the DLBCL-3 patient aged 57 years); 1 young and 2 older patients had mutations in NF-κB pathway genes (CARD11, MYD88, and CD79)50; 4 patients had other rearrangements (BCL6, CCND3, or CCND1); MYC and BCL2 rearrangements were absent in all cases51; and no age-related site pattern was observed as a previous study did.52 Rather, we noted a lymphoma IGH sequence pattern not reported previously: after the exclusion of DLBCL-3 case (the MUM1− case with CCND1 rearrangement), for all IGH-sequenced IRF4-rearranged cases, only IGH D-J segment genes (but not V genes) were identified in annotating obtained sequences,34 in contrast to all the V-D-J segment genes identified for BCL2-rearranged cases (supplemental Figure 9A). Potential B-cell receptor silencing (no IgM/IgG/IgA expression)39 was present in 3 cases (the other 2 cases had 5% and 30% of IgM expression).

PD-L1 rearrangements, which are associated with certain subtypes of DLBCL and efficacy of PD-1 inhibitors,53, 54, 55 were identified by Hi-C sequencing in 3 patients (Table 2; Figure 5). All cases showed high PD-L1 expression on IHC and mfIHC24 and variable levels of cognate interactions between PD-L1 and T-cell PD-1, indicated by color mixing of PD-L1 and PD-1 immunofluorescence (Figure 5). Complicatedly, tumor cells also had high membranous PD-1 expression in DLBCL-67 (a representative case with high nuclear GSDMD expression in a previous study56), some of which interacted with PD-L1 (Figure 5A). In addition, tumor nuclear PD-1 expression was high in DLBCL-110 (Figure 5B; supplemental Figure 9B) but did not interact with PD-L1. Both DLBCL-67 and DLBCL-110 patients had poor clinical outcomes, and their T cells were most likely Ki-67− (Figure 5A-B). In addition, Hi-C detected nonrecurrent rearrangements, including BIRC2::MALT1 fusion (also detected by targeted RNA sequencing49), ATM::DDX10 fusion, and an internal gene rearrangement affecting the ALK regulatory region (supplemental Figure 9C-E), as well as a few copy number alterations associated with some complex rearrangements. Prognostic analysis and identification of additional prognostic biomarkers

The prognostic significance of Hi-C–detected guideline-based gene rearrangements was analyzed using Kaplan-Meier curves and log-rank test. In the overall cohort, MYC rearrangements, PD-L1 rearrangements, and CCND1 rearrangements were significantly associated with or showed trends toward poorer OS and/or PFS (Figure 6A-B). GCB-associated BCL2 rearrangements were associated with significantly poorer PFS only in the GCB subtype, with a lower P value than FISH-detected BCL2 rearrangements (Figure 6A). Discussion

Accurate detection of hallmark rearrangements for B-cell lymphoma diagnosis is critical for clinical decision-making and precision medicine. FFPE-based Hi-C sequencing uses unique sample preparation chemistry to overcome the low efficiency of traditional short-read NGS for detecting DNA fusions and calls rearrangements by well-established pipelines. In this retrospective study, the Hi-C assay detected 124 of 128 clinically relevant rearrangements (98%) with high specificity (124/127 [98%]) in 159 archival FFPE tissues. FISH missed 25 of 106 MYC, BCL2, and BCL6 rearrangements (23.6%), including a FISH-cryptic MYC rearrangement in a HGBCL with MYC and BCL6 rearrangements, a provisional double-hit lymphoma entity according to International Consensus Classification (ICC) guidelines.36,37 Although FISH can detect rearrangement in situ in a spatially resolved manner, tissue fixature–related issues, crushed, folded tissues, or poor morphology (in 9 of 159 cases, 5.7%), faint FISH signals, low rearrangement levels due to either subclonal events or low tumor content, and difficulties in interpretating complex FISH patterns4,5,16,57 reduced the sensitivity and specificity of rearrangement detection by FISH, whereas they had less impact on Hi-C sequencing, using total DNA samples.

Hi-C sequencing has the advantage of genomic coverage, whereas FISH requires the correct anticipation of biomarkers to be tested based on the suspected diagnosis. The importance of detecting all clinically relevant gene rearrangements is demonstrated by Hi-C detecting 3 CCND1::IGH rearrangements in cyclin D1+ cases and 1 non-IG CCND1 rearrangement in a cyclin D1− case in this DLBCL study cohort. A diagnostic dilemma is posed, because other genetic and IHC results for these 4 cases mostly favored the initial diagnosis of DLBCL. Further clinical and therapeutic studies are warranted because 2 of 4 patients treated with standard R-CHOP experienced early progression (<1 year), and 3 of 4 died within 26 months (including the cyclin D1− case); however, 1 cyclin D1+ patient had long-term survival.

Moreover, without the need for anticipation and multiple FISH tests, Hi-C sequencing detected PD-L1,24,55,58 MALT1,59 ALK,60 and ATM61 rearrangements in 6 patients, who did not have long-term survival except 1 patient with primary testicular DLBCL, as well as CCND3 and IRF4 rearrangements in 11 patients. One PD-L1–rearranged case, the ALK-rearranged case, and the MYC/BCL6 double-hit case additionally identified by Hi-C, were all classified as the testicular lymphoma tumor (TLT) subtype of primary testicular DLBCL in our previous study.62 However, as a limitation of this study, orthogonal verification using FISH and/or NGS was not performed for all Hi-C–identified rearrangements.

Owing to the complexity and cost of applying multiple FISH probe sets, FISH analyses may not test all suspicious rearrangements or use both break-apart and dual fusion probes, and are often performed sequentially to untangle diagnostic ambiguity, which results in a longer time to results. As novel informative biomarkers continue to be discovered for diagnosis, subclassification, and treatment guidance, the cost of all necessary FISH testing can be prohibitive. On the contrary, the implementation of Hi-C assay in clinical practice also has barriers, including a lack of sophistication for higher complexity molecular testing in some clinical labs currently. Based on results in our and other studies, future studies could develop more targeted gene panels to reduce the costs, as well as increase sequencing depths and delineate fine rearrangement structures for better functional consequence prediction.

In summary, FFPE-based Hi-C sequencing provides a whole-genome, unbiased approach for detecting all rearrangements and fusions, which helps improve the diagnosis and management of large B-cell lymphomas. Our study suggests that Hi-C sequencing represents a sensitive, robust, and comprehensive alternative to FISH for genomic structural variation analysis, which may improve the workflow of pathology laboratories for precise diagnosis, prognostication, and treatment optimization.
