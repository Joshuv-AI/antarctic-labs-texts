Prognostic characteristics of disulfidptosis-related genes across cancers and their potential implications in osteosarcoma
Authors: Jinqiu Wang, Hui Huang, Dehuai Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveDisulfidptosis, a newly discovered mechanism of cell death, may play a significant role in cancer initiation, progression, and prognosis. However, studies on the prognostic role of Disulfidptosis-Related Genes (DRGs) across cancers remain limited. This study aims to systematically explore the prognostic value of DRGs in various cancer types by constructing a prognosis model based on DRGs and analyzing their associations with tumor biological characteristics.MethodsThis was a pan-cancer bioinformatics study combined with in vitro qRT-PCR validation. Public transcriptomic and clinical data from cancer patients were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). Samples were randomly divided into training and validation cohorts at a 1:1 ratio. In the training cohort, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was used to identify prognosis-related DRGs, followed by multivariate Cox regression to construct a DRG-based risk score. The prognostic value of the risk score was evaluated using Cox regression, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, and nomogram construction. Gene set activity analysis was performed to assess the associations between the DRG score and tumor-related biological processes, including angiogenesis, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), and cell cycle activity. To further validate the expression patterns of key DRGs in osteosarcoma, osteosarcoma-related transcriptomic data from TARGET and normal tissue data from GTEx were analyzed. The 16 selected DRGs were intersected with osteosarcoma-related differentially expressed genes, and 10 overlapping genes were further validated by qRT-PCR in osteosarcoma cell lines and normal osteoblasts.ResultsKey DRGs identified via LASSO regression showed significant prognostic value in pan-cancer analysis. The resulting risk model effectively stratified patients by survival outcomes and performed well in both training and validation cohorts, indicating strong clinical potential. SsGSEA revealed associations between DRG risk scores and malignant tumor features such as angiogenesis, EMT, and cell cycle dysregulation. Differential expression and GO enrichment analyses indicated that related genes were involved in metabolism, apoptosis, and immune processes. qRT-PCR validation in normal osteoblasts (hFOB) and seven osteosarcoma cell lines showed that NDUFA11 and NDUFS1 were generally downregulated, whereas ACTB, WASF2, FLNA, PRC1, ACTN4, PGD, RAC1, and FLNB were generally upregulated in most osteosarcoma cell lines compared with hFOB cells. These expression patterns were broadly consistent with the bioinformatics results and support the potential relevance of these genes in osteosarcoma progression.ConclusionBy constructing a prognostic model based on DRGs, this study reveals the significant prognostic value of DRGs across pan-cancers and further validates their association with tumor malignant characteristics. The results suggest that DRG score can serve as an effective prognostic indicator for cancer patient survival and have specific implications in osteosarcoma. In the future, DRG-based scoring systems may serve as novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets.Keywords: disulfidptosis, disulfidptosis-related genes, pan-cancer analysis, osteosarcoma, prognostic signature

Introduction

The occurrence and progression of tumors is a complex biological process involving interactions between various cell types and molecular mechanisms. In this process, cell death plays a crucial role as an essential biological mechanism for maintaining tissue homeostasis. Depending on the triggering mechanism and process, cell death can be classified into apoptosis, necrosis, necroptosis, ferroptosis, pyroptosis, autophagic cell death, and others. These forms of cell death affect the physiological activities and survival of specific cells through various mechanisms. 1 These novel forms of cell death not only play a role in cellular physiology but also play important roles in tumor progression and metastasis.

Disulfidptosis, newly identified by Liu et al., is distinct from other programmed cell death forms, including apoptosis, ferroptosis, cuproptosis, and necroptosis. 2 As a novel form of cell death, it plays an important role in the occurrence and development of various diseases, especially closely related to cancer.3,4 For example, Disulfidptosis is induced by abnormal disulfide bond formation in intracellular proteins, primarily due to NADPH depletion in SLC7A11-high-expressing cells under glucose deprivation, leading to the abnormal accumulation of cystine and other disulfide bonds, ultimately causing disulfide bond stress and rapid cell death. Current studies indicate that glucose transporter (GLUT) inhibitors induce disulfidptosis in SLC7A11-high-expressing cancer cells, thereby inhibiting tumor growth. 5 Disulfidptosis is thought to play a significant role in tumor initiation and progression, especially in the response of tumor cells to antioxidant therapies. Moreover, it may interact with other forms of cell death to form a complex cell death network that regulates the dynamic balance of the tumor microenvironment.

Existing studies primarily focus on the role of disulfidptosis in specific tumor types, while research on its universality and prognostic value across pan-cancer is still limited. Therefore, systematically studying the expression patterns of DRGs across various tumor types and their relationship with patient prognosis holds significant scientific and clinical value.

This study aimed to systematically characterize the prognostic and biological relevance of disulfidptosis-related genes (DRGs) across cancers using TCGA pan-cancer data. Rather than developing only another prognostic scoring model, we sought to establish a pan-cancer analytical framework to evaluate how DRGs are associated with survival outcomes and multiple malignant biological features, including angiogenesis, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), cell cycle activity, immune subtypes, tumor microenvironment characteristics, stemness indices, and drug sensitivity. In addition, we further explored the potential implications of this DRG-related framework in osteosarcoma through differential expression analysis and qRT-PCR validation.

Through this study, we aimed to provide a broader biological context for understanding the role of disulfidptosis in tumor progression. We propose that the DRG-based signature in this work should be interpreted not only as a prognostic tool, but also as a hypothesis-generating framework that may help link disulfidptosis-related transcriptional patterns with tumor aggressiveness and microenvironmental heterogeneity. These findings may offer useful clues for future mechanistic studies and for the identification of potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics software and analysis parameters

Bioinformatics analyses were performed using R software (version 3.5.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; https://www.r-project.org/). The main R packages used in this study included glmnet for LASSO regression, survival for Cox regression analysis, limma for differential expression analysis, sva for batch-effect correction, GSVA for gene set activity scoring, clusterProfiler for GO and KEGG enrichment analysis, ImmuneSubtypeClassifier for immune subtype analysis, ggplot2 for data visualization, and corrplot for correlation heatmaps. TCGA pan-cancer transcriptomic and clinical data were obtained from the UCSC Xena Browser. Osteosarcoma transcriptomic data were obtained from the TARGET Osteosarcoma project (TARGET-OS) through the GDC Data Portal, and normal control data were obtained from the GTEx project. Differentially expressed genes were defined using |log2 fold change| ≥ 2 and FDR-adjusted P < 0.05. Enrichment results with Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Drug sensitivity correlations were analyzed using the GSCA platform, and FDR < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Disulfidptosis biomarkers

Disulfidptosis, distinct from previously reported forms of cell death such as apoptosis, ferroptosis, necroptosis, and cuproptosis, is a rapid form of cell death induced by disulfide stress caused by abnormal cystine accumulation under glucose-deprivation conditions. To construct the initial pool of disulfidptosis-related genes (DRGs), candidate genes were collected from published disulfidptosis-related studies and relevant literature. Genes were included if they had been reported to be associated with disulfide stress, cystine accumulation, NADPH depletion, redox homeostasis, SLC7A11-related metabolic vulnerability, or cytoskeleton remodeling. 6 Genes related to redox-regulatory and cell death-associated processes were also considered as supplementary candidates. In total, 48 candidate genes were aggregated as the initial DRG pool for subsequent LASSO regression and prognostic signature construction.

Patients and data sets

Pan-cancer transcriptomic and clinical data were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) through the UCSC Xena Browser (https://xenabrowser.net/). The dataset used in this study consisted of TCGA pan-cancer RNA-sequencing expression profiles and corresponding clinical information, including 9,550 samples from 32 tumor types. The included TCGA projects were ACC, BLCA, BRCA, CESC, CHOL, COAD, DLBC, ESCA, GBM, HNSC, KICH, KIRC, KIRP, LAML, LGG, LIHC, LUAD, LUSC, MESO, OV, PAAD, PCPG, PRAD, READ, SARC, SKCM, STAD, TGCT, THCA, THYM, UCEC, and UCS. Samples without complete survival information were excluded from survival-related analyses. The remaining samples were randomly divided into a training cohort and a validation cohort at a 1:1 ratio using R software (version 3.5.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; https://www.r-project.org/). The training cohort contained 4,775 samples, and the validation cohort contained 4,775 samples.

Data acquisition and differential expression analysis for osteosarcoma

To validate the expression patterns of DRGs in osteosarcoma, an independent osteosarcoma-related cohort was constructed using data from the TARGET and GTEx projects. RNA-sequencing data for osteosarcoma tissues were obtained from the TARGET osteosarcoma project (TARGET-OS) through the Genomic Data Commons Data Portal (GDC; https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), including 88 osteosarcoma tissue samples with HTSeq-FPKM expression data. Normal tissue expression data were obtained from the Genotype-Tissue Expression project (GTEx; https://gtexportal.org/), including 396 normal skeletal muscle tissue samples used as non-tumor controls.

Before differential expression analysis, FPKM values were transformed to log2(FPKM + 1). After merging the TARGET and GTEx expression matrices, batch effects were corrected using the ComBat function from the sva R package. Differential expression analysis between osteosarcoma and normal control samples was performed using the limma R package. P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate method. Genes with |log2 fold change| ≥ 2 and FDR-adjusted P < 0.05 were defined as differentially expressed genes. The resulting osteosarcoma-related differentially expressed genes were intersected with the 16 selected DRGs for subsequent validation.

Construction of prognostic DRG signature

First, univariate Cox regression analysis was performed to screen genes associated with prognosis. Then, the R package “glmnet” was used to perform least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis to further filter genes significantly associated with prognosis (p<0.05) and select genes with non-zero penalty coefficients. Next, multivariate Cox regression analysis was used to determine the regression coefficients (β) for each gene, and a prognostic model based on these key genes was constructed. The risk score for each patient in the TCGA database was then calculated, and the patients were divided into high-risk and low-risk groups using the median risk score as the cutoff value.

Biological process z-score evaluation

Using an algorithm proposed by Lee et al., which integrates feature gene expression to reflect pathway activity, 7 we selected genes associated with angiogenesis, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT, GO:0001837), the cell cycle, and the DRGs initial gene set. The z-score algorithm in the R package GSVA was used for analysis. The activity of each gene set was expressed as angiogenesis z-score, EMT z-score, cell cycle z-score, and DRGs z-score.

GO and KEGG enrichment analysis

The R package “ClusterProfiler” was used for Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis to explore the functional differences and related biological pathways of differentially expressed genes between high-risk and low-risk groups. Enrichment significance was defined by a strict threshold: pathways with a Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-value< 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Enrichment analysis revealed the roles of these genes in their respective pathways and how they contribute to tumor progression and biological characteristics.

Immune subtype analysis

Immune subtype analysis was performed using the R package “LIMMA” to investigate the expression differences of selected genes across six different immune subtypes. ImmuneSubtypeClassifier (v0.1.0) R software package was used for immune subtype analysis, and box plots were drawn to demonstrate the relationship between gene expression patterns and immune subtypes. Further visualizations were performed using the R package “ggplot2” to present the relationship between gene expression levels and immune subtypes. Systematic analysis of cancer type molecular subtypes and the immune microenvironment helps understand how tumor-infiltrating immune cells affect molecular subtypes, revealing the potential impact of the immune microenvironment on tumor prognosis. 8

Tumor microenvironment analysis

Within the tumor microenvironment, gene expression often exhibits a positive correlation with stromal cell scores, suggesting that higher gene expression levels correspond to a higher cell content in the tumor stroma. We extracted tumor microenvironment score data and compared the expression of each gene with the microenvironment scores, visualizing the results using the R package “corrplot” to reveal the potential relationship between genes and the tumor microenvironment.

Tumor stem cell correlation

We integrated data related to tumor stem cells, including genomic variation (DNAss) and transcript expression (RNAss), for multi-level analysis. This in-depth exploration investigates how genomic variation affects tumor stem cell functions and their role in tumor development. Combining DNAss and RNAss data helps identify genes critical for maintaining tumor stem cells, providing new potential targets for tumor therapy. Additionally, we calculated tumor stem cell scores based on expression data and combined them with methylation data to obtain the tumor stem cell index. Correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationship between gene expression and RNAss, DNAss, and visualized using the R package “corrplot” to further validate the role of these genes in tumor stem cell functions.

Gene set mutation analysis

Gene set mutation analysis was performed using the bioinformatics platform (https://www.sxdyc.com/singleCollectionTool), targeting the 16 selected DRGs. A heatmap of gene set single nucleotide variation (SNV) mutation frequency, a waterfall plot, and a bar chart of genomic copy number variation (CNV) mutations were generated. This analysis helps understand the mutation frequency of these genes in different tumor types and their potential impact on tumor initiation and progression.

Drug sensitivity analysis

Drug sensitivity analysis was performed using the Gene Set Cancer Analysis (GSCA) website (https://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/GSCA), which integrates cancer genomics data to evaluate the relationship between gene expression and drug sensitivity. In this study, we selected the 16 DRGs identified in the prognostic signature, including ACTB, ACTN4, CYFIP1, FLNA, FLNB, INF2, MYL6, NDUFA11, NDUFS1, NUBPL, OXSM, PGD, PRC1, RAC1, SLC7A11, and WASF2, for drug sensitivity analysis. The expression data for these genes were obtained from public databases, including multiple cancer types. Drug sensitivity data were mainly obtained from the GDSC database through the GSCA platform. We first performed correlation analysis of drug sensitivity and gene expression data based on these databases using the GSCA platform. The GSCA platform calculated the Pearson correlation coefficient and corresponding false discovery rate (FDR) between gene expression and drug sensitivity. Negative correlation values indicate that higher gene expression may increase drug sensitivity, while positive correlation values suggest that higher gene expression may lead to drug resistance. To ensure the reliability of the results, all correlation analyses with FDR values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The GSCA platform’s results helped us select drugs whose sensitivity is related to target gene expression, providing a theoretical basis for subsequent experimental design.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 3.5.2; https://www.r-project.org). Data from the training and test sets were standardized using zero-mean normalization before analysis. Kaplan–Meier survival curves and log-rank tests were used to compare survival outcomes between the high- and low-risk groups. Cox regression analysis was used to evaluate the prognostic value of the DRG score. A nomogram based on the DRG score was constructed and evaluated using time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and decision curve analysis (DCA), as described by Vickers and Elkin. 9 The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to assess prognostic performance. A p-value< 0.05 was considered statistically significant. No copyrighted clinical scoring scales were used in this study. The DRG score and nomogram were constructed based on the authors’ own analysis of publicly available datasets; therefore, no additional copyright permission was required.

Results

Identification of a 16-DRG signature for pan-cancer

First, in the TCGA pan-cancer training set, we input the initial 48 DRGs biomarkers and performed LASSO regression analysis (Figure 1(a) and (b)), identifying 19 DRGs-associated genes with non-zero coefficients. We then conducted pairwise correlation analysis of these genes and found significant expression pattern similarities between some gene pairs (Figure 1(c)). Specifically, INF2 was significantly correlated with ACTN4, ACTB with RAC1, FLNA with ACTN4, FLNA with ACTB, FLNA with WASF2, and CYFIP1 with WASF2. To reduce model complexity and minimize potential overfitting, we further evaluated the 19 candidate genes based on pairwise correlation analysis and their predictive contributions during multivariate Cox modeling. TKT, RPN1, and SULT1A1 were excluded because they showed unstable or redundant predictive contributions, whereas the remaining 16 DRG-associated genes were retained for subsequent analyses. This rigorous filtering step ensured the statistical independence and robustness of the final prognostic signature.

Based on the 16 selected key genes, we constructed a prognostic model using Cox regression analysis and calculated a DRG score for each patient based on the normalized expression levels of these genes and their corresponding regression coefficients (Figure 1(d)). Additionally, we assessed the impact of each gene on patient prognosis and evaluated each gene’s Hazard Ratio (HR). These results provided essential insights into the prognostic value of DRGs across different cancer types.

Panorama of DRGs score in cancers

The DRGs score exhibited clear organ specificity. Tumors from the brain, stomach, and esophagus generally had higher DRG scores, while tumors from certain exocrine glands, such as prostate adenocarcinoma, thyroid cancer, and thymoma, typically had lower scores (Figure 2(a)). These differences may be related to the biological characteristics of different organs and the diversity of their tumor microenvironments. This suggests that the DRGs score is not just a simple scoring system, but may reflect molecular differences between various cancer types.

To further validate the clinical prognostic significance of the DRGs score, we performed survival analysis in the TCGA training cohort. Based on the median DRGs score, patients were divided into high-risk and low-risk groups. Patients in the high DRGs score group generally exhibited poorer survival outcomes, specifically having lower disease-specific survival (DSS), overall survival (OS), and progression-free interval (PFI) compared to the low score group (Figure 2(b)). These results indicate that the DRGs score, as a potential biomarker, is closely associated with the prognosis of cancer patients.

Further univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis revealed that, in most cancer types, a higher DRGs score was associated with a poorer prognosis (Figure 2(c)). This correlation was consistent across different tumor types, and the DRGs score could independently predict patient survival, highlighting its clinical value as an independent prognostic factor. In the TCGA training cohort, the DRGs score also demonstrated strong predictive ability, with a significant negative correlation between the DRGs score and OS in cancers such as low-grade glioma (LGG), adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC), and lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) (log-rank test: LGG p<0.001, ACC p<0.001, LUAD p=0.017) (Figure 2(d)). Additionally, multivariate Cox regression analysis combining DRGs score and cancer type further confirmed that the DRGs score was an independent predictor of OS in the TCGA test cohort (HR=1.22, p<0.001).

These results strongly demonstrate the broad applicability of the DRGs score in various cancers, with consistent prognostic prediction capability across different tumor types. The combination of cancer type and DRGs score in multivariate analysis not only strengthens the role of DRGs score in tumor prognosis but also provides a new perspective for future cancer prognostic evaluation models. However, it is noteworthy that despite the high predictive value of DRG scores across various tumor types, differences between cancer types still suggest that the application of the DRGs score should be evaluated in conjunction with the specific clinical context. This finding provides a theoretical basis for the future clinical application of DRGs score as an adjunct tool and suggests that future studies can further explore how to optimize the DRGs score model to improve its predictive capacity in personalized medicine.

Establishment of a nomogram based on the DRGs score for clinically predicting survival of pan-cancer

To provide an actionable and quantitative tool for clinically predicting the likelihood of adverse events, we constructed a comprehensive nomogram combining the DRGs score and multiple clinical pathological features. This nomogram integrates patient age, cancer type, and DRGs score to enhance the accuracy of survival predictions (Figure 3(a)). In this way, the nomogram can not only provide precise prognostic assessments for individual patients but also offer a straightforward, easy-to-use tool for clinicians to assist in decision-making.

When evaluating the performance of the nomogram, overall survival (OS) was used as the primary predictive indicator. Calibration curves (Figure 3(b)) showed that the nomogram’s predictions closely matched the actual survival outcomes, with the calibration curve almost overlapping the ideal standard curve and a slope approaching 1, indicating that the nomogram’s performance was very close to the real survival data with good accuracy.

Further performance evaluation was conducted by comparing the predictive effectiveness of the nomogram with that of the DRGs score alone. We calculated the area under the curve (AUC) for both the nomogram and the DRGs score alone in the training set (TCGA_train) and test cohort (TCGA_test). The results showed that the AUC values for the nomogram were 0.80 and 0.80, while the AUC values for the DRGs score alone were 0.72 and 0.71, respectively (Figure 3(c)). This indicates that the nomogram, which combines the DRGs score with clinical pathological features, has a higher predictive efficiency than the DRGs score alone and can more accurately assess the patient’s survival risk.

Additionally, we conducted decision curve analysis (DCA) to further evaluate the net benefit (NB) of the nomogram in clinical application. The results showed that both the nomogram and the DRGs score alone exhibited high net benefits across a risk threshold range from 0 to 1, with the nomogram exceeding 15% at the maximum net benefit (Figure 3(d)). This suggests that combining the DRGs score with other clinical features is crucial for enhancing the clinical benefit of survival prediction.

Overall, the DRG score-based nomogram we developed not only improved the accuracy of survival predictions but also provided clinicians with an intuitive, user-friendly tool. It considers both the DRGs score and key clinical pathological features to provide more personalized prognostic evaluations for patients across different cancer types. The application of this predictive model helps optimize clinical decision-making and provides more precise treatment strategies for patients.

The DRGs signature and malignant features of the tumor

During the transformation from normal cells to a malignant state, tumor cells typically acquire rapid proliferation, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) activity, and angiogenesis ability, which are classic features of malignant tumors 10 . Previous studies have suggested that some DRGs may be associated with tumor progression-related processes, including angiogenesis, EMT, cell cycle regulation, and the transition of tumor cells from a quiescent phase to a proliferative phase. 11 To investigate the relationship between DRGs and these malignant features, we used the z-score algorithm to quantify the activity of tumor-promoting networks, angiogenesis, EMT, and the cell cycle.

By analyzing the TCGA pan-cancer cohort, we found that the DRG score was significantly correlated with angiogenesis, EMT, and cell cycle activity (Figure 4(a)). Specifically, the DRG score showed a weak negative correlation with angiogenesis z-score (R = -0.11, p < 2.2e-16), but positive correlations with EMT z-score (R = 0.29, p < 2.2e-16) and cell cycle z-score (R = 0.58, p < 2.2e-16). These results suggest that tumors with higher DRG scores may exhibit stronger EMT-related features and more active cell cycle activity, whereas their association with angiogenesis appears weaker and directionally negative. In most cancer types, DRG activity was closely associated with malignant biological features, particularly EMT and cell cycle activation (Figure 4(b)), suggesting a potential relationship between DRGs and tumor invasiveness, proliferation, and malignant progression.

Enrichment analysis of differential genes in high and low risk groups

Through Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG data analysis, we explored the biological functions and pathways enriched in the upregulated and downregulated genes. Both upregulated and downregulated genes were enriched in biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF). The upregulated genes were mainly enriched in biological functions related to nuclear division and mitosis (Figure 5(a)), while downregulated genes were enriched in amino acid metabolism, catabolic processes, and enzyme activity (Figure 5(b)).

In the KEGG enrichment analysis, the upregulated genes were primarily enriched in key pathways such as the cell cycle, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, and the IL-17 signaling pathway. The enrichment of the cell cycle pathway suggests active proliferation of tumor cells, while the IL-17 signaling pathway may play a significant role in immune responses and immune evasion in tumor cells (Figure 5(c)). On the other hand, downregulated genes were mainly enriched in metabolic-related pathways, including thyroid hormone synthesis, PPAR signaling, adrenergic signaling, and tyrosine metabolism. These downregulated genes are closely related to metabolic regulation and hormone synthesis in tumor cells, potentially indicating that tumor cells adapt to their rapid growth by adjusting their metabolic environment (Figure 5(d)). Overall, these enrichment analysis results not only reveal the multiple regulatory roles of tumor cells in biological functions but also provide important clues for further understanding the molecular mechanisms of tumors and identifying potential therapeutic targets.

Expression of different immune subtypes

According to the immune subtype classification described by Thorsson et al., tumor samples were classified into six immune subtypes: C1 wound healing, C2 IFN-γ dominant, C3 inflammatory, C4 lymphocyte depleted, C5 immunologically quiet, and C6 TGF-β dominant. We analyzed the expression patterns of the 16 key genes across these immune subtypes. 12 As shown in Figure 6, all 16 genes displayed significant subtype-dependent expression differences among the six immune subtypes. For example, ACTB and MYL6 showed relatively higher expression in the C1 and C2 subtypes, whereas PRC1 showed higher expression in C1 and C2 but lower expression in C3 and C5. In contrast, NDUFS1 and SLC7A11 exhibited relatively higher expression in the C5 subtype. These results suggest that the expression of DRGs varies across distinct immune microenvironmental states and may be associated with immune subtype heterogeneity in pan-cancer.

The expression levels of key genes in osteosarcoma

Based on the differential expression analysis of the osteosarcoma cohort described in the Methods section, we evaluated the dysregulation of DRGs in osteosarcoma. The volcano plot showed the overall distribution of differentially expressed genes between osteosarcoma and normal control samples, with several selected DRGs highlighted (Figure 13(a)). After intersecting the 16 selected DRGs with the osteosarcoma-related differentially expressed genes, we identified 10 overlapping genes, including NDUFA11, NDUFS1, FLNA, FLNB, ACTB, ACTN4, PGD, PRC1, RAC1, and WASF2 (Figure 13(b) ). Subsequently, qRT-PCR was performed to validate the expression patterns of these 10 genes in osteosarcoma cell lines and normal osteoblasts. The results showed that NDUFA11 and NDUFS1 were generally downregulated in osteosarcoma cell lines, whereas ACTB, WASF2, FLNA, PRC1, ACTN4, PGD, RAC1, and FLNB were generally upregulated in most osteosarcoma cell lines compared with hFOB cells (Figure 13(c)). These findings were broadly consistent with the bioinformatics analysis and support the potential relevance of these genes in osteosarcoma progression.

Discussion

Cancer-related mortality is primarily attributed to tumor metastasis rather than the primary tumor itself. This reflects the complexity of tumor biology, as patients often die from organ failure, cancer-related thrombosis, or other complications caused by metastatic dissemination. 17 The metastatic characteristics of tumors involve not only the invasion and migration of tumor cells but are also closely related to immune evasion, angiogenesis, and cell-cell interactions in the tumor microenvironment. Additionally, tumor heterogeneity, drug resistance, and immune escape mechanisms make tumor progression unique for each patient. These factors collectively exacerbate the complexity and difficulty of cancer treatment. 18 This biological complexity poses significant challenges to the development of pan-cancer prognostic systems, especially those that integrate different molecular and clinical features of tumors.

In this study, we used a pan-cancer strategy to investigate the clinical and biological relevance of disulfidptosis-related genes (DRGs). Importantly, the value of this work is not limited to the construction of a prognostic signature. Instead, our analyses suggest that DRGs are broadly associated with multiple malignant features across cancers, including survival heterogeneity, angiogenesis, EMT, cell cycle activity, immune subtype differences, tumor microenvironmental patterns, stemness characteristics, and drug response. Therefore, the proposed signature may serve not only as a predictive model but also as a descriptive framework for understanding the broader tumor-associated context of disulfidptosis.

Compared with studies that mainly report a DRG-based risk score in a single cancer type, our work extends the analysis to the pan-cancer level and further provides osteosarcoma-focused validation. These findings do not establish a direct mechanistic link between each identified gene and disulfidptosis in osteosarcoma. However, they do indicate that DRG-related transcriptional patterns may reflect biological processes relevant to disulfidptosis, such as redox regulation, cytoskeleton-associated remodeling, and cellular stress adaptation. In this way, our study advances the current understanding of disulfidptosis by connecting this emerging form of cell death with broader malignant phenotypes and tumor heterogeneity. Specifically, our results indicate that genes associated with disulfidptosis are enriched in signaling pathways such as the IL-17 pathway. IL-17 promotes tumorigenesis through mechanisms like inducing inflammatory responses, modulating immune cell infiltration, and facilitating immune evasion. 19 These observations suggest that DRGs may be associated with immune evasion- and metastasis-related processes through their links with the tumor microenvironment, extracellular matrix dynamics, immune responses, and angiogenesis. This relationship is also consistent with observations from other forms of regulated cell death, such as ferroptosis, in which cell death-related pathways interact with the tumor microenvironment. A more comprehensive understanding of these interconnected mechanisms may provide further insights into tumor biology and help identify potential therapeutic strategies. 20

Additionally, our analysis reveals a significant correlation between the expression of DRGs and patient survival across various tumor types. For example, in aggressive cancers such as sarcoma and triple-negative breast cancer, high expression of DRGs is associated with poor prognosis. This highlights the prognostic value of DRGs and their potential utility as biomarkers for cancer diagnosis and treatment. Beyond survival outcomes, future studies may further explore whether DRG expression patterns are associated with age-related molecular heterogeneity in osteosarcoma. Previous studies have reported age-dependent molecular variations in osteosarcoma, suggesting that pediatric, adolescent, and adult patients may differ in genomic features and potential therapeutic vulnerabilities. Therefore, longitudinal studies with age-stratified cohorts are needed to determine whether DRG-related signatures are linked to early-onset or late-onset osteosarcoma and whether they can further improve risk stratification. 21

To investigate the therapeutic potential of DRGs, we analyzed their relationship with drug sensitivity using the Gene Set Cancer Analysis (GSCA) database. Our study found that ACTN4 is a promising biomarker, as its high expression correlates with sensitivity to the Hsp90 inhibitor 17-AAG. ACTN4 is a cytoskeletal regulator involved in cell migration, adhesion, and invasion, suggesting that it may modulate tumor response to treatment. Importantly, several key genes validated in our osteosarcoma cell lines are biologically linked to redox regulation and cytoskeleton-associated remodeling, two processes related to disulfidptosis.22,23 For instance, PGD contributes to NADPH production and redox homeostasis, whereas ACTB, FLNA, and RAC1 are involved in actin cytoskeleton regulation, which is known to undergo aberrant cross-linking and mechanical collapse during disulfidptosis. 24 The upregulation of these redox-related and cytoskeleton-associated genes supports their potential biological relevance to disulfidptosis-related processes. However, further mechanistic studies are needed to determine whether these genes directly regulate this cell death pathway in osteosarcoma. These findings provide a rationale for exploring disulfidptosis-related vulnerabilities as potential therapeutic targets, particularly in drug-resistant tumors. 25 For example, ACTN4 may serve as a potential biomarker associated with both treatment sensitivity and tumor invasiveness, thereby providing clues for personalized treatment strategies. We further explored the relationship between DRGs, the tumor microenvironment, and tumor stemness. Our analysis showed that DRG expression was associated with RNA expression-based stemness score (RNAss) and DNA methylation-based stemness score (DNAss), suggesting that these genes may be linked to stemness-related features and genomic instability in cancer. Tumor stem cells are characterized by self-renewal capacity and differentiation potential and are important contributors to tumor progression, treatment resistance, recurrence, and metastasis. Therefore, DRGs may participate in the connection between cellular stress responses and malignant phenotypes, although further mechanistic studies are needed to confirm their direct regulatory roles.23,25–27

Additionally, our study revealed significant associations between DRG expression and stromal or immune scores, suggesting that DRGs may be related to the shaping of the tumor microenvironment. Immune cell infiltration, including CD8+ T cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells, plays important roles in immune surveillance, immune escape, and tumor progression. At the same time, stromal components and extracellular matrix remodeling are involved in angiogenesis, tumor invasion, and metastasis.28,29 Therefore, DRGs may be linked to an immune- and stromal-related microenvironment that supports tumor growth and progression. However, these findings are based mainly on correlation analyses, and further experimental studies are needed to determine whether DRGs directly regulate immune infiltration, stromal remodeling, or tumor microenvironment formation.

Although our findings provide new insights into the role of disulfidptosis in cancer, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, our analysis was primarily based on publicly available datasets and a limited number of DRGs, which may not fully capture the breadth of this mechanism across all cancer types. Second, while we identified correlations between DRGs and clinical outcomes, further experimental validation in larger and more diverse cohorts is needed to confirm their relevance in prognosis and treatment. Third, the normal controls used for osteosarcoma differential expression analysis were obtained from GTEx skeletal muscle tissues rather than matched normal bone or osteoblast tissues. Therefore, these bioinformatics results should be interpreted with caution and were complemented by qRT-PCR validation in hFOB cells and osteosarcoma cell lines. Finally, the interactions between disulfidptosis and other forms of cell death, such as ferroptosis and necroptosis, warrant further investigation to clarify their collective impact on tumor behavior and treatment response.

Overall, our findings suggest that disulfidptosis-related transcriptional patterns are associated with multiple malignant features across cancers. Further functional and clinical studies are needed to clarify their mechanistic roles and therapeutic value.

Conclusions

This study systematically evaluated the prognostic and biological relevance of DRGs across cancers and further explored their potential implications in osteosarcoma. The DRG-based signature was associated with patient prognosis and several malignant tumor features, including EMT, cell cycle activity, tumor microenvironment characteristics, stemness-related scores, and drug sensitivity. In osteosarcoma, several key DRGs showed dysregulated expression and were further validated by qRT-PCR. These findings suggest that DRGs may provide useful clues for prognosis assessment and future studies on tumor progression. Further mechanistic and clinical validation is needed to confirm their functional roles and therapeutic value.
