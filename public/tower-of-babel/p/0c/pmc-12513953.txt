Risk factors for postoperative bile leakage and mortality following bilio-enteric anastomosis: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Anisse Tidjane, Aicha Bengueddach, Nacim Ikhlef, Sif Islam Meharzi, Hakim Larbi, Juba Mansouri, Benali Tabeti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBilio-enteric anastomosis surgery (BEA) is a critical surgical procedure used to restore bile flow in patients with biliary obstruction or after bile duct resection. Despite its importance, the procedure is associated with complications, such as bile leakage and postoperative mortality. Identifying the risk factors for these complications can enhance the surgical outcomes and patient safety. This study aimed to identify the factors associated with bile leakage and postoperative mortality following BEA.MethodsWe conducted a prospective cohort study at our department of Hepatobiliary surgery, from November 2020 to April 2022. Data on demographics, preoperative conditions, surgical details, and outcomes were collected. Univariate and multivariate binary logistic regression analyses were used to identify predictors of bile leakage and 90-day mortality.ResultsWe included 107 adult patients who underwent BEA. Bile leakage occurred in 28% of the cases, and postoperative mortality rates were 2.8% at 30 days and 4.7% at 90 days. Factors significantly associated with bile leakage included: surgery performed for cancer (OR = 3.661, 95% CI [1.243–10.782], P = 0.019) and blood transfusion (OR = 3.834, 95% CI [1.153–12.750], P = 0.028). Independent predictors of 90-day mortality were preoperative serum total bilirubin levels (OR = 1.095, 95% CI [1.003-1.195], P = 0.042) and postoperative biliary peritonitis (Bile leakage Grade C) (OR = 35.747, 95% CI [3.068–416.5], P = 0.004).ConclusionsBEA performed for cancer and blood transfusion are independent factors associated with a higher incidence of bile leakage. In contrast, preoperative bilirubin levels and postoperative biliary peritonitis are independent factors associated with an increased risk of 90-day mortality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00423-025-03740-6.

Introduction

Bilio-enteric anastomosis is a surgical procedure that entails establishing a connection between the biliary tree and the small intestine, typically the jejunum or the duodenum. This procedure is primarily employed to reinstate the bile flow from into the digestive system. It becomes necessary when biliary obstructions occur secondary to various pathologies or when resections of the main bile duct are performed as a surgical procedure, due to both benign and malignant conditions.

Various surgical techniques exist for restoring biliary-enteric continuity. The biliary tree is most commonly connected to the jejunum, either through a Roux-en-Y anastomosis or an Omega loop, and less somme times bile tree is anastomosed to the duodenum. The anastomosis can involve the bile duct, common hepatic duct, first or second order hepatic duct branches, intra-hepatic bile duct and rarely, the gallbladder. These connections can be performed in an end-to-side or side-to-side manner, using either continuous or interrupted sutures [1].

This surgery is associated with complications, the main complication is bile leakage occurring in about 10% of cases, and this surgery is associated with a low rate of mortality (1–2%).

It’s crucial to identify patients at a higher risk of complications and mortality. Understanding these risk factors can significantly contribute to better identifying patients who may be at higher risk. Identifying these patients can lead to refining surgical techniques and improving post-operative care, ultimately reducing risks and enhancing the overall safety and success of the procedure. In response to these critical questions, our study was conducted to provide insights into these risk factors.

Materials and methods

This observational, prospective cohort study was conducted in the department of hepatobiliary surgery, EHU-November 1 st, 1954 Hospital, a specialized center for hepatobiliary and pancreatic surgery in Algeria. The objectives of this study were to identify factors associated with the occurrence of bile leakage and postoperative mortality following bilio-enteric surgical anastomosis.

According to the International Study Group of Liver Surgery, bile leakage was defined as a bilirubin level in the drain fluid at least 3 times the serum bilirubin concentration on or after postoperative day 3 or as the need for radiologic or operative intervention resulting from biliary collections or bile peritonitis [2]. Postoperative mortality was defined as death occurring on or before the 90 th postoperative day, regardless of the cause.

The study was conducted from November 1, 2020, to April 31, 2022. During this 18-month study period, enrollment was temporarily halted in July and August 2021 due to the disruptions caused by the COVID-19 pandemic [3].

All patients who underwent open or laparoscopic bilio-enteric anastomosis surgery during the study inclusion period were considered for inclusion. The study included only adults aged > 18 years old who agreed to participate to this observational study. Patients who underwent any associated major surgery, such as a major hepatectomy (involving more than 3 liver segments) or a duodeno-pancreatectomy, were excluded.

Ethical approval for the study protocol was obtained from the ethics committee at EHU-1st November 1954, Oran, Algeria. in November 2021. The study was conducted in compliance with ethical guidelines, ensuring patient confidentiality and obtaining informed consent from all participants. The study was prospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04665427).

Data collection involved the collection of 56 key variables for each patient, encompassing patient demographics, pre-surgery health conditions, surgical details, and outcomes. Follow-up assessments were conducted daily during postoperative hospitalization and periodically every week after discharge, with follow-up extending to the 90 th postoperative day.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 24. Univariate binary logistic regression analysis was initially conducted to identify variables associated with postoperative bile leakage and 90-day mortality. Variables with a p-value < 0.10 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariate binary logistic regression analysis. In the final multivariate analysis, only variables with a p-value < 0.05 were retained to determine their independent associations with the outcomes. Results are expressed as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and p-values.

Results

During the inclusion period, a total of 107 patients were included in the study involving 54 women (50.5%), Sex Ratio was 0.98. The mean age of our patients was 60.9 ± 12.4 years, with an age range from 26 to 85 years. Comorbidities were observed in 49.5% of cases, and notably, 45 patients (42.1%) had a history of upper gastrointestinal surgery. Additionally, 13 cases (12.1%) underwent before surgery an endoscopic procedure, including biliary stenting.

Cancer was the primary diagnosis upon admission in 69 cases (64.5%). Procedures included hepatico-jejunostomy in 56 cases (52.3%), choledocho-duodenostomy in 50 cases (46.7%), and intrahepatic cholangio-jejunostomy on the S3 bile duct in one case (0.9%). Laparoscopic surgery was performed in 12 cases (11.2%), with one case necessitating conversion to open surgery. Additionally, associated procedures included cholecystectomy in 25 cases (23.4%), extra-hepatic bile duct resection in 18 cases (16.8%), minor hepatectomy in 5 cases (4.7%), lymphadenectomy in 10 cases (9.3%), and associated digestive diversion in 5 cases (4.7%).

Blood transfusion was administered during or after surgery in 14 cases (13%), and mainly the transfusion was given in ICU in case of complication after surgery in 10 cases (9,3%). The primary postoperative complication observed was bile leakage in 30 cases (28%), According to the ISGLS classification, bile leakage was graded as follows: grade A in 11 cases (10.3%), grade B in 14 cases (13.1%), and grade C in 5 cases (4.7%). Post operative hemorragy occurred in one case (0.9%). Redo surgery was necessary in 7 cases, and it was performed by laparoscopy in 4 cases. The reasons for revision surgery were biliary peritonitis (Grade C bile leakage) in 5 cases, haemorrhage in one case, and laparoscopic revision with no intra-operative findings in one case.

There were three complications after discharge necessitating readmission. The first patient had a severe SARS-CoV-2 infection, requiring admission to the intensive care unit and died on day 67. The second patient developed an ischemic stroke. The third patient was readmitted for a revision laparoscopy due to a Grade C bile leak after accidental drain dislodgement on the sixth day post-discharge. Postoperative mortality rates were 2.8% at the 30 th postoperative day and increased to 4.7% by the 90 th postoperative day.

Analysis of postoperative bile leakage

In univariate binary logistic regression analysis, BEA performed for any cancer disease and Gallbladder cancer are associated with a significantly increased incidence of postoperative bile leakage with respectively (OR = 3.95, 95% CI=[1.37–11.41], P = 0.011) and (OR = 4.99, 95% IC=[1.099–22.142], P = 0.037). Additionally, higher volume of intraoperative blood loss (OR = 1.008, 95% CI=[1.001–1.016], P = 0.021), Giving blood transfusion (OR = 4.330, 95% IC=[1.347–13.747], P = 0.014) and GEA associated to minor liver resection (OR = 11.692, 95% IC=[1.250–109.40], P = 0.031) was associated with an elevated risk of external bile leakage (Table 1).

Discussion

Understanding the factors predisposing patients to adverse postoperative outcomes is paramount in optimizing patient care and surgical outcomes. The present study aimed to elucidate the factors contributing to bile leakage and postoperative mortality following billion-enteric surgical anastomosis. Bilio-enteric anastomosis is crucial for restoring the bile flow in cases of biliary tree obstruction or resection, but it is not devoid of complications [4].

Bile leakage represents a significant challenge among these complications, occurring in 28% of cases in our cohort. This rate is higher than that reported in other series, where the highest rate is up to 10% in the series by Zafar et al. [5], and much lower in others [4, 6]. However, most of these studies were published before 2011, and the definition of bile leakage needed to be clarified and varied from study to study before standardized definitions and grading systems were published [2]. In our study population, bilirubin in drain liquid was evaluated systematically from day 3 until drain removal at any suspicion of abnormal colouration of the drained liquid.

This may be the main reason our study’s bile leakage rate was high. We systematically monitored bile leakage by prospectively measuring bilirubin levels in drain fluid, which led to the detection of a higher rate of Grade A bile leakage (10.3%). This minor leakage is usually overlooked in clinical practice, but our rigorous detection method captures these cases.

According to our inclusion criteria, we excluded patients who underwent Whipple procedures or major hepatectomies due to their distinct risk profiles for postoperative bile leakage. In major hepatectomy, bile leakage can arise from the raw hepatic surface rather than the bilio-enteric anastomosis itself, making it difficult to accurately attribute the source of the leak [7]. Similarly, in Whipple procedures, the presence of postoperative pancreatic fistula is a known confounding factor. Pancreatic secretions have an erosive effect on the bilio-enteric anastomosis, significantly increasing the risk of combined bile leakage and pancreatic fistula. Including these two procedures could introduce bias and reduce the specificity of our findings regarding bile leakage following bilio-enteric anastomosis [8, 9].

In our analysis, BEA performed for cancer disease, BEA performed for the management of gallbladder cancer, blood transfusion, and the volume of intraoperative blood loss are factors associated with a higher rate of bile leakage in the univariate analysis. However, in multivariate analysis, only BEA performed for cancer disease and blood transfusion are independent factors associated with postoperative bile leakage. The retrospective series of Antolovic et al. analysing outcomes of hepaticojejunostomy performed in a cohort of 519 patients showed other factors independently associated with the occurrence of postoperative bile leakage such as preoperative radiochemotherapy, high preoperative cholinesterase level, redo after liver transplantation and liver resection [4]. In our series, only 2 (1.9%) patients had neoadjuvant chemotherapy for pancreatic cancer. This factor was not associated with a higher rate of BL, and no BL occurred in these two patients. In the retrospective series of Kadaba et al., including 462 BEA, other independent factors were associated with the occurrence of bile leakage, such as surgery performed for bile duct injury and anastomosis performed above the biliary confluence [10]. In another prospective series that included 63 hepaticojejunostomy performed for the management of strictured bile duct injury, postoperative bile leakage occurred in 17.5% of cases [11].

In our series, and according to our analysis, BEA performed for bile duct injury was not an independent factor associated with BL. It is important to emphasize that BEA for bile duct injury was performed in 18.7% of our population, a higher percentage compared to the 4.2% reported in the series by Kadaba et al. [10].

In the retrospective series of Zafar et al., including 79 patients having a biliary enteric anastomosis for benign disease, the analysis showed that a low serum Albumin level and the ASA statute were independent factors associated with the occurrence of early postoperative complications [5]. According to our analysis, the serum total protein level and the ASA statute were not associated with the higher incidence of postoperative bile leakage in our study population.

According to our analysis, perioperative blood transfusion was an independent factor associated with bile leakage. In a retrospective cohort study analyzing blood transfusion and its impact on the occurrence of anastomotic leakage in patients operated on for colorectal cancer, the incidence of anastomotic leakage was significantly higher in the group of patients who had preoperative blood transfusion. However, this study only included a univariate analysis [12]. Another retrospective study gives a similar conclusion regarding blood transfusion and the risk of anastomotic leaks in patients operated on for Gastro-oesophageal junction cancer [13].

In a randomized prospective study comparing continuous sutures with interrupted sutures in patients undergoing biliary-enteric anastomosis, primarily for malignant disease, the endpoint was to compare outcomes between the two groups. There was no difference in the incidence of postoperative bile leakage between the two suture technique groups [14]. In our study, there was no difference in the type of suture, the material used for the suture, or the diameter of the surgical suture thread.

Studies have extensively examined how postoperative complications impact oncologic outcomes, such as recurrence and survival rates. However, fewer investigations address cancer itself as an independent risk factor for complications [15, 16].

Our study highlights that patients undergoing surgery for cancer are at a higher risk of postoperative bile leakage compared to those with benign conditions. This may stem from anatomical distortions, fibrosis, or bile duct invasion by malignancies, complicating dissection and reconstruction. Despite the anticipated impact of neoadjuvant therapy on tissue integrity, our analysis did not find it to be a significant factor for bile leakage. Similarly, albuminemia, often linked to malnutrition and poor outcomes, was not associated with increased bile leakage risk in our cohort.

During the follow-up period, one case of embolism and no pulmonary infection were observed among our study population. This absence may also be attributed to the relatively small sample size, which may not have been large enough to capture less common adverse events.

The overall mortality rate associated with BEA surgical procedures remains relatively low, ranging from 1 to 6% [5, 10], In our cohort, the mortality rate was 2,8% on day 30 and 4,7% on day 90; this variation in mortality rate is the fact that some studies included cases of duodenopancreatectomy and cases of associated major hepatectomy; these surgeries are associated with a high rate of postoperative mortality [10, 17]. On the other hand, some studies have reported postoperative mortality rates on Day 30 after surgery [5, 10], Implementing this approach may drastically reduce mortality rates among patients who require monitoring until Day 90 after surgery.

After reviewing factors influencing postoperative mortality, we found a study that analyzed the factors affecting postoperative mortality in surgeries for obstructive jaundice. This study showed in a multivariate analysis that serum bilirubin levels, hematocrit, and malignant disease significantly impacted overall postoperative mortality. However, in this study, less than half of the studied population had BEA as the surgical management for obstructive jaundice [18].

Wronka et al. highlighted the impact of preoperative serum bilirubin level on postoperative outcomes and mortality in patients undergoing curative resection of hilar cholangiocarcinoma [19]. In other studies, the preoperative serum bilirubin level was associated with prolonged postoperative hospitalization [20], and increased rate of overall early postoperative morbidity in BEA patients [11].

Our analysis found that preoperative serum bilirubin levels and the occurrence of postoperative biliary peritonitis (bile leakage Grade C) were independent factors associated with a higher rate of 90-day postoperative mortality. In the series of Denci et al., biliary peritonitis in gallbladder perforation is associated with a high mortality rate up to 15% [21], In our series, among the five patients who developed postoperative biliary peritonitis, the mortality rate was as high as 20%.

The analysis of bile leakage as a factor influencing 90-day mortality did not initially show any statistically significant influence. However, when the subgroup of patients with Grade C bile leaks was included as a risk factor, the multivariate analysis revealed that this factor significantly affected the 90-day mortality rate. The retrospective series of DeCastro et al., including 1033 hepaticojejunostomy, showed no mortality in the group of patients having bile leakage (2.3%) despite the high rate of patients requiring relaparotomy [6].

While prior studies have examined bile leakage and mortality following bilio-enteric anastomosis (BEA), significant gaps remain in understanding procedure-specific risk factors, largely due to heterogeneous cohorts and inconsistent complication reporting. Our prospective analysis addresses these limitations by focusing exclusively on isolated BEA cases and applying standardized leak detection protocols, thereby disentangling anastomotic risk from confounding surgical interventions (e.g., hepatectomy, Whipple procedures). In our study, we excluded confounding factors that could obscure the origin of bile leakage—such as Whipple procedures and major hepatectomies—while, on the other hand, adopting a more inclusive approach regarding underlying pathologies and types of bilio-enteric anastomosis.

Notably, we identify cancer-related reconstruction and perioperative blood transfusion as independent predictors of leakage—factors underexplored in the existing literature. These findings underscore the need for tailored perioperative strategies in high-risk patients and should prompt multicenter collaborations to validate risk stratification models and refine technical approaches (e.g., anastomotic techniques, transfusion practices) in both malignant and benign contexts. In this regard, identifying at-risk patients and implementing systematic clinical, biological, and radiological screening protocols could facilitate early detection of bile leakage and improve outcomes.

Regarding postoperative mortality, our analysis identified preoperative hyperbilirubinemia as an independent predictor of death, irrespective of nutritional status, underlying pathology, or type of BEA surgery, a finding confirmed through multivariate analysis. This highlights that jaundiced patients face elevated mortality risk regardless of disease etiology or nutritional optimization. Crucially, this raises the question of whether preoperative biliary drainage (endoscopic or radiological) could mitigate this risk by reducing bilirubin levels prior to surgery.

Future multicenter studies should aim to validate this association and investigate the potential benefits of preoperative drainage, along with tailored surgical strategies (e.g., selective drainage), to improve outcomes in high-risk subgroups.

Despite our study’s strengths, including a prospective cohort design and rigorous data analysis, several limitations should be acknowledged. These include the single-centre nature of the study and the relatively small sample size, especially the mortality rate, which did not allow for the emergence of some risk factors in the multivariate analysis of mortality. Future research with larger, multicenter cohorts is needed to validate our findings.

Our study provides valuable insights into the risk factors contributing to bile leakage and postoperative mortality following bilio-enteric surgical anastomosis. By identifying these factors and elucidating their clinical implications, we aim to facilitate risk stratification, enhance patient selection criteria, and ultimately improve the safety and success of this important surgical procedure. Future research with larger, multicenter cohorts is needed to validate our findings.
