Integrating microplastics into thermal biology in an insect
Authors: Z R Stahlschmidt, K Ngeow, J Ryu, R Aujla, R Wang, S Mothukuri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicroplastics (MPs) are small-sized (< 5 mm) bits of plastic present in all types of environments, including terrestrial ecosystems that are rapidly warming. Yet, the biological interplay between MPs and temperature is poorly understood in terrestrial animals. Here, we addressed three hypotheses to determine how: (1) temperature influences biological responses to MPs, (2) MPs influence thermal biology, and (3) temperature and MPs combine to influence the acquisition and allocation of resources. Specifically, we fed field crickets (Gryllus lineaticeps) different concentrations of nylon (polyamide) microfilaments while they were maintained at 23 °C, 28 °C, or 33 °C. Despite ingesting 2.5-fold more MPs, warmer individuals did not absorb more MPs into their bodies. Exposure to MPs increased investment into somatic tissue and self-maintenance, but individuals consuming MPs still had lower desiccation tolerance. Warming and MPs both promoted food consumption, but they differentially affected the life-history tradeoff between investment into self-maintenance vs. reproduction. In sum, appetite, life-history strategy, and dynamics in the digestive tract may be critical to animals simultaneously exposed to warming and MPs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10646-026-03061-4.Keywords: Environmental change, cricket, life history, stress tolerance, temperature, tradeoff

Introduction

Over 400 million tons of plastic are produced each year, making plastics a ubiquitous feature of environmental change (Geyer 2020). Microplastics (MPs; < 5 mm) and associated contaminants are an omnipresent risk to biological systems (from inducing oxidative stress in animals to destabilizing ecosystems: reviewed in Sridharan et al. 2021; Das 2023), and they have been found in many organismal systems, including a range of animal tissues (Charlton-Howard et al. 2023; Yang et al. 2023; Nihart et al. 2025; Wang and Zhang 2025). The number of papers on MPs published annually has increased nearly 100-fold in the past decade (Gopalakrishnan et al. 2023), and scientists have begun to investigate how biological responses to MPs are influenced by other components of environmental change. In aquatic animals, elevated temperatures associated with global climate change can exacerbate MP toxicity related to growth, reproduction, and survival (fishes: Wen et al. 2018; Hasan et al. 2023; crustaceans: Han et al. 2021; Gholamhosseini et al. 2023; Klasios et al. 2024). Yet, the evidence for additive or synergistic costs of MPs and warming is equivocal (aquatic insect: Silva et al. 2022; crustacean: Chang et al. 2023a; frog: Park and Do 2024). Global warming is greatest over land (IPCC 2022), and terrestrial ecosystems are home to Earth’s most biodiverse habitats (Anthony et al. 2023) and commonly contain MPs (reviewed in Sridharan et al. 2021; Rafa et al. 2024; Zeb et al. 2024). In fact, insects in the field can contain two to three orders of magnitude more MPs than fish from comparable regions (reviewed in Li et al. 2024). Yet, the interplay between temperature-dependent biological phenomena (i.e., thermal biology) and MPs in terrestrial animals is not understood.

Terrestrial animals, including insects, are exposed to MPs in their food (reviewed in Khan et al. 2024; Sucharitakul et al. 2024), and elevated temperatures increase energy expenditures leading to increased food consumption for most animals (i.e., ectotherms) (Gillooly et al. 2001; Angilletta 2009). Plastics can release contaminants into the environment (e.g., bisphenol A [BPA] and phthalates: Teuten et al. 2009), and warming can increase the toxicity of such leachates from MPs in aquatic systems (Uguen et al. 2024). However, the gastrointestinal tract (GIT) can mitigate the costs of warming-induced MP exposure by blocking MP interactions with delicate internal tissues. First, gut microbiota is best-studied in mammals where it can mediate MP-related systemic toxicity (reviewed in Pan et al. 2024). Second, animals may limit the translocation of MPs through tight junctions (vertebrates) or septate junctions (insects) in the epithelium that regulate the paracellular absorption of larger molecules (and, presumably, MPs) traveling through the GIT (Karasov 2011; Beyenbach 2012). Last, some animals (fish) can rapidly and selectively excrete MPs through the GIT whereby the gut transit time for MPs is significantly shorter than that for food (Roch et al. 2021). Only 0.3% or fewer of ingested MPs are translocated across the GIT in some groups (rodents: Carr et al. 2012), indicating the threat of warming-induced exposure to MPs may be minimized by GIT dynamics in some animals.

However, warming may reduce the effectiveness of GIT’s defense against MP toxicity, as elevated temperatures increase the bioaccumulation of MPs in aquatic animals (fish: Wen et al. 2018; crustacean: Na et al. 2023). These studies suggest some translocation of MPs may be passive (i.e., a lack of strong regulation) whereby the number of MPs ingested positively correlates with the number of MPs absorbed through the GIT lining. Further, the regulation of MP translocation may become impaired at higher temperatures because digestive physiology is strongly influenced by temperature in ectotherms (lizard: Angilletta et al. 2002; insects: Clissold and Simpson 2015; salamander: Fontaine et al. 2018; fish: Adams et al. 2022). Therefore, increasing temperatures may make animals particularly vulnerable to MPs—warmer animals ingest more MPs that release more toxic leachates, and ingested MPs may be absorbed (bioaccumulated) at a higher rate.

Microplastics can also physically damage and impair the GIT, resulting in inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis in vertebrates and invertebrates (Fan et al. 2025; reviewed in Hirt and Body-Malapel 2020; Ding et al. 2023; Richard et al. 2024). Perturbations to the digestive tract have broad implications given the GIT’s fundamental role in digesting food and maintaining water balance in insects (Chapman et al. 2012). Thus, physical or oxidative damage due to MPs can impair nutrient absorption or water reabsorption across taxa (reviewed in Fröhlich 2024; Khan et al. 2024). The resultant reduction in available resources could, in turn, decrease water balance or investment into self-maintenance leaving animals vulnerable to warming-related stressors, such as heat and desiccation (IPCC 2022; but see Chang et al. 2023a [crustacean]). Self-maintenance can trade off with reproduction when resources are limited, and this classic life-history tradeoff is influenced by combinations of stressors, such as heat waves and predation risk, and the scarcity of food and mates or water (insect: Padda and Stahlschmidt 2022; Stahlschmidt et al. 2024a, b). Temperature could similarly modulate the effects of MPs on allocation to important traits and affect life-history tradeoffs. Therefore, thermal biology and MP biology may be highly interconnected— warming may increase the risk of MP exposure and alter life-history strategies while MP exposure may reduce tolerance to warming-related stressors.

To integrate MPs into thermal biology, we factorially manipulated temperature (23 °C, 28 °C, or 33 °C) and the concentration of nylon (polyamide) MPs in food (control [zero added], low, or high: see below). We used the variable field cricket (Gryllus lineaticeps) because its stress tolerance, investment into reproduction and soma, and food consumption are influenced by temperature and other environmental factors (Padda and Stahlschmidt 2022; Bonfoey et al.2023; Stahlschmidt et al. 2024a, b). We tested three fundamental hypotheses linking thermal biology and MP biology:

Temperature influences MP biology. Here, we predicted the ratio of MP absorption (number of MPs absorbed into the body cavity [hemocoel] divided by number of MPs ingested) will be greater at higher temperatures because the combined effects of MPs and heat will reduce digestive capabilities similar to other taxa (fish: Wen et al. 2018). In turn, animals in warmer conditions will absorb significantly more MPs than those in cooler conditions.

Exposure to MPs influences thermal biology. Here, we predicted that higher concentrations of MPs in food will reduce both heat and desiccation tolerance because MP-exposed individuals will have fewer available resources due to perturbations in the GIT, which is vital to water balance and nutrient absorption in insects (Chapman et al. 2012). Negative energy and water balance due to food and water limitation, respectively, can reduce heat tolerance in insects (Da Lage et al. 1989; Nguyen et al. 2017; Mir and Qamar 2018; Johnson and Stahlschmidt 2020; but see Gonzalez et al. 2024).

Here, we predicted antagonism between warming and MPs related to food consumption and the tradeoff between investment into reproductive and somatic tissues during early adulthood. Materials and methods

Study system

Gryllus lineaticeps is largely found in California, U.S., which is increasingly warm and dry (Weissman and Gray 2019; IPCC 2022). Like other Gryllus crickets (Zera and Larsen 2001; Zera 2005), G. lineaticeps is wing-dimorphic where adults are either long-winged and potentially flight-capable, or short-winged and flightless. We only used short-winged crickets in the study, and we acquired them from a long-term colony that we subsidized every 1–2 years by progeny from females collected from a natural population (Sedgwick Reserve [SR], Santa Ynez, California, U.S.) that predominantly expresses the short-winged phenotype (authors pers. obs.; L.A. Treidel pers. comm.). We reared G. lineaticeps in standard conditions (14 h:10 h light: dark cycle with ad libitum access to water, commercial dry cat food (Complete Nutrition, Special Kitty®, Bentonville, AR: 33% protein, 12% fat, 4% fiber; 3513 kcal/kg), and cardboard egg cartons for shelter) at 28 ± 1 °C. When they reached adulthood (i.e., within 1 d of adult molt; Day 1), we weighed each female and individually housed it in a 1.9 L transparent plastic container along with a cotton-plugged water bottle as a drinking source and a cardboard shelter. We then assigned each cricket to one of two experiments (see below).

Experiment 1: Effects of temperature on biological responses to MPs

We used a 3 × 3 factorial design to study the independent and interactive effects of temperature (23 °C, 28 °C, or 33 °C) and nylon MP exposure (control [zero added], low, or high: see below) on the estimated mass of reproductive and somatic tissues gained, the amount of food consumed, and the amount of MPs absorbed into the body (n = 27–36 for each of the nine treatment combinations). At adulthood (< 1 d post-adult molt; Day 1), we transferred crickets in their containers to incubators (model I-36, Percival Scientific, Inc., Perry, IA, U.S.) maintained at constant 23 °C (n = 105), 28 °C (n = 101), or 33 °C (n = 101) because these temperatures reflect the range of thermal microclimates used by G. lineaticeps in the field (Stahlschmidt et al. 2022a, b). Incubators had a 14 h:10 h light: dark cycle with 20–40% relative humidity.

We offered Day 1 crickets a diet of dried, ground cat food (< 1,100 μm greatest dimension) in pre-weighed, spill-resistant petri dishes. For two groups, we mixed nylon microfilaments (Bright red nylon flocking, Flocking Unlimited) into the food at either low or high levels (i.e., 0.6% [n = 100] or 1.2% [n = 106] w/w, respectively) by pulsing in a blender (magic bullet, Capbran Holdings, LLC, Los Angeles, CA, U.S.) for 5–10 s. For a third group, we offered additive-free food (n = 101). Using a dissecting microscope (S8 APO, Leica Microsystems), we imaged 60 filaments atop a glass stage micrometer and analyzed width (diameter) and length using imaging software (ImageJ, v1.5, National Institutes of Health) to determine the size of filaments (mean ± s.e.m.; diameter: 16.8 ± 0.3 μm; length: 816.6 ± 1.0 μm). The concentrations and sizes of MPs that G. lineaticeps are exposed to in the field is unknown. However, the concentrations we used were within the range of levels of plastic contamination reported in soils (Fuller and Gautam 2016; Okoffo et al. 2022), and MP filaments or fibers found in insects in the field range from < 5 μm to > 1,300 μm in length (reviewed in Li et al. 2024). A recent study on crickets used similar levels of MP exposure in ground food with polyester fibers (Fudlosid et al. 2022), and we adjusted the concentrations based on the difference in density between nylon and polyester.

We used nylon as a MP polymer type because it has been found in ecosystems worldwide, insects, and human food sources (Shahu Hamid et al. 2018; Sfriso et al. 2020; Corami et al. 2022; Yadav et al. 2022; Li et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2024). Nylon MPs are released during the washing and drying of clothing (Choi et al. 2022), and they can influence animal behavior and physiology (crustacean: Cole et al. 2019; terrestrial worm: Lahive et al. 2019; fish: Zhang et al. 2022a, b; insect: Zhong et al. 2022; fish: Choi and Kim 2023; crustacean: Fang et al. 2023; fish: Hasan et al. 2023; mollusk: Pan et al. 2025). To promote consumption of the MPs, we used (1) filaments of a similar size to food particles (both ≲ 1 mm in greatest dimension), and (2) red filaments because crickets lack red photoreceptors (reviewed in Levy et al. 2024). Pilot data indicated that G. lineaticeps readily consumed the filaments when they were mixed into ground cat food, and that filament-filled feces were evacuated within 4 h for all temperature treatments (Fig. S1). The number of MP filaments in food trays did not increase after high-MP food was eaten (Fig. S2), which would have indicated that crickets avoided the MPs and focused their feeding on food particles. Crickets did not avoid MP-contaminated food (Fig. 1b), further indicating their MP exposure was relatively proportionate to the concentration of MPs in food.

During the first 5 d of adulthood at 28 °C, female short-winged Gryllus crickets can consume their body mass in food to fuel a dramatic (up to 100-fold) increase in ovary mass (Zera 2005; Stahlschmidt 2022). Therefore, after 5 d (i.e., Day 6), we re-weighed each surviving cricket (n = 18 crickets died) and removed its food tray. We euthanized and stored crickets at -20 °C prior to dissection. We acclimated food trays to room conditions to match the conditions trays were weighed at on Day 1 overnight. Then, we removed non-food items (e.g., fecal pellets) from the trays, and we re-weighed the trays to determine the consumption of food after subtracting the mass attributed to MPs (i.e., 0.6% and 1.2% for low and high MP treatments, respectively).

After 7–28 d of storage at -20 °C, we performed dissections under a dissecting microscope (Fig. S3; S8 APO, Leica Microsystems) to determine reproductive investment and to count the number of MP filaments found within the hemocoel. Ice crystallization during storage may have reduced the integrity of the GIT, leading to the confounding effect of post-mortem translocation of MPs into the hemocoel. Therefore, we conducted a validation study demonstrating that crickets stored in the freezer for 7 d or 28 d did not exhibit different levels of MP translocation relative to freshly euthanized crickets (Fig. S4). Likewise, a separate validation study demonstrated that − 20 °C storage reduced estimated reproductive investment by approx. 2%, but there were no differences between individuals stored for 7 d vs. 28 d (Fig. S5).

Prior to each dissection, we cleaned all instruments with moist paper towels and inspected instruments under the microscope to ensure they were free of MPs to prevent MP contamination. We first made a longitudinal incision along the ventral midline to expose the body cavity (hemocoel) and internal organs. Then, we carefully severed the GIT at the pharynx while keeping the posterior end of the GIT intact. We inspected the pharyngeal incision to ensure that no MP filaments leaked out of the GIT and into the hemocoel. After counting MP filaments on the hemocoel-facing surface of the GIT, we used forceps to rotate the GIT posteriorly so that it was positioned posterior to the anus. Once the GIT was moved out of the way, we visually investigated the entirety of the hemocoel for red MP filaments, which were highly conspicuous under the microscope. We counted the number of MP filaments found in the hemocoel (including those attached to the GIT) to determine the number of MPs translocated or absorbed through the GIT and into the body cavity.

We accounted for the effects of MP ingestion on MP absorption by estimating the MP absorption ratio in low-MP and high-MP crickets. We did not observe any MPs in the hemocoels of control-MP crickets. We determined MP absorption ratio as the number of absorbed MPs divided by the estimated number of MPs consumed, which was based on the amount and treatment of food consumed. Also during dissections, we removed ovaries from crickets and then weighed ovaries as a proxy for fecundity (Roff 1994).

We estimated investment into and allocation to reproductive and non-reproductive (somatic) tissues during early adulthood as previously described (Stahlschmidt et al. 2023, 2024a, b). We used the difference between final body mass and fresh ovary mass to estimate final somatic mass. It is not possible to non-destructively determine the mass of crickets’ ovary or somatic tissue, which is required to precisely measure how much ovary or somatic tissue that each cricket gained over time. However, fresh ovary mass comprises 0.98% of total body mass, on average, at the onset of adulthood in G. lineaticeps (Stahlschmidt et al. 2022a, b). Therefore, we estimated ovary mass and somatic mass at the start of the experiment, and we compared these values to final values to estimate how much reproductive and somatic tissue was gained during the experiment.

Experiment 2: Effects of MP exposure on heat and desiccation tolerance

We determined the effects of MP exposure (control [zero added], low, or high: see above) on heat and desiccation tolerance. We provided each cricket (n = 202) with one of the three food treatments (see above). After 5 d at 28 °C, we randomly assigned one group of the crickets (n = 92) to static heat trials as described previously (Bonfoey et al. 2023). We first placed each cricket into a 50 ml conical tube with a wet cotton ball at room temperature to acclimate for 1 h, and then we placed each cricket into an open glass jar (473 ml) maintained at 45 °C submerged in a hot water bath. We flipped the crickets onto their dorsum each minute, and we used the time at which each cricket was unable to right itself (min.) as an estimate of heat tolerance. We randomly assigned the remaining crickets (n = 110) to desiccation trials in room at 20–40% relative humidity and 28 ± 1 °C. We removed water bottles from crickets’ individual containers. We checked crickets daily, and we used the duration of life without drinking water (d) as an estimate of desiccation tolerance.

Statistical analyses

We tested data for normality before analyzing using SPSS (v.28 IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). We determined two-tailed significance at α < 0.05. To examine the effects of treatments (temperature and MP exposure), we performed linear model analyses on the (1) amount of food consumed, (2) estimated MP absorption ratio, (3) number of MP filaments absorbed, and estimated mass of (4) reproductive and (5) somatic tissues gained in Experiment 1. For Experiment 2, we performed linear model analyses on heat tolerance and desiccation tolerance with MP exposure as the main effect. For all models, we included treatments as main effects and initial (Day 1) body mass as a covariate to account for differences in body size.

For Experiment 1, our factorial design obligated nine treatment combinations, making it impractical to examine pairwise differences that account for multiple comparisons (e.g., Bonferroni adjustments for 36 comparisons). Further, we did not detect evidence of hormetic effects because intermediate treatment levels (i.e., 28 °C and low-MP diet) tended to result in intermediate trait values. Therefore, we focused our tests on the main and interactive effects of temperature and MP treatments. For Experiment 2, we used Sidak tests to perform pairwise comparisons between the three MP treatments.

We report significant results (P values < 0.05) for main effects below (Results) and in Figs. 1 and 2, and we report full results in Tables 1 and 2.

Results

Heavier individuals and those exposed to higher temperatures and nylon MPs exhibited greater food consumption (Fig. 1a; Table 1a). Individuals in cooler, low MP treatments absorbed MPs at a relatively high ratio (Fig. 1b; Table 1b). Higher MP exposure—but not higher temperature— increased the total number of MPs absorbed into the body (Fig. 1c; Table 1c). That is, increased MP consumption (Fig. 1a) at warmer temperatures (Fig. 1b) was offset by reduced MP absorption ratio, thereby leading to no effect of temperature on the absolute number of MP filaments absorbed into the body (Fig. 1c).

Heavier individuals and those exposed to higher temperatures increased reproductive investment, and higher temperatures also decreased somatic investment (Fig. 1d, e; Table 1d, e). In contrast, MP exposure increased somatic investment (Fig. 1e; Table 1e). Desiccation—but not heat— tolerance was reduced by MP exposure (Fig. 2; Table 2).

Discussion

Terrestrial animals are simultaneously exposed to rapid warming and MPs (Geyer 2020; Sridharan et al. 2021; IPCC 2022; Zeb et al. 2024), and animals’ ability to adaptively respond to these emergent features of environmental change may hold the key to their success. Yet, the interconnectedness of thermal biology and MP biology is poorly understood. Here, we manipulated temperature and nylon MP exposure in a field cricket during early adulthood, an energetically expensive life stage (King et al. 2011; Stahlschmidt 2022). Despite ingesting 2.5-fold more MPs, warmer animals did not absorb more MPs into their bodies (Fig. 1a, c). Exposure to MPs increased investment into somatic tissue and self-maintenance, but animals consuming MPs still had lower desiccation tolerance (Figs. 1e and 2b). Warming and MPs both promoted food consumption (Fig. 1a), but they differentially affected the life-history tradeoff between investment into self-maintenance vs. reproduction during early adulthood (Fig. 1d, e). Below, we discuss these results linking thermal and MP biology in relation to our three hypotheses.

Warming did not increase the absorption (translocation) of ingested nylon MPs (Hypothesis 1)

An animal’s exposure to MPs is often measured as the amount of MPs found in its feces or the GIT (birds: Carlin et al. 2020; crustacean: Aytan et al. 2022; fishes: Zazouli et al. 2022; birds: Keys et al. 2023; whale: Torres et al. 2023; reviewed in Pérez-Guevara et al. 2021), even though many ingested MPs are excreted in feces and never interact with cells beyond the GIT (mammals: Carr et al. 2012; fish: Dawson et al. 2025). Therefore, it is important to determine the translocation (or absorption) of MPs across the GIT lining and into the body cavity. In G. lineaticeps, the absorption ratios of 817 μm filaments were dramatically higher than 2 μm MPs absorbed by rodents (2–11% vs. <0.3%, respectively: Fig. 1b; Carr et al. 2012). In vertebrates, only MPs < 150 μm in greatest dimension have been shown to translocate across the GIT lining (reviewed in Hirt and Body-Malapel 2020; Ma et al. 2021). Therefore, insects’ internal tissues may exhibit significant exposure to MPs. The concentration of MPs found in the internal tissues of other animals is increasing (e.g., human brain: Nihart et al. 2025)—thus, insects may offer important insight into animals’ systemic and cellular responses to MPs.

For Hypothesis 1, we predicted warming would increase the amount of MP absorption and result in significantly more MPs in the hemocoel due to the combined costs of MPs and heat to digestive capabilities (Wen et al. 2018). Instead, warming did not affect the overall number of MPs absorbed (Fig. 1c) even though it dramatically increased the ingestion of MPs (Fig. 1a), and warming reduced the ratio of MP absorption (Fig. 1b). These results suggest that warming may improve the regulatory ability of the GIT in insects to reduce the translocation of MPs into the hemocoel. Warmer conditions associated with climate change can provide benefits to temperate insects (Adamo and Lovett 2011; Dale and Frank 2017; Grainger and Gilbert 2017), potentially through improved digestive performance. For example, higher temperatures can reduce MP-GIT interactions by increasing microbial degradation of MPs and/or increasing gut transit speed, which reduce the time for leachates to be released from MPs and for biofilms of pathogenic bacteria to form on MPs (our study: Fig. S5; fish: Volkoff and Rønnestad 2020; insect: Matyakubov and Lee 2024). In addition, the properties of MP fibers themselves could help explain the lower ratios of MP absorption that we observed at a higher temperature (30 °C). Fiber rigidity may be required to pierce the lining of the GIT and facilitate translocation, and nylon is approx. 25% more flexible at 30 °C relative to 20 °C (Toray 2025). Therefore, effects of warming on MP translocation from the GIT may be driven by both physiological processes and physical properties of MPs.

Exposure to nylon MPs reduced tolerance to desiccation, but not heat (Hypothesis 2)

Microplastics can perturb the GIT in vertebrates and invertebrates (Fan et al. 2025; reviewed in Hirt and Body-Malapel 2020; Ding et al. 2023; Richard et al. 2024) thereby impairing the digestive system’s fundamental role in absorbing nutrients and maintaining water balance (reviewed in Chapman et al. 2012; Fröhlich 2024; Khan et al. 2024). As a result, exposure to MPs may make animals more vulnerable to stressors, which elicit physiological responses that are energy- or water-intensive, including heat and water limitation. Although MPs often initiate physiological stress responses (e.g., reviewed in Sun et al. 2021), the effects of MP exposure on functional whole-animal responses (e.g., heat or desiccation tolerance) are unexplored. Thus, for Hypothesis 2, we predicted that individuals ingesting higher concentrations of MPs would exhibit reduced tolerance to two climate-related stressors: heat and desiccation. We found mixed support for this prediction because desiccation (but not heat) tolerance was negatively affected by MP exposure (Fig. 2). We offer four potential explanations for these results that are not mutually exclusive of one another.

Heat tolerance is prioritized over desiccation tolerance when mounting a stress response to MP exposure. Terrestrial animals, particularly arthropods, upregulate their heat stress mechanisms in response to non-heat stressors (reviewed in Rodgers and Isaza 2021; 2023). Therefore, insects may be predisposed to maintaining systems of heat tolerance that adaptively respond to MP-related stress.

Therefore, insects may be predisposed to maintaining systems of heat tolerance that adaptively respond to MP-related stress.

Water balance and, thus, desiccation tolerance are prone to MP-related damage to the GIT. We detected MPs in the GIT lumen and in the hemocoel (in which Malpighian tubules float freely), and MP ingestion leads to histological damage of the GIT in other insects (Ding et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2020, 2024a, b). Limiting water loss from the GIT involves ion recycling and water reabsorption by Malpighian tubules and the hindgut (reviewed in O’Donnell 2022). Thus, MP-related damage to both the GIT lining and Malpighian tubules may entail larger costs to desiccation tolerance than to heat tolerance.

Water balance and, thus, desiccation tolerance are prone to MP-related damage to the GIT. We detected MPs in the GIT lumen and in the hemocoel (in which Malpighian tubules float freely), and MP ingestion leads to histological damage of the GIT in other insects (Ding et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2020, 2024a, b). Limiting water loss from the GIT involves ion recycling and water reabsorption by Malpighian tubules and the hindgut (reviewed in O’Donnell 2022). Thus, MP-related damage to both the GIT lining and Malpighian tubules may entail larger costs to desiccation tolerance than to heat tolerance.

Heat and desiccation tolerance rely on internal resource stores that are differentially affected by MP exposure. Acute heat challenges draw upon energy resources, and an abundance of energy can be stored in insect fat body tissue (Tomanek 2010; Li et al. 2019). When food is abundant (e.g., in our study), individuals can increase food consumption to increase energy storage, even when exposed to MPs (Fig. 1a). In contrast, water storage may be limited. In the short-term absence of ingested water, insects can access water in their hemolymph. Longer periods without water require insects to produce water metabolically by oxidizing substrates, particularly stored glycogen because it releases water molecules to which its bound (reviewed in O’Donnell 2022; Sinclair et al. 2024). If MP exposure impairs water reabsorption and storage (see [2] above), then insects exposed to MPs may be less prepared for periods of water limitation. Related, Gryllus individuals that gain more body mass or store more fat during adulthood have less water storage (Stahlschmidt and Chang 2021; Harter and Stahlschmidt 2025), potentially reflecting a tradeoff between energy balance and water balance. Thus, an MP-induced increase in feeding and somatic investment (Fig. 1a, e) may entail costs (rather than benefits) to desiccation tolerance.

Warming and nylon MPs increased appetite and had opposing effects on a life-history tradeoff (Hypothesis 3)

During warming, ectotherms (i.e., most animals) are expected to ingest more food due to increased metabolic expenditures at higher temperatures (Gillooly et al. 2001; Angilletta 2009). Yet, food sources are increasingly contaminated with MPs (Sridharan et al. 2021; Zeb et al. 2024), which can reduce appetite (crab: Watts et al. 2015; rodent: Stojanovic et al. 2024; fish: Zhang et al. 2024a, b; but see Huang et al. 2023 [fish]). Therefore, we predicted antagonistic or opposing effects of temperature and MP exposure on the acquisition of this critical resource—warming would increase food consumption while MP exposure would reduce it. Our results indicated that warming increased food consumption dramatically (150% increase between 23 °C and 33 °C), but also that high levels of MPs in food increased food consumption by an average of 13% across all temperatures (Fig. 1a; Table 1a). The concentrations of MPs we added to food (up to 1.2% w/w) were sublethal and potentially on the lower end of physiological relevance. Aquatic organisms and other insects often exhibit hormetic responses to MPs where low dosages of MPs are beneficial to growth, reproduction, and survival, putatively due to activation of antioxidant systems—yet, high dosages of MPs are toxic (Sun et al. 2021; Shen et al. 2023). Shifts in food consumption may play a critical role in these hormetic patterns if lower levels of MPs stimulate appetite and facilitate benefits while higher levels depress appetite and lead to toxicity. However, we did not detect hormesis in our results as the high-MP treatment had a consistently larger effect than the low-MP treatment, which was intermediate to control and high-MP treatments (Fig. 1). Hormesis related to MP exposure may be context- or taxon-specific, and future work should explore potential hormetic thresholds related to MP exposure.

Resources from food are invested into key life-history traits, such as reproduction and self-maintenance, and animals can shift their life-history investment strategies in response to environmental stressors (Ricklefs and Wikelski 2002; Stark et al. 2004; Schultner et al. 2013; Bonfoey et al. 2023). For example, G. lineaticeps prioritizes reproduction at the expense of soma in response to elevated temperatures during early adulthood (Stahlschmidt et al. 2023, 2024a, b), and ovary mass was 5-fold greater at 33 °C relative to 23 °C in our study (Fig. 1d). However, we predicted an opposing strategy in response to MP exposure because the stress induced by MPs (e.g., damage to the GIT lining and Malpighian tubules, as well as perturbations of the gut microbiome) was expected to facilitate investment into self-maintenance. In support, MP treatment had a stronger effect on soma than initial body mass (Table 1e)—somatic tissue was, on average, > 60% heavier in females fed high-MP food relative to those fed control food (Fig. 1e). This difference equated to 50 mg of somatic tissue whereas crickets only consumed an estimated 3.3 mg of MPs (Fig. 1a, e). Relative to control crickets, MP-exposed crickets ate more food at higher temperatures (Fig. 1a), and they allocated those additional resources to somatic tissue (Fig. 1e) rather than to reproduction (Fig. 1d).

We did not measure the macronutrient composition of somatic tissue, but much of the somatic mass gained was likely in the form of fat body (Stahlschmidt and Chang 2021), which is the major energy storage tissue in insects and is also highly metabolic and important in immunity, detoxification, and the production of circulating proteins (Li et al. 2019). Despite increased investment into self-maintenance, MP-exposed individuals were still less tolerant of desiccation (Figs. 1e and 2b) indicating that MP-related adjustments in life-history strategy may be insufficient in response to dry conditions, which are increasingly common in many global regions, including within the geographical range of G. lineaticeps (Weissman and Gray 2019; IPCC 2022).

The fates of ingested nylon MPs: Spatial dynamics and physiological responses

In vertebrates, over 90% of ingested MPs are excreted or evacuated from the GIT in feces (Carr et al. 2012; Peng et al. 2022; Dawson et al. 2025). In a cricket, we show that 2–11% of ingested MP filaments are not excreted and instead translocate across the GIT lining (Fig. 1b, c). The peritrophic envelope and mucus layer of the insect GIT are the major physical barriers preventing the translocation of pathogens and other foreign bodies (Chapman 2012; Khan et al. 2023). Yet, adhesive proteins and glycoproteins found in the basal lamina of the GIT (i.e., surface interfacing with the hemocoel) may play a role in preventing absorbed MPs from moving freely in the hemocoel and damaging sensitive internal tissues. In support, approx. 88% of the MPs absorbed by crickets were attached to the basal lamina of the GIT (Fig. S4), suggesting that their GIT may offer protection from MPs at both the apical surface and basal lamina.

When foreign objects are introduced into insects’ hemocoel, they are often melanized and encapsulated by the immune system. Thus, an insect’s immunocompetence can be estimated by its ability to melanize a nylon filament inserted through its exoskeleton and into its hemocoel (reviewed in Moreno-Garcia et al. 2013). However, the MPs we detected in the hemocoel, including those attached to the basal lamina, were bright red and did not exhibit any discernable melanization. We offer three potential explanations for these results. First, the size of nylon filaments used to assess encapsulation are approx. an order of magnitude larger than the nylon filaments used in our study (i.e., diameter: 100–255 μm vs. 17 μm, respectively) (Koskimaki et al. 2004; Simmons et al. 2005; Contreras-Garduno et al. 2006). Insects are likely under selective pressure to recognize larger foreign bodies that pose a substantial threat (e.g., parasitoid eggs). Second, nylon encapsulation studies often involve altering the surface of filaments by roughening or weathering them with fine sandpaper, and insects’ melanization response can be strongly influenced by the surface characteristics (e.g., charge and functional groups) of non-biological foreign bodies, including plastic beads (Zahedi et al. 1992; Gorman et al. 1998; Lavine and Strand 2001). Related, the biological effects of virgin nylon MPs (similar to those used in our study) on midges are significantly stronger after MPs have been UV-weathered (Khosrovyan and Kahru 2021), potentially because weathered (but not virgin) MPs are more likely to induce a costly immune response. Last, nylon encapsulation studies are typically performed by puncturing the exoskeleton to insert the filaments (Koskimaki et al.2004; Simmons et al. 2005; Contreras-Garduno et al. 2006; reviewed in Moreno-Garcia et al. 2013), which triggers a response that rapidly recruits melanizing hemocytes to the site of the wound, whereas our study introduced filaments into the GIT. In sum, the biological effects of some ingested MPs may not be mediated by a robust melanization response, which can cause self-damage in insects (Sadd and Siva-Jothy 2006).

Conclusions

Many factors can influence animals’ sensitivities to MPs (e.g., the size, shape, concentration, duration of exposure, and polymer type of MPs: reviewed in Sun et al. 2021; Shen et al. 2023), and here we examined the role of temperature in biological responses to nylon MPs given its central link to global climate change (IPCC 2022). Warming and MP exposure both increased food consumption, but they had opposing effects on the classic life-history tradeoff between investment into soma vs. reproduction during early adulthood (Fig. 1d, e), suggesting that some animals may struggle to effectively respond to their increasingly hot, MP-contaminated environments. We show that warming-induced exposure to MPs may be mitigated by regulation of MP absorption in the GIT that, in turn, limits MP-related damage to sensitive internal tissues (Fig. 1b, c). Therefore, our work adds to growing evidence that GIT dynamics underlie many MP-related effects in animals (reviewed in Hirt and Body-Malapel 2020; Ding et al. 2023; Khan et al. 2024; Richard et al. 2024). The GIT is essential to maintaining energy and water balance in vertebrates and invertebrates, and it contains a microbiome that aids in digestion, detoxification, and protection from pathogens (Chapman et al. 2012; Jang and Kikuchi 2020; Fröhlich 2024; Khan et al. 2024). Although not assessed in our study, other lab-reared crickets can degrade MPs in their GIT (Ritchie et al. 2024), and wild insects tend to harbor more diverse gut microbiomes than their lab-reared counterparts (Tinker and Ottesen 2021; Brown et al. 2023). Thus, insects in nature may be well-suited to offset the emerging threat of increasing MP exposure. Further, the brain-gut-microbiome axis has an important role in appetite in vertebrates (Cryan et al. 2019; Blanco et al. 2021), and recent work indicates a similar axis in insects (Avila et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2022a, b; Li et al. 2023; Zeng et al. 2024). This axis may play an increasingly important role in mediating biological effects of MPs given connections among climate warming, food consumption, and life history (Gillooly et al. 2001; Angilletta 2009; Sun et al. 2021; Shen et al. 2023).
