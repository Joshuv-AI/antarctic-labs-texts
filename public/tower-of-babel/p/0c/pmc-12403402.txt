Prevalence and factors associated with acute malnutrition among children aged 6–59 months in West Wollega, Oromia, Ethiopia, 2024
Authors: Keneni Ephrem Dibisa, Gemechis Mengesha, Getachew Kenea, Mengistu Tamiru Dinka, Getahun Fetensa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study investigates acute malnutrition among children aged 6–59 months in conflict-affected districts of western Ethiopia. It addresses the lack of localized data by examining the prevalence and key contributing factors, including maternal health, child feeding practices, and healthcare access. Findings aim to inform targeted, multisectoral interventions to improve child nutrition in similar crisis-affected settings.MethodA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted from June 1 to July 1, 2024, involving 513 children aged 6–59 months. A proportionate sample was selected from each Woreda using systematic sampling, with one child randomly chosen from households with multiple eligible children. Trained enumerators collected data using a pre-tested questionnaire, and nutritional status was assessed using mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) measurements. Data were entered into Epi-data version 3.1 and exported to SPSS version 25.0 for analysis. Descriptive statistics were performed, followed by bivariable and multivariable logistic regression to identify factors associated with acute malnutrition, with statistical significance declared at P-value < 0.05.ResultsA total of 498 participants were included within the study with a response rate of 97%. The largest proportions (44.4%) were aged 24–59 months. Acute malnutrition prevalence was 20.7% [17.1% to 24.3%] with 6.2% having severe malnutrition and 14.5% moderate. Key risk factors for acute malnutrition included food insecurity with (AOR: 1.81, 95% CI: 1.03–3.17), pre-lacteal feeding (AOR: 2.01, 95% CI: 1.09–3.68), maternal malnutrition (AOR: 4.11, 95% CI: 2.40–7.05), less than four antenatal visits of current child pregancy (AOR: 3.94, 95% CI: 1.64–9.45), and bottle-feeding (OR: 2.79, 95% CI: 1.51–5.14).Conclusion and recommendationThe severe burden of acute malnutrition demands immediate, comprehensive action. An integrated approach is essential, emphasizing improved access to nutritious food, strengthened maternal and child health services, and increased caregiver awareness. Key strategies include merging maternal and child health initiatives, encouraging exclusive breastfeeding, ensuring regular antenatal care, and promoting appropriate infant feeding. Together, these efforts can play a crucial role in lowering acute malnutrition rates and enhancing child health in the community.

Introduction

Malnutrition, characterized by an inadequate intake of essential nutrients necessary for optimal growth and development, can manifest as either under-nutrition or over-nutrition [1]. It is a condition where nutrient intake doesn’t meet the body’s needs, affects children’s growth and survival, particularly those under five. It includes undernutrition (wasting, stunting, underweight) and overnutrition (overweight, obesity) [2]. Acute malnutrition in children aged 6–59 months is categorized as severe acute malnutrition (SAM) or moderate acute malnutrition (MAM), with SAM requiring immediate care and MAM needing less intensive treatment [3, 4].

Nearly 24 million children under five suffer from SAM, with the majority in Africa and Asia. SAM contributes to 40–50% of child deaths, highlighting the need for effective prevention and management [5]. Sub-Saharan Africa, including Ethiopia, bears a disproportionate burden of acute malnutrition due to poverty, food insecurity, and limited access to health services [6]. In Ethiopia, over half of children under five suffer from malnutrition. Stunting affects 43% in rural areas versus 29% in urban, while wasting is 12% in rural areas and 8% in urban areas [7].

Acute malnutrition stems from factors like food insecurity, poor infant feeding, inadequate maternal nutrition, and limited healthcare access [8]. Practices such as pre-lacteal feeding raise malnutrition risks, while lack of antenatal care and maternal malnutrition worsen vulnerabilities. Poor feeding and hygiene practices lead to infections, creating a cycle of poor health [9, 10].

Acute malnutrition has severe immediate and long-term effects on health and development. Affected children are more vulnerable to infections like diarrhea and pneumonia, worsening their nutritional status. Long-term, it can lead to stunted growth, cognitive impairments, and reduced educational and economic opportunities. Untreated severe acute malnutrition greatly raises the risk of death [11, 12].

Efforts to combat acute malnutrition include the Community-Based Management of Acute Malnutrition (CMAM) model, which combines community outreach, Supplementary Feeding Programs (SFP) [13], Outpatient Therapeutic Programs (OTP), and Inpatient Treatment Centers for severe cases with medical complications. While these interventions have been effective in many areas, challenges persist in regions like Ethiopia’s West Wollega Zone, where malnutrition rates and related risk factors are not well-documented [14].

This study fills important gaps in existing research by examining acute malnutrition in conflict-affected areas such as West Wollega. It offers localized data, investigates various risk factors, and differentiates between severe and moderate malnutrition. The findings provide valuable insights into obstacles to antenatal care and the socioeconomic factors driving bottle-feeding, guiding the development of targeted public health strategies.

Methods and materials

Study area and period: This study was conducted in selected woredas of West Wollega Zone, Oromia, and located 441 km from Addis Ababa. The climate is “Woyena Dega,” characterized by nearly seven months of rainfall from March to September. According to the 2024 GC, West Wollega has a population of 2,042,319, with an estimated 335,961 children aged 6–59 months. The zone is bordered by East Wollega to the east, Benishangul to the south, Ilu Abba-Bor to the north, and Kellem Wollega to the west. It hosts diverse ethnic groups and various organizations providing healthcare through six government hospitals, two non-governmental hospitals, 70 health centers, and 488 health posts.

Study design

A community-based cross-sectional study was employed from June 1 to July 1, 2024.

Source Population: All children aged 6–59 months and their mothers or caregivers who were present during the data collection and had lived in the woredas of West Wollega Zone for at least the past six months.

Study Population: Children aged 6–59 months and their mothers or caregivers living in randomly selected woredas of the West Wollega Zone during the study period.

Study Unit: Individual children aged 6–59 months and their respective mothers or caregivers from randomly selected kebeles and households.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: All the 6–59-month-old children in the randomly selected household, along with their mothers or caregivers.

Exclusion criteria: Children who were seriously sick at the time of the study were not included due to measurement difficulties and the enormous impact on the nutritional condition of the participants. Children with impairments that impact anthropometric measures and those without caregivers present during the study were not included.

Sampling procedure/technique

The study employed a random sampling method to select 14 woredas from the 22 in West Wollega Zone. In each woreda, 30% of kebeles were randomly chosen by lottery, resulting in 96 kebeles. Within these, households with children aged 6–59 months were selected using systematic random sampling. A starting household was randomly chosen, and subsequent households were selected at regular intervals determined by a k-value specific to each woreda, based on the number of households and the population of eligible children. The k-values used were: every 5th in all woredas. Health extension workers provided lists of eligible households, and one child per household was selected by lottery if multiple were present. Data collection tools and procedures

Data collection for the study was conducted through face-to-face interviews using a pre-tested, standardized questionnaire adapted from reliable sources. Trained data collectors administered the interviews across the selected kebeles. To assess nutritional status, both children aged 6–59 months and their mothers or primary caregivers were measured using Mid-Upper Arm Circumference (MUAC) tapes. Prior to measurement, the MUAC tapes were checked for cleanliness, and participants were comfortably seated. For children, the MUAC was measured at the midpoint of the left upper arm with the arm relaxed and extended, ensuring the tape was placed snugly but not tightly. Maternal nutritional status was assessed using MUAC, which is a practical and widely used tool in resource-limited settings like Ethiopia, especially for pregnant and lactating women. Measurements were taken on the left arm using a non-elastic MUAC tape. Based on standard thresholds, women were categorized as having Severe Acute Malnutrition (SAM) (MUAC < 21 cm), Moderate Acute Malnutrition (MAM) (MUAC 21–23 cm), or Normal nutritional status (MUAC ≥ 23 cm). Fourteen trained nurses carried out the measurements, supervised by seven qualified personnel to ensure consistency and accuracy. Women with conditions that could affect the reliability of MUAC readings were excluded from the assessment.

Data quality management

The principal investigators conducted a one-day orientation, and training for the data collectors, covering key topics such as the study’s objectives, a thorough explanation of the questionnaire, usage of the data collection tools, interview techniques, and instructions for conducting structured interviews with mothers or caregivers. The training also included instructions on taking anthropometric measurements and adhering to fieldwork ethics.

Throughout the data collection period, supervisors provided daily oversight and performed quality checks. To ensure data validity and reliability, the principal investigator and two supervisors closely monitored the data collectors. A pre-tested questionnaire which was used for a data collection instrument was initially tried out on a small segment of the target population on 5% (26 participants) of the total sample before the main study. The pre-test aimed to assess the questionnaire’s clarity, relevance, and consistency. Based on the feedback, necessary revisions were made to ensure the final version was culturally suitable, easy to understand, and effective in collecting accurate and reliable data.

Based on the pretesting results and feedback, the questionnaire was revised as needed. The pre-tested data was excluded from the main analysis. To ensure consistency, the data collection instrument was first developed in English, then translated into Afaan Oromo, and subsequently translated back into English.

Data processing and analysis

Data from the questionnaires were manually reviewed for accuracy before being entered into Epi-Data version 3.1. Descriptive statistics were then conducted using SPSS version 25 to analyze frequency distributions and identify any missing values. Bivariable analysis was performed to investigate crude associations between dependent and independent variables. Variables with a p-value less than 0.25 from the bivariate analysis were included in a multivariable logistic regression model to determine predictors of malnutrition. Binary logistic regression was used to evaluate the crude associations between independent variables, with odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals employed to quantify these relationships. Statistical significance was defined as p ≤ 0.05. Model testing included evaluating multicollinearity among predictor variables, assessing model fit with the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, and examining robustness through various diagnostic checks. These procedures confirmed that the final model was dependable and accurately reflected the relationships between the variables.

Operational and definitions of terms

Acute malnutrition (wasting): defined by a mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) of less than 12.5 cm.

Bilateral Pitting Edema: Identified when thumb pressure applied to both feet for 3 s leaves an indentation after removal.

Critically III: Refers to children with severe chronic conditions such as severe pneumonia, tuberculosis (TB), congestive heart failure (CHF), diabetes mellitus (DM), or HIV/AIDS.

Dietary Diversity Score (DDS): Calculated as the number of different food groups consumed by the child in the 24 h preceding the data collection, Acceptable Ranges for Food Diversity Score:Low Diversity: Consumption of fewer than 4 food groups, indicating limited dietary variety and potential food insecurity, Moderate Diversity: Consumption of 4–6 food groups, reflecting a reasonable level of dietary variety and access to food. And High Diversity: Consumption of more than 6 food groups, suggesting good dietary variety and better access to a wide range of nutrients [17].

Food Security Status refers to consistent access to sufficient, safe, and nutritious food for an active, healthy life. It involves four dimensions: availability, accessibility, utilization, and stability, which collectively address food production, economic access, nutritional knowledge, and reliability over time [18].

Results

Sosio-demographic characteristics of respondents

A total of 498 participants were included in the study, with a high response rate of 97%. The majority of mothers/care givers were aged 20–29 years (61%), and most (96.45%) were married. Educationally, 28.9% of mothers/care givers had completed secondary school, and 11% were government employees. Most participants (98.6%) were from the Oromo ethnic group, with 68.9% identifying as Protestant religion followers. Almost half of husbands worked as farmers (51.8%), and only 9% were employed in government roles. More than half of the participants (62.2%) had adequate food access and utilized it effectively, indicating good food security.

However, only 40.2% reported having extra food during pregnancy or lactation, which is important for maternal and child health. The findings revealed that 85.9% of the surveyed mothers had utilized family planning in the past, demonstrating widespread use of these services. Discussions

Acute malnutrition in children and maternal malnutrition is a significant public health concern that necessitates targeted interventions and support. Addressing malnutrition in both children and mothers is vital for improving overall health outcomes, reducing mortality rates, and promoting long-term well-being. In this study, the prevalence of acute malnutrition among screened children was found to be 20.7% with a 95% confidence interval ranging from [[17.1% to 24.3%]].

The prevalence of acute malnutrition shows regional variation across Ethiopia. It is significantly lower than the 52.2% reported in Tigray, likely influenced by conflict and displacement. However, it is slightly higher than the rates observed in Shashamane (19.91%) and North Central Ethiopia (20.2%). These differences may stem from factors such as study period, socioeconomic conditions, healthcare accessibility, maternal health, and environmental or man-made stressors, emphasizing the need for tailored, region-specific interventions [19, 20].

This implies that there should be aggressive intervention on screening and management of acute malnutrition within the study area. It is clear that children with moderate to severe acute malnutrition have three to nine times higher mortality than well-nourished children [21].

A multivariable analysis identified five key factors associated with acute malnutrition in children. Specifically, pre-lacteal feeding increases the risk of malnutrition by a factor of four. Children whose mothers had fewer than four prenatal care visits were also four times more likely to be diagnosed with acute malnutrition. Additionally, children with a history of bottle feeding were three times more likely to experience acute malnutrition. These findings are consistent with research conducted in North Central Ethiopia [22].

Limited access to healthcare facilities and trained professionals can also hinder mothers’ understanding of proper breastfeeding techniques. Socioeconomic disparities can also influence breastfeeding choices, making it crucial to address these factors to promote optimal breastfeeding practices [23]. This implies that to improve child nutrition, the promotion of better child caring practices by improving the practice of parents on appropriate infant feeding is essential [24].

Children diagnosed with acute malnutrition due to food insecurity are more likely to experience this condition compared to those with stable food access, a finding that aligns with research from Sodo town in southern Ethiopia [7].

Several factors contribute to this heightened risk in food-insecure children. These include limited access to nutrient-rich foods, reduced dietary diversity, increased vulnerability to infections, poor health and sanitation conditions, psychological stress, and restricted healthcare access. Food insecurity can lead to a lack of essential nutrients, a narrowed diet, and weakened immune responses, making children more susceptible to infections. Psychological stress may further disrupt normal metabolic functions and delay the recognition and treatment of acute malnutrition in these children. This observation is consistent with studies conducted in various regions of Ethiopia [16, 26]. Therefore, it enhance the need for robust public health initiatives and resource allocation both locally and nationally to effectively prevent and manage acute malnutrition, particularly within the first 1,000 days of life [15].

Moreover, the increased likelihood of children developing acute malnutrition when their mothers are affected is influenced by multiple factors, including the mother’s and child’s nutritional status, breastfeeding practices, socioeconomic conditions, genetic factors, and healthcare access [27].

Strengthening community-based interventions for newborn care, particularly through the engagement of female community health volunteers and health workers, is essential to mitigate the long-term impacts of this issue [28].

Mothers who attend fewer antenatal care (ANC) follow-up visits are at a higher risk of having children diagnosed with acute malnutrition due to factors such as inadequate education, missed screening opportunities, limited access to healthcare, poor maternal health, and social determinants of health. Regular ANC follow-ups play a critical role in preventing malnutrition in children similar in research done in Nigeria [25].

It implies that a concerted effort should be made at all levels to improve maternal and child health services to tackle the problems [26]. Children who were bottle-fed early in life are three times more likely to experience acute malnutrition compared to those who were not bottle-fed. The increased risk associated with bottle feeding stems from several factors. These include insufficient nutrient intake from formula milk, improper feeding practices, and the absence of immune benefits provided by breast milk. Furthermore, issues such as overfeeding or underfeeding can contribute to malnutrition. Additionally, bottle feeding may reduce the crucial bonding and emotional support provided through breastfeeding, which can adversely affect the child’s overall health and nutritional status. This highlights the need for targeted interventions to promote and support breastfeeding practices, as well as proper feeding guidelines, to enhance children’s nutritional outcomes and overall well-being [27].

Conclusions

The prevalence of acute malnutrition among study area was significantly high. Factors like food insecurity, pre-lacteal feeding practices, maternal nutrition, antenatal care utilization, and feeding practices were significantly associated with acute malnutrition. A comprehensive strategy that focuses on enhancing access to nutritious foods, advocating for breastfeeding, improving maternal health, increasing antenatal care visits, and educating caregivers on proper feeding practices is essential for alleviating the burden of acute malnutrition among children. To prevent acute malnutrition in children, it is crucial to address factors such as food insecurity, pre-lacteal feeding practices, maternal nutrition, antenatal care utilization, and feeding practices.
