A survey of Australian consumers' and dentists’ perceptions of saliva as a biofluid for periodontitis diagnosis
Authors: Ruiyi Sun, Sarina Amidi, Jia Ying Yee, Andrew Liaw, Sepanta Hosseinpour, Sašo Ivanovski, Necla Asli Kocak Oztug, Pingping Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis survey aimed to explore the attitudes, knowledge, and acceptance levels of consumers and dentists towards saliva as a diagnostic tool for periodontitis, in order to identify the potential advantages and barriers to its implementation in periodontitis diagnosis.MethodsWith approved human ethics from The University of Queensland (2023/HE000413), this cross-sectional questionnaire study included 69 consumers and 32 clinicians, all of whom completed the survey between September 2023 and March 2024. We proposed a four-step saliva diagnosis workflow: (1) Collect saliva at home or in a dental clinic; (2) Send the sample to the laboratory for analysis; (3) Provide test results to the dentist and patient; (4) Schedule further consultation or treatment if necessary. Attitudes towards the proposed saliva diagnosis protocol were analysed in relation to consumers’ demographic characteristics and clinicians’ professional backgrounds.ResultsMost consumers surveyed were aged 20–29, while most clinicians (~ 56%) were general dentists. Approximately 80% of consumers expressed optimistic views on saliva diagnostics, whereas around 50% of clinicians were uncertain about the overall accuracy, despite recognizing the value of saliva tests. Both consumers and clinicians indicated that a chair-side or at-home rapid antigen test (RAT) could enhance the accuracy of saliva testing and address transport issues.ConclusionThe potential for saliva-based diagnostics for periodontitis shows promising acceptance among both consumers and clinicians. However, further modifications and specifications of the proposed workflow are needed to enhance its viability and accuracy.Clinical relevanceThis study highlights significant consumer interest in saliva diagnostics for periodontitis, indicating a preference for a shift toward personalised, non-invasive diagnostic methods. The mixed responses from clinicians highlight the need for ongoing education and evidence to foster confidence in saliva-based testing, potentially improving early detection and treatment outcomes in periodontal care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07062-3.Keywords: Saliva diagnostics, Periodontitis, Consumer attitudes, Clinician acceptance, Diagnostic tools, Early detection

Introduction

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory condition of the oral cavity that affects millions globally, posing significant risks to both oral and systemic health [1, 2]. Traditional diagnostic methods, such as manual probing and radiographs, are partly effective in the clinical setting, however, suffer from being resource-intensive, time-consuming, and retrospective in nature, reflecting past rather than real-time disease [3]. Recently, saliva has emerged as a promising alternative for diagnosing periodontitis due to its rich biological content, including proteins, antibodies, extracellular vesicles and microbiota [4–9]. Saliva is a biological fluid that potentially offers a non-invasive, cost-effective, and easily accessible biofluid for detecting periodontitis-associated biomarkers [10–12], which may serve as a screening or diagnostic tool for both consumers and clinicians.

Over the past few decades, saliva diagnostics have gained significant attention, with increasing research exploring their feasibility in detecting oral and systemic diseases [13, 14]. Saliva-based diagnostics have been applied in testing for systemic conditions, such as Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19), HIV and Cushing’s syndrome [4, 15, 16]. Recent research has identified potential salivary biomarkers related to inflammatory cytokines (i.e. interleukin IL-1β, IL-6, matrix metalloproteinase 8, MMP-8) and bone remodelling (RANKL/OPG ratio), which are positively correlated to the severity of periodontitis [6, 17]. Clinical cross-sectional studies have shown that salivary biomarkers can effectively distinguish periodontitis from health. For example, cytokines such as active MMP-8 in oral rinse have demonstrated diagnostic utility [18, 19]. Recent cross-sectional studies have also highlighted the role of salivary extracellular vesicles (EVs) in disease progression, sectional studies [5, 8], including alterations in global 5-methylcytosine (5mC) levels [20], TNF-α mRNA [21] and host-EV-associated cytokines IL-6 and IL-8 [22], further supporting its diagnostic potential. These findings highlight saliva as a non-invasive, accessible, and complementary biofluid for periodontitis detection, motivating further investigation into novel biomarkers and integrated diagnostic approaches that could enhance early detection, monitor disease progression, and inform precision periodontal care.

Several review papers summarised current clinical research that suggested that saliva diagnosis for periodontitis is promising as a future alternative or supplement to clinical examinations [8, 17, 23–25]. However, the field is still relatively underexplored, and the acceptance of salivary diagnostics among dental clinicians and the general public remains uncertain.

Although saliva diagnostic tests for periodontitis are still in their infancy and require further research and validation, understanding clinicians’ and consumers’ perspectives is crucial in facilitating the successful integration of saliva-based diagnostics into routine dental practice.

The aim of this study is to explore the attitudes, knowledge, and acceptance levels of saliva-based diagnostic methods for periodontitis among dentists and patients. By examining these perspectives, we seek to identify potential barriers and facilitators to the adoption of saliva diagnostics, ultimately contributing to more effective and patient-centred periodontitis management.

Method

Study design and participants

The cross-sectional questionnaire study was approved by the University of Queensland Human Ethics Research Office (approval number 2023/HE000413). The survey was carried out following the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

This cross-sectional survey study was designed to investigate and analyse the association of personal factors of consumers and their acceptance of salivary diagnosis for periodontitis. This study recruited consumers from the public as well as dental clinicians across Australia and New Zealand between September 2023 and March 2024. Before completing the questionnaire, all participants provided consent by selecting a mandatory option that stated, “By checking this box, you agree to give consent for using your responses as primary data for this research.” This ensured that their agreement was obtained prior to proceeding with the survey questions. The sample size was not pre-determined, and the maximum number of participants were recruited within this 7-month period.

The inclusion criteria for consumers were: (a) aged 18 to 69 (with an option to specify age), (b) not enrolled as dental students, and (c) willingness to participate in the study. For clinicians, the inclusion criteria were: (a) age 18 or older, (b) registered as general dentists or specialists practising in Australia or New Zealand and c) willingness to participate in the study. Exclusion criteria for both consumers and clinicians: (a) participants younger than 18 or older than 69 years, (b) unable to read English and (c) unwilling to provide consent.

Questionnaire

The surveys were conducted and distributed via Qualtrics XM, with two different sets of questionnaires designed for consumers (Supplementary Information 1) and clinicians (Supplementary Information 2). Survey questionnaires were aimed at evaluating the perceptions of dental clinicians and consumers in relation to saliva as a biofluid for the diagnosis of periodontitis with two key focus areas: (1) To determine the acceptance of saliva as a diagnostic tool among consumers and clinicians, and (2) To determine the general perception of clinicians towards utilising saliva for diagnosing periodontitis. To ensure data privacy in accordance with the human ethics application, the survey was administered via UQ’s secure Qualtrics XM platform without collecting personal identifiers, with all anonymized responses stored confidentially and accessible only to the research team.

The questionnaire consisted of four sections: demographic information (age, gender), knowledge, attitude, and acceptance of saliva diagnosis. Responses were grouped into ‘agree’ (combining ‘strongly agree’ and ‘agree’), ‘neutral’ and ‘disagree’ (combining ‘disagree’ and ‘strongly disagree’) categories for ease of interpretation in Table 1. Two questionnaires - one for clinicians and one for consumers (patients and/or members of the public) - were developed to explore perspectives on saliva-based diagnosis for periodontitis. Questions were informed by literature review, expert input, and stakeholder feedback. While not formally validated, the questionnaires were pilot tested with small groups from clinician and public to ensure clarity and relevance. The full set of questions is provided in the Supplementary Information.

Basic demographics of 101 participants: 69 consumers and 32 clinicians

For consumers, the outcome variables included opinions on the reliability and efficiency of saliva tests, as well as their willingness to use these tests for early periodontitis detection and monitoring of disease severity. For clinicians, the outcome variables focused on the perceived efficiency, feasibility, and willingness to use saliva tests as an adjunct to traditional diagnostic methods.

Key explanatory variables for consumers included oral hygiene habits (brushing and flossing) and a history of periodontitis, as those with prior experience may have greater awareness of periodontal health and disease. For clinicians, the primary explanatory variable was their profession, with periodontists having more exposure to periodontitis than general dentists.

Confounding variables included consumer age and previous exposure to saliva diagnosis, as both could influence understanding and perceptions of periodontitis and saliva diagnostics, potentially affecting outcomes.

We propose a four-step saliva-based diagnostic protocol for periodontitis: (1) Saliva is collected at home or in the clinic per manufacturer guidelines, (2) Sent to the laboratory for analysis, (3) Results are shared with the dentist and patient, (4) Follow-up consultation or treatment is arranged if needed. We then assessed consumer and clinician acceptance of this workflow regarding its reliability, convenience, and effectiveness in detecting early disease and severity in periodontitis.

Statistical analysis

The data were recorded and analysed in GraphPad Prism 10.1.2. Clinician and consumer perceptions of the advantages and disadvantages of salivary diagnosis were compared using bar graphs. A Chi-square test was adopted to evaluate the associations between the acceptance of the saliva diagnosis workflow and each explanatory variable (e.g., oral hygiene habits, age, gender). A p < 0.05 was statistically significant between variables.

Results

Characteristics of patients and clinicians

A total of 83 consumer responses and 50 clinician responses were received. Incomplete survey responses, where not all questions were answered, were discarded after review, resulting in 69 consumer and 32 clinician responses.

In this study, there were 101 participants in total (Table 1), comprising 69 consumers and 32 clinicians. Of the 69 consumers surveyed (Table 1), the majority were in the 20–24 years old age group (63.8%). The second biggest group comprised of 25–29-year-olds (18.8%). A large proportion of survey respondents were female (78.3%; males = 21.7%). More than half of the respondents reported daily flossing habits (55.1%), had no previous known history of periodontitis (66.7%) and only experienced occasional bleeding when brushing their teeth (56.5%). 75.4% of consumers reported brushing two times a day. Age (p = 0.99), gender (p = 0.77), flossing habit (p = 0.42), history of periodontitis (p = 0.37), gingival symptoms (p = 0.22), and toothbrushing habit (p = 0.63) showed no statistically significant association with participants’ perceptions of the saliva diagnosis workflow. Of the 32 clinicians surveyed (Table 1), the study participants included 18 general dentists (56.3%), 9 periodontists (28.1%), 1 prosthodontist (3.13%), 3 dental hygienists (9.38%), and 1 endodontist (3.13%). Approximately a third of clinicians surveyed (32.1%) routinely used periodontal pocket depths and the presence of bleeding on probing for diagnosing periodontitis. Furthermore, an estimated 25% of clinicians utilised periodontal charting of all teeth and an orthopantomogram radiograph to assist in reaching a periodontitis diagnosis. Using CPITN/PSR or other lesser-known methods was the least popular method amongst participating clinicians. Dentist occupation (p = 0.6) was not significantly associated with participants’ perceptions of the saliva diagnostic workflow.

Consumer responses indicated mostly positive attitudes towards saliva diagnostics. Most participants either “agreed” or “strongly agreed” with the reliability (~ 50%) and effectiveness (~ 60%) of saliva-based tests. Early detection potential was rated highly, with around 55% expressing agreement or strong agreement. However, perceptions of disease severity diagnosis were more mixed, with nearly 30% of respondents indicating a neutral stance and fewer agreeing with its efficacy.

It was observed that most consumers’ pre-existing knowledge on saliva diagnosis stemmed from previous firsthand experience with COVID-19 tests, whilst a smaller proportion of consumers developed their understandings through the internet or university lectures which described the usefulness of saliva tests to ‘detect biomarkers of systemic diseases, genetically relevant information’ and ‘test DNA for individual susceptibility to certain diseases’.

Clinician feedback showed some variation in opinions. Firstly, most clinicians agreed or strongly agreed with the overall efficiency and efficacy of saliva diagnostics (~ 65%). However, the potential for saliva tests to fully substitute traditional methods received less support, with approximately 30% agreeing and a large portion (45%) remaining neutral. While reliability for early detection received moderate support (around 40% agreement), the reliability of severity detection was more critically viewed, with nearly 50% expressing neutral or negative responses.

Consumer’s suggestions on the proposed workflow

For the workflow provided (Fig. 1a), most consumers were concerned about the saliva collection methods and the level of support and quality control:

Support with an infographic/video explanation from the manufacturer (or similar) could help with independent, at-home process.

Need to give very clear instructions for those doing at home so they collect the sample accurately.

Another concern was related to the influence of sample transportation on the accuracy of test results:

There might be a delay in the delivery of salivary samples to the lab, which might lead to an inaccurate result, thus I reckon it would be best if the testing is done in the dental clinic itself and analysis will be carried out on the same day.

Further research into whether or not results would differ with temperature excursions in transit to a lab, i.e. does the saliva react differently in warmer/colder conditions.

Other suggestions included having instructions on patient follow-up after testing, with a preference for getting the results straight after like the COVID rapid antigen test (RAT) so minimizing the frequency of visiting the dentist.

Clinician’s suggestions on the proposed workflow

From the clinician’s perspective, a concern regarding the workflow provided (Fig. 1a) was the rigour of the sample collection protocol. Patient compliance for at-home saliva collection was questioned and clinicians preferred to complete the test chairside under professional supervision, which would ideally provide an instant result:

Simple detection (e.g. colour change or line appearing on my RAT test kit) in the presence of exosome so that saliva doesn’t need to be transported to the lab and the result is readily available.

More immediate interpretation of results - ideally a colour change would be best.

In-chair result would be best if possible, as sending to a lab would cost more so would have to increase the cost of test.

On-the-spot results rather than waiting for lab results to avoid the need for a second appointment.

Clinician’s opinions toward saliva diagnosis for periodontitis

Among all the aspects of saliva testing that were queried, clinicians showed various levels of disagreement on reliability, substitutability, reliability in early disease detection and severity detection of saliva diagnostics for periodontitis (Fig. 2a). However, approximately 50% of clinicians agreed or strongly agreed with all aspects of saliva testing, with a more neutral (38%) and disagree (30%) response for the potential efficiency and efficacy of saliva testing.

Opinions of both (a) clinicians and (b) consumers on the reliability of saliva as a diagnostic method, its use for early detection, its effectiveness for assessing disease severity, and overall efficacy

Figure 2b) illustrates consumer opinions on saliva diagnosis through four survey questions. For the first, 43% of consumers agreed on the ‘reliability of saliva in detecting periodontitis’. Nearly 50% were willing to undergo saliva testing for early detection if recommended by their dentist. Similarly, over 49% agreed to use saliva testing to assess the severity of periodontitis. Lastly, more than 46% believed ‘salivary diagnostics would be a more efficient and effective method for diagnosing periodontitis’.

Advantages and disadvantages of saliva diagnosis from the perspective of consumers and clinicians

Figure 3a) presents consumer and clinician opinions on the advantages and disadvantages of saliva diagnosis. The bar graph shows that both groups had similar views on “stability and transportability” (8% vs. 6%), “feasible self-collection” (14% vs. 15%), “portable platforms” (11% vs. 13%), “accuracy” (7% vs. 9%), “non-invasive” (16% vs. 18%), and “safe collection” (10% vs. 12%). Consumers rated “affordability” (16% vs. 10%) and “easy collection” (19% vs. 16%) slightly higher than clinicians.

The advantages (a) and disadvantages (b) of saliva diagnosis, and the comparison between opinions of clinicians and consumers

With respect to disadvantages, Fig. 3b) showed similar proportions of consumers and clinicians selected “false positive/negative” (17% each), “longer sample collection time” (7% vs. 9%), “lack of standardization” (17% each), and “lower analyte levels” (11% vs. 13%). However, more consumers highlighted contamination (23% vs. 19%) and salivary variation (14% vs. 11%), while more clinicians viewed saliva diagnosis as unsuitable for xerostomia patients (14% vs. 10%).

Discussion

Considering the limited depth of dental knowledge among consumers, our survey was designed to use a straightforward survey to assess their perceptions on the acceptance of the salivary diagnosis of periodontitis (early or severity detection), if provided by a dental professional. The results showed that consumers had a generally positive attitude towards saliva diagnosis, which could be explained by the rapport and relationship built between dentists and consumers, as well as the existence of other commercially available saliva diagnostic tools on the market (e.g. COVID-19).

In dentistry, it is crucial to provide patient-centred care, which is facilitated by the trusting relationship between dentist and consumer [26]. This trust improves patient compliance, engagement in care, and effectiveness of treatment, leading to better oral health outcomes and quality of life for patients [26, 27]. Mutually, the social aspect of patient-dentist relationships can also benefit dentists in their ability to alleviate suffering from oral diseases [28]. Further, for patients who are sensitive to periodontal probing or suffer from dental anxiety, saliva-based diagnostics could be a less invasive alternative [29]. Together, existing relationships between dentists and patients (consumers) may contribute to a wider level of acceptance when a new diagnostic product is proposed.

Commercially available saliva tests that are readily available on the market may also encourage wider acceptance of saliva as a diagnostic tool for other health-related contexts, such as periodontitis. With the global phenomenon of the COVID-19 pandemic, the RAT became a universally accepted point-of-care device for virus detection, owing to its high sensitivity, ease of use and fast result acquisition [30–33]. In this survey, most of the respondents who had pre-existing knowledge of saliva diagnosis were previous users of RAT. This common familiarity among existing saliva point-of-care devices likely reduces the resistance for consumers to adopt such a method in the dental setting (i.e., periodontitis detection).

It is noteworthy that approximately 80% of consumers gave a positive opinion on the proposed workflow, as compared to only slightly over 50% of clinicians responding positively. This discrepancy observed in clinicians compared to consumers may be explained by their higher level of understanding of the intricacies of disease diagnosis and detection in the clinical setting, which leads to a more critical analysis of the feasibility of the proposed workflow. Similarly, consumers mostly viewed saliva diagnostics as reliable and effective, especially for early detection, whereas clinicians with inherently greater knowledge and experience were more cautious, believing that saliva diagnostics may provide adjunctive benefits, but cannot replace traditional methods for diagnosis and disease severity assessment.

With respect to the advantages and disadvantages of saliva diagnostics, it was evident that consumers are more inclined to believe that saliva diagnosis offers better affordability and easy collection. This likely draws from consumers’ experiences using at-home saliva-based COVID-19 kits versus professional laboratory PCR tests which offered non-invasive ease of sampling, convenience and cost-effectiveness. Moreover, the potential to complete a readily available saliva diagnostic test for periodontitis without the need to physically attend a professional dental appointment may be considered a significant financial, psychological and time-saving benefit for consumers.

Our survey data revealed that consumers showed concern in relation to the effect of external factors such as dietary/handling contamination and individual variation of salivary components on the reliability of saliva samples. Additionally, the variability in salivary components can be further confounded by genetics, social history and overall health. Therefore, to ensure the maintenance of rigour with sample collection, there should be highly standardized protocols, such as the avoidance of consuming food and drinks for a certain period of time prior to saliva testing.

Systematic reviews of randomized clinical trials (RCTs) [34–38] highlight distinct diagnostic profiles for saliva, gingival crevicular fluid (GCF), and serum/plasma in periodontitis due to differences in biomarker concentrations. GCF, a serum transudate, is highly specific to local inflammation but requires site-specific, technique-sensitive collection [4]. Serum and plasma reflect systemic conditions and are less specific to oral disease, necessitating trained personnel for collection. In contrast, saliva offers a non-invasive, practical alternative with moderate specificity, capturing both local and systemic biomarkers—making it suitable for large-scale screening [5, 7]. However, further validation is needed to directly compare these biofluids, as findings remain inconsistent. For instance, IL-33 levels are elevated in plasma, saliva, and GCF in chronic periodontitis patients [34]. Another review of 17 studies (16 observational, 1 RCT) involving 1,354 patients and 989 controls found higher plasma uric acid but lower salivary uric acid in periodontitis cases compared to controls [35]. These discrepancies highlight the need for standardized protocols, comparative studies, and professional input to clarify the diagnostic value of saliva versus GCF and serum/plasma in periodontitis.

Chairside diagnostic kits are rapid point-of-care tools that detect oral diseases like periodontitis and caries by analysing biomarkers in saliva, GCF, or plaque. PerioSafe® (for humans) and OraStripdx (for dogs and cats) measure active MMP-8, a marker of periodontal tissue destruction. For caries, Oral BioTech’s CariScreen® provides a quick, 1-minute bacterial risk assessment. These kits offer non-invasive sampling, immediate results, and improved patient compliance but have limitations in accuracy, biomarker range, and require clinician training. Overall, chairside POCT kits show promise for early, convenient oral disease diagnosis and management.The survey results did not demonstrate statistical significance between the opinions toward the proposed workflow and the age, gender, oral hygiene practice, and history of periodontitis for both consumers and clinicians. This is likely attributed to the small sample size seen in both study groups and larger populations need to be analyzed to further validate our findings.

Another limitation of this survey was the younger age of participants which restricts the generalisability of our results, as older populations may express more resistance to novel products. Inherently, recall bias may also occur for consumers about their known history of periodontitis, and we aimed to minimise these confounders through the construction of questionnaires with non-technical language for ease of understanding by the general public. In addition, bias due to the desirability to conform to perceived social norms may arise in consumer responses about oral hygiene practices. Therefore, to maximise the validity of survey responses, privacy and confidentiality was emphasised in the participants’ information sheet, at the start and the end of the questionnaire. For consumers, this study did not capture the full age range of potential participants, nor did it assess education level, dental visit frequency, prior experience with remote saliva collection, willingness to provide specific saliva volumes, or any preferred timing or restrictions for sample collection. Additionally, information from dental professionals - such as years in practice, patient volume, and practical considerations for saliva sampling (e.g., preferred time of day, sample volume) - was not explored. Future studies should include a broader demographic and assess participants’ dental knowledge, prior exposure to saliva diagnostics, and sampling preferences. Deeper engagement with dental professionals on clinical experience and collection logistics could enhance the development of practical diagnostic strategies.Our four-step saliva diagnosis workflow—(1) saliva collection at home or clinic, (2) laboratory analysis, (3) result sharing, and (4) follow-up consultation—offers a practical framework for saliva-based periodontitis testing. Feedback from consumers and clinicians highlighted key areas for improvement. Consumers stressed the need for clear, multimedia-supported instructions to ensure accurate at-home collection and raised concerns about sample transport affecting test accuracy. Clinicians preferred rapid, chairside testing to enhance reliability and reduce patient visits. To address these concerns, future test development should focus on improving at-home collection support, stabilizing samples during transport, advancing point-of-care technologies for immediate results, and implementing clear follow-up protocols. Integrating these insights will help create more effective, user-friendly diagnostics aligned with clinical workflows.

Conclusion

Our survey identified an overall greater acceptance of the use of saliva diagnostic tools for periodontitis diagnosis amongst consumers, whereas clinicians often were more critical of its implementation and workflow within the dental setting. Considering the various advantages and disadvantages of a saliva point-of-care diagnostic test for periodontitis, this survey identified a broadly positive outlook for such a workflow which can ultimately enhance the viability and accuracy of patient-centered oral health care.
