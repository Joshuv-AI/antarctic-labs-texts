Paragonimiasis in a child with massive pericardial effusion as the primary manifestation: a case report
Authors: Zanhong Xiao, Yichu Chen, Changjian Li, Yong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParagonimiasis in children is rare as isolated pericardial effusion, especially when presenting despite the absence of elevated eosinophilia. We report such a case to highlight this atypical manifestation.Case presentationA 10-year-old male child was admitted to the hospital with “pericardial effusion found for 5 days”. The initial differential diagnosis included infectious, autoimmune, and neoplastic causes of pericarditis. Laboratory tests on admission showed normal serum and pericardial effusion eosinophils, and the pericardial effusion was positive for paragonimiasis IgG. Multiple cardiac ultrasounds revealed a large pericardial effusion and progressive pericardial thickening (up to 6.5 mm) with nodular changes. After praziquantel deworming and resection of the pericardial mass, pathology confirmed necrosis with granulomatous inflammation, consistent with parasitic infection and ruling out other etiologies in the differential. The child recovered well after surgery, and the pericardial effusion disappeared.ConclusionChildren with paragonimiasis may present with isolated pericardial lesions but normal range of eosinophils, and early recognition and confirmation of the diagnosis is essential for prognosis.Keywords: Paragonimiasis, Pericardial effusion, Children, Eosinophils, Praziquantel, Pericardial mass

Background

Paragonimiasis is a foodborne zoonosis caused primarily by Paragonimus westermani; 90% of cases occur in Asia, with an estimated 20 million cases [1]. Typical clinical manifestations include cough, chest pain, and hemoptysis (in the thoracic type) or abdominal pain and diarrhea (in the abdominal type). In contrast, reports of solitary pericardial involvement are sporadic, and there are virtually no childhood cases of isolated pericardial involvement. This report details a distinctive pediatric case presenting with isolated pericardial effusion in the absence of elevated eosinophilia, ultimately confirmed by histopathology after pericardiectomy, due to paragonimiasis to increase pediatric clinicians’ awareness of this rare clinical presentation.

Case presentation

A 10-year-old male child was admitted to our hospital on February 2, 2025, with a 5-day history of pericardial effusion after a local hospital ultrasound had described “large pericardial effusion” that continued to increase. On admission, physical examination revealed a weight of 35 kg and height was 145.0 cm, with a body mass index of approximately 16.6 kg/m², which is within the normal range for his age, indicating good nutritional status. Temperature was 37.2 °C, pulse 101 beats/min, respiratory rate 19 breaths/min, blood pressure 111/71 mmHg, with distant heart sounds and no murmur heard, the liver was palpable 1 cm below the costal margin, soft in consistency and non-tender, no skin rash, subcutaneous nodules. Initial laboratory tests on admission revealed a peripheral blood routine examination revealed leukocytes at 7.74 × 10^9/L, neutrophils at 4.94 × 10^9/L, and eosinophils at 0.01 × 10^9/L. The serum high-sensitivity C-reactive protein was 24.70 mg/L, the T-cell spot test for tuberculosis and tuberculin skin test was negative. All values were within approximately normal ranges for liver and renal function, cardiac enzymes, electrolytes, coagulation profile, and troponin. On February 7, cardiac ultrasound revealed a large amount of fluid in the pericardial cavity and multiple nodular, mildly echogenic echoes within the pericardial cavity (Fig. 1). The child then underwent pericardial puncture with an indwelling drainage catheter. Moderate-to-large amounts of turbid, yellow fluid (≈ 300–400 mL/day) containing flocculent material was drained. Microscopic examination showed scattered lymphocytes and a few mesothelial cells; acid-fast bacilli were not detected. Cytology of the drained fluid showed a positive Rivalta test, cytology suggesting that the red blood cell count was 2000 × 10^6/L, protein content was 57.1 g/L, and eosinophils accounted for 2% of total cells, consistent with a non-hemorrhagic inflammatory effusion. Microbiological investigations, including bacterial culture and metagenomic next-generation sequencing of the pericardial fluid, revealed no typical pathogens. Pericardial drainage fluid parasite antibody test (ELISA method): positive for Paragonimus IgG. A repeat history revealed that the patient had consumed raw river crabs 3 years earlier. After admission, he underwent pericardial puncture drainage and received praziquantel (225 mg/kg total, administered over 3 days, 3 times a day) for oral antiparasitic treatment. Following 3 days of antiparasitic treatment, the drained pericardial effusion was significantly reduced. The pericardial drainage tubes were then withdrawn in preparation for discharge. The child presented with transient fever on February 22, and cardiac ultrasound monitoring showed diffuse pericardial thickening, with a thickened portion up to 5 mm long. After transfer to the cardiac surgery unit, he underwent pericardiectomy, pericardial adhesion release, and closed thoracic drainage on February 27 under general anesthesia. Intraoperatively, thickened, calcified pericardial tissue was seen (Fig. 2), and the postoperative specimen was sent for examination: (pericardial) tissue necrosis with granuloma formation (Fig. 3). Chest X-ray at 1 week after surgery (March 7) showed a reduced cardiothoracic ratio, and cardiac ultrasound showed minimal pericardial effusion, localized thickening, and normal left ventricular segmental and overall systolic function. At 1 and 3 months postoperatively, the child experienced no special discomfort, and the cardiac ultrasound results generally returned to the normal range.

During the diagnostic process, differential diagnoses were considered, including bacterial pericarditis, tuberculous pericarditis, myocarditis, and autoimmune diseases as potential causes of pericardial effusion. However, these were systematically ruled out based on the negative results from microbiological studies, the absence of typical clinical signs and symptoms, and the negative T-cell spot test for tuberculosis. The presence of positive Paragonimus IgG antibodies in the pericardial fluid, combined with the patient’s history of consuming raw river crabs, strongly suggested that the pericardial effusion was caused by a parasitic infection. The decision was made to proceed with surgical intervention to address the complications associated with the parasitic infection.

Discussion and conclusions

Although the number of cases has declined in recent years, paragonimiasis still poses a health problem in Asian countries that is difficult to ignore [2]. Paragonimiasis is a foodborne disease caused by Paragonimus infection, typically resulting from the ingestion of raw or undercooked freshwater crabs and crayfish that contain cysticerci [3]. Therefore, the history should be taken carefully and comprehensively to avoid misdiagnosis and underdiagnosis due to omission of crucial epidemiologic history. The clinical manifestations of this disease are complex and varied, with the main symptoms including shortness of breath, fever, cough, and abdominal pain. The fever is mostly mild to moderate, and half of the children experience vomiting and abdominal pain. However, the children in this case only had intermittent fever and pericardial effusion during the disease, with no other specific manifestations, which is inconsistent with the typical morbidity characteristics of this disease, and has particular reference value. At present, paragonimiasis with massive pericardial effusion is very rare in clinical practice, and only a few cases have been reported so far [4]. The present case of a child with simple massive pericardial effusion and completely normal eosinophils also provides us with a new reference.

The mechanism by which Paragonimus invades the pericardium is not yet fully understood. The most probable route is the direct penetration of the pericardium by larvae or adult worms from adjacent lung tissue or the pleural cavity, which has been documented in previous case reports [5]. Concurrently, after the parasites become localized, they incite an inflammatory process characterized by mechanical damage and host immune responses. Pathologically, this manifests as tissue necrosis, inflammatory cell infiltration, and fibrinous exudation, leading to pericardial effusion, thickening, and adhesions [6]. Parasitic infections are often accompanied by elevated eosinophils, which may be a crucial indicator of paragonimus infection [7]. However, elevated eosinophils are not specific, as allergic reactions, blood diseases, and even tumors may cause eosinophil abnormalities, so eosinophils cannot be used as the sole basis for diagnosing parasitic infection. Peripheral blood and pericardial fluid examinations in this child did not indicate a significant elevation of eosinophils. Therefore, parasitic infections cannot be completely ruled out in children who do not have elevated eosinophils, which makes the diagnosis and management of parasitic infections challenging. In this atypical clinical scenario, targeted serology proved indispensable. The positive Paragonimus IgG ELISA on pericardial fluid was the pivotal diagnostic finding. This test provides high specificity for chronic infection, directly implicating the parasite at the site of disease. Unlike the non-specific clue of eosinophilia, the fluid-based IgG result offered definitive, localized evidence, which was critical for differentiating the etiology and initiating directed antiparasitic treatment. When encountering febrile patients with serous-cavity effusion in whom eosinophilia is absent, blood, pericardial fluid eosinophils are not high in patients, purely bacterial, tuberculosis infection, tumors, connective tissue disease and other common causes cannot be explained, even from non-infected areas should be carefully asked about whether there is a history of raw or semi-raw freshwater crabs, crayfish, and improve the antibody to paragonimiasis, adult antigen skin test and other related examinations, to facilitate the early detection and targeted treatment can minimize morbidity in affected children. In clinical practice, when encountering patients with fever and serous cavity effusion without elevated eosinophils in blood or effusion fluid—and when common causes such as bacterial or tuberculous infection, tumors, or connective tissue diseases have been ruled out—clinicians should consider parasitic infections such as paragonimiasis. Even in patients from non-endemic areas, a careful history regarding consumption of raw or undercooked freshwater crabs or crayfish should be obtained. Appropriate investigations, including paragonimiasis antibody testing and adult antigen skin tests, are recommended to enable early detection. Targeted treatment initiated promptly can alleviate symptoms and improve outcomes in affected children.

The choice between pericardiocentesis drainage and surgical treatment options for pericardial effusion due to parasitic infection is inconclusive [8]. In this case, we first treated the child medically, including drainage of the pericardial effusion and antiparasitic therapy. In this case, the pericardial effusion disappeared during treatment, and there were no other clinical signs, so the child’s condition appeared to be improving. After antiparasitic treatment, the child developed a recurrence of fever. Given this clinical change, cardiac ultrasound was performed, which suggested diffuse pericardial thickening, suggesting that simple drainage of the pericardial effusion was ineffective, so pericardiectomy was performed. The procedure can adequately drain the pericardial cavity, obtain pericardial fluid specimens and wall pericardial specimens for examination, and remove fibrin exudates and adhesion bands in the pericardial cavity, thus reducing the incidence of constrictive pericarditis. Upon postoperative review, no recurrence of pericardial effusion or pericardial constriction was observed, indicating that surgical intervention was safe and effective for both diagnosis and treatment. In the context of this case, in a child with a large amount of pericardial effusion, the disappearance of the pericardial effusion does not mean that the child is cured, and even pericardial adhesion or pericardial constriction may be present. Therefore, dynamic evaluation of the condition, such as cardiac ultrasound, is necessary. In children whose condition responds poorly to medical therapy, early surgical intervention may prevent progression to constrictive pericarditis. In this child, intraoperatively we removed a large amount of granulation and necrotic tissue in the pericardial cavity, and this proliferative tissue could not be removed by drainage alone, suggesting the need for surgery.

In conclusion, in children with isolated pericardial effusion presentations, parasitic infections need to be excluded when necessary, and timely and dynamic evaluation and weighing of treatment options are critical.
