LPA4 in the spinal dorsal horn is a potential therapeutic target for inflammatory pain and neuropathic mechanical hypersensitivity
Authors: Takahisa Shimoda, Simeng Ma, Tomoya Tanaka, Haruto Taniguchi, Kenta Yamamoto, Satoru Yoshikawa, Kazue Hisaoka-Nakashima, Yoki Nakamura, Norimitsu Morioka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryNeuropathic pain is a major cause of chronic pain, but effective treatments remain limited. To investigate the role of lysophosphatidic acid receptor 4 (LPA4) in pain processing, we generated LPA4-knockout rats and evaluated inflammatory and multiple neuropathic pain models. LPA4 deficiency attenuated second-phase formalin responses and reduced mechanical hypersensitivity in the spared nerve injury and lumbar spinal stenosis models, without affecting baseline locomotor activity or mechanical sensitivity. RNAscope revealed Lpar4 expression in the spinal dorsal horn, mainly in astrocytes. Consistent with this distribution, spared nerve injury-induced astrocyte and microglial activation in the dorsal horn was suppressed in LPA4-knockout rats. Bulk RNA sequencing identified interleukin 1β as a candidate downstream mediator, and LPA-induced Il1b mRNA upregulation was not observed in LPA4-knockout astrocytes. These findings suggest that spinal LPA4 may contribute to inflammatory pain and neuropathic mechanical hypersensitivity, potentially through glial activation and interleukin 1β-related mechanisms in the spinal dorsal horn.

Introduction

Neuropathic pain, which arises from a lesion or disease of the somatosensory nervous system, significantly impairs lives of patients.1,2 Although several drugs are available for the treatment of neuropathic pain, they often present issues such as adverse effects and a lack of efficacy, meaning that there is a gap in effective medical treatments.3,4 Consequently, there is a need to identify novel therapeutic target molecules for neuropathic pain.

The spinal dorsal horn is a primary integrator of nociceptive input. Following peripheral nerve injury, activity-dependent plasticity in the dorsal horn initiates central sensitization, which is sustained by ongoing peripheral drive or by intrinsic molecular and cellular changes.5 Recent research indicates that glial cells, in addition to neurons, play a role in the development and maintenance of pain.6,7 Notably, spinal cord glial cells such as microglia and astrocytes have been implicated in chronic pain regulation. Moreover, inflammatory pain mechanisms can intersect with neuropathic pain through shared spinal dorsal horn processes such as central sensitization and glial activation.8

Numerous pain-inducing molecules have been identified, and lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) is recognized as a pain trigger.9,10 LPA is a multifunctional lipid mediator that binds to its receptors and subsequently initiates downstream signaling. LPA receptors can be classified into six subtypes (LPA1–6) and belong either to the endothelial differentiation gene (EDG) family (LPA1–3) or to the non-EDG family (LPA4–6).9,10 LPA receptors are expressed in various tissues and have different downstream signals, thereby affording them a variety of physiological and pathological roles.10 Several LPA receptors are reported to be associated with pain.10 For example, LPA1 is involved in the initiation of neuropathic pain through Schwann cell demyelination.11 In addition, LPA5 regulates pain through cyclic AMP-responsive element-binding protein (CREB) phosphorylation in neurons of the spinal dorsal horn.12 Cerebrospinal fluid concentrations of LPA exhibit a significant association with pain symptoms in patients with neuropathic pain and lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS), thus demonstrating a link between LPA and pain in humans.13,14 Collectively, these findings indicate that the LPA signal may have an essential role in neuropathic pain.

LPA4 (encoded by LPAR4) is an LPA receptor subtype that is involved in vascular and lymphatic vessel formation, adipocyte differentiation, and tumor initiation.15,16,17 In the mouse nervous system, LPA4 is required for the morphological organization and migration of newborn cortical neurons,18 and LPA4 is reportedly expressed in astrocytes as well as neurons in mice.19 Furthermore, single-cell transcriptomic data from the Human Protein Atlas indicate the enrichment of LPA4 expression in astrocytes.20,21 However, the physiological and pathological functions of LPA4 in the nervous system, particularly its role in modulating pain transmission, remain largely unknown. Considering the established link between LPA signaling and neuropathic pain, together with the presence of LPA4 within the central nervous system, we hypothesized that LPA4 might contribute to the mechanisms underlying pathological pain states.

In the present study, we used CRISPR-Cas9 to generate LPA4-knockout (KO) rats to directly test whether LPA4 contributes to pain regulation. We evaluated inflammatory pain and multiple models of neuropathic pain to investigate the role of LPA4 in pathological pain.

Results

LPA4-KO rats exhibit normal activity and baseline mechanical responses

To investigate the role of LPA4, we generated LPA4-KO rats using CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing (Figure 1A). Two single-guide RNAs were designed to flank and delete the entire coding region of the rat Lpar4 locus, thereby achieving complete deletion. We then performed genotyping and quantitative reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analyses to confirm the deletion of the Lpar4 locus and loss of Lpar4 transcripts. Because partial lethality has been reported in LPA4-deficient mice,15 we examined whether this phenotype was present in our rat model. We compared the numbers of live-born male pups between wild-type (WT) and KO litters and found no significant differences (data not shown), suggesting that LPA4 deletion does not impair viability in male rats.

After confirming LPA4 deficiency, we conducted behavioral tests to evaluate baseline activity and pain responses. First, we investigated the behavior of LPA4-KO rats, using the open-field test. We observed no significant differences between WT and LPA4-KO rats in terms of locomotor activity or rearing behavior (Figures 1D and 1E). Subsequently, we assessed the responses to acute mechanical stimulation using the von Frey filament test (Figure 1F). We observed no significant differences between WT and LPA4-KO rats in their responses to mechanical stimuli.

Together, these data indicated that LPA4 deletion in rats does not impair viability, locomotor activity, rearing behavior, or baseline mechanical sensitivity.

LPA4-KO rats exhibit attenuated inflammatory and neuropathic pain-related responses

Next, we investigated the role of LPA4 in pain regulation by using multiple pain models. First, we performed the formalin test, which is a widely used model for evaluating inflammatory pain. WT rats exhibited the typical biphasic pain response, whereas LPA4-KO rats showed normal first phase responses but significantly reduced second-phase behaviors (Figures 2A and 2B). Furthermore, LPA4-KO rats exhibited significantly reduced formalin-induced mechanical hypersensitivity compared with WT rats (Figure 2C). We subsequently evaluated the effects of LPA4 deficiency on neuropathic pain, using the spared nerve injury (SNI) model. Body weight and mechanical withdrawal thresholds were measured before and after SNI surgery in WT and LPA4-KO rats. No significant differences in body weight were observed between WT and LPA4-KO rats throughout the experiment (Figure 2D). However, although mechanical pain thresholds significantly decreased in WT rats following SNI surgery, mechanical hypersensitivity did not develop in LPA4-KO rats through day 28 post-SNI (Figure 2E). Additionally, contralateral mechanical pain thresholds remained stable in both the WT and LPA4-KO rats (Figure 2F). These results indicated that LPA4 deletion prevented mechanical hypersensitivity initiation or development in the neuropathic pain model.

To explore whether the pain resistance of LPA4-KO rats extends to other neuropathic pain models, we investigated the role of LPA4 in a different neuropathic pain model. Previous studies have reported elevated LPA concentrations in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients with LSS, and increased LPA concentrations in the spinal cord have also been observed in cauda equina compression (CEC) models, which reproduce the key pathophysiological features of LSS.14,22 Thus, to investigate the involvement of LPA4 in LSS, we established an LSS model and measured body weight and mechanical withdrawal thresholds before and after LSS surgery. No significant differences in body weight were observed between WT and LPA4-KO rats throughout the experiment (Figure 2G). After LSS surgery, bilateral mechanical thresholds were significantly reduced in WT rats, whereas no such reduction was observed in LPA4-KO rats (Figures 2H and 2I). These results indicated that LPA4 deletion prevents the development of mechanical hypersensitivity not only in the SNI model but also in the LSS model.

Intrathecal Lpar4 siRNA administration modestly attenuates mechanical hypersensitivity in CCI rats

On the basis of previous reports of LPA4 expression in the central nervous system, we investigated the specific role of LPA4 in spinal pain signal transmission. Using a chronic constriction injury (CCI) model, we evaluated the effects of intrathecal administration of siRNA targeting Lpar4 (Lpar4 siRNA). CCI rats treated with Lpar4 siRNA displayed a modest but significant increase in mechanical withdrawal thresholds on day 7 after intrathecal dosing (Figure S1). These findings suggest that spinal LPA4 contributes, at least in part, to the maintenance of mechanical hypersensitivity in the CCI model.

Astrocytes are a major cellular source of LPA4 in the rat spinal dorsal horn

To better understand the potential role of LPA4 in the spinal dorsal horn, we sought to identify the specific cell types that express LPA4 in the spinal dorsal horn. Initially, we tested commercially available anti-LPA4 antibodies for immunohistochemistry, using tissues from WT and LPA4-KO rats; however, no clear genotype-dependent difference in staining was observed, suggesting that these antibodies lacked sufficient specificity under our experimental conditions (data not shown). We, therefore, used RNAscope in situ hybridization (ISH). Lpar4 mRNA signals were clearly observed in the dorsal horn of WT rats but were absent in LPA4-KO rats (Figure 3A). These results confirmed the probe specificity and demonstrated the presence of Lpar4 mRNA in the spinal dorsal horn. In addition, RT-qPCR analysis in WT rats showed that Lpar4 mRNA expression was markedly lower in the dorsal root ganglion (DRG) than in the spinal dorsal horn (Figure S2). Next, we combined RNAscope ISH with immunohistochemistry, using cell type markers for neurons (neuronal nuclei [NeuN]); astrocytes, glial fibrillary acidic protein [GFAP], and microglia, ionized calcium binding adaptor molecule 1 [Iba1]). Lpar4 mRNA co-localized predominantly with GFAP-positive astrocytes; it showed intermediate co-localization with NeuN-positive neurons and sparse co-localization with Iba1-positive microglia (Figures 3B–3D). LPA4-KO rats show reduced glial activation in the spinal dorsal horn after SNI

Given the astrocytic enrichment of LPA4 in the dorsal horn (Figure 3), we assessed glial activation at day 28 post-SNI, using Iba1 and GFAP immunohistochemistry (Figures 4A and 4B). In WT rats, SNI induced morphological changes consistent with glial activation, including hypertrophic features in both astrocytes and microglia. In contrast, these morphological changes were attenuated in LPA4-KO rats. We, therefore, quantified microglia and astrocyte numbers and evaluated the fluorescence intensities of Iba1 and GFAP in the ipsilateral dorsal horn (Figures 4C–4F). In WT rats, SNI increased all four measures relative to sham, whereas LPA4-KO rats showed no SNI-induced increases. Transcriptomic profiling suggests interleukin 1β as a downstream mediator of LPA4 signaling

To clarify how LPA4 contributes to neuropathic pain signaling, we performed bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of ipsilateral dorsal horn tissue from WT and LPA4-KO rats at day 28 post-SNI and conducted exploratory differential expression analysis. We initially focused on gene transcripts that were upregulated or downregulated by SNI relative to sham in WT rats (Figure S3A). Subsequently, we examined gene expression in SNI WT versus SNI LPA4-KO rats (Figure S3B). In the same dataset, Lpar4 transcripts were detected in the dorsal horn, but their expression was not increased after SNI (Figure S3C). Although global transcriptional shifts were modest in LPA4-KO rats, our analysis highlighted Il1b mRNA, which encodes the proinflammatory cytokine interleukin 1β (IL-1β). SNI robustly induced Il1b mRNA in the WT dorsal horn, whereas this increase was blunted in LPA4-KO rats (Figure S3D). To further assess the relationship between LPA4 signaling and Il1b expression, we examined primary astrocytes prepared from WT and LPA4-KO rats. LPA stimulation significantly increased Il1b mRNA expression in WT astrocytes, whereas this response was not observed in LPA4-KO astrocytes (Figure S4). These findings support the idea that LPA4 contributes to LPA-induced Il1b upregulation in astrocytes.

Discussion

Neuropathic pain significantly impairs quality of life and affects approximately 7%–10% of the population.1 However, existing treatments are inadequate because of limitations in their efficacy and safety.2 In the present study, we demonstrated that LPA4-KO rats showed attenuated pain-related behavior in the formalin test and reduced neuropathic mechanical hypersensitivity in multiple models, despite having no effects on baseline mechanical sensitivity or locomotor activity. Together, these results suggest that LPA4 may serve as a novel therapeutic target for inflammatory pain and neuropathic mechanical hypersensitivity.

Rodents, and particularly rats, are widely used in pain research because of their suitability for behavioral tests.23 We generated LPA4-KO rats using CRISPR-Cas9 technology and confirmed that Lpar4 expression was completely absent at both the genomic and mRNA levels. The LPA4-KO rats exhibited no abnormalities in baseline mechanical sensitivity, locomotor activity, or rearing behavior, indicating that LPA4 is not essential for normal pain perception or general behavior.

Previous studies have revealed that LPA1 and LPA5 contribute to the initiation and development of neuropathic pain11,12; however, the role of LPA4 in pain signaling has remained unclear. We hypothesized that LPA4 may be involved in pain control because it is expressed in astrocytes in the central nervous system, and glial cells such as astrocytes are involved in chronic pain.7,19,21 Notably, mechanistic studies of LPA4 have been limited by a lack of selective LPA4 inhibitors. To investigate whether LPA4 is involved in pain transmission, we evaluated pain sensitivity in LPA4-KO rats by using various pain models. First, we performed a formalin test to model inflammatory pain. LPA4-KO rats showed normal acute pain responses in the first phase but significant reductions in pain behavior in the second phase. The finding that an effect of LPA4 deficiency was observed in the second phase rather than the first phase of the formalin test suggests that LPA4 is involved in the control of persistent inflammatory responses and the transmission of pain based on these responses, rather than in acute pain transmission. It has also been reported that the activation of glial cells in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord is involved in second-phase responses.24,25,26,27 Furthermore, LPA4-KO rats had completely suppressed post-second-phase mechanical hypersensitivity. The long-term development of mechanical hypersensitivity following formalin administration has been previously reported and is thought to involve central sensitization and the activation of spinal glial cells.28 It can, therefore, be hypothesized that LPA4 in glial cells is involved in the exacerbation of formalin-induced hypersensitivity.

Next, we examined whether LPA4 is involved in neuropathic pain, using the SNI model, which is widely used in neuropathic pain research. SNI-induced mechanical hypersensitivity did not develop in LPA4-KO rats. This finding suggests that LPA4 contributes to the initiation or development of SNI-induced mechanical hypersensitivity and that its deletion suppresses this process. Furthermore, because long-term pain behavior was also completely suppressed in LPA4-KO rats, it is possible that LPA4 is involved in the maintenance of neuropathic pain. Given the reported involvement of glial cells in the spinal dorsal horn in the onset and maintenance of SNI-induced mechanical hypersensitivity (in particular, the importance of astrocytes), the present results suggest an important role of LPA4 in their regulation.6

In previous studies, elevated LPA concentrations in the cerebrospinal fluid have been reported in patients with LSS, and increased LPA concentrations in the spinal cord have also been demonstrated in the CEC model, which is a disease model of LSS.14,22 Furthermore, increased Lpar1, Lpar5, and Lpar6 mRNA expression has been reported in this model.22 Further experiments investigated the analgesic effects of an inhibitor of autotaxin, which is an LPA-producing enzyme, using mechanical compression of the DRG as another LSS-related model.29 In both cases, the specific involvement of LPA receptors was not demonstrated. In the present study, we, therefore, developed the LSS model to explore the effects of pain using LPA4-KO rats. The LSS model LPA4-KO rats exhibited attenuated mechanical hypersensitivity. These findings suggest that LPA4 may be involved in pain signaling in LSS. Although previous reports have not shown an increase of Lpar4 mRNA in peripheral tissue or spinal cord in the CEC model,22 it is interesting to note that even in studies in which the LPA4-deficient mouse model of diet-induced obesity exhibited a metabolically healthy obese phenotype, Lpar4 mRNA was unchanged.16 From these findings, we speculate that LPA4 may contribute to disease pathology, even in the absence of mRNA upregulation. These results support the idea that LPA4 contributes to mechanical hypersensitivity in multiple neuropathic pain models and may be involved in both the initiation and maintenance of chronic pain states.

Previous reports have noted that LPA4 is expressed in astrocytes in the central nervous system of both humans and mice.19,20,21 We, therefore, investigated the role of spinal LPA4 in neuropathic pain by using RNA interference in a rat CCI model. Intrathecal administration of two independent Lpar4 siRNAs modestly attenuated mechanical hypersensitivity in the rat CCI model. However, because in vivo knockdown in the spinal dorsal horn was not clearly confirmed, these findings should be considered supportive information.

In the current study, ISH was performed to identify cells expressing Lpar4 in the rat spinal cord. Lpar4 was mainly expressed in GFAP-positive astrocytes in the spinal dorsal horn, consistent with previous reports in humans and mice.19,20,21 Given that astrocytes are involved in pain maintenance and development, these results suggest that astrocytic LPA4 may contribute importantly to pain regulation.30,31 However, it is worth noting that Lpar4 is also expressed in neurons and microglia, though less frequently than in astrocytes. Therefore, because Lpar4 was also detected in a subset of neurons, and a global LPA4-KO rat was used in this study, the contribution of neuronal LPA4 to pain regulation cannot be excluded. Our immunohistochemical analysis revealed that the activation of astrocytes and microglia that was observed in the spinal dorsal horn after SNI was not increased in LPA4-KO rats. These results suggest that LPA4 may contribute to the progression of neuropathic pain through glial activation. However, because a global LPA4-KO rat was used in this study, we cannot exclude the possible contribution of LPA4 outside the spinal dorsal horn, including in DRG cells. Notably, RT-qPCR analysis in WT rats showed that Lpar4 mRNA expression was markedly lower in the DRG than in the spinal dorsal horn, supporting the relevance of spinal dorsal horn LPA4 in the pain-related phenotype observed here.

To elucidate the downstream molecular mechanisms of LPA4, we performed bulk RNA-seq of spinal dorsal horn tissue at day 28 post-SNI. Among the genes with significantly altered expression levels, we focused on Il1b. This inflammatory cytokine was increased after injury in WT rats but was suppressed in LPA4-KO rats. IL-1β plays a central role in the regulation of neuroinflammation and pain,32 and its expression increases in various tissues, including the spinal cord, in the SNI model33 and increases in spinal cord glial cells during pain.34,35 In addition, LPA can engage IL-1β signaling, and IL-1β-neutralizing antibodies can attenuate LPA-evoked pain.36 Furthermore, because the mechanical hypersensitivity observed after spinal nerve ligation is suppressed in both early and late phases by intrathecal administration of IL-1β-neutralizing antibodies, this suggests that IL-1β may be involved in pain information processing at the spinal cord level, particularly in the dorsal horn.37 In primary astrocytes, LPA increased Il1b mRNA expression in WT cells, whereas this response was not observed in LPA4-KO cells. Together with the suppression of Il1b mRNA expression in LPA4-KO rats, these findings support the idea that astrocytic LPA4 may contribute to pain signaling, at least in part, through IL-1β-related mechanisms.

Limitations of the study

We acknowledge several limitations in this study. First, all analyses of LPA4-KO rats were performed using males. Because Lpar4 is located on the X chromosome, genetic modification in males results in a complete hemizygous null state. This eliminates genotype heterogeneity, enabling clear phenotypic analysis. Furthermore, this approach avoids the risk of pain threshold variability due to the estrous cycle. Therefore, males were used for this initial investigation, and the role of LPA4 in female rats remains a topic for future research. Second, we did not assess basal heat- or cold-evoked nociceptive responses, nor did we evaluate heat- or cold-evoked hypersensitivity in the pain models used in this study. Therefore, our conclusions are limited to the pain-related behaviors and mechanical hypersensitivity assessed in the present study. Third, RNA-seq analysis was performed only at day 28 after SNI, and the observed gene expression changes were more modest than expected, raising the possibility that data from earlier time points would be more informative. In addition, because this RNA-seq analysis was exploratory, further protein-level analyses are necessary to elucidate the detailed functions of IL-1β in future studies. Finally, the commercially available antibodies tested did not demonstrate sufficient specificity between WT and LPA4-KO tissues, preventing a reliable assessment of LPA4 levels, and we did not examine pain state-dependent changes in Lpar4 expression in the inflammatory pain model.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

Animal experiments using WT and LPA4-KO rats were conducted in accordance with the “Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals” established by Hiroshima University, and all experimental procedures involving animals were approved by the Committee of Research Facilities for Laboratory Animal Science of Hiroshima University (Permit Number: A25-21).

Male WT and LPA4-KO Sprague-Dawley rats (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from Asahi Kasei Therapeutics Corporation (Shizuoka, Japan). The animals were maintained in a vivarium with a room temperature of 22°C ± 2°C and a 12-h light/dark cycle (lights on/off at 8:00 a.m./8:00 p.m.); food and water were available ad libitum during the experimental period.

The LPA4-KO rats were generated at the Institute of Immunology Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). The experimental procedures for generating the LPA4-KO rats were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Institute of Immunology Co., Ltd. (Approval Number: 1142). Briefly, two crRNAs targeting the rat Lpar4 gene (rLpar4 crRNA-1: 5′-TAGCGGTCCTGCCGCAAAGA-3′, rLpar4 crRNA-2: 5′-CCCCTACACCTTAGCATGAT-3′), tracrRNA, and Cas9 protein were prepared. These were introduced into the fertilized eggs of Sprague-Dawley rats using electroporation, according to established methods.43 Mosaic founders were screened for targeted deletions, and selected mosaic male were bred with female WT Sprague-Dawley rats to generate F1 progeny.

Genotyping was performed to confirm the Lpar4 gene alteration. Before the behavioral tests, ear tissue was collected from each rat via ear punching under isoflurane anesthesia. DNA was isolated from these samples using the Kaneka Easy DNA Extraction Kit version 2 (Cat: # KN-T110005, Kaneka, Hyogo, Japan). The extracted DNA served as a template for PCR amplification using TaKaRa LA Taq with GC Buffer (Cat: # RR02AG, Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan) and the following primers: 5′-GGCAAGTGTTATAGCTCTGGGAC-3′ (rLpar4upF1), 5′-GTTTCACAGTCAAGAATTAGTGGCT-3′ (rLpar4upR1), and 5′-GAAAGAAAGGGTGCATTGTTGAG-3′ (rLpar4dwR1). Finally, the PCR amplicons were visualized on an agarose gel.

The LPA4 siRNA animal experiments were conducted at Jubilant Biosys Limited (Bengaluru, India), a CPCSEA-registered facility, under contract with Asahi Kasei Therapeutics Corporation. The experiments were conducted in accordance with applicable institutional and national guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals and were reviewed and approved by Jubilant’s Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (Approval number: IAEC-JDC-2019-182R).

Primary cell culture

Primary cultured astrocytes, from neonatal Sprague-Dawley rats, were prepared as previously described.44 Primary astrocytes were isolated from mixed culture after 3 weeks. Isolated primary astrocytes were plated into 96well plate (2.5 × 104 cells/well). After five days, the medium was replaced with serum-free Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) with 0.1% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA). The cells were used for experiments on the following day.

Method details

mRNA preparation and reverse transcription from tissue samples

The dorsal horn of the spinal cord was harvested from each rat. Tissue from individual rats was pooled into single tubes, and total RNA was purified using the Monarch Total RNA Miniprep Kit (Catalog # NEB #T2110, New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Reverse transcription

The concentration of the prepared total RNA samples was determined by measuring absorbance at 260 nm. For the reverse transcription reaction, 1 μg of total RNA was used as a template. Total RNA (1 μg) was denatured at 70°C for 10 min, after which a reaction mixture was added containing 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM deoxynucleotide triphosphates, 2.5 μM random primers, reverse transcription buffer, Moloney murine leukemia virus reverse transcriptase, and RNase inhibitor. The total 30 μL reaction mixture was then incubated at 25°C for 10 min, 42°C for 60 min, and 99°C for 5 min. The resulting complementary DNA (cDNA) was stored at −20°C.

Quantitative real-time PCR

For quantitative PCR analysis, a 20-μL reaction was set up containing 2.0 μL (0.2 μg) of cDNA template, 0.2 μM of forward/reverse primers, and THUNDERBIRD Next SYBR qPCR mix (Toyobo Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan). Amplification and real-time detection were performed on a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System with a three-step cycling protocol. The expression levels were normalized using glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (Gapdh) as a housekeeping gene. The specific primers were as follows: 5′-AGCCCAGAACATCATCCCTG-3′ (Gapdh forward), 5′-CACCACCTTCTTGATGTCATC-3′ (Gapdh reverse), 5′-TGCTTAGAACCCTCCGCAAG-3′ (Lpar4 forward), and 5′-GAAGGTTTGGGGGTCAGAGG-3′ (Lpar4 reverse).

SNI model

The SNI model was established based on a previously described method45 with some modifications. Under isoflurane anesthesia, the skin on the left thigh of each rat was incised approximately 2 cm. After exposing the sciatic nerve, the common peroneal and tibial nerves were ligated with 5-0 silk sutures and transected distally to the ligation sites (the sural nerve was preserved). The incision was then closed with 3-0 silk sutures. In the sham control group, the left thigh was similarly incised, and the sciatic nerve was exposed, but no nerve ligation or transection was performed. The incision was then closed in the same manner as in the model rat.

LSS model

The LSS model was established based on a previously described method46 with some modifications. Following anesthesia, a skin and muscle incision was made at the level of the Th13–L3 vertebrae. Next, the paravertebral muscles at the L1–L2 intervertebral space were dissected to expose the vertebrae. A round silicone rubber cord (1 mm in diameter, 4 mm in length) was then inserted into the L2 epidural space without performing a laminectomy. The incision was closed with 3-0 silk sutures, and cefazolin sodium (40 mg/kg) was administered intraperitoneally to prevent infection. In the sham group, the paravertebral muscles were similarly removed to expose the vertebrae, but no cord was inserted.

CCI model

The CCI model was established based on a previously described method with some modifications.47 Under isoflurane anesthesia, following shaving and disinfection of the lateral thigh, the sciatic nerve was exposed and loosely ligated at four points using 4-0 chromic catgut sutures. Sham-operated animals underwent nerve exposure without ligation. After surgery, animals were returned to their home cages for recovery.

Intrathecal catheterization and siRNA treatment

To enable intrathecal (i.t.) administration, a PE-10 catheter (INSTECH, USA) was implanted at the L5–L6 intervertebral level. The catheter was carefully inserted into the subarachnoid space via the intervertebral foramen to avoid spinal cord damage. The external end of the catheter was sealed, and rats were individually housed postoperatively. Two types of LPA4 siRNA were prepared (LPA4 siRNA No.1 A-096715-13, No.2 A-096715-14), and non-targeting siRNA was used as a control (Dharmacon, Cat. No. D-001910-01). siRNA (10 μg/5 μL) was administered intrathecally on days 14, 16, 18, and 20 after CCI surgery.

Open field test

Basal locomotor activity was assessed in WT and LPA4-KO rats using the open field test. Rats were individually placed in the center of a cubical chamber (48 × 48 × 48 cm). Total horizontal movements were quantified over a 5-min session using an automatic actigraphy system (SCANET MV-40; Melquest, Toyama, Japan), which detected interruptions of near-infrared photobeams. Prior to testing, animals were habituated to the dimly lit (with indirect white lighting) testing room for 30 min.

von Frey test

The assessment of mechanical hypersensitivity was performed in accordance with the methods described in our previous study.48 In brief, hind paw mechanical thresholds (g) were determined using von Frey filaments. Following a 30-min habituation period, rats were tested by applying various filaments to the plantar surface of the hind paw. A positive nocifensive response included licking, biting, shaking, or withdrawal of the paw. Filaments were applied up to a maximum intensity of 26 g. Each filament was applied five times to the same paw, with a minimum 10-s interval between stimulations. The mechanical threshold was defined as the lowest filament intensity that produced a positive response in at least three of five applications.

Formalin test

The formalin test was performed to evaluate inflammatory pain-like behaviors. Rats received an intraplantar injection of 0.2 mL of 1% formalin solution into the hind paw. Pain-related behaviors, including cumulative time spent flinching/lifting the injected paw and licking/biting the injection site, were measured. The behavioral response was segregated into two distinct phases: Phase 1, representing the acute response (0–20 min post-injection), and Phase 2, reflecting a more sustained response (20–120 min post-injection). Behavioral metrics were recorded at 5-min intervals for quantitative assessments, and cumulative totals for both phases were calculated for comparison. Mechanical hypersensitivity, triggered by the inflammatory response, was assessed using von Frey filaments 2 h after formalin administration.

Perfusion, fixation, and tissue preparation

For tissue collection, rats were deeply anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg) and 2% isoflurane. After a midline skin incision, transcardial perfusion was initiated through the left ventricle: first with 250 mL of saline to clear the vasculature, and then with 150 mL of 4% (weight [w]/volume [v]) paraformaldehyde for fixation. This fixative was prepared by combining 8% (w/v) paraformaldehyde with 0.2 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) in a 1:1 ratio. Spinal cord dorsal horn tissue was then dissected and further fixed in 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde for a minimum of 24 h. In preparation for cryosectioning, the tissue underwent cryoprotection by immersion in 30% (w/v) sucrose solution (derived from a 1:1 dilution of 60% (w/v) sucrose in 0.2 M phosphate buffer [pH 7.4]) at 4°C for 7 days. Using a cryostat (CM3050 S II, Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany), 30 μm frozen sections were generated for immunohistochemical analysis after the tissue as embedded in Shandon M-1 Embedding Matrix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), and 14 μm frozen sections for ISH were obtained after the tissue was embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (Catalog # 45833, Sakura Finetek, Tokyo, Japan).

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry was performed on 30 μm frozen sections. Initially, sections were rinsed three times for 5 min each with glycine-containing phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). To minimize non-specific binding, the sections were then blocked for 2 h at room temperature (22°C–26°C) with a solution comprising 10% serum, 0.05% Tween 20, 0.1% Triton X-100, and 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS. Following blocking, sections received three additional 5-min washes with glycine-containing PBS. Primary antibodies targeting GFAP (astrocyte marker; Catalog #G3893, RRID: AB_477010, 1:1,000, Merck, Boston, MA, USA) and Iba1 (microglial marker; Catalog # 019–19741, RRID: AB_839504, 1:1,000, Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Osaka, Japan) were diluted 1:500 in 3% BSA-PBS and applied to the sections. Incubation was performed at 4°C for 72 h. After primary antibody incubation, the sections were thoroughly washed with 0.1% BSA-PBS (six 5-min washes). Fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-mouse immunoglobulin G [IgG], Catalog # A32723, RRID: AB_2633275, 1:500, Thermo Fisher Scientific; Alexa Fluor 555-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG, Catalog # A32732, RRID: AB_2633281,1:500, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; 1:2000) were prepared in 3% BSA-PBS, added to the sections, and incubated for 2 h at 4°C in the dark. Excess secondary antibody was removed by six 5-min washes with 0.1% BSA-PBS, and the sections were then mounted under coverslips. Finally, images of the stained specimens were captured at 20× magnification using an all-in-one fluorescence microscope (BZ-900 Biorevo, Keyence, Osaka, Japan). The acquired images were analyzed using CellProfiler.

RNAscope ISH

RNAscope ISH was performed using the RNAscope 2.5 HD Reagent Kit-RED (Catalog # 322350, Advanced Cell Diagnostics, Newark, CA, USA). For co-detection with immunohistochemistry, the RNA-Protein Co-Detection Ancillary Kit (Catalog # 323180, Advanced Cell Diagnostics) was also utilized. Staining was conducted with the reagents provided in each kit according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Briefly, 14 μm tissue sections were heated at 60°C for 30 min, followed by fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 15 min at 4°C. Next, the sections were dehydrated through an ethanol series (50%, 70%, and twice in 100% EtOH) and air-dried at room temperature. Target retrieval was performed by immersing the sections in a preheated 1× Co-Detection Target Retrieval solution (98°C–102°C) for 5 min, followed by washes in distilled water. Sections were then treated with RNAscope Hydrogen Peroxide for 10 min at room temperature, followed by washes in distilled water and 1× PBS containing Tween 20. A hydrophobic barrier was drawn around the tissue to prevent drying. The sections were promptly incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against GFAP (1:200), NeuN (neuronal marker; Catalog # ABN78, RRID: AB_10807945, 1:100, Merck), and Iba1 (1:100), diluted in Co-Detection Antibody Diluent.

Next, sections were treated with Protease Plus for 30 min at 40°C in a preheated oven. After washes in distilled water, the sections were incubated with the preheated LPA4 probe for 2 h at 40°C, ensuring complete tissue coverage. Unbound probe was removed by washes in 1× Wash Buffer at room temperature. Subsequently, the signal amplification steps were performed sequentially as per standard RNAscope protocols: AMP1 (30 min at 40°C), AMP2 (15 min at 40°C), AMP3 (30 min at 40°C), AMP4 (15 min at 40°C), AMP5 (30 min at room temperature), and AMP6 (15 min at room temperature). Each amplification step was followed by washes with 1× Wash Buffer.

Signal detection was performed following the RNAscope protocol. Sections were incubated with a Fast RED solution for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. After washes, Co-Detection Blocker was applied for 15 min at 40°C, and the sections were washed again. For immunofluorescent co-staining, sections were then incubated for 60 min at room temperature with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-mouse IgG; Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG, Catalog # A32731TR, RRID: AB_2866491, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and DAPI, diluted in Co-Detection Antibody Diluent, to visualize GFAP, NeuN, or Iba1 along with nuclear staining. Finally, the sections were washed sequentially with distilled water, PBS containing Tween 20, and PBS containing 0.1% BSA before being mounted. Images were captured at 60× magnification using a Leica Stellaris 5 laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems). The acquired images were analyzed using CellProfiler.

Il1b expression analysis in rat primary astrocytes

Rat primary astrocytes were treated with lysophosphatidic acid (LPA, 1 μM) for 30 min, after which the culture medium was removed. Total RNA was extracted and cDNA was synthesized using the TaqMan Gene Expression Cells-to-CT Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR was then performed using TaqMan Gene Expression Master Mix. TaqMan Gene Expression Assays for Gapdh (Rn01775763_g1) and Il1b (Rn00580432_m1) were used, and gene expression levels were normalized to Gapdh.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

Total RNA was processed for sequencing using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Sequencing was conducted on the Illumina NovaSeq platform in paired-end mode (150 bp × 2) (performed by Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan). Raw sequencing reads were subjected to quality control using fastp (v0.22.0). High-quality reads were aligned to the rat reference genome (mRatBN7.2) using STAR aligner (v2.7.9a). Gene-level counts and transcripts per million (TPM) were calculated using RSEM (v1.3.1). Differential gene expression analysis was performed with DESeq2 (v1.38.3) in R (v4.2.3).

Quantification and statistical analysis

All data in the present study are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean. Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 7 (RRID: SCR_005375; GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). Statistical comparisons were made using repeated measures two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or two-way ANOVA. All ANOVA analyses were followed by post hoc tests for multiple comparisons. Direct comparisons between two groups were assessed using an unpaired t test. A p value <0.05 was considered significant.
