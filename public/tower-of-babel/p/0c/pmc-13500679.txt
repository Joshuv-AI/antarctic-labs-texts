Synthesis of poly(4HB-co-PhLA) and poly(3HB-co-4HB-co-PhLA) as sustainable hot-melt adhesives using engineered Escherichia coli
Authors: Minju Kang, Youngjoon Lee, Gi Bae Kim, Sang Yup Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConventional petroleum-based adhesives are non-biodegradable and raise environmental concerns. Polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHAs), bacterial polyesters synthesized from renewable carbon sources, are promising adhesive candidates due to biodegradability and structural diversity. Here, we report aromatic PHA copolymers, poly[4-hydroxybutyrate(4HB)-co-phenyllactate(PhLA)] and poly[3-hydroxybutyrate(3HB)-co-4HB-co-PhLA], exhibiting thermal stability and hot-melt adhesive (HMA) properties. Escherichia coli strains are engineered to produce these polymers from glucose and polymer biosynthesis is enhanced by optimizing heterologous expression, introducing additional CoA transferases and temporally regulating gene expression. We applied in silico simulations to systematically identify engineering targets that improve the synthesis of multiple monomers. The final engineered strains produce 10.2 g l−1 of poly(32.7 mol% 4HB-co-PhLA) and 52.8 g l−1 of poly(3HB-co-2.7 mol% 4HB-co-5.6 mol% PhLA). The resulting polymers demonstrate adhesive performance and reusability, expanding the available polymer property portfolio. Overall, we present a sustainable and expandable strategy for the microbial production of reusable, bio-based HMAs with tunable adhesive properties and practical application potential.Subject terms: Biomaterials, Metabolic engineering, Biopolymers

Introduction

Adhesives are polymer-based materials that bond two distinct substrates and serve essential functions in a wide range of applications, such as medical bandages, packaging, electronics, and biomedical devices1,2. As demand for versatile and high-performance bonding agents grows, the global adhesive market is expected to reach approximately USD 93.3 billion by 20283. Commercial adhesives are typically classified based on their curing or solidification mechanism into solvent-based adhesives, reactive adhesives, and hot-melt adhesives (HMAs). Among these, HMAs are solvent-free solid adhesives that are applied in the molten state and have achieved broad industrial adoption owing to their rapid adhesion, mechanical robustness, and absence of volatile organic compound emissions1,4. Despite their versatility and strong performance, HMAs and other commercial adhesives predominantly rely on petroleum-derived, non-biodegradable polymers, resulting in limitations in environmental sustainability and end-of-life management.

Petrochemical-based adhesives not only require manufacturing processes that involve toxic catalysts and hazardous chemicals, but also exhibit poor degradability after disposal and may release toxic substances during long-term decomposition4,5. In addition, even when biodegradable packaging is used, the presence of nondegradable adhesives can compromise the overall compostability and circularity of the product6. Therefore, development of biodegradable adhesives from renewable sources can play an important role in addressing the plastic waste crisis and facilitating the transition toward a sustainable circular economy5. In response to this issue, the production of adhesive polymers from renewable biomass resources such as lignocellulosics and soy proteins has been extensively studied7,8. However, these alternatives have encountered challenges, as they are often water-soluble, limiting their practical application, or require additional chemical processes involving toxic catalysts during production.

Among the various adhesive candidate materials, polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHAs) produced through bacterial fermentation from renewable carbon sources have attracted prominent interest as adhesive materials due to their biodegradability and structural diversity9–11. PHAs contain ester linkages and terminal hydroxyl groups that enable physical interactions with various substrate surfaces, supporting their potential application as adhesives11. For example, medium-chain-length PHAs, which are flexible and rubbery, have been proposed as bioadhesives12. In another study, stereomicrostructure control of poly(3-hydroxybutyrate) [P(3HB)] synthesized from lactone enabled reusable HMAs with adhesive strength up to 9.5 MPa by tuning viscoelasticity and crystallinity, achieving a melting temperature (Tm) of around 100–170 °C and a degradation temperature (Td) around 250 °C11. Copolymers comprising 3HB and 4-hydroxybutyrate (4HB) repeating units have also been reported for HMAs application13. One notable example is a terpolymer comprising D-lactate, glycolate, and 4HB, which is a non-natural PHA exhibiting high viscosity and stickiness14. This terpolymer demonstrates that the physical properties of PHAs can be tailored by adjusting monomer composition, providing a strategy to design materials with desired characteristics for adhesion. However, these previously reported PHAs-based adhesives are derived exclusively from aliphatic polyesters and therefore exhibit relatively low thermal stability and a narrow processing window15. Since HMAs rely on thermal softening and solidification4, securing a wide processing window and high thermal stability is essential for HMAs development4.

In this study, we report the development of non-natural aromatic PHAs, poly(4HB-co-PhLA) and poly(3HB-co-4HB-co-PhLA). In these polymers, the incorporation of 4HB units imparts sticky and viscous properties, while aromatic monomer D-phenyllactate (PhLA) enhances thermal stability and mechanical strength16. These polymers were produced through a one-step fermentation process from glucose by introducing biosynthetic pathways into E. coli strains (Fig. 1a). Systems metabolic engineering was subsequently applied to enhance production performance and an in silico simulation using genome-scale metabolic model (GEM) incorporating the copolymer biosynthesis reaction enabled integrated evaluation of multiple precursor biosynthetic routes. The thermomechanical properties and adhesive performance of the produced polymers were evaluated to demonstrate their potential applicability as bio-based HMAs with practical adhesive performance.

Results

Microbial production of poly(4HB-co-PhLA) by feeding sodium 4HB

Metabolic pathways for the biosynthesis of poly(4HB-co-PhLA) were designed and validated. To synthesize this copolymer, both 4HB and PhLA must be converted into their CoA-activated forms, 4-hydroxybutyryl-CoA (4HB-CoA) and D-phenyllactyl-CoA (PhLA-CoA), by CoA-transferase and subsequently polymerized by PHA synthase (PhaC). We have demonstrated in our previous studies that PhLA can be converted to PhLA-CoA by HadA (isocaprenoyl-CoA:2-hydroxyisocaproate CoA-transferase) from Clostridium difficile, and polymerized by the PhaC1437TM, engineered PhaC1 from Pseudomonas sp. MBEL 6-1916,17. We also found that HadA exhibits in vitro activity toward 4HB16. Given the broad substrate specificity of PhaC1437TM, we reasoned that employing both HadA and PhaC1437TM could enable the copolymerization of PhLA and 4HB.

To evaluate the feasibility of the engineered metabolic pathway for poly(4HB-co-PhLA) production, PhLA was supplied endogenously by using E. coli TLF17BPAL17, a strain previously engineered for PhLA overproduction, as the host, while 4HB was provided exogenously by adding varying concentrations of sodium 4HB (Na4HB) into the culture medium14. Plasmid pPs619C1437TM-HadA, harboring the hadA and the phaC1437TM genes, was transformed into the E. coli TLF17BPAL strain. When the resulting strain was cultivated in shake flasks, poly(4HB-co-PhLA) was successfully produced, and the 4HB molar fraction increased from 14.9 mol% to 36.7 mol% as the Na4HB feeding concentration increased from 0.2 to 4 g l−1 (Fig. 1b), indicating the polymer composition can be tuned by adjusting precursor availability. The highest polymer titer (0.20 g l−1) was achieved at 1 g l−1 Na4HB supplementation. However, further increasing the Na4HB concentration to 4 g l−1 led to a reduction in polymer production (0.15 g l−1).

To confirm the polymer structure, the synthesized polymer containing 24.4 mol% 4HB was extracted and analyzed by NMR spectroscopy. Characteristic signals corresponding to both 4HB and PhLA units14,17 were observed, together with spectral features indicative of polymerization, supporting assignment to poly(4HB-co-PhLA). (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Data 4).

To assess the thermal behavior and feasibility of the produced poly(4HB-co-PhLA) as HMAs, the glass-transition temperature (Tg) and adhesive strength of the polymers with varying 4HB compositions were measured using differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) and lap shear adhesion test, respectively (Fig. 1d). The Tg of the polymers decreased as the 4HB molar fraction increased, reaching a minimum of 3 °C in the copolymer containing 36.7 mol% 4HB. Increasing Na4HB supplementation resulted in greater incorporation of the more flexible 4HB units, leading to a gradual decrease in Tg.

Lap shear adhesion tests were conducted on stainless-steel substrates using the extracted polymers (Supplementary Fig. 2). Among the tested compositions, the copolymer containing 24.4 mol% 4HB showed the highest adhesion strength of 4.44 MPa, whereas the PhLA homopolymer, which lacks 4HB units, exhibited substantially weaker adhesion (0.39 MPa). These composition-dependent adhesion behaviors are likely attributable to differences in the structural characteristics of the constituent monomers. Whereas 4HB contains four main-chain carbon atoms and more carbon-carbon single bonds, conferring greater conformational flexibility that can enhance adhesion18, PhLA has a shorter two-carbon main chain and a rigid aromatic ring that restricts chain rotation and increases stiffness19. Consistent with this, poly(PhLA) and copolymers with higher PhLA content exhibited elevated Tg values, reflecting increased chain rigidity and reduced adhesion strength20.

Overall, incorporation of an appropriate fraction of 4HB units is expected to enhance adhesive properties. Considering polymer production, Tg, and adhesion performance collectively, a 4HB molar fraction of approximately 24–34 mol% was selected as the optimal target composition range.

Metabolic engineering for the production of poly(4HB-co-PhLA) from glucose

To produce poly(4HB-co-PhLA) without precursor supplementation, metabolic engineering was performed to enable in vivo generation of 4HB from glucose. First, to construct a biosynthetic pathway for 4HB production, two heterologous genes encoding succinate semialdehyde dehydrogenase (sucD) and 4-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase (4hbD) from Clostridium kluyveri21 were introduced (Fig. 1a). Plasmids pPs619C1437TM, pBBR1_HadA, and pACYC184KS_s4D that harbor phaC1437TM, hadA, and sucD–4hbD genes, respectively, were constructed for heterologous gene expression. Second, metabolic flux toward 4HB was enhanced by eliminating competing pathways. Because the 4HB precursor, succinate semialdehyde, is degraded in E. coli by succinate semialdehyde dehydrogenases, the yneI and gabD genes encoding these enzymes21 were deleted from the E. coli TLF17BPAL strain, generating the 4P0 strain.

The 4P0 strain harboring plasmids pPs619C1437TM, pBBR1_HadA, and pACYC184KS_s4D was cultivated in MR medium containing glucose as the sole carbon source. However, the produced polymer did not contain detectable 4HB units. We hypothesized that insufficient optimization of heterologous gene expression limited 4HB generation and subsequent polymer biosynthesis. Therefore, to achieve polymer production within the desired 4HB composition range, six plasmid-based gene-expression systems (ES1–ES6) were constructed, where ES1 system corresponds to the initial plasmid set (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Table 1).

These expression systems (ES1–ES6) were introduced into the 4P0 strain, and shake-flask cultivation was conducted to compare poly(4HB-co-PhLA) production among the resulting recombinant strains (Fig. 2a). In the ES1–ES3 systems, sucD and 4hbD genes were expressed under the strong constitutive trc promoter to enhance 4HB biosynthesis. However, the corresponding strains produced polymers with low 4HB molar fractions (0.5, 12.4, and 14.1 mol%, respectively) and low polymer titers (0.07, 0.06, and 0.02 g l−1, respectively). This tendency has also been reported previously14, presumably due to the cytotoxicity of 4HB and its intermediate, succinate semialdehyde22.

In contrast, when sucD and 4hbD were expressed under the relatively weak constitutive PphaC1 promoter derived from the Cupriavidus necator phaC1 operon (ES4 system), polymer production increased to 0.14 g l−1 with a 4HB fraction of 18.8 mol%. In the ES5 system, the origin of replication of the plasmid overexpressing sucD and 4hbD under PphaC1 was changed from ColE1 to pBBR1 to reduce plasmid copy number, yielding plasmid pBBR1_phas4D. The 4P0 strain harboring ES5 system achieved a higher polymer titer (0.17 g l−1) and an increased 4HB fraction (20.5 mol%) compared with ES4 system.

In the ES6 system, all four heterologous genes were co-expressed on a single plasmid under the PphaC1 promoter to reduce plasmid maintenance burden. However, the corresponding strain exhibited decreased polymer titer (0.11 g l−1) and 4HB fraction (18.6 mol%) relative to ES5, likely due to impaired cell growth and expression instability arising from simultaneous expression of multiple genes from a high-copy-number plasmid. Considering both polymer titer and 4HB molar fraction, ES5 was selected as the optimal expression system. Nevertheless, the 4HB fraction remained below the target range, prompting further pathway optimization.

To further increase the 4HB molar fraction, residual concentrations of 4HB and PhLA in the culture medium were quantified to assess monomer secretion prior to polymerization. In cultures of the 4P0 strain harboring ES5 system, 0.04 g l−1 of residual 4HB was detected, whereas PhLA was not observed, indicating that the catalytic activity of HadA was insufficient to convert all intracellular 4HB to 4HB-CoA before secretion (Fig. 2b). Based on Na4HB-supplemented cultures that achieved up to 36.7 mol% 4HB incorporation, we hypothesized that expanding the intracellular 4HB-CoA pool would enhance substrate availability for PhaC1437TM and increase 4HB incorporation into the copolymer.

Accordingly, four candidate CoA transferases previously reported to exhibit activity toward 4HB were evaluated by introducing each gene into pBBR1_phas4D of the ES5 system (Fig. 2b). These candidates included Pctcp540, an engineered Clostridium propionicum Pct variant23,24, OrfZ from C. kluyveri21, Pctcn from C. necator, and AbfT from Clostridium aminobutyricum25. Expression systems incorporating these CoA transferase candidates in the ES5 system were designated as ES7-1 to ES7-4 (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Table 1).

Shake-flask cultivation of strains harboring ES7-1 and ES7-2 yielded polymers containing 32.8 mol% and 31.0 mol% of 4HB, respectively, higher than the ES5 control (20.2 mol%). However, both strains accumulated γ-butyrolactone (GBL), a byproduct likely formed via spontaneous lactonization of 4HB-CoA (Supplementary Fig. 3), a phenomenon previously reported in CoA-thioester-related pathways26,27. Because this diversion could reduce effective 4HB-CoA availability, ES7-2 was selected over ES7-1 due to lower GBL accumulation and higher polymer titer. In fed-batch fermentation, the 4P0 strain harboring ES7-2 system produced 7.2 g l−1 of poly(4HB-co-PhLA) containing 27.8 mol% 4HB (Fig. 2c), although GBL accumulation remained substantial (3.7 g l−1).

To mitigate byproduct formation, a flux-control strategy was implemented to temporally regulate 4HB-CoA biosynthesis and reduce spontaneous lactonization. Previous studies have shown that both precursor toxicity and timing of PHA biosynthetic gene expression influence polymer accumulation28,29. Because polymer accumulation increased during the stationary phase (~60 h), stationary-phase promoters (SPPs) from E. coli, including promoters of bolA, ficA, dps, treA, sodC, yliH, and gadB, were selected29,30 and used to replace PphaC1 in pBBR1_phas4D_orfZ. The resulting expression systems (ES8-1 to ES8-7) were co-introduced with pPs619C1437TM_HadA into the 4P0 strain (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Table 1). Among these, the strain harboring ES8-2 exhibited the highest polymer titer among the tested systems in shake-flask culture, producing 0.27 g l−1 polymer with a 4HB fraction of 32.6 mol% and minimal GBL accumulation (0.007 g l−1) (Fig. 2d). In fed-batch fermentation, the 4P0 strain harboring ES8-2 exhibited increased 4HB incorporation during the stationary phase, reduced GBL accumulation (0.5 g l−1), and produced 8.6 g l−1 of poly(26.5 mol% 4HB-co-PhLA) (Fig. 2e).

Strain engineering for the enhanced production of copolymer

To enhance copolymer production, expression of endogenous genes previously reported to promote the biosynthesis of either 4HB or PhLA was modulated to redirect carbon flux toward the respective monomer precursors. For 4HB biosynthesis, the yqhD gene, encoding an NADPH-dependent 4-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase, was selected for overexpression to increase flux toward 4HB31, while the sucCD genes, encoding succinyl-CoA synthetase involved in succinate formation, were selected for downregulation31. For PhLA biosynthesis, aroGfbr (encoding a feedback-resistant mutant of E. coli 3-deoxy-D-arabinoheptulosonate-7-phosphate synthase) and pheAfbr (encoding a feedback-resistant E. coli chorismate mutase/prephenate dehydratase) were chosen for overexpression to strengthen the shikimate pathway17,32. In addition, the tktA gene, encoding transketolase, was selected for overexpression to increase erythrose-4-phosphate (E4P) availability and support cofactor regeneration32 (Fig. 1a).

The selected overexpression target genes were individually cloned under the trc promoter, whereas sucCD was downregulated using a small regulatory RNA (sRNA) expression cassette33 (Supplementary Data 1 and Supplementary Method 1). Each constructed plasmid was introduced into the 4P0 strain harboring the ES8-2 system, and the resulting strains were evaluated for polymer production.

In shake flask cultivation, overexpression of tktA increased the polymer titer to 0.22 g l−1 compared with the ES8-2 control strain (0.20 g l−1), while maintaining the 4HB fraction within the target range (Supplementary Fig. 4). Consequently, the promoter of the chromosomal tktA gene was replaced with the trc promoter to enable stable overexpression without additional plasmid burden, generating the 4P1 strain. In contrast, yqhD overexpression and sucCD downregulation did not result in significant improvements in polymer titer. Overexpression of aroGfbr-pheAfbr increased polymer production (0.20 g l−1) but reduced the 4HB molar fraction below the target range (13.5 mol%). These results indicate that further improvement of copolymer production while maintaining the desired monomer composition requires a more systematic and balanced optimization of flux toward both monomer biosynthesis pathways.

Accordingly, a computational strategy was implemented to systematically enhance poly(4HB-co-PhLA) production. While in silico identification of engineering targets for single-product biosynthesis has been widely explored, predicting targets for copolymer production remains challenging because it requires simultaneous balancing of fluxes toward distinct metabolic nodes (in this case, 4HB and PhLA). This complexity is further compounded because many key intermediates are essential for cellular growth and primary metabolism, complicating the identification of effective intervention points. Consequently, previous computational studies aiming to improve co-production often restricted their analyses to a single monomer16,34.

To overcome this limitation, the copolymerization reaction was explicitly incorporated into a GEM, and iBridge35 was applied to systematically identify gene targets that enhance flux toward both monomers simultaneously. The copolymer biosynthetic pathway was reconstructed in EC_iCH36036, an E. coli GEM integrating proteomic constraints, by adding reactions associated with heterologous gene expression (Supplementary Note 3). A composite objective function was defined as a weighted average of 4HB and PhLA fluxes, with weights corresponding to the desired copolymer composition. Based on experimental results, the target molar ratio of 4HB to PhLA was set to 3:7. Using this expanded model, iBridge was employed to predict genetic interventions that could enhance flux toward both monomers and thereby improve copolymer production.

A total of six downregulation target genes (serA, alaA, alaC, icd, accA, and accC) and two upregulation target genes (pckA and ilvE) were identified based on normalized contribution scores35 (Supplementary Table 2). To experimentally evaluate these candidates, plasmids encoding sRNA cassettes33 targeting each downregulation gene and plasmids expressing the upregulation targets under the trc promoter were constructed (Supplementary Data 1 and Supplementary Method 1).

Each downregulation target was first evaluated individually by introducing the corresponding plasmid into the 4P1 strain harboring ES8-2, followed by shake-flask cultivation. Among these strains, downregulation of icd, alaA, or accC increased polymer titers to 0.26, 0.24, and 0.24 g l−1, respectively, compared with the control strain (0.21 g l−1), while maintaining the desired 4HB molar fractions (27.3, 25.3, and 25.6 mol%, respectively) (Fig. 3a). To reduce plasmid burden, the native promoters of icd, alaA, and accC were replaced with the weaker Anderson synthetic promoter BBa_J23117 (https://parts.igem.org). However, promoter replacement of icd or accC caused unexpected growth retardation, whereas downregulation of alaA did not adversely affect growth. The strain with chromosomally downregulated alaA was therefore designated 4P2 and used for subsequent engineering.

Next, the two upregulation targets were evaluated by introducing the corresponding plasmids into the 4P2 strain harboring ES8-2. Overexpression of pckA increased the polymer titer to 0.26 g l−1 compared with the control (0.23 g l−1), while maintaining the 4HB fraction within the desired range (33.1 mol%) (Fig. 3b). Accordingly, the native promoter of pckA was replaced with the trc promoter, generating the 4P3 strain. When the 4P3 strain harboring ES8-2 was subjected to fed-batch fermentation, it produced 10.2 g l−1 of poly(4HB-co-PhLA) containing 32.7 mol% 4HB (Fig. 3c), demonstrating the effectiveness of the computational framework integrating copolymer biosynthesis into the GEM and applying iBridge for rational strain design.

Production of poly(3HB-co-4HB-co-PhLA)

Building on the production of poly(4HB-co-PhLA), we next explored one-step biosynthesis of poly(3HB-co-4HB-co-PhLA) to further expand the polymer property portfolio. To enable incorporation of 3HB units, a metabolic pathway was constructed by introducing two additional heterologous enzymes from C. necator, acetoacetyl-CoA synthase (PhaA) and acetoacetyl-CoA reductase (PhaB), which convert acetyl-CoA to 3-hydroxybutyryl-CoA (3HB-CoA) via acetoacetyl-CoA (Fig. 1a). Previous studies have demonstrated that 3HB-CoA can be copolymerized with PhLA-CoA by PhaC1437TM to form poly(3HB-co-PhLA)17.

To enable terpolymer biosynthesis, the 4P3 strain was transformed with plasmids pPs619C1437TM-HadA-PhaAB and pBBR1_ficAs4D_orfZ. In shake-flask cultivation, the resulting strain produced 1.05 g l−1 of poly(3HB-co-1.9 mol% 4HB-co-6.3 mol% PhLA). Under fed-batch fermentation, 52.8 g l−1 of poly(3HB-co-2.7 mol% 4HB-co-5.6 mol% PhLA) was produced, representing the highest production titer of an aromatic terpolymer (Fig. 3d). The chemical structure of the terpolymer was confirmed by NMR spectroscopy, in which distinct 1H and 13C resonances corresponding to 3HB, 4HB, and PhLA units were observed14,17 (Supplementary Fig. 5, Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Data 4).

To further increase the molar fractions of 4HB and PhLA in the terpolymer, the expression level of PhaAB was modulated by cloning the phaA and phaB genes into the lower-copy-number plasmid, generating plasmid pBBR1_s4D_phaAB. This plasmid was introduced into the 4P3 strain together with plasmid pPs619C1437TM-HadA-PhaAB. In shake-flask cultivation, the resulting strain produced 0.41 g l−1 of poly(3HB-co-4.4 mol% 4HB-co-22.1 mol% PhLA).

Thermal properties and molecular weight of the produced aromatic PHAs

The produced polymers with varying molar fractions of 3HB, 4HB, and PhLA were extracted and subjected to comprehensive thermal characterization. Using the strains developed in this study, poly(3HB-co-2.7 mol% 4HB-co-5.6 mol% PhLA) [34P−1], poly(3HB-co-1.9 mol% 4HB-co-6.3 mol% PhLA) [34P-2], poly(3HB-co-4.4 mol% 4HB-co-22.1 mol% PhLA) [34P-3], poly(32.7 mol% 4HB-co-PhLA) [4P−1], and poly(24.4 mol% 4HB-co-PhLA) [4P-2] were prepared. For comparison, P(3HB) and poly(3HB-co-6.5 mol% PhLA) [3P] were also prepared by culturing E. coli XL1-Blue harboring plasmid pPhaC1437-phaAB and E. coli TLF17BPAL harboring plasmid pPs619C1437TM-HadA-phaAB, respectively. The produced polymers were analyzed for their thermal properties and molecular weight distributions.

DSC was performed to characterize the thermal behavior of the polymers (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Fig. 6 and Supplementary Table 3). In contrast to P(3HB), a highly crystalline polymer, the 4HB–PhLA copolymers (4P-1 and 4P-2) exhibited amorphous behavior, while the 3HB–4HB–PhLA terpolymers (34P-1, 34P-2, and 34P-3) showed progressively reduced crystallinity as the fractions of 4HB and PhLA increased. Copolymerization of 4HB and PhLA disrupts the regular chain packing of 3HB, resulting in reduced crystallinity and enlarged amorphous regions37,38. To further examine the crystalline structure and crystallinity of the produced polymers, X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was additionally performed (Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Tables 4 and 5). As the fractions of 4HB and PhLA increased, the amorphous area increased while the degree of crystallinity decreased, further supporting the DSC results. The presence of amorphous domains is advantageous for adhesive applications, as increased chain mobility enhances interfacial wetting and adhesion while preserving dimensional stability20. Consistent with this interpretation, isotactic P(3HB), which exhibits high crystallinity, has been reported to show negligible adhesion11.

Regarding glass-transition behavior, the Tg generally increased with higher PhLA content. 34P-1 and, 34P-2 exhibited Tg values below 5 °C, whereas 4P-1 and 4P-2 showed Tg values of 10.0 °C and 11.3 °C, respectively (Supplementary Table 3). These values are comparable to or lower than those reported for other HMAs containing aromatic-monomers39–41. Because Tg should be lower than the application temperature, these results indicate suitability of produced polymers for room-temperature HMAs applications, although flexibility under low-temperature conditions may be limited (Supplementary Note 4).

In terms of melting behavior, P(3HB), 3P, 34P-1, and 34P-2 exhibited two melting temperatures (Tm1 and Tm2), attributed to melting–recrystallization–remelting behavior commonly observed in PHAs42, along with the presence of a cold-crystallization temperature (Tcc), indicating residual crystalline structures. As the molar fractions of 4HB and PhLA increased, Tm1 of the terpolymers decreased from 160.9 °C to 118.4 °C, while Tm2 decreased from 171.2 °C to 129.6 °C. Correspondingly, the enthalpy of fusion (ΔHf) decreased from 115.7 J g−1 to 4.2 J g−1 (Supplementary Table 3). Such reductions in Tm1, Tm2, and ΔHf are advantageous for industrial polymer processing, as they facilitate melt processing at lower temperatures43.

Thermal degradation behavior was evaluated by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Fig. 8, and Supplementary Table 3). Decomposition temperatures corresponding to 5%, 30%, 50%, and 95% weight loss (Td5, Td30, Td50, and Td95), as well as the thermal heat-resistance index (THRI)44, were determined. While the Td5 (onset decomposition temperature44) values of 3HB-containing polymers (P(3HB), 3P, 34P-1, 34P-2, and 34P-3) were ranged from 274 to 284 °C, 4P-1 and 4P-2 began decomposing above 300 °C, and their Td50 values were approximately 40–50 °C higher than those of the 3HB-containing polymers. These degradation temperatures are higher than the reported processing and application temperatures for representative polyurethane-based hot-melt adhesive systems, including thermoplastic polyurethane HMAs operated at approximately 120 °C39 and polyurethane reactive HMAs, operated at 160–170 °C45. In addition, the THRI values [4P-1 (158.8 °C) > 4P-2 (157.5 °C) > 34P-3 (142.7 °C) > 34P-1 (140.6 °C) > 34P-2 (139.3 °C) > 3P (138.9 °C) > P(3HB) (137.8 °C)] further indicate that the aromatic PHA-based HMAs developed in this study exhibit enhanced thermal stability by suppressing thermal decomposition (Supplementary Table 3). This enhancement, reflected in an increased Td50, offers several practical advantages for both processing and recycling. First, it provides a broader processing window. Conventional PHAs such as PHB typically suffer from a narrow processing window because their melting temperature (Tm) is close to their degradation temperature (Td, ~200 °C)15. In contrast, the improved thermal stability of aromatic PHAs expands this window, thereby reducing the risk of polymer degradation during melt processing. Second, this feature is also beneficial for recyclability. A higher Td helps maintain molecular weight and mechanical integrity during repeated melting and solidification cycles, enabling more stable material performance throughout multiple recycling steps. Together, the DSC and TGA results indicate that incorporation of 4HB and PhLA lowers the melting temperature, while PhLA enhances thermal degradation resistance, thereby widening the thermal processing window of the polymers.

The molecular-weight distributions were analyzed by gel permeation chromatography (GPC) (Supplementary Table 6). The number-average molecular weights (Mn) of the 4P-1 and 4P-2 were 4,237 Da and 4,331 Da, respectively, with corresponding polydispersity indices (PDI) of 1.5 and 1.5. These values are comparable to those of poly(PhLA) (4481 Da)17, indicating that incorporation of 4HB did not significantly affect molecular weight relative to poly(PhLA). For the 3HB–4HB–PhLA terpolymers (34P-1, 34P-2, and 34P-3), molecular weight decreased with increasing 4HB and PhLA content. Specifically, 34P-1 exhibited the highest Mn (39,490 Da) with a PDI of 2.6, whereas 34P-2 and 34P-3 showed Mn values of 21,467 Da (PDI = 1.9) and 13,518 Da (PDI = 2.5), respectively, indicating an inverse relationship between molecular weight and the combined molar fractions of 4HB and PhLA.

Rheological properties and adhesion performance of produced aromatic PHAs

Among the aromatic PHAs produced, 4P-1 and 34P-1, which exhibited the highest production titers in fed-batch fermentation, were selected as representative samples for further rheological and mechanical analyses. To characterize the thermal processing window and viscoelastic transitions relevant to HMAs applications, dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA) was performed. In oscillatory temperature-sweep mode, both 4P-1 and 34P-1 exhibited crossover points between the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) at around 149 °C (Supplementary Fig. 9a and 9b), corresponding to a transition from elastic (solid-like) to viscous (melt-like) behavior. The melting temperatures determined by DMA were higher than those obtained by DSC, consistent with previous reports41. The higher G′ of 34P-1 relative to 4P-1 over the temperature range of 120–160 °C indicates that 34P-1 behaves as a stiffer and less flowable material than 4P-1. Then, to examine the melt flow in the molten state, frequency-sweep tests were conducted at 160 °C and the complex viscosity (η*) was measured. The η* decreased in the order P(3HB) > 3 P > 34P-1 > 4P-1, indicating that increasing the molar fractions of 4HB and PhLA is associated with a reduction in η* (Supplementary Fig. 9c). This reduction implies improved melt flow and spreadability, which govern wetting and interfacial coverage of adhesive during bonding (Supplementary Fig. 10)20.

The adhesive performance of 4P-1 and 34P-1 was compared with several commercial adhesives using lap-shear adhesion tests. The references included ethylene–vinyl acetate (EVA) copolymers (representative HMAs), silicone adhesives, styrene–butadiene rubber (SBR)/rosin-based adhesives, and multipurpose glues. Detailed sample preparation procedures are described in the Methods section. On stainless steel substrates, 4P-1 exhibited an adhesion strength of 4.58 MPa, which was 9% higher than that of EVA (4.20 MPa) and 25% higher than silicone adhesives (3.65 MPa). These results indicate that 4P-1 shows comparable initial lap-shear strength to these commercial adhesives under the tested ambient conditions. In contrast, 34P-1 showed a lower adhesion strength of 1.32 MPa, yet remained higher than that of multipurpose glues, suggesting applications requiring moderate bonding strength (Fig. 4c).

To investigate substrate-dependent adhesion behavior, adhesion strength was measured on stainless steel, aluminum, glass, and wood substrates (Fig. 4d). 4P-1 exhibited the highest adhesion strength on stainless steel, followed by aluminum, and outperformed both EVA and 34P-1 on these metallic substrates. Interestingly, a different trend was observed for wood. While 4P-1 showed reduced adhesion strength on wood (0.12 MPa), 34P-1 exhibited stronger adhesion on wood (1.76 MPa) than on stainless steel substrate (1.32 MPa), reaching a strength comparable to EVA (1.88 MPa). Failure modes of the two polymers were analyzed on each substrate to identify the locus of failure and adhesion behavior, thereby suggesting substrate-dependent adhesion mechanisms (Supplementary Fig. 11). On stainless steel substrate, 4P-1 exhibited mixed failure, comprising both adhesive and cohesive failure (Supplementary Fig. 12a), indicating strong interfacial bonding46. In contrast, 34P-1 showed predominantly adhesive failure (Supplementary Fig. 12b), reflecting weaker interfacial interactions and limited wetting. On wood substrates, 4P-1 showed evidence of penetration into the wood surface (Supplementary Fig. 12c), likely due to its high wettability, whereas 34P-1 exhibited cohesive failure (Supplementary Fig. 12), indicating that failure occurred within the adhesive layer rather than at the interface. Furthermore, when wood joints bonded with 34P-1 were subjected to tensile loading perpendicular to the bonding interface, substrate failure was observed (Supplementary Fig. 12e), demonstrating strong interfacial adhesion. On glass substrates, all adhesives caused substrate failure due to the intrinsically low mechanical strength of glass, indicating that the adhesive joints maintained sufficient adhesion up to the point of fracture (Fig. 4d).

These substrate-dependent adhesion behaviors likely arise from the combined effects of adhesive properties and the chemical composition and morphology of the substrate surfaces. For metallic substrates, the strong adhesion of 4P-1 may be attributed to interactions between the polymer and surface oxides or hydroxyl groups, which facilitate hydrogen bonding and van der Waals interactions41 with ester functionalities and the polar 4HB moiety (Supplementary Fig. 13a). In addition, aromatic rings in PhLA may participate in π–cation interactions with metal surfaces47,48, while π–π stacking among aromatic moieties enhances cohesive strength within the polymer matrix44 (Supplementary Fig. 13b). The relatively low η* and molecular weight of poly(4HB-co-PhLA) could further promote wetting and interfacial spreading, leading to strong adhesion on metals. In contrast, 34P-1 has a higher η* and contains a larger fraction of less polar 3HB units (Supplementary Fig. 13a), which may limit spreading and weaken interfacial interactions, resulting in adhesive failure and lower adhesion strength on metals. This shows that although viscosity is an important factor for evaluating adhesion characteristics, other factors such as crystallinity and composition should be considered collectively.

Wood substrates, unlike metals, consist primarily of lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose, forming a rough and porous structure rich in polar functional groups such as hydroxyl, carboxyl, and ester groups49. This architecture enables mechanical interlocking and facilitates secondary interactions, including hydrogen bonding and dipole–dipole interactions49,50. Owing to its semi-crystalline nature, higher η*, and greater hydrophobicity, 34P-1 may achieve an optimal balance between penetration and mechanical interlocking with the wood surface while maintaining strong interfacial interactions with polar groups. The presence of 3HB units contributes to crystalline domain formation, enhancing cohesive strength within the polymer network. Consequently, 34P-1 exhibited cohesive failure under shear loading and substrate failure under tensile loading on wood, indicating the formation of a strong and continuous interfacial bond (Supplementary Fig. 13c). In contrast, the relatively low η* and molecular weight of 4P-1 may have led to excessive penetration into the porous wood structure, depleting the interfacial adhesive layer and reducing apparent adhesion strength50. The force–displacement curves obtained from the lap-shear tests were further analyzed to estimate the work of adhesion and distance of failure (Supplementary Fig. 14). Compared with EVA, 4P-1 and 34P-1 exhibited lower work of adhesion and shorter failure distances, indicating lower energy dissipation and more limited deformation during shear-induced failure.

Recyclability was evaluated by measuring the adhesion strength of 4P-1 after repeated thermal reprocessing on stainless steel substrates. Adhesion strength was retained at approximately 97.4%, 71.4%, 67.2%, and 62.7% of the initial value after the second, third, fourth, and fifth reprocessing cycles, respectively, demonstrating the reusability of 4P-1 as a sustainable adhesive (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 15).

Finally, practical validation of the aromatic PHAs as HMAs was performed. Stainless steel substrates bonded with 4P-1 and wood substrates bonded with 34P-1 were able to support loads of 14 kg and 10 kg, respectively, without failure (Fig. 4f–h and Supplementary Movie 1). Applicability in conventional HMA formats was further evaluated using a glue gun, the most common application method for commercial HMAs. Owing to its suitable crystallinity and crystallization rate, 34P-1 could be molded into a 10-mm-diameter stick resembling a typical EVA glue stick and extruded through a glue gun (Supplementary Fig. 12f,g). In contrast, molding and extrusion of 4P-1 could not be achieved due to its low viscosity and limited crystallinity. Further optimization of Molecular weight and crystallization behavior may enable glue gun format processing of 4P-1.

Peeling and open-time tests were further conducted to evaluate practical handling and debonding behavior under different application conditions. In the PET film/stainless steel peeling configuration, EVA showed higher peel resistance than 4P-1 and 34P-1, suggesting that the aromatic PHAs have lower peel toughness under this thin-film geometry. However, when kraft paper was bonded with 34P-1, substrate failure was observed, similar to EVA, indicating that the adhesive joint was stronger than the paper substrate itself (Supplementary Fig. 16a, b). In the open-time test, both 4P-1 and 34P-1 exhibited markedly longer open times than EVA, with values of 580 s and 196.7 s, respectively, compared with 15 s for EVA, indicating an extended working window for bonding (Supplementary Fig. 16c). Taken together, these results suggest that 4P-1 may be suitable for metal bonding applications requiring high shear adhesion and extended positioning time, such as temporary fixation of stainless-steel components, metal sheet positioning, and detachable film attachment on metal surfaces. In contrast, 34P-1 may be more suitable for paper- or wood-based packaging applications, such as kraft paper bonding, cardboard sealing, paperboard assembly, and other lignocellulosic substrates, owing to its strong bonding to cellulosic materials and compatibility with glue-gun-processable HMA formats.

Biodegradation of produced aromatic PHAs

PHAs are generally regarded as biodegradable polyesters10, and their degradation can proceed through both bulk and surface erosion mechanisms51. Bulk erosion is associated with hydrolytic chain scission within the polymer matrix, resulting in molecular weight reduction, whereas surface erosion occurs through enzymatic or microbial cleavage at the polymer surface, leading to mass loss. To experimentally assess the degradability of the produced aromatic PHAs, we performed enzymatic degradation assays using Candida antarctica lipase B (CalB), an enzyme reported to hydrolyze both aliphatic and aromatic polyesters, including P(3HB) and poly(butylene adipate-co-terephthalate) [PBAT]51–53. After 96 h of lipase treatment, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis revealed surface morphological changes, including pore formation and increased surface roughness (Fig. 5a-d and Supplementary Fig. 17). Consistently, poly(4HB-co-PhLA) showed a 16.4% decrease in Mn and a 3.9% mass loss after 96 h, while poly(3HB-co-4HB-co-PhLA) exhibited a 17.5% decrease in Mn and a 10.4% mass loss under the same conditions (Fig. 5e-h). Although 4P−1 had an amorphous character and a lower molecular weight, its initial mass loss was lower than that of 34P−1, suggesting that the early-stage enzymatic degradation of aromatic PHAs is not governed solely by crystallinity or molecular weight. Instead, differences in aromatic monomer content38, hydrophilic/hydrophobic balance, and enzyme accessibility to hydrolysable ester bonds may collectively influence the degradation behavior51.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a bacterial platform for the bio-based production of aromatic PHA copolymers as sustainable HMA candidates using systems metabolic engineering. This system offers advantages over conventional petrochemical adhesives by enabling the bio-based production of biodegradable materials that address the growing demand for renewable and recyclable alternatives.

We confirmed that HadA and PhaC1437TM enable the biosynthesis of poly(4HB-co-PhLA) with tunable 4HB content, resulting in composition-dependent thermal and adhesion behaviors. E. coli strains were further engineered to produce aromatic PHA copolymers directly from glucose by introducing a 4HB biosynthetic pathway, and polymer production was systematically enhanced via optimization of heterologous gene expression systems, introduction of additional CoA transferases, and application of SPPs to regulate precursor supply and suppress by-product formation. By incorporating the copolymer biosynthetic pathway into a GEM and applying iBridge, we identified gene targets that simultaneously enhanced fluxes toward both 4HB and PhLA. These results highlight the importance of balancing the supply of multiple monomers and suggest that such multi-precursor flux control strategies can be broadly applicable to other biosynthesis systems. The final engineered strain produced 10.2 g l−1 of poly(4HB-co-PhLA) within the desired 4HB fraction range, and the introduction of PhaAB further enabled one-step biosynthesis of poly(3HB-co-4HB-co-PhLA) terpolymers, achieving production titers of up to 52.8 g l−1.

Comprehensive thermal, rheological, and adhesion analyses indicated that incorporation of 4HB and PhLA improved adhesion and widened the processing window, while PhLA incorporation enhanced thermal stability. The copolymer 4P-1 exhibited adhesion strength comparable to commercial adhesives on metal substrates, along with recyclability, whereas the terpolymer 34P-1 showed high adhesion strength and cohesive failure on wood substrates. In addition to these material properties, the early-stage enzymatic degradation behavior of the produced aromatic PHAs was experimentally assessed using a lipase-mediated degradation assay. SEM images after lipase treatment showed surface roughening, pore formation and localized erosion together with the observed decreases in Mn and mass. These results suggest that bio-based aromatic PHAs can be tuned to achieve a balance among thermal stability, processability, and adhesion performance for HMAs applications while providing experimental support for their susceptibility to enzymatic degradation.

Despite these advances, several challenges remain to be addressed. First, the molecular weight of the produced poly(4HB-co-PhLA) remains in the oligomeric range, which may lead to reduced cohesive strength, creep resistance, energy dissipation, and processability, particularly under elevated-temperature or sustained-load conditions. In fact, 34P-1 and 34P-2 possess similar molecular structures and PhLA compositions. However, 34P-1, which has approximately twofold higher Mn and Mw (Supplementary Table 6), exhibited higher thermal stability, as shown in Fig. 4b. This observation suggests that molecular weight contributes not only to thermal robustness but also potentially to the cohesive integrity required for demanding applications. Consistently, 4P-1 and 34P-1 showed lower work of adhesion and shorter failure displacement than those measured for commercial EVA in lap-shear tests, as well as lower peel resistance in the PET film/stainless steel peeling configuration. These results indicate lower energy dissipation and more limited resistance to deformation or peeling under the tested geometries. However, the substrate failure observed for kraft paper bonded with 34P-1 suggests that these limitations are substrate- and loading-mode-dependent. Therefore, increasing molecular weight by engineering PhaC variants with reduced chain-transfer activity and enhanced polymerization efficiency, together with further formulation optimization, will be important for improving toughness, peel resistance, creep resistance, and applicability under demanding conditions. Second, precise control over monomer compositions can broaden the applicability of the resulting polymers by tuning the mechanical and thermal properties of the copolymers. For instance, increasing the fraction of the flexible 4HB unit is expected to enhance chain mobility and adhesive properties, whereas incorporation of the aromatic PhLA unit may improve rigidity and thermal stability14,16,17. In this paper, monomer composition was regulated by employing different enzymes for the biosynthesis of specific monomers or by modifying gene expression systems (Fig. 2). Moreover, systems metabolic engineering methods such as the use of synthetic promoters (https://parts.igem.org), ribosome binding sites (RBS), and terminators with varying strengths54,55, and the implementation of genetic circuits for fine-tuned control of metabolic fluxes can further enable precise modulation of monomer composition56. Third, incorporation of additional candidate monomers, and control of sequence architecture including stereoregularity could, enable finer tuning of thermal and adhesive properties. Such control will be beneficial for the systematic development of HMAs. Finally, further refinement of both the metabolic pathway and production process will be necessary for large-scale implementation, safety, and overall environmental impact to ensure the responsible deployment of these bio-based HMA candidates.

In conclusion, we have developed a microbial platform for the bio-based production of aromatic PHAs with tunable thermal and adhesive properties. This platform provides a basis for the development of renewable and recyclable alternatives to petrochemical adhesives. Its versatility enables the extension of the biosynthetic framework to a wide range of functional monomers and engineered polymers, supporting the future development of materials for diverse industrial applications.

Methods

Bacterial strains and plasmids

Bacterial strains, plasmids and primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Data 1 and 2. E. coli XL1-Blue and its derivative strains were used as the base strain and engineered for PHA production throughout this study. Construction of all plasmids and Gibson assembly were carried out according to standard procedures57,58. Detailed procedures for plasmid construction and strain generation are described in Supplementary Methods 1 and 2. The primers were synthesized by Genotech (Daejeon, Republic of Korea). E. coli DH5α was used as a host strain for routine gene cloning and was cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium (10 g l−1 tryptone, 5 g l−1 yeast extract and 10 g l-1 NaCl) or on LB agar (LB supplemented with 1.5% (w/v) agar) plates at 37 °C with appropriate antibiotics when necessary: 25 μg/mL kanamycin (Km), 50 μg/mL ampicillin (Ap), and/or 17 μg/mL chloramphenicol (Cm). Cell growth was monitored by measuring the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) using an Ultrospec 3000 spectrophotometer (Amersham Biosciences, Uppsala, Sweden). Details of the reagents used in this study are listed in Supplementary Data 3.

Flask cultivation and fed-batch fermentation

For flask cultivations and polymer production cultures, MR medium which containing 6.67 g KH2PO4, 4 g (NH4)2HPO4, 0.8 g citric acid, 5 mL of trace metal solution (TS) (containing 10 g FeSO4·7H2O, 2 g CaCl2, 2.2 g ZnSO4·7H2O, 0.5 g MnSO4·5H2O, 1 g CuSO4·5H2O, 0.1 g (NH4)6Mo7O24·4H2O, and 0.02 g Na2B4O7·10H2O per liter of 0.5 M HCl) per liter was used and the pH was adjusted to 7.0 using NaOH before sterilization. Glucose, MgSO₄·7H₂O, and thiamine were sterilized separately and subsequently added to the MR medium to final concentrations of 20 g l−1, 0.8 g l−1, and 10 mg l−1, respectively. Seed cultures were grown overnight in a rotary shaker at 200 rpm and 30 °C. Thereafter, 1 mL of the seed culture was inoculated into 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 100 mL of prepared medium, supplemented with 50 μg ml−1 Ap, 25 μg ml−1 Km, and 17 μg ml−1 Cm as required. Na4HB was synthesized from GBL59.

The fed-batch fermentation was carried out in a 6.6 L Bioreactor (BioFlo 320, Eppendorf, Germany) containing 1.8 L of MR medium supplemented with 20 g l−1 of glucose, 10 mg l-1 of thiamine, and when required, 1.6 g l−1 of yeast extract. Seed cultures were grown overnight in a rotary incubator at 200 rpm and 30 °C until the OD600 reached 2–3, then inoculated into the bioreactor. Dissolved oxygen was maintained above 40% by air supplementation (2 vvm) and automatic impeller control (200–1000 rpm), with pure oxygen supplied when necessary. The culture pH was controlled at 7.00 by addition of 28% (v/v) NH₃ solution, and the temperature was maintained at 30 °C. The feeding solution, consisting of 700 g glucose, 15 g MgSO₄·7H₂O, 0.25 g thiamine, and 6 ml TS per litre, was automatically added when the pH rose above 7.02, indicating depletion of the carbon source.

For measurements of metabolite concentrations, including glucose, organic acids, PhLA, and GBL, please refer to Supplementary Method 3.

Polymer analysis

Polymer accumulation and monomer composition were analyzed by gas chromatography (GC). The cultures were harvested by centrifugation at 3580 × g and 4 °C. The resulting cell pellets were washed twice with distilled water and dried at 70 °C for 24 h. Intracellular polymers were converted to the corresponding hydroxymethyl esters via acid-catalyzed methanolysis60, followed by GC analysis with Agilent 6890N system (Agilent, CA, USA) equipped with an Agilent 7683 automatic injector, a flame ionization detector, and a fused silica capillary column (ATTM-Wax, 30 m × 0.53 mm, 1.20 μm film thickness; Alltech, KY, USA). The accumulation of the polymer was calculated by weight percentage of the polymer relative to the dry cell weight (wt%; %wt of polymer/wt of dry cell weight). For polymer purification, a solvent extraction method was applied61. The cell pellet was washed with distilled water, freeze-dried, and subjected to Soxhlet extraction with chloroform at 55 °C overnight. The extract was then filtered, concentrated, and precipitated with ice-cold methanol, followed by incubation at 4 °C. The recovered polymer was lyophilized and subsequently characterized by NMR analysis.

For gel permeation chromatography (GPC), and structural characterization performed by NMR spectroscopy, please refer to Supplementary Method 4.

Thermomechanical property analysis

The Tg value of polymers was determined by DSC using a DSC 204 F1 Phoenix instrument (Netzsch, Selb, Germany). Approximately 4–10 mg of the sample was sealed in a pierced aluminum pan and heated, cooled, and reheated at a rate of 10 °C min−1 from −30 to 220 °C under a nitrogen flow (20 ml min−1) to ensure an inert atmosphere. The Tg, Tm1, Tm2 and Tcc values were determined from the second heating scan. The P(3HB) obtained in this study was assumed to be 100% crystalline, and the degree of crystallinity was determined based on its ΔHf of 103.7 J g⁻¹. The degradation temperature (Td) of polymers was measured through TGA using MaxRes TGA (TGA2, Mettler Toledo, OH, USA). Approximately 10 mg of DSC samples were heated from 30 °C to 600 °C at a rate of 10 °C min⁻¹ with N2 flow of 30.0 ml min−1. The thermal heat-resistance index (THRI) was calculated as follows: THRI = 0.49 × [Td5 + 0.6 × (Td30 – Td5)]44.

DMA was performed using a modular compact rheometer (MCR 302, Anton Paar Ltd, Austria) equipped with an 8-mm diameter parallel-plate geometry. Oscillatory temperature-sweep measurements were conducted from 100 to 160 °C within the linear viscoelastic strain range using a strain of 1% and a heating rate of 2 °C/min. For oscillatory frequency sweep measurements, the samples were preheated at 150 °C for 1 min, and subsequently compressed by a measuring plate. Oscillatory shear tests were performed at a constant temperature of 160 °C over an angular frequency range of 0.1–100 rad/s. Each measurement was conducted once to obtain the rheological properties.

Samples for lap-shear testing were prepared following a modified ASTM D1002 protocol. The metal substrates were cleaned with isopropyl alcohol prior to testing. Approximately 100 mg of polymer was placed between two substrates (thickness: ~2 mm, overlap area: ~25 × 10 mm) in a single lap-shear configuration and heated to melt at 175 °C. Upon cooling to room temperature, the adhesive solidified and bonded to the substrates. The specimens were pressed with a 2 kg roller, clipped with a binder clip, and then cooled at room temperature for 24 h prior to testing. The samples were affixed into the screw-tight grip frame of a universal testing machine (UTM) (Instron 5583, Instron, USA) with a test speed of 5.0 mm min−1 at ambient conditions. Adhesive strength (MPa) was calculated by dividing the maximum force (N) measured by the instrument by the surface area (mm2) of the applied adhesive. The reprocessing tests followed similar procedures to the preparation of lap-shear specimens with slight modifications. After each lap-shear test, the polymer-applied area of the failed substrates was reheated to melt for complete separation of the substrates, the adhesive remaining on the substrate surfaces was remelted and reused to reassemble the lap joint. The reassembled substrates were then joined, clipped with binder clips, and the subsequent lap-shear tests followed the same procedure as described above. Melted EVA from a hot glue gun was applied to a substrate, overlaid with another substrate, and fixed with binder clips. The same lap-shear geometry and conditioning period were used for other commercial adhesives at room temperature. For XRD, peeling test, open-time test and biodegradation, please refer to Supplementary Method 5.

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Sample sizes were chosen based on common practice in microbial cultivation, polymer characterization and material testing. No data were excluded from the analyses. The experiments were not randomized. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment. Most experiments were performed using n = 3 biologically independent cultures or independently prepared samples, except fed-batch fermentations, which were performed in duplicate. Samples were allocated to experimental groups according to the planned experimental conditions, including strain, plasmid system, polymer type, substrate type or assay condition. Experimental conditions were kept consistent within each assay. Statistical analyses were performed using Student’s t-test as indicated in the figure legends. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.005, ***P < 0.0005, determined by a Student’s t test.
