A chromosome-scale genome assembly of the aromatic ornamental Coleus hadiensis reveals terpene synthase clusters and a Lamiaceae-conserved floral repressor candidate
Authors: Yoshikazu Furuta, Hiroki Sugimoto, Ritsuko Kitagawa-Yogo, Hinano Mizuno, Akari Nakasone, Hidenori Tanaka, Nobuhiko Muramoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAromatic ornamentals are increasingly valued for indoor use because plant-associated volatiles can shape sensory experiences and have been discussed in relation to comfort and well-being, thereby driving demand for compact species with fragrance and controllable flowering. Coleus hadiensis (syn. Plectranthus hadiensis) is considered a promising indoor aromatic ornamental because of its fragrance and compact growth habit. However, genomic resources for Coleus remain limited, and the gene families associated with terpenoid biosynthesis and flowering regulation have not been systematically characterized.ResultsHere, we present a chromosome-level reference genome of C. hadiensis generated using long-read sequencing together with Hi-C scaffolding and provide genome-wide annotation of repeats, terpene-related gene families, and flowering-associated regulators. The assembled 1.83 Gbp genome exhibited a highly repeat-rich genomic architecture dominated by Copia-type long terminal repeat retrotransposons with insertion-time estimates peaking approximately 1–1.5 million years ago. We identified 128 terpene synthase coding sequences distributed among multiple subfamilies, several of which formed tandem clusters. A genome-wide survey of phosphatidylethanolamine-binding protein genes further identified candidates associated with flowering regulation, including an FT-like regulator that exhibited repressor activity in a heterologous system.ConclusionsOur analyses of the chromosome-scale genome of C. hadiensis provide new insights into genomic features associated with fragrance and flowering traits and establish a foundation for understanding fragrance- and flowering-related characteristics and for advancing genomics-assisted breeding in C. hadiensis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09605-4.

Background

A growing body of research has examined how the visual and olfactory properties of indoor plants influence the quality of built environments, including perceived comfort, stress reduction, and broader aspects of human well-being [1–4]. Accordingly, interest in the fragrance of aromatic ornamentals as a functional horticultural trait for indoor use has increased in recent years. Although fragrance-associated traits have been investigated mainly in the context of essential oil production and utilization [5], they are also relevant to indoor environments because plant-emitted volatiles can influence sensory experiences and have been explored for their effects on stress-related responses in humans [6, 7]. Collectively, these observations emphasize the importance of identifying compact aromatic ornamental species suitable for indoor environments and breeding improved cultivars optimized for indoor use.

Coleus hadiensis, also known as Plectranthus hadiensis, is a Lamiaceae species widely cultivated as an aromatic ornamental (Fig. 1A). Its extracts contain terpenoids and flavonoids that reportedly exhibit antibacterial and antioxidant activities [8, 9]. The leaves possess subcuticular oil reservoirs at the apices of glandular trichomes, from which volatile compounds are released when the surface is rubbed [10]. Together with its compact growth habit, these characteristics are consistent with increasing demand for plants that contribute to indoor environments while remaining easy to maintain. C. hadiensis therefore represents a practical target for breeding aimed at optimizing fragrance intensity and composition together with flowering behavior for use in living and working spaces.

Despite their potential as indoor ornamentals, species of Coleus and its close relative Plectranthus have been studied mainly for ethnobotanical applications, and the genetic basis of their phenotypic traits remains insufficiently characterized [11]. Several previous studies using transcriptomic datasets and short-read-based assemblies identified genes in C. hadiensis and Coleus amboinicus (syn. Plectranthus amboinicus) and biosynthetic gene clusters in Coleus barbatus (syn. Plectranthus barbatus) associated with terpenoid production [12–14]. Among flowering-associated regulators, key genes such as FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT) and TERMINAL FLOWER 1 (TFL1) have undergone lineage-specific expansion within Lamiales [15]. However, flowering-related regulators in Coleus and Plectranthus have not yet been systematically characterized. Because terpenoid production varies across developmental stages, integrating knowledge of flowering-associated development with fragrance-related traits is important for breeding [16, 17].

In this context, high-quality reference genomes provide an important basis for the systematic identification and functional analysis of genes underlying these traits and offer substantial advantages relative to transcriptomes or short-read-based assemblies. However, genomic resources for Coleus and its close relatives remain scarce. For C. hadiensis, the chloroplast sequence currently represents the longest publicly available sequence [18]. To the best of our knowledge, no genome sequence that assembled in chromosome-scale has yet been reported for the genus Coleus, whereas the available sequence of C. barbatus remains fragmented [14]. Even within the same tribe, Ocimeae [19], whole-genome resources are concentrated in only a limited number of genera, including Ocimum and Lavandula (the genera of basil and lavender, respectively) [17, 20]. Filling this gap in genomic resources is important for breeding and for comparative analyses of the evolution of fragrance repertoires and genome structure among closely related species.

Here, we present a chromosome-level reference genome of C. hadiensis generated using long-read sequencing and Hi-C scaffolding together with genome-wide annotation of repeats, terpene synthase (TPS) genes, and flowering-associated regulators. These analyses establish a foundation for understanding the genomic basis of key ornamental traits and for advancing genomics-assisted breeding in C. hadiensis.

Methods

Plant material collection and short-read sequencing

Fresh young leaves were collected from C. hadiensis plants purchased from Charm (Gunma, Japan) and maintained at Toyota Central R&D Labs., Inc. (Aichi, Japan) as the “TCRDL” line. Genomic DNA was isolated from 100 mg of young leaves using the DNeasy Plant Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). The extracted DNA was sent to Seibutsu-Giken (Kanagawa, Japan) for sequencing on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Genome size was estimated through k-mer analysis using GenomeScope v2.0.1 [21]. Reads were assembled using Abyss 2.3.5 [22], and the assembled sequences were searched for rbcL and matK loci using Blastn [23]. The detected C. hadiensis rbcL and matK sequences were subsequently analyzed against the nt database using Blastn [23], and a maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed with MEGA 11 [24].

Cytogenetic analysis

Roots of C. hadiensis grown hydroponically in 1 g/L Hyponex Powder solution (Hyponex Japan, Osaka, Japan) were used for cytogenetic analysis. Approximately 40 root segments, each approximately 5 mm from the root tip, were collected and incubated overnight in deionized water at 4 °C. Samples were subsequently transferred to fixative solution (ethanol: acetic acid = 3:1, v/v) and fixed at room temperature for at least 8 h. Fixed root samples were stored at − 20 °C until use. Staining of root-cell chromosomes with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) and fluorescence image acquisition were performed by Chromosome Science Labo Inc. (Sapporo, Japan) using a Leica CW4000 Cytogenetic Workstation (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).

Sequencing and de novo assembly of long reads and Hi-C library

Genomic DNA of C. hadiensis was isolated from 1.0 g of young leaves using Genomic-tip 20G (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). For HiFi sequencing, libraries were prepared from 2 µg of genomic DNA using the SMRTbell prep kit 3.0 (PacBio, Menlo Park, CA) together with the Vega polymerase kit (PacBio, Menlo Park, CA), followed by sequencing on the Vega system for two runs. For additional long-read sequencing, 1 µg of the same genomic DNA was used to prepare a library with the Ligation Sequencing Kit V14 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK), and sequencing was conducted on PromethION 2 integrated (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK) using a PromethION Flow Cell R10.4.1 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK). For Hi-C library preparation, 0.5 g of young leaves was processed using the Proximo Hi-C (Plant) Kit (Phase Genomics, Seattle, WA), and sequencing was subsequently performed on the NextSeq 1000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA).

For genome assembly, HiFi reads were first filtered for read quality (Q30) using Seqkit v2.4.0 [25] and then assembled de novo using hifiasm 0.25.0-r726 [26], with AAACCCT designated as the telomere sequence through the --telo-m option. Before scaffolding, filtered reads were analyzed using PMAT v1.5.3 [27] to separate organellar genomes, producing mitochondrial and plastid master sequences of 313 and 152 kb, respectively. Contigs matching organellar sequences with 100% identity were identified and removed using Blastn [23]. The remaining contigs were scaffolded with Hi-C reads using Haphic 1.0.6 [28], with options specifying 15 chromosomes and the restriction sites “GATC, CTNAG, TTAA, GANTC” used during library preparation. The resulting assembly was visualized and manually curated using Juicebox 2.17.00 [29], and the remaining gaps were subsequently filled using additional PromethION reads together with LR_Gapcloser [30].

Genome annotation

For annotation of the assembled genome sequence, repetitive elements were first identified and masked. Miniature inverted-repeat transposable elements (MITEs) were detected using MITE-Hunter [31]. Long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons and additional repetitive elements were identified using RepeatModeler with the -LTRstruct option. The genome sequence was soft-masked using RepeatMasker, and the resulting soft-masked assembly was subsequently used for ab initio gene-model construction with Augustus [32]. Previously generated RNA-seq datasets of C. hadiensis [13] (Accession Nos. DRR635850–DRR635857) were incorporated as transcript-based hints. In parallel, protein sequences from Ocimum basilicum [20] were mapped to the unmasked genome using GeMoMa [33] to obtain high-quality coding-sequence predictions informed by a closely related species. The resulting annotation sets were combined using GffRead and GffCompare [34], yielding a total of 56,218 predicted genes. Functional annotation was performed using Hayai-Annotation v3.2 [35]. Annotated proteins were further assigned KEGG Orthologs (KOs) using KofamScan [36]. Genes involved in flavonoid biosynthesis and terpene precursor biosynthesis were identified based on KOs assigned to genes included in the KEGG Pathway maps map00941 (Flavonoid biosynthesis), map00944 (Flavone and flavonol biosynthesis), and map00900 (Terpenoid backbone biosynthesis).

Finally, CpG and CHG methylation profiles were extracted from mappings of PromethION 2 integrated reads against the assembled C. hadiensis genome using modkit (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK). Densities per 1-Mb window for coding-sequence coverage, retrotransposon coverage, and methylation were visualized using Circos v0.69-8 [37].

The assembly and gene annotation were evaluated using BUSCO 5.8.3 [38] with the eudicots_odb10 dataset. Gene annotation quality was further assessed by counting genes assigned functional annotations other than “Uncharacterized protein”.

Phylogenetic analysis

Protein sequences annotated from representative Lamiaceae species and outgroup species were downloaded and evaluated for completeness using BUSCO 5.8.3 [38], confirming complete-gene ratios above 90% for all analyzed genomes (Table S3). Protein sequences were filtered to remove isoforms using mmseqs2 16.747c6 [39] and subsequently analyzed with OrthoFinder v3.1.0 [40]. After pruning and concatenating the resulting gene trees, a species phylogeny was constructed from the concatenated alignment using iqtree 3.0.1 [41] and MCMCtree 4.10.9 [42]. Divergence times were calibrated using values obtained from TimeTree [43]: Oryza sativa versus Arabidopsis thaliana (142.1–163.5 Ma), A. thaliana versus Solanum lycopersicum (111.4–123.9 Ma), and S. lycopersicum versus Sesamum indicum (75.8–96.6 Ma). Gene-family expansion and contraction analyses were conducted using CAFE5 [44]. Synonymous substitution rates among C. hadiensis paralogs and between orthologs of C. hadiensis and closely related species were calculated using WGD v2.0.38 [45].

Retrotransposon analysis

For interspecific comparison of repetitive-element and LTR retrotransposon content, genome assemblies of Lamiaceae species classified as “Chromosome” or “Complete” were downloaded (Table S4) and analyzed using RepeatModeler and RepeatMasker as described above. Repetitive-element classifications were obtained using the buildSummary.pl script included with RepeatMasker. The same analyses were additionally performed for A. thaliana and Zea mays as controls.

Mutational distances between paired LTRs and filtering of intact LTR retrotransposons were determined using LTR_retriever v3.0.4 [46]. Insertion times were estimated with the Kimura 2-parameter model using a neutral mutation rate of 1.5 × 10− 8 substitutions per bp per year. For transcriptional analysis of intact LTR retrotransposons, RNA-seq reads [13] were mapped against the assembled C. hadiensis genome using STAR with the option “--outFilterMultimapNmax 1000” to permit multimapping, followed by estimation of read counts using Telescope v1.0.3 [47, 48].

TPS analysis

Coding sequences were classified as TPS genes when both TPS-associated domains, IPR001906 and IPR005630, were detected using InterProScan [49]. The same TPS classification pipeline was also applied to protein sequences from plant species included in a previous comparative analysis of TPS gene numbers [50]. The 128 detected sequences were searched against the ClusteredNR database using Blastp [23] to identify the closest homologs (Table S5). For phylogenetic analysis, sequences were aligned together with representative control sequences used for subfamily assignment, and a maximum-likelihood tree was constructed using MEGA 11 [24, 51]. Biosynthetic gene clusters were identified using plantiSMASH 2.0.4 [52].

Transcription of each TPS gene was analyzed using RNA-seq datasets from leaves (n = 3), stems (n = 2), and roots (n = 3) of C. hadiensis obtained in a previous report [13] (Accession Nos. DRR635850–DRR635857). RNA-seq reads were mapped to the C. hadiensis assembly using STAR, and transcript abundance was estimated as transcripts per million (TPM) using RSEM [47, 53]. Uniquely mapped read rates were 82%–90% in leaves, 88%–94% in stems, and 65%–70% in roots, whereas total unmapped read rates were 5%–10% in leaves, 4%–10% in stems, and 28%–33% in roots, respectively. TPM values showed reasonable reproducibility among biological replicates (Fig. S1).

Computational and experimental analysis of flowering genes

Coding sequences possessing the phosphatidylethanolamine-binding protein (PEBP) family domain (Pfam accession No. PF01161) were extracted as flowering-related genes. A maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed for the identified genes together with flowering-related genes from A. thaliana and P. frutescens using MEGA 11 [24]. Transcription of each gene was analyzed using the RNA-seq dataset from the previous report [13] together with STAR and RSEM [47, 53]. Homologs of ChFT genes were considered present when reciprocal top hits were identified by Blastp searches between the target species and C. hadiensis [23].

To construct a plasmid expressing the AtFT gene (At1G65480) under the control of the Cauliflower mosaic virus (CaMV) 35S promoter (pBI 35Spro: AtFT), AtFT cDNA containing additional cloning sequences at both termini was amplified using the primers XbaI-BamHI-F2 (5′-ttggagagaacacgggggactctagaggatccccgggtggtcagtc-3′) and pBI121-SacI-R1 (5′-ttgaacgatcggggaaattcgagctc-3′). The amplified fragment was cloned into the BamHI/SacI sites of pBI121 using the In-Fusion Dry-Down PCR Cloning Kit w/Cloning Enhancer (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan), thereby generating pBI 35Spro: AtFT.

To construct plasmids expressing each ChFT gene under the control of the CaMV 35S promoter (pBI 35Spro: ChFT1–5), DNA fragments encoding Arabidopsis codon-optimized versions of ChFT1 through ChFT5, including additional cloning sequences at both termini, were synthesized by ThermoFisher Scientific (Waltham, MA). These fragments were amplified using the primers XbaI-BamHI-F2 and pBI121-SacI-R1 and subsequently cloned into the BamHI/SacI sites of pBI121 using the In-Fusion Cloning System, generating pBI 35Spro: ChFT1, pBI 35Spro: ChFT2, pBI 35Spro: ChFT3, pBI 35Spro: ChFT4, and pBI 35Spro: ChFT5.

The resulting DNA constructs were transformed into wild-type A. thaliana Col-0 plants using the floral-dip method [54]. T1 plants were selected on Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium containing 30 µg/mL kanamycin.

For flowering assays, T2 seeds were sown aseptically on MS solid medium (0.5% [w/v] gellan gum) supplemented with 1% (w/v) sucrose. After stratification for 3 d at 4 °C, plants were grown in a controlled chamber at 22 °C and approximately 60% relative humidity under long-day conditions (16 h:8 h, light: dark, 45 µmol m–2 s–1 LED light [PLANTFLEC, Yellow color, Nippon Medical & Chemical Instruments]). Seven days after imbibition, seedlings grown on solid medium were transferred to Supermix A soil (Sakata, Kanagawa, Japan) and fertilized weekly with Hyponex powder (0.5 µg/L; Hyponex Japan, Osaka, Japan). Flowering time was scored as leaf number by counting rosette leaves when the primary shoot reached 1 cm in length. Boxplots were generated using standard settings: median (central mark), 25th and 75th percentiles (box edges), whiskers representing the most extreme non-outlier data points, and outliers shown as open circles.

All datasets were assessed for normality using Shapiro–Wilk tests. For multiple comparisons, data that did not follow a normal distribution were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by the Steel–Dwass test.

Results

Assembly and annotation of the C. hadiensis genome

To characterize the genome of C. hadiensis, genome size was first estimated through k-mer analysis of short-read sequencing data, yielding an estimated genome size of 1.93 Gbp with low heterozygosity (0.244%) (Fig. S2). Species identity was verified using the chloroplast barcode loci rbcL and matK (Fig. S3), which have been widely used as a standard two-locus DNA barcode system for land plants because of their sequence recoverability, sequence quality, and discriminatory power [55]. Cytological analysis of root-tip cells revealed a chromosome number of 2n = 30 (Fig. S4), which was consistent with a previous report [56].

We generated 71 Gb of Q30-filtered PacBio HiFi reads (Table S1) and assembled them into contigs totaling 1.83 Gb with a contig N50 of 57.1 Mbp. After organellar sequences were removed, the assembly was scaffolded using 104 Gb of Hi-C reads, resulting in 15 pseudomolecules together with 212 unplaced contigs containing 95 gaps (Fig. S5). The number of pseudomolecules was consistent with the cytologically inferred chromosome number (2n = 30). Subsequent gap filling using Oxford Nanopore long reads reduced the number of scaffold gaps to 28. Assembly completeness was further supported by BUSCO analysis [38], which recovered 97.4% of single-copy orthologs (Table S2). Based on these results, the 15 pseudomolecules were considered chromosome-scale assemblies of the C. hadiensis genome and were used for downstream analyses.

Gene annotation predicted 56,218 protein-coding genes (Fig. 1B), among which 382 genes were annotated with two or more transcript isoforms. BUSCO evaluation of the annotation recovered 94.9% of single-copy BUSCOs. Functional annotation assigned product descriptions or gene names to 76.4% of predicted proteins. Gene-dense regions were enriched toward chromosomal ends, whereas repetitive sequences were concentrated in central chromosomal regions. By integrating these annotations with transcriptome datasets from leaves, stems, and roots of C. hadiensis [13], we examined the presence and transcription of genes involved in flavonoid biosynthesis and terpene-precursor production. Genes required for flavone biosynthesis, including luteolin-associated pathways, were present and highly transcribed across all tissues. In contrast, genes associated with isoflavone, flavonol, and anthocyanin biosynthesis were absent or only weakly expressed, suggesting that these compounds are not major metabolites in C. hadiensis (Fig. S6). This observation is consistent with a previous report showing that flavonoids isolated from C. hadiensis extracts were predominantly flavone derivatives [8]. Genes involved in both the mevalonate and methylerythritol phosphate (MEP) pathways, which produce terpene precursors, were also present and highly transcribed in all analyzed tissues, indicating active terpene-precursor biosynthesis (Fig. S7).

Comparative ortholog analysis across Lamiaceae further suggested that Coleus and Ocimum diversified within approximately the last 10 million years (Fig. 1C, Table S3). Gene-family expansion and contraction analysis indicated substantial contraction along the C. hadiensis lineage after divergence from Ocimum basilicum. Expanded gene families were associated with redox-related functions, including alternative oxidase, superoxide-generating NADPH oxidase, and ubiquinol:oxygen oxidoreductase, whereas contracted gene families were enriched for stress-related functions such as responses to water deprivation, cold, and abscisic acid. Previous studies have suggested that highly productive glandular trichomes require reinforced redox control [57, 58]; therefore, these expansions may be associated with maintenance of secondary metabolite production in C. hadiensis glandular trichomes. In contrast, contraction of stress-associated gene families may reflect the environments in which C. hadiensis is distributed, including dry woodland and rocky grassland habitats [59]. However, because gene-family expansion and contraction analyses reflect relative copy-number variation rather than direct redox or stress-response activity, the physiological relevance of these changes remains to be experimentally validated.

We next analyzed the distribution of synonymous substitution rates (Ks) among coding regions and compared the results with four Lamiaceae species (Ocimum basilicum, Lavandula angustifolia, Isodon lophanthoides, and Perilla frutescens) together with one Lamiales species (Sesamum indicum). The major peak in the paralogous Ks distribution (self-Ks) of C. hadiensis occurred at a lower Ks value than the orthologous Ks peak of the C. hadiensis–S. indicum comparison (Fig. S8), suggesting that a whole-genome duplication event occurred after divergence of these two lineages. However, the broad distribution of self-Ks values prevented more precise temporal placement relative to other recent lineage-divergence events.

Repeat-rich structure of the C. hadiensis genome

Overall, the genome of C. hadiensis was highly repetitive, with repetitive elements accounting for 86.4% of the assembled sequence. Repeat annotation revealed strong enrichment of Copia-type LTR retrotransposons (44.7%), followed by Gypsy-type LTR retrotransposons (9.9%). To compare repeat content across Lamiaceae species, genome sequences from 42 Lamiaceae species together with two outgroups (A. thaliana and Zea mays) were analyzed under identical conditions. Among the examined species, C. hadiensis exhibited the highest overall repeat proportion and the highest Copia-type fraction (Fig. 2A, Table S4). Notably, the repeat proportion of C. hadiensis exceeded that of Z. mays (83.8%), which is a species with a well-characterized repeat-rich genome [60]. Across Lamiaceae lineages, the relative contributions of Copia- and Gypsy-type LTR retrotransposons varied considerably and did not follow a consistent clade-wide pattern, suggesting that Copia enrichment in C. hadiensis is lineage specific. Consistent with this interpretation, insertion-time estimates revealed pronounced peaks for both Copia- and Gypsy-type LTR retrotransposons at approximately 1–1.5 million years ago, which is more recent than the estimated divergence of Coleus from Ocimum (Fig. 2B).

We additionally identified 1,409 intact LTR retrotransposons based on the presence of both terminal repeats and coding domains. Among these elements, 710 were classified as Copia-type and 662 as Gypsy-type. Despite the genome-wide abundance of Copia-derived sequences, the numbers of intact Copia and Gypsy elements were comparable, consistent with extensive post-insertion degradation and inactivation of Copia elements. Genome-wide methylation profiling further showed that methylation density was positively associated with Copia-type LTR retrotransposons, whereas this relationship was not clearly observed for Gypsy-type elements (Fig. S9). Mapping of RNA-seq datasets detected transcripts originating from a subset of intact LTR retrotransposons (Fig. 2C), indicating that some copies remain transcriptionally active under the analyzed conditions.

Terpene synthase gene cluster

Lamiales species exhibiting diverse terpene-derived aromas often show expansion of TPS gene families [20, 61]. In C. hadiensis, our previous transcriptome-based analysis identified 26 expressed TPS genes spanning multiple subfamilies [13]; however, transcriptome datasets alone were insufficient to resolve the complete TPS repertoire and its genomic organization. We therefore used the chromosome-scale genome assembly to characterize the full complement of TPS genes and examine their chromosomal distribution.

By searching for conserved TPS domains, we identified 128 TPS genes among the annotated loci (Fig. 3A, Table S5). TPS gene numbers were additionally compared across multiple plant species by reanalyzing protein sequences using the same pipeline applied to C. hadiensis (Fig. 3B, Table S6). This analysis showed that C. hadiensis ranked seventh among the 75 examined species, indicating that C. hadiensis possesses a relatively large TPS repertoire relative to many other plant species.

Phylogenetic classification using representative TPS sequences assigned 45, 61, 3, 13, and 5 genes to the TPS-a, TPS-b, TPS-c, TPS-e/f, and TPS-g subfamilies, respectively, whereas one gene remained unclassified. Together, TPS-a and TPS-b accounted for 83% of the total TPS repertoire. Many members of these two subfamilies were located within tandem clusters of closely related sequences, which is consistent with recent local duplication events within these groups.

Transcription of annotated TPS genes was analyzed by mapping RNA-seq datasets obtained in a previous study [13]. Under the sampled conditions, transcription above the detectable threshold was observed for only a minority of TPS loci (Fig. 3). Within the TPS-b subfamily, the limonene synthase gene previously validated by cloning experiments (PhTPS1) [13] exhibited the strongest transcription in both leaves and stems. Several additional TPS genes belonging to the TPS-a, TPS-b, and TPS-e/f subfamilies were also highly transcribed in leaves and/or stems. Homology-based annotation further suggested that some transcribed TPS genes are similar to characterized enzymes involved in the biosynthesis of compounds such as endo-fenchol, germacrene, β-caryophyllene, bicyclogermacrene, ent-kaurene, and ent-copalyl diphosphate in other Lamiaceae species. These annotations identify candidate TPS genes potentially associated with major volatile and diterpene biosynthetic pathways in C. hadiensis, although biochemical validation will be required to confirm these functional assignments.

TPS genes showed nonuniform chromosomal distribution and frequently occurred in close proximity, forming tandemly arranged clusters. In particular, prominent clustering was observed near the terminal region of chromosome 13 (Fig. 4A). One representative cluster spanned 353 kb and contained 11 TPS-b genes, including one truncated copy (Fig. 4B). Sequences derived from LTR retrotransposons were interspersed throughout this region, indicating that the TPS cluster is embedded within a repeat-rich genomic environment.

We further investigated whether TPS genes formed biosynthetic gene clusters together with genes encoding proteins required for downstream terpenoid biosynthesis reactions. In total, 59 biosynthetic gene clusters were identified across the genome, among which five included TPS genes identified in the present analysis (Table S7). All five clusters contained one or more cytochrome P450 (CYP) genes, most of which possessed domains annotated as CYP71-like, CYP76-like, or CYP86A (Fig. 4C, Fig. S10, Table S8). Because these CYP gene classes are known to participate in terpenoid biosynthesis, the CYP genes within these five clusters are also highly likely to contribute to terpenoid synthesis together with TPS genes. Similar to the pattern observed for TPS genes, many CYP genes exhibited transcription below the detectable level under the analyzed conditions.

The FLOWERING LOCUS T/TERMINAL FLOWER 1 gene family in C. hadiensis

Flowering is among the most important traits in horticultural and ornamental plants, and precise regulation of flowering is essential for both productivity and aesthetic value. Among the major regulators of floral transition, FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT), a principal component of florigen, plays a central role in promoting flowering. FT belongs to the phosphatidylethanolamine-binding protein (PEBP) family, which also includes closely related homologs such as TERMINAL FLOWER 1 (TFL1) and A. thaliana CENTRORADIALIS (ATC). Despite their sequence similarity, FT- and TFL1-like proteins frequently exhibit antagonistic functions. In general, FT promotes floral transition, whereas TFL1 and related anti-florigenic proteins repress flowering or maintain indeterminate meristem identity [62]. However, the functional distinction between FT-like and TFL1-like proteins cannot always be inferred solely from overall sequence similarity. Consequently, functional characterization is required to determine whether individual FT/TFL1 family members act as flowering promoters or repressors in a given species [63, 64].

Previous photoperiod experiments demonstrated that Coleus species exhibit short-day flowering responses [65]. Consistent with these observations, C. hadiensis did not flower under our long-day growth conditions, suggesting short-day-type flowering behavior. To characterize flowering-associated regulators in C. hadiensis, we identified genes encoding PEBP domains and examined their phylogenetic relationships with FT/TFL1 family members from A. thaliana and P. frutescens (Fig. 5A). In the C. hadiensis genome, 13 PEBP-domain genes were identified; among them, four grouped within the FT-like subfamily, whereas five clustered within the TFL1-like subfamily together with TFL1/ATC homologs. We designated five FT-like or phylogenetically intermediate genes as ChFT1–ChFT5, including three FT-like genes and two genes positioned between the FT-like and TFL1-like clades (Fig. 5A).

RNA-seq analysis of C. hadiensis grown under long-day conditions detected FT/TFL1 family transcripts in multiple tissues (Fig. 5A). Among the PEBP-domain genes, ChFT1 transcripts accumulated most abundantly in leaves under long-day conditions. Several additional genes also exhibited tissue-associated expression patterns, with transcripts detected predominantly in leaves (i.e., ChFT3 and ChT1ch01G00218200), stems (i.e., ChT1ch13G00085700 and ChT1ch01G00218200), and roots (i.e., ChT1ch13G00155500, ChT1ch11G00140400, ChT1ch13G00085700, and ChT1ch11G00095100) (Fig. 5A). To examine the evolutionary conservation of these FT homologs, homologs of each ChFT gene were searched across Lamiaceae, Lamiales, and additional plant species. Comparative analysis identified homologs of ChFT1 and ChFT2 in other Lamiaceae species, supporting conservation of related FT-like genes across this family (Fig. 5B; Table S9).

To determine whether these C. hadiensis FT homologs exert promotive or repressive effects on flowering, transgenic A. thaliana lines overexpressing each ChFT gene (ChFT1–ChFT5) under the CaMV 35S promoter were generated (Fig. 6). Under the tested growth conditions, 35Spro: AtFT plants flowered earlier and produced fewer rosette leaves than wild-type Col-0 plants. Among the ChFT homologs, 35Spro: ChFT2 plants exhibited a comparably strong early-flowering phenotype, whereas 35Spro: ChFT3 plants also flowered earlier than wild-type plants but later than 35Spro: AtFT and 35Spro: ChFT2 plants (Fig. 6A, C). In contrast, 35Spro: ChFT1, 35Spro: ChFT4, and 35Spro: ChFT5 plants flowered later than wild-type plants, with 35Spro: ChFT1 exhibiting the strongest delay phenotype. 35Spro: ChFT4 plants showed a moderate delay, whereas 35Spro: ChFT5 plants flowered only slightly later than the wild-type (Fig. 6B, D). Collectively, these heterologous overexpression assays suggest that ChFT2 and ChFT3 possess flowering-promotive activity, whereas ChFT1 and ChFT4 exhibit flowering-repressive activity. Among these genes, ChFT1 displayed the strongest repressive effect under the tested conditions, which is consistent with its high expression under long-day conditions.

Discussion

We generated a chromosome-scale reference genome for C. hadiensis and used it to investigate genomic features associated with fragrance and flowering traits. Our analyses suggest that the large genome size of C. hadiensis is associated with Copia-dominated LTR retrotransposon expansion and that TPS loci are unevenly distributed across chromosomes, with several occurring in clustered arrangements. In addition, we identified an FT homolog candidate, ChFT1, that functions as a floral repressor in a heterologous system, thereby providing a foundation for further investigation of flowering regulation in this lineage.

Among currently available Lamiaceae genomes, C. hadiensis represents an extreme case of repeat accumulation and exhibits a strongly Copia-skewed LTR composition, highlighting that repeat-driven genome expansion can proceed through lineage-specific processes rather than through a uniform pattern across plant lineages [66]. Insertion-time estimates placed the major amplification peak at approximately 1–1.5 Ma, which is substantially more recent than the inferred Coleus–Ocimum divergence. This result indicates that much of the LTR accumulation in C. hadiensis reflects a recent amplification event, with Copia-derived sequences contributing substantially to genome expansion. In this context, the observation that methylation density was more strongly associated with Copia-derived sequences than with Gypsy-derived sequences (Fig. S9) is consistent with the widely observed plant-genome dynamic in which transposable-element amplification is followed by methylation-based silencing as a primary host defense mechanism limiting further activity [67, 68]. Beyond their contribution to genome-size evolution, Copia-rich and methylation-associated repeat landscapes can also influence the local genomic environment of neighboring genes. In addition, LTR remnants are increasingly recognized as potential sources of cis-regulatory sequences that may contribute to expression variability of adjacent loci [69]. This possibility warrants additional investigation, particularly to determine whether LTR-derived sequences influence regulation of nearby genes, including terpene-associated loci, in ways relevant to breeding.

The chromosome-scale assembly clarifies both the complete TPS repertoire and its genomic organization, revealing 128 TPS genes, many of which are concentrated at specific chromosomal regions and include multiple tandemly arranged clusters, especially on chromosome 13 (Fig. 4A). Under the analyzed conditions, transcription was not detected for many annotated TPS genes in any sampled tissues, which is consistent with observations reported for TPS genes in other plant species. For example, the patchouli (Pogostemon cablin) genome, another Lamiaceae species, contains 266 full-length TPS genes, among which 151 of 266 genes (56.77%) were reported to show almost no detectable expression in RNA-seq datasets [61]. Similarly, an analysis of Rosaceae species reported multiple TPS genes containing both conserved domains that were not expressed in any analyzed tissues [70]. These nontranscribed TPS genes may instead be active only under specific developmental stages, tissue types, or environmental conditions not represented in the present dataset. Accordingly, an important value of the reference genome is the definition of the complete TPS candidate repertoire together with its chromosomal organization. The clustered arrangement of TPS loci is consistent with local duplication as a major driver of repertoire expansion and further suggests possible routes for terpene diversification through sequence divergence among paralogs. At the same time, such clustering raises the possibility of coordinated regulation through shared local regulatory architectures [51]. Delineation of TPS clusters and their chromosomal boundaries therefore provides practical targets for marker development and genomics-assisted breeding, including analyses of cluster haplotypes or structural polymorphisms that may affect gene content and expression.

Although genetic validation was not performed directly in C. hadiensis, the chromosome-scale genome provides a systematic framework for investigating flowering-associated regulators and establishes a rational starting point for dissecting their functions in this lineage. Our genome-wide survey of PEBP genes clarified the copy-number composition and phylogenetic placement of FT/TSF- and TFL1/CEN/BFT-clade homologs in C. hadiensis, thereby positioning these candidate regulators within a comparative Lamiales framework. Within this repertoire, we identified ChFT1 as an FT-like gene conserved among Lamiaceae species that exhibited repressor activity in a heterologous system, supporting its candidacy as a regulator of photoperiod-responsive flowering control. Consistent with broader FT/PEBP-family studies, closely related paralogs can function antagonistically in flowering regulation across multiple species and contribute to photoperiodic adaptation. For example, the BvFT1/BvFT2 pair in sugar beet integrates seasonal and photoperiodic cues, AFT and CsFTL3 in chrysanthemum mediate flowering responses under night-break and short-day conditions, and SP5G/FT-related regulators in tomato underlie long-day repression and day-neutral flowering through cis-regulatory variation [71–73]. Together, these examples demonstrate how lineage-specific diversification within the FT/PEBP family can generate both promotive and repressive functions in a daylength-dependent manner. The reference genome presented here further facilitates evaluation of conservation patterns and gain/loss events among such homologs across Lamiaceae and related lineages with improved phylogenetic resolution. Because ChFT genes were expressed under the control of the constitutive 35S promoter, however, the flowering-time phenotypes observed in this study should be interpreted cautiously, as they may reflect ectopic or non-physiological expression rather than native gene function.

Conclusions

Overall, our analyses of the chromosome-scale genome of C. hadiensis provide new insights into genomic features associated with fragrance and flowering, including repeat landscapes, TPS gene organization, and flowering-associated candidate genes. These findings establish a foundation for future functional studies in C. hadiensis and support subsequent investigations linking genome structure with ornamental traits relevant to breeding.
