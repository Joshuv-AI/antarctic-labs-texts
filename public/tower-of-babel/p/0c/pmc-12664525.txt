Targeted ErbB4 receptor activation ameliorates neuronal deficits via DOCK3 signaling in a transgenic mouse AD model
Authors: Chong Liu, Yan Zhao, Ji-ji Dao, Wei Zhang, Jie Liu, Yu-ke Ma, Chen-meng Qiao, Chun Cui, Shuang-xi Chen, Yan-qin Shen, Wei-jiang Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccumulating evidence has highlighted the critical involvement of ErbB4 receptor in the onset and progression of Alzheimer's disease (AD). Utilizing a small molecule ErbB4 receptor agonist (E4A) identified through virtual screening, it was observed that activation of ErbB4 receptor significantly ameliorated the cognitive behavioral deficits in APP/PS1 mice. Additionally, E4A treatment enhanced the expression of DOCK3 and SIRT3, leading to improvements in synaptic and mitochondrial dysfunction within the hippocampus of these mice. E4A also attenuated the activation of the TLR4-NF-κB-NLRP3 pathway, thereby reducing neuroinflammation and the formation of β-amyloid (Aβ) plaques. In vitro studies revealed that E4A partially mitigated the impact of hippocampal neuronal damage on microglial inflammation, which was partly compromised by the silencing of DOCK3. Collectively, our data suggest that targeted activation of ErbB4 receptor may treat AD via DOCK3 signaling by inhibiting neuronal damage and subsequent neuroinflammation, thereby offering a viable strategy for this neurodegenerative disease.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease (AD), ErbB4 receptor, Small molecule agonist, DOCK3, Synapse

Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a progressively advancing neurodegenerative disorder as a leading cause of mortality and disability among the elderly population, which necessitates the urgent development of effective treatments and amelioration of associated dementia symptoms [1]. The primary pathological features include amyloid plaque deposition, intraneuronal neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs), and neuronal loss mainly in the cerebral cortex and hippocampus [2]. The misfolding and subsequent accumulation of extracellular β-amyloid (Aβ) are implicated in progressive memory loss and cognitive decline over time [[3], [4], [5]]. Despite significant advancements in elucidating the pathogenesis of AD in recent years, the efficacy of current therapeutic approaches remains suboptimal [6,7].

Neuregulins (NRGs) play a crucial role in brain development and neuronal protection [8]. ErbB4, a key receptor mediating the physiological functions of NRG1, is expressed in critical brain regions of adult animals. Initial studies demonstrated a significant increase in the immunoreactivity of ErbB4 phosphorylation in the brain tissue of both AD patients and transgenic AD mice, suggesting that aberrant activation of ErbB4 receptor may contributes to the pathological progression of AD [9,10]. However, transgenic 5xFAD mice exhibit NRG1-ErbB4 signaling pathway impairment, accompanied by neuronal network dysfunction in the hippocampal Schaffer collateral pathway [11]. Additionally, Aβ triggers pathological activation of ErbB4 receptor, thereby involving ferroptosis-related proteins such as c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) and p38, instead of activating the classical NRGs-ErbB4 signaling pathway molecules [12]. Conversely, NRG1 pretreatment inhibits JNK activation and mitigate associated neuronal damage [12], indicating that exogenous administration of NRG1 or its mimics could reverse the aberrant activation of ErbB4 receptor, thereby presenting a potential therapeutic target for AD.

The high cost of the recombinant protein and its limited ability to traverse the blood-brain barrier (BBB) significantly impedes further optimization and clinical application of rNRG1. In contrast, employing small molecule compounds as agonists can address these drawbacks [13]. We thus screened and identified a small molecule compound, 4-bromo-1-hydroxy-2-naphthoic acid (C11H7BrO3, E4A), which can target and activate ErbB4 receptor through molecular docking technology. E4A can prevent neuronal senescence induced by d-Galactose through the inhibition of the ferroptosis pathway [14]. However, the precise mechanisms by which ErbB4 receptor activation mediates the inhibition of neuronal death and neuroinflammation in AD require further elucidation.

Dedicator of cytokinesis 3 (DOCK3) is a critical member of the DOCK family [15]. Its levels are markedly reduced in the brain tissues of patients with sporadic AD [16]. DOCK3 is instrumental in regulating morphogenesis, migration, neuronal development, cell division, adhesion, and neurite growth [17]. Furthermore, DOCK3 plays a pivotal role in facilitating optic nerve regeneration [18]. Additionally, DOCK3 interacts with presenilin 1 (PS1) to modulate amyloid precursor protein (APP) processing. DOCK3 can expedite intracellular APP degradation, thereby significantly reducing Aβ secretion [16]. These findings collectively suggest that DOCK3 may involve in the activation of ErbB4 receptor, thereby playing a pivotal role in mitigating Aβ-induced neurotoxicity [19].

Synaptic loss correlates strongly with cognitive deficits in AD [20,21]. Aβ can induce oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, both of which exacerbate neuronal damage [21,22]. Mitochondrial deacetylase sirtuin 3 (SIRT3) is an important regulator of reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. SIRT3 overexpression can reduce the levels of ROS and improve mitochondrial function as a potential therapeutic approach for synapse damage induced by oxidative stress [23,24]. Additionally, SIRT3 facilitates the preservation of mitochondrial function upon kainic acid (KA)-induced neurotoxicity [25]. Furthermore, it was recently reported that Tirzepatide attenuates high fat diet-related cognitive impairment by up-regulating SIRT3 expression to suppress NLRP3 neuroinflammation signaling [26].

We hypothesize that targeted activation of ErbB4 receptor may reverse cognitive and behavioral deficits in AD by enhancing DOCK3 signaling, which in turn leads to the overexpression of SIRT3 and improved mitochondrial function, thus ameliorating synaptic damage. Our primary data suggest that ErbB4 receptor agonist may treat AD via DOCK3 signaling by inhibiting neuronal synaptic damage and subsequent neuroinflammation, thereby offering a viable strategy for this neurodegenerative disease.

Materials and methods

Animals and drug administration

Animal experiments were conducted at the Animal Experiment Center of the Wuxi School of Medicine, Jiangnan University, following approval from the Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee of Jiangnan University and in accordance with the general regulations of the National Institutes of Health (approval code: JN. No. 20230228t0320515). The ErbB4 receptor agonist, C11H7BrO3 (4-bromo-1-hydroxy-2-naphthoic acid, S987115, Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai) Trading Co. Ltd., China), which targets the conserved S42, L96, and Y123 sequences within both human and mouse ErbB4 receptor motifs, was previously identified through virtual screening (Fig. S1).

Six-month-old APP/PS1 double transgenic AD mice and wild-type littermates were procured from Chang Zhou Cavens Laboratory Animal Ltd. Prior to the commencement of the study, the mice were maintained in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment with a 12-h light/dark cycle at 25 ​°C and were given ad libitum access to food and water. In this experimental study, twelve wild-type littermates were randomly allocated to the wild-type (WT) control group (n ​= ​12). Additionally, twenty-four transgenic APP/PS1 mice were divided into two groups: the APP/PS1 control group (n ​= ​12) and the APP/PS1+C11H7BrO3 (E4A) treatment group (n ​= ​12). Following a one-week acclimation period, mice in the APP/PS1+C11H7BrO3 group received daily intraperitoneal injections of C11H7BrO3 at a dose of 400 ​ng/kg body weight. Concurrently, mice in both the WT control and APP/PS1 control groups were administered an equivalent volume of 0.1 ​M PBS [27]. Behavioral assessments were conducted two weeks post-treatment, and brain tissues were harvested upon completion of the behavioral study.

Behavioral assays

The behavioral experiments were conducted using a randomized double-blind design, allowing the mice a minimum acclimation period of 30 ​min to adjust to the experimental environment prior to testing. The sequence of behavioral assessments was structured to administer less demanding tasks first, followed by more challenging ones. The testing protocol included the open field test, novel object recognition test, Y-maze, and Morris water maze (MWM).

The open field experiment

The open field experiment is employed to assess anxiety and depression in mice. The experimental protocol comprises an adaptation phase followed by a testing phase. During the adaptation phase, mice are permitted to freely explore the test field (50 ​× ​50 ​cm2) for a duration of 5 ​min. Subsequently, their exploratory trajectories and behavioral parameters are recorded by a video tracking system for an additional 5 ​min. These parameters include the total distance traveled and the distance moved within the central area. The experimental apparatus is sanitized with 75 ​% ethanol between each subject to prevent contamination.

The novel object recognition experiment

The novel object recognition test is specifically utilized to assess learning and memory capabilities. The experimental protocol was structured into three distinct phases: an adaptation phase, a familiarization phase, and a testing phase, each with a duration of 24 ​h. In the adaptation phase, mice were introduced to the experimental setting and permitted to explore freely for a duration of 5 ​min to reduce any fear responses induced by novelty. During the familiarization phase, two identical cylindrical blocks were placed on opposite sides of the experimental apparatus, with each block positioned 5 ​cm from the corners of the enclosure. The mice were subsequently positioned at the center of the side used on the preceding day, oriented away from the blocks, and allowed a 5-min period to explore the blocks and their environment freely. After a 24-h interval, one of the cylindrical blocks was replaced with a conical object, and a video tracking system was utilized to record the mice's exploration patterns and behavioral responses during the ensuing 5-min session.

The Y-maze experiment

The Y-maze experiment serves as an assessment method for spatial recognition memory capabilities. This experiment was structured into a training phase followed by a testing phase. During the training phase, a baffle was employed to obstruct the arm, positioning the mice to face the wall from the starting arm. The mice were then allowed to explore the Y maze freely for a duration of 5 ​min. Following an intermission of 1 ​h, the mice were again permitted to explore the arms freely for an additional 5 ​min. During this period, the frequency of consecutive entries into the three arms in an alternating sequence was recorded. The percentage of spontaneous alternation behavior was subsequently calculated by determining the ratio of consecutive alternations to the total number of arm entries minus two, multiplied by 100 ​%.

The Morris water maze

The Morris water maze (MWM) is a well-established experimental paradigm employed to evaluate hippocampal dependence and long-term spatial memory. This task is performed in a circular pool with a diameter of 120 ​cm and walls 50 ​cm high, where the water temperature is meticulously maintained at 23 ​± ​1 ​°C. A hidden platform, 10 ​cm in diameter, is situated 1 ​cm below the water's surface. During the training phase, mice are subjected to four trials per day in the MWM from days 1–6, being introduced into various quadrants of the pool while oriented toward the wall. Mice that successfully identify the platform within 60 ​s are allowed to remain on it for 10 ​s, whereas those that fail to find the platform are gently guided to it and permitted to stay for 30 ​s. The escape latency and path length of the mice in locating the hidden platform are recorded. On the seventh day, a probe trial is administered to assess memory consolidation, during which the platform is removed from the maze, and the mice are given 60 ​s to swim freely. The frequency with which each mouse crossed over the previous platform location, as well as the proportion of time spent exploring the quadrant, was systematically observed and recorded.

Cell processing

The HT22 mouse hippocampal neuron cell line and the BV2 mouse microglia cell line were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (CL-0493A and CL-0697, respectively; Wuhan, China) and were cultured in DMEM high-glucose medium (D211113, BasalMedia Technologies Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at 37 ​°C with 5 ​% CO2.

In vitro gradient concentration treatment of C11H7BrO3 in hippocampal neuronal cells

This study employed a cellular model to examine the effects of varying concentrations of C11H7BrO3 on HT22 hippocampal neurons. The study involved the categorization of cells into four distinct groups: a control group and three experimental groups subjected to varying concentrations of C11H7BrO3, specifically low (2 ​nM), medium (5 ​nM), and high (10 ​nM) doses. This methodological approach was designed to identify the optimal dosage for subsequent in vitro experiments.

C11H7BrO3 treatment of an AD cell model pathologically induced by Aβ

The investigation focused on assessing the reparative effects of C11H7BrO3 on AD injury in vitro, as well as elucidating the underlying mechanisms, utilizing HT22 hippocampal neurons model pathologically induced by Aβ. The neuronal cells were allocated into four groups: a control group, a C11H7BrO3-treated group, an Aβ-induced group, and a combined Aβ+C11H7BrO3 treatment group. The control group was not treated with vehicle, C11H7BrO3 group was treated with 10 ​nM C11H7BrO3 for 24 ​h, Aβ group was damaged with 10 ​μM ​Aβ for 12 ​h, Aβ+C11H7BrO3 group was damaged with 10 ​μM ​Aβ for 12 ​h, and then treated with 10 ​nM C11H7BrO3 for 24 ​h. In this study, the effect of C11H7BrO3 on the modulation of ErbB4/DOCK3 signaling pathway and the repair of synaptic and mitochondrial damage in HT22 ​cells under AD in vitro was studied. In addition, BV2 cells were treated with a conditioned medium of HT22 ​cells to assess the effect of neurons on microglia by assessing the activation of inflammatory factors.

Effect of DOCK3 silencing on C11H7BrO3 treatment in AD cell model pathologically induced by Aβ

First, the DOCK3 silent concentration gradient model of HT22 hippocampal neurons was established, and the experiment was divided into 6 groups: the concentration gradient of NC group (10, 20, 50 ​nM) and si group (10, 20, 50 ​nM), and the optimal concentration was selected for the next test. Under the condition of Aβ pathology, the experiment of DOCK3 silenced and C11H7BrO3 treated HT22 hippocampal neurons model was divided into 4 groups: NC ​+ ​control group, NC ​+ ​C11H7BrO3 group, si ​+ ​control group, si ​+ ​C11H7BrO3 group. The experimental groups were categorized according to the following treatment protocols: the NC group and the si group were exposed to 10 ​μM ​Aβ for 12 ​h, followed by transfection with either 50 ​nM si-NC or si-DOCK3 for 24 ​h, and subsequent treatment with vehicle control for 24 ​h. In contrast, the NC ​+ ​C11H7BrO3 and si ​+ ​C11H7BrO3 groups underwent exposure to 10 ​μM ​Aβ for 12 ​h, transfection with 50 ​nM si-NC or si-DOCK3 for 24 ​h, and subsequent treatment with 10 ​nM C11H7BrO3 for an additional 24 ​h. In addition, BV2 cells were treated with a conditioned medium of HT22 ​cells to assess the effect of neurons on microglia by assessing the activation of inflammatory factors.

Cell transfection

DOCK3 siRNA and NC siRNA were transfected into HT22 hippocampal neurons using LipoRNAi™ reagent (C0535, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). DOCK3 siRNA sequence is as follows: sense (S): GCAGAUCAGUGAACGUUUTT, antisense (AS): AAACCGUUCACUGAUCUGCTT. NC siRNA sequence is as follows: S: UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT, AS: ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT.

Evaluation of oxidative stress in hippocampal neurons in vitro

The protein concentration of HT22 hippocampal neurons was measured by BCA method. The levels of total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD, A001-1-2, Jiancheng, Nanjing, China), glutathione (GSH, A006-2-1, Jiancheng, Nanjing, China), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px, A005-1-2, Jiancheng, Nanjing, China) and malondialdehyde (MDA, A003-1-2, Jiancheng, Nanjing, China) were measured using a commercial kit as instructed by the supplier. Their concentrations were measured by optic density (OD) values using a microplate reader (HTX/2, BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA).

Determination of ATP content in hippocampal neurons in vitro

The HT22 hippocampal neuron protein and HT22 hippocampal neuron culture supernatant were separately extracted. ATP content was determined using a kit (A095-1-2, Jiancheng, Nanjing, China), and their concentration was measured using a microplate reader (HTX/2, BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA) based on the OD value.

Golgi staining and analysis

Golgi-Cox staining was performed using the Fast Golgi Staining Kit (BA1808, Saint-Bio, Shanghai, China). Briefly, the mice’ brains were immersed in the Golgi-Cox solution for 14 days. The serial coronal sections (100 ​μm thick) were obtained, stained by working liquid, and coverslipped. The branched total dendritic length was determined using Neuroexplorer software and Sholl analysis. Sholl analysis was performed by counting the dendrites traversing a series of concentric circles at 10-μm intervals from the cell body. To assess dendritic spine density in the hippocampal CA1, CA3, and dentate gyrus (DG) regions, the number of spinous processes was quantified within randomly selected dendritic segments measuring 10 ​μm in length. The dendritic spine density was calculated by dividing the number of spinous processes by the length of the dendritic segment.

Tissue immunofluorescence staining

Post-perfusion, the brains were extracted and immersed in sucrose solutions of increasing concentrations (20 ​%, 25 ​%, and 30 ​%) and subsequently stored at 4 ​°C. The brains were then embedded in Tissue Tek O.C.T. compound (4583, Sakura Finetek, USA) and sectioned into serial coronal slices with a thickness of 8 ​μm for immunofluorescence staining. After washing three times with PBS, the tissue sections were subjected to antigen retrieval for 60 ​min in citrate antigen retrieval solution (pH 6.0) at 99 ​°C. Following a blocking step with 10 ​% goat serum, the tissue sections were incubated overnight at room temperature with a primary antibody mixture. Specifically, the sections were treated with a mouse anti-β3-tubulin antibody (1:100, sc80016, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA) in combination with the following antibodies: rabbit anti-p-ErbB4 antibody (1:200, bs-3220R, Bioss, Beijing, China), rabbit anti-SYP antibody (1:200, AF8091, Bioss, Beijing, China), rabbit anti-SIRT3 antibody (1:200, bs6105R, Bioss, Beijing, China), and rabbit anti-DOCK3 antibody (1:200, 20683-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China). Additionally, the sections were incubated with mouse anti-Aβ antibody (1:200, 803001, Biolegend, Wuhan, China) in combination with rabbit anti-IBA1 antibody (1:100, bs-1363R, Bioss, Beijing, China) and rabbit anti-GFAP antibody (1:200, BA0056, Boster, Wuhan, China). Following three washes with PBS (5 ​min each), the tissue sections were further incubated for 2 ​h with a mixture of Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (1:200, BA1126, Boster, Wuhan, China) and Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:200, BA1142, Boster, Wuhan, China). Samples were followed by mounting with an anti-fade mounting solution (P0131, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Images were finally acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Axio Imager Z2, Carl Zeiss AG, DE).

Cell immunofluorescence staining

Hippocampal neuronal cells were washed with pre-cooled PBS and fixed with 4 ​% paraformaldehyde. Non-specific antigen binding was blocked using 10 ​% goat serum, followed by incubation with the antibody mixture overnight at 4 ​°C overnight. Specifically, the fixed cells were treated with mouse anti-β3-tubulin antibody (1:100, sc80016, Santa Cruz biotech, USA) in combination with the following antibodies: rabbit anti-SYT1 (1:200, GB111130-100, Servicebio, Wuhan, China), rabbit anti-GAP43 antibody (1:1000, GB15094-100, Servicebio, Wuhan, China), rabbit anti-p-ErbB4 (1:200, bs-3220R, Bioss, Beijing, China), rabbit anti-SYP (1:200, AF8091, Bioss, Beijing, China), rabbit anti-PSD95 (1:200, GB11277-100, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) and rabbit anti-DOCK3 (1:200, 20683-1-AP, proteintech, Wuhan, China). After being rinsed 3 times with PBS (5 ​min each), the cells were incubated with a mixture of Alexafluor 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (1:200, BA1126, Boster, Wuhan, China) and Alexafluor 594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:200, BA1142, Boster, Wuhan, China) for 2 ​h. Samples were subsequently mounted using an anti-fade mounting solution (P0131, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Images were finally acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Axio Imager Z2, Carl Zeiss AG, DE).

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

According to the manufacturer's instructions for the immunoprecipitation kit (BK0004, ACE, Changzhou, China), HT22 ​cells were lysed on ice using the IP lysis buffer for 10 ​min. The lysate was then centrifuged at 13,000 ​g at 4 ​°C for 10 ​min, and the supernatant was collected. Subsequently, 20 ​μL of well-mixed magnetic bead suspension and 10 ​μL of rabbit anti-DOCK3 antibody (20683-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) were added sequentially and incubated together overnight at 4 ​°C on a shaker. Following incubation, the immune complexes were washed three times with Wash Buffer. After high-temperature denaturation, Western blot analysis was conducted assess the protein levels of DOCK3 and PS1 (the primary antibodies used in this study are listed in Table S1).

Western blot analyses

Total proteins were extracted from HT22, BV2 cells and the hippocampal tissues of mice, respectively. The proteins were subsequently separated via SDS-PAGE, transferred onto a PVDF membrane (FFP39, Merck Millipore Ltd, Darmstadt, Germany), and blocked with 3 ​% BSA at room temperature for 1 ​h. This procedure was followed by an overnight incubation with primary antibodies at 4 ​°C. Primary antibodies used in this study are listed in Table S1. The membrane underwent three washes with TBST (each lasting 10 ​min) and was subsequently exposed to a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-coupled secondary antibody (1:2000, A0192, A0208, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at ambient temperature for 1 ​h. Protein bands were visualized using the enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) system, and the band densities were quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.6.0, NIH, Bethesda, MA, USA).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analyses

The mouse hippocampal tissue was sectioned into 1 ​mm3 cubes and immersed in a 2.5 ​% glutaraldehyde solution at 4 ​°C for 24 ​h in the absence of light. Subsequently, the samples underwent treatment with a 5 ​% osmium tetroxide solution for approximately 1.5 ​h. Following staining with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, ultrathin sections measuring 50–70 ​nm in thickness were rinsed with deionized water and examined using a transmission electron microscope (HT7700, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Mitochondrial damage was characterized based on morphological features, with criteria for severely compromised mitochondria encompassing extensive fragmentation or dissolution of mitochondrial cristae, pronounced rupture of the outer membrane, and vacuolization [[27], [28], [29], [30], [31]]. ImageJ software (version 1.6.0, NIH, Bethesda, MA, USA) was employed to quantitatively assess mitochondrial damage indicators per unit area.

Mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) and mitochondrial mass

To assess the mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) and mitochondrial mass, processed HT22 hippocampal neuronal cells were incubated with 1X TMRE dye (C2001S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and 100 ​nM Mito-Tracker Green (C1048, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 30 ​min. Following incubation, the cells treated with Mito-Tracker Green underwent washing and nuclear staining procedures. The fluorescence intensity of the stained samples was then quantified using an inverted microscope (Ti-U, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Furthermore, the relative levels of Δψm and mitochondrial mass were evaluated through an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (Synergy H4, BIO-TEX, Winooski, Vermont, USA).

Statistical analyses

Data are presented as the mean ​± ​standard error of the mean (SEM). Differences among groups were evaluated using one-way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (Dotmatics), version 8.0. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

ErbB4 small molecule agonist ameliorates cognitive behavioral impairments in APP/PS1 mice

Behavioral assessments were conducted to examine the potential effects of an ErbB4 small molecule agonist on cognitive behavioral alterations in APP/PS1 mice (Fig. 1A). Initially, anxiety levels in the mice were assessed using the open field test. The findings indicated that administration of E4A significantly increased the duration of activity, the distance traveled in the central area, and the overall distance covered, compared to vehicle-treated APP/PS1 mice. However, these parameters remained substantially lower when compared to the wild-type control group (Fig. 1B and C). The findings indicate that administering an ErbB4 small molecule agonist ameliorates anxiety levels in APP/PS1 mice. Subsequently, a novel object recognition experiment was conducted to evaluate the exploratory capabilities of the mice. The results revealed that compared to wild-type mice from the same litter, APP/PS1 mice exhibit decreased recognition efficiency (the ratio of the time an animal spends exploring a novel object to the time it spends exploring a familiar object) for novel and familiar objects, as well as a reduction in total distance traveled. In contrast, treatment with the ErbB4 small molecule agonist improved recognition efficiency for a novel object, and also increased the total distance traveled by APP/PS1 mice (Fig. 1D and E). These results suggest that the ErbB4 small molecule agonist enhances exploratory behavior in APP/PS1 mice.

Subsequently, the Y-maze assay was utilized to assess the cognitive abilities of mice, with a particular emphasis on their learning and memory capacities. The results indicated that APP/PS1 mice exhibited a significant reduction in the spontaneous alternation rate compared to wild-type mice from the same litter. However, following treatment with the ErbB4 small molecule agonist, APP/PS1 mice demonstrated a significant enhancement in the spontaneous alternation rate within the Y-maze paradigm, suggesting an improvement in their learning and memory functions (Fig. 1F and G). Furthermore, a water maze experiment was employed to further evaluate spatial learning and memory performance. The findings demonstrated that, in comparison to wild-type mice from the same litter, APP/PS1 mice exhibited a significant reduction in both the frequency of platform entries and the duration spent on the platform. Additionally, there was a decrease in the time and distance of activity within the target quadrant, the effective area, and the platform. The average swimming speed exhibited a significant decrease as well. However, administration of the ErbB4 small molecule agonist resulted in a significant enhancement in both the frequency of platform entries and the duration spent on the platform by APP/PS1 mice. Furthermore, there was an improvement in the time and distance spent in the target quadrant, effective area, and platform, as well as a significant increase in average swimming speed (Fig. 1H and I). These findings imply that activation of ErbB4 through a small molecule agonist augments exploratory behavior, spatial learning and memory capabilities in APP/PS1 mice, thereby improving overall cognitive function.

ErbB4 small molecule agonist reduces synaptic dysfunction in hippocampal neurons of APP/PS1 mice

Synapses are crucial for the transmission of neuronal information, and modifications in their quantity and morphology can impact synaptic plasticity, thereby influencing learning and memory capabilities. In this study, we employed Golgi staining and transmission electron microscopy to examine the morphological alterations, dendritic spine modifications, and synaptic ultrastructural changes in hippocampal neurons of APP/PS1 mice treated with the ErbB4 small molecule agonist. Quantitative analyses were conducted to assess neuronal dendritic complexity, dendritic spine density, and alterations in synaptic number and synaptic interface structure, with the aim of evaluating synaptic plasticity. Initially, Golgi staining demonstrated that, relative to wild-type mice from the same litter, neurons in the hippocampal CA1, CA3, and DG regions of APP/PS1 mice exhibited a reduction in dendritic length and branching points, indicating decreased dendritic complexity. In contrast, administration of the ErbB4 small molecule agonist led to an increase in dendritic length and branching points, thereby enhancing dendritic complexity in APP/PS1 mice (Fig. 2A–C).

To assess alterations in hippocampal synaptic density, we examined the dendritic spines of hippocampal neurons in these mice. In the hippocampal regions CA1, CA3, and DG of APP/PS1 mice, neurons exhibited a reduced dendritic spine density, characterized by a decrease in mature mushroom-shaped spines. Conversely, treatment with the ErbB4 small molecule agonist resulted in an enhanced dendritic spine density and an increase in mature mushroom-shaped spines in these mice, as depicted in Fig. 2D–F. The results suggest that the activation of ErbB4 via the small molecule agonist modulates the morphology of hippocampal neurons, increases dendritic complexity, and facilitates synaptic maturation, ultimately enhancing synaptic function.

Further analysis using electron microscopy revealed that in comparison to their wild-type littermates, APP/PS1 mice demonstrated a significant reduction in synaptic quantity, an increase in the width of hippocampal synaptic clefts, a decrease in postsynaptic density thickness, and a shortened active zone length at synapses. Conversely, treatment with the ErbB4 small molecule agonist led to an increased number of synapses and enhanced synaptic interface structure in APP/PS1 mice (Fig. 3A and B). These findings imply that the small molecule agonist-induced ErbB4 activation appears to improve the ultrastructural integrity of synapses, thereby augmenting synaptic efficacy.

The degenerative alterations in hippocampal neurons have been demonstrated to influence microglial inflammation. To explore this issue, we collected supernatants from Aβ-treated hippocampal neurons, both with and without the addition of the ErbB4 small molecule agonist, to assess their effects on microglial activation. Our findings demonstrate that compared with the control group, the expression levels of TLR4 and p–NF–κB proteins in microglial cells were increased in the Aβ group. Therefore, it can be concluded that the ErbB4 small molecule agonist not only alleviates hippocampal neuron damage under Aβ pathological conditions but also lowers microglial inflammation, providing a multi-faceted approach for repairing neurodegenerative changes.

Discussion

The Neuregulin1 (NRG1) and its receptor ErbB4 are particularly significant due to their involvement in various physiological and pathological contexts. NRG1 and ErbB4 have been identified as crucial components in peripheral myelination, neuromuscular junction formation, and the plasticity of cortical neuronal circuits [34]. The significance of NRG1/ErbB4 signaling has been extended to its roles in the central nervous system's development and function.

In the context of AD, there have been conflicting reports regarding the role of NRG1/ErbB4 signaling. Down-regulation of NRG1/ErbB4 signaling has been found to be essential for optimal cognitive function, whereas overexpression of NRG1 impairs these cognitive functions [35]. Some studies have indicated that disruptions in NRG1/ErbB4 signaling pathways lead to deficits in synaptic plasticity, marked by a decrease in parvalbumin (PV) interneurons and resulting in persistent long-term potentiation (LTP) in aged AD mouse models [36]. Conversely, the administration of NRG1 has been found to mitigate cognitive deficits and maintain dendritic spine density in AD mouse models [34]. Furthermore, systemic delivery of Nrg1β1 through ErbB4 receptors has shown significant neuroprotective effects in PD models, effectively rescuing dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra [37].

We thus employed virtual screening technology to identify a small molecule compound targeting ErbB4, and simulate the binding interaction of NRG1 and ErbB4 receptor for the treatment of AD. It has been demonstrated that AD can compromise the integrity of the BBB, leading to its structural and functional disruptions [38]. This BBB disruption enhances the permeability of small molecule agonists such as E4A, thereby increasing their therapeutic efficacy. The primary findings indicate that the exogenous small molecule agonist can significantly ameliorate cognitive decline, anxiety-like behavior, and novel object recognition deficits in AD mouse models. These therapeutic effects are attributed to the specific binding and activation of ErbB4 receptor by the small molecule compound, in conjunction with the modulation of mitochondrial function via DOCK3 signaling, thereby providing neuroprotection.

DOCK3 gene plays a crucial role in cellular signaling, particularly in the central nervous system, where it regulates neurite and axonal growth [[39], [40], [41]]. It functions as a guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF) for the Rho GTPase Rac1, which is essential for actin dynamics and microtubule assembly [40]. Mutations or deletions in DOCK3 have been linked to severe neurodevelopmental disorders, presenting as developmental delay, hypotonia, and intellectual disabilities [40,42]. Moreover, findings have suggested DOCK3's role in epileptogenesis via the Rac1 pathway, implicating it further in neuronal synaptic plasticity and cytoskeleton rearrangement [43]. These insights underline the significance of DOCK3 not only in normal neurodevelopment but also in understanding neurodevelopmental disorders.

In vitro, both Western blot and immunofluorescence analyses demonstrated that the administration of an ErbB4 agonist significantly elevated the protein expression levels of key synapse-related molecules, including SYP, SYT1, and PSD95 in HT22 neuronal cell lines. Conversely, downregulation of DOCK3 via siRNA markedly attenuated the effects of the small molecule agonist on these proteins. These findings collectively suggest that DOCK3 partially mediates the synaptic enhancement induced by ErbB4 activation. The in vitro findings were substantiated by observations in APP/PS1 mice, which demonstrated reduced activation of ErbB4 receptor downstream signaling molecules, such as AKT, c-Src, Erk, and STAT5. Conversely, administration of the small molecule led to enhanced activation of these signaling pathways, suggesting that the small molecule acts as an ErbB4 receptor agonist in AD. Furthermore, electron microscopy analysis indicated that treatment with the small molecule agonist significantly ameliorated the submicroscopic structural damage to neuronal synapses. This reversal is likely attributable to the enhanced expression of SYP, SYT1, and PSD95.

SIRT3 deficiency has been demonstrated to heighten neuronal susceptibility to glutamate-induced calcium overload and oxidative stress, both of which are pivotal in the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases such as AD and Huntington's disease (HD) [44]. Furthermore, impaired mitochondrial function can result in synaptic deficits, a common feature in neurodegenerative disorders [45]. The absence of SIRT3 disrupts the homeostasis of mitochondrial protein acetylation, causing hyperacetylation of critical mitochondrial proteins and thereby exacerbating synaptic dysfunction [44]. The present results demonstrated that enhanced SIRT3 expression was associated with the restoration of mitochondrial integrity and the preservation of synaptic ultrastructure in ErbB4 agonist-treated AD mice. This suggests that ErbB4 activation contributes to the maintenance of synaptic function, at least partially, through the upregulation of SIRT3 expression and the consequent enhancement of mitochondrial functions.

Chronic neuroinflammation often results in neuronal damage and contributes to disease progression. In neurodegenerative diseases such as AD, PD, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), microglia become activated and can exhibit both protective and detrimental effects on neurons [[46], [47], [48]]. When microglia are primed by neurodegenerative processes, they may respond excessively to subsequent inflammatory stimuli, exacerbating neuronal injury [49,50]. Additionally, the interactions between neurons and microglia represent a key process for neuroimmune regulation and subsequent effects on CNS integrity. It was recently reported that neuron-derived extracellular vesicles modulate microglia activation and function, supporting that neurons critically regulate microglia activity and control inflammation via EV-mediated neuron-glia communication [51]. Additionally, extracellular α-synuclein released from neuronal cells has also been reported to activate inflammatory responses in microglia as an endogenous agonist for Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2) [52]. It can thus be deduced that neuronal injury alleviation in response to ErbB4 activation may down-regulate overactivation of microglial cells, thus reducing neuroinflammation under pathological AD conditions.

Collectively, our data suggest that targeted activation of ErbB4 receptor may treat AD via DOCK3 signaling by inhibiting neuronal damage and subsequent neuroinflammation, thereby offering a viable strategy for this neurodegenerative disease.
