Influenza B mosaic HA mRNA-LNP vaccines are cross-reactive and protective in mice
Authors: Irene González-Domínguez, Marta Boza, Adam Abdeljawad, William Shea, Eduard Puente-Massaguer, Mitali Mishra, Nicholas Lemus, Tsoi Ying Lai, Hiromi Muramatsu, Kieu Lam, James Heyes, Jose Luis Martínez-Guevara, Florian Krammer, Peter Palese, Norbert Pardi, Weina Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInfluenza B viruses cause about one-third of annual influenza infections, leading to significant respiratory illness. Vaccination remains the most effective strategy to mitigate the health and socio-economic burdens of influenza. Recent advances focus on developing a universal influenza B vaccine using chimeric hemagglutinin (HA) (cHA) and mosaic HA (mHA) constructs. cHA vaccines are engineered by replacing the entire head domain of influenza B HA with those from exotic avian influenza viruses, while mHA vaccines involve substituting only the immunodominant epitopes in the HA head domain. In this study, we assessed the immunogenicity of cHA and mHA vaccines delivered via the nucleoside-modified mRNA-lipid nanoparticle platform. Sequential immunization with these constructs in murine models stimulated immune responses targeting conserved HA epitopes, enhancing cross-protection against diverse influenza B virus strains. Notably, mHA vaccination induced higher levels of cross-reactive antibodies and antigen-specific CD4+ T cells compared to cHA, resulting in superior protection against the distantly related B/Lee/1940 influenza virus strain in both passive transfer and direct challenge mouse models. These findings highlight the potential of the mHA vaccination strategy and represent a significant step toward the clinical development of broadly protective influenza B vaccines.Keywords: MT: delivery strategies, mRNA vaccine, universal influenza vaccine, HA stalk antibodies, non-neutralizing antibodies, immuno-subdominant epitopes, T cell immunity, in vivo protection, nucleoside modification, lipid nanoparticle

Introduction

Influenza is a contagious respiratory disease caused by influenza A and B viruses in humans.1,2 Historically, influenza B viruses have attracted less attention since they lack an animal reservoir3,4 and are less antigenically diverse than influenza A viruses.5 Despite their low pandemic risk, influenza B viruses account for 20%–30% of all influenza cases.4,6 The first influenza B virus was isolated in 1940 (B/Lee/1940), which later diverged into two antigenically distinct lineages that co-circulated for several decades: the B/Yamagata/16/1988-like (Yamagata) strains and the B/Victoria/2/1987-like (Victoria) strains.3,7,8 Since the beginning of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, the Yamagata-lineage has not been identified (https://www.who.int/tools/flunet/flunet-summary, as of August 20, 2024),9 which has motivated the recommendation against its inclusion in the 2024–2025 influenza vaccine formulation.10,11 Pre-existing immunity against that lineage will promptly disappear for the new generations, bringing a new risk of re-emergence of antigenically similar viruses. In this unprecedented epidemiological context, the development of a universal influenza B virus vaccine that protects against all known influenza B viruses is of great importance.

Influenza B viruses undergo frequent changes to evade host pre-existing immunity. These mutations are mainly accumulated in their surface glycoproteins, the hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA) through a process known as antigenic drift.12 The HA glycoprotein is divided into two domains: the globular head domain and the stalk domain. The head domain contains the receptor binding site, which allows influenza viruses to bind to sialic acids and enter into the host cell. The head domain of influenza B virus also contains the four major antigenic regions, defined as the 120 loop, the 150 loop, the 160 loop, and the 190 helix.13,14 These immunodominant sites are the main target for neutralizing antibodies with HA inhibition (HI) activity and represent the main locations of antigenic drift. Current seasonal influenza virus vaccines aim to match vaccine strains with the circulating viruses.3 The selection of these vaccines is based on HI-active antibodies, which makes their protection strain specific with poor induction of cross-reactive antibodies to other strains.

With the aim to generate a more cross-protective influenza vaccine, we have developed two universal vaccination strategies, the chimeric HA (cHA) approach and the mosaic HA (mHA) approach.15,16 Both strategies were designed with the rationale of eliciting antibodies against the immuno-subdominant and more conserved epitopes of the influenza B HA protein. These strategies consist of replacing either the whole head domain or the immunodominant antigenic sites in the head domain of the influenza B HA with those from exotic avian influenza A HAs to which humans are naive (Figures 1A and 1B).17,18 Sequential vaccination with these candidates has proven to enhance the immune responses toward the conserved regions of the HA with different vaccine platforms for group 1, group 2 influenza A,19,20,21,22,23,24 and for influenza B.17,18,25 In the present work, we performed a head-to-head comparison of the immunogenicity of these vaccines using the nucleoside-modified messenger RNA (mRNA)-lipid nanoparticle (LNP) platform. mRNA-LNP vaccines represent an effective strategy with high capacity for rapid development and market implementation26 as demonstrated for severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) vaccines against COVID-19.27 In previous studies, we showed that mRNA-LNP vaccines encoding for transmembrane HA antigens are immunogenic and protective against influenza A and influenza B virus infections.28,29,30 Here, we studied the immunogenicity of mHA and cHA universal influenza B vaccine candidates in a dose-escalation experiment, where the humoral, cellular, and in vivo cross-protection have been evaluated in the mouse model.

Results

Design of influenza B cHA and mHA vaccine immunogens

Two different influenza B cHA and mHA immunogens, in which the entire head domains (cHA) or the major antigenic sites (mHA) of the B HA had been replaced by the corresponding sequences from H5 (A/Vietnam/1203/2004, H5N1-PR8-IBCDC-RG/GLP) and H13 (A/black-headed gull/Sweden/1/1999, H13N6) avian influenza A viruses, were used as universal influenza B vaccine candidates (Figures 1A and 1B). The cHA and mHAs are based on B/Brisbane/60/2008 (BBris) and B/Phuket/3073/2013 (BPhu) HAs, resulting in the cH13/BBris, mH13/BBris, cH5/BPhu, and mH5/BPhu immunogens.25 BBris and BPhu belong to the Victoria-lineage and Yamagata-lineage influenza B viruses, respectively. Previous studies have shown that sequential vaccination with these heterologous constructs induces durable, cross-reactive antibody responses against both lineages when delivered as inactivated vaccines.25,31

In vitro expression of cHA and mHA immunogens was assessed in mRNA transfection experiments in human embryonic kidney 293 T (HEK293T) cells. Surface expression of the four vaccine candidates was analyzed through immunostaining with anti-HA polyclonal sera followed by flow cytometry. Cells transfected with firefly luciferase (Luc)-encoding mRNA served as negative controls. All four constructs were successfully expressed on the cell membranes 24 h post-transfection (Figure 2). Candidates derived from B/Bris showed higher surface expression levels, likely due to their greater antigenic similarity to the B/Malaysia/2506/2004 (Victoria-like) virus used in generating the polyclonal sera.

Binding assays using antibodies CR8033 and CR8059 (which target the receptor binding site and the vestigial esterase in the head domain, respectively), and CR9114 (which targets the central stalk domain)32 demonstrated strong interaction with the mHA constructs, confirming correct presentation of conserved epitopes (Figure S1).17 For cHA constructs, no binding was detected with CR8033 or CR8059, consistent with the complete absence of the influenza B globular head domain.32 Interestingly, CR9114 also failed to bind cHA constructs, suggesting a potential conformational alteration in this key epitope compared to wild-type influenza B HAs.

The mRNAs were subsequently encapsulated in LNPs as previously described.33 The final hydrodynamic diameter values of the mRNA-LNP formulations are shown in Figure S2.

Vaccination with influenza B cHA and mHA mRNA-LNPs elicits robust humoral immune responses

Immunogenicity of cHA and mHA vaccines was assessed in BALB/c mice following a three-dose sequential vaccination regime (Figure 3A). Quadrivalent influenza vaccine (QIV, Flulaval) was given as a prime vaccination to mimic human pre-existing immunity to influenza viruses. Three weeks after prime vaccination, one dose of either chimeric or mosaic H13/BBris were given at the indicated doses. Next, 4 weeks after boost, a second boost with either chimeric or mosaic H5/BPhu immunogens were administered as shown in Figure 3B. This vaccination regimen was selected to balance memory imprinting with conventional boosting timelines while avoiding full response waning as performed in previous work.17,25,29,31 A dose-escalation design was followed in which doses of 0.05, 0.5, or 5 μg of mRNA per mouse were evaluated (Figure 3B). To assess the role of QIV priming, a control group received two doses of mRNA-LNP encoding firefly Luc (Luc, prime-only). Additional controls included mice vaccinated with split inactivated vaccines containing 1 μg of HA31 per dose and naive mice, serving as positive and negative controls, respectively. Serum samples collected after the final boost demonstrated seroconversion in all vaccinated groups against B/Phuket/3073/2013 and B/Austria/1359417/2021 viruses (Figure S3).

Cross-reactive antibody responses were examined by ELISA against three different recombinant proteins. The cH7/BYam protein (Figure 3C) contains an H7 head domain fused to a BYam HA stalk domain, while the mH11/BYam protein (Figure 3D) features H11 sequences substituted into major antigenic sites of the BYam HA. Accordingly, ELISA against cH7/BYam measures stalk-specific antibodies, whereas mH11/BYam detects both subdominant head and stalk responses. Of note, no H7-specific antibodies were detected in any of the groups, supporting the specificity of the assay, despite the presence of H13 and H5 anti-head antibodies in the cHA-vaccinated groups (Figure S3). Antibody binding to B/Lee/1940 HA (Figure 3E) was used as a surrogate of broad cross-protection, in agreement with previous studies.31 Post-prime and post-boost IgG titers were measured to assess whether a two-dose vaccination strategy was required to induce these cross-reactive antibodies.

QIV-only (prime-only) immunization produced low levels of cross-reactive antibodies (Figures 3C–3E), consistent with previous results after administration of two QIV doses in mice.31 In contrast, mHA vaccination elicited higher titers targeting subdominant head epitopes (Figure 3D) and comparable stalk-specific IgG titers (Figure 3C) to those elicited by cHA, both in a dose-dependent manner. These findings correlated with elevated antibody binding titers to the B/Lee/1940 protein (Figure 3E) and wild-type viruses (Figure S3), which were boosted after the two-dose vaccination schedule compared to prime-only groups. IgG2a and IgG1 subclasses against B/Lee/1940 were also examined for intermediate and high doses, respectively (Figure 3F). The cHA vaccine induced a higher IgG2a/IgG1 ratio, while mHA vaccination yielded a more balanced subclass response.

HI assays were then performed using sera against homologous mHA viruses (mH5/BPhu and mH13/BBris) and B/Lee/1940 (Figure 3G). HI activity could not be assessed for cHA viruses, as viable B cHA viruses have not yet been rescued.17 While HI antibodies against mHA viruses were induced by mHA mRNA vaccines, none of the vaccines generated detectable HI activity against B/Lee/1940. Notably, mHA vaccines, but not cHA vaccines, induced strong cross-neutralizing antibodies (Figure 3H). Consistent with prior observations for stalk-specific antibodies, cHA vaccines elicited antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) in a dose-dependent manner, as did mHA mRNA-LNP vaccines, with the high-dose group showing the strongest activity (Figure 3I).

Overall, both cHA and mHA mRNA-LNP vaccines induced potent cross-reactive humoral responses targeting non-HI reactive, subdominant epitopes. The mHA immunogens produced broader and stronger cross-reactive antibodies with both neutralizing and Fc-mediated effector functions.

Chimeric HA and mHA mRNA-LNP vaccination stimulate antigen-specific T cell immunity

The induction of T cell responses was evaluated using an intracellular cytokine staining assay (Figure 4 and S4). One week after the second boost vaccination, mice were euthanized and spleens were collected (Figure 4A). Single-cell suspensions of splenocytes were generated and stimulated with an HA-peptide pool, and the presence of cytokine producing antigen-specific T cells was determined by flow cytometry. Antigen-specific CD4+ and CD8+ CD44hi+ T cells were detected in both cHA- and mHA-immunized mice. mHA vaccines induced a higher level of IFN-γ (Figures 4B and 4C), IL-2 (Figures 4D and 4E), and TNF-α (Figures 4F and 4G) and minimal IL-4 (Figure S4) in CD4+ T CD44hi+ T cells when administered with the 5 μg vaccine dose. Additionally, polyfunctional CD4+ CD44hi+ T cells with increased levels of double and triple cytokine positive cells were detected, which have been described to provide the best protection to control viral infections (Figure S4).20,34

On the contrary, cHA and mHA vaccines induced IFN-γ (Figures 4H and 4I), although not being statistically significant, and minimal IL-2 (Figures 4J and 4K), TNF-α (Figures 4L and 4M), and CD107+-expressing (Figure S4) CD8+ CD44hi+ T cells. Interestingly, intermediate doses seem to provide a similar trend, which has been also observed in other studies (Figure S4).35

Mosaic HA mRNA-LNP vaccination provides superior cross-reactive antibody-mediated immune protection in a serum passive transfer experiment

Antibody-mediated protection was evaluated in vivo in a serum passive transfer experiment (Figure 5A). Briefly, 100 μL of pooled sera of each vaccine group collected 4 weeks after the second boost was administered intraperitoneally (i.p.) 2 h prior to infection with a mouse-adapted B/Lee/1940 virus. Body weight loss and survival after administration of low-dose (0.05 μg, Figures 5B and 5F), intermediate-dose (0.5 μg, Figures 5C and 5G), and high-dose (5 μg, Figures 5D and 5H) of vaccines were measured. mHA split-vaccinated, prime-vaccinated mice followed by two doses of Luc mRNA-LNP (only prime group) and a naive mice group were used as comparators (Figures 5E and 5I). In general, pronounced morbidity was observed for all groups likely due to the small amount of serum and the high mLD50 dose used in the study. Mice vaccinated with cHA constructs succumbed to infection a few days after the controls did at the three doses tested, whereas partial protection was achieved in mice that received mHA vaccines (low-dose: 20% p <∗, intermediate-dose: 80% p <∗∗, high-dose: 60% p <∗). Overall, mHA vaccines provided superior protection against the phylogenetically distant B/Lee/1940 virus thus confirming that protective cross-reactive antibodies were elicited.

Chimeric HA and mHA mRNA-LNP vaccination provides cross-protection against direct B/Lee/1940 virus challenge

The cross-protection conferred by the cHA and mHA mRNA-LNP vaccines was tested in a direct challenge experiment with the B/Lee/1940 virus 4 weeks after administration of the second booster vaccine (Figure 6A). Body weight loss and survival after administration of low-dose (0.05 μg, Figures 6B and 6F), intermediate-dose (0.5 μg, Figures 6C and 6G), and high-dose (5 μg, Figures 6D and 6H) vaccines were measured. Mice vaccinated with inactivated split mHA influenza B virus vaccine (mHA split), Luc mRNA-LNP group (prime only), and unvaccinated mice (naive) were used as comparators (Figures 6E and 6I). As expected, compared to the serum passive transfer experiments (Figure 5), higher protection from morbidity and mortality was observed for all groups.

One dose of QIV (prime only) was able to provide complete protection from lethality against this mouse adapted virus, in agreement with previous results in this model.31 Low-dose cHA and mHA vaccination did not provide additional protection in survival or weight loss compared to prime only group. On the contrary, an advantage for mHA mRNA-LNP vaccines was observed at the high-dose group (Figure 6D), in which animals recovered body weight completely 7 days after virus challenge (Mann-Whitney test p <∗∗).

Discussion

Nucleoside-modified mRNA-LNP has proven to be a fast and flexible vaccine platform.26 Its safety and effectiveness were seen in the fight against COVID-19 with millions of doses administered in a very short period of time.27 On May 31, 2024, a second mRNA-based vaccine against respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) received US FDA approval (mRESVIA from Moderna), and many other mRNA-LNP-based vaccines against several infectious diseases,26,36 including influenza, are in clinical development.37 Furthermore, current efforts by several health agencies will facilitate the accessibility of this vaccine platform in low- and middle-income countries and their use for pandemic preparedness through the mRNA technology transfer programme (Global mRNA Technology Transfer Programme - MPP (medicinespatentpool.org).

Here, we assessed the immunogenicity of universal influenza B virus vaccine candidates using mRNA-LNPs in mice. Our vaccination approach is based on sequential administration of mRNA-encoded cHA and mHA immunogens, in which the immunodominant regions of the HA were replaced by those of exotic avian influenza viruses.17,18 Sequential administration of H13/BBris-derived immunogens followed by H5/BPhu-derived immunogens was chosen to repeatedly boost subdominant head and stalk-directed responses. Earlier work with split inactivated mHA formulations showed that reversing this order does not substantially alter the magnitude or quality of the elicited responses in mice, emphasizing that repeated exposure to conserved epitopes is likely more important than the specific head sequence order.31

Building on prior evidence that membrane-bound HA is more immunogenic than soluble HA for group 1 influenza viruses,38 cHA and mHA mRNAs encoding membrane-anchored H13/BBris- and H5/BPhu-based chimeras were formulated as mRNA-LNPs and shown to be expressed on the surface of transfected HEK293T cells, with antibody binding data indicating that mHA preserves key conserved epitopes, whereas cHA likely induces conformational changes in the stem region that merit structural investigation.

In BALB/c mice, the mHA approach was superior to the cHA approach at inducing cross-reactive antibodies with neutralizing and ADCC activities, which correlated with a higher antibody-mediated protection in the absence of HI activity against the phylogenetically distant B/Lee/1940 virus, which serves as a relevant surrogate for broad influenza B protection.31 Complete protection in the serum passive transfer experiment might have been obtained if a higher serum dose (200 μL) was administered; however, this was not possible due to limited sera collection.

mHA vaccination also increased binding antibody titers against wild-type B/Phuket/3073/2013 and B/Austria/1359417/2021 viruses, highlighting their cross-protective potential against contemporary strains. Furthermore, mHA stimulated a balanced IgG2a/IgG1 antibody subclasses and elicited HA antigen-specific CD4+ T cells to a higher extent than cHA vaccination. Finally, vaccine-induced protection was evaluated in a direct challenge model. Although this study does not provide a direct vaccine efficacy comparison to QIV, reduced morbidity with mHA mRNA-LNP vaccination at 5 μg dose was observed compared to cHA vaccinated and QIV-only vaccinated mice suggesting the superiority of the mHA mRNA-LNP vaccine. Future studies using a second animal model will further validate these findings and facilitate direct comparison with the current standard of care.

Overall, one of the ultimate goals of influenza virus vaccine research is to generate a universal vaccine that will protect against all influenza A group 1, group 2, and influenza B viruses.20,22 In previous studies, other influenza B virus antigens such as NA, nucleoprotein (NP), or matrix 2 (M2) have been evaluated in monovalent and multivalent mRNA-LNP formulations in mice.29 Hence, a second-generation universal influenza vaccine combining mHA approaches with other immunogens may enhance the protection provided by the formulations tested in this study. These studies will bring light for the development of a universal multicomponent influenza mRNA-LNP vaccine using these universal vaccination strategies.

Materials and methods

Design of universal influenza B vaccine candidates

cHA and mHA immunogens were designed as previously described.17,18,25 Briefly, two cHA and mHA gene segments were designed by replacing either the whole head domain (cHA) or the major antigenic sites (mHA) of the HA gene sequences of B/Phuket/3073/2013 and B/Brisbane/60/2008 with the corresponding sequences of H5 (A/Vietnam/1203/2004, H5N1-PR8-IBCDC-RG/GLP) and H13 (A/black-headed gull/Sweden/1/1999, H13N6) to generate cH5/BPhu, mH5/BPhu cH13/BBris, and mH13/BBris, respectively. Sequences for the mHA antigens were reported previously.25

Cells, viruses, and vaccines

HEK 293 T (CRL-3216, ATCC) cells were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% v/v fetal bovine serum (FBS) and penicillin (100 U/mL)-streptomycin (100 μg/mL) (P/S). HEK 293 T cells were grown in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 at 37°C. The influenza B virus strains B/Phuket/3073/2013, B/Austria/1359417/2021, mH5/B/Phuket/3073/2013, mH13/B/Brisbane/60/2008, and B/Lee/1940 were grown in 10-day-old embryonated chicken eggs (AVSBio) for 72 h at 33°C.25 Eggs were then placed at 4°C overnight before allantoic fluid was harvested. Harvested allantoic fluid was centrifuged at 4,000× g for 10 min at 4°C to pellet debris. Purified virus stocks were further collected after sucrose cushion ultracentrifugation as previously described.25 Virus stocks were then aliquoted and stored at -80°C until use. Split mH5/BPhu and mH13/BBris vaccines were produced from allantoic fluid as previously described.39

mRNA-LNP production

mRNA-LNP vaccines were produced as previously described.33 Briefly, the genes for codon-optimized firefly Luc and full-length cH5/BPhu, mH5/BPhu cH13/BBris, and mH13/BBris were synthesized (Genscript). Constructs were ligated into mRNA production vectors, vectors were linearized, and a T7-driven in vitro transcription reaction (Megascript, Ambion) was performed to generate mRNA with 101 nucleotide-long poly(A) tails. Capping of mRNA was performed in concert with transcription through addition of a trinucleotide cap1 analog, CleanCap (TriLink), and m1Ψ-5′-triphosphate (TriLink) was incorporated into the reaction instead of uridine triphosphate (UTP). Cellulose-based purification of mRNA was performed as described.40 mRNA integrity was then assessed on an agarose gel before storing at −20°C. Nucleoside-modified mRNAs were encapsulated in LNP (Genevant Sciences) using a controlled mixing process (US 9005654) in which an aqueous solution of mRNA in acetate buffer at pH 5 was combined with an ethanolic solution of lipids, containing a polyethylene glycol-conjugated lipid, an ionizable lipid, cholesterol, and distearoylphosphatidylcholine at a lipid-to-drug ratio of 20:1 weight/weight. Ethanol was removed by ultrafiltration, followed by buffer exchange into Tris-sucrose buffer at pH 8. The formulations were adjusted to 0.5 mg/mL and sterile filtered through a 0.2 μm polyethersulfone membrane. Aliquots were stored frozen at -80°C until use. The study used an LNP formulation (LNP H) that is well-characterized in our previous work.41

mRNA transfection

HEK 293 T cell transfection was performed in 96-well plates with TransIT-mRNA (Mirus Bio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions: mRNA (0.3 μg) was combined with TransIT-mRNA reagent (0.34 μL) and boost reagent (0.22 μL) in 17 μL of serum-free medium, and the complex was added to 6 × 104 cells in 183 μL complete medium. Forty-eight hours after the transfection of Luc, cH5/BPhu, mH5/BPhu cH13/BBris, and mH13/BBris mRNA-transfected HEK 293 T cells were collected and washed once with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), containing 1% v/v FBS. Next, cells were incubated with anti-HA human monoclonal antibodies (CR8033, CR8059, and CR9114)32 (7.5 μg/mL) or with mouse polyclonal antibody (1:500) obtained from mice infected with a sublethal dose of B/Malaysia/2506/2004 virus in V-bottom 96-well plates (Greiner) at 4°C for 30 min. After washing with PBS containing 1% v/v FBS, cells were incubated with anti-human Alexa Fluor 488 (1:500, Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A-11013) and anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 647 (1:500, Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A-21235) secondary antibody in the dark at 4°C for 30 min. After washing, samples were fixed with 4% (v/v) formaldehyde diluted in PBS at room temperature for 5 min. The samples were washed and re-suspended in FACS (fluorescence-activated cell sorting) buffer (PBS containing 1% bovine serum albumin, and 2 mM ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid [EDTA]) and stored at 4°C until analysis. Flow cytometric data were acquired on a FACS CantoII cytometer (BD Biosciences). Thirty thousand events were recorded per sample and data were analyzed with the FCS Express 7 software (De Novo Software) and FlowJo (FlowJo Software).

Immunization studies

For animal immunizations, 8- to -10-week-old female BALB/c mice (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories) were used. Experiments were performed in accordance with protocols approved by the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Mice were primed with seasonal QIV (Flulaval Quadrivalent 2022/2023, GSK) at a dose of 1 μg HA per mouse. Then, two booster doses of mRNA-LNP vaccines at 0.05, 0.5, or 5 μg of mRNA per mouse were administered in intervals of 3–4 weeks. Mice vaccinated with mRNA-LNP encoding for firefly Luc and an unvaccinated group (naive) were included as controls. Vaccines were administered intramuscularly (i.m.) diluted in a total volume of 100 μL of PBS (pH 7.4, Gibco). After the final immunization, mice were bled and euthanized at defined time points. After euthanasia, spleen and blood were collected. Blood was obtained by cardiac puncture (terminal procedure) or cheek bleeding, and sera were isolated by low-speed centrifugation and stored at −20°C until use.

Passive transfer and challenge studies

For passive transfer experiments, 8- to -10-week-old female BALB/c mice (Jackson Laboratories) received 100 μL of pooled sera via i.p. injection 2 h before the virus challenge. Mice were infected intranasally (i.n.) with 5 murine 50% lethal doses (5× mL D50) of the mouse-adapted B/Lee/1940 virus in a volume of 30 μL of sterile PBS (Gibco) after anesthesia with a ketamine/xylazine cocktail administered i.p. In direct virus challenge experiments, mice were challenged 3–4 weeks after the second boost. Animals from passive transfer and direct challenge experiments were monitored for survival and weight loss for 14 days post-challenge and were scored dead and humanely euthanized if they lost more than 25% of their initial body weight.

ELISA

Recombinant HA proteins were produced using the baculovirus expression system as described previously42,43 and recombinant viruses were produced and purified as previously described.25 Antigens were coated onto Immulon 4 HBX 96-well microtiter plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 2 μg/mL in 1× coating buffer (SeraCare Life Sciences Inc.) at 50 μL/well overnight at 4°C. All plates were washed 3 times with 225 μL PBS containing 0.1% (v/v) Tween 20 (PBST) and 220 μL of blocking solution (3% v/v goat serum, 0.5% w/v non-fat dried milk powder in PBST) was added to each well and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Individual serum samples were serially diluted 3-fold in blocking solution followed by a 2 h incubation at room temperature. ELISA plates were washed 3 times with PBST and 50 μL of secondary antibody conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) was added. For total IgG quantification, a 1:3,000 dilution of anti-mouse IgG (H&L) peroxidase conjugated (Cytiva) in blocking solution was added. For IgG1 and IgG2a quantification, a 1:2,000 dilution in blocking solution of anti-mouse IgG1 or anti-mouse IgG2a (Abcam) was added, respectively. After 1 h, plates were washed 3 times with PBST and developed using SigmaFast o-phenylenediamine dihydrochloride solution (Sigma-Aldrich) for 10 min. Reactions were stopped by adding 50 μL 3 M hydrochloric acid (HCl), and the absorbance was measured at 492 nm on a plate reader. The average plus 3 standard deviations of absorbance values of blank wells was used as a cutoff to determine endpoint titers and the area under the curve (AUC) using GraphPad Prism v.10.0.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA, www.graphpad.com). The IgG2a/IgG1 ratio was calculated by dividing the IgG2a endpoint over IgG1 endpoint values.

Hemagglutination inhibition (HI) assay

Mouse sera were treated with receptor destroying enzyme (RDE II; Denka Seiken) to eliminate non-specific inhibitors in the sera. Briefly, serum was mixed with RDE in a 1:3 ratio (v/v). RDE-treated samples were incubated at 37°C for 18–20 h and the reaction was stopped by the addition of 2.5% (v/v) sodium citrate solution in a 1:3 ratio. The samples were then heat-treated at 56°C for 30 min. Serum was finally diluted with sterile PBS to reach a final dilution of 1:10. To perform HI assays, virus stocks were diluted in PBS to a final HA titer of 8 HA units (4 wells of HA) per 50 μL sample. 2-fold dilutions (25 μL) of RDE-treated serum in PBS prepared in 96-well V-bottom microtiter plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were then combined with 25 μL of the diluted virus. The plates were incubated for 30 min at room temperature to allow HA-specific antibodies to bind to the viruses. Then 50 μL of a 0.5% v/v suspension of turkey red blood cells (Lampire) were added to each well. HI titers were defined as the reciprocal of the highest dilution of serum that inhibited hemagglutination of red blood cells.

MNT assay

Microneutralization (MNT) assay was done as described earlier. Influenza virus (B/Lee/1940) was first tittered in Madin-Darby Canine Kidney (MDCK) cells by using a 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50)37 MDCK cells were plated in a 96-well plate at a concentration of 1.8 × 104 cells per well and allowed to grow overnight at 37°C in 5% CO2 until they reached 90% confluence the next day. Virus stock was diluted in PBS to a final titer of 100 TCID50 per 50 μL. RDE-treated pooled mice sera, prepared as mentioned above, was 2-fold serially diluted. Half of the volume from the serial serum dilutions was incubated at a 1:1 volume ratio with the adjusted virus stock for 1 h at room temperature to allow sera antibodies to bind to virions. Later, the serum-virus mixture was added to the monolayer of MDCK cells after they were washed twice with sterile PBS and incubated for 1 h at 33°C. The remaining half volume of the serial serum dilutions were supplemented with a 1:1 addition of 1X (minimum essential medium) MEM with 2 μg/mL tosyl phenylalanyl chloromethyl ketone (TPCK)-treated trypsin. After 1 h of incubation, the virus-serum mixture was removed, and cells were washed again with sterile PBS. The serial serum dilutions were added to the MDCK cells and incubated for 2 days at 33°C. An HA assay was used as the readout for the microneutralization assay using 0.5% chicken red blood cells. The micro-neutralization titer was determined as the last well in which hemagglutination inhibition occurs.

ADCC reporter assay

To assess the Fc-mediator functions of serum antibodies in immune response, ADCC reporter bioassay kit (Promega Life Sciences) was used. Briefly, MDCK cells were seeded on a flat-bottom white 96-well plates (Costar Corning) at 2 × 104 per well. After 18 h of incubation at 37°C, the MDCK cells were washed once with warm PBS and then infected with B/Lee/1940 virus at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 5 for single-cycle replication. The infected cells were then incubated at 33°C for 24 h. The next day, the culture medium was removed and 25 μL of assay buffer Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI 1,640 supplemented with 4% low-IgG FBS) was added to each well. Pooled mouse sera was serially diluted in assay buffer and added in a volume of 25 μL/well in duplicates and was incubated with the infected MDCK cells for 30 min at 33°C. Genetically modified Jurkat cells expressing the mouse FcγRIV with a Luc reporter gene under transcriptional control of the nuclear factor-activated T cell promoter were added at 7.5 × 104 cells in 25 μL/well (Promega). Cells were then incubated for another 6 h at 33°C. At the end of the incubation, 75 μL of Bio-Glo Luc assay reagent (Promega Life Sciences) was added to each well and incubated at room temperature for 10 min before taking the reading. Luminescence was quantified using a Synergy 4 microplate reader (BioTek Instruments). The area under the curve (AUC) of the fold induction was measured as relative light units (RLUs) and calculated as follows: (RLUinduced−RLUbackground)/(RLUuninduced−RLUbackground) and plotted using Graphpad Prism 10 software.

Spleen processing and intracellular cytokine staining

Intracellular cytokine staining was performed as previously described.44 Splenocytes were resuspended in complete RPMI 1640 media (cRPMI, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% w/v FBS (Gibco), 100 U/mL of P/S (Gibco), and 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco). Cells were seeded in V-bottom 96-well plates (CELLSTAR, Greiner Bio-One North America Inc., Monroe, NC, USA) at an average of 2 ×106 cells/well in cRPMI media containing anti-mouse CD28 (1:500, BD), brefeldin A (1:1,000, GolgiPlug, BD Biosciences), and monensin (1:1,000, GolgiStop, BD Biosciences). Splenocytes were stimulated with a pool of B/Malaysia/2506/2004 HA peptides (NR-18967, BEI Resources) at a final individual peptide concentration of 5 μg/mL at 37°C with 5% CO2 10–12 h. Negative control cells were stimulated with an equivalent volume of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Positive control cells were stimulated with a cocktail containing phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (0.5 μg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich) and ionomycin (1 μg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich). The unstimulated control cells were only treated with the cRPMI media. After stimulation, cells were washed with PBS containing 2% v/v FBS and centrifuged at 500× g for 5 min and then stained with Zombie Red diluted in PBS (1:500, BioLegend) at RT in the dark for 15 min. Cells were washed in PBS containing 2% v/v FBS (500 × g for 5 min) and incubated with surface staining cocktail containing Fc Block CD16/CD32 1:50 (BD) and the anti-mouse antibodies BV711 CD3 (1:300, clone 17A2 BioLegend Cat#100241, AB_2563945), Pacific Blue CD4 (1:400, clone GK1.5 BioLegend Cat#100428 RRID: AB_493647), PerCP/Cy5.5 CD8 (1:200, clone 53–6.7 BioLegend Cat#100734 RRID: AB_2075238) BUV 737 CD44 (1:400, clone IM7, BD Biosciences Cat# 612799) at 4°C in FACS buffer for 30 min. Cells were washed in FACS buffer and then incubated in fixation/permeabilization buffer (CytoFix, BD Biosciences) at 4°C for 5 min. After fixation, cells were washed in 1× permeabilization buffer (CytoPerm, BD Biosciences), then incubated with the intracellular staining cocktail containing anti-mouse antibodies Alexa Fluor 647 IFN-γ (1:400, clone XMG1.2 BioLegend Cat#505814, RRID:AB_493314), Alexa Fluor 488 TNF-α (1:300, clone MP6-XT22 BioLegend Cat#506313 RRID:AB_493328), PE/Cy7 IL-2 (1:300, clone JES6-5H4, BioLegend Cat#503832 RRID:AB_2561750), BV421 IL-4 (1:400, clone 11B11, Biolegend, Cat#504127), and BUV395 CD107 (1:400, clone 1D4B, BD Biosciences, Cat# 565533), 1× permeabilization buffer at 4°C for 1 h. Samples were then washed in 1× permeabilization buffer and resuspended in PBS for acquisition. Samples were acquired on an Aurora spectral cytometer (Cytek, Fremont, CA, USA) using SpectroFlo software (Cytek), with the relevant single fluorochrome compensation controls set by the daily acquisition of Cytometer Setup and Compensation beads (Ultracomp beads, Invitrogen). Analysis was performed with FCS Express 7 and analyzed using the GraphPad Prism v.10.0.0. Briefly, the percentage of cytokine CD4+T-CD44+T or CD8+T-CD44+T cells was calculated, and the value of stimulated groups was subtracted from that of the unstimulated condition.

Statistics

Man-Whitney test was used to compare cytokine expressing T cells and weight loss in direct challenge experiments. Mantel-Cox test was performed for survival analyses and non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis tests corrected using Dunn’s test were performed for multiple comparisons on IgG antibody titers and HI titers using GraphPad Prism v.10.0.0.
