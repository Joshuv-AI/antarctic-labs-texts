Defining optimal treatment strategies for nasopharyngeal carcinoma based on the ninth edition TNM staging system: insights from a multicenter cohort study in an endemic region
Authors: Gaoyuan Wang, Lanqing Huo, Xiaodong Zhu, Feng Lei, Liangfang Shen, Kunyu Yang, Yaqian Han, Guanjie Qin, Minyu Wen, Jibin Li, Jun Ma, Xiaojing Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe ninth edition AJCC/UICC staging system (TNM-9) for nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) was released in 2025, prompting a need to redefine evidence-based treatment regimens. This study aimed to identify optimal therapies under the new criteria.MethodsIn this multicenter cohort, we enrolled patients from endemic Chinese regions (2016‑2017) with non‑metastatic (M0) or de novo metastatic (M1) NPC. Propensity score matching (1:1 or 1:M) and inverse probability of treatment weighting minimized selection bias. Survival was compared via Kaplan‑Meier and Cox models.ResultsThe cohort comprised 6049 non-metastatic patients restaged by TNM-9 criteria, categorized into the following risk groups: low-risk (stage IA), intermediate-risk (stage IB and T3N0), high-intermediate-risk (T3N1), and high-risk (T1-3N2 and stage III). Additionally, 127 de novo metastatic patients were enrolled. Survival between concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) and intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) alone in the stage IA group was not significantly different (hazard ratio [HR]: 0.50; 95% CI: 0.05–5.50; P = 0.562). However, within the intermediate-risk group, CCRT demonstrated significantly improved 5-year OS (97.9% vs. 91.0%; P = 0.001). Among high-risk patients, induction chemotherapy (IC) followed by CCRT with or without adjuvant chemotherapy resulted in superior 5-year OS (83.9% vs. 78.6%; P = 0.003). Although reclassification under TNM-9 affected only a limited number of patients (n = 301), this subgroup exhibited a substantial survival benefit from the addition of IC (5-year OS: 83.2% vs. 66.4%; P = 0.001). A comprehensive treatment strategy combining systemic therapy, locoregional radiotherapy (LRRT), and maintenance therapy (MT) was associated with significantly prolonged survival (HR: 0.36; 95% CI: 0.23–0.55; and HR: 0.45, 95% CI: 0.25–0.79). Furthermore, local consolidation therapy of metastases (LCT) in M1a was associated with a non-significant trend longer median OS (89.0 vs. 62.4 months; P = 0.122).ConclusionsUnder the TNM-9, management recommendations were as follows: radical IMRT alone for stage IA; CCRT for stage IB and T3N0; and high-intensity therapy for high-risk disease, including reclassified subsets. For metastatic disease, the addition of LRRT and MT to systemic therapy was associated with improved survival in both M1a and M1b patients, whereas a trend toward potential benefit from LCT was observed only in M1a subgroup.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-026-05085-3.Keywords: Nasopharyngeal carcinoma, TNM-9, Risk-stratified treatment, Overall survival

Background

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), a malignant epithelial tumor, is highly prevalent in Southeast Asia and North Africa [1, 2]. Over the past several years, the eighth edition of American Joint Committee on Cancer/Union for International Cancer Control (AJCC/UICC) tumor-node-metastasis staging system (TNM-8) had been widely adopted globally, serving as a crucial framework for delineating tumor invasion, directing therapeutic interventions, and forecasting patient outcomes [3]. The management of non-metastatic NPC is stratified according to this staging system: definitive intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) constitutes the cornerstone of treatment for stage I disease and for intermediate-risk cases (stage II and T3N0) lacking adverse features [2, 4, 5] whereas cisplatin-based concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) is mandated for the remaining intermediate-risk patients [6, 7] For locoregionally advanced NPC (stage III and IVA excluding T3N0 in TNM-8), induction chemotherapy (IC) followed by CCRT is the standard treatment, and the addition of adjuvant metronomic chemotherapy confers a further survival advantage in higher-risk patients [8–10].

The ninth edition of the AJCC/UICC TNM staging system (TNM-9) was promulgated in 2025 to enhance prognostic precision and has been implemented globally. In TNM-9, N1-2 disease presenting with advanced extranodal extension (ENE) is now reclassified as N3; the criteria of T4 category is more comprehensive than previous edition, incorporating the concepts of unequivocal radiological cranial nerves and inferior orbital fissure involvements [11, 12]. Significant modifications in the new staging system are the consolidation of T1-2N0 into stage IA, the classification of T1-2N1 as stage IB, and the division of M1 disease into M1a (≤ 3 lesions) and M1b (> 3 lesions) based on metastatic burden [11, 12] These revisions have subsequently induced shifts in stage distribution—for example, certain cases previously considered early-stage are now classified as locoregionally advanced, and novel prognostic subgroups have been introduced. Consequently, there is a pressing need to determine how existing treatment guidelines and original clinical trial conclusions should be adapted to this new framework.

To address this gap, we conducted a multicenter, retrospective study using clinical data from patients with newly diagnosed NPC across six medical institutions in endemic regions of China. This multicenter study seeks to elucidate the optimal therapeutic plan for each risk subgroup (M0 to M1). It also examines the profiles of patients whose stages were altered by TNM-9 and investigates effective treatment strategies for them.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

A total of 6049 patients with newly diagnosed, histologically proven non-metastatic (M0) NPC between January 2016 and December 2017 were identified from six participating institutions and constituted the primary study cohort. The contributing centers and respective patient numbers were as follows: Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center (SYSUCC; n = 4485), Guangxi Medical University Cancer Hospital (n = 479), Zhongshan City People’s Hospital (n = 384), Xiangya Hospital of Central South University (n = 301), Union Hospital of Tongji Medical College (n = 296), and Hunan Cancer Hospital (n = 104). Additionally, the study included 127 patients from SYSUCC who were newly diagnosed with de novo metastatic (M1) NPC and had received at least two cycles of first-line palliative chemotherapy during the same period. The flow of this study is detailed in Fig. S1 of Additional file 1. The protocol of this study was approved by the Ethics Committee of all centers and performed in accordance with the guidelines of the World Medical Association (Declaration of Helsinki). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Diagnosis and treatment

All patients underwent a comprehensive pretreatment evaluation, which included a thorough physical examination (head and neck examination, cranial nerve assessment, and palpation of cervical lymph nodes), hematologic and biochemical tests, fiberoptic nasopharyngoscopy, histopathological confirmation, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the nasopharynx and neck, and whole-body staging via chest/abdominal radiography combined with a whole-body bone scan or positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT). We conducted a re-examination of the images focusing on the key anatomical structures revised in the TNM-9 and each patient was reclassified according to both the TNM-8 and TNM-9 staging system. Additional information is available in the Additional file 2 [11–18].

In M0 cohort, all patients underwent definitive IMRT. Target volumes were delineated in accordance with our institutional treatment protocol, as well as the International Commission on Radiation Units and Measurements Reports 50 and 62 [19, 20]. The prescribed doses were 66–72 Gy in 28–33 fractions to the primary gross tumor volume, 64–70 Gy in 28–33 fractions to volume of positive lymph nodes, 60–63 Gy in 28–33 fractions to high-risk clinical target volume, and 54–56 Gy in 28–33 fractions to low-risk clinical target volume. Treatment was delivered once daily, five days per week. Complementing radiotherapy, multimodal systemic therapy was administered to over 95% of M0 patients, encompassing IC, CCRT, and/or adjuvant chemotherapy (AC). Specific regimens of IC and CCRT are provided in Table S1 of Additional file 1.

In de novo metastatic patients, local therapy was incorporated after at least two cycles of systemic therapy. The prescribed dose for locoregional radiotherapy (LRRT) targeting the primary tumor and neck nodes ranged from 54 to 72 Gy. Local consolidation therapy (LCT) for metastatic deposits (e.g., in hepatic, pulmonary, osseous, or other sites) constituted interventions such as palliative radiotherapy, surgical resection, ablation, and other focal modalities. For a subset of patients who achieved a marked or complete response after comprehensive treatment, maintenance therapy (MT), defined as the prolonged administration of a relatively low‑intensity, well-tolerated treatment following initial response to sustain disease control and delay recurrence, was administered.

Follow-up and outcome

Patients underwent a systematic follow-up schedule: approximately every 3 months for the first 2 years, every 6 months for the subsequent 3 to 5 years, and annually thereafter until death or loss to follow-up. The clinical endpoints of locoregional relapse, distant metastasis, or disease progression were corroborated by a comprehensive assessment that included symptomatic evaluation, serological profiles, and diagnostic imaging (utilizing computed tomography, MRI, whole-body bone scintigraphy, or PET-CT, either alone or in combination). Histopathological confirmation via biopsy was obtained whenever clinically warranted.

The primary endpoint was overall survival (OS), measured as the time from the first treatment to death from any cause or patient censoring at the final follow-up. The secondary endpoints were disease-free survival (DFS) for M0 patients or progression-free survival (PFS) for M1 patients, defined as the duration between the initial treatment and local or distant relapse, the first documented disease progression, death from any cause, or censoring at the final follow-up.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and graphical visualizations were conducted using SPSS (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), R studio (a language and environment for statistical computing; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; https://www.R-project.org/), GraphPad Prism 10.1.2, and OriginPro 2024 (64bit) SR1. Continuous variables were summarized as medians (with interquartile range [IQR]) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as percentages and compared using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Time-to-event endpoints were evaluated with Kaplan-Meier methodology, and between-group differences were assessed with the log-rank test. Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to ascertain independent prognostic factors, with outcomes expressed as hazard ratios (HR) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). The proportional hazards (PH) assumption of the Cox regression model was assessed by testing the correlation between Schoenfeld residuals and survival time. A global test and separate tests for each covariate were performed. For covariates that violated the PH assumption (p < 0.05) were estimated using robust standard errors (sandwich estimator), which provide valid inference in the presence of non‑proportional hazards [21]. To address potential confounding, propensity score matching (PSM) at 1:1 or 1:M ratios and inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) were employed. For additional details, see the Additional file 2 [22–26]. Statistical significance was defined by a two-sided P-value of less than 0.05.

The population was predominantly male (72.3%; n = 4376), with a median age of 46 years (IQR: 38–54). Detailed demographic and clinical characteristics for the entire cohort and individual centers are presented in Table 1. With a median follow-up duration of 70.50 months, the corresponding 5-year OS and DFS rates were 87.0% and 79.3%, respectively.

Optimal treatment strategies for non-metastatic NPC

Guided by TNM-9 risk stratification, we investigated risk-adapted treatment strategies [2, 4, 27]. The key question for low- and intermediate-risk patients was the incremental benefit of CCRT over IMRT alone. Using PSM, 104 matched pairs of stage IA (low-risk) patients were generated (1:1; matched for age, sex, and T category) (Additional file 1: Table S2). For the intermediate-risk group, patients were matched 1:4 (age, sex, and T&N category) due to the high baseline CCRT use (Additional file 1: Table S3). CCRT provided no significant benefit over IMRT alone in low-risk patients (5-year OS: 98.0% vs. 99.0%, P = 0.562; 5-year DFS: 94.2% vs. 94.2%, P = 0.992) (Fig. 1A–B and Fig. S2A of Additional file 1). By contrast, intermediate-risk patients receiving CCRT exhibited significantly improved survival compared to those receiving IMRT alone (5-year OS: 91.0% vs. 97.9%, P = 0.001; 5-year DFS: 82.1% vs. 91.5%, P = 0.029; Fig. 1C–D). Multivariable analysis confirmed CCRT as an independent favorable factor specifically for intermediate-risk patients (HROS: 0.18, 95% CI: 0.05–0.60, P = 0.005; HRDFS: 0.45, 95% CI: 0.21–0.96, P = 0.040; Additional file 1: Fig. S2B), establishing a differential treatment effect based on risk stratification.

Kaplan–Meier curves of OS and DFS for patients in M0 cohort after PSM. A–B Low risk group (stage IA); C–D Intermediate risk group (stage IB and T3N0); E–F. High-intermediate risk (T3N1); G–H. High risk (T1-3N2 and stage III)

We next assessed whether the addition of induction IC improved survival in locoregionally advanced NPC (LA-NPC), which were categorized as stage II and III excluding T3N0. Nevertheless, survival analysis (Additional file 1: Fig. S3A–B) and multivariate Cox regression model (Additional file 1: Fig. S3C–D) demonstrated that IC did not significantly improve outcomes in this overall population. Given that the T3N1 subgroup constitutes a considerable proportion with documented heterogeneity in our previous study [11], we designated this subgroup as the intermediate-high-risk group within the LA-NPC cohort, classifying all remaining patients as the high-risk group. Propensity score matching was performed separately within each subgroup at ratios of 1:1 and 1:2, respectively (Table S4 and Table S5 of Additional file 1). In the high-intermediate-risk group, no significant survival benefit was observed with IC (HROS: 1.23, 95% CI: 0.80–2.06, P = 0.309; HRDFS: 1.11, 95% CI: 0.76–1.60, P = 0.591) (Fig. 1E–F and Fig. S2C of Additional file 1). High-risk patients who received IC followed by CCRT (± AC) experienced significantly improved 5-year overall survival compared to those who did not (83.9% vs. 78.6%, P = 0.003; Fig. 1G–H), despite the former group having less favorable baseline characteristics even after matching. This survival advantage was further confirmed on multivariable Cox regression model, which indicated that IC was independently associated with superior OS and DFS in the high-risk subgroup (HROS: 0.75, 95% CI: 0.62–0.91, P = 0.003; HRDFS: 0.84, 95% CI: 0.72–0.99, P = 0.035; Additional file 1: Fig. S2D).

Treatment outcomes in patients reclassified under TNM-9 staging system

Of the 6049 non-metastatic NPC patients, 121 individuals were reclassified from T3 to T4 and 182 (94 with N1 and 88 with N2 disease under TNM-8) were upstaged to N3 according to TNM-9 criteria, respectively, resulting in a more advanced overall stage (Fig. 4A–C). Figure 4D illustrated the migration patterns of T and N stage groupings between the TNM-8 and TNM-9 staging systems for all M0 patients. Overall, 301 patients had an upward migration of either T or N category from TNM‑8 to TNM‑9. Among these, 271 were administered a combination treatment regimen involving at least CCRT (Table S6 of Additional file 1 and Fig. 4E). However, the subgroups of patients who were upstaged from T1–3N1 to T1–3N3 (n = 74) and from T3N0–1 to T4N0–1 (n = 71) exhibited a more substantial change in overall stage (Fig. 4E). Exploring therapeutic approaches in this subset is thus of higher clinical relevance. Survival analysis demonstrated that the use of IC followed by CCRT (± AC) was associated with significantly improved outcomes in the whole patients with changed classification (HROS=0.46, 95%CI: 0.28–0.75, P = 0.001; HRDFS=0.64, 95%CI: 0.42–0.97, P = 0.035; Fig. 4F–G). Notably, in the patients who were upstaged from N1 to N3 with T1-3 category—now largely classified as more advanced disease under TNM-9, IC showed a significant improvement effect on prognosis (5-year OS: 89.4% vs. 60.3%, P < 0.001; 5-year DFS: 72.3% vs. 40.5%, P = 0.004; Fig. 4H–I). However, the same results were not observed in the group reclassified from T3 to T4 with N0-1 of TNM-9 (Fig. 4J–K).

Patient characteristics of M1 cohort

We identified 127 patients with newly diagnosed metastatic NPC from SYSUCC (2016–2017) who met the eligibility criteria (Fig. S1 of Additional file 1 and Table 3). Table 3 shows the baseline characteristics of them. According to TNM-9, 64 and 63 patients were classified as M1a and M1b, respectively. Compared to the M1a subgroup, M1b patients presented with more advanced nodal disease (N2-3: 93.7% vs. 81.3%) and a higher incidence of multi-organ metastasis (49.2% vs. 6.4%). While M1b patients generally received more intensive palliative chemotherapy, they were less likely to undergo LRRT (54.0% vs. 79.7%) or LCT for metastases (15.9% vs. 26.6%). A comprehensive breakdown of LCT modalities (radiotherapy, surgery, ablation) is available in Table S7 of Additional file 1.

Treatment intensification in de novo metastatic NPC

Given the central role of systemic therapy in metastatic NPC [28–30], we evaluated the additional benefit of LRRT, MT, and LCT in the M1 cohort. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the various treatment methods are summarized in Table S9 of Additional file 1. Among the 127 eligible M1 patients, there were 85 patients treated with LRRT, of whom showed longer median OS (62.40 vs. 19.07 months, P < 0.001) and PFS time (33.33 vs. 13.10 months, P < 0.001; Fig. 5A–B). Similarly, patients who received MT experienced superior 5-year OS (63.0% vs. 33.6%, P = 0.005) and PFS (46.4% vs. 21.9%, P = 0.016; Fig. 5C–D) compared to those who did not. In contrast, LCT directed at metastatic sites was not associated with a statistically significant improvement in survival (HROS=0.74, 95% CI: 0.44–1.23, P = 0.242; HRPFS = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.42–1.16, P = 0.161), though a non-significant trend toward prolonged median OS (63.73 vs. 47.73 months) and PFS (33.33 vs. 19.00 months) was observed (Fig. 5E–F). Multivariate Cox analysis further confirmed LRRT and MT were associated with better OS and PFS (Fig. 5G–H).

The survival benefit associated with LRRT and MT was consistently observed across both M1a and M1b subgroups (Additional file 1: Fig. S4A–H). Notably, an observational trend toward longer OS and PFS with LCT was observed only in the M1a subgroup (median OS: 88.97 vs. 62.40 months, P = 0.122; median PFS: 45.40 vs. 30.93 months, P = 0.171; Additional file 1: Fig. S4I–J). No such trend was observed in M1b disease (Additional file 1: Fig. S4K–L), suggesting that the therapeutic value of LCT is hypothesis‑generating and warrant further investigation. We further performed subgroup analyses to investigate the effect of LCT. As shown in Fig. 5I–J, the effect of LCT appeared to differ according to the number of metastatic organs (interaction P values for OS and PFS: 0.008 and 0.018, respectively). Additionally, there were some suggestive trends toward potential interactions with respect to M classification and the presence of liver metastasis. Taken together, these findings raise the possibility that LCT might be more beneficial for NPC patients with a lower metastatic burden, although this remains to be confirmed.

Discussion

The adoption of the TNM-9 staging system for NPC in 2025 represents a substantial revision with direct implications for clinical management [12]. The resultant uncertainty regarding risk-adapted treatment, especially for reclassified patients, formed the rationale for our investigation. In this large, multicenter study, we stratified patients into five distinct risk subgroups: low risk (stage IA), intermediate risk (stage IB and T3N0), high-intermediate risk (T3N1), high risk (T1-3N2 and stage III), and de novo metastatic, based on the TNM-9 criteria [11, 12]. We systematically assessed the applicability of established treatment paradigms within each subgroup and sought to define optimal therapeutic approaches for patients affected by staging migration.

Our findings provide clarity to a long-debated area in NPC management. We establish that IMRT alone is sufficient for stage IA (T1-2N0), but that CCRT is essential for the updated intermediate-risk group (stage IB and T3N0). Regardless of whether it is the eighth or ninth staging of the disease, T1N0 was undoubtedly treated with radiotherapy alone [2]. The management of tumors now classified as T1-2N1 and T2N0 under previous systems has been historically contentious. While an early phase III trial demonstrated a benefit for CCRT over two-dimensional radiotherapy [31], subsequent studies yielded conflicting results [32–34]. Notably, a recent phase III RCT by Tang et al. found IMRT alone to be non-inferior to CCRT in selected intermediate-risk patients lacking poor prognostic features(all lymph nodes < 3 cm, no radiologic ENE, negative level IV/VB lymph nodes, and cell-free Epstein-Barr virus DNA [cfEBV DNA] load < 4000 copies/mL) [5, 35–39]. It is crucial to contextualize these prior trials: the patient populations defined as “intermediate-risk” in the era of the 7th or even earlier versions of staging are not equivalent to the group defined by TNM-9. The TNM-9 system has rationally consolidated the relatively low-risk T2N0 subgroup with T1N0 into stage IA, as these tumors are unlikely to harbor high-risk features (e.g., lymph nodes > 3 cm, ENE, or high cfEBV DNA load). It is reasonable for patients in stage IA to receive only definitive IMRT, which is a highly precise and low-toxicity treatment method. By reclassifying the favorable T2N0 subgroup to stage IA, the current intermediate-risk group is inherently enriched with patients more likely to possess high-risk features. This is further compounded by the downgrading of adjacent soft tissue invasion (medial pterygoid, lateral pterygoid, prevertebral muscles) from T4 to T2 since TNM-8 [3], a feature often associated with greater tumor burden and a potential need for more intensive therapy. Within this context, our results robustly demonstrate that CCRT provides superior outcomes compared to IMRT alone for these patients.

The current treatment paradigm for high-risk LA-NPC, comprising IC followed by CCRT ± metronomic adjuvant therapy, was reaffirmed in our TNM-9 high-risk cohort [9, 10]. This consistency is expected, as a small portion of patients with advanced ENE (clear extension into adjacent structures such as muscle, skin, or the neurovascular bundle) [18], unequivocal radiological involvement of cranial nerves, or inferior orbital fissure extension were upstaged to stage III under the framework of TNM-9; the high-risk disease composition is fundamentally preserved [11, 12]. Thus, prior therapeutic conclusions remain applicable to our high-risk patients. A notable finding concerns the subsets of patients (n = 301, 5.0%) reclassified from N1-2 to N3 or T3 to T4 category due to advanced ENE and newly documented radiological involvement of cranial nerves or inferior orbital fissure based on central review. These patients unequivocally warrant similarly intensive chemoradiotherapy to achieve curative intent. From a clinical standpoint, the most consequential change should be the upstaging of T1-3N1 (n = 74, 1.2%) and T3N0-1 (n = 71, 1.2%) disease into stage III, which directly impacts risk stratification and therapeutic strategy. In this specific subgroup, IC provided positive efficacy only for those upstaged from N1 to N3. This may not be difficult to understand: since distant failure which is more likely to be driven by ENE is a principal cause of death in NPC [35, 40, 41], the intensified chemoradiotherapy that these patients received aligns with biological rationale. While this analysis represents a major strength of our study, the sample size of patients upgraded from T3 to T4 was relatively small (n = 121). This limited sample size may have resulted in insufficient statistical power to detect a significant survival benefit from IC in this specific subgroup, as shown in Fig. 4J–K. Therefore, the lack of statistical significance should not be interpreted as evidence of no treatment effect; rather, it highlights the need for larger, preferably prospective, studies to definitively evaluate the role of IC in T3‑to‑T4 upstaged patients.

It is noteworthy that in the current study, stage II (excluding T3N0) and stage III were classified as LA-NPC. However, neither survival analysis nor multivariate Cox regression model provided definitive evidence of a prognostic benefit from IC in this overall cohort. We hypothesized that the inclusion of a substantial proportion of relatively low-risk T3N1 cases may have obscured the potential benefit for the whole LA-NPC [42]. Consequently, we reclassified these T3N1 patients by extracting them from the high-risk group and establishing a distinct intermediate-high-risk category. The outcome became readily apparent: the survival benefit of IC was primarily driven by patients with T1-3N2 and stage III disease, whereas T3N1 subgroup derived no significant advantage. This heterogeneity, long recognized but seldom addressed in prior trials [8–11, 43], is now being clarified by prognostic risk-stratification studies. Du et al. and Li et al. proposed that T3 disease with early skull-base invasion (ESBI)—limited to the pterygoid process and/or sphenoid bone base—constitutes a favorable subgroup that may be more appropriately classified as T2 and does not benefit from IC [11, 44, 45]. Furthermore, Zhu et al. defined “severe skull base foramen invasion” (SBFI) beyond the foramen lacerum or pterygopalatine fossa as a high-risk feature within T3, with outcomes comparable to T4 disease and potential benefit from IC [46]. These findings underscore the need for finer anatomical risk stratification within the T3 category to better guide therapy and inform future staging revisions.

We further evaluated treatment strategies for metastatic NPC within the context of the TNM-9 system, which subclassifies M1 disease into M1a (≤ 3 lesions) and M1b (> 3 lesions) [11, 12]. Our investigation affirms the survival benefit of combining systemic therapy with either LRRT or MT across all M1 patients, reinforcing their role as integral components of a multimodal approach [47–50]. The utility of local therapy appears to be stage-dependent: LCT for metastatic sites showed promise only in the lower-burden M1a subgroup. This observation underscores a fundamental principle of metastatic disease management: the therapeutic window for local interventions narrows with increasing disease burden. In particular, M1b patients typically have more widespread metastases and should prioritize whole-body systemic therapy. Since other factors, including the specific involvement of the liver or multiple organs, are decisive for prognosis [51, 52], the existing classification thus lacks sufficient discriminative power to guide nuanced treatment decisions, particularly regarding local intervention. Thus, we advocate for future iterations of the staging system to integrate these additional risk modifiers, creating a more robust framework for personalizing treatment in metastatic NPC. However, it should be emphasized that our recommendations regarding LRRT, MT and LCT for metastatic NPC are derived from a treatment era prior to the widespread adoption of immune checkpoint inhibitors. Currently, the combination of chemotherapy with PD-1 blockade has become the standard first‑line therapy for recurrent or metastatic NPC, based on landmark trials such as JUPITER‑02, RATIONALE‑309, and CAPTAIN‑1st [28, 30, 53]. The addition of immunotherapy significantly prolongs PFS and OS, and may alter the biological behavior of metastatic lesions as well as the host immune response. Effective systemic chemo‑immunotherapy could potentially downstage or even eradicate oligometastatic disease, possibly reducing the need for immediate local therapy. On this condition, LRRT or LCT may still play a role in eradicating radioresistant clones or relieving symptoms, but its timing might be deferred until after an initial course of systemic therapy to assess response. Consequently, the necessity, optimal timing, and potential added value of LRRT or LCT in the M1 setting warrant re‑evaluation.

Despite the comprehensive nature of our analysis, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, this study did not investigate the impact of plasma cfEBV DNA levels, a well-established non-anatomical biomarker critical for diagnosis, risk stratification, and prognostication in NPC, on treatment decision-making. Longitudinal monitoring of cfEBV DNA dynamics via liquid biopsy enables the identification of distinct risk subgroups and provides real‑time information on recurrence risk [54, 55], thereby supporting a risk-adaptive paradigm for clinical decision‑making [56]. Furthermore, integrating dynamic cfEBV DNA measurements with longitudinal MRI data obtained during treatment and follow‑up can enhance the precision of risk stratification and increase the flexibility of treatment strategies [57, 58]. Moving forward, there is an urgent need to standardize cfEBV DNA testing for incorporation into the conventional TNM staging system. We next aim to develop a novel staging system for NPC using MRI after IC, enabling more precise management of the disease. Second, with immune checkpoint inhibitors now improving outcomes in recurrent or metastatic NPC and being increasingly incorporated into the management of non-metastatic disease [59–61], the absence of immunotherapy data in our cohort may limit the contemporary generalizability of our findings. In the immunotherapy era, the combination of radiotherapy with immunotherapy may yield synergistic antitumor effects through immunogenic cell death and modulation of the tumor microenvironment [62], suggesting that IMRT could be strategically integrated to enhance systemic responses. Furthermore, the tumor immune microenvironment, particularly the presence of tertiary lymphoid structures, has been associated with favorable responses to immunotherapy and improved survival in multiple solid tumors, including NPC [63, 64]. Integrating these dynamic and biological indicators with TNM‑9 staging could significantly enhance the precision of risk stratification and inform personalized de‑escalation or intensification of therapy. Finally, as a retrospective study conducted in endemic regions, our findings may be subject to selection bias, exacerbated by the multi-center design with imbalanced patient contributions and the relatively small sample size of the M1 cohort. Future prospective cohort studies are warranted to validate such multi‑dimensional prediction models and to establish their clinical utility in guiding immunotherapy and combined modality treatment for NPC.

Conclusions

In summary, this study provides evidence-based treatment guidance for the TNM-9 staging era: curative-intent radiotherapy alone is sufficient for stage IA disease, while CCRT is necessary for stage IB and T3N0. For high-risk LA-NPC (stage II to III, excluding T3N0-1), a high-intensity regimen of IC followed by CCRT with or without AC constitutes the optimal strategy, a recommendation that holds even for patients reclassified under the new staging system. The standard treatment for T3N1 disease remains undefined and warrants further exploration. In M1 disease, the integration of LRRT and MT with systemic treatment significantly improves survival in both M1a and M1b patients, whereas LCT for metastases showed a potential signal of efficacy that appeared to be predominantly restricted to the M1a subgroup. These findings offer a roadmap for clinical decision-making while underscoring the need for further refinement of risk stratification.
