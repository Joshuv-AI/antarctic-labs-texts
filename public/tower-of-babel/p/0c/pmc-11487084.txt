Modelling the spatial variability and uncertainty for under-vaccination and zero-dose children in fragile settings
Authors: Victor A. Alegana, Johnson M. Ticha, Jason M. Mwenda, Reggis Katsande, Marta Gacic-Dobo, Maria Carolina Danovaro-Holliday, Charles W. Shey, Kalu A. Akpaka, Lawrence N. Kazembe, Benido Impouma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Universal access to childhood vaccination is important to child health and sustainable development. Here we identify, at a fine spatial scale, under-immunized children and zero-dose children. Using Chad, as an example, the most recent nationally representative household survey that included recommended vaccine antigens was assembled. Age-disaggregated population (12–23 months) and vaccination coverage were modelled at a fine spatial resolution scale (1km × 1 km) using a Bayesian geostatistical framework adjusting for a set of parsimonious covariates. There was a variation at fine spatial scale in the population 12–23 months a national mean of 18.6% (CrI 15.8%–22.6%) with the highest proportion in the South-East district of Laremanaye 20.0% (14.8–25.0). Modelled coverage at birth was 49.0% (31.2%–75.3%) for BCG, 44.8% (27.1–74.3) for DTP1, 24.7% (12.5–46.3) for DTP3 and 47.0% (30.6–71.0) for measles (MCV1). Combining coverage estimates with the modelled population at a fine spatial scale yielded 312,723 (Lower estimate 156055–409266) zero-dose children based on DTP1. Improving routine immunization will require investment in the health system as part of enhancing primary health care. The uncertainties in our estimates highlight areas that require further investigation and higher quality data to gain a better understanding of vaccination coverage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74982-5.

Introduction

Childhood immunization is an important intervention that helps prevent an estimated 2.5 million child deaths1,2. It is also an important sustainable developmental goal (SDG) 3.b indicator of the proportion of children covered by all vaccines in their national programme3. Gavi 5.0 strategy, which is aligned with SDGs and with the Immunization Agenda 2030 (IA2030)1,4, outlines “leaving no one behind with immunization” by reaching zero-dose and missed communities with an equity goal formulated as health systems reaching all zero-dose and under-immunized children with the full range of vaccines as the first step towards providing integrated Primary Health Care (PHC) services. Under-vaccination and zero-dose (defined as children 12–23 Children 12 to 23 months that have not received any doses of each of the following vaccines as per the most recent nationally representative survey: Bacille Calmette-Guérin (BCG), polio, pentavalent, and measles-containing vaccines (MCV)5) results from a combination of several demand and supply side factors of the health system including access6,7. Moreover, zero-dose children are less likely to receive other recommended vaccines and are an important global health agenda8,9. It is important to understand the prevalence of zero-dose and under-immunization in fragile settings, which are vulnerable to crises, conflict or violence, and natural disasters. Without vaccination, children are at risk of many infectious diseases contributing to high levels of mortality in Africa10–12.

In fragile settings, violence, political instability or natural disasters constitute some of the drivers impacting the demand and supply of vaccines to reach the most vulnerable populations13. Conflict and violence impact access to health services and overall service delivery. In addition, health systems in these settings are chronically underfunded and are often sustained largely by international development assistance14,15. Together with low domestic spending on health and suboptimal infrastructure and economic development, these settings require concerted efforts are needed to identify and reach zero-dose and marginalised communities. Identifying children or communities under-immunized at a very fine spatial scale is beneficial to tailoring or targeting vaccination appropriately and maximizing impact in physically marginalised communities16. To accurately map vaccine coverage at a fine scale, it’s important to have equally detailed estimates of the population at matching spatial resolution to count the number of children who haven’t received any vaccinations. Traditional population data, such as the census, is available at coarse spatial resolution, in five-year age groups. The national average obscures sub-national or fine-scale population variations17. In fragile settings, population mobility or demographics could further impact previous census counts and coverage18. Fine-scale mapping of population coverage by age will improve the precision of age-specific zero-dose mapping17. This can enhance the understanding of geographic inequalities as previously conducted in a few African countries19,20. Such estimates can then be used to improve the estimation of age-specific morbidity or mortality.

Here, statistical modelling is used to predict the likelihood of vaccination at a fine-spatial scale (1 × 1 km) using a hierarchical Bayesian framework, the age-specific proportion of the population of children 12–23 months, and associated uncertainty (exceedance and non-exceedance probability) from the most recent geolocated nationally representative household survey in Chad. The products from modelling were then used to quantify patterns of zero-dose and under-immunization in children 12–23 months. The modelling framework adjusted for contextual effects.

Methods

Study area and rationale for selection

Chad is a vast Sudanian-Saharan country in the heart of Africa with a land surface area of 1,284,000 km2. It is bordered by Libya to the north, Sudan to the east, Niger, Nigeria and Cameroon to the west, and the Central African Republic to the south. The last population census in Chad was conducted in 2009 and estimates based on national projections stood at 17.9 million in 2022, of which 18% were under the age of five. The country is classified as being affected by fragile and conflict situations (FCS). The Uppsala Peace Index has documented ongoing multiple yearly violent conflicts since the 1970s21. The 2019 UN Global Peace Index which ranked 163 independent states and territories according to their level of peacefulness, placed Chad at 136 out of 163, with a very low peacefulness score of 2.59122. This worsened further in the 2023 update to a score of 2.699. By 2022, the national immunization programme introduced BCG (Baccille Calmette Guérin at birth), DTP- Hepatitis B- Hib (Haemophilus influenzae type B) vaccine (at 6, 10 and 14 weeks), Oral polio vaccine at birth (OPV) and three doses at 6, 10 and 14 weeks) and IPV (Inactivated polio vaccines), Td (Tetanus toxoid at birth, 6 months), Measles-containing vaccines (1 and 2nd dose), Meningococcal A conjugate vaccine (at 9 months), and YF (Yellow fever) vaccines. In Chad, according to the WHO recommendations adopted by the EPI, a child is fully vaccinated after receiving BCG, three doses of DTP-HepB-Hib, three doses of polio vaccine, measles vaccine, yellow fever vaccine, and a dose of polio vaccine at birth, all before the age of 12 months.

Data sources

Nationally representative survey data on vaccination

Data were obtained from the most recent nationally representative and geo-located DHS23. The DHS is a multi-stage and stratified cluster-sampled survey that employs a structured interview approach for women 15 to 49 years of age. DHS clusters are selected at the first stage followed by a random selection of households within each cluster. Data collected within DHS not only include the general health of children but also their vaccination status. Basic vaccination status for children between 12–23 months was extracted on BCG, OPV, the doses of DTP and MCV. For each vaccine antigen, the proportion of children vaccinated was extracted at each geolocated cluster adjusting for the sampling design. Only the most recent geo-located survey data was used for prediction.

Data for estimating accessibility to health facilities providing vaccination services

Accessibility to the nearest vaccination centres (health facilities) was modelled based on travel time. A most recent geo-reference health facility database for Chad was used24 and updated with online data sources available at the Humanitarian Data Exchange (HDX) portal https://data.humdata.org/. Health facilities in Chad were either classified as hospitals or health centers which also served as primary care facilities. Information on whether a health facility was a vaccination centre, or not, was not available, and therefore all health facilities were used in the analysis. A road spatial dataset was assembled from the OpenStreetMap (OSM, 2017). In this analysis, only roads classified as primary (or trunk roads) and secondary roads were used. Lower-tier class roads were excluded due to uncertainty in mapping all tertiary roads, streets, link roads and footpaths at a national level in Chad. A land cover map from Medium Resolution Imaging Spectrometer (MERIS) GlobCover product (http://ionia1.esrin.esa.int/)25 at a 300-m spatial resolution for SSA was used to demarcate the land use or land cover classifications. Digital elevation data (topography) was downloaded from the NASA’s Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM) (https://www2.jpl.nasa.gov/srtm/). SRTM has a spatial resolution of 90 m approximately at the equator and is archived using 5° by 5° tiles.

Population denominators

Fine-resolution population maps are important denominators in the estimation of the number of children under-vaccinated26. Maps of fines scale population distribution were obtained from WorldPop (www.worldpop.org) but were not available for 12–23 months children. The Worldpop methodology for modelling population at a fine spatial scale is described elsewhere and is adjusted to match UN population estimates27. The methodology combines data from various population and housing censuses and human settlements using a random forest approach to generate a gridded prediction of population density at 1 × 1 km spatial resolution approximately27,28. Here we combined this fine-spatial resolution with the modelled proportion of children 12–23 months to obtain actual population numbers at fine-spatial resolution. A Bayesian hierarchical spatial model was implemented in R-INLA29–31 to produce a continuous map of the proportion of the population 12–23 months at 1 × 1 km spatial resolution using the cluster locations (longitude and latitude), and selected covariate for population. More details of this population modelling methodology are available in Supplementary Information 1 (SI 1).

Modelling travel times to health facilities

By combining land cover (GlobCover), elevation, and roads data in AccessMod version 5 software a raster surface of travel times to the health facilities (n = 468), was generated32. The WHO-developed AccessMod tool has been used in many studies previously32–35. First, 23 classifications for broad land use or land cover in GlobCover were reduced to 6 major classes of crop cover, forest, grassland, urban areas, bare ground, and permanent water or snow. Secondly, a secondary land cover map was created by combining the land use or land cover classification of major roads. A combined walking and motorised travel, as well as elevation, was included. Travel speed (impedance) was then assigned to the 7 resulting classes (80 kms per hour (kmh−1)) for a major road with motorised travel, 4 kmh−1 with walking correction on vegetation patches, and for desert landscapes, a 2 kmh-1 speed was used)32. The methodology for deriving travel time in each class, excluding the motorised roads class, included a slope correction derived from elevation data with travel speeds calculated for each degree rise of slope based on Tobler’s equation (V = 6*exp(-3.5abs[Tan(slope in degrees/57.296) + 0.05])36 where V is the calculated speed. Hence, on flat terrain, the walking pace was approximately 5.0kmh−1 while for a 20° rise in slope, the pace is lower (1.4 kmh-1). Gridded travel times to major hospitals, health centres and to medical clinics or dispensaries were derived separately and considered in the covariate selection procedure for the prediction of vaccination coverage.

Selection of geospatial covariates for prediction of vaccination coverage and population-age at fine scale

Covariates are useful in the predictive modelling of variables of interest i.e., vaccination coverage17,19,20,37. A parsimonious set of covariates was selected from candidate lists consisting of land surface temperature, precipitation, conflict, and the three gridded travel time to health facilities (major hospitals, health centres and clinics or medical centres) modelled from a combination of roads, landcover and topography. Conflict data version 2022 was obtained from the Armed Conflict Location & Event Data Project (ACLED) (www.acleddata.com). These data were geolocated in space since conflicts were focalised in smaller localities and could impact access to vaccination at a fine spatial scale38. Only conservative definitions of reported violence areas with more than 5 deaths were included as major conflict areas for 2013–15 period and kernel density method was applied to derive a density map in ArcGIS 10.8 (ESRI, Redlands). The rate of annual precipitation data and temperature were obtained from the WorldClim bioclimatic dataset39,40.

All contextual suites of covariates were considered for both coverage and population mapping. In bothe cases a selection procedure was implemented to select a parsimonious set of covariates that were important predictors of vaccination coverage and population prevalence, respectively. This was computed using non-spatial generalised linear regression models implemented in the bestglm package in R41. Only statistically important covariates selected based on this procedure were then used in subsequent modelling.

Bayesian geostatistical modelling of vaccination coverage and population age 12–23 months

A hierarchical Bayesian model-based geostatistical (MBG) method42 was used to spatially interpolate the adjusted (sampling design) cluster-level data for vaccination coverage and mean proportion of the population aged 12–23 months, independently. For coverage, five main outcomes were analysed on coverage, all based on the extracted data on BCG, OPV, DTP and MCV. The WHO currently recommends that the BCG vaccine should be given at birth, Polio at birth (OPV0) in addition to the three doses of polio (OPV1-3)and DTP(1–3) should be given at 6, 10 and 14 weeks, and the measles vaccine should be given at 9 months43.

The modelling framework was applied to the outcome variables and prediction aided by the selected set of gridded covariates to compute the mean at 1 × 1 km17. Estimation of uncertainty via exceedance probability

Understanding of vaccination coverage at fine spatial resolution requires evaluating of confidence in the vaccination programme stratification. The Bayesian approach allows for the prediction of uncertainty arising from a combination of several factors not limited to inadequate survey input data (suggesting further sampling needs) and/or inherent variability in pixel predictions. Here a 90% certainty in the predictions of coverage being < 40% (Non-Exceedance Probability, NEP) was used for statistical confidence in the predictions for programmatic certainty. There are currently no guidelines or existing literature that outline how to establish the threshold for vaccination programs beyond the 90% coverage target. For many other EPI programs, vaccination targets may vary depending on the specific context. For instance, identifying areas with low coverage of essential vaccines may necessitate setting lower thresholds for carrying out Periodic Intensification of Routine Immunization (PIRI) activities, based on the available funding. Extracting zero-dose and un-vaccinated children by antigen

Estimating zero-dose children is important for planning and resource allocation to the national EPI. In addition, patterns of under-vaccination at fine spatial scales could show persistent marginalised communities. Firstly, the modelled estimates of children 12–23 months were combined with a population counts map at fine spatial resolution to extract the number of children in representative at-group of 12–23 months at 1km by 1km. Secondly, the total gridded population counts were then combined with modelled mean vaccination coverage to compute the un-vaccinated total (BCG, DTP, MCV and OPV) by antigen and zero-dose based on DTP1 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$({ ZeroDose}_{i}={POP}_{i }\times \left(1-{DPT}_{1i}\right))$$\end{document}. Here counts at a fine scale were then aggregated at the second administrative level (admin 2 or districts) to compare the variation of zero-dose children for each antigen.

Discussion

This study modelled heterogeneities in vaccine coverage, age-structured distribution of population, and the associated uncertainty. It used these estimates to quantify the number of children in Chad who did not receive various doses (BCG, DTP1-3, OPV0,1,2,3 and MCV1). A variation at a fine-scale spatial at sub-national levels was observed. A single national average estimate of coverage or children by age masks the subnational heterogeneities in population and coverage with uncertainty in later overlapping across sub-national borders. There was a marked dropout between DTP1 and DTP 3 of 20%. The estimated national average number of zero-dose children based on DTP1 was 312,723 but this varied by district and by region. Using probabilistic estimation for each antigen highlighted differences in the uncertainty of estimates, emphasising the need for better sampling in these fragile settings.

In IA2030 and Gavi 5.0, achieving equity in immunization coverage is a top priority. The goal is to ensure all children receive all the recommended vaccines. In Chad, out of the 101 districts, 13 districts mostly from Mandoul and Mayo Kebbi Est and Ouest, had immunization coverage greater than 70% for BCG or OPV2. An estimated 82,516 children lived in these districts of which 29.9% (24,654) were estimated as zero-dose children based on DTP1. A further 39 districts had coverage between 50 and 70% for the basic vaccines given at birth with an estimated 241,319 children 12–23 months living in these areas. Of these, 43.0% (103,831) were classified as zero-dose children. In the remaining districts with less than 50% coverage and where approximately 259,151 children of 12–23 months resided, a majority 70.0% (184,239) were classified as zero-dose children. It’s important to implement catch-up programs in areas with low coverage and to focus on reaching the last mile of children in areas with higher coverage. Older children or populations in low-coverage districts could have also potentially missed out on essential vaccines. The prevalence of zero doses in these areas could be more focalized at the community level. In urban areas such as N’Djamena, precision mapping and targeting immunization activities to underserved communities or urban slum areas could reduce the prevalence of zero-dose children. Understanding other demand and vaccine supply factors requires improved data collection and could improve vaccine tailoring and reduce uncertainty in coverage estimation.

National representative and geolocated surveys, although cross-sectional, were used in mapping coverage at a fine spatial scale. The Bayesian approach allowed for quantifying programmatic uncertainty with a threshold of 40% coverage. There is no recommended threshold for national EPI programmes and it can be adjusted based on different contexts. one could tailor this threshold in different contexts. The use of NEP has been used in other disease mapping52. Figure 4 maps can be used by EPI programmes to target vaccination in the red areas i.e., marginalised or hard-to-reach populations where at a 90% confidence level, vaccination coverage is less than 40% and the majority of children in these communities miss recommended vaccines. It is worth noting that surveys only provide a snapshot of coverage at a specific point in time. In this study, vaccines that are routinely delivered such as DTP3 had the lowest coverage suggesting an overall weakness of routine health system delivery.

Measles coverage was slightly higher at 47.0% (30.6 – 71.0). It is expected that in this setting with weak health system, SIAs could have a greater effect in reaching more children. This however does not translate to effectiveness or reduction in susceptibility or risk of transmission of measles due to the complexities in immunity and age of infection16. SIAs and other periodic Intensive immunization activities are more intensive, require careful evaluation and can still be affected by timing53. Routine data in the setting that evaluates intensive immunization programmes such as SIAs is likely to be less reliable to generate estimates of coverage or zero-dose and certainty due to issues around the estimation of the denominator resulting in administrative coverage that sometimes exceeds 100%54. Further, routine data represent only those reaching the health care system and required adjustment for access or use of immunization services to generate overall population coverage estimates55,56.

Polio, like measles, is sometimes addressed through SIAs, and previous studies have highlighted issues with data quality. For instance, there is a weak relationship between the number of SIAs conducted at the country level and the reported number of polio doses given in African countries57. In Chad, due to weak routine delivery, only a few children receive the polio vaccine at birth23, and most children receive these doses through SIAs. Caregivers report low coverage at birth, which is reflected in the polio coverage map in Fig. 2 (OPV 0). There are additional limitations related to data for the multi-dose vaccines such as polio and DTP since the vaccination record may have gaps. The DHS Program assumes that any missing vaccinations have not been given and adjusts the record accordingly. For example, if a child’s record shows only DTP2 and there is no record of DTP1, it is assumed that DTP1 has been given but not DTP2. This also applies to polio 1–3 where if a child’s record includes Polio 1 and Polio 3 but not Polio 2, it is assumed that Polio 1 and Polio 2 have been given but not Polio 3. This means that the logic is based on the number of known vaccinations given, rather than the order in which they are recorded in the vaccination history. This impact the interpretation of Fig. 2 in the sense that coverage for one of the doses may be impacted by gas in the vaccination history.

Increased sampling in these fragile settings could further be impacted by conflict, i.e., areas with ongoing instability or violence are unlikely to be sampled. Approaches used in reaching out to refugees and internally displaced individuals, under complex emergencies would provide valuable lessons that can be adopted to reach out to areas with frequent and intense exposure to conflicts58,59.

While vaccine coverage modelling shows potential for improved identification of zero children, the study had several other limitations. First, the survey data represented the only geolocated data that could lend to the methodology applied here. In other national surveys, the multiple indicator Cluster Survey (MICS) is often hampered by quality issues and is not geolocated in space. There are also some documented data quality challenges in national survey data used for the estimation of coverage60,61. Nonetheless, the lack of geographic coordinates in MICs suggests these surveys are deficient in the precision mapping of zero dose to identify marginalised populations at a fine spatial scale. In this study, independent model assumptions were adopted for each vaccine antigens. Due to limitations in the vaccination history, the assumptions of independence may not necessarily hold for multi-dose vaccines and lending to tricky interpretation of true coverage of these doses. Future studies could improve on these nuances through use of a different modelling structure. Additionally, in this article, we assessed the association of vaccination with conflict in an exploratory and covariate selection procedure for which the association was statistically weak. Reaching people in fragile and conflict-affected settings is documented12,62 but will require an expanded understanding of other population-level contextual, vaccine demand and uptake, cultural factors, socio-demographic factors and supply-side factors of vaccine delivery not considered here63–65.

Conclusion

There is a need to improve data and sampling in fragile settings to improve understanding of coverage, uncertainty, and zero dose children. These include building data acquisition processes and capacities within EPI programmes and the statistical handling of this information within a multi-layered data platform to make more effective vaccination catch-up plans. Improving routine immunization will require investment in the health system as part of improving primary health care (PHC) services. This study illustrates the importance of estimation of age-disaggregated population and vaccination coverage at a fine spatial resolution in identifying un-vaccinated children and this is beneficial to EPI programmes and child health.
