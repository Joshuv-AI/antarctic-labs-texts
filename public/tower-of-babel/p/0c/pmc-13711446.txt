A Narrative Review of Remote Biospecimen Collection Product and Implementation Considerations for Sexual Health Clinical Research
Authors: Keith J. Horvath, Sarah E. Rutstein, Ryan P. Coyle, Cheryl Jennings, Rahim Z. Miller, Justin Unternaher, Kelly Bojan, Grace M. Aldrovandi, K. Rivet Amico, Peter L. Anderson, Nadia Dowshen, Sybil Hosek, Lisa Hightow-Weidman, Mark A. Marzinke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Remote biospecimen collection (RBSC), such as home-based HIV testing and self-collection of dried blood spots (DBS) to assess adherence to antiretroviral drug regimens, has become an important tool to support clinical research because it can facilitate participant reach, lower participant burden, and reduce discomfort with attending research visits in clinic spaces. RBSC is common in sexual health clinical intervention trials to assess the impact of an intervention or program on primary and secondary outcomes. However, the products and strategies employed to successfully implement RBSC can vary widely across studies. The purpose of this narrative review is twofold. First, to provide an overview of currently available RBSC products, their attributes, and their suitability for various clinical research applications. Considerations for blood-based and volumetric absorptive microsampling collection products, as well as self-administered sexually transmitted infection testing and other specimen types, are described. Second, RBSC implementation considerations at each stage of the collection process are discussed primarily from the perspective of the authors’ collective expertise and experience with RBSC in HIV prevention and treatment clinical research. Successful RBSC product selection and implementation require a multidisciplinary team approach that is participant-centered to achieve high return rates, and ample time should be allotted to address the complexities of the procedures and processes. This review of RBSC product and implementation considerations offers an actionable framework and roadmap when considering incorporation of RBSC into clinical trials research.Trial registrationN/A.

Introduction

Remote biospecimen collection (RBSC) has become an important tool to support clinical research. The inclusion of RBSC as part of clinical research procedures offers several benefits, such as facilitating expanded participant reach by engaging participants who may not reside near a study site, minimizing participant burden for in-person visits, and reducing the stigma that some participants may feel about attending clinic or research site-based visits for specimen collection [1, 2]. Greater participant inclusion and flexibility in data collection procedures in clinical research can lead to richer and more nuanced and generalizable data to understand disease incidence, participant behaviors, and intervention effectiveness [3, 4].

In HIV treatment and prevention research, the use of RBSC for viral load monitoring and pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) adherence assessments are both feasible and highly acceptable [5, 6]. Home collection strategies for other sexually transmitted infections (STIs) are increasingly being integrated into clinical practice, thereby expanding access to sexual health screening [7, 8]. These advances complement the shift towards the use of telehealth, which accelerated during the COVID-19 pandemic [9, 10]. Despite the success of RBSC strategies for clinical care and the potential advantages for clinical research, particularly on sensitive topics such as sexual health, reports on approaches and best practices to optimize RBSC for clinical research are limited and do not largely consider the unique needs of special populations, such as persons who reside in remote regions of the United States (U.S.) or adolescents and young adults (AYA).

This narrative review draws on expertise from researchers with extensive experience in implementing RBSC in sexual health clinical research studies, specifically in HIV prevention and treatment research. The review focuses on U.S.-based implementation of RBSC in research settings which requires consideration of several factors, including the extent to which a chosen product aligns with study aims, the feasibility of participant self-collection [1], and the distinct privacy and confidentiality concerns that RBSC may introduce. Research teams must pay particular attention to kit assembly and distribution, specimen return workflows, the provision of incentives, and mechanisms for returning results to participants. The goal of this review is to provide practical suggestions to guide study teams to optimize RBSC within clinical research and serve as a starting point for developing best practices for RBSC.

Methods

We use a narrative review framework to guide the results in this paper rather than a systematic review because the data needed to assess the success of many of the RBSC procedures described in this review are embedded in intervention trials that would be difficult, if not impossible, to rigorously extract. Moreover, in comparison to a systematic review that uses a structured search and reporting process on a narrowly defined research question, a narrative review has greater latitude to review broader topics and may incorporate author expertise and experience to inform the interpretation of results [11]. This paper incorporates aspects of multiple forms of narrative reviews [12], including a narrative overview through a review of current available RBSC products and a commentary on the collective experience of the authors in implementing RBSC procedures. For this narrative review, we draw on over a decade of authors’ experiences with RBSC as study principal investigators, laboratory directors, clinical research coordinators, healthcare providers, and community youth representatives in primarily HIV prevention and treatment research and interventions with AYA. In addition, we conducted targeted literature reviews and reviewed study and laboratory protocols to inform this review.

Broadly, we divide this narrative review into two primary sections. First, we review currently available RBSC products and considerations for the use of those products to inform product selection, with a particular focus on microsampling devices and other minimally invasive sampling strategies used in infectious disease research, including assessment of anti-infective drug adherence, HIV-1 RNA (viral load), and STIs. Next, we provide a review of RBSC implementation considerations in a largely chronological format (i.e., from what needs to be established before sample collection kits are sent out to what happens after the participant successfully returns a sample collection kit) that relies primarily on the authors’ collective expertise and experience. Since our goal is to provide a roadmap for study teams to optimize RBSC integration into clinical research, we do not focus here on remote sampling for the purposes of clinical care (e.g., RBSC via phlebotomy services for clinical testing). Although we focus on topics most relevant to conducting RBSC in HIV prevention and treatment research and intervention given the authors’ expertise, many of the same product and implementation considerations will be relevant to other health research areas and settings.

RBSC Product/Application Considerations

Background

Infectious diseases and public health home testing technologies for pregnancy, HIV, and COVID-19 have significantly enhanced health management by providing patients with convenient, confidential, and rapid access to health information [13]. These tests require regulatory approval and characterization of analytical and clinical parameters. Upon approval by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), these tests may be considered FDA-cleared for at-home use. Since their initial approval in 1976, advancements in home-based pregnancy tests now enable detection of human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) in urine with high sensitivity, allowing for pregnancy detection within days of conception [14]. At-home HIV testing kits, like the OraQuick® self-test, became available in the early 2000s, enabling users to obtain results privately within 20 min [15]. These kits, used in both clinical and research settings, have been shown to increase testing rates, particularly among populations that may avoid medical facilities due to structural barriers [16-18]. By facilitating earlier diagnosis of HIV, home testing has contributed to earlier treatment initiation and greater linkage to care or PrEP services [19]. The SARS-CoV-2 pandemic further accelerated the development and deployment of antigen and PCR-based home testing kits to detect active infections, which proved crucial for controlling viral spread [20].

While home-based tests can facilitate telehealth and reduce barriers to participation in clinical and public health research, not all testing can be performed remotely; some tests still require analysis in centralized or specialized laboratories [21]. Recently, there has been an increased focus on home-based or other RBSC strategies [22]. With appropriate instructions, collected biospecimens can be returned to testing facilities. Depending on the biospecimen type and application, these tests may be classified as laboratory-developed tests (LDTs). LDTs are designed, validated, and utilized within a single laboratory to meet specific medical or research needs; these tests may also encompass modifications to commercially manufactured assays (e.g., utilizing an FDA-cleared test on an alternative specimen type). Regardless of the specific RBSC product or strategy employed, close collaboration between the research team and testing laboratory is essential to ensure logistic feasibility and maintain specimen viability for post-collection analyses. RBSC devices are stratified here by specimen source and application.

Blood-Based Collection Products

Blood-based collection devices, mainly as dried blood spots (DBS), have been used to monitor HIV viral load [23] and drug resistance [24, 25], and to evaluate adherence to antiretroviral therapy (ART) or PrEP [26-29]. An overview of collection devices, including manufacturer, specimen type, sampling volume, and regulatory approval status, is summarized in Table 1.

Whole blood collected as DBS is the classic home-collected specimen and has been used extensively in research settings. This collection strategy involves the absorption of blood to cellulose or cotton-based filter paper. Among several commercially available DBS collection cards, the cotton linter-based Cytiva Whatman™ 903 Protein Saver card is FDA-cleared and is widely used in research settings. This card has been used extensively to measure intraerythrocytic drug concentrations for tenofovir-diphosphate (TFV-DP) and emtricitabine-triphosphate (FTC-TP), as well as to estimate plasma concentrations of cabotegravir (CAB) and rilpivirine (RPV) [26-29]. However, while the collection device itself is FDA-cleared, its test-specific applications, such as viral load testing or drug measurements, may be classified as LDTs. Blood may be collected from venipuncture or, more commonly, as capillary blood via fingerstick. For some analytes (i.e., the substances being measured in a given biospecimen), capillary and venous blood are not interchangeable.

DBS cards have several advantages, including ease of collection, small sample requirements, and low sample management/transport costs. However, collection quality is an important pre-analytical consideration for DBS, as blood must be wicked from a blood drop as opposed to pressed onto the filter paper in order to ensure appropriate quality for analytical testing workflows. RBSC kits must be assembled prior to shipment and generally include lancets, silica gel packs, humidity indicators, and biosafety bags. Depending on the analyte, foil packs or cardboard shipping boxes may be required for post-blood collection.

Application-specific considerations include adequate sample volume, analyte stability, the influence of hematocrit on estimating plasma concentrations from whole blood, and the optimal extraction solution for removing analytes from filter paper [30, 31]. For LDTs, including the modification of an FDA-cleared test for a DBS-collected specimen source, these test characteristics must be evaluated to ensure appropriate analytical performance and result interpretation. For example, many DBS applications require 10 μL – 50 μL of spotted blood for testing; however, minimum spotted volume is assay-dependent. Further, due to the displacement of plasma by the cellular and protein components of blood, drug concentrations from DBS may not correlate directly with plasma measurements. For CAB and RPV, correction factors of 1.79 and 1.14, respectively, were used to estimate plasma concentrations from whole blood [26]. Stability of different analytes in a drug combination can also differ. For emtricitabine/tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (F/TDF), TFV-DP is stable in blood tubes for up to 24 h prior to DBS spotting, whereas plasma FTC continues to be taken up by erythrocytes and is converted to FTC-TP, producing falsely elevated FTC-TP measurements [30]. After DBS spotting, TFV-DP and FTC-TP are stable at room temperature for five and nine days, respectively [30], providing additional flexibility in shipping timeframes to a centralized facility for storage. Once stored at −20 °C or colder, analytes remain stable for much longer periods of time (> 5 years). In most circumstances, DBS are considered exempt, non-regulated materials in the U.S. and are not subject to regulation as hazardous materials; however, DBS must be properly packaged for shipping as per the United States Postal Service (USPS) Domestic Mail Manual guidelines [32]. If the biospecimen is known to be infectious, additional labeling may be required, including affixing a UN3373 label (Biological Substance, Category B) for liquid blood biospecimens [33] to the outer box. However, exempt/non-regulated DBS samples still require three levels of packaging (e.g., sample receptacle, specimen bag [with biohazard symbol], and outer box) [34, 35]. Specimen-handling considerations, including room temperature tolerance thresholds and shipping requirements, should be considered when supporting RBSC workflows and should be included in protocol and participant-facing instructions.

While Whatman™ 903 Protein Saver cards are commonly used in pharmacokinetic (PK) studies, several other DBS collection devices are available and have been used for drug assessments, including Whatman™ FTA DMPK-C Cards, which are cellulose-based and additive-free [36] and PerkinElmer™ 226 Spot Saver cards, which are FDA-approved for PK studies [37]. Koster and colleagues evaluated the performance of various DBS cards for immunosuppressants and observed differences in analyte recovery and analytical performance parameters, highlighting the need for each DBS card type to be evaluated to determine its suitability for its intended test application [38]. Additionally, the HemaSep™ (Ahlstrom-Munskjö, Helsinki, Finland) plasma separator cards, which filter the aqueous portion of blood on filter cards, have also been evaluated for PK analyses, but have not been widely implemented in clinical research studies [39]. Beyond drug analyses, Whatman™ FTA DMPK-A and B cards facilitate cellular lysis and protein denaturation and have been used for molecular analyses [40].

DBS-based collection devices have also been used to monitor HIV-1 viral loads [41-43]. Waters and colleagues [41] demonstrated that spotted whole blood and plasma could serve as appropriate specimen sources for HIV-1 viral load monitoring. Currently, commercial assays, including the Hologic Aptima™ HIV-1 Quant Dx Assay, can accommodate DBS collected on Ahlstronm/Munktel TFN or Whatman™ 903 cards [44]. DBS samples collected in resource-limited settings serve as appealing alternatives for HIV-1 viral load monitoring, but significant trade-offs exist [23]. Low volumes of blood (~ 25 μL) can compromise assay sensitivity. For instance, the stated limit of quantitation (LOQ) for the Hologic Aptima™ assay using DBS is 883 copies/mL, compared to 20 copies/mL for plasma. Therefore, while DBS-based sample collection may be appropriate for monitoring viral load in someone poorly adherent to ART or detecting virologic failure at a cutoff of 1000 copies/mL, it lacks the sensitivity to identify acute HIV infection or early-onset virologic failure. Other collection devices, including the HemaSpot ™ (Spot On Sciences, Austin TX), which is an alternative microsampling device that is comprised of an absorbent collection matrix with desiccant, have been used to monitor HIV-1 viral loads in research settings [45]; however, they are also limited by sensitivity challenges, as the blood volume collected typically can only detect viral loads > 1000 copies/mL. Device availability and supply chain issues are additional implementation barriers, which may compromise the ability to use the HemaSpot ™ (as well as other microsampling collection devices) at scale in observational or interventional trials, in both domestic and international settings. While feasible, microsampling for HIV-1 RNA testing presents significant diagnostic utility challenges due to insufficient sensitivity, especially in the context of low-level viremia (< 200 copies/mL) and the use of long-acting antiretrovirals [46, 47].

Tasso, Mitra, and Other VAMS

Volumetric absorptive microsampling (VAMS) devices are an alternative to DBS as they facilitate accurate sample collection volume and minimize within-blood spot heterogeneity. While several devices, like the Mitra VAMS devices, rely on fingerstick collections, other products, like the Tasso collection devices, collect blood from the upper arm, and may mitigate some collection-associated discomfort with finger sticks. While the cost per sample collected is higher due to specialized devices, benefits include increased patient acceptability [48], precise “tip” volumes, reduced participant/patient burden, and pre-packaged products with plain-language instructions. VAMS kits using Mitra and Tasso devices include foil biospecimen bags to protect light-sensitive analytes. For specimens collected with Mitra and/or Tasso-M20/T20 devices, extraction units may differ across collection devices, which can impact interpretation of results. For example, blood extracted from a Mitra or Tasso-M20/T20 may be in units (drug amount) per tip instead of units per spot; however, based on the specific absorption volume, results from different DBS-derived RBSC devices may be converted and reported uniformly (e.g., as ng/mL). Table 1 describes these blood-based devices in greater detail.

Applications involving the Tasso-M20/T20 and Mitra devices include the quantification of nirmatrelvir/ritonavir concentrations in SARS-CoV-2 treatment [49, 50] and gire-destrant, which is being evaluated as a treatment option for ER+ breast cancer [51]. More recently, the longer half-life ethanol metabolite, phosphatidylethanol 16:0/18:1 (PEth), was evaluated in liquid whole blood, DBS, and Tasso-M20 devices to assess the suitability of the Tasso-M20 in PEth quantification [52]. When compared to whole blood, VAMS-collected blood for PEth correlated highly with liquid whole blood across a dynamic measuring range, demonstrating the feasibility of this collection device for analyte quantitation [52]. VAMS strategies using Mitra tips have been preliminarily evaluated for TFV-DP and FTC-TP following tenofovir-based PrEP exposure [53, 54].

RBSC using VAMS devices may increase sampling frequency in clinical research, providing home collection convenience while reducing healthcare facility burdens [22]. Thorough onboarding via in-person or rigorous virtual training is essential to ensure sample collection success, clarify study expectations, and address participant concerns; otherwise, return rates and sample quality may decline over time [2, 55].

Self-administered Sexually Transmitted Infection (STI) Testing

For both clinical and research purposes it is critical for investigators to be able to collect STI samples from each anatomic site where infections may occur. Self-collected vaginal, anorectal or oropharyngeal swabs, or urine are all feasible methods for self-collection of samples for chlamydia, gonorrhea, and trichomoniasis [56, 57]. Specimen testing for STIs should be conducted in a Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA)-certified laboratory [58]. Most over-the-counter RBSC kits include collection devices (e.g., swab, urine collection tube), plain language collection instructions, transport media, and guidance for specimen submission, along with online clinician access for result review and referral. The type of swab, transport medium, and anatomic collection site will depend on the analytical methodology employed for organism detection [59-61], as well as organism stability post-collection [62]. Cotton swabs may be used for molecular testing, but swabs with wooden shafts should be avoided as they may contain components that interfere with PCR-based assays. Synthetic swabs such as rayon, polyester, and flocked are more commonly used for diagnostic testing. Table 2 provides an overview of the advantages and disadvantages of commonly used swab types in STI testing.

Zasada and colleagues evaluated the volume of liquid adsorbed by various swab types and their specimen release properties, reinforcing that the swab structure and composition impact sample collection and release efficiency [63]. In clinical practice, flocked swabs are commonly used for viral studies as they collect more cells and viral particles from mucosal surfaces. However, while flocked swabs have collection advantages over other swab types, they have been associated with more discomfort and participant resistance during specimen collection [64]. Therefore, participant acceptability and site of collection (e.g., oral, vaginal, rectal, lesion, etc.) may be considered when selecting the type of swab to use for RBSC. The impact of transport medium on specimen stability is an additional consideration. Some media can stabilize organisms for culturing, but for standard nucleic acid amplification assays, dry swabs are adequate, since viable organisms are not required for this type of diagnostic testing [62]; therefore, in settings where there are concerns in the transport of swabs in liquid media, dry swabs for nucleic acid amplification-based molecular analyses may be the preferable workflow. However, it is important to coordinate with the testing laboratory to ensure that the collection device (swab) is validated and there is adequate infrastructure and capacity to accommodate the appropriate swab type. If the collected swab will be used for quantitative analysis, it is important to standardize the swab type based on the desired test application to mitigate inconsistencies in analytical outputs; volume recovery, participant acceptability, and the need for transport medium should also be considered in these scenarios.

Samples collected for STI testing, with or without transport media, must be shipped according to UN3373 guidelines using packing instruction 650, which involves a triple-layer system. Swabs must be placed into a primary receptacle that is both rigid and watertight. The primary specimen must then be placed into secondary packaging that is also watertight and able to withstand 95 kPa pressure. The secondary packaging must include sufficient absorbent material to contain the full volume of the specimen in the event of breakage or leaking during transport, and the specimen must be placed into a rigid outer box that meets the packing requirements. Finally, the outer box must include proper labeling (e.g., Biological Substance, Category B, UN3373 diamond symbol, emergency contact). If shipping via the USPS, authorization from the Director of Product Classification at Postal Service Headquarters may be needed. Ideally, specimens should be delivered within 24 h of collection, but this may require special courier services, which are associated with additional costs. These considerations should be considered during study design and implementation.

Other Specimen Types

Objective measures of product adherence are crucial for interpreting study endpoints in clinical studies, including HIV prevention or HIV status-neutral trials. However, as HIV prevention drug delivery methods evolve to include approved or investigational topical products, bioanalytical tools for evaluating product use beyond blood are becoming necessary. One example is measuring residual dapivirine concentrations from intravaginal rings. In clinical trials, used rings were returned for measuring remaining dapivirine and establishing cutoffs to interpret product use [65, 66]. Recently, a tenofovir (TFV)-containing rectal douche for HIV prevention was evaluated in several phase I trials, including among youth [67, 68]. To evaluate rectal douche use, a method was developed to detect fecal coliform bacteria on used enema tips as markers for use [69]. This approach assesses TFV-douche use via enema tip insertion into the rectum. As the enema tip is not viewed as a biological material, it can be easily stored and shipped for microtiter plate testing.

Urine has also been used as a less-invasive strategy to evaluate drug exposure and short-term antiretroviral use. An antibody-based tenofovir point-of-care test can detect the presence of TFV in urine [70], with a cutoff of 1500 ng/mL. At this analytical cutoff, the device can classify 98% of participants who took a dose of F/TDF within the preceding 24–48 h [71]. While reports have suggested that real-time PrEP adherence testing can build stronger communication with study participants and affirm self-reported adherence, the availability of real-time results to participants may also increase medication use only in the period proximate to a study visit, a phenomenon also known as white-coat dosing [72]. While there may be further utilization of urine as a specimen source for RBSC workflows, the point-of-care TFV test has not received widespread adoption as an RBSC strategy in clinical trials.

Hair is a longer-term marker for drug intake, reflecting exposure over the prior 6–8 weeks, and has been utilized both in evaluating exposure to antiretrovirals, antimycobacterial, and antibiotics [73-75]. The use of hair as a biomatrix is appealing since it can be self-collected and does not have the special shipping requirements associated with other biological matrices. However, clear instructions regarding sample collection, specimen labeling to delineate the region of hair most proximate to the scalp, heterogeneity in hair type and the use of hair relaxers or dyes, are all confounders on the overall utility of hair as an interpretable biomatrix. In a recent study evaluating the feasibility of RBSCs from hair among people with HIV, more than 75% of participants provided insufficient or improperly labeled hair samples, suggesting the more limited applicability of remote hair sampling [76]. Currently, for oral and long-acting medications, blood-based RBSC remains the gold standard.

Regulatory Considerations

The FDA defines microsampling collection supplies and products (lancets, DBS card, Tasso, etc. as noted in Tables 1 and 2) as medical devices used for specimen collection and transport [77]. FDA clearance does not automatically endorse every conceivable analytical method performed using samples from these devices. As per CLIA, all clinical diagnostic testing must be performed in certified laboratories, and LDTs must be validated or verified for their intended use. Home collection devices are regulated by the FDA based on intended use, risk, and unsupervised collection and must be cleared, approved, or authorized for home use. Specimens containing infectious agents must follow Department of Transportation and International Air Transport Association (IATA) regulations, and violations can incur substantial fines [78]. Additionally, states like New York, California, and Florida impose additional reporting requirements, with New York mandating assay approval, while California and Florida require laboratory licensure and potential physician involvement. These federal and state frameworks govern home collection device distribution and reporting of LDT results to study participants.

RBSC Implementation Considerations

Once the products or applications are chosen to meet the goals of the study, study teams must consider how to protocolize and implement RBSC. Although some aspects of the collection process will be dictated by the tools selected (e.g., specimen type, maximum allowable days between collection and processing), there are many choices for successful implementation that can vary between studies. Below we provide experience-based insights and recommendations for overarching considerations that may impact each step of the implementation process, as well as detailed considerations and questions that should be addressed at each step in the implementation process (see Table 3).

Flexibility in the Implementation Process

To maximize flexibility—while still maintaining a high degree of study rigor—in the RBSC process, we recommend establishing a clear team structure, that includes the laboratory director, and ample time for staff training on RBSC protocols (see Table 3 for more information). Once enrollment begins, a study team member should oversee the RBSC process and attend regular team meetings to provide updates on pre-defined metrics (e.g., valid return rates) of RBSC in use and report emerging problems that may need immediate attention.

Importantly, study teams must also determine the acceptable time frame in which the specimens must be returned around an ideal study visit date (i.e., a return window), while still maintaining study rigor. For example, if study visits occur every 90 days over 12 months, the study team must clearly define the return window before and after each study visit that the specimens will be accepted for inclusion in analyses for that study visit. While the return of the specimen as close to the ideal study visit date is desired for analytical and interpretive reasons, a return window that is too restrictive may compromise return rates. Therefore, we recommend allowing for as much time around each study visit to return the specimen without compromising the interpretation of results. Specimen tracking systems and coordination between sites, data management centers, and receiving laboratories are required to ensure appropriate management and follow-up of anticipated sample collection events. We also advise that a discussion with the analytical team should occur to understand the impact of the original and any modified return window(s) on the interpretation of study results to maintain the overall integrity of the study (i.e., by setting an overly generous return window).

On-time collections may be aided by text message reminders to participants when the window opens, and on the last day of the window if the sample has not yet been collected [79]. An additional strategy that may enhance timely RBSC is conducting virtual visits in which a study team member directly observes or is available to the participant during the sample collection process [see, for example, 80]; this provides reassurance on the timing and completeness of remote sample collections during the scheduled virtual study visit (similar to studies using virtual direct observation for medication adherence [81]); however, the use of live, virtual support may not be amenable to all study designs or acceptable to all participants. Time-stamped video recordings are another option to gather information on specimen collection relative to a visit or target window [a process used in [82], and their asynchronous nature allows participants to collect at times when study personnel are not available (e.g., outside of work hours, on weekends or holidays, etc.) while maintaining privacy at the time of collection.

When agreeing upon RBSC return windows, study teams should anticipate a wide variety of participant-specific scenarios that may disrupt the return timeline. For example, rural participants may have limited access to delivery carriers (e.g., FedEx, UPS, DHL) or other delivery options (e.g., drop-off points or scheduled home pick-up services), which may delay specimen delivery and return. When designing specimen return procedures, study teams can support these participants by implementing flexible return timelines or alternative logistics solutions, such as coordinated pick-up or partnerships with accessible community-based sites (e.g., pharmacies, clinics, or CBOs). If the specimens must be returned promptly to maintain specimen stability and the day of arrival for processing is of concern, a calendar that clearly depicts which days of the week the specimen(s) should be shipped may be provided to participants (see Fig. 1 for an example). Another common issue that may arise during RBSC is that some participants may change addresses or their phone number frequently. Tailored study engagement strategies, such as additional check-ins between study visits can be implemented to promote rapport with study team and confirm the participants’ preferred contact information. Such accommodations are critical for promoting equity, maintaining engagement, and minimizing differential attrition in RBSC protocols.

Privacy Considerations

Participant privacy, and the potential threats to it, in the context of RBSC procedures, is critical to consider optimizing kit completion rates (i.e., returning a valid sample or uploading a test result into an online portal). Research around community perceptions of specimen research suggests that minoritized groups have particular concerns around the lack of transparency in specimen use and the need for privacy in the process [83-85]. Because many U.S. states mandate reporting of reactive HIV/STI test results, study teams must be transparent and clearly communicate these obligations as it may impact participants’ willingness to enroll in the study and, if enrolled, to complete RBSC procedures. Moreover, study teams must determine whether to communicate test results to participants depending on several factors (e.g., clinical utility, timeliness of specimen processing). For instance, DBS samples used to assess PrEP adherence are often batched for analyses at the end of the study to reduce expense and because they are not intended to inform clinical care, whereas remote HIV testing requires timely disclosure and referral due to its health implications and associated reporting requirements. Because RBSC in the context of a research study is a departure from usual clinical care but may entail similar testing processes, transparency during the consent process about how results are used, reporting requirements, and limits to privacy is essential to maintain trust and avoid perceived privacy breaches.

Another key privacy concern is with the packaging of the kits. Generally, any shipped materials should be sent using plain packaging that does not inadvertently disclose the purpose of the study or the contents of the package. This may be of particular concern for AYA who may live with family, guardians, or roommates, who may be unaware or potentially disapproving of participation in sexual health research or for whom their participation may reveal some aspect of their identity (i.e., HIV status) for which they may be subject to stigma or discrimination [86, 87]. The kit should also contain a return package, which should be in plain packaging material and list the same return and ship-to addresses (i.e., of the laboratory that will receive the package for processing) to help maintain the confidentiality of the contents and avoid inadvertent disclosure of study participation or other personal health information [85]. Moreover, laboratories or third-party vendors who are contracted to receive and process specimens, may have specific requirements about whether they are able or willing to accept specimens labelled with personal health information, such as name or date of birth. In such cases, the research team may pre-label specimen return labels with a unique code that the laboratory or third-party vendor can use to communicate receipt and results to the study team.

RBSC remains a relatively new approach, and participants will likely have questions throughout the process. Clear and confidential communication is critical, and study teams should document communication strategies for responding to RBSC questions that maximize privacy, including confirming with each participant which method of communication (texting, phone calls, email) that they are most comfortable receiving study messages [88]. Communication should also avoid sensitive words or phrases that may disclose a diagnosis, identity, behavior or the purpose of the research like “HIV,” “viral load,” and “PrEP,” especially if there is a chance that someone other than the participant may intercept communication with participants [89]. Study teams may want to implement a system to confirm that they are speaking or corresponding with a participant in the study, such as requiring a password or verifying personal details.

Special Population Considerations

Understanding the individual and collective needs and concerns of the community(ies) the study is serving is important. For example, adolescent participants who are not living independently may require that the package be sent to an alternative location other than their home or obtain permission from their parents to safely receive it, as described above with respect to privacy considerations for this population. Importantly, age of majority varies by state and this may have implications for research participation more broadly, including consent to participate in RBSC procedures. Communities that have been socially marginalized in the U.S. may be particularly concerned about privacy, as well as about the language and visuals used in kit instructions. As such, careful considerations of ways to discreetly provide test kits to persons and ensuring that instructions are tailored to these communities will increase trust and engagement. Important questions to understand about the community include: (1) What motivates participants in the community(ies) to engage in research?; (2) What are their concerns about research and the research process they are expected to undertake (including RBSC)?; (3) What is their experience with RBSC?; and (4) What is the expected literacy-level of the target community? Answers to these questions will help determine how the RBSC process can be better tailored to a particular community.

Community-feedback, through a youth advisory board or community participatory board depending on the study population, on the planned RBSC approach and recommendations help to minimize avoidable barriers and adopt procedures that foster engagement is recommended. These groups offer crucial input into perception and appropriateness of materials and instructions. In addition to logistics (perceived burden of receipt, use and return of collection kits), teams should explore trust, skepticism, the role of incentives, and privacy needs with the community [90]. If community feedback is sought, the study timeline should be adjusted to accommodate feedback sessions, and ideally the budget should include funds to compensate community members for their efforts.

Step-by-step Considerations for RBSC

We present the considerations in a timeline format, from steps that should be taken before enrollment through preparing and sending the kit, to steps that occur when the kit is in the participant’s possession, as well as the return of specimens to the study team. The final section of the table considers steps that may be necessary if the sample is invalid and needs to be re-collected. For each step, we also provide special considerations to alert the reader to nuances in that step that we have encountered in our own studies, which may require an alteration to the RBSC process. In addition, we provide examples of specimen instructions tailored to youth as supplementary material (Online Resource 1).

Discussion

RBSC can help study teams more effectively achieve their study aims and improve the generalizability of findings by expanding participant reach, reducing participant burden, and potentially lowering the stigma associated with clinic-based biospecimen collection [85, 91]. However, there are important considerations in choosing the appropriate RBSC product to collect valid data, as well as in implementing effective procedures to ensure high-quality specimen collection in a way that is acceptable to participants. While use of RBSC in clinical research is growing, logistical considerations may vary considerably based on study aims, available financial and personnel resources, and participant attitudes and needs. For these reasons, it is not possible to standardize implementation protocols across all studies. We provide here a foundation for best practices and considerations for how to implement RBSC in clinical research, with the hope that the recommendations will continue to be refined as we gain more experience and data on how to optimize RBSC in research settings.

A multidisciplinary team is needed to choose study products and effectively implement RBSC procedures. Of primary importance is the need to discuss with the laboratory director or manager what tests would meet the goals of the study and what requirements each test has with respect to collection, time of return, and what is considered a valid sample. Other important team members include the statistician to not only confirm how test results from RBSC will be analyzed, but also to inform the team on issues related to the return window for specimens and how missing tests will be handled in analyses. Once the RBSC protocol is developed, research staff who will be implementing the protocol will need to be trained and the success of RBSC procedures monitored through regular reports. Taken together, study teams will need to consider RBSC early in the development process (rather than as an afterthought once the other parts of the protocol are developed) to allocate the necessary time and resources to ensure the success of data capture from RBSC.

The choice of product should align with study aims, as well as the ability for participants in the study to meet the requirements for timely return of a valid sample. For example, if the study enrolls primarily rural participants, it may not be feasible to include products that require a 24-hour return of the sample for storage or processing given the logistic barriers to shipping in these locations. Importantly, study teams must be aware of regulatory requirements and state reporting requirements for reactive HIV, STI and other tests that may be used in RBSC in clinical trials, as these factors may influence product choice and RBSC implementation procedures. While we reviewed common products that our teams and others have used in RBSC, we have also occasionally experienced disruptions in acquiring certain products from manufacturers; this can include inventory issues as well as import and tariff considerations. We advise closely monitoring the amount and rate of use of product during the study, including typical order arrival times by vendor, to coordinate timely purchase of the product from the manufacturer to avoid such disruptions.

The implementation considerations presented here are primarily informed by the need to develop participant-centered procedures that optimize valid sample return rates. Across the entire timeline, study teams will need to incorporate appropriate flexibility in their procedures, and consider the particular privacy concerns and other unique participant characteristics that require the tailoring of procedures. We recommend setting aside adequate time in the study timeline to develop specific RBSC procedures, tailor kit packaging and instructions, and provide robust training to staff to implement RBSC procedures with fidelity, among other RBSC activities. Finally, regular monitoring of the success of RBSC implementation should be conducted so that any modifications can be made in a timely way to minimize missing data.

There are several limitations of this narrative review of RBSC products and implementation considerations. First, we did not conduct a systematic review due to the potential missing or heterogeneous available study design details and potential bias in extracting RBSC information across numerous intervention trials. In addition, a narrative review provided more flexibility in incorporating our collective experience in implementing RBSC, which was important since, to our knowledge, this has not been documented to date. A second limitation is that this narrative review was conducted by researchers with experience primarily in adolescent HIV prevention and treatment clinical research. As such, the information provided here may not fully reflect considerations for products and implementation procedures in other fields or populations, although we believe many recommendations will still be relevant. Third, we do not review RBSC for clinical care, which may differ with respect to what products may be selected and procedures used when working with patients. Finally, the use of RBSC for clinical trials is an evolving field, and the products reviewed here and recommendations for implementation will likely be different in the future. It will be important to update the information we provide here to keep pace with new developments in RBSC.

This review of RBSC product and implementation considerations provides an actionable framework and roadmap when considering incorporation of RBSC into clinical trials research. In doing so, we hope to achieve several goals. First, providing accessible practical guidance on RBSC product choice and the implementation process to researchers will allow them to more successfully carry out RBSC. For researchers who are new to conducting RBSC, the recommendations provided here may provide strategies to avoid mistakes that are otherwise easy to make without prior experience. For experienced research teams, these guidelines may be a helpful training resource. Second, we hope our recommendations promote the use of strong RBSC protocols that help participants who may be skeptical of engaging in clinical research feel more comfortable with the process. Having a positive experience in research studies that use strong RBSC protocols may increase the likelihood of participation in future research projects, which may increase the reach and impact of clinical research. Finally, while we focused our review here on clinical research, many of these considerations may be relevant for clinical care that would benefit from RBSC to inform healthcare practice. Ultimately, providing guidance on RBSC for research settings may inform clinical practice as remote testing becomes more common and accepted. We anticipate that these recommendations will continue to evolve as RBSC is incorporated into more clinical research and these collection strategies become more acceptable to diverse study populations.
