Reappraisal of Surgical Threshold in Patients With Ascending Aortic Dilation
Authors: Milind Y Desai, Mohammed Abusafia, Shada Jadam, Vidyasagar Kalahasti, Susan Keen, Lifu Deng, Susan Ospina, Sana Sultana, Andrew Gaballa, Patrick Vargo, Xiaoying Lou, Eric E Roselli, Brian P Griffin, Lars G Svensson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIndexing ascending aortic dimensions to height, rather than absolute diameters, may better identify at-risk ascending aortopathy (AA) patients who could benefit from earlier surgery.ObjectivesThis study evaluated whether height-indexed aortic dimensions improve long-term risk stratification in AA patients who do not meet current surgical guidelines.MethodsA total of 11,083 patients (68 ± 13 years, 79% men, 28% with bicuspid aortic valve, 2,196 with aortic surgery and 8,887 unoperated) with AA (4-5 cm) diagnosed on echocardiography (2010-2023) were included. Patients with syndromic aortopathy and acute aortic dissection/rupture at presentation were excluded. The maximum AA diameter, AA height index (AHI) (cm/m), and AA cross-sectional area-to-height (CSAH) (cm2/m) index were calculated. The primary outcome was mortality.ResultsThe mean AA diameter, AHI, and CSAH were 4.4 ± 0.2 cm, 2.53 ± 0.3 cm/m, and 8.8 ± 1 cm2/m, respectively. At 6 ± 4 years, there were 84 (8.3%) deaths in the operated group and 1,058 (11.9%) in the unoperated group. There were 128 aortic dissections (1.4%) and 4 aortic ruptures (0.04%) in the unoperated group. Dividing the unoperated sample into a derivation cohort (n = 3,887), spline analysis demonstrated an increase in hazard for death in women with AA diameter ≥4.5 cm and in all patients with AHI ≥2.5 cm/m or CSAH ≥9 cm2/m. Validation in a separate cohort (n = 5,000) confirmed that AHI ≥2.5 cm/m and CSAH ≥9 cm2/m were associated with higher long-term mortality (both P < 0.05).ConclusionsIndexing AA dimensions to height better identifies high-risk AA patients, particularly women, who fall below surgical criteria. Further studies are needed to determine if this cohort may benefit from earlier intervention at lower size thresholds.Key words: ascending aortopathy, size thresholds

Ascending aortopathy (AA), a largely asymptomatic condition, typically has a benign course but often presents with serious, potentially fatal complications, including aortic dissection, rupture, and death.1, 2, 3 Once diagnosed, often inadvertently on routine or pre-emptive screening (in the setting of a family history), the management involves watchful waiting and control of cardiovascular comorbidities until the aortic dimensions reach a surgical threshold (typically 5.5 cm). Once this threshold is reached, prophylactic aortic replacement (for primary aortic pathology) is recommended by the guidelines. In selected cases of AA with concomitant severe aortic valvular disease, prophylactic aortic surgery is recommended by guidelines at lower size threshold of 4.5 cm. This is because without prophylactic repair, despite its low procedural risk, particularly at experienced aortic centers, approximately 22% of patients experiencing acute aortic dissection or rupture die before receiving medical attention.2 However, societal guideline thresholds for surgical intervention have varied over time and continue to evolve with suggestion that indexing might be preferred in many cases over absolute diameters.4, 5, 6 There is a lack of definitive clinical trial evidence for recommended intervention thresholds; such a trial might not be ethically tenable in the setting of modern guideline recommendations. As a result, controversy persists regarding the optimal size threshold for prophylactic aortic intervention, partly due to an incomplete understanding of the natural history of AA. Prior natural history studies were limited by factors such as referral bias, gaps in follow-up, and small sample size of patients with unoperated moderate and large aneurysms undergoing longitudinal observation. Furthermore, earlier prophylactic repair in current practice further limits the availability of longitudinal data for patients with moderate to severely dilated aneurysms.

However, observations from the International Registry of Acute Aortic Dissection demonstrate that 4 of 10 dissections occur below 5.0 cm, challenging the reliance on absolute diameter alone and a potential recognition that we might need to revisit the size-based thresholds in recommendation of prophylactic aortic surgery.7 Furthermore, improved risk stratification for patients with borderline aortic sizes, especially women, has been emphasized by recent investigations.8, 9, 10, 11 Thresholds for height-indexed metrics such as the aortic height index (AHI) and cross-sectional area to height (CSAH) index have shown value in identifying at-risk patients who do not meet absolute diameter criteria, based on prior observational cohorts tracking long-term outcomes (dissection and death) in patients with bicuspid aortic valves (BAVs) and thoracic aortic aneurysms. Despite validation across different smaller AA populations,12,13 these metrics remain underutilized and refined surgical thresholds based on height-indexing have not been universally adopted. In a large, real-world population of AA patients not meeting current primary guideline criteria for prophylactic AA surgery, we sought to evaluate whether indexing aortic dimensions to height is associated with improved long-term risk stratification.

Methods

Study population

This was an observational cohort study of consecutive adult patients (≥18 years) with an AA between 4 and 5 cm, diagnosed on echocardiography between 2010 to 2023 at our large tertiary referral center. We excluded patients with syndromic aortopathy (Marfan syndrome, Loeys-Dietz syndrome, vascular Ehler-Danlos syndrome, and Turner syndrome) and those with acute aortic dissection/rupture as an initial presentation. The diagnosis was established by experienced cardiologists interpreting transthoracic echocardiograms. Appropriate approval was obtained from the institutional review board with a waiver of individual informed consent (Cleveland Clinic IRB 24-760, approved on August 19, 2024).

Study design

Clinical data were manually extracted from electronic medical records, where it was entered prospectively at the time of the initial encounter. History of various cardiovascular risk factors were recorded based on the individual patient. AA etiology was further classified as BAV–associated AA or nonsyndromic AA. Medication use at baseline was recorded. Body surface area (BSA) was calculated using the DuBois formula: 0.007184 × heightˆ0.725 (cm) × weightˆ0.425 (kg).14

Imaging

All patients underwent comprehensive echocardiograms performed using commercial systems (Philips Medical Systems, Siemens Medical Solution Inc, and General Electric) and interpreted per guideline-based recommendations.15 Measurements included left ventricular ejection fraction, aortic valvular regurgitation severity, and mean aortic valve gradient. Aortic root and ascending aortic diameters were obtained in the parasternal long-axis view at end-diastole using the leading-edge-to-leading-edge technique.15,16 All measurements were retrieved from an electronic database and/or collected manually. AHI, defined as the maximum aortic diameter (cm) divided by height (m), was calculated. Similarly, CSAH index, defined as πr2 (cm2) divided by height (m), where r was the aortic radius, was calculated in every patient. Where available (n = 4,791), AA measurements were recorded from contrast-enhanced computed tomographic scans performed within 1 year of the transthoracic echocardiogram. Data were extracted from radiology reports using a custom natural language processing algorithm based on pattern-matching.

AA was classified on transthoracic echocardiography, as follows: mild (4-4.4 cm) and moderate dilation (4.5-4.9 cm). By study design, patients with maximal diameter ≥5 cm were not included in the current study. Using AHI, a threshold of >2.43 to 3.20 cm/m was considered mildly and >3.21 cm/m severely increased risk aortopathy, as previously described.12 In addition, a ratio of aortic (area to height ≥10 cm2/m (maximum number from either aortic root or ascending aortic measurement) was considered abnormal, as has been previously described.17, 18, 19 All AA measurements (diameter, AHI, and CSAH) represent the maximum number from either the proximal thoracic aorta (either aortic root or ascending aorta).

Surgical details

Operative interventions during follow-up were reviewed and categorized as aortic valve replacement/repair in combination with ascending aortic surgery. Surgical indications were based on contemporary practice guidelines and primarily due to aortic valve disease, including severe symptomatic aortic stenosis or regurgitation, mixed aortic valve disease (severe stenosis with moderate regurgitation and vice versa), and asymptomatic severe aortic regurgitation with severe left ventricular dilation.4, 5, 6 Although all patients undergoing surgery also had concomitant ascending (or aortic root) dilation ≥4.5 cm, none had an aneurysmal aorta (≥5 cm) or a primary indication for elective concomitant ascending aortic surgery.

Outcomes

A primary outcome of all-cause death was recorded. Mortality data were obtained from review of medical records, observation of death certificate, or state and nationally available databases, with subsequent confirmation with a family member. Aortic dissection, intramural hematoma, and rupture events which occurred during follow-up, but at least 6 months after initial evaluation, were also recorded from electronic medical records following a review of a diagnostic imaging study near the event. Follow-up was defined as the time from initial echocardiogram to death (last query February 2025).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR) and compared using the Student's t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages and compared using chi-square testing. Spearman correlation coefficient was used to measure correlation between continuous variables. Univariable and multivariable cause-specific Cox regression analyses were performed to test the association between various clinically relevant variables and outcomes. HRs and 95% CIs were reported. Kaplan-Meier curves were used to illustrate survival, and differences were assessed using the log-rank test. Subsequently, we randomly divided the study sample into derivation and validation groups. Penalized splines were used (knots placed at quartiles) to characterize relative hazards for the primary outcome of death over the range of various AA thresholds (diameter, AHI, and CSAH index) in the derivation sample. They were used in survival analysis to model complex, nonlinear relationships, and time-varying effects without assuming constant HRs over time, unlike standard Cox models. In addition, receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was performed and areas under the curve were reported for various AA dimensions (diameter, AHI, and CSAH). Using the AA thresholds obtained from the derivation sample in spline analysis, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed in the validation sample using log-rank statistic or generalized Wilcoxon statistic, as appropriate. Analyses were performed using SPSS (version 27) and R (version 4.1.1). A 2-tailed P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The observational cohort consisted of consecutive adult patients (≥18 years) with an AA (between 4-5 cm), diagnosed on echocardiography between 2010 to 2023 at our large tertiary referral center. Patients with syndromic AA (n = 330) were excluded because of significantly lower thresholds for elective aortic repair, as recommended by guidelines.4, 5, 6 In addition, patients who initially presented to our center in the setting of an acute aortic dissection/rupture were also excluded (n = 1,179). The final study sample included 11,083 patients (8,887 unoperated and 2,196 patients who underwent elective aortic repair during follow-up). Baseline clinical and imaging characteristics of the final study sample, separated into operated vs unoperated patients, are shown in Table 1. The mean age was 68.1 ± 13 years and majority of the patients were men (78.6%) and White (89.5%). The proportion of cardiovascular comorbidities was high with 80.5% with hypertension, and 72.3% with dyslipidemia. The vast majority of patients were on appropriate background therapy with 84.2% on beta blockers, 67.4% on angiotensin inhibitors/receptor blockers and 69.9% on statins. The mean maximal AA diameter, AHI, and CSAH were 4.4 ± 0.2 cm, 2.53 ± 0.3 cm/m and 8.8 ± 1 cm2/m, respectively. By study design, no patients had an ascending (or root) aortic aneurysm (≥5 cm). The mean aortic arch diameter was 3.3 ± 0.5 cm and mid-descending aortic diameter was 2.5 ± 0.7 cm. In the subgroup of patients (n = 4,791) with a contrast-enhanced computed chest tomogram performed within 1 year of transthoracic echocardiogram, the average maximal AA diameter was 4.5 ± 0.6 cm with a significant correlation with echocardiographic measurements (r = 0.79; P < 0.001).

Surgical outcomes

Of the 2,196 patients in the operated group, 1,640 (75%) patients underwent elective aortic repair within the first 6 months (mean time of 36 ± 39 days), whereas the rest 556 patients underwent an operation after at least 6 months (mean time 476 ± 349 days) of initial presentation. The primary indications for an operation were presence of significant aortic valvular disease, either stenosis (n = 1,515, 69%) or regurgitation (n = 681, 31%). At the time of surgery, the mean aortic valve gradient was 37.8 ± 5 mm Hg, 40.8% had ≥ II + aortic regurgitation, and the mean maximal ascending diameters at the time of surgery was 4.7 ± 0.2 cm, respectively. No patient had an aneurysm (≥5 cm).

Survival analysis in the total study sample

At a mean follow-up of 6 ± 4 years (range 0-15 years), the breakdown of deaths was as follows: operated group (84 [8.3%] deaths, of which only 13 [0.6%] were in-hospital deaths) and unoperated group (1,058 [11.9%] deaths) (log-rank P value between operated and unoperated groups <0.001). Although the overall proportion of long-term deaths were lower in the operated group, the 1, 2, and 3-year survival were similar in both groups (99%, 98%, and 97%, respectively, in the operated group and 97%, 96%, and 95%, respectively, in the unoperated group). In addition, there were 132 (1.5%) aortic dissections/ruptures in the unoperated group and none in operated group. Details of cause-specific Cox regression analyses (for the endpoint of death and cardiovascular surgery during follow-up) are shown in the Supplemental Tables 1 and 2 in the supplemental file. Within the operated group, there was no difference in in-hospital or long-term survival in patients based on aortic diameter cutoff of greater or lesser than 4.5 cm (P = 0.89).

Additional sensitivity analyses, based on the presence or absence of BAV, are shown in Supplemental Table 3 and the Supplemental Appendix.

Additional analysis in the unoperated group

Since the primary reason to undergo cardiac surgery in the current study sample was predominantly due to concomitant valvular disease (with no patient meeting the independent criteria for elective aortic replacement surgery), subsequent analyses were performed in the unoperated patients. Supplemental Table 4 shows the association between various thresholds of maximum AA diameter, AHI, and CSAH in the unoperated patients. Using various AA thresholds (maximum diameter, AHI, and CSAH), we analyzed the long-term outcomes (death and aortic dissection/rupture) in the unoperated group, as well as separated on basis of sex. These results are shown in Table 2. Although a higher proportion of events (death or dissection/rupture) occurred in the higher risk groups (aortic diameter >4.5 cm, AHI >2.43 cm/m, or CSAH 10 cm2/m), most deaths (60.7% in women and 56.5% in men) and aortic dissection/ruptures (58.8% in women and 52% in men) occurred in patients with maximal AA diameter <4.5 cm. Similarly, most deaths (95.1% in women and 62.4% in men) and aortic dissection/ruptures (94.1% in women and 56.1%) occurred in patients with AHI between 2.43 to 3.20 cm/m. A significant proportion of deaths (67.4% in women and 85.5% in men) and aortic dissection/ruptures (67.6% in women and 82.7%) occurred in patients with CSAH <10 cm2/m.

Discussion

In a large, real-world population patients with an AA between 4 to 5 cm (without a documented aneurysmal dilation ≥5 cm), we make the following observations. All prophylactic aortic replacement surgeries were performed as an adjunct operation in conjunction with significant aortic valvular disease (either stenosis or regurgitation) as the primary indication. None of these patients met the primary guideline criteria for prophylactic isolated AA surgery. Within the unoperated group, the majority of deaths (57.4%) and aortic dissection/ruptures (53.8%) occurred in patients with maximal AA diameter <4.5 cm. In the derivation sample, using penalized spline analyses, we demonstrate an absolute AA diameter of ≥4.5 cm identified women (but not men) at a higher risk for long-term death. Also, within the derivation sample, the hazard of death increased at AHI ≥2.5 cm/m and CSAH ≥9 cm2/m. Applying these derived thresholds in the validation sample, we demonstrate that patients with AHI ≥2.5 cm/m and CSAH ≥9 cm2/m had significantly worse long-term survival vs those with AHI <2.5 cm/m or CSAH <9 cm/m, respectively (Central Illustration). However, the survival of patients with AA diameter of <4.5 cm was similar to those with aortic diameter of ≥4.5 cm.

Key Findings of the Current Study

In 11,083 patients (68 ± 13 years, 79% men, 28% with bicuspid aortic valve, 2,196 with aortic surgery, and 8,887 unoperated) with ascending aortopathy (AA), there were 84 (8.3%) deaths in operated group and 1,058 (11.9%) deaths, 128 aortic dissections (1.4%), and 4 aortic ruptures (0.04%) in the unoperated group. Within the derivation cohort of the unoperated group, there was an increase in hazard for death in women with AA diameter ≥4.5 cm and in all patients with AHI ≥2.5 cm/m or CSAH ≥9 cm2/m. Validation in a separate cohort confirmed that AHI ≥2.5 cm/m and CSAH ≥9 cm2/m were associated with higher long-term mortality (both P < 0.05). Indexing AA dimensions to height better identifies high-risk AA patients, particularly women, not meeting surgical criteria but could benefit from earlier intervention at lower size thresholds.

Current 2022 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association and 2014 European Society of Cardiology guidelines recommend surgical aortic replacement when AA diameter exceeds 5.5 cm (except in situations with significant aortic valve pathology where the recommendation is to replace the ascending aorta at ≥ 4.5 cm), irrespective of valve morphology, body size, or sex.4,5 However, accumulating evidence suggests that these fixed thresholds may inadequately capture risk, particularly in women and smaller individuals.11 In a recent study of more than 14,000 patients with AA, women had worse long-term survival despite a lesser cardiovascular comorbidities, lower AA size, and higher proportion of AA surgery.11 Indexed metrics such as CSAH index and AHI, initially validated in patients with Marfan syndrome, tricuspid and BAV disease,17,18 have demonstrated broader applicability in accounting for body size, height, sex differences, and the irregular, often elliptical geometry of the AA. Consequently, reliance on absolute AA diameter alone may lead to under-recognition of high-risk patients, underscoring the need for more individualized risk stratification. Indexed size thresholds for AHI (2.43 cm/m cutoff) and CSAH (10 cm2/m) were informed by large observational cohorts tracking long-term outcomes (dissection, death) in patients with BAVs and thoracic aortic aneurysms. However, despite validation across different smaller AA populations and prior published height-based nomograms,12,13 AHI and CSAH metrics remain underutilized and refined surgical thresholds based on height-indexing have not been universally adopted. In addition, a prior study has also demonstrated using nomograms based on indexing AA diameter to BSA might have clinical utility. However, it is well-known that BSA is prone to significant fluctuations and hence not reliable. Compared to prior reports, the current study has a much larger sample size with a long follow-up, including patients with various etiologies of AA (e.g., BAV and trileaflet valve associated aortopathy) and excluded patients with ascending aortic diameters ≥5 cm, as these patients are typically referred for a surgical evaluation. In addition, we also excluded syndromic AA patients as the guidelines already recommend different (lower) size thresholds for elective aortic surgery. There are emerging data that ascending aortic length might provide incremental value in diagnostic and prognostic value in management of acute and chronic aortic syndromes.20,21 In a prior report, an AA length ≥13 cm was associated with a 5-fold increase in adverse events and an AA length of ≥11 cm was suggested as a possible cutoff for elective aortic surgery.21 However, this needs additional validation.

Data from International Registry of Acute Aortic Dissection7,22 highlight this issue, showing that 40% of patients with type A dissection had an aortic diameter <5.0 cm. More recently, Grego et al23 reported a moderate increase in acute aortic event risk at AHI >2.9 cm/m. The latest European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery/Society of Thoracic Surgeons guidelines acknowledge this by recommending a surgical threshold of 5.0 cm in patients shorter than 1.69 m.24 Additional studies, including Acharya et al25 and Masri et al13 demonstrated increased complications in patients with CSAH >10 cm2/m who did not otherwise meet the surgical criteria, and confirmed a 4-fold increase in mortality and 78% of deaths in the 4.5 to 5.5 cm diameter range occurring in patients exceeding this indexed threshold. As a result, the most recent iteration of the aortic guidelines also recommend elective AA replacement in selected scenarios: rapid growth >0.5 cm/year (class 1), aortic diameter ≥5 cm (class IIa), CSAH ≥10 cm2/m (class IIa), and AHI ≥3.21 cm/m (class IIb), in addition to an absolute ascending aortic diameter threshold of 5.5 cm (class I).4,5 However, these indexed criteria remain underutilized in clinical practice.

The findings of the current study expand on these data, showing that risk begins to rise at even lower thresholds, with AHI ≥2.5 cm/m and CSAH ≥9 cm2/m marking earlier inflection points that identify AA patients at a higher risk of long-term death. Furthermore, majority of aortic dissections/ruptures occurred below the previously established high-risk thresholds in patients with maximal AA diameter <4.5 cm reinforcing the limitations of absolute diameter-based risk assessment. The use of indexed AA measurements also appears to be critical in sex-based risk stratification. The TITAN: SvS trial emphasized that women are three to ten times more likely than men to meet indexed surgical criteria, despite having similar absolute diameters.26 Our findings are consistent with these observations, revealing increased mortality in women with ascending aortic diameters >4.5 cm. This is aligned with prior studies showing that women tend to present later, experience higher complication rates, and are often under-recognized when absolute diameter alone is considered.3,27,28

Our study offers several strengths. It represents one of the largest contemporary AA cohorts with comprehensive echocardiographic and tomographic imaging, combined with extended long-term follow-up. The substantial female representation allowed for robust sex-specific analyses, confirming that indexed aortic measurements outperform absolute diameter in identifying high-risk women. In addition, the size and granularity of the data set enabled detection of lower mortality risk inflection points at various indexed measurements than previously recognized. Clinically, these findings support broader incorporation of indexed aortic dimensions in risk stratification and surgical decision-making. Indexed metrics (at thresholds even lower than previously described) may identify a subset of higher-risk patients who do not meet the current guideline surgical criteria but may potentially benefit from earlier evaluation and consideration for an earlier surgical intervention after shared decision-making. This is particularly relevant in high-volume aortic centers such as ours, where elective aortic replacement is performed with a low in-hospital mortality rate of 0.6%.29 These data support a shift toward earlier intervention in appropriately selected patients, especially if performed at experienced center with very low operative mortality.

Study Limitations

Our study is not without limitations. The observational study design introduces the potential for selection bias, and residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Accordingly, the study establishes associations rather than causality. Determining cause-specific mortality is inherently challenging in an observational cohort; therefore, all-cause mortality, which is considered robust, was used as the primary endpoint.30 It is possible that patients with an aortic dissection/rupture with resultant sudden death without an autopsy were under-recognized and hence, misclassified. As this is a single-center study conducted at a high-volume tertiary referral center, generalizability to broader or community-based populations may be limited and external validation might be needed. We do not have granular details of the recovery time in individual patients from their aortic surgery. Most of the patients were White. Not all patients with AA underwent tomographic imaging. For the current outcomes study, the primary objective of this study was to develop aortic size thresholds that are associated with survival among consecutive patients meeting the inclusion criteria. Due to lack of uniform availability of computed tomography scan data, only echocardiographic aortic measurements were included. CSAH measurements on echocardiography may be inaccurate in the setting of an eccentrically dilated aortic root. However, where available, there was a significant correlation between CT and echocardiographic measurement. Data on AA length were not available and hence, not reported. The current cross-sectional study does not report serial changes in AA dimensions or ambient blood pressure monitoring over time and hence, ascertaining actual aortic dimensions or blood pressure at the time of the downstream dissection/rupture is not possible. In the current study, all elective aortic replacements were performed at a lower threshold of ≥4.5 cm, since it was performed as a concomitant procedure to aortic valve surgery, according to guidelines.4, 5, 6 As a result, to develop newer thresholds, we excluded patients who met the guideline criteria for an earlier prophylactic aortic replacement as well as those with syndromic aortopathies. Hence the current results are not representative of the overall patients presenting with AA and could potentially be biased. Future prospective studies to validate these results should include need for elective aortic surgery as part of a composite endpoint.

Conclusions

In patients with AA not at a guideline threshold for prophylactic aortic surgery, relying on absolute AA diameters may underestimate the risk of mortality, especially in women. Indexing aortic dimensions to height (either AHI or CSAH) may identify a group of higher-risk patients at a lower indexed size threshold who do not meet the current guideline criteria for prophylactic aortic surgery but may potentially benefit from it. Further studies are needed to determine if a possible shift toward earlier intervention in appropriately selected patients, especially if performed at an experienced center with a very low operative mortality, is beneficial.

COMPETENCY IN CLINICAL KNOWLEDGE: In a study of 11,083 patients with AA, spline analysis demonstrated an increase in hazard for death in women with AA diameter ≥4.5 cm and in all patients with AHI ≥2.5 cm/m or aortic cross sectional area to height ratio ≥9 cm2/m. Validation in a separate cohort (n = 5,000) confirmed that AHI ≥2.5 cm/m and CSAH ≥9 cm2/m were associated with higher long-term mortality (both P < 0.05).

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: Indexing AA dimensions to height better identifies high-risk AA patients, particularly women, not meeting surgical criteria but could benefit from earlier intervention at lower size thresholds.
