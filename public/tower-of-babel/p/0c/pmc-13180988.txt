Enhancing island biogeography: improving identification of potential species pools via environmental filtering
Authors: Claudia Nuñez-Penichet, Jorge Soberón, Marlon E. Cobos, Fernando Machado-Stredel, A. Townsend Peterson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A successful theory in ecology is the equilibrium species numbers on islands. Most of it is about the total number of species on “islands” (sensu lato), ignoring complications like species identities, history, and environmental differences. We propose an operational approach for identification of potential species pools, moving from predicting only numbers toward a better understanding of species identities. This procedure includes two main steps: (1) provide an initial pool of species names, and (2) refine that list via niche-based environmental filtering. To provide the initial species pool (step 1), we explored two regions of different sizes. For step 2, we used ecological niche modeling to separate potential colonizers from species that do not have any distributional potential on the island(s) in question. As an example, we used data on butterflies of the Caribbean Islands. We found that, regardless of the size of the pool, our methods were able to refine (reduce) potential species pools without increasing omission errors. Our predicted species pools, however, show apparent “errors” of commission—we interpret these species as representing as-yet unrealized potential colonizers. Overall, this approach allows researchers to refine estimates of species pools, and thereby improve the ability to predict potential local species richness and species identities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43084-9.

Introduction

The theory of island biogeography ranks among the most successful of ecological theories1. Its great success has been owing, in no small part, to the fact that it organizes complex phenomena in terms of two simple quantities: area and degree of isolation. Challenging this theory, however, Ricklefs2 suggested that the time has come to initiate a new research program that includes both historical factors and regional processes, challenging ecologists to: “ask whether generalities can emerge from this research programme, or merely a set of unique cases for which one cannot distinguish the underlying processes controlling patterns of diversity.”

In another important paper, Ricklefs3 discussed the importance of local versus regional processes in assembling biotic communities. This interesting discussion immediately begs the question of what the pool of potential colonizing species of an area is. Previous authors had defined a regional fauna (a pool) as “all the species which could eventually colonize a location…”4, or “A regional species pool comprises all species available to colonize a focal site”5. A problem with these definitions, however, is their lack of detail: what is “eventually?” and how to decide what “comprises all species available”? Without answers to these questions, species pools will remain, in practice, arbitrary and non-operational.

A more practical way of defining the pool of species in a given region was proposed by Borreegard et al.6. The idea of these authors was to build the pool of species from the “dispersion field”7 of a locality. The dispersion field of a site is the set of species in localities that share at least one species with the focal site8. This definition is precise, quantitative, and operational, and produces a list of potential colonizing species, but has the problem of requiring detailed knowledge of the distribution of every species. Such information is available, at least at coarse-grained resolution (e.g., 1° grid cells), only for vertebrates9, so the potential utility of the method is limited.

In this contribution, we propose a distinct method by which to define pools of potential colonizing species, which follows the general definition of species pools proposed by Cornell and Harrison5. We define a focal zone for which the pool is required, and identify regions that could potentially provide colonizers to the focal zone, and compile comprehensive lists of species in those regions. Then, we “filter” that list using ecological niche modeling (ENM) approaches. We demonstrate that ENM approaches have the potential to reduce lists of potential colonizer species by eliminating species that have no potential to maintain populations in the focal area, and thereby are not part of the potential pool of colonizer species. We use the term “niche” in its restricted, Grinnellian sense (i.e., response curves in a space of non-interactive, or “scenopoetic” variables;10), which contrasts with the sense used by Cornell and Harrison5. Restricting niches to a space of non-interactive variables, one loses generality, but gains considerably in tractability of calculations10. Since we concentrate on regional and environmental factors, we partially address the challenge posed by Ricklefs2.

Results

Jaccard analysis

We obtained high values of the Jaccard index for comparing the two source pools options compared with the actual pattern of presences and absences. Values ranged 0.89–0.97 and 0.83–0.96 in the country and ecoregion source pools, respectively (Table 1). In both source pools, Cuba presented the lowest value, and the Virgin Islands had the highest values (Table 1). Given that no data from the Caribbean Islands were used in developing the models that made these predictions, this initial result indicates simply that the ecological niche modeling approach had good predictive value as to which species would be able to maintain populations on which islands.

Omission and commission errors analysis

For the two source pools analyzed, the ecological niche models were able to predict all butterfly species reported to be present in Hispaniola, Cuba, Puerto Rico, and the Bahamas (Fig. 1). In the case of the other four island complexes considered (Jamaica, Windward Islands, Leeward Islands, and Virgin Islands), our method missed up to three species per island that were actually present in such islands. As some of the species omitted in an island were the same species as were omitted for another island, we ended up with a total of only five species being omitted in the country source pool (Phoebis sennae, Eurema daira, E. lisa, E. nicippe, and Nathalis iole), and three for ecoregion source pool (P. sennae, E. daira, and Ascia monuste), all from Pieridae (Fig. 1). These omissions do not appear to be related to the difference of environments between islands and the source pool area, as we detected, with the mobility-oriented parity (MOP) metric, high values of difference in both islands with no species omitted and islands with omitted species (Supplementary Figure S1 and Supplementary Figure S2).

For both source pools, Hispaniola was the island showing most commission errors, whereas the Virgin Islands had the fewest (Fig. 1). For most islands studied, commission rates were higher than omission rates, with the exception of the Virgin Islands (Fig. 2). This pattern was observed for both countries and the ecoregions as potential source pools.

Exploring the relatively few cases of omission of species in model predictions for species actually present on islands, omissions resulted from the lack of representation of the species’ niche in certain pairs of environmental variables (Figs. 3 and 4) (Note that any point outside a lower-dimensional niche will be also outside larger-dimensional ellipsoidal niches of which the lower-dimensional ones is an orthogonal projection.). For the species wrongly predicted as not finding suitable conditions on Jamaica (E. lisa, E. nicippe, Nathalis iole) and from the Windward and Leeward Islands (P. sennae, E. lisa), a combination of low temperature and low precipitation (in the wet season) was absent from those islands (Fig. 3b, d). For the three species wrongly predicted as not finding suitable conditions on the Virgin Islands (P. sennae, E. daira, E. lisa), on the other hand, the omission resulted from cold environments not represented in those islands (Fig. 3d). These omission errors may best be interpreted as the available conditions (and consequently the existing ecological niche) for the continental portions of the distributions of these species, not extending sufficiently to include the conditions manifested on the island.

Procrustes analysis

Correlations among the three presence-absence matrices (PAMs) were high: 0.975 (countries and ecoregions), 0.749 (atlas and ecoregions), and 0.781 (atlas and countries). The corresponding p-values (permutation test with 999 replicates) for the sum of squares were p = 0.001, p = 0.004, and p = 0.002, respectively. These significant correlations between PAMs indicate considerable shared structure and pattern in these matrices. A Procrustes residuals analysis indicated that the Bahamas and the Windward Islands are responsible for the discrepancies among PAMs, since differences for these two islands between the two pools were the only ones above the 75% quantile of the Procrustes residuals, with the Bahamas contributing 43.6% of the total sum of squares of the difference, and the Windward Islands 23.5%.

Discussion

In this contribution, we present a method by which to filter large pools of species to identify a more true pool of actual potential colonizer species for an island or set of islands. We achieve this filtering using a simple ecological niche modeling methodology. Our method indeed identified a large number of species that had or had not actually colonized the target islands in our case study; importantly, however, our approach had very few omission errors (Figs. 1 and 2). Such omissions would reflect genuine failures in our approach to identify potential colonizer species: those species were not identified as potential colonizers, and yet are present on the islands in question—however, such failures were very few in number. Correlations among the distance matrices formed by the atlas (true occurrences) data and those based on model predictions from two source regions were all high and significantly different from random.

Our approach depends on a key assumption: that if a climatic combination in a potential target area (e.g., on an island) falls “inside” the niche of a species, then that species is considered as a potential colonizer species. This assumption makes the choice of climatic variables crucial. We used variables related to temperature and precipitation, and found a small number of species that represent model omissions, having colonized in spite of the island conditions not being represented in the source-area-estimated niche. We fit our models only across the source areas, regardless of whether species ranged farther afield, and used niche-modeling methodologies that do not extrapolate or do it safely; these methodological decisions can be relaxed and explored in future studies. Another possible explanation may be that island subpopulations have evolved in their niche dimensions, in other words, that niches of some species may be composed of locally differentiated sub-niches11, but such an inference should be subjected to further in-depth study.

Our filtering method also assumes that distributions are based on current niche variables (climates, in our example). However, it is most likely that distributions are the result of the history of past niche conditions12. One way of including such past conditions is to make predictions constrained by the niche history, that is, by proposing mechanism-explicit models (the mechanism may be based on demographic or contagion —cellular automata— models), and then simulating the distributional dynamics of the species in the pool. These would be a hugely demanding computational tasks, although software is becoming increasingly available and could be applied to specific examples of particular interest13–17.

A more theoretical observation is that any point outside a lower-dimensional niche (such as the 2-dimensional niches shown in Figs. 3 and 4) will also be outside any higher-dimensional niches of which the lower-dimensional one is an orthogonal projection. This observation follows from how higher-dimensional objects are projected to lower dimensions, by matrix multiplication18. The logical converse of the above is that a point falling outside a higher-dimensional niche may be inside a lower-dimensional niche, which underlines the importance of selecting niche dimensions appropriately. Indeed, Stockwell19 even proposed defining niches using as many dimensions as possible, but this approach will not only produce very small niche volumes, but also add much redundancy to the modeling exercise. As we have shown in this study, a few biologically relevant variables may capture the essence of factors affecting the distribution of a species.

We derive two conclusions: first, the environmental filtering method proposed here works substantially well for our dataset, in the sense that it predicts PAMs that are significantly and highly correlated with one derived from an authoritative atlas, though with the inclusion of additional species that are potential colonizers of the island(s) in question. Second, given that the correlation between the distance matrices of the two geographic pools assessed in our example was almost absolute, source area choice and source pool size appear not to be particularly relevant. Of course, before accepting this tentative hypothesis, it is necessary to test more source pool regions of other sizes, in other regions and with other taxa.

The source pool concept has been key in understanding the processes that shape local communities20, being used in studies to estimate local richness5, as well as other attributes of ecological communities such as dissimilarity21,22 and phylogenetic dispersion23,24. Source pools have been defined using biogeographic or physiographic regions4,25,26, have been based on regions defined by the similarity of species assemblages through clustering or network analyses27,28, or on overlap of species’ geographic ranges (i.e., assemblage dispersion fields,6,7). More recently, theoretical models that consider the history of climatic cycles in a region have been proposed, highlighting the importance of mainland-island connectivity through suitable areas (i.e., Constraint-based model of Dynamic Island Biogeography29. Environmental filtering, explored in this contribution, is considered an important factor in the assembly and structure of local communities30–32, but has not yet been incorporated in powerful ways into island biogeographic studies.

Our approach performed well in reducing (filtering) a source pool to a smaller number of species, yet without increasing omission error rates markedly. Our results, of large commission errors, confirm the long-held belief that ecological niche modeling algorithms “overpredict”33,34, which is not a model failure, but rather a failure of how researchers interpret niche model predictions: they are not predictions of presence, but rather predictions of suitability for presence of the species. By separating “performance” of model predictions into sensitivity versus specificity, we note that these model predictions indeed are prone to overprediction, which is key in identifying species that represent potential (but not yet realized) colonizers for a region in question. The low rates of underprediction (i.e., omissions) suggest that our models indeed are successful in filtering the potential colonizer pool to a lower, and more biologically meaningful number of species.

Our results focus on two families of butterflies. How general may they be? This needs to be tested, but we propose that the general method of filtering by ENM (ecological niche modeling) would apply to other biological groups. It is known that ENM predicts merely potential distributions and that other factors determine actual distributions35. Prominent among these are dispersal capabilities and biotic interactions36. Our prediction then will be that when modeling good dispersers, the proportion of commission errors will decrease, since the M circle in a BAM diagram36 would be larger. Similarly, we predict fewer commission errors when modeling generalist feeders. Perhaps these predictions may be tested using a Structural Equation Modeling Approach37.

Methods

Target area and source pools

As the target area to test our approach, we selected all of the Caribbean Islands with an area greater than 50 km2, including the Bahamas, Greater Antilles, and Lesser Antilles (note that we will hereafter refer to both islands and island groups as “islands”; Fig. 5). From these islands, we removed Trinidad and Tobago, in view of their close association with the South American continent. We divided the Lesser Antilles into Windward Islands and Leeward Islands to match the format used in the authoritative species list that we used (see below). We selected two options regarding source regions (i.e., geographic regions holding species that may have served as the source pool for island colonizer species): (1) all countries around the Caribbean Sea (“countries”; including 14 countries; Fig. 5), and (2) all ecoregions surrounding the Caribbean Sea (“ecoregions”; including 68 ecoregions; Fig. 5). A spatial layer to represent country outlines was obtained from the GADM website (https://gadm.org/), whereas a layer for ecoregions was obtained from Olson et al.38. We explored species pools defined initially by these two regions to test whether the size of the source pool has an effect on our results.

We used the set of species of butterflies from the families Pieridae (sulfurs) and Papilionidae (swallowtails) as our case study. From an authoritative atlas39, we obtained a list of all species of those two families reported in the Caribbean Islands (Supplementary Table S2). This data set was taken as the “true” list of species present in the Caribbean Islands.

Lists of species for the two source regions (hereafter termed initial source pools) were considerably harder to determine, as no comprehensive list of butterfly species is available for these regions. As such, to obtain species lists for the source pools, we obtained the list of species from the two families that had been reported inside the source pool areas using the tools and data from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF; 10.15468/dl.d75k8f; 10.15468/dl.z5w2t6). These lists were curated to match a standard taxonomy (i.e., synonyms were combined with the accepted name), and vagrant, migratory, and invasive species taxa were removed (Fig. 6). We also removed endemic species to the Caribbean Islands from the final lists, as the question at hand requires source pool species to be present in source regions. Using the remaining names, we obtained two lists of pool species: 98 species for ecoregions adjoining the Caribbean Sea, and 176 species for countries adjoining the Caribbean Sea.

Species data

We downloaded primary occurrence data corresponding to each of the species of interest included in our final source pool regions from GBIF, which comprised a total of 1,425,368 occurrences. We then performed the conventional series of cleaning steps40, removing records without coordinates, duplicates, dubious occurrences (e.g., points falling in the ocean), and records with coordinates of 0°,0°41. To reduce the effects of spatial autocorrelation and consequent model overfitting42, we thinned occurrences for each species by removing one from each pair of occurrences that were < 15 km from one another. After data cleaning and thinning, we had a total of 68,482 records for species selected under the country source areas, and 21,517 for the ecoregion source areas. All of these processes were done in R 4.3.143 using base functions and the package ellipsenm44.

Ecological niche modeling

Dozens of methods permitting ecological niche modeling have been developed and proposed45,46. Here, we fit multidimensional ellipsoids, which are equivalent to Mahalanobis-distance modeling47. We used this method for several reasons. First, it can be shown that Maxent, the most popular such algorithm48 fits exponentials of quadratic forms49. Quadratic forms are ellipsoids (when the matrix defining the quadratic form is positive definite), paraboloids, and hyperboloids. The last two predict the weird effects in which extreme values of variables are more suitable than their intermediate values, and thereby are not particularly biologically realistic. Ellipsoids, however, are much more in agreement with biological intuition50. As such, since biologically sensible Maxent models are exponentials of ellipsoids, we decided to model them directly as ellipsoids. Second, ellipsoids are natural models of the fundamental niche50,51 the one appropriate for the sort of application we intend. Third, ellipsoids are presence-only methods that do not require a specification of absences or background environments52. Fourth, ellipsoids are practical and fast, with fewer arbitrary decisions in the fitting process than when using an algorithm like Maxent. Finally, software in R is available to fit them44. For all of the above, we used multidimensional ellipsoids for our niche models (Fig. 6).

We created ellipsoid envelope models only for the species that had > 5 occurrences, and with data producing positive definite variance-covariance matrices (to ensure that they are ellipsoids53. We used species occurrence data obtained for the two source pools in the context of four environmental variables. These variables were selected to represent important factors that may affect these species’ geographic distributions: (1) maximum temperature of warmest month (bio 5), (2) minimum temperature of coldest month (bio 6), (3) precipitation seasonality (bio 15), and (4) precipitation of wettest quarter (bio 16). The variables were downloaded from WorldClim version 2 (http://www.worldclim.com/version2) at 2.5’ (~ 4 km) spatial resolution, masked to areas that cover the two source pools considered and the Caribbean Islands. Since all species were modeled using the same predictor variables, and variables are the only parameters to be changed, we tested the predictive ability of these ellipsoidal models via a cross-validation procedure. We ran a total of 10 iterations of cross-validation; for each iteration, a random sample of 75% of the data was used to fit an ellipsoid, excluding 5% of marginal data. Then, we quantified the proportion of remaining data that was predicted inside the ellipsoid and used that as a measure of sensitivity. We computed the mean and standard deviation of sensitivity across iterations to get a general view of the predictive ability of the models to be created. Ellipsoids had an average sensitivity of 0.81 with a standard deviation of 0.18; sensitivity was > 0.8 for models of species with > 40 records (Supplementary Figure S3).

With the data prepared and using the R package ellipsenm44, we created ellipsoidal models of covariance, excluding 5% of marginal data (i.e., omitting the occurrences with the longest Mahalanobis distances from the centroid). We created 10 replicates for each species based on distinct subsamples of 75% of the occurrence data, and used the mean (of the centroids and covariance matrices) of the replicates to represent the niches of each of the species.

Finally, we used the mobility-oriented parity (MOP) metric54 to explore how different environments in the Caribbean Islands were compared to those in the source pool areas. In this analysis, areas in the islands with higher difference values and outside the ranges of the conditions present in source pool areas indicate non-analogous conditions and higher uncertainty in model predictions. We performed MOP analyses for each source pool (countries and ecoregions) using the mop R package55.

Presence-absence matrices

We created three presence-absence matrices (PAMs) for presence of species on different Caribbean Islands: one with the species of the target area (the Caribbean Islands), and the other two with the species corresponding to the two potential source pools (countries and ecoregions, one for each; Fig. 6). In these matrices, species are in the columns and islands are in the rows; values of one represent presences and values of zero represent absences. The PAM for the target area had a species to be present in a specific island if the authoritative atlas reported it as present on that island, and absent otherwise. The PAMs for the source pools were created considering that the species was present on an island if the species’ ecological niche model predicted suitable climatic conditions to exist on that island. If no climatic suitability was detected on an island for a specific species, the species was considered absent. The PAMs were created in R with the package biosurvey56,57.

Jaccard, omission and commission errors, procrustes analysis

To explore how similar the species predicted as present in an island by the ecological niche models (considering suitable climatic conditions) were to the ones reported in the authoritative atlas, we calculated the Jaccard index. This assessment was done in R using the vegan package58.

We obtained the number and identity of the species that were under-predicted (omission) or over-predicted (commission) in each of the Caribbean Islands (target area) for the two source pools considered (countries and ecoregions) compared to the actual pattern of presences and absences. We also calculated the omission and commission rates across all species for a given island. We then explored the environmental space of the Caribbean Islands via a two-dimensional representation of the niche of the species being omitted (species present in an island that were not anticipated as finding suitable conditions by our models) to detect the climatic dimensions (environmental variables) influencing this result. To this end, we created orthogonal projections of the species niche in a two-dimensional space using the R packages ellipse59 and terra60. We visualized these results over the environmental cloud of points corresponding to the islands and source regions.

Finally, since we were interested in how to obtain a pool of species, we compared the results of using the countries and the ecoregions. This step was done by performing a Procrustes test61 on a non-metric multidimensional scaling of the Jaccard matrices of the country and ecoregion PAMs. This permutation test (n = 999, vegan package in R) was run on the two ordinations; it is a one-tailed test that counts the number of random residual sums of squares (ss) smaller than or equal to the observed sum value62. Ss is related to the correlation between PAMs by r = sqrt (1 − ss), so a test for ss is also a test for the correlation.
