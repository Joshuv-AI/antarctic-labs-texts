Optimizing Primary Closure of Anterolateral Thigh Flap Donor Sites Through Literature Review and Clinical Analysis
Authors: Ching-En Chen, Chun-Yu Ma, Tien-Hsiang Wang, Yu-Chung Shih, Chih-Hsun Lin, Szu-Hsien Wu, Hsu Ma, Cherng-Kang Perng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:The anterolateral thigh (ALT) flap is widely used in reconstructive surgery due to its reliable vascular pedicle, abundant perforators, and favorable donor site. Although skin grafting is common for donor-site closure, it has limitations such as reduced durability and functional restrictions. This study proposed a comprehensive algorithm for ALT flap donor-site closure.Methods:A literature review was performed to summarize existing strategies for ALT flap donor-site closure. Based on these findings, an initial algorithm was proposed to guide donor-site closure. This algorithm was subsequently applied in clinical practice by a single surgeon between 2021 and 2023. A retrospective analysis of patients who underwent ALT flap reconstruction during this period was conducted to evaluate clinical outcomes.Results:A total of 37 patients were managed using the initial clinical algorithm, most of whom were men and underwent reconstruction for head and neck cancer. Primary closure was achieved in 91.9% of cases, including 70.3% by direct closure. In cases where direct closure was not feasible, supplementary flaps were used to achieve tension-free closure. Both flap width and postharvest defect width were identified as key factors influencing the feasibility of direct closure, with an optimal flap width cutoff of 7.75 cm. These findings guided the refinement of the clinical decision-making algorithm.Conclusions:Donor site management remains an important consideration in ALT flap reconstruction. A stepwise approach combining planning and surrounding supplementary flaps enabled primary closure in most cases.

Takeaways

Question: Could primary closure of the anterolateral thigh (ALT) flap donor site always be done?

Findings: Across 3 years, 37 ALT flap cases were managed using a literature-based algorithm. Although 70.3% of cases achieved direct primary closure (including 21.6% via skin-splitting), the remaining were successfully closed using adjacent perforator (13.5%) or anteromedial thigh keystone flaps (8.1%), resulting in an overall primary closure rate exceeding 90%. A flap width cutoff of 7.75 cm was later identified to guide the planning.

Meaning: Incorporating preoperative flap design and adjacent flap techniques into a stepwise algorithm allowed primary closure of ALT donor sites in the majority of cases.

INTRODUCTION

The anterolateral thigh (ALT) flap, vascularized by the lateral femoral circumflex artery (LCFA) system, has become a workhorse of modern reconstructive surgery due to its versatility and numerous advantages. These advantages include a long, reliable vascular pedicle, abundant and consistent cutaneous perforators, customizable thickness, a favorable donor site, and the feasibility of a 2-team approach. Additionally, the donor-site scar is typically well concealed beneath clothing, optimizing aesthetic outcomes. Although a flap width of 6–10 cm is generally recommended for direct primary closure to reduce the risk of acute compartment syndrome, previous studies have shown that primary closure is more feasible when the defect is less than 16% of the mid-thigh circumference1 or in patients with a deep tissue-to-thigh circumference ratio less than 0.83 at the reference point.2 Although skin grafting is frequently used for donor-site closure, it has notable disadvantages, including limited durability, adhesions, muscle herniation, contour irregularities, pigmentation changes, and a significantly higher rate of hip and knee motion restriction.3,4

With increasing focus on patient quality of life beyond surgical outcomes, optimizing closure of ALT flap donor sites remains a significant challenge. This study retrospectively analyzed clinical cases to validate and refine an algorithm developed from a literature review, providing a practical framework to improve donor-site closure strategies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A clinical algorithm for ALT flap donor-site closure was developed based on a systematic review of 32 relevant articles published between 1998 and 2020. (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays the review of literature on assisted closure techniques for ALT flap donor sites, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E393.) (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 2, which displays the review of literature on delayed primary closure of ALT flap donor sites, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E394.) This algorithm was subsequently applied in clinical practice. (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 3, which displays the flowchart of strategy of primary closure of ALT donor site in review literature. W/T: ratio of defect width to thigh circumference, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E395.) We retrospectively analyzed the medical records of the patients who underwent ALT flap reconstruction by a single surgeon between 2021 and 2023, with donor sites managed according to this protocol. All ALT flaps were generally designed in a fusiform shape, with the width minimized whenever possible, including the use of techniques such as skin-splitting when appropriate. Feasibility of primary closure was assessed intraoperatively by the microsurgical team.

Patient demographics, comorbidities, surgical indications, donor-site details, operative records, and complications were collected and analyzed. Statistical analysis was performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (version 21, IBM Corp.). Descriptive statistics summarized the data, and group differences were tested using the Mann-Whitney U test and χ2 or Fisher exact test. Univariate and multivariate regression analyses identified factors linked to donor-site complications, with confounders adjusted by backward stepwise elimination. Receiver operating characteristic curves were used to find optimal cutoff values, with AUC calculated to assess accuracy. A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. This study followed the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional review board.

RESULTS

An analysis was conducted on 37 ALT flap reconstructions (Table 1). The mean patient age was 52.9 years, with a mean body mass index (BMI) of 22.8 kg/m2. Male patients comprised 73.0% of the cohort. Hypertension and diabetes were present in 13.5% and 10.8% of cases, respectively, and 48.6% of patients were current or former smokers. The most common indication for ALT flap reconstruction was head and neck cancer (64.9%). Donor sites were more frequently located on the left thigh. The mean ALT flap dimensions were 16.9 cm in length and 7.8 cm in width, with the final harvested defect averaging 21.2 cm in length and 10.0 cm in width. In line with the principle of minimizing additional incisions and using adjacent tissue whenever feasible, direct primary closure was achieved in 70.3% of cases, including 8 (21.6%) preoperatively designed with skin-splitting techniques. Perforator-based flaps were used in 13.5% of cases, and anterior medial thigh keystone flaps (AMT-KFs) in 8.1%. Skin grafting was required in only 8.1% of cases. All supplementary flaps remained well perfused postoperatively, with no major complications or need for reoperation.

Patients were first stratified into 3 groups based on the flap width: 8 cm or less, 8–10 cm, and greater than 10 cm. All patients in the 8 cm or less group (n = 8) achieved direct primary closure without requiring supplementary flaps (Table 2). Among patients with flap widths of 8–10 cm (n = 20), direct closure was feasible in 15 cases; the remaining 5 required supplementary closure using either perforator-based flaps or AMT-KFs. In the greater than10 cm group (n = 9), direct closure was achieved in only 3 cases. Of the remaining 6 patients, 3 underwent supplementary flap reconstruction and 3 required split-thickness skin grafting (STSG).

However, to establish an objective reference for determining the feasibility of direct donor-site closure, patients were categorized into 2 groups: those who underwent successful direct primary closure (n = 26) and those for whom direct closure was not feasible (n = 11). No significant differences were found between the groups in terms of age, BMI, comorbidities, smoking history, surgical indication, or donor-site laterality. Analysis revealed that both flap width (7.3 ± 1.5 versus 8.9 ± 1.9 cm, P = 0.004) and defect width after ALT flap harvest (9.4 ± 1.4 versus 11.4 ± 1.9 cm, P = 0.001) were key determinants of closure feasibility (Table 3). Univariate logistic regression analysis identified both ALT flap width and defect width after flap harvest as significant predictors of direct closure feasibility. In multivariate analysis, only defect width remained an independent predictor (Table 4). Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis yielded an optimal flap width cutoff of 7.75 cm (AUC 0.795, sensitivity 90.9%, specificity 65.4%, P = 0.005) and a defect width cutoff of 9.50 cm (AUC 0.841, sensitivity 100%, specificity 57.5%, P = 0.001) (Fig. 1). These findings guided the development of a revised algorithm for ALT donor-site closure, incorporating the identified cutoff values to improve decision-making and optimize outcomes. DISCUSSION

Various strategies for ALT donor-site closure have been reported and can be broadly categorized into primary and delayed primary closure. The latter typically involves the use of distraction techniques, such as external tissue expanders.3,5–8 Clinically, in addition to direct primary closure, multiple approaches have been proposed to achieve tension-free closure and minimize donor-site morbidity. These include preoperative strategies such as tissue expansion and modified flap designs, including preexpanded,9–12 parallelogram,13 tube-shaped,14 and skin-splitting15–19 techniques. Intraoperatively, closure can be facilitated by adjacent residual cutaneous perforator flaps, including perforator-based propeller flaps,4,19–21 2 V-Y advancement flaps,14,22 anteromedial thigh (AMT) V-Y advancement and transposition flaps,23–25 and keystone flaps.26,27 In cases where no significant perforators are available, nonperforator-based techniques, such as rectangular advancement flaps,28 medial bipedicle flaps,29 and groin flaps,30–32 have also been described. Although the former algorithm included a wide range of closure techniques, only 4 were ultimately applied in our 3-year clinical experience. (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 3, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E395.) This may reflect a tendency to favor approaches that are more straightforward and reproducible.

In keeping with our principle of minimizing additional incisions and using adjacent tissue, direct primary closure was prioritized whenever feasible. To facilitate this, flap widths were intentionally designed to be less than 8 cm, a threshold previously reported to allow for direct closure in up to 90.1% of cases,25 or otherwise minimized while still adequately addressing the defect. For example, in circumferential neoesophageal reconstruction after total pharyngolaryngectomy, a minimum flap width of 9.4 cm has been recommended to ensure sufficient luminal diameter, optimal speech function, and a reduced risk of fistula or stricture formation.13 To enable primary donor-site closure in such scenarios, modified ALT flap designs such as parallelogram (Figs. 3A, B) and tubed configurations have been introduced, maintaining narrow flap widths without compromising reconstructive goals.13,14 Additionally, the skin-splitting or “kiss” flap technique33 uses reliable cutaneous perforators from the same branch of the lateral circumflex femoral artery as the ALT flap, facilitating direct donor-site closure (Figs. 3C, D). Preoperative Doppler mapping aids in identifying multiple perforators, enabling flap division into smaller paddles to facilitate closure.

When the defect width unavoidably exceeded 8 cm and the feasibility of direct closure was uncertain, we first evaluated the possibility of using a skin-splitting design and planned it preoperatively. Nevertheless, based on our experience, skin perforators cannot always be reliably identified before surgery, and in some cases, only a single sizable perforator from the ALT flap pedicle is present. In such situations, the flap must be harvested as a single skin paddle, making skin-splitting infeasible. Thanks to the rich vascular anatomy at the ALT donor site, perforator propeller flaps serve as a reliable option for donor-site closure.20 We then explored the distal thigh area for additional sizable perforators, starting with those arising from alternative branches of the LCFA. These perforators were often identified during initial dissection but ultimately not incorporated into the ALT flap pedicle. For instance, when the ALT flap was based on a perforator from the oblique branch, we assessed the periphery of the defect after harvest for additional perforators from the descending branch. If identified, a perforator-based flap can be designed from the distal thigh and transposed to the central defect, enabling primary closure (Fig. 4). Adjunctive techniques, such as supercharging of the ligated perforator, can further optimize distal perfusion and flap viability when needed.21 In the literature, perforator-based propeller flaps have been successfully used for ALT donor-site closure, managing defects ranging from 9.5 to 12.7 cm in width. Complications were rare, and no postoperative gait disturbances or limitations in range of motion were reported.20,21

If no suitable perforators were found in the distal thigh, we turned to the medial thigh to identify perforators for designing an AMT flap. This flap, typically supplied by branches from the descending branch of LCFA or the superficial femoral artery, provides a good match in skin texture and color while preserving sensation.23,24,34 Reported cases have demonstrated reliable outcomes using V-Y advancement or transposition designs, with minimal complications and no observed functional deficits. However, most defects managed with AMT flaps measured approximately 7.8 cm, suggesting limited usage for larger wounds.23,24 The keystone-designed perforator island flap has also been used successfully for donor-site closure. Unlike traditional perforator flaps, keystone flap does not require skeletonized dissection but still benefits from perforator identification to maintain viability.26,27 By redistributing tension effectively,35 it achieves primary closure in moderate-sized defects while minimizing morbidity. Clinical results have shown good functional preservation and low complication rates.26 To reduce intramuscular dissection, tissue trauma, and operative time, we preferred the use of a simple keystone flap based on AMT perforators (AMT-KF) when feasible (Fig. 5). If none of these options were feasible, STSG was reserved as the final reconstructive approach.

Compared with a previous study reporting a primary closure rate of only 45.8% for donor sites with flap widths greater than 8 cm,25 our stepwise decision-making algorithm yielded significantly improved outcomes. This algorithm prioritized direct closure, followed by the use of perforator flaps from other LCFA branches, then AMT-KF, with STSG reserved as the final option. Among patients with ALT flap widths greater than 8 cm (n = 26), only 3 required STSG, and all had flap widths exceeding 10 cm (Table 1). In the subgroup with flap widths greater than 10 cm (n = 8), 5 cases were closed primarily using supplementary flaps. Implementation of this algorithm achieved a direct primary closure rate of 70.3%, which increased to 91.9% with the inclusion of perforator-based techniques. Consistent with previous literature, defect width was identified as the primary limiting factor for direct closure.1 In our analysis, a cutoff value of 9.50 cm was established, beyond which direct closure was generally unachievable, necessitating supplementary flap techniques or STSG. However, because defect width can only be assessed after ALT flap harvest, it offers limited usage for preoperative planning. To overcome this limitation, we used flap width as a practical surrogate and identified a preoperative cutoff value of 7.75 cm, higher than the 6 cm threshold reported in earlier studies,25 thereby underscoring the importance of meticulous flap design. Based on these findings, we proposed a revised clinical algorithm to guide decision-making (Fig. 2).

This study has several limitations. First, although various strategies and reviews for ALT donor-site closure have been reported,14,19,25,32 their heterogeneity precluded comprehensive categorization or analysis. Second, the small sample size and single-surgeon experience may introduce bias. Third, our focus was restricted to single-stage primary closure in trauma or malignancy cases, without using preoperative or postoperative adjunctive techniques. Fourth, although previous studies emphasized the ratio between thigh circumference and flap width, this metric was unavailable due to the retrospective nature of our study. Finally, as all patients were Asian with generally lower BMI compared with Western populations, the generalizability of our findings may be limited.

CONCLUSIONS

Although reconstruction of the primary defect is the main focus, optimizing donor-site closure is equally important. This study revisited available techniques and proposed a stepwise, clinically applicable algorithm. By integrating careful flap design with adjacent perforator flap options when needed, primary closure was successfully achieved in more than 90% of cases.
