The use of surgical template for palatal fistula repair in cleft palate using tongue flap: 3 case report
Authors: Coen Pramono
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Cleft palate may be found in many forms, and cleft palate repair aims to reconstruct the palatal defect by positioning the palatal muscle sling, providing for adequate palatal movement and good speech, and isolating the oral cavity from the nasal cavity. The presence of palatal defects in babies, children, adolescence, or adults may cause problems during feeding, eating, drinking, and swallowing, with the potential for liquids or foods to come out of the nose and significantly affected to speech. Cleft palate repair aims to repair palatal defect to provide an anatomical foundation for adequate palatal movement, isolate the oral cavity from the nasal cavity. The variation in cleft width, laterality, and velopharyngeal dysfunction among individuals correlates with a risk of failure in cleft palate closure [1].

Correction of cleft palate is usually done by the use of local flaps, but in such cases, complications might occur at the site of flap closure due to some conditions, such as the presence of large palatal defects. The failure is usually found in the presence of a fistula after the primary closure. This correction of the fistula might be difficult due to the fact that the local soft tissue condition has lost its elasticity or that not enough palatal tissue can be used as local flaps. According to most surgeons, the best effort for cleft palate repair surgery is carried out between 6 and 18 months of age [3]. Cleft palate primary repair in adolescence or adult patients has some surgical problems mostly associated with a wide range of defects and a small amount of palatal tissue, and the most difficult is secondary repair [4].

Reconstruction of the cleft palate is challenging and complex due to the amount of tissue left for primary closure after excision, compromised vasculatures on the repaired cleft palate, and limited pedicled flaps around the lesion [3].

The use of a tongue flap does not exactly fulfill the requirement for palatal repair as it has a different tissue texture as compared to the palatal soft tissue. However, the use of a tongue flap has some advantages because of its size, flexibility, mobility, good vascularization, and position near the opposite of the defect; therefore, the dorsal tongue flap can be considered the best compared to other flaps for reconstruction of fistula after cleft palate closure [5].

The use of tongue flaps gained popularity after the detailed study reported by Bracka in 1984. The rich of blood supply from the lingual artery and its four branches and its great extent of anastomotic network with the contralateral side contribute to the versatility of the tongue flap [6,7]. The study showed that the vascular anatomy of the dorsal mucosa has a distinctive vascular pattern that commends the choice of posteriorly-based midline flaps for closure of the defect of the soft palate and posterior part of the hard palate. A study of the vascular supply of the tongue by Bracka used the arterial corrosion cast technique, as shown in Fig. 1a, b, c, and d, which supported the consideration of the use of a tongue flap for palatal fistula closure [6].

Recurrent palatal fistula are a common complication in cleft palate repair, with an incidence rate ranging from 4 % to 35 % [6,8,9]. The main causes of surgical failures are the size of the defect, poor surgical technique, an inadequate amount of palatal tissue, poor tissue quality, poor vascular supply, and infection. Wound dehiscence can occur in partial or total separation issues of the previously well-approximate tissue due to excessive tension across the repair or if the tissues were too thin or traumatized postoperatively. Infection might also interfere with the surgical wound and usually manifest as fistula formed with variable sizes in part or completed of the palate repair. Clinically, it may present as an open wound infection with redness, purulent exudate, and pain.

The diversity of fistula forms after first surgery is related to the difficulty of making an exact design of the tongue flap. The aim of this report is to show an alternative measurement technique on the tongue necessary for fistula closure. The flap design can be difficult, as the flap should be enough in form and the amount to close the fistula exactly in accordance with the fistula form. The use of tongue flaps was showed with convenience because of their position located in the surface center of the floor of the mouth and mobile, therefore ease in positioning according to the flap form and makes this method a better choice for the closure of anterior palatal fistula than any other tissue. The type of fistula in the anterior palatal regions, usually found in irregular forms, and the quality of the surrounding palatal mucosa, makes difficult to predict the design of the tongue flap [10].

The report describes how to measure the size and shape of the palatine fistula using an alumina foil as a template. By placing and positioning the template on the tongue's surface, this template can be help the surgeon to have a good orientation how to make incisions and can also be used as simulation graft material to close the existed palatine fistula. The article serves as a manual for surgeons to help with palatine fistula closure procedures during surgery. In maxillofacial surgeries, templates are frequently used therefore this study will have an impact on the surgeons who execute the surgery. Based on the description above, we report 3 cases of palatal fistulae repair, and the report is based on the surgical case report (SCARE) 2020 guidelines [11].

Presentation of the case

Three patients, ages 13 and 19, asked for repair of their fistula in the anterior and mid-palate regions. Pass-history: all cases had received multiple surgical corrections, and the results showed with fistula in the anterior region of their palatal regions, as shown in Figs. 2a, 3a, and 4a. Studies to prepare the templates were made using a gypsum model taken from all patients, as shown in Figs. 2b, 3b, and 4b. The size of the fistula varied from 2,1 × 2 cm, 1,3 × 1.5 cm, and 3.1 × 1.7 cm. Based on these study models, surgical template were made using alumina foil that placed and traced on the surface of the fistula on gypsum models as shown in Figs. 2c, 3c, and 4c. The exact form of the fistula that drawn on the alumina foil then cut about 3 mm away from the edge of fistula to facilitated flap placing and suturing as shown in Figs. 2d, e, 3d, e, 4d, and e. Discussion

There are several treatment options for palatal fistula correction or defect. These include local tissue flaps. Revision of palatoplasty, regional flaps such as buccal mucosal flaps and pharyngeal flaps (either anterior or posterior-based), microvascular free tissue transfer radial forearm flaps, and prosthetic obturators were reported by some authors [7,[12], [13], [14]]. The application of tongue flaps for reconstruction of the hard palate was presented by Guerrero-Santos and Altamirano [12]. The use of this technique is based on the rich of vascular supply of the tongue derived from the lingual artery and its four branches. The useful study by Barcka in 1981 gave significant support for the advantage of the use of tongue flaps. The rich vascular supply from the lingual artery and its four branches and the extensive anastomotic network with the contralateral side contribute to the versatility of the tongue flap [6,15]. The difficulty in predicting the amount of tongue flaps related to the form and size of the fistula necessary to close the fistula was one of the main problems in forming good flaps. The use of surgical template significantly helped during the process of drawing the marking procedure on tongue surface. The alumina foil is easy to mould during the tracing process following the defect situation on gypsum models and can be sterilized under autoclave use saturated steam under pressure of approximately 15 pounds per square inch to achieve a chamber temperature of at least 250 °F (121 °C) for usual prescribed time of 30 min [16]. The result of the tissue amount measured under the guidance of an individual surgical template resulted in a suitable form that effectively closed even large palatal fistula. The result was that none of the patients developed flap necrosis, there was no bleeding, and all patients showed no functional disturbances of the tongue. Speech and eating activity were improved in all cases.

Conclusion

The use of a surgical template made from alumina foil during anterior palatal fistula correction may serve to support an easy procedure for preparing an individual form of tongue flap and may reduce the time of surgery. The use of a tongue flap for correction of the anterior palatal fistula showed convenience and was a good choice for managing difficult and challenging corrections of the anterior palatal fistula.
