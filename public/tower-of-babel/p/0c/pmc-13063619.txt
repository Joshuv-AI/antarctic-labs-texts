Effect of family-centered empowerment model on self-efficacy of children with leukemia
Authors: Omayma Mustafa Abu Samra, Sahar Farouk Hashem, Hend Wageh Abozed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Childhood leukemia is the most prevalent pediatric malignancy and remains a major contributor to childhood mortality, placing multiple burdens on affected families. Family engagement, a core element of the Family-Centered Empowerment Model (FCEM), aims to strengthen caregivers’ understanding of the disease, enhance their competencies, and improve children’s quality of life and self-efficacy (SE). This study aimed to examine the impact of the FCEM on the SE of children with leukemia. A quasi-experimental design was conducted at the Pediatric Oncology Department, Oncology Center Mansoura University (OCMU), using a convenient sample of children with leukemia and their mothers. Data was collected using a structured interview questionnaire, the Family-Centered Empowerment Scale, and the Self-Efficacy Questionnaire for Children. Pre- and post-intervention assessments were performed to evaluate changes in family empowerment and children’s SE. Results indicated that implementation of the FCEM was associated with significant improvements in mothers’ knowledge, family empowerment, and children’s SE, with large effect sizes observed across outcomes. However, due to the quasi-experimental design without a control group, the findings should be interpreted with caution. In conclusion, the FCEM shows potential value as a supportive intervention in pediatric oncology settings to enhance parental knowledge and promote improvements in children’s SE.

Introduction

Cancer remains the second leading cause of death among children worldwide, with approximately 400,000 new cases diagnosed annually among individuals aged 0–19 years [1]. Leukemia is among the most common pediatric malignancies, accounting for nearly 30% of all childhood cancer cases, with acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) and acute myeloid leukemia (AML) being the predominant subtypes [2–4]. Globally, ALL alone represents about 19% of pediatric cancers and continues to pose a substantial burden across both high-income and low- and middle-income countries [5, 6]. In Saudi Arabia, leukemia constitutes 34.6% of reported childhood cancer cases, highlighting its significant national impact [7].

Despite advances in treatment, conventional therapeutic modalities, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and surgery, are frequently associated with considerable adverse effects. A substantial proportion of pediatric patients experience chemotherapy-related toxicities, which can compromise treatment adherence and quality of life [8]. These challenges extend beyond the child, placing considerable psychological, physical, social, and financial burdens on parents and caregivers [9]. Reduced survival and poorer outcomes have been associated with treatment-related complications, delayed detection of relapses, limited healthcare access, and inadequate caregiver knowledge regarding symptom recognition and management [10, 11]. Parents of children with leukemia often experience elevated levels of psychological distress and increased healthcare utilization, reflecting the complex demands of caregiving in pediatric oncology [12]. Moreover, families must navigate these challenges within diverse cultural and social contexts, which may further complicate their ability to meet their child’s multifaceted health needs while maintaining family stability [13, 14].

In recognition of the central role of families in pediatric oncology care, family-centered care (FCC) has been widely endorsed as a standard approach that fosters collaboration between healthcare professionals and families and emphasizes parental involvement in decision-making and daily care [15, 16]. Building upon these principles, the family-centered empowerment model (FCEM) was developed to enhance families’ knowledge, coping skills, and confidence in managing chronic pediatric illnesses through a structured, multi-stage educational and participatory framework [17, 18].

Self-efficacy (SE), defined as an individual’s belief in their capacity to organize and execute actions required to achieve desired outcomes, represents a key determinant of effective disease management [19]. In pediatric chronic illness, higher parental SE has been associated with improved treatment adherence, better symptom control, and enhanced quality of life [20]. Empowerment-based interventions, particularly those delivered by pediatric nurses, play a pivotal role in strengthening families’ SE by providing tailored education, psychosocial support, and practical skills training, ultimately contributing to more favorable clinical and psychosocial outcomes [21, 22].

Accordingly, the present study aimed to examine the effect of implementing the FCEM on SE outcomes among pediatric patients with leukemia.

Significance of the study

The abrupt onset of cancer, together with its intensive long-term treatment and the immediate—and often enduring—complications, makes it an exceptionally challenging condition for families to navigate [23]. Understanding the disease-related changes experienced by the affected child and family members may provide a greater sense of security and enable families to manage the situation more effectively [23]. Many parents of children with leukemia report a substantial caregiving burden associated with managing the illness as a chronic condition. Creating opportunities for parents to actively participate in their child’s care, engage in caregiver support groups, and maintain open communication with nurses can enhance caregiver competence and foster empowerment [24].

Within the context of FCC, nursing interventions aim to strengthen families’ abilities to overcome health-related challenges and promote overall well-being [25]. Family empowerment represents a core component of this approach, emphasizing the family’s needs as the foundation of care and encouraging active participation through structured educational strategies [14]. Integrating empowerment-based models into pediatric oncology has been shown to reduce anxiety, enhance confidence, and build resilience among families, ultimately supporting better psychological adjustment and improved adherence to treatment [26].

Therefore, this study highlights the essential role of the family as a primary support system in helping children navigate the complexities of leukemia treatment. By prioritizing family involvement, the FCEM not only strengthens children’s sense of control and SE but also equips caregivers with the knowledge and confidence needed to provide effective support, fostering a more holistic and sustainable approach to leukemia care [27].

Operational definitions

Family-Centered Empowerment Model (FCEM)

The family-centered empowerment model is a structured framework designed to promote active family participation in the care and management of children with special health care needs. It provides a systematic approach for identifying families’ physical and psychological concerns, as well as their immediate caregiving needs [28].

Self-Efficacy (SE)

Self-efficacy refers to an individual’s perceived ability to perform novel or challenging tasks and to cope effectively with adversity across multiple domains of functioning [29]. It also encompasses the belief in one’s capacity to organize, execute, and sustain actions required to achieve designated performance outcomes [30].

Methods

Research design

A quasi-experimental design employing a one-group pretest–posttest approach was utilized in this study. This empirical design is commonly used to evaluate the effects of an intervention on a target population in situations where random assignment is not feasible. In the one-group pretest–posttest design, a single cohort of participants is assessed prior to the intervention (pretest), exposed to the treatment or independent variable, and subsequently reassessed (posttest) to determine changes in outcomes attributable to the intervention [31].

Study setting

The study was conducted at the Pediatric Oncology Department at Oncology Center Mansoura University (OCMU). The department, located on the seventh floor, had a capacity of 40 beds and provided comprehensive services for the diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up of various pediatric tumors referred to by the Children’s Hospital and other departments. Oncology cases were referred to the unit from different governorates across the Delta region, as it served as the specialized pediatric oncology center in the area. The department administered chemotherapy to children, and when surgical intervention or radiotherapy was required, patients were referred to the corresponding specialized departments.

Study subjects

A convenience sample of mothers and their children with leukemia was recruited from the previously mentioned setting. Participants were included if they met the following criteria: children aged 6–18 years, of both genders, diagnosed with leukemia at least six months prior, free from any other chronic illnesses or disabilities, free from developmental delays, neurological or behavioral problems, and willing to participate in the study. Mothers’ characteristics were not restricted.

Scoring system

Mothers’ knowledge was evaluated using a structured scoring system in which each response was assigned 2 points for a fully correct answer, 1 point for a partially correct answer, and 0 points for “don’t know” responses. The cumulative knowledge score for each participant was used to categorize overall knowledge levels: a score exceeding 75% was considered good, a score between 50% and less than 75% was considered average, and a score below 50% was regarded as poor.

Scoring system

The total FCES score was calculated by summing the scores of the three domains and dividing by the total number of items. Each response was rated on a five-point Likert-type scale, where 1 = never, 2 = seldom, 3 = sometimes, 4 = often, and 5 = very often. Higher scores indicated greater levels of family empowerment in the corresponding domain. All items were scored in the same direction, with no reverse-coded items. The total score was calculated by summing all item scores, yielding a theoretical range from 34 to 170. Family empowerment levels were categorized as high (≥ 127.5; ≥ 75% of the maximum score), moderate (102– < 127; 60– < 75%), and low (< 102; < 60%). The Arabic version of the scale was developed through forward–backward translation, reviewed by bilingual experts for semantic and conceptual equivalence, and pilot-tested for cultural appropriateness.

Emotional SE assessed the child’s perceived ability to manage negative emotions. The questionnaire consisted of 24 items, with 8 items measuring academic SE, 9 items measuring social SE, and 7 items measuring emotional SE. Children rated their ability to perform each task on a five-point Likert scale, where 1 indicated “not at all” and 5 indicated “very well.” Higher scores reflected greater perceived SE in the corresponding domain.

Scoring system

The subscales of the SEQ-C were calculated by summing the item scores within each domain and dividing by the number of items, thereby obtaining the average score for each subscale. A total SE score was derived by summing the scores across all 24 items. The possible total scores ranged from 24 to 120, with higher scores indicating greater perceived SE. This scoring system allowed for both domain-specific and overall assessment of children’s self-perceived capabilities in academic, social, and emotional contexts.

Tools validity

To establish content validity, a panel of five experts, two from the field of Community Health Nursing and three from Pediatric Nursing, reviewed the tools for content relevance, clarity, applicability, and comprehensiveness. Their feedback was incorporated to refine the instruments.

Tools reliability

The reliability of the data collection tools was assessed using Cronbach’s Alpha coefficient. The results indicated that the tools included homogeneous items, reflecting moderate to high internal consistency. Specifically, the knowledge assessment tool demonstrated Cronbach’s alpha of 0.903. The SEQ-C showed internal consistency values of 0.697 for the academic SE subscale, 0.943 for the social SE subscale, and 0.922 for the emotional SE subscale. The FCES demonstrated a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.899, confirming the overall reliability of the instruments.

Administrative process

A formal letter from the Dean of the Faculty of Nursing, Mansoura University was submitted to the Director of OCMU to obtain written permission for conducting the study. The letter outlined the objectives of the research and requested access to the annual statistics regarding the number of children diagnosed with leukemia who attended the center.

Pilot study

A pilot study was conducted on 10% of the study sample, comprising seven mothers, to assess the clarity, feasibility, and applicability of the data collection tools. The pilot study aimed to identify any potential difficulties or limitations that might arise during data collection. Based on the findings, necessary modifications were made to refine the instruments. Participants involved in the pilot study were not included in the main study sample.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS for Windows, version 20.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using Shapiro–Wilk test and expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between pre- and post-intervention mean scores were conducted using paired-samples t-tests.

Comparisons of categorical variables were performed using the Chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test when appropriate. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to examine relationships between continuous variables. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d to estimate the magnitude of intervention effects, and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were computed for mean differences to assess the precision of the estimates. Effect sizes were interpreted as small (0.2), medium (0.5), and large (0.8) according to Cohen’s criteria.

The internal consistency of the study questionnaires was assessed to evaluate reliability. Statistical significance was considered at P < 0.05.

Discussion

Leukemia remains the most prevalent childhood malignancy and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality, requiring prolonged and complex management that depends heavily on collaboration between children, families, and multidisciplinary healthcare teams [41, 42]. In this context, the FCEM has been proposed as a potentially effective approach to strengthen family involvement, enhance disease-related knowledge, and promote children’s SE [43], Sargazishad et al., 2018; [44]. Accordingly, the present study explored the impact of FCEM on children’s SE among those with leukemia.

The demographic profile of the studied children, characterized by a predominance of males and adolescents aged 11–15 years, is consistent with earlier findings [29, 34]. This distribution may reflect the higher biological susceptibility of males to hematologic malignancies and the increased detection of leukemia during pre-adolescence and adolescence. Such patterns highlight the importance of designing age-appropriate and gender-sensitive educational and empowerment interventions.

With respect to clinical characteristics, most children had been diagnosed with leukemia for more than one year, and acute lymphoblastic leukemia represented the predominant subtype. This finding aligns with Khanjari et al. [45] and supports existing epidemiological evidence that ALL remains the most common pediatric leukemia subtype. Prolonged disease duration may intensify caregiving demands, emphasizing the importance of sustained parental education and psychosocial support.

The sociodemographic characteristics of participating mothers revealed multiple vulnerabilities, including limited income, low educational attainment, and minimal external support. These factors may compound caregiving burden and influence parents’ capacity to manage complex treatment regimens. Consistent with Shoghi et al. [46], the predominance of housewives among caregivers highlights the central role of mothers in pediatric oncology. Differences observed compared with Kharyal et al. [29] likely reflect contextual socioeconomic factors that can affect parental coping abilities and access to resources.

Psychological distress among parents of children with leukemia is well documented [47, 48]. In this study, the limited psychological, social, and financial support reported by most mothers suggests an unmet need that may increase emotional strain and hinder effective caregiving. Enhanced social support has been shown to buffer stress, reduce anxiety and depression, and improve adaptive coping among caregivers [49, 50], emphasizing the importance of integrating psychosocial support within pediatric oncology services.

The implementation of the FCEM was associated with notable improvements in mothers’ knowledge regarding leukemia, reflecting the potential effectiveness of structured educational interventions. This improvement aligns with previous studies demonstrating gains in parental knowledge following empowerment-based interventions [49, 51, 52]. Enhanced parental knowledge may facilitate better symptom recognition, adherence to treatment regimens, and timely healthcare seeking, which collectively contribute to improved clinical outcomes.

Furthermore, the significant increase in family empowerment scores after the intervention supports the core principles of the FCEM, which emphasizes participatory learning, skill development, and reinforcement strategies [53]. These findings are consistent with Arief et al. [14], Crespo et al. [54], Boshagh et al. [36], and Nachshen & Mines [55], who reported improvements in caregiving competence and family functioning following empowerment-based interventions. Such improvements underscore the pivotal role of pediatric nurses in fostering family resilience and adaptive coping.

A central finding of the present study was the enhancement of children’s SE following FCEM implementation. This result is consistent with previous evidence linking empowerment strategies to improved psychosocial and clinical outcomes [56–58]. Improved SE may help children cope more effectively with treatment-related challenges, adhere to therapeutic regimens, and maintain psychological well-being, ultimately supporting better quality of life.

Recent controlled studies have further highlighted the potential effectiveness of structured family empowerment interventions. Randomized controlled trials and systematic reviews indicate that family-centered educational programs can enhance caregivers’ knowledge of pediatric leukemia and improve children’s psychosocial outcomes, including coping skills and adherence to treatment regimens [59–61]. These findings reinforce the value of integrating evidence-based empowerment strategies in pediatric oncology care, while also emphasizing the need for additional rigorously designed controlled studies to strengthen causal conclusions.

Moreover, the observed positive correlations between maternal knowledge, family empowerment, and children’s SE further highlight the interconnected nature of family functioning and pediatric health outcomes. These findings are in line with Alhani et al. [18], who reported that empowerment-based interventions positively influence multiple aspects of family and child well-being.

Conclusion

Based on the findings of the present quasi-experimental study, the implementation of the FCEM was associated with significant improvements in the SE of children with leukemia and their families, as well as in mothers’ knowledge levels. The intervention emphasizes self-empowerment, active participation, and shared decision-making, which may contribute to improved psychosocial and health-related outcomes. However, due to the absence of a control group and randomization, the findings should be interpreted with caution.

Implications for nursing practice and future research directions

The findings of the present study have important implications for nursing practice and future research. Integrating the FCEM into pediatric oncology can significantly enhance parents’ knowledge, confidence, and engagement, thereby improving clinical outcomes for children with leukemia. Nurses play a crucial role in this process by adopting a collaborative, family-centered approach that promotes shared decision-making and provides tailored educational interventions, particularly for less-educated caregivers who may require additional support. Strengthening communication, offering continuous guidance, and establishing structured support networks can further sustain empowerment and reduce caregiver stress. Additionally, the results highlight the need for incorporating empowerment-focused competencies into nursing curricula and ongoing professional development.

For future research, employing randomized controlled designs with appropriate control groups with larger, multi-center samples is strongly recommended to strengthen causal inference and enhance the methodological rigor of intervention studies in pediatric oncology settings. Furthermore, longitudinal follow-up assessments are also suggested to evaluate the sustained effects of the FCEM on SE and other psychosocial and clinical outcomes over time.

Comparative studies exploring alternative psychosocial interventions, as well as the use of digital or technology-based platforms for delivering empowerment programs, are also needed. Furthermore, investigating the social, cultural, and psychological factors influencing empowerment will help refine and tailor future interventions for families of children with cancer.

Limitations and strengths of the study

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the quasi-experimental one-group pretest–posttest design without a control group limits the ability to establish causal relationships between the intervention and the observed outcomes. The absence of randomization also increases the possibility of selection bias.

Second, the study was conducted in a single oncology center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare settings or populations. The relatively small sample size further restricts the external validity of the results.

Third, the reliance on self-reported measures for assessing mothers’ knowledge, family empowerment, and children’s SE may introduce response bias and social desirability bias. Additionally, participants’ awareness of being involved in an intervention study may have influenced their responses (Hawthorne effect).

Finally, the follow-up period was relatively short, and therefore the long-term sustainability of the intervention effects could not be determined. Future randomized controlled trials with larger, multi-center samples and longer follow-up periods are recommended to confirm and extend these findings.

However, study also possesses notable strengths. The use of a structured FCEM allowed systematic and consistent intervention delivery, enhancing the reliability of the results. The study comprehensively examined both caregiver and child outcomes, including knowledge, family empowerment, and children’s SE across multiple domains. Additionally, the findings provide practical and evidence-based insights that can inform nursing practice, curriculum development, and the design of future interventions aimed at improving care for children with leukemia and supporting their families.
