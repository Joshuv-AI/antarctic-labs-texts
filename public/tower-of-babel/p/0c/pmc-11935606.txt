Annual Cost of Implementing Intensive Systolic Blood Pressure Goals in the United States
Authors: Jordan B. King, Swati Sakhuja, Catherine G. Derington, Paul Kolm, Jennifer S. Herrick, Ransmond O. Berchie, Jincheng Shen, Tom Greene, Gabriel S. Tajeu, George Howard, Emily B. Levitan, Paul Muntner, Monika M. Safford, Jian Ying, William S. Weintraub, Andrew E. Moran, Adam P. Bress, Brandon K. Bellows
Published: 2024

In SPRINT (Systolic Blood Pressure Intervention Trial), an intensive systolic blood pressure (SBP) goal (<120 mm Hg), compared with a standard goal (<140 mm Hg), reduced rates of cardiovascular disease (CVD) events and overall mortality rates but increased rates of serious adverse events (SAEs). 1 Achieving an intensive SBP goal in the United States would require substantial investments and changes in clinical practice. We projected the annual cost changes resulting from treating US adults meeting SPRINT eligibility criteria to an intensive versus standard SBP goal.

SPRINT data (http://www.biolincc.nhlbi.nih.gov/home) and National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey data (www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes) are publicly available. The cost inputs we used are available upon reasonable request. The Insitutional Review Boards at the University of Utah and Columbia University approved the study.

We estimated annualized direct health care costs by applying costs from national sources and published literature to individual participant data from SPRINT. Total CVD and SAE hospitalization costs were from the National Inpatient Sample. Antihypertensive medication costs from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services National Average Drug Acquisition Costs, which provides pharmacy acquisition costs for generic medications by dosage strength, were applied to the medication regimen for each study visit. Office visit and laboratory monitoring costs were from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services Physician and Laboratory Fee schedules. Chronic health care costs were from the Medical Expenditure Panel Survey. All costs are presented in 2021 US dollars.

Annualized direct health care costs were calculated in SPRINT participants as the mean total undiscounted costs incurred during the trial divided by mean total years of follow‐up. We previously developed an algorithm to predict SPRINT participants' CVD benefit, specifically the absolute risk reduction in CVD events, associated with intensive versus standard SBP goals. 2

Costs were estimated separately by a randomization arm and by predicted CVD benefit and were reported as chronic health care (chronic CVD management and background health care costs unrelated to CVD care), acute CVD event hospitalizations, office visits, and laboratory monitoring, SAEs, and antihypertensive medications.

Using a health care sector perspective (ie, all direct health care costs regardless of the payer), we applied the mean annualized direct health care costs from SPRINT to the estimated 14.1 million US adults meeting SPRINT eligibility criteria previously identified in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey. 2 Of these, 7.1 million had low predicted CVD benefit, and 7.0 million had medium to high predicted CVD benefit. 2 The budget impact was estimated as the difference in costs for intensive versus standard SBP goals overall and by predicted CVD benefit. CIs for all estimates were derived from 2000 nonparametric bootstrap samples of SPRINT participants.

The mean annual cost per person with low predicted CVD benefit was $10 337 (95% CI, $10 125–$10551) versus $10 214 (95% CI, $10 023–$10442) for intensive versus standard SBP goals, respectively; for people with medium to high predicted benefit, the cost was $17 066 (95% CI, $16 786–$17353) versus $16 918 (95% CI, $16 638–$17243). An intensive versus standard SBP goal increases costs by $135 (95% CI, −$125 to $386) per person per year, overall, or $1.91 billion in the entire population (95% CI, −$1.76 to $5.46 billion) (Figure); intensive SBP goals were cost saving in 16.1% of bootstrap samples. Acute CVD event and chronic health care costs decreased (combined: −$4.25 billion [95% CI, −$1.03 to −$7.49 billion]), and antihypertensive medications, SAEs, and office visits and monitoring increased (combined: $6.13 billion [95% CI, $5.01–$7.26 billion]). In US adults with a predicted low and medium to high CVD benefit, an intensive goal increases total costs by $0.87 billion (95% CI, −$1.36 to $3.08 billion) and $1.04 billion (95% CI, −$1.90 to $3.93 billion), respectively.

The United States spends $355.1 billion (inflated to 2021) annually on CVD, $78.5 billion of which is on hypertension. 3 Prior analyses have estimated the number of CVD events and deaths prevented, the increase in SAEs, and the cost‐effectiveness of intensive SBP goals in the United States. 2 , 4 , 5 Yet the high hypertension prevalence in the United States means that implementing intensive SBP goals nationwide poses significant challenges. These include but are not limited to disparities in access to hypertension management (eg, affordability of medications and access to providers) and that the financial burden of implementation will fall largely on local health care systems. Addressing these multifaceted obstacles is essential to achieving the widespread clinical benefits of intensive SBP goals.

Our analysis has known limitations. The predicted CVD benefit, health care use, and medication adherence were from participants in a clinical trial setting, which may differ from real‐world settings. We did not account for potentially substantial savings from non–CVD‐related benefits (eg, delayed dialysis, reduced dementia). Though our inputs generally reflect national averages, payment for health care services in the United States is complex, and not all costs may be captured by our inputs or reflect costs incurred by local health care systems.

Our findings suggest that treating all SPRINT‐eligible US adults to an intensive SBP goal could increase direct health care costs by nearly $2 billion per year, approximately $135 per person treated. 3
