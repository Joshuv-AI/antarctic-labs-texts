Systematic reviews of medical machine learning: limitations of pooling AUCs
Authors: Amulya Gupta, Rahel Sileshi, Jeffrey Thompson, Amit Noheria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe medical literature has seen rapid growth in studies that develop and evaluate machine learning (ML) classifiers for diagnostics, accompanied by an increase in systematic reviews for synthesizing this evidence. Many such reviews use meta-analyses that pool performance metrics such as area under the receiver–operating characteristic curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity across ML studies. In our view, quantitative pooling of such metrics is statistically and conceptually inappropriate in most settings, except when the same model is being tested across different samples. ML classifiers differ fundamentally in training data, model specification, and validation strategies. As a result, there is no underlying ‘true AUC’ that pooling aims to recover. In addition, AUC is a sampling-dependent, non-linear, and bounded measure whose value depends on relative ranking within a dataset, which makes it unsuitable for aggregation. Pooling sensitivity and specificity presents similar problems due to threshold dependence, inconsistent reporting practices, and inappropriate weighting by test-set size. Outside limited scenarios in which aggregation may be reasonable, undue emphasis on pooled performance estimates obfuscates critical descriptors of model validity and generalizability. We propose that systematic reviews of medical ML models prioritize structured, descriptive synthesis of key study characteristics, including dataset composition, validation strategy, input modalities, outcome definitions, data augmentation, independent validation, and deployability. When summary performance measures are presented, they are better presented as stratified summaries in subgroups of comparable studies. A concerted discourse on standardizing reporting standards is essential to guide systematic reviews that meaningfully inform the clinical utility of ML models.

Introduction

Over the past several years, there has been rapid growth in the medical literature describing the development and evaluation of machine learning (ML) classifiers for diagnostic, prognostic, and related clinical applications. This expansion has been accompanied by a corresponding rise in systematic reviews that synthesize evidence across studies, each addressing a particular classification problem. For example, a recent article in this journal by Liu et al. summarized the use of electronic health record-based ML models for cardiovascular disease risk prediction.1 More broadly, a PubMed search for the terms ‘systematic review’ and ‘machine learning’ yields over 4000 results published between 2020 and 2025.

When conducted rigorously, systematic reviews can provide valuable overviews of existing evidence that can help identify gaps in the literature and barriers to real-world implementation. However, based on our experience as readers and reviewers, many contemporary systematic reviews of ML classifiers in medicine exhibit recurring methodological and interpretive shortcomings.2

The most concerning practice is the routine inclusion of meta-analyses that pool model performance metrics such as the area under the receiver-operating characteristic curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity across ML studies. As a representative example, the aforementioned article by Liu et al. pooled AUCs from 32 distinct models for cardiovascular disease risk prediction, reporting pooled estimates of 0.865 (95% confidence interval 0.812–0.917) for random forest models and 0.847 (0.766–0.927) for deep learning models.1 In another study, Shojaei et al. pooled results from 25 ML studies on hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, reporting a sensitivity of 0.89 (0.83–0.93) and specificity of 0.87 (0.76–0.94).3 In this point-of-view article, we discuss why pooling these metrics raises several statistical and logical concerns. The key assumption for a meta-analysis is the presence of an underlying estimand representing a single true effect; pooling disparate ML models violates this assumption because such an estimand is either not well-defined or non-existent. We also outline key tenets of systematic reviews of ML models that are frequently overlooked.

Why is pooling AUC as effect size inappropriate?

Several recently published meta-analyses report pooled estimates of AUC, sensitivity, and specificity for ML models across studies using fixed- or random-effects models for effect sizes.1,3–10 The first and most obvious limitation of this approach is that it pools performance metrics across fundamentally different ML models evaluated in different study populations, even though a well-defined underlying estimands for such syntheses is lacking. Traditionally, fixed- and random-effects meta-analytic models were developed to aggregate effect sizes for interventions or exposures, with the goal of improving precision in estimating a well-defined underlying parameter. In a fixed-effects framework, this parameter represents a single common true effect, whereas in a random-effects framework it represents the mean of a distribution of true effects. In both cases, the core assumption is that each study provides a noisy but informative estimate of the same underlying quantity, and that pooling improves statistical power and generalizability. This premise does not hold for ML model performance metrics. Each ML classifier represents a distinct analytical tool, trained on a specific dataset and defined by its feature set, preprocessing pipeline, modelling architecture, hyperparameter choices, and validation strategy. The resulting heterogeneity is not merely statistical but also methodological and conceptual. There is no single latent ‘true AUC’ or ‘true sensitivity/specificity’ that these studies seek to recover, which makes the use of traditional meta-analytic paradigm inappropriate.

Even if one assumes the existence of an underlying performance metric, or considers scenarios in which the same model is evaluated across multiple studies, AUCs remain inappropriate for pooling because they are inherently sampling dependent. The contribution of each observation to the AUC depends on its relative rank within the distribution of predicted probabilities in a given sample. This property fundamentally distinguishes AUC from traditionally pooled effect measures such as continuous variables (e.g. systolic blood pressure) or comparative metrics (e.g. mean difference, odds ratio, risk ratio), as summarized in Table 1. For such measures, the contribution of each observation to the study-level estimate is independent of the contributions of other observations. Consequently, under repeated resampling of the population (the central premise of frequentist inference), the individual contribution of an observation to the final estimate would remain unchanged. In contrast, an observation’s contribution to AUC is intrinsically dependent on its rank relative to other observations within the sample, which is unstable with resampling, even when the same ML model is applied (Figure 1).

Differences between traditional measures and AUC in the context of meta-analyses

Furthermore, AUC is a non-linear, bounded metric with properties that further complicate aggregation. Its null value is 0.5, which represents a non-discriminatory classifier, and it is symmetric around this point. An AUC of 0.4 does not indicate worse discrimination than 0.5 but instead reflects discrimination in the opposite direction, which may still carry meaningful information if the directionality is reversed. Treating AUCs as linearly comparable effect sizes ignores this structure and can produce misleading pooled estimates and confidence intervals.

Pooling sensitivities and specificities is also problematic, as these parameters vary with one another across decision thresholds. There is no standardized way of reporting these metrics in ML studies. Some studies report sensitivity and specificity at the threshold that maximizes the Youden index (Sensitivity + Specificity—1), while others report them at arbitrarily chosen probability thresholds. For example, in their work on ECG-based detection of reduced ejection fraction, Bachtiger et al. reported performance at the threshold corresponding to the maximum Youden index, whereas in a similar study, Sangha et al., reported performance at a manually selected probability threshold that achieved sensitivity >90% in internal validation sets.11,12 Therefore, combining sensitivities and specificities from a study report that optimized the Youden index with one that optimized for detection (high sensitivity), or another that optimized for exclusion (high specificity), makes little conceptual sense.

An additional statistical peculiarity in such analyses concerns the appropriate weighting of study-level performance metrics. In conventional meta-analyses of effect sizes, random-effects models weight studies by the inverse of their total variance. For sensitivity and specificity, within-study variance can be estimated using logit-based transformations. In contrast, variance estimates for AUC are inconsistently reported and, in many cases, entirely absent from the primary studies. When reported, the methods used to derive variance of AUC are heterogeneous, and commonly employed approaches can be biased estimates of the true variance.13

A further challenge is that reported AUCs may be derived from fundamentally different validation strategies, i.e. cross-validation, internal hold-out validation, or external validation. Cross-validation refers to repeatedly partitioning a single dataset into training and testing subsets (e.g. k-fold splits) and averaging performance across these resampled splits. For example, in 10-fold cross-validation, the dataset is partitioned into 10 approximately equal subsets, the model is trained on 9 subsets and evaluated on the remaining subset, and this process is repeated 10 times so that each subset serves as the test set once; a ‘95% confidence interval’ is then often derived from the empirical distribution of the 10 fold-specific AUCs (e.g. mean ± 1.96 × standard error, or percentile-based bounds), and therefore reflects variability due to data partitioning and model refitting within the same dataset rather than uncertainty in performance estimates from a single model tested in a single sample. Internal hold-out validation refers to splitting the original dataset once into separate training and test sets, with performance evaluated on the held-out portion. In this setting, variance is typically estimated analytically (e.g. via asymptotic or bootstrap-based methods) from a single test sample and reflects sampling variability arising from the finite size of that sample, rather than an empirical distribution of performance across differently trained models. External validation involves evaluating the model in a completely independent dataset, often from a different population or setting, with variance similarly estimated using analytical methods. Therefore, confidence intervals derived from cross-validation are not directly comparable to those from internal or external validation, and treating them as equivalent in meta-analysis can lead to inappropriate variance estimates and misleading study weights.

Finally, there is an inherent absurdity in weighting studies based solely on test-set variance, which is itself largely driven by test-set sample size. Consider a study in which an ML model is trained on 10 000 patients and evaluated on a test set of 1000 patients, compared with a study in which a model is trained on 1500 patients and evaluated on another 1500 patients. The former model is likely to be better trained and more stable due to the substantially larger training sample, yet conventional inverse-variance weighting would favour the latter study because of its larger test set. Even if pooling were conceptually defensible, such weighting schemes fail to reflect the aspects of model development that most strongly determine generalizability.

When and how can one pool performance metrics?

An attempt at pooling performance metrics may be fruitful when the identical final trained ML model is evaluated across multiple populations. In this setting, the problem approximates to classical evaluations of diagnostic test performance across populations.

When the same model output probability threshold is applied across studies, simple or weighted averages of sensitivity and specificity can represent a reasonable approach to pooling.14 In addition, diagnostic odds ratios, which combine sensitivity and specificity into a single measure, are amenable to pooling using the fixed- and random-effects models. Summary receiver operating characteristic curves and hierarchical summary receiver operating characteristic models are other common approaches that have the additional advantage of allowing for differing probability thresholds across studies. In fact, some of the published meta-analyses on ML classifiers have used these approaches, although they used them to aggregate different models.15,16 A detailed discussion of the assumptions underlying these methods, and guidance on their selection, is available elsewhere.17–19

What should a systematic review for medical ML models then provide?

While pooling performance metrics across heterogeneous machine learning models may be problematic, systematic reviews of such studies can still provide an important summary of the current state of the literature for a given medical application. Several core characteristics of ML studies remain highly informative when synthesized descriptively. We therefore outline a set of data elements that we believe are particularly relevant and should be considered for summarization (Table 2). Importantly, this list is not intended to be comprehensive or prescriptive, and authors of systematic reviews may choose to summarize additional or alternative information depending on the specific needs of their inquiry.

For instance, if hypertension is used as a model input and is defined using prospectively measured blood pressure values, the resulting feature is likely to be applicable in bedside settings. In contrast, if hypertension is defined using an EHR diagnostic code, the feature may be noisy and ambiguous. How to report performance metrics in systematic review instead?

As outlined above, the evaluation and comparison of ML performance metrics are governed by multiple methodological considerations. Consequently, there is no single optimal approach to summarizing model performance across studies. Unless a review is explicitly framed as a head-to-head comparison of models evaluated under comparable conditions, authors should refrain from emphasis on a single pooled performance metric.

Regardless, one pragmatic descriptive approach is to report the median performance metric, along with the range or interquartile range, within well-defined strata (e.g. by dataset, validation strategy, or input modality). This approach reduces the influence of extreme values, whether spuriously high due to data leakage or unusually low due to poor training or overfitting, while maintaining consistency within each comparison group (Figure 2). For example, one could summarize median AUC values for models that have undergone validation in the USA, or report median performance within a specific large dataset such as the UK Biobank. In our opinion, stratification by validation strategy is probably the most important, as model performance can differ substantially depending on the type of validation performed. Stratification by input modality may also become important when certain models rely on complex or non-standard inputs, as this can affect real-world deployment and applicability. Authors of systematic reviews should also consider stratification by country or geographic region in settings where clinical coding practices, disease prevalence, or patient characteristics differ substantially. Additional stratifying variables (e.g. public availability of the model or outcome definition) may also be relevant for certain reviews.

Unlike meta-analytic pooling, this approach is more statistically honest and preserves transparency by anchoring performance metrics within coherent strata, avoiding imposing a single, poorly specified summary estimand across heterogeneous studies, and allowing readers to directly interpret typical reported performance (e.g. median AUC), without collapsing results into a potentially misleading weighted aggregate with ill-defined statistical bounds.

We recently conducted a systematic review of ML models for predicting atrial fibrillation ablation outcomes, where we developed the graphical representation shown in Figure 3 to summarize model performance in a descriptive format (R code provided in Supplementary material).25 This visualization has several advantages. It conveys multiple dimensions of information simultaneously and allows readers to more easily scan and compare studies than with tabular summaries alone. Crucially, it shifts emphasis away from a single summary metric and may facilitate contextualization of reported performance measures. In that analysis, studies were grouped by geographic region, validation strategy, and training sample size, although alternative grouping strategies are feasible depending on the research question.

We acknowledge, however, that even such graphical summaries cannot fully capture the nuanced methodological differences that influence the interpretation of ML model performance. As such, these representations can be viewed as complementary tools rather than substitutes for detailed, study-level critical appraisal.

Another, more methodologically rigorous, approach is to evaluate model performance within a common benchmarking dataset by applying published models to the same underlying data. This enables direct comparison under standardized conditions and circumvents many of the comparability issues inherent to cross-study synthesis. However, its feasibility may be limited in practice, as it requires access to a patient-level dataset with appropriate input modalities as well as access to underlying weights and architectures of the evaluated models, which are often unavailable or insufficiently specified in published studies.

In the spirit of aggregation?

One could argue that the goal of pooling is not to recover a well-defined underlying estimate, but rather to provide a reasonable measure of central tendency that addresses a broader question, namely, ‘How well does ML generally perform in diagnosing this condition?’ Under this interpretation, meta-analytic models can be viewed not as rigorous inferential tools, but as pragmatic methods for aggregating performance metrics.

Even under this more permissive framing, however, ML poses unique conceptual difficulties. ML performance is not an intrinsic property of an algorithmic class, but a result of specific human design choices, including model specification, feature engineering, training strategy, and sample size. Performance therefore follows a winner-take-all dynamic, in which a small number of well-specified models could dominate clinical relevance, while poorly trained or underpowered models may contribute little meaningful information. Aggregating performance metrics from poorly specified models together with those from well-specified models does not yield a useful summary of ‘general’ ML performance, but instead dilutes the performance estimate of models that are plausibly deployable. Post hoc selection by restricting pooling to models that meet predefined performance thresholds, however, introduces circularity and undermines the methodological neutrality that systematic reviews are intended to preserve.

Conclusions

In systematic reviews of ML models, meta-analytic pooling of AUCs and other performance metrics across different models and studies is generally statistically and conceptually inappropriate. Importantly, this practice diverts attention from critical characteristics that are essential for evaluating ML models but cannot be captured meaningfully by single summary performance estimates. These include the size and representativeness of the training data, validation strategy, input modalities, outcome definition, data augmentation, and the feasibility of real-world clinical deployment. The authors should make an attempt to descriptively elaborate on these metrics. Additionally, even when summary measures are used, they are best presented in carefully designed strata based on study methodology. A concerted discourse among editors, authors, and readers is needed to standardize reporting of performance metrics in systematic reviews on ML. Establishing such a framework would enhance the relevance, clarity, and interpretability of these reviews.
