Subclinical myocardial dysfunction detected by speckletracking in asymptomatic individuals with exerciseinduced premature ventricular complexes
Authors: Muhammet Geneş, Uygar Çağdaş Yüksel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveExercise-induced premature ventricular complexes (EI-PVCs) are key indicators of subclinical cardiac dysfunction and elevated cardiovascular risk. However, their clinical significance and prognostic value remain unclear, particularly in asymptomatic individuals without structural heart disease. This study uses advanced echocardiographic techniques to investigate the relationship between EI-PVCs and subclinical myocardial dysfunction.MethodsAn observational case-control study was conducted with 141 participants (82 controls and 59 cases). Two-dimensional speckle tracking echocardiography (2D-STE) was utilized to assess myocardial function, including global longitudinal strain (GLS).ResultsParticipants with exerciseinduced PVCs exhibited significantly greater left ventricular mass index (80.1 ± 15.7 vs. 74.3 ± 12.7 g/m²; p = 0.035), markedly reduced global longitudinal strain (–19.6 ± 2.0% vs. − 22.2 ± 1.4%; p < 0.001), and elevated myocardial performance index (0.42 ± 0.07 vs. 0.38 ± 0.06; p = 0.002) compared to controls, despite similar conventional systolic parameters.ConclusionThis study highlights EI-PVCs as early markers of subclinical myocardial dysfunction, warranting detailed echocardiographic evaluation in affected individuals. The findings. underscore the need for further research into the burden, morphology, and patterns of EI-PVCs to refine cardiovascular risk stratification and management strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12947-025-00353-3.Keywords: Cardiovascular risk, Exercise-induced premature ventricular complexes, Subclinical myocardial dysfunciton, Speckle tracking echocardiography

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) represent a primary cause of global morbidity and mortality, necessitating robust risk stratification strategies [1]. In this regard, exercise stress testing (EST) is a pivotal diagnostic tool, extending beyond the identification of coronary artery disease to detect exercise-induced premature ventricular complexes (EI-PVCs) [2, 3]. Although traditionally considered benign, emerging evidence suggests that the presence of EI-PVCs may be associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular and all-cause mortality [4, 5]. The multifactorial pathophysiology of EI-PVCs is rooted in conditions that augment myocardial electrical heterogeneity, including autonomic imbalance, subclinical myocardial ischemia, structural heart diseases, and sequelae of myocarditis. Specifically, insufficient post-exercise vagal reactivation can create a substrate for this arrhythmia by facilitating re-entrant circuits and triggering ectopic foci [6–8] Indeed, EI-PVCs have been shown to be associated with findings of subclinical myocarditis on cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, even in individuals with preserved left ventricular function [9]. These findings suggest that EI-PVCs may be a harbinger of underlying myocardial pathology, raising the critical question of whether these arrhythmias are merely markers of pre-existing disease or actively contribute to the pathological process.

Recent investigations increasingly implicate EIPVCs as markers of subclinical myocardial injury or occult electrophysiological derangements [5, 10] Conventional echocardiographic parameters—most notably left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF)—typically capture only more advanced stages of myocardial dysfunction and lack sensitivity for detecting subtle, preclinical changes. In contrast, twodimensional speckletracking echocardiography (2DSTE), which quantifies global longitudinal strain (GLS), enables objective assessment of myocardial deformation, facilitating the early identification of subclinical dysfunction that eludes traditional measures [11, 12]. In patients with PVCs, cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) represents an important non-invasive imaging method for the identification of concealed cardiac structural abnormalities, that may be missed by conventional echocardiography [13]. However, this advanced imaging technique is burdened by high costs and limited local availability which may restrict its use in clinical practice. In this context, STE may be a more cost-effective and widely available tool for routine evaluation of subtle myocardial abnormalities.

The clinical significance of exercise-induced EI-PVCs remains controversial, particularly in asymptomatic individuals without underlying heart disease. This pioneering study aims to elucidate the association between EI-PVCs and subclinical myocardial dysfunction using an advanced imaging modality, 2D-STE. By evaluating early alterations in myocardial mechanics (strain), the study aims to determine whether EI-PVCs exert a direct effect on the myocardium or serve as an initial manifestation of occult cardiomyopathy. Although the cross-sectional design precludes establishing causality, this research is intended to generate new hypotheses regarding the pathophysiological basis of EI-PVCs and to provide a robust foundation for future prospective cohort studies.

Methods

Study population

This observational case–control study was conducted prospectively in the Department of Cardiology at Sincan Training and Research Hospital between December 2024 and April 2025. Eligible participants were identified consecutively via medical record review and enrolled until the predetermined sample size was achieved. All subjects underwent a standardized clinical assessment comprising blood pressure measurement, anthropometric evaluation, treadmill exercise stress testing, and resting transthoracic echocardiography. The protocol received ethical approval from the Çankırı Karatekin University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Protocol No. 17; Approval Date: 2024), and the study was conducted in strict accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant prior to enrollment.

Inclusion criteria

Asymptomatic individuals who were referred to the cardiology outpatient clinic for mandatory pre-employment treadmill exercise testing due to high-risk occupational requirements were considered eligible for the study. Participants were included if no PVCs were detected on a 12-lead resting surface electrocardiogram (ECG), but complex PVCs were observed during the recovery phase of the exercise test. Additionally, only individuals with negative cardiovascular stress test results were enrolled.

Exclusion criteria

Individuals younger than 18 or older than 70 years of age were excluded from the study. Those with a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) below 55% or with exercise stress test findings suggestive of coronary artery disease were also excluded. To ensure an asymptomatic cohort, individuals with a history of angina, claudication, left ventricular hypertrophy on resting ECG (assessed using the Estes criteria), myocardial infarction, stroke, cardiac surgery, or any vascular surgery other than varicose vein procedures were not included. Furthermore, individuals receiving antiarrhythmic medications for any reason, as well as those diagnosed with atrial fibrillation, bundle branch blocks, or other arrhythmic disorders, were excluded. Subjects with a history of syncope or a family history of sudden cardiac death were also not eligible. Known cardiovascular risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and hyperlipidemia constituted exclusion criteria. Finally, individuals with psychiatric disorders that could impair decision-making capacity and hinder study participation were also excluded.

The study’s case and control groups were selected based on strict inclusion and exclusion criteria to ensure scientific rigor and homogeneity. The case group consisted of 59 asymptomatic individuals presenting with EI-PVCs detected during treadmill exercise stress testing. These participants were included if they exhibited PVCs in the recovery phase of exercise but showed no signs of structural heart disease, as confirmed by resting surface ECG and transthoracic echocardiogram (ECHO). The number of 59 in the EI-PVC group reflects the total number of patients meeting these criteria during the selected time frame.

In contrast, the control group comprised 82 asymptomatic individuals who underwent similar testing but displayed no PVCs during exercise or recovery. The size of the control group was chosen to closely match the case group, ensuring a balanced statistical comparison. Careful attention was given to demographic and clinical alignment between the two groups to facilitate meaningful comparisons and minimize confounding variables. Thus, the selection of 82 and 59 participants reflects a combination of study design, statistical requirements, inclusion criteria, and the natural distribution of the target population. This approach enhanced the reliability of the study’s findings and ensured a fair comparison between the groups.

Methods

All participants underwent a comprehensive clinical evaluation to assess their cardiovascular risk profiles and overall health status. As part of this assessment, detailed medical histories were obtained, and fundamental physiological parameters such as heart rate (HR) and arterial blood pressure (BP) were measured. Additionally, participants’ heights and weights were meticulously recorded to calculate body mass index (BMI). No large language models (LLMs) or similar artificial intelligence-based tools were utilized during the preparation phase of the study.

Echocardiographic examination

All participants underwent transthoracic echocardiographic examinations with a Philips Epiq 7 ultrasound system equipped with a 3.5 MHz transducer, performed before the treadmill exercise test and in accordance with standard protocols. Conventional echocardiographic measurements, including left ventricular mass index (LVMI), relative wall thickness (RWT), left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), diastolic function parameters, and left atrial volume index (LAVI), were assessed according to the current guidelines of the American Society of Echocardiography (ASE) and the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) [14, 15]. Myocardial performance index (MPI), serving as a comprehensive indicator of global myocardial function, was calculated using Doppler-derived time intervals with the following formula: MPI = (Isovolumic Contraction Time + Isovolumic Relaxation Time) / Ejection Time.

For advanced deformation analysis, left ventricular global longitudinal strain (LV-GLS) was measured from apical two-, three-, and four-chamber views using two-dimensional speckle-tracking echocardiography (2D-STE). All quantitative analyses, particularly the 2D-STE measurements, were performed offline using QLAB 13 software by two experienced cardiologists (M.G. and U.Ç.Y.) who were blinded to participants’ group allocation (case [EI-PVC] or control group). Segmental function was evaluated using a 17-segment polar map that displays peak systolic strain values.

Exercise stress test

Participants underwent a symptom-limited treadmill exercise stress test based on the modified Bruce protocol (GE Healthcare, Freiburg, Germany). Systolic and diastolic blood pressure measurements as well as 12-lead ECG recordings were systematically obtained at rest, during each stage of exercise, and at the 2nd, 4th, and 6th minutes of recovery. The test was terminated upon reaching 90% of the age-predicted maximum heart rate or earlier if clinical endpoints such as angina, hypotension, significant arrhythmias, ischemic ECG changes, or extreme fatigue were observed. ECG data were manually reviewed by experienced cardiologists and validated using computer-assisted analysis software. PVCs were defined as ventricular depolarizations with QRS-T complex durations of ≥ 0.12 s without a preceding P wave.

Exercise test parameters

Asymptomatic individuals who exhibited complex or high-grade premature ventricular complexes (PVCs) during the recovery phase of the exercise stress test were included in the study. The complexity of PVCs was assessed based on the following criteria: the presence of more than 10 PVCs per minute (frequent PVCs), observation of PVCs with different morphologies (multifocal PVCs), detection of two or more consecutive PVCs (couplets or non-sustained ventricular tachycardia), and the occurrence of a PVC on the preceding T wave (R-on-T phenomenon). The presence of at least one of these findings was defined as high-grade PVC and was used as the inclusion criterion for the case group [10, 16].

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics, including mean, standard deviation, frequency, and percentage, were used to summarize the variables. Before conducting group comparisons, the normality of data distribution was evaluated using skewness, kurtosis, and the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test to determine the applicability of parametric statistical methods. When the normality assumption was met, an Independent Samples t-test was used to analyze the differences between the two groups.In cases where the normality assumption was violated, the Mann-Whitney U test was utilized as a non-parametric alternative. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was adopted for all statistical analyses. Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software, version 26 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA)(Fig. 1).

To assess the reproducibility and reliability of global longitudinal strain (GLS) measurements, echocardiographic images from a randomly selected subgroup of 20 participants were reanalyzed. For intra-observer variability, the first observer (M.G.) reassessed the same images two weeks apart. To assess inter-observer variability, a second observer (U.Ç.Y.) independently analyzed the same images from the 20 participants.Variability was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). The ICC values for intra-observer and inter-observer agreement were 0.95 (95% confidence interval, 0.91–0.98) and 0.92 (95% confidence interval, 0.88–0.96), respectively. These results demonstrate excellent reproducibility of GLS measurements in our study.

Result

A total of 141 individuals successfully completed the study protocol. The Case Group consisted of 59 patients who exhibited pre-ventricular contractions (PVCs) during the recovery phase of the treadmill exercise stress test, and the Control Group comprised 82 healthy participants without PVCs.The baseline demographic, clinical, and biochemical characteristics of the study participants are summarized in Table 1. The two groups were well-matched regarding age, gender distribution, body mass index (BMI), and smoking status. Furthermore, standard biochemical parameters, including renal function tests (creatinine, GFR), electrolytes (sodium, potassium), and lipid profile components (total cholesterol, LDL-C, HDL-C) were comparable between the groups, with no statistically significant differences observed for any of these variables. Triglyceride levels, while numerically higher in the Case group, did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.065).

Hemodynamic responses during the treadmill exercise stress test are presented in Table 2. Both groups demonstrated a similar exercise capacity, as indicated by comparable metabolic equivalents of task (METs) achieved (p = 0.435). Resting and peak heart rates and blood pressures (systolic and diastolic) were also broadly similar between the Case and Control groups, with no statistically significant differences detected, although peak heart rate showed a trend towards being lower in the Case group (p = 0.077).

Conventional echocardiographic findings and global longitudinal strain (GLS) measurements are detailed in Table 3. Standard structural and functional parameters, such as interventricular septal and posterior wall thickness in diastole (IVSd, PWd), left ventricular mass and volume indices (LV Mass, EDV, ESV), left atrial volume index (LAVi), ejection fraction (EF), and tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE), did not show statistically significant differences between the two groups. However, significant differences were observed in parameters indicative of altered left ventricular mechanics and diastolic function. The Case group exhibited significantly higher left ventricular mass index (LVMI) (80.12 ± 15.69 g/m² vs. 74.27 ± 12.70 g/m², p = 0.035) and relative wall thickness (RWT) (0.46 ± 0.06 vs. 0.42 ± 0.05, p < 0.001) compared to the Control group, suggesting concentric remodeling. Diastolic function assessment revealed significantly impaired relaxation and filling patterns in the Case group, characterized by a higher myocardial performance index (MPI) (0.42 ± 0.07 vs. 0.38 ± 0.06, p = 0.002), higher E velocity (83.17 ± 18.26 cm/s vs. 81.40 ± 13.95 cm/s, p = 0.021), lower A velocity (70.08 ± 17.61 cm/s vs. 56.95 ± 12.90 cm/s, p < 0.001), a higher E/A ratio (1.20 ± 0.33 vs. 1.53 ± 0.75, p < 0.001), a higher E/e’ ratio (7.64 ± 1.72 vs. 6.07 ± 1.41, p < 0.001), and a shorter deceleration time (DT) (138.35 ± 30.29 ms vs. 159.69 ± 39.82 ms, p = 0.004). Most importantly, global longitudinal strain (GLS) was significantly impaired in the Case group (-17.58 ± 2.00% vs. -21.22 ± 1.38%, p < 0.001) compared to the Control group, indicating the presence of subclinical cardiac damage despite preserved left ventricular ejection fraction.

Table 4 summarizes the distribution of characteristics and anatomical origins of complex exercise-induced premature ventricular complexes (PVCs) in the EI-PVC group (n = 59). In this group, the mean PVC burden was 8 ± 3. Among complexity features, where individual participants could exhibit multiple types, repetitive PVC forms (couplets or NSVT) were the most prevalent, identified in 35 (59.3%) participants. Other complexity findings included multifocal PVCs in 19 (32.2%) participants, frequent PVCs (> 10 per minute) in 7 (11.8%) participants, and the R-on-T phenomenon in 4 (6.7%) participants. Regarding the predominant anatomical origin of PVCs, the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) was the most common source, identified in 32 (54.2%) participants, followed by the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) in 18 (30.5%) participants, and an apical origin in 9 (15.3%) participants.

Discussion

This study is the first to employ advanced echocardiographic techniques to investigate the association between EIPVCs identified during the recovery phase of stress testing and subclinical myocardial injury in asymptomatic individuals without overt structural heart disease undergoing routine evaluation. Our primary and most compelling observation was a marked reduction in left ventricular GLS in the EIPVC cohort, despite their demographic profiles, baseline biochemical parameters, and exerciseinduced hemodynamic responses being indistinguishable from those of the control group (Table 3). Importantly, conventional echocardiographic indices—including left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF)—remained within normal ranges and did not differ between groups.

PVCs, although frequently regarded as benign, have been implicated in the development of PVCinduced cardiomyopathy when present at high burdens [17]. In contrast, the significance of lower PVC burdens—and in particular those emerging during exercise stress testing—remains poorly defined [10, 18]. In this study, we uniquely investigated PVCs detected during the recovery phase of routine preemployment exercise stress testing in active, asymptomatic individuals without evidence of structural heart disease, employing both detailed conventional echocardiography and GLS analysis. Our approach revealed that recoveryphase PVCs are associated with subtle alterations in myocardial function and structure: despite a preserved left ventricular ejection fraction and other standard systolic indices, participants with exerciseinduced PVCs exhibited significantly impaired GLS, indicating subclinical myocardial dysfunction. The methodological design of our study specifically excluded established confounding factors such as hypertension and diabetes, allowing for a focused evaluation of the isolated association between EI-PVCs and subclinical myocardial dysfunction. The detection of significant myocardial deformation, even within this “low-risk” and entirely asymptomatic population, further underscores the importance of our findings. Notably, the association between EI-PVCs and subclinical myocardial impairment in the absence of additional comorbidities suggests that this relationship may also exist—and perhaps be even more pronounced—in higher-risk patient groups. These results reinforce the hypothesis that EI-PVCs may serve as an early marker of myocardial injury, independent of traditional cardiovascular risk factors.

GLS has emerged as a more sensitive marker of subclinical myocardial deformation than conventional echocardiographic indices such as EF [11]. In our cohort, individuals exhibiting exerciseinduced PVCs during the recovery phase maintained a normal EF, yet demonstrated a pronounced reduction in GLS. This dissociation suggests that PVCs may not merely reflect isolated electrical phenomena, but rather serve as harbingers of underlying subclinical myocardial injury or contribute directly to myocardial remodeling through repetitive electrical and mechanical stress [9]. Indeed, just as PVCs can be provoked under controlled exercise conditions, everyday physical exertion and psychosocial stressors could similarly trigger ventricular ectopy; over time, these recurrent PVCs may accumulate to produce clinically relevant subclinical cardiac damage.

In addition to the observed GLS impairment, individuals with recoveryphase PVCs exhibited pronounced structural remodeling and diastolic dysfunction (Table 3). Specifically, elevated left ventricular mass index (LVMI) and relative wall thickness (RWT) are indicative of concentric remodeling, while abnormal diastolic indices—namely increased myocardial performance index (MPI), altered E/A and E/e′ ratios, and reduced deceleration time (DT)—reflect compromised ventricular relaxation and filling. Concentric remodeling and diastolic dysfunction are recognized as early pathophysiological precursors in the progression to heart failure and frequently co-occur [19, 20]. The presence of these subclinical alterations despite preserved ejection fraction underscores that myocardial disease or adaptive remodeling processes can initiate prior to overt systolic impairment [21].

The relationship between exerciseinduced PVCs and the subclinical myocardial alterations we observed invites two principal mechanistic explanations. First, EIPVCs—potentially arising from discrete ectopic foci or unique activation sequences—may subject the myocardium to repetitive abnormal stretch and contraction patterns, fostering microscopic fibrosis, concentric remodeling, and, over time, overt mechanical dysfunction manifested by impaired GLS and diastolic indices. Alternatively, EIPVCs might simply reflect an underlying myocardial substrate—such as earlystage cardiomyopathy, focal interstitial fibrosis, or a heritable channelopathy—that predisposes both to ectopy and to subtle structural and functional changes. Although our case and control groups were stringently matched for demographics and conventional clinical risk factors (Table 1), precluding overt confounding, we cannot exclude the presence of a covert myocardial pathology. Furthermore, It has been reported that PVCs emerging during the recovery phase may exhibit distinct autonomic and electrophysiological characteristics compared to those observed during exercise or at rest. This suggests that different EI-PVC phenotypes may diverge in terms of clinical outcomes and prognosis. Indeed, recent studies have demonstrated that PVCs detected during the recovery phase can be associated with subclinical myocardial deformation and that there is significant heterogeneity among EI-PVC subgroups. These findings highlight the importance of detailed characterization of PVC timing, morphology, and origin [10, 22].

In our study, a comprehensive analysis of the characteristics and anatomical origins of PVCs detected in the EI-PVC group (Table 4) provided essential insights into the potential underlying mechanisms and clinical implications of these arrhythmias. The mean PVC burden of 8 ± 3 per minute indicates a noteworthy level of ectopic activity, while the high prevalence of repetitive forms (couplets or NSVT present in 59.3% of cases) suggests the existence of an arrhythmogenic substrate rather than isolated electrical events. The predominance of PVCs originating from the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT, 54.2%) is generally consistent with the literature, where RVOT-derived PVCs are often considered benign; however, the presence of complex features in these cases may indicate a clinical profile distinct from typical benign RVOT ectopy. Additionally, the presence of multifocal PVCs in about one-third of participants (32.2%) and the detection of the R-on-T phenomenon in a smaller subset (6.7%) reflect underlying myocardial electrical heterogeneity and potentially an increased arrhythmic risk.

Taken together, especially when evaluated alongside our findings of subclinical myocardial dysfunction such as reduced GLS, these results support the hypothesis that EI-PVCs are not merely transient electrical phenomena but may also represent underlying structural or functional myocardial alterations. Therefore, detailed characterization and localization of PVCs in asymptomatic individuals with EI-PVCs appear to be important for more accurate risk assessment and the development of individualized follow-up strategies.

The clinical implications of our findings are substantial. Individuals exhibiting PVCs during the recovery phase of exercise stress testing should undergo additional evaluation for subclinical myocardial injury using advanced, highsensitivity modalities—most notably GLS—even when ejection fraction remains preserved. Such an approach may enable the early detection of incipient cardiac pathology and refine risk stratification. Importantly, prospective studies are required to ascertain whether these subclinical alterations predict future cardiac events—including heart failure, malignant arrhythmias, and increased mortality. Confirmation of their prognostic significance would justify the implementation of tailored surveillance and therapeutic strategies for this atrisk population.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. It was conducted at a single center, and due to exclusion criteria, such as the presence of comorbidities and the use of medications that could affect left ventricular systolic function, the sample size was limited. As a result, the generalizability of the findings to a broader population is restricted. However, these strict exclusion criteria were a deliberate methodological choice to test the study’s primary hypothesis free from confounding factors and thus maximize internal validity. Secondly, given the multidimensional and complex nature of left ventricular deformation, our study focused solely on longitudinal strain measurements. The absence of radial and circumferential strain parameters precluded a more comprehensive assessment of left ventricular function. Lastly, due to time and budget constraints, rhythm Holter monitoring was not performed to evaluate the daily frequency, morphology, and patterns of PVCs, nor was coronary anatomy imaging conducted in individuals with EI-PVC. Furthermore, autonomic tone, which is a known modulator of both ventricular ectopy and myocardial function, was not directly assessed (e.g., via heart rate variability). Future studies with larger sample sizes, multicenter designs, and comprehensive three-dimensional strain analyses—encompassing longitudinal, radial, and circumferential strain—could enhance the accuracy of the findings and contribute to a deeper understanding of left ventricular dysfunction.

Conclusion

In our study, significant subclinical myocardial deformation was detected in the left ventricle of individuals with EI-PVCs through GLS analysis. Our findings suggest that EI-PVCs could serve as an early marker for underlying subclinical myocardial dysfunction. Early identification of potential cardiac dysfunction may help predict increased cardiovascular risk in these individuals and contribute to the optimization of risk management strategies. However, there is currently no consensus in the literature or current guidelines regarding the clinical diagnosis, follow-up, and treatment approaches for EI-PVCs. These results underscore the need for close monitoring of individuals with EI-PVCs, detailed cardiovascular risk evaluation, and the development of definitive diagnostic criteria and standardized treatment protocols. Multicenter, randomized controlled trials with larger patient populations and long-term follow-up are necessary to establish more precise and standardized diagnostic, treatment, and follow-up strategies for individuals with EI-PVCs.
