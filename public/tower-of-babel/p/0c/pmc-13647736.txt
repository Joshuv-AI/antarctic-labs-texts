Facilitators of good consultations in primary health care clinics in Botswana: a qualitative study of patients’ and doctors’ views
Authors: V. Setlhare, S. Madiba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe behaviours and attributes of doctors affect the quality of consultations. Although good consultations improve the quality of care, there is a dearth of evidence on factors that facilitate good consultations.AimThe aim of the study was to explore factors that facilitate good consultations in Botswana public primary healthcare (PHC) clinics from the perspective of doctors and patients.SettingWe conducted the study in public (PHC) clinics in four health districts of Botswana. Patients with a wide range of acute and chronic diseases sought health care from these clinics. Nurses, pharmacists, and non-specialist doctors were some of the healthcare (HCWs) who gave services to these patients.MethodsThis was a qualitative study in which consenting adult participants (doctors and patients) with consultation experience were purposively selected to ensure variety in age, gender, work experience, place of residence, education and employment status. Participants were recruited from public PHC clinics in several health districts in Botswana. Audiotaped semi-structured interviews were used to collect data, until saturation. Data were analysed for themes.ResultsSeventeen doctors and 32 patients participated in this study.Thematic analysis of the data showed that participants thought the facilitators of good consultations were, creating a conducive environment for the consultation, providing enough time for the consultation, continuity of care, effective communication, and creating an environment to maintain confidentiality.ConclusionThe facilitators of good consultations included creating rapport between doctors and patients, improving the ambience of the consultation rooms, ensuring continuity of care, maintaining effective and mutually acceptable communication, and satisfying patients’ desire for the confidentiality of consultations. On deep reflection, we can see that these facilitators are subject to central government, local government, clinic, and individual level factors. These factors interact with each other and are subsumed in a complex interactive health system.

Background

The consultation is an essential component of health care and it is regarded as the most important healthcare process [1, 2]. The behaviours and attributes of doctors are central to the consultation and affect consultation quality [3]. Therefore, doctor patient interaction during consultation is the foundation on which quality health care is built [3–5]. The consultation is crucial in healthcare because it affects adherence and health seeking behaviour [6]. It lies at the core of healthcare provision, because good consultations improve health outcomes [7, 8]. We define a good consultation as one in which a patient’s needs have been met and the doctor or healthcare worker thinks he has provided a service that is mutually satisfying. Therefore, facilitating a good consultation, that improves the quality of healthcare, should be one of the main objectives of health systems.

The consultation is complex as it involves many factors that contribute to doctor patient interaction [9, 10]. There are several inhibitors and facilitators of the doctor-patient interaction during the consultation [11]. Facilitators of the consultation are the factors that need to be present for the consultation to meet the expectations of the patient and the doctor. It is however crucial for doctors to always remember that the consultation, or doctor patient encounter, is primarily to satisfy the needs of the patient.

Patient-centeredness leads to improved patient satisfaction with the consultation as well as the care received [12, 13]. Patients want to be active participants in decisions about their health [14]. Patient-centred care (PCC) is therefore, likely to bring about patient satisfaction with care received and PCC is increasingly being recognized as key to quality healthcare in many settings [12, 15, 16]. PCC is a model in which doctors are encouraged to partner with patients to design and deliver personalized care [13]. However the extent to which PCC is applied in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) is not clear [16].

Adequate time provision is essential for good consultations but is challenging to implement, especially in an environment of healthcare worker (HCW) shortages and high patient volumes [17]. Adequate time provision ensures effective communication and diagnosis during consultations [18], thus facilitating good consultations.

Continuity of care (COC) can be viewed as the provision of uninterrupted quality care, by one or more service providers, over a long period [19, 20]. COC has been associated with increased patient satisfaction, greater adherence, reduced health service utilisation, and number of complications [19, 21]. In many settings in low- and middle-income countries, COC has not yet been widely feasible [19, 22]. This is because of loss of patients’ records, transfers of HCWs, rotation of HCWs among a group of clinics, and migration of patients. However COC is necessary in LMIC health systems, to improve consultations and access to quality care [22].

Many facilitators of the consultation are beyond patient or doctor attributes. Health facility factors, such as good infrastructure, and ensuring privacy and confidentiality, have been associated with improved doctor patient interactions [23, 24]. Long waiting times are inversely related to patient satisfaction [25], and this probably contributes to unsatisfactory doctor patient interaction during consultations. Quality consultations affect patients’ willingness to reveal information, adherence to treatment, patient satisfaction and improve health outcomes [11]. This makes it very important to facilitate good consultations.

There is a need for a deeper understanding of the facilitators of the consultation in Botswana and other LMICs. This may help improve health outcomes. The Botswana public healthcare clinics do not have adequate equipment and technology for disease diagnosis. On the contrary the clinics lack equipment, have equipment shortages, and even the equipment that is available breaks down because of inadequate servicing. Equipment challenges compromise health care [26]. Thus, presently the public primary healthcare clinics depend, to a large extent, on good consultations for healthcare. The aim of this study was to explore the facilitators of a good consultation in primary healthcare facilities in Botswana, from the perspective of doctors and patients.

Methods

This qualitative study used semi-structured interviews conducted with 49 doctors and patients in primary health care (PHC) settings in Botswana between September 2018 and October 2019. Seven public PHC clinics located in four health districts of Botswana were selected purposively such that there was a geographical, tribal, and socioeconomic diversity of patients. These were busy clinics by Botswana standards, that provided services to around 1,000 patients per week. The selected clinics provided emergency and chronic care treatment for many conditions like trauma, HIV infection, TB, sexually transmitted infections, hypertension, diabetes, and mental health. They also provided child health services, and maternity services. The clinics were run by nurses mostly, but selected clinics had a doctor in attendance daily, or once, or twice weekly. All selected clinics had a doctor present at least once a week. Public PHC clinics provide free services to about 93% of the Botswana population. In 2019, out of 4,925,429 consultations in public clinics, the three most common consultations were for “other diseases” (34%), hypertension (12.5%) and musculoskeletal problems (11.6%) [27].

Patients 18 years and older who had consulted a doctor at least twice in the previous 12 months were eligible to participate. Purposive sampling to ensure participant variation [28] was applied in sampling so that participants varied in residence, age, gender, occupational status, educational attainment, and medical conditions. This variety ensured that essential and variable features of the consultation, as experienced by diverse patients, informed study findings [29].

The study population also included non-specialist doctors who visit primary healthcare clinics at least once a week. These doctors were eligible to participate if they had practised medicine in Botswana for two years or more and worked in the selected clinics during the study period. Variety in doctors was applied using the same purposive sampling guide as for patients.

Sampling was purposive as described above. Patients and doctors were recruited from seven public PHC clinics in four widely separated districts of Botswana. Patients were recruited before or after collecting their medications, in the clinic, and most patient interviews were done in a room that was provided by the clinic. Doctors were recruited when an opportunity arose during their busy schedules, in the same clinics. Most doctor interviews were conducted away from the clinics at a venue that was mutually acceptable to the doctor and research assistant.

Ethical considerations: This study was approved by the Sefako Makgatho Health Sciences University Research Ethics Committee (SMUREC/M/115/2018/PG) and it was also approved by the Ministry of Health (Botswana), Health Research and Development Division (HPDME 13/18/1). Prior to recruiting patients in the clinics, the matron was shown the ethical approvals for the research, and the research was explained to her. This was done to alleviate any misunderstandings about the presence of research assistants in the clinics, and to help secure a room for conducting patient interviews. Though the participants were made aware that they were participating in a study, the effect of observation was minimised because the research assistants were trained on making participants comfortable [30]. The research assistants were female adults who were trained in how to conduct interviews by the second author. They were indigenous university graduates who spoke both the local and English language fluently. These research assistants had prior experience of being treated in public PHC clinics and consulting with doctors. In the final analysis the effect of observation may not be completely avoided in qualitative research [31]. Participants gave informed written consent to participate in the study after it was explained to them. For participants who could not read nor write, we were going to ask them to write an X against their name to indicate consent, but we did not encounter this problem in this study.

Study data was stored in a password-locked laptop that was could be accessed by the researcher only.

A pilot study was done in one public clinic. The pilot study helped the research team to assess the extent to which participants understood the questions in the interview guide, the appropriateness of the questions, how best to ask the questions, and the length of the interviews. This information helped the study team to adapt the interview guide to suit the context.

Face-to-face interviews with patients and doctors using interview guides with open ended questions, were conducted. Separate interview guides (included as a supplementary document) for doctors and patients were designed after reviewing the literature on the topic [32].

Participants were asked to discuss what could be done to facilitate consultations, and what patients, doctors, government, and the community could do to facilitate consultations, respectively. Probes, reflecting back, requests for further explanations, and iterative questioning were used to gather deep data. Patient interviews were conducted in a local language while doctor interviews were in English. The interviews were audio-recorded after giving a full explanation of the research, discussing ethical aspects that the participants needed to know, and obtaining written informed consent. Field notes and interview notes were collected. Data was collected until saturation [28]. Data saturation was deemed to be reached in an interview when a participant was overly repeating himself, despite probes for more information [33]. Participant recruitment was stopped when new interviews were increasingly diminishing in yielding new data [34]. Four models of data saturation have been described, and we used the model that determines data saturation at the data collection stage [35]. It was the point at which there were diminishing returns of new information from new interviews. Data saturation was reached after 32 patient and 17 doctor participants were interviewed.

A short demographic tool was administered at the end of the interview. Small tokens of appreciation (about US$2 or some refreshments) were given to patients at the end of the interview.

Data were analysed for themes as described by Braun and Clarke [36], by VS, and SM who were qualitative researchers who taught qualitative research at postgraduate level and have qualitative research publications. VS is a family physician who worked in the public PHC clinics in Botswana for many years and is familiar with patients and healthcare workers in this setting. SM was trained as a registered nurse and midwife before pursing an academic career in universities as a public health specialist. We used a realist, semantic approach to the analysis because we wanted to present the perceptions, reality, and lived experiences of participants as communicated to research assistants during interviews.

VS listened to audiotapes of the interviews as they came in from the research assistant and he recognised patterns in what participants were saying. The interviews were transcribed verbatim. VS, and SM read all the transcripts (patient and doctor transcripts) several times to familiarize themselves with the data. They independently coded two transcripts (one for patient participants and the other for doctor participants), line by line, using manual coding. They independently underlined or circled the salient sentences or phrases which conveyed the main point or points of each line, until a transcript was fully analysed. They coded (gave a name) to each underlined or circled sentence or phrase. They discussed the codes that they independently came up with from the patient transcript, and from the doctor transcript. From the patient codes, VS and SM agreed, after several discussions, on the codes that would be used to code the rest of the patient data set. They then looked at their codes from the doctor transcript and after many discussions agreed on the codes that would be used to code the doctor data set.

Then the authors, together, engaged in an iterative process of grouping and regrouping the patient codes to create themes and subthemes. They revisited the created themes and subthemes several times, redefining and regrouping them until the final patient codebook was agreed upon. A similar process was followed for doctor codes to arrive at the final codebook for the doctor data set. VS then uploaded all the transcripts and final codebooks into NVivo 12 software for coding all the transcripts; the patient and doctor data sets being analysed separately, using their respective codebooks. New codes emerged during NVivo 12 coding, and VS and SM discussed them and reached consensus on these codes. The new codes were slotted into the existing themes and subthemes, or into new themes and subthemes, after VS and SM reached consensus. On conclusion of the NVivo 12 coding, VS and SM engaged in cycles of re-examining the themes and subthemes from each data set, forming new themes and subthemes for each until they were satisfied with the analysis.

The themes and subthemes from the two data sets were later merged to represent the findings of the study. This merging was done through iterative cycles of merging and breaking themes and subthemes until the authors were satisfied that they had created themes and subthemes that represented the whole data set (patients and doctors).

For rigour and credibility, the data was collected by two research assistants to minimise data collection bias. We used investigator triangulation throughout all stages of data analysis, and interpretation to enhance the credibility of the study [37]. The two authors initially coded two transcripts independently as described above, and later iteratively discussed and agreed on the codes, themes, and subthemes through several rounds of discussions. Data triangulation was applied because data was collected from patients and doctors.

VS achieved prolonged engagement with the data by listening to the audio recordings of the interviews repeatedly and by reviewing translations of local language transcripts. He also reviewed the back translations of the English language translations of the local language transcripts. Other strategies of [38] credibility included transcribing the audio recordings verbatim. Bracketing was used throughout the data analysis process [39]. The two authors tried to be always aware of their preferences (one author was a family physician, and the other was a registered nurse and midwife) as to what facilitators of should be. They tried not to allow these preferences to influence the way they engaged with the data. Using the NVivo12 software program for data analysis, also improved rigour. NVivo made it easier to determine the frequency of each code and this helped us understand the weight of each code.

Results

A few of the participants whose interviews were moved forward did not turn up for interviews. Their ages ranged from 19 to 67 years with a mean age of 38 years and most of them were female (22/32). Half of them reported being employed and more than half (18/32) had secondary or tertiary school education. The shortest interview was 16 min. 43 s. While the longest interview was 41 min. 71 s. The average time for the interviews was 30 min 08 s, and most interviews took 30 to 40 min. Thus, the length of the interviews were within the expected time limits of semi-structured interviews [40, 41].

The main themes that describe facilitators of the consultation were (1) creating a conducive environment, (2) time provision, (3) confidentiality and privacy, and (4) effective communication. The identified subthemes were a pleasant physical environment, making the patient feel welcome, building trust, mutual respect, adequate consultation time, and detailed explanations.

Creating a conducive environment for consultation

The three subthemes of a conducive environment were, a good social environment, a good physical environment, mutual respect, and trust building.

Pleasant physical environment

Participants believed that patients want to be consulted in a physically comfortable and welcoming environment.

‘The consultation should be in a screened room with comfortable chairs for both the doctor and the patient. A clean room with a good atmosphere, laid out properly so that the patient can feel relaxed and not be anxious or scared of telling the doctor what is happening” (34-year-old doctor with 9 years of experience).

‘The consultation should be in a screened room with comfortable chairs for both the doctor and the patient. A clean room with a good atmosphere, laid out properly so that the patient can feel relaxed and not be anxious or scared of telling the doctor what is happening” (34-year-old doctor with 9 years of experience).

“Facilities must improve, and people must not be in bad infrastructures…… They must install air-cons in your rooms to avoid being scorched by the heat as we converse”, (a female, 67year old patient).

“Facilities must improve, and people must not be in bad infrastructures…… They must install air-cons in your rooms to avoid being scorched by the heat as we converse”, (a female, 67year old patient).

Social environment

Participants felt that doctors should create an environment that is welcoming to patients. They mentioned that doctors’ personal conduct and appearance should be welcoming to patients.

‘Show patients that they are most welcome in your consultation room, because that is what determines a good consultation process’ (31-year-old doctor with 6 years of service).

‘We should be approachable, as we should be approachable from our appearance to the attitude we portray toward the patient’ (29 years old doctor with 5 years of service).

Time provision

Participants mentioned that adequate time provision was an important facilitator of the consultation as it improved the chances of correct diagnosis and care.

‘Basically, patients think that spending much time with them will lead to the right diagnosis and the accurate diagnosis leads to correct prescriptions’ (a 31-year-old doctor with 6 years of service).

‘Basically, patients think that spending much time with them will lead to the right diagnosis and the accurate diagnosis leads to correct prescriptions’ (a 31-year-old doctor with 6 years of service).

‘Because when you explain to the doctors, they don’t just rush the consultation process because they are short of time…, not rushing through the consultation is important because whatever you come to consult about, you can talk about it freely’ (27-year-old female).

Doctors mentioned the need to involve the patient meaningfully in decision making. They believed that the treatment plan should be guided by the patient’s understanding, beliefs, and concerns.

‘Medicine is not the patriarchal system it used to be you know. It is an agreement between the doctor and the patient”, (37-year-old doctor with 12 years of service).

Continuity of care

Continuity of care was found to facilitate the consultation. Participants agreed that seeing the same doctor over time was beneficial.

It should be a relationship in which whenever I go to see the doctor, I tell the doctor what happened since we last met. The doctor should know that when I left him, I noticed this and that, in such a manner’ (31-year-old female patient).

The doctor should know that when I left him, I noticed this and that, in such a manner’ (31-year-old female patient).

‘Patients like to see the same doctor regularly but don’t get that privilege because obviously it’s a rotation of the doctor system’ (37-year-old doctor with 12 years of service).

Discussion

The main themes that describe facilitators of a good consultation were creating a conducive environment, time provision, confidentiality and privacy, and effective communication.

It is important to have good consultations because patients’ impression of the consultation and care received, can impact adherence to the treatment plan, taking medications as prescribed, and keeping scheduled visits [6].

Good consultation facilitators included creating a conducive environment, time provision, confidentiality and privacy, as well as effective communication. The attributes of the doctor and patient, factors of the health facility, and health system factors play an integral part of these facilitators of a good consultation [42]. This corroborates the World Health Organization framework to strengthen the doctor-patient relationship which stipulated that doctor factors, patient factors, and health system factors can facilitate or inhibit the doctor–patient relationship [3]. In our study health system factors included providing a good physical environment, ensuring privacy, ensuring continuity of care, and providing enough time for the consultation. Doctor factors included effective communication, respecting patients, building trust, providing a good social environment, and ensuring confidentiality. Patient factors included effective communication, joint decision making, and respecting doctors. We can infer, therefore, that the health system is indeed a complex system in which many factors (including health system, doctor and patient factors) interact and affect each other to impact consultations. Thus, it is a well-resourced health system with adequate numbers of doctors, good infrastructure and other resources that can generate facilitators of good consultations. Facilitators of good consultations, or doctor patient interactions, can be used to improve the consultation and the quality of healthcare [11].

Study participants felt that the environment should be aesthetically and emotionally conducive to a good consultation. We also found that the patients preferred a physical environment that is comfortable, provides privacy, and is pleasant. The WHO framework recognises the need for provision of dignity, respect and comfort by the doctor as central to the healing process [3]. Consistent with the WHO framework, this study and previous studies show that a pleasant physical environment enhances the consultation and has a positive effect on health outcomes [43]. Furthermore, studies have shown that a pleasant physical environment has a placebo effect, and a positive effect on the perceptions of the consultation [43, 44].

A welcoming social environment facilitates good consultations [32, 45]. In this study as in other studies [46], patients valued consulting a doctor with welcoming and friendly manners. Other studies have also highlighted the importance of positive doctor social skills during the consultation [47, 48].

Nonverbal communication, such as an agreeable welcoming facial expression and respect, helps patients relax and feel at ease to engage the doctor. Our findings align with previous research [49–51]. We found that, while treating patients with respect was important to them, so was the mutual respect that patients and doctors give each other. Mutual respect was identified as a main component of quality processes and relationship aspects of consultations [11]. Doctors who exhibit pleasant nonverbal communication are perceived to offer better care and result in patients being satisfied with their consultation and treatment [52–54].

Study findings showed that adequate time provision facilitated the consultation, and this is in line with findings in other studies Another key facilitator of the consultation is adequate consultation time. Research has shown that longer consultations are likely to involve discussion of important issues, allow patients time to ask questions, and result in improvements in diagnosis, treatment, and overall care [55]. As in other studies, study findings were that adequate time provision also allows doctors to thoroughly explain the condition and treatment plan to the patients, provides time for thorough physical examination, and doctors and patients did not like being rushed, during consultations [10, 56–59].

Continuity of care (COC) was also identified by participants as a facilitator of the consultation. Continuity of care is repeated contact between a patient and a doctor that gives patients and physicians the opportunity for improved understanding of each other’s agenda [19]. As in other studies, patients in the current study valued COC as it facilitated doctor patient interaction [46, 60]. Furthermore, continuity of care often results in the patient and the doctor building a relationship of trust [61].

COC also refers to a sustained doctor patient relationship, which catalyses the delivery of PCC during consultation [20]. Doctors stressed that adopting PCC would enhance the consultation and this is consistent with findings in another study [62].

Good communication facilitated consultations because this enabled patients to understand their care and to make informed decisions. Patients preferred doctors to speak their local language, provide a detailed explanation of their condition, the treatment plan, and investigations. Providing detailed explanations facilitates patient understanding and empowers the patient to assume a more active role in improving his health [6]. We also found that doctors and patients agreed that when doctors listen and engage in relaxed, unhurried discussions with patients, a shared understanding of patients’ problems is reached. Some of the key elements of the PCC expressed by doctors was to reach a shared understanding of patients’ problems and a treatment plan that is guided by patient preferences, values, and cultural beliefs that are in line with the common understanding of PCC [63].

According to the WHO framework of action, maintaining confidentiality of the doctor-patient relationship is a fundamental element of ethical practice by doctors [3]. In our study, doctors were seen as responsible for creating an environment to ensure privacy and confidentiality during consultations. Privacy and confidentiality increased patient satisfaction with consultation, facilitated communication between doctor and patient, and built trust in the doctor. Patients said that lack of privacy, such as constant interruptions by other clinicians and nurses and the presence of interpreters during consultation, eroded patient satisfaction with the consultation. Patients perceived interruptions as breaches of privacy and confidentiality, which resulted in some of them preferring to avoid disclosing all their problems to the doctor. The findings are substantiated by prior research [60, 23, 64]. In many settings, as in the current study, physical changes to infrastructure may be necessary to improve privacy and confidentiality [23].

Doctors believed that they had a responsibility to build trust, which is essential to develop good doctor-patient interaction, leading to patients’ willingness to reveal confidential information and adhere to the treatment plan. These views are reported in other studies [6, 54, 65, 66]. There was a link between providing detailed explanations to patients and building trust. The findings are consistent with prior studies [10, 67]. Patients identified being honest, reliable, and acting in the patient’s best interests as facilitators of trust and confidence in the doctor to heal the patient.

The main themes that describe facilitators of a good consultation were creating a conducive environment, time provision, confidentiality and privacy, and effective communication. These factors act and interact in a complex health system that includes central government, central government health administrators, district health administrators, health facility administrators, doctors, nurses, patients, and other important stakeholders. Adequate health financing to provide adequate and suitably trained human resources for health, medicines, health equipment, and laboratories are necessary prerequisites for the facilitators of good consultations to be effective [42]. Therefore, adequate health funding at central government level can help in the provision of adequate health resources which can be made available in public clinics. When clinics are well resourced, it becomes easier for facilitators of good consultations to be generated and implemented in public clinics; thus, improving the quality of care and health outcomes (Fig. 1). This study highlights the fact that facilitating good consultations and improvement of care in public PHC clinics needs to be approached from a holistic and systems paradigm [68].

However, these prerequisites (adequate health funding, for provision of adequate resources for health, and ensuring that public clinics have the resources to provide services optimally), do not act in a linear manner. They are embedded in a complex system whose components (central government, health administrators at different levels, HCWs and other resources) interact to create an environment that facilitates good consultations (Fig. 2).

The contribution of this study is that it reveals the facilitators of good consultations in the Botswana context. This is very important for implementation research as it informs the determinants to be considered in projects that seek to improve consultations in Botswana public clinics. This study also contributes in that it shows that consultations will not necessarily improve by just teaching HCWs (pharmacists, nurses, and doctors) how to conduct good consultations. This study draws attention to the multiplicity of factors that affect consultations, that need to be addressed to improve consultations.

It may be said that the findings of this study are obsolete because the data were collected about six years ago. However our findings are corroborated by the findings of a similar more recent study [69].

Limitations

We excluded nurses who conduct most of the consultations in Botswana PHC clinics. Qualitative research is labour intensive, and nurses were excluded, to do thorough interviews within the resources and time that the study allowed. Ideally it would have been more appropriate to interview all cadres of HCWs who consult with patients in public PHC clinics. This would have given a holistic picture of consultations and their facilitators, but we were constrained by time and other resources. Physicians who were no longer consulting in PHC clinics were not included as participants. This is because we wanted to get data that was relevant to the time of the study. What was preferable in the past may not have been preferable during the study e.g., doctor centred consultations.

Conclusions

We explored the facilitators of a good consultation in Botswana public PHC clinics by doing semi-structured interviews with 32 patients and 17 doctors across Botswana. The facilitators of a good consultation included creating a conducive social and physical environment, providing enough time for the consultation, ensuring continuity of care, having confidentiality and privacy for the consultation, and engaging in effective communication. The facilitators of a good consultation interact and are subsumed in a complex system of factors, that can exist in an environment of adequate human and non-human resources for health.

Future quantitative studies should be done to ascertain the representativeness of these findings to patients and doctors located in public PHC clinics in Botswana. There should also be implementation studies to improve healthcare in public PHC clinics, based on the facilitators of good consultations.

Recommendations

Health funding should be increased and used prudently.

Public PHC clinics should be refurbished to provide a conducive physical environment, and privacy.

The number of doctors should be increased to provide patients with enough consultation time and continuity of care.

Doctors should receive continuing medical education that includes an emphasis on good doctor-patient interactions, to improve the quality of consultation.
