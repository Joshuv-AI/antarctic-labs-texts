Impact of enhanced recovery after surgery protocol on postoperative complications and hospital stay following laparoscopic distal gastrectomy for gastric cancer
Authors: Jun Cheng, Han Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLaparoscopic distal gastrectomy is a standard surgical treatment for gastric cancer, but traditional postoperative care often results in prolonged recovery and hospital stays. Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols shown improved postoperative outcomes in various surgical disciplines, but their effect on gastric cancer surgery outcomes requires further investigation.MethodsThis retrospective before–after study included 182 patients who underwent laparoscopic distal gastrectomy for gastric cancer. The control group (n = 85; January 2021–December 2022) received standard perioperative care, while the ERAS group (n = 97; January 2023–June 2025) followed an enhanced recovery protocol. Primary outcomes were postoperative length of stay (LOS) and overall 30-day complication rate.ResultsThe ERAS group showed significantly shorter times to first flatus (38.45 vs. 52.67 h, P < 0.001) and reduced LOS (6.23 vs. 9.45 d, mean difference − 3.28 d, P < 0.001). VAS pain scores were significantly lower in the ERAS group at 24 and 48 h postoperatively (P < 0.001). Multivariable regression confirmed ERAS as an independent predictor of reduced LOS (B = − 3.254; P < 0.001). Overall complications were numerically lower (10.31% vs. 18.82%; adjusted OR 0.473; P = 0.093) but did not reach significance (achieved power 37.4%). Patient satisfaction (PSQ-18) was significantly higher in the ERAS group (P < 0.001).ConclusionThe ERAS protocol was associated with significantly reduced LOS, improved pain management, and enhanced patient satisfaction following laparoscopic distal gastrectomy for gastric cancer. These findings suggest the adoption of ERAS pathways in gastric cancer surgery to optimize recovery outcomes.Keywords: Enhanced recovery after surgery, Gastric cancer, Distal gastrectomy, Postoperative complications

Introduction

Gastric cancer remains one of the most common malignancies worldwide and represents a significant global health burden. According to GLOBOCAN 2022 data, gastric cancer ranked fifth in incidence and fourth in cancer-related mortality globally, with approximately 968,350 new cases and 659,858 deaths reported annually [1].

The incidence of gastric cancer demonstrates substantial geographic variation, with the highest rates observed in East Asia, Eastern Europe, and South America [2]. Risk factors include Helicobacter pylori infection, dietary factors such as high salt intake and consumption of preserved foods, smoking, and genetic predisposition [3,4]. For patients with resectable gastric cancer, surgical resection remains the cornerstone of curative treatment. Distal gastrectomy with D2 lymphadenectomy has been established as the standard surgical approach for tumors located in the lower two-thirds of the stomach [5]. With advances in minimally invasive surgery, laparoscopic distal gastrectomy has gained widespread acceptance as an oncologically safe and technically feasible alternative to open surgery, offering potential benefits including reduced blood loss, shorter hospital stay, and faster recovery [6,7].

Despite improvements in surgical techniques, traditional perioperative care pathways have been associated with variable recovery outcomes and prolonged hospital stays following gastric cancer surgery. Enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocols represent evidence-based perioperative care pathways designed to minimize surgical stress and accelerate functional recovery [8]. Recent systematic reviews have demonstrated that patients managed according to enhanced recovery protocols experience significantly reduced hospital length of stay and improved perioperative outcomes in gastrointestinal surgery [9,10]. However, the implementation of ERAS protocols in gastric cancer surgery and their impact on postoperative complications remain areas requiring further investigation, with existing studies showing heterogeneous results.

This retrospective study aimed to evaluate the effects of implementing an enhanced recovery after surgery protocol on postoperative complications and hospital length of stay in patients undergoing laparoscopic distal gastrectomy for gastric cancer, comparing outcomes between patients managed with traditional perioperative care and those following an enhanced recovery pathway.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Qianjiang Central Hospital between January 2021 and June 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Qianjiang Central Hospital (approval number: 2026LW001), and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the Helsinki Declaration.

A total of 182 consecutive patients who underwent laparoscopic distal gastrectomy (LDG) for gastric cancer during the study period were included. The ERAS protocol was formally implemented at our institution in January 2023. Patients were divided into two groups based on the perioperative care protocol in effect at the time of surgery: the control group (n = 85) comprised all consecutive patients who received traditional perioperative care January 2021 and December 2022, while the enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) group (n = 97) included patients managed according to the ERAS protocol from January 2023 to June 2025. This before–after design ensured that all patients within each period received uniform perioperative management according to the institutional protocol in effect at that time (Fig. 1).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (1) age ≥ 18 years old; (2) histologically confirmed gastric adenocarcinoma; (3) tumor located in the lower two-thirds of the stomach suitable for distal gastrectomy; (4) clinical stage I-III according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging system; [11] (5) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification I-III; [12] (6) complete medical records.

Patients were excluded if they had: (1) distant metastasis or locally advanced unresectable disease; (2) prior gastric surgery or upper abdominal surgery; (3) intraoperative conversion to open surgery or alternative procedures; (4) severe cardiopulmonary dysfunction precluding laparoscopic surgery; (5) emergency surgery for complications such as perforation or bleeding; (6) concurrent malignancy.

Perioperative care protocols

Patients in the control group received standard postoperative care that were in place prior to the implementation of the enhanced recovery pathway. This approach included routine preoperative fasting from midnight before surgery, standard bowel preparation with polyethylene glycol, and intravenous fluid administration. Postoperative management involved maintaining nasogastric tube drainage until return of bowel function, typically for 48 to 72 h after surgery. Patients were kept fasting until passage of flatus and received intravenous crystalloid fluid therapy. Ambulation was encouraged beginning on the second or third postoperative day, though the timing was individualized based on patient tolerance and clinical assessment. Pain management relied primarily on opioid-based analgesia with patient-controlled analgesia pumps during the initial postoperative period, transitioning to oral analgesics as tolerated. Antiemetic medications were administered on an as-needed basis in response to symptoms of nausea or vomiting. Oral intake was gradually reintroduced starting with clear liquids after confirmation of bowel function recovery, typically on the fourth or fifth postoperative day, with advancement through a staged dietary progression. Thromboprophylaxis with low molecular weight heparin was initiated on the evening of surgery and continued throughout hospitalization. Urinary catheters were typically maintained until the second or third postoperative day. A prophylactic closed-suction abdominal drain was routinely placed near the anastomosis at the conclusion of surgery and removed at the surgeon’s discretion based on output volume and character, typically on postoperative day 4 to 5.

The ERAS group followed an evidence-based multicomponent protocol designed to minimize surgical stress and optimize postoperative recovery, based on the ERAS Society guidelines for perioperative care in gastrectomy [8]. Preoperative counseling sessions were conducted to educate patients regarding the expected postoperative course, pain management strategies, early mobilization goals, and dietary progression. Patients were instructed to consume clear carbohydrate-rich beverages until four hours before anesthesia induction rather than undergoing prolonged fasting. Mechanical bowel preparation was not routinely performed. Standardized anesthetic protocols emphasized short-acting agents and goal-directed fluid therapy to avoid both hypovolemia and fluid overload. Multimodal analgesia was implemented, combining preoperative administration of acetaminophen and gabapentin with intraoperative local anesthetic infiltration at trocar sites and postoperative scheduled non-opioid analgesics including acetaminophen and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs when not contraindicated, with opioids reserved for breakthrough pain. Prophylactic antiemetic therapy using a combination of ondansetron and dexamethasone was administered intraoperatively to all patients. Nasojejunal feeding tubes were placed before emergence from anesthesia. Patients received limited intravenous fluids intraoperatively and postoperatively, with transition initiated by administering water through the nasojejunal feeding tube 24 h after surgery, followed by progression to oral intake. The dietary progression began with small volumes of water, advancing to clear liquids and then to semi-liquid diet within the first 48 to 72 h as tolerated. Active mobilization began on the evening of surgery, with patients encouraged to ambulate with assistance and sit out of bed for increasing periods. Urinary catheters were removed within 24 h postoperatively. A prophylactic abdominal drain was placed near the anastomosis in all patients and removed when the output was less than 100 mL per day with no evidence of anastomotic leakage, typically on postoperative day 2 to 3. Thromboprophylaxis with low molecular weight heparin was initiated preoperatively and continued postoperatively.

Discharge criteria were applied uniformly to both groups and included: (1) tolerance of semi-liquid diet for at least 24 h without nausea or vomiting; (2) adequate pain control with oral analgesics (VAS ≤ 3 at rest); (3) independent ambulation without assistance; (4) passage of flatus or stool; (5) afebrile status (body temperature < 37.5 °C for ≥ 24 h); (6) no clinical evidence of untreated surgical complications; and (7) no ongoing requirement for intravenous fluids or medications.

Surgical technique

All surgical procedures were performed by experienced surgeons with expertise in laparoscopic gastric cancer surgery. Laparoscopic distal gastrectomy with D2 lymphadenectomy was performed using a five-port technique. The extent of lymph node dissection followed National Guidelines for Diagnosis and Treatment of Gastric Cancer in China [13]. Reconstruction was performed using Billroth II or Roux-en-Y anastomosis based on tumor location and surgeon preference. Intraoperative frozen section analysis of the proximal resection margin was performed at the surgeon’s discretion when the gross margin appeared insufficient.

Observational indicators and study outcomes

Study outcomes were classified as follows: Hospital length of stay (LOS) and overall 30-day postoperative complication rate were designated as primary outcomes. Time to first flatus, postoperative pain intensity, total opioid consumption, incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), patient satisfaction, and 30-day readmission rate constituted secondary outcomes. Time to first ambulation and time to oral intake were recorded as process measures, as these variables are directly influenced by protocol-specific care instructions and reflect adherence to the care pathway rather than independent treatment effects.

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were extracted from electronic medical records, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and preoperative comorbidities. Tumor characteristics including location, differentiation, and pathological stage were recorded. ASA physical status classification was documented based on preoperative anesthesia assessment. Operative parameters including surgical duration, estimated blood loss, and reconstruction method were recorded from operative reports.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Distribution normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using independent samples t-test; mean differences with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, and compared using chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when for expected cell counts less than 5; odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs were reported where applicable. Multivariable linear regression was performed to evaluate the independent association between ERAS protocol and postoperative LOS. Multivariable logistic regression was used to assess the independent association between ERAS protocol and overall postoperative complications. Both models were adjusted for age, sex, BMI, ASA classification, pTNM stage, and reconstruction type. Postoperative complications were stratified by Clavien–Dindo classification into minor (Grade I–II) and major (Grade III–V) categories. Post hoc power analysis was performed using G*Power version 3.1 (Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Germany) to estimate the achieved statistical power for detecting the observed difference in overall complication rates at a two-sided significance level of 0.05.A two-sided P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The two groups demonstrated comparable baseline characteristics with no statistically significant differences (Table 1). Both groups showed similar age distributions, with males comprising the majority of patients in each cohort, consistent with the epidemiology of gastric cancer. BMI values were comparable between groups (P > 0.05). The prevalence of comorbidities including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, anemia, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease did not differ significantly between the control and ERAS groups (P > 0.05). ASA physical status classification distribution was similar across groups (P > 0.05), with the majority of patients classified as ASA II. Tumor characteristics including location, differentiation grade, and pathological stage were comparable between groups. All patients in both groups underwent D2 lymphadenectomy, with comparable numbers of lymph nodes retrieved (P > 0.05). R0 resection was achieved in 95.29% of control group patients and 95.88% of ERAS group patients (P = 1.000). Operative parameters including surgical duration, estimated blood loss, and reconstruction method distribution were similar between the two groups (P > 0.05).

Postoperative recovery

As shown in Table 2, time to first flatus was notably shorter in the ERAS group (mean difference − 14.22 h; 95% CI, − 17.37 to − 10.98; P < 0.001). The ERAS group achieved substantially earlier ambulation (mean difference − 14.37 h; 95% CI, − 15.97 to − 12.77; P < 0.001) and oral intake following surgery (mean difference − 48.00 h; 95% CI, − 52.09 to − 43.92; P < 0.001). Postoperative LOS was significantly reduced in the ERAS group compared to the control group (mean difference − 3.28 d; 95% CI, − 3.91 to − 2.65; P < 0.001) .

Postoperative complications and pain intensity

The overall incidence of postoperative complications tended to be lower in the ERAS group compared to the control group, though this difference did not reach statistical significance (10.31% vs. 18.82%, OR 0.50; 95% CI, 0.21–1.17; P = 0.096) (Table 4; Fig. 2A). When stratified by Clavien–Dindo classification, minor complications (Grade I–II) occurred in 7.22% of ERAS patients versus 9.41% of control patients (P = 0.589), while major complications (Grade III–V) occurred in 3.09% versus 9.41%, respectively (P = 0.077) (Fig. 2B). Post hoc power analysis indicated that the achieved power for detecting the observed difference in overall complication rate was 37.4%, confirming that the study was underpowered for this endpoint. Analysis of specific complication types revealed that postoperative bleeding occurred in 5.88% of patients in the control group and 3.09% in the ERAS group (P = 0.473). Surgical site infection was observed in 7.06% of control patients compared to 4.12% in the ERAS cohort (P = 0.519). Anastomotic leakage, one of the most serious complications following gastrectomy, occurred in 5.88% of control patients and 3.09% of ERAS patients (P = 0.473). Although individual complication rates did not demonstrate statistically significant differences between groups, the ERAS protocol showed a numerical trend toward lower complication rates.

Pain intensity assessed by visual analog scale scores demonstrated marked differences between the two management approaches across the early postoperative period (Fig. 2C). Preoperatively, both groups showed comparable VAS scores (1.23 ± 0.45 in the control group vs. 1.18 ± 0.42 in the ERAS group, P > 0.05). At 24 h after surgery, patients in the control group reported a mean VAS score of 5.67 ± 1.45, whereas the ERAS group demonstrated significantly lower pain levels with a mean score of 3.34 ± 1.18 (P < 0.001). This substantial reduction in early postoperative pain persisted at 48 h, with control group patients reporting a mean VAS score of 3.78 ± 1.23 compared to 2.23 ± 0.98 in the ERAS group (P < 0.001). Both groups exhibited declining pain scores over time, reflecting natural recovery progression; however, the ERAS group consistently maintained significantly lower pain intensity throughout the observation period. These findings highlight the effectiveness of multimodal analgesia strategies incorporated within the enhanced recovery protocol in achieving superior pain control during the critical early postoperative phase.

Multivariable regression analysis

Multivariable regression analysis adjusting for age, sex, BMI, ASA classification, pTNM stage, and reconstruction type was performed for both primary outcomes (Table 3). Linear regression confirmed that ERAS protocol implementation was independently associated with a significant reduction in postoperative LOS (B = − 3.254; 95% CI, − 3.860 to − 2.647; P < 0.001), with the model explaining 41.4% of the variance in LOS (R² = 0.414). None of the other covariates reached statistical significance. Logistic regression for overall postoperative complications demonstrated an adjusted odds ratio of 0.473 (95% CI, 0.197–1.133; P = 0.093) for the ERAS group, suggesting a 52.7% reduction in odds of complications that did not reach statistical significance, consistent with the underpowered nature of this endpoint.

Other postoperative outcomes

As presented in Table 4, the ERAS group exhibited significantly lower total opioid consumption during hospitalization compared to the control group (mean difference − 14.22 mg MEQ; 95% CI, − 16.94 to − 11.51; P < 0.001). The incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting was significantly reduced in patients following the ERAS protocol (OR 0.44; 95% CI, 0.20–0.96; P = 0.042). Thirty-day readmission rates did not differ significantly between groups (OR 0.69; 95% CI, 0.18–2.65; P = 0.567). Patient satisfaction scores assessed using the PSQ-18 instrument were significantly higher in the ERAS group (mean difference 7.89; 95% CI, 5.51–10.27; P < 0.001).

Discussion

This retrospective before–after study evaluated the association between ERAS protocol and postoperative outcomes in patients undergoing laparoscopic distal gastrectomy for gastric cancer. After adjustment for potential confounders including age, sex, BMI, ASA classification, pTNM stage, and reconstruction type, ERAS implementation was independently associated with significantly reduced postoperative LOS. While a numerical trend toward lower overall complication rates was observed in the ERAS group, this difference did not reach statistical significance.

The observed association between ERAS implementation and improved recovery parameters is consistent with recent evidence. A comprehensive meta-analysis encompassing 17 randomized controlled trials, demonstrated that ERAS programs significantly reduced hospital length of stay, time to first flatus, and postoperative pain scores in patients undergoing distal gastrectomy [18]. Similarly, another meta-analysis of minimally invasive gastrectomy studies reported that ERAS protocols were associated with shortened LOS without increasing complication or readmission rates [19]. A recent multicenter randomized controlled trial further confirmed the benefits of ERAS in laparoscopic distal gastrectomy, reporting a clinically meaningful reduction in postoperative LOS [20]. These improvements may be driven by several potential mechanisms. Early enteral feeding via nasojejunal tube or early oral intake can promote physiologic gastrointestinal recovery by stimulating bowel motility and maintaining gut mucosal integrity, enabling transition to oral medications and facilitating discharge readiness [21]. ERAS protocols have been shown to attenuate the surgical stress response, as reflected by lower acute phase reactant levels and improved immune function [21]. Standardized preoperative counseling sets appropriate expectations and empowers patients to participate actively in their recovery. Minimizing routine nasojejunal feeding tube placement duration eliminates associated discomfort and mechanical barriers to mobilization [22].

Effective pain management through combined multimodal analgesia is a key component of successful ERAS implementation in gastric cancer surgery. The combination of preoperative gabapentin, intraoperative local anesthetic techniques, and scheduled postoperative non-opioid analgesics creates synergistic analgesia while minimizing opioid-related adverse effects [23]. This approach holds particular importance in elderly gastric cancer patients who may have reduced physiologic reserve and increased susceptibility to opioid-related complications. Current ERAS Society guidelines for gastrectomy emphasize high-level evidence supporting multimodal, opioid-sparing analgesia approaches, including regional techniques such as transversus abdominis plane blocks and local anesthetic infiltration to improve postoperative recovery [8]. Beyond respiratory safety, opioid minimization reduces incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting, constipation, and potential for persistent postoperative opioid use [23]. The improved pain control observed in our ERAS cohort likely contributed to earlier mobilization and enhanced recovery trajectory, as adequate analgesia enables patients to engage in rehabilitation activities essential for physiologic restoration.

The overall complication rate showed a reduced trend in the ERAS group, though it did not reach statistical significance. This finding is consistent with several meta-analyses that have shown while ERAS significantly improves recovery parameters, effects on major complications may require larger sample sizes to detect [18]. Anastomotic leakage remains a serious concern following gastrectomy, and our study showed comparable rates between groups, suggesting that early oral feeding within the ERAS protocol does not increase this risk. Nevertheless, given the limited sample size and low event rate, this study was not adequately powered to draw definitive conclusions regarding anastomotic safety, and larger prospective studies are needed. This observation aligns with growing evidence that early enteral nutrition is safe following gastric cancer surgery [24]. Recent evidence demonstrates that adherence to individual ERAS protocol components significantly influences clinical outcomes, with higher compliance rates correlating with shorter LOS and fewer complications [25]. Enhanced preoperative optimization ensures patients enter surgery in optimal physiologic condition. Early mobilization reduces thromboembolic risk and promotes pulmonary function [22] Goal-directed fluid therapy prevents both volume overload and hypovolemia-related organ dysfunction [26].

Beyond traditional clinical outcomes, the benefits of ERAS should be understood within a broader perioperative optimization. As outlined by a prehabilitation programs integrating exercise training, nutritional optimization, and psychological preparation can enhance patients’ physiological reserve, thereby amplifying the benefits of postoperative ERAS protocols [27] Patient satisfaction represents an increasingly recognized dimension of surgical quality that extends beyond traditional clinical outcomes. Recent research on patient-reported outcomes following gastric cancer surgery has demonstrated that patients report significant improvements in quality of life when perioperative care is optimized [28]. The enhanced satisfaction observed in our ERAS cohort likely reflects multiple factors including improved pain management, reduced nausea, earlier return to oral intake, and shorter hospitalization. The psychological benefits of participating in an organized recovery program with clear milestones and expectations may also contribute to improved patient experience.

This study possesses several limitations requiring acknowledgment. The retrospective design and temporal separation of study groups introduce potential for confounding from concurrent institutional changes or evolving patient populations. ERAS implementation represents an institutional culture change that may coincide with other quality improvement initiatives, making it difficult to isolate the independent effect of the protocol. A learning curve effect during the early implementation phase may have also influenced outcomes. Single-center data limit generalizability to institutions with different resources, expertise, or patient demographics. Sample size constraints may have limited statistical power for detecting differences in individual complications. Furthermore, individual-level adherence to specific ERAS components was not systematically documented; recent studies have demonstrated that protocol compliance significantly correlates with clinical outcomes. The PSQ-18 assessments was administered at discharge without blinding assessors to group allocation, which may have introduced response bias. Future prospective randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes would strengthen causal inference regarding ERAS benefits. Investigation of long-term outcomes including oncological outcomes and sustained quality of life improvements would elucidate whether perioperative care pathways influence overall prognosis. ERAS protocols may facilitate timely return to intended oncologic therapy, but this hypothesis requires prospective validation. Cost-effectiveness analyses examining healthcare resource utilization would inform institutional decisions regarding ERAS implementation.

Conclusion

This retrospective before–after study suggests that ERAS protocol implementation in laparoscopic distal gastrectomy for gastric cancer was associated with accelerated postoperative recovery, including improved pain management, earlier gastrointestinal function restoration, and reduced LOS. Although a numerical trend toward reduced complications was observed, the study was not adequately powered to confirm a significant effect on this endpoint. The ERAS approach demonstrated a comparable safety profile to traditional care while enhancing patient satisfaction. These findings support the feasibility of ERAS adoption in gastric cancer surgery settings, though prospective randomized trials with larger sample sizes are warranted to confirm these observations and evaluate long-term oncologic outcomes.
