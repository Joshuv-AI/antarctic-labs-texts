No Evidence of Impact of Genetic Sex on Hyperthermia‐Induced Neural Tube Defects in Chicken Embryos
Authors: Yiting Wang, Aimee K. Ryan, Anna K. Naumova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIn mammals, cranial neural tube defects (NTDs) are more likely to occur in females. It has been proposed that the sex bias in NTDs was due to the presence of the inactive X chromosome in the somatic cells of female embryos and that the inactive X acted as a heterochromatic sink, competing for silencing factors with the rest of the genome. Such competition increased the likelihood of abnormal gene regulation. To test this hypothesis, we used an animal model that lacks dosage compensation, the chicken Gallus gallus . If the heterochromatic sink hypothesis was correct, no sex bias will be found in chicken embryos with cranial NTDs.MethodsWe used in ovo heat treatment to induce NTDs in chicken embryos and PCR genotyping to determine embryos' genetic sex. Embryo phenotypes were examined using a dissecting microscope. Embryos were staged according to Hamburger and Hamilton criteria and scored for NTDs.ResultsWe observed a statistically significant increase in NTDs following heat treatment (38.1% in the treatment group vs. 5.9% in the control group), but no sex bias. Next, we asked if heat treatment led to a developmental delay and found a significant association between the numbers of somite pairs and heat treatment and a reduced number of somites in the chicken embryos with cranial NTDs.ConclusionIn ovo hyperthermia during the developmental window when the neural tube is formed dramatically increases the rate of NTDs and delays the development of chicken embryos. However, there is no sex bias with respect to NTD risk.

Introduction

Females are at higher risk of developing cranial neural tube defects (NTD) with a nearly 2:1 ratio of female versus male infants born with cranial NTDs (reviewed in (Juriloff and Harris 2012)). However, the female sex bias is lost in countries with fortification of food with folic acid, suggesting the importance of folic acid metabolism and/or epigenetic factors, such as DNA or protein methylation, for the pathogenesis of NTD (Liu et al. 2018; Poletta et al. 2018). Excess of females with exencephaly and cranial NTDs is also observed in certain mouse models of NTD, including hyperthermia‐induced NTDs (Lundberg et al. 2003; Seller and Perkins‐Cole 1987; Webster and Edwards 1984). Moreover, mouse studies using the four core genotypes model demonstrated that presence of two X chromosomes rather than female biological sex contributed to the risk of NTDs (Chen et al. 2008). In mammals, the presence of two or more X chromosomes in the embryo triggers X‐chromosome inactivation (XCI) that results in only one X being active in any given somatic cell, whereas the other is epigenetically silenced (reviewed in (Disteche and Berletch 2015)). Therefore, it has been proposed that the cause of the higher risk of cranial NTDs in some individuals with more than one X is the inactive X chromosome acting as a heterochromatic sink, i.e., competing with autosomes for epigenetic silencing factors (Blewitt et al. 2005; Juriloff and Harris 2012; Robinson et al. 2013). Lower supply of silencing factors causes dysregulation of autosomal gene expression during neural tube formation (Juriloff and Harris 2012). Such a mechanism would explain the excess females among cranial NTD cases and would be consistent with the disappearance of this female excess following folic acid supplementation. However, an alternative explanation for the sex bias would be that X‐linked genes that escape X‐inactivation in both humans and mice contribute to the pathogenesis of NTDs in females.

We posit that if the heterochromatic sink hypothesis were correct, no sex bias would be found among NTD cases in an organism that lacks dosage compensation. Therefore, we examined the genetic sex of chicken embryos with hyperthermia‐induced NTDs. In the chicken ( Gallus gallus ), females are the heterogametic sex and carry the chromosomes Z and W, whereas males are the homogametic sex with two Z chromosomes (Smith and Sinclair 2004). Based on current literature, there is no evidence of chromosome‐wide dosage compensation in the chicken sex chromosomes (Itoh et al. 2007). Hence, we predict that, in contrast to humans and mice, no sex bias will be found among NTD cases in chicken embryos.

Materials and Methods

Hyperthermia Treatment

Fertilized eggs from hens of the Lohmann breed were purchased from Ferme GMS (Saint‐Roch‐de‐l'Achigan, Quebec, Canada) and subjected to in ovo hyperthermia treatment. Fertilized eggs were randomized and incubated at 38.5°C for 30 h in an air incubator, then transferred to a water bath at 47°C (treatment group) or 38.5°C (control group) for 3 h, then transferred back to the 38.5°C air incubator. Embryos and extraembryonic membranes (EEM) were collected after a total incubation time of 50 h for morphological analysis or genotyping, respectively. All embryos were handled according to the Canadian Council of Animal Care guidelines.

Genotyping

DNA was extracted from the extraembryonic membranes using Qiagen DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, ND). DNA samples were genotyped using PCR using two primer pairs targeting the Z and W chromosomes. For genotyping, primers targeting the JAK2 locus on the Z chromosome were designed: JAK2_F 5′‐TAGTGCTGCCTCCTGAGTTT‐3′ (ChrZ: 27,537,608–27,537,627) and JAK2_R 5′‐TGCTGAAGTCAATAGGGTGGA‐3′ (ChrZ: 27,537,754‐27,537,774). For the chromosome W, primers for the ChrW: 4,915,611–4,915,984 locus (EE06 primers) (Itoh et al. 2001) were used. PCR was performed using a standard protocol with 1.5 mM MgCl2 and annealing temperature of 55°C.

Morphological Analysis

Embryos were subjected to morphological analysis using a Leica M125 dissecting microscope. Embryos were staged according to Hamburger and Hamilton (HH) criteria (Hamburger and Hamilton 1992) and scored for NTDs, which were identified as openings between the two neural folds using brightfield microscopy as we have done previously (Legere et al. 2024). Dorsal and ventral images of the embryos were taken using a Zeiss Axiophot compound microscope and AxioCamMRc camera with Axiovision v4.7.1.0 software. Numbers of pairs of somites were determined when possible. If the numbers of somites were different on the left and right, the lower number was used for calculation.

Statistical Analysis

Power calculations were conducted before embryo collection started to estimate the sample size needed to distinguish the 2:1 sex ratio (as observed in humans and mice with NTD) from the expected Mendelian 1:1 sex ratio (https://www.stat.ubc.ca/~rollin/stats/ssize/b1.html). Data were analyzed using Pearson's chi‐square test and online tools (https://www.quantitativeskills.com/sisa/).

Results

In Ovo Hyperthermia Increases the Risk of Developing NTDs in Chicken Embryos Without Sex Bias

In a total of 15 replicate experiments, we collected data from 348 eggs in the treatment group and 144 in the control group. The treatment protocol was designed to induce NTDs at the time of cranial neural tube closure. Fertilized eggs were subjected to hyperthermia treatment at 30 h (~HH8; 4 somites), which is when the first contact between the neural folds is made at the level of the midbrain (Van Straaten et al. 1996). Following the 3 h hyperthermia treatment, embryos are ~HH9‐ (6 somites), and the neutral tube closure will have continued in the anterior and posterior directions and will complete by the 9‐somite stage cranially, and the 14‐somite stage caudally (Van Straaten et al. 1996). Embryos were collected at 50 h (~HH13‐14; 17–22 somites) at which point the neural tube is closed. Gonadal sex of the embryos cannot be established morphologically at this stage because gonads start to develop at embryonic day 3 (> 72 h) with differentiated gonads only forming at embryonic day 6 (Ayers et al. 2013), while we collected the embryos after 2 days of incubation at 50 h.

In the control group, 23 (16%) embryos were lost or broken during dissection, 7 (4.9%) eggs were unfertilized, 6 (4.1%) embryos could not be scored for NTDs because they did not develop a neural tube, and 6 (4.1%) embryos could not be scored for NTDs (with neural tube). Hence, a total of 102 (70.8%) embryos were informative for NTDs, among which 6 (5.9%) developed NTDs (Table 1).

Next, embryos were genotyped for sex‐chromosomal markers. If the sex‐chromosome complement had no impact on the risk of developing NTDs following heat treatment, one expected to observe equal numbers of female and male embryos with NTDs. Indeed, the observed numbers were not significantly different from the expected based on Mendelian ratios: 54 females and 51 males (Table 1). We next compared the numbers of females and males in the treatment/no NTD and control groups. There was a slight excess of males in the treatment/no NTD group as well as in the control/no NTD group (Table 1), but neither comparison was significantly different from the NTD group or the expected Mendelian 1:1 ratios (Table 1). Studies in humans and mice reported excess females with cranial NTDs (Lundberg et al. 2003; Seller 1987; Seller and Perkins‐Cole 1987). In our treatment dataset, numbers of females and males with cranial NTDs showed no significant departure from the expected 1:1 ratio.

Hyperthermia Leads to Developmental Delay in Both Males and Females

To test the possibility that heat‐induced NTDs were associated with a developmental delay, we examined the numbers of somite pairs in different experimental groups (Figure 1C). We observed significantly fewer somite pairs in treated versus untreated embryos (Figure 1C). Within the treated group (Figure 1D), we observed significantly fewer somite pairs in embryos with NTDs vs. embryos without NTDs, and in embryos with cranial vs. caudal NTDs, but no difference between female and male embryos, either with or without NTDs. Among the treated embryos, the somite‐pair number was significantly lower in the cranial NTD than in caudal NTD groups, in both sexes. Therefore, we observed a significant association between treatment, NTD phenotype, but not sex, with the numbers of somites (Figure 1).

In conclusion, in ovo heat treatment of chicken embryos during the developmental window when the neural tube is formed dramatically increases the rate of NTDs and delays embryo development. However, there is no evidence of a significant difference between the sexes with respect to NTD risk.

Discussion

Our analysis of in ovo hyperthermia‐induced NTDs in chicken embryos shows no significant sex differences in the occurrence of NTDs, which does not refute the hypothesis that the X chromosomes contribute to the pathogenesis of NTD in mammalian females. We also suggest an alternative hypothesis that the increased risk of cranial NTDs in mammalian females is associated with higher dosage of X‐linked genes that escape XCI. Since the female bias is observed in both humans and mice, the X‐linked gene candidate must escape XCI in both humans and mice. Only four genes escape XCI in both organisms: lysine demethylase 6A (KDM6A/Kdm6a), lysine demethylase 5C (KDM5C/Kdm5c), DEAD box helicase 3, X‐linked (DDX3X/Ddx3x), and eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2, subunit 3, structural gene X‐linked (EIF2S3X/Eif2s3x) (Balaton et al. 2021; Berletch et al. 2015). It is worth noting that KDM6A is implicated in cell migration and null mouse embryos die at midgestation and display abnormal neural tube closure (Shpargel et al. 2012; Wang et al. 2012). Moreover, loss of Ddx3x in mouse embryos is also associated with lethality and abnormal neural tube closure (Chen et al. 2016). Chicken orthologs for the four X‐linked escape genes that are shared between mice and humans reside on chromosome 1. Therefore, in chicken all four have the same number of copies in both sexes. If the sex bias were due to a higher dosage of one of these genes in mammalian females and lower dosage in mammalian males, no such effect would be detected in the chicken model. However, our model does not allow distinguishing between the effects of XCI as a potential heterochromatic sink, as proposed by Juriloff and Harris (Juriloff and Harris 2012) and the dosage effects of X‐linked genes escaping XCI. Further studies in other organisms may help separate the role of XCI from that of X‐linked gene dosage in the etiology of NTDs.

Our data also suggest that in ovo hyperthermia during early stages of development is associated with delayed development determined based on somite pairs. Remarkably, the delay is most pronounced in the embryos with cranial, but not caudal NTDs. It is important to note that the NTDs observed in our experiment are not due to a delay in development. The cranial portion of the neural tube normally closes by the time the chicken embryo reaches 9–10 somite pairs, whereas the average number of somite pairs in embryos with cranial NTDs was 13.2 in females and 12.9 in males (Figure 1D). By these somite stages, all cranial regions should be closed.

Moreover, it has been reported that male embryos develop faster than females in birds with higher numbers of somite pairs after 36 h of incubation (Tagirov and Golovan 2015; Tagirov and Rutkowska 2014). No sex bias in the numbers of somite pairs was found in our datasets after 50 h of incubation. This discrepancy may be due to different genetic backgrounds of the chicken embryos in the two studies, or the transient nature of the effect of sex on developmental dynamics in chicken, as our data were collected at a later time point.
