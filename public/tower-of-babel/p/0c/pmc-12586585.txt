Conditional UNet emulation of CMAQ simulations for fine particulate matter concentration prediction
Authors: Yohan Lee, Junghyun Park, Jinseok Kim, Jung-Hun Woo, Jong-Hyeon Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) pollution poses significant health risks, necessitating accurate predictions for effective management measures for the mitigator of PM2.5. While the Community Multiscale Air Quality (CMAQ) model is widely utilized to simulate PM2.5 concentrations, its computational demands often limit its application in testing various emission reduction scenarios. This study introduces a data-driven approach to emulate CMAQ simulations through AI surrogate model with a conditional U-Net architecture. The model, trained on a dataset of CMAQ-simulated PM2.5 concentrations from across South Korea in 2013, incorporates region-specific emission source activity data as inputs. It accurately predicts hourly changes in PM2.5 concentrations, facilitating the estimation of monthly and annual average concentrations under diverse emission reduction scenarios. Demonstrating robust performance in replicating the spatial and temporal patterns of CMAQ simulations, this surrogate model significantly cuts computational costs and times. Our findings offer policymakers a practical and efficient tool for evaluating the effectiveness of emission control strategies and enhancing decision-making processes in air quality management.

Introduction

Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) pollution has emerged as a significant public health concern, with numerous studies linking PM2.5 exposure to various adverse health outcomes, including respiratory and cardiovascular diseases1,2. In South Korea, PM2.5 concentrations are influenced by both transboundary pollution from China and domestic emissions of PM2.5 precursors. Although the contribution of Chinese emissions cannot be directly controlled, the impact of domestic precursor emissions on PM2.5 levels cannot be overlooked. To mitigate this, the South Korean government has implemented various strategies, including regional investigations, PM2.5 concentration simulations, and quarterly emission reduction measures3. During high pollution episodes, the government also enacts emergency emission reduction actions4. Therefore, testing multiple emission policy scenarios through simulations that consider a range of physical and chemical factors, such as annually changing meteorological conditions, land use, and base year concentrations, is crucial for determining rational and appropriate policies to effectively manage PM2.5 pollution in South Korea.

The Community Multiscale Air Quality (CMAQ) model, a Chemical Transport Model (CTM) developed by the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), is extensively used worldwide for predicting air pollutant concentrations5. In South Korea, CMAQ-based air quality models have been actively employed to study and design emission reduction measures for various air pollutants, including domestic PM2.5, and to analyze the contributions of regional precursor emission sources to PM2.5 concentrations6–8. However, CMAQ is computationally intensive, limiting its application in testing multiple emission reduction scenarios. Developing rational and efficient PM2.5 reduction measures requires consideration of not only precursor emissions but also a range of complex factors such as regional economic activities, seasonal variations in human activities, and public acceptance of proposed policies. To design effective and feasible PM2.5 reduction strategies, testing a wide array of scenarios is necessary. Yet, due to the computational limitations of CMAQ, the number of scenarios that can be evaluated within a feasible timeframe remains constrained. Consequently, there is a growing demand for methods that can adequately emulate the physico-chemical processes of CMAQ while reducing its computational demands, especially computational time.

To overcome these limitations, various studies have developed surrogate models for CMAQ, typically utilizing essential input data for calculating pollutant concentrations for a target year and employing statistical methods to emulate CMAQ simulation results with a minimal number of scenarios. One successful method, the response surface model (RSM), uses polynomial functions (pf-RSM) to statistically determine the coefficients of a polynomial function based on precursor emissions (including \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{NO}{x}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{SO}{2}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{NH}_{3}$$\end{document}, VOCs) for each grid in the target region9,10. DeepRSM enhances this approach by incorporating deep learning techniques, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), to additionally learn spatial information11.

The U-Net architecture, originally developed for biomedical image segmentation, has gained prominence in environmental modeling due to its ability to capture both local and global spatial features through its encoder-decoder structure with skip connections. The architecture’s strengths include its capability to preserve fine-scale spatial details while learning hierarchical features, making it suitable for spatially distributed environmental data. However, traditional U-Net applications face limitations including the requirement for complex input data (meteorological conditions, detailed emission inventories) and challenges in incorporating temporal dynamics effectively. Furthermore, recent studies have utilized U-Net architecture to emulate CMAQ simulations at various temporal scales, inputting emission maps, land use, and meteorological grid information into a deep learning model structured around U-Net12,13. However, these studies face common limitations: the required input data often include complex meteorological variables (which policymakers cannot control) and detailed grid-specific emission data (which are difficult to modify directly in policy implementation), thereby limiting their direct applicability for evaluating emission reduction measures that focus on sector-specific or region-specific control strategies. Additionally, in data-driven approaches using only U-Net, the prediction performance has been relatively inadequate for making reliable policy decisions. This study builds upon and extends our previous work14, which developed initial frameworks for atmospheric modeling using deep learning approaches. While our previous research focused on foundational methodologies and was limited to static spatial predictions, the present study introduces several significant advancements. First, we develop a conditional U-Net architecture specifically designed for CMAQ emulation that uses simplified, policy-relevant input variables, substantially enhancing practical applicability for decision-makers. Second, and most notably, we achieve successful prediction of hourly temporal dynamics by incorporating time information directly into the conditional U-Net structure, eliminating the need for computationally heavy sequential modeling architectures such as LSTMs or Transformers. This represents a major breakthrough from our previous work’s static predictions to dynamic temporal emulation of CMAQ’s hourly pollutant evolution. Third, we provide comprehensive validation through SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis, demonstrating that our model successfully captures the underlying physical-chemical processes despite using a purely data-driven approach. These advancements collectively represent a significant step forward in making AI-based air quality modeling tools both accessible and reliable for policy applications.

Building on these foundations, this study presents a methodological contribution to the field of atmospheric chemistry modeling by establishing a novel framework for high-speed CMAQ emulation. We focus on demonstrating the feasibility and computational advantages of this approach, providing the foundation for future comprehensive validation and operational deployment. Specifically, we introduce a conditional U-Net model that predicts ground-level PM2.5 concentrations at given time steps using a 119-dimensional input (control matrix) composed of activity data from seven emission sources (power plants, industry, mobile sources, residential, agriculture, solvent, and others) across 17 regions in South Korea, coupled with time sequence information (Figure 1). Unlike previous U-Net based studies that relied on complex inputs like meteorological conditions and land use, our model uses a simplified input structure focused on emission control factors, significantly enhancing its applicability for policy-making scenarios. The conditional U-Net model demonstrates superior predictive performance compared to existing U-Net models, thanks to its unique architecture and training approach. The incorporation of sinusoidal time embedding enables our model to effectively capture temporal characteristics, enhancing its accuracy within the trained sequence. The model’s ability to accurately emulate CMAQ simulations–achieving computational speedup of approximately 288,000 times (from 24 hours per scenario to 0.3 seconds)–while maintaining high fidelity to the original CMAQ outputs makes it a valuable surrogate model for rapid policy scenario evaluation and decision support. The main contributions of this study are threefold: We develop an artificial intelligence model that emulates CMAQ simulations using a purely data-driven approach, providing a more accessible and computationally efficient alternative to traditional CMAQ modeling. Results

The emulation model developed in this study delivers results in three key areas. First, we evaluate the model’s ability to accurately emulate CMAQ calculations, comparing its performance across three dimensions: spatiotemporal point-wise predictions evaluated at each spatial grid cell of the 67\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}82 domain and each hourly time step, performance consistency across different test scenarios comprising the 60 evaluation scenarios, and temporal accuracy assessed across all 984 hourly time steps within each season. This comprehensive analysis ensures a thorough understanding of the model’s emulation capabilities. Second, we assess the model’s explanatory power using SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) values15. While the absence of relevant data for 2013 prevents a quantitative analysis of the contribution from each emission source sector, we provide a qualitative examination of contribution trends by referring to results from CMAQ contribution analyses for different years within the same Korean domain8. Third, we compare the computational efficiency of the emulation model, a critical aspect in evaluating its practicality. By demonstrating the computational savings achieved by substituting CMAQ with the conditional U-Net, we highlight the practical advantages of our approach.

We examined three studies: the DeepRSM model, a widely used CMAQ emulation that incorporates physico-chemical principles11, and two data-driven studies employing the U-Net architecture, targeting different species and regions, akin to our study’s structure12,13. As each study uses distinct performance metrics, we evaluated our model against all referenced metrics for a comprehensive comparison. Initially, we used the MeanNE metric from DeepRSM for comparison; although specific numbers were not disclosed, we ascertained that its performance in predicting daily and monthly PM2.5 concentrations fell below 5%. The performance of our model at each time resolution is displayed in the last row, highlighting that our results surpass those of the three compared models, validating the effectiveness of our approach. Notably, although all three models take a reasonable approach by utilizing CMAQ input data as closely as possible, our model differs structurally from the others in several key aspects. Specifically, our conditional U-Net architecture incorporates: (1) a simplified input representation using regional emission activity factors rather than detailed grid-specific emissions, (2) sinusoidal time embedding for temporal dependencies instead of relying on meteorological data, and (3) a conditional framework that directly processes policy-relevant variables. These structural innovations contribute to its superior performance through several mechanisms: the simplified input representation reduces overfitting risk and computational requirements while focusing on policy-controllable variables; the sinusoidal time embedding enables effective temporal modeling without requiring meteorological forecasting capabilities; and the conditional framework allows direct manipulation of emission scenarios without complex preprocessing of meteorological or land-use data. This study successfully demonstrates a transformative methodological advancement in atmospheric chemistry modeling through the development of a conditional U-Net-based surrogate model for CMAQ. The consistent performance across all seasons, scenarios, and temporal scales validates the robustness of our approach within the defined scope.

The primary objective of this study was to develop a computationally efficient surrogate model that accurately reproduces hourly PM2.5 concentration distributions from CMAQ simulations for specific emission scenarios under given meteorological conditions. Comparing to other existing model with emission inventories as input variables, our model uses activity levels of emission source sectors in each region of South Korea as input variables to predict PM2.5 concentrations at specified time points for a given scenario. We based our simulations on the 2013 scenario for South Korea, with activity levels varied from 0.5 to 1.5 times their base values. These data were used to train a deep learning model structured around a conditional U-Net. Our study shows that the developed model not only surpasses other existing models in overcoming their limitations but also achieves high predictive performance and explanatory power, while requiring significantly less time for training and prediction comparable to CMAQ. These results underscore the potential of our approach to provide a more accessible and computationally efficient tool for air quality modeling and policy analysis, enabling policymakers to investigate a broader array of emission reduction scenarios without extensive computational resources.

The conditional U-Net model demonstrated exemplary performance in emulating CMAQ simulations. Furthermore, the conditional U-Net model enhances policy applicability by directly utilizing policy-relevant input data. It takes activity levels of seven emission source sectors across 17 regions in South Korea as input, which are variables that policymakers can directly manipulate. The use of policy-relevant variables in the model’s input structure increases its relevance and utility in air quality management and policymaking contexts. These findings indicate that data-driven approaches and deep learning techniques, such as the conditional U-Net, offer substantial promise for tackling complex air quality modeling challenges and providing efficient, effective tools for policy analysis and decision support.

The SHAP analysis provides compelling evidence that our model learns physically meaningful relationships despite being purely data-driven. The model successfully identifies known atmospheric chemistry patterns without explicit programming: industrial emissions dominate in Ulsan, power plant contributions show expected regional transport patterns, and seasonal variations align with established atmospheric dynamics. This emergent physical consistency–arising solely from data–validates our approach as more than simple pattern matching. The fact that the model reproduces sector-specific contribution patterns consistent with CAMx source apportionment studies (Kumar et al., 2022) is particularly noteworthy, as these relationships were never explicitly provided during training. While comprehensive sensitivity analysis would provide additional validation, the current SHAP results demonstrate that the conditional U-Net captures essential atmospheric chemistry relationships, supporting its use as a rapid screening tool for policy analysis within the demonstrated scope. The SHAP analysis offers initial insights into the model’s inner mechanisms and suggests interpretability potential, which could be vital for policymakers and stakeholders who depend on these predictions for informed decision-making once proper validation is completed. The apparent alignment between the conditional U-Net model and CMAQ contribution patterns suggests the potential of data-driven approaches to learn atmospheric relationships, though establishing reliable and interpretable results requires the comprehensive validation efforts we are committed to pursuing in future research.

Scope boundaries and future research opportunities

Study Scope and Methodological Focus: This study establishes a methodological framework for high-speed CMAQ emulation, demonstrating that deep learning can achieve extraordinary computational efficiency (288,000\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} speedup) while maintaining high fidelity (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {r}^{2}$$\end{document} >0.99) to the original model. Within this defined scope, we provide rigorous validation through 5-fold cross-validation, comprehensive performance metrics across seasons and scenarios, and SHAP-based evidence of physical consistency. The focus on methodological innovation is deliberate and follows established practice in machine learning research, where feasibility demonstration precedes comprehensive validation.

Scope Boundaries: The current implementation operates within specific boundaries that define the demonstrated capabilities rather than limit the methodology’s potential. The model is trained and validated exclusively on 2013 meteorological conditions within the South Korean domain, limiting generalizability to other time periods or geographic regions. The approach uses CMAQ version 4.7, which lacks recent improvements in atmospheric chemistry parameterizations. PM2.5 predictions represent total mass concentrations without chemical speciation, which significantly constrains the model’s ability to capture complex atmospheric chemistry processes. Specifically, PM2.5 comprises multiple chemical components including primary carbonaceous aerosols, secondary organic aerosols, and inorganic species such as sulfate, nitrate, and ammonium. The formation of secondary inorganic aerosols is particularly sensitive to ammonia availability, exhibiting highly nonlinear relationships that vary spatially and temporally based on temperature, humidity, and precursor concentration ratios. Our simplified approach cannot explicitly represent these chemistry-dependent processes, potentially limiting performance in regions or periods where secondary aerosol formation dominates total PM2.5 mass, or where ammonia-limited versus NOx-limited chemical regimes significantly influence aerosol yields from precursor emissions. The evaluation demonstrates emulation accuracy relative to CMAQ rather than validation against atmospheric observations or alternative modeling approaches.

Future Research Opportunities: The methodological framework established here opens multiple avenues for advancing atmospheric chemistry modeling. The demonstrated computational efficiency enables entirely new research directions: (1) Ensemble modeling with thousands of members for robust uncertainty quantification, previously computationally prohibitive with CMAQ; (2) Real-time optimization of emission control strategies using the surrogate model within optimization algorithms; (3) Integration with machine learning weather prediction systems for fully AI-based air quality forecasting; (4) Extension to multi-pollutant systems leveraging the U-Net’s capacity for multi-channel outputs. Near-term validation priorities include observational comparison using Korean EPA monitoring networks, systematic sensitivity analysis through controlled perturbation experiments, and expansion to multiple meteorological years. The framework’s modular design facilitates these extensions without fundamental architectural changes, positioning this work as a foundation for next-generation air quality modeling systems that combine physical understanding with computational efficiency.

Additionally, integrating our emulation model with health impact assessment tools could enable the prediction of expected health outcomes under various emission scenarios. Such integration would provide valuable insights for policymakers and public health officials in evaluating and optimizing air quality management strategies, though this must await proper validation of the underlying atmospheric chemistry representation.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the feasibility of using conditional U-Net architecture to emulate CMAQ simulations for PM2.5 concentration prediction, representing an important step toward developing computationally efficient tools for policy scenario evaluation. The model uses region-specific emission source activity data as inputs and successfully emulates hourly PM2.5 concentration changes from CMAQ simulations, enabling the estimation of monthly and annual average concentrations under various emission reduction scenarios while significantly reducing computational costs. The demonstrated emulation performance and SHAP analysis results suggest that the conditional U-Net architecture can learn meaningful relationships between emission activities and PM2.5 concentrations, though comprehensive validation against independent data remains an important area for future work.

While this approach shows promise for developing computationally efficient policy support tools, we emphasize that the current work represents a proof-of-concept rather than a fully validated atmospheric chemistry model. The study’s primary contribution lies in demonstrating that complex CMAQ input-output relationships can be approximated using simplified, policy-relevant inputs through advanced deep learning architectures. This foundation enables future development of policy-oriented tools, though rigorous validation against observational data and systematic atmospheric chemistry experiments remains essential.

We hope this work contributes constructively to ongoing efforts in the atmospheric modeling community to develop efficient policy support tools. By transparently presenting both the potential and limitations of our approach, we aim to encourage further research into hybrid modeling approaches that combine the physical rigor of traditional atmospheric models with the computational efficiency of modern machine learning techniques. Future work will focus on comprehensive validation and extension of this approach to establish its reliability for real-world policy applications.

As the field of air quality modeling continues to evolve, integrating our data-driven approach with traditional simulation tools like CMAQ holds significant promise for enriching our understanding of atmospheric processes and aiding informed decision-making. Through our innovative methodology and ongoing research efforts, we are committed to unleashing the full potential of this approach across various applications, ultimately enhancing air quality and public well-being. The advancements achieved in this study, together with the directions proposed for future research, are poised to contribute significantly to the development of effective strategies for mitigating air pollution and safeguarding public health.

Methods

CMAQ simulation and data preparation

In this study, the Community Multiscale Air Quality (CMAQ) model, developed by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), was used to simulate PM2.5 concentrations over South Korea. CMAQ is a comprehensive air quality modeling system that integrates various processes, including emissions, transport, chemical transformations, and deposition of air pollutants. The model has several key features, such as a modular structure for easy inter-component compatibility, the ability to handle multiple pollutants simultaneously, and applicability across different spatial scales19.

The CMAQ modeling system integrates multiple atmospheric processes through its Chemical Transport Model (CCTM) and associated preprocessing components for meteorology, emissions, photolysis, land use, and boundary conditions17.

CMAQ version 4.7 with the SAPRC-99 chemical mechanism was used to simulate PM2.5 concentrations over South Korea. While version 4.7 is an older release compared to current CMAQ versions, it provides a stable and well-validated framework for this study. However, it should be noted that newer CMAQ versions include enhanced aerosol parameterizations such as semi-volatile primary organic aerosols and the volatility basis set for organic aerosols, which may affect model performance. The choice of version 4.7 was based on data availability and consistency with existing emission inventories for 2013. The anthropogenic emissions were processed using the SMOKE-Asia emission modeling system, which utilized the CREATE v3.0 emission inventory20,21. The CMAQ simulations were conducted for four representative months in 2013 (January, April, July, and October) to capture seasonal variations in meteorological conditions and emissions. Each monthly simulation covered 40 consecutive days (including some days from adjacent months to ensure stable boundary conditions), providing a total of 984 hourly time steps per season for model training and evaluation. Two nested domains were used: an outer domain covering East Asia with a 27 km horizontal resolution (174 x 128 grid cells) and an inner domain focusing on South Korea, which is the target domain of our study, with a 9 km horizontal resolution (67 x 82 grid cells). The vertical resolution of the model consisted of 30 layers.

To prepare the input data for the CMAQ simulations, a total of 119 emission scenarios were designed based on variations in the activity levels of different emission source sectors across 17 regions in South Korea. The number of scenarios (119) was determined through systematic consideration of multiple factors. First, the 119-dimensional parameter space (7 sectors \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} 17 regions) requires adequate sampling density to capture nonlinear response surfaces while avoiding both overfitting and underfitting. Based on established sampling guidelines for high-dimensional response surface modeling, a minimum of 10–15 samples per dimension is recommended, suggesting approximately 1200-1800 scenarios would be ideal. However, computational constraints of CMAQ simulations, where each scenario requires approximately 24 hours of computation time, limited our feasible range to approximately 120 scenarios per season. The choice of 119 scenarios represents a carefully balanced compromise that provides sufficient coverage for training a robust surrogate model while remaining computationally feasible within the constraints of the national research project timeline and available computational resources. The emission scenarios were constructed using a control matrix, which consists of control factors representing the activity levels of 7 emission source sectors (power plants, industry, mobile sources, residential, agriculture, solvent, and others) in each of the 17 regions. The control factors in the control matrix were generated using Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS), a statistical method for generating a near-random sample of parameter values from a multidimensional distribution22. LHS was employed to ensure an efficient and unbiased sampling of the control factor space, covering a wide range of potential emission changes. By using LHS, the control factors were varied from 0.5 to 1.5 times the base case values, allowing for a comprehensive exploration of the response surface of PM2.5 concentrations to emission changes. The use of LHS helps to minimize the number of CMAQ simulations required to capture the variability in PM2.5 concentrations while maintaining a good coverage of the parameter space. The resulting dataset consists of hourly PM2.5 concentrations for each grid cell across South Korea under different emission scenarios, along with the corresponding control factor values for each region and sector. This dataset serves as the basis for training the conditional U-Net model to emulate the CMAQ simulations and predict PM2.5 concentrations under various emission reduction scenarios. For the development and evaluation of the model presented in this study, data simulating PM2.5 hourly concentration changes for a total of 119 scenarios for each season were prepared in advance. Of these, 59 data sets were used to train the models, and 60 data sets were used to evaluate the trained models. Since each scenario contains 40 days of hourly concentration data, the total number of samples used in the actual training and evaluation is 56,640 and 57,600, respectively. Of the 56,640 samples used for training, 5,664 samples were used as validation data to verify the training process.

Given the high-dimensional parameter space (119 input dimensions) and complex neural network architecture, overfitting is a critical concern. Therefore, to ensure robust model development and minimize this issue, we performed rigorous 5-fold cross-validation during the training process. This standard machine learning practice involves dividing the training data into five equal folds, where each fold serves as a validation set once while the remaining four folds are used for training. This approach provides multiple performance estimates across different data splits, ensuring that the model generalizes well to unseen data rather than memorizing specific training patterns. Specifically, our cross-validation procedure ensures that: (1) the model never sees validation data during training, preventing data leakage; (2) performance is evaluated across diverse emission scenarios and temporal conditions; and (3) the model’s ability to learn physical relationships rather than spurious correlations is validated. The cross-validation results showed remarkably consistent performance across all five folds (standard deviation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$< 2\%$$\end{document} for MeanNE), with minimal variance in validation metrics, providing strong evidence that our model learns generalizable atmospheric chemistry patterns rather than overfitting to specific training samples. Furthermore, the high performance on completely held-out test scenarios (60 scenarios never seen during training or validation) with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r^{2}$$\end{document} >0.99 further confirms the model’s generalization capability. The final model performance reported in this study represents the average performance across all five folds, providing a more reliable and statistically robust estimate of the model’s true generalization capability than single train-test splits.

Architecture overview

The conditional U-Net is a deep learning architecture based on the U-Net, which consists of encoder and decoder layers connected by skip connections23. Unlike the traditional U-Net that only takes image information as input, the conditional U-Net accepts additional data with different meanings or formats as conditional inputs, which are added to each layer of the U-Net. Recently, the conditional U-Net has been actively utilized in the field of generative artificial intelligence, particularly in diffusion models such as the stable diffusion model, which has successfully performed image generation tasks24,25. In the stable diffusion model, the conditional U-Net takes noised images along with denoising steps and encoded text tokens as conditional inputs.

The conditional U-Net employed in this study is inspired by the stable diffusion model, adopting the structure of taking image and time sequence information as inputs while excluding the encoded text token component. Our model takes the control factor map information and the corresponding time sequence of the hourly PM2.5 concentration changes obtained from CMAQ simulations as inputs. The time sequence is encoded before being fed into the model. This structure is designed to predict the concentration distribution at a specific time step for a given control map and time sequence, considering each time step as a condition.

The specific computation process of the model is as follows. First, the control matrix consisting of 17 regions and 7 sectors is converted into an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$82\times 67\times 7$$\end{document} map through a gridding process. Next, the gridded map and the integer time step (ranging from 0 to 983 in this study, as we trained the model on 40 days of data) are input into the model. Before being fed into the conditional U-Net, the time step is encoded using the sinusoidal time embedding method, which will be explained in a later section. The control map and the encoded time are then passed through the conditional U-Net, which outputs the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$82\times 67$$\end{document} PM2.5 concentration distribution for each time step. The model is trained by comparing the predicted PM2.5 concentration at a specific time step with the corresponding CMAQ-simulated PM2.5 concentration. The mean square error is used as the loss function. The Adam optimizer26 is employed for updating the model parameters, with a fixed learning rate of 0.0005 and a maximum epoch of 300. No additional techniques are applied, and the default layer settings in TensorFlow are followed. The model structure and hyper-parameters, i.e. layer dimension or filter number, are shown in Figure 6.

Gridding strategy for control matrix

In the case of the control matrix to be used as input to the model, it is basically a one-dimensional vector consisting of activity values for each of the 7 sectors of 17 regions, so it cannot be calculated by CNN in an ideal state. Therefore, in this study, we specified the index corresponding to each region on a map of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$82\times 67$$\end{document}, so that each grid has 7 sectors, and created a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$82\times 67\times 7$$\end{document} matrix as an input to the conditional U-Net. We refer to this process as the ”gridding strategy,” which was implemented in practice by developing a custom layer that performs the above gridding and stores the index information for each local government as a fixed variable. Therefore, users who will use this model in the future should refer to the index reference data used in the model version for the gridding technique, and the actual use is designed to enter only a one-dimensional control matrix. The administrative districts and grid index information for each province in Korea used in this study were referenced in the literature27.

Sinusoidal time embedding method

In this study, we employ the sinusoidal time embedding technique, originally used in the Transformer architecture, for positional encoding of time steps. Positional encoding is crucial in sequence-to-sequence models, such as the Transformer, to provide the model with information about the relative position of each element in the input sequence. In the context of the Transformer, sinusoidal time embedding allows the model to learn relative positions and capture temporal dependencies effectively28,29.

Sinusoidal time embedding plays a significant role in capturing the temporal dynamics of PM2.5 concentrations. The input to our model is a simple integer value corresponding to each time step, rather than specific dates or meteorological conditions. As these integer values are relative and lack inherent temporal meaning, it is essential to transform them into a representation that encodes the relative and periodic nature of the time steps. By applying sinusoidal time embedding, we convert the integer time steps into a continuous, high-dimensional space where the model can learn the relative positions and temporal dependencies between the time steps. This approach represents a significant advancement over our previous work, which was limited to static spatial predictions without temporal dynamics. The incorporation of time information into the conditional U-Net structure enables successful prediction of hourly pollutant evolution without requiring complex sequential architectures such as LSTM or GRU networks that are typically needed for time-series modeling. This embedding enables the model to capture the periodic patterns and trends in PM2.5 concentrations over time, which is crucial for accurate prediction and emulation of CMAQ simulations. Moreover, the sinusoidal time embedding helps the model to generalize well to unseen time steps, as the encoding is based on the relative position rather than absolute values. This property is particularly important in our study, as we aim to develop a model that can be applied to various time periods and locations without being limited to specific dates or conditions.
