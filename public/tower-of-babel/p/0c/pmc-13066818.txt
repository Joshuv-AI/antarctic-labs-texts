Involvement of Phytohormone Responses of Rubber Leaves after Pestalotiopsis microspora Infection Revealed with Transcriptome Analysis
Authors: Afdholiatus Syafaah, Fetrina Oktavia, Masaharu Kawauchi, Yuko Makita, Minami Matsui, Stephany Angelia Tumewu, Masafumi Shimizu, Kazuki Sugekawa, Yoshiharu Y. Yamamoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The circular leaf disease, caused by Pestalotiopsis spp., is a new primary foliar disease characterized by yellow-brown lesions that develop into dark-brown lesions on rubber leaves. This disease, named leaf fall disease, has recently led to significant economic losses in countries that produce natural rubber. However, molecular mechanisms that are involved in the interaction between P. microspora and rubber tree (Hevea brasiliensis) remain unclear. In this study, we conducted transcriptome analysis using RNA-seq of a susceptible rubber clone (GT1) after an inoculation with the mycelial plug of P. microspora. Among 84,443 rubber genes, the analysis revealed 3,799, 7,274, and 4,678 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) at 2, 4, and 6 days post-infection (dpi), respectively. Our Gene Ontology analysis of the DEGs detected the GO terms of the general hypersensitive response and systemic acquired resistance, which is known to be mediated by salicylic acid and hydrogen peroxide. We also detected the GO terms related to abscisic acid and ethylene responses. In addition, the detection of these two phytohormone transcriptional responses suggests the possible involvement of abscisic acid and/or ethylene in the process that led to the leaf fall symptom in susceptible rubber. Further, our promoter analysis to extract putative promoter elements responsive to the pathogen supported ethylene signaling activation by the pathogen infection. In addition, we extracted general Regulatory Element Groups that are position-dependent cis-regulatory elements, and established molecular markers for quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction. Our study provides genomic information and convenient tools for facilitating further exploration of Hevea-Pestalotiopsis interaction.

The rubber tree, known as Hevea brasiliensis, originating from the Amazon River basin in South America, has been widely planted on a large scale in Southeast Asia. The rubber tree produces latex, which is high-molecular-weight cis-1,4-isoprene polymer (Distler, 2001) that is responsible for the elasticity of natural rubber.

However, pathogenic fungal diseases, including Rigidoporus (Oghenekaro et al., 2014), Colletotrichum (Aliya et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2018), Corynespora (Manju et al., 2001), and Phytophthora (Viswanathan et al., 2005), are challenging diseases that pose many problems such as economic damage to rubber cultivation. These pathogens attack rubber plants in nurseries, as well as immature and mature plantations in the field, and infect either leaves, roots, or stems. These pathogens reduce the rubber quality and lead to significant losses in rubber density (Oktavia et al., 2021).

Recently, a circular leaf diseases caused by Pestalotiopsis spp is a new major foliar disease causing yellow-brown lesions on leaves, which then develop into dark brown lesions (Aliya et al., 2022; Darojat et al., 2023, 2024; Febbiyanti and Fairuzah, 2019; Kusdiana, 2020). This pathogen leads to massive leaf fall post-infection, thereby severely reducing latex production (Matsui et al., 2025). The disease causes big economic loss in natural rubber-producing countries because of the damage to latex production.

In the context of plant defense mechanisms, plant immunity is identified with a particular collaboration between constitutive and inducible defense mechanisms (Ferreira et al., 2007), including the accumulation of phytoalexins, pathogenesis-related proteins, phenolic compounds, and antimicrobial compounds (Montesinos, 2000). Plant immunity systems have been categorized into pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP)-triggered immunity and effector-triggered immunity (ETI) (Jones and Dangl, 2006). Plants can recognize PAMPs via plant pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs) located on their cell membranes. The successful pathogen secretes effectors, to enable its invasion to the host plants. Single effectors are perceived by nucleotide-binding site-leucine-rich repeat receptor proteins in plant cells, which trigger ETI to activate a stronger immune response against pathogen infection (Jones and Dangl, 2006; Zipfel, 2009, 2014).

Recent gene expression studies of rubber have enabled the identification of several genes associated with plant defense responses. For example, Koop et al. (2016) conducted transcriptome analysis to identify putative genes expressed in Pseudocercospora ulei-infected rubber trees. P. ulei infection activated the 21 upregulated genes involved in reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, ROS-scavenging systems, and leaf senescence. Ribeiro et al. (2021) reported that Corynespora cassiicola infection activated common defense response in both tolerant and susceptible rubber trees, comprising the activation of genes for redox signaling, pathogenesis-related proteins, and secondary metabolism. Zhang et al. (2022) successfully identified the calcium-dependent protein kinase (HbCDPK) genes from rubber, which are responsive to pathogenic fungi and multiple hormonal signals.

To understand the molecular mechanisms of infection of recently developed leaf fall disease and of protection by rubber, genome-wide analysis of the transcriptional response to the pathogen is indispensable. Therefore, we surveyed the response and attempted to characterize the event to improve understanding of the leaf fall disease physiology. Our results provide a basis for molecular breeding of disease-resistant rubber clones.

This study aimed to investigate the following key areas: (1) identification of the P. microspora responsive genes in the rubber genome, (2) extraction of promoter elements from the rubber genome, and (3) development of molecular markers of quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) for physiological studies of the disease. Information obtained from this study provided valuable information for understanding the rubber leaf’s defense mechanisms against P. microspora infection.

Materials and Methods

Plant growth conditions and inoculum preparation

Rubber plants, a Pestalotiopsis-susceptible clone GT1, were cultivated in a greenhouse maintained at 25°C. The rubber leaf used for the experiment was cut off in the mature stage immediately before experiments. In this study, a Japanese strain of Pestalotiopsis microspora strain MAFF-246053 (recently classified as Neopestalotiopsis sp., obtained from NARO Gene Bank, https://www.gene.affrc.go.jp/index_en.php) was used as a challenged pathogen. Tumewu et al. (under review), tested the pathogenicity of multiple P. microspora Japanese strains and found that this strain can cause necrotic lesions on GT1, similar to that of Indonesian isolate, which cause leaf fall disease. The strain was then cultured on potato sucrose agar (PSA) at 28°C in the dark for 7 days to obtain a robustly growing colony.

Inoculation method

A detached rubber leaf was washed with running ion-exchanged water and placed in a plastic translucent box lined with sterile tissue paper to maintain humidity. The adaxial surface was punctured with sterilized syringe needles to inoculate the leaf, thereby creating six clustered inoculation spots. Mycelial plugs, measuring 6 mm in diameter, were cut from the actively growing edge of a single-spore colony of P. microspora using a sterile cork borer for the pathogen-inoculated (PI) samples. These plugs were then placed upside-down at the inoculation sites, thereby ensuring direct contact between the mycelial surface and the leaf. This treatment is hereafter referred to as “PI.” A plug from PSA was utilized for the uninfected control samples, after carefully abrading the lower epidermis at a defined site. Hereafter, this treatment is referred to as “mock” (M). Moist sterile tissue paper was placed over the plugs for both PI and M samples. The leaves were then incubated in a plant growth chamber (BioTron LH-241PFDT+SP; NK System, Osaka, Japan) maintained at 25°C under a 12-h light/dark cycle. Leaf tissue surrounding each inoculation site was collected at 2, 4, and 6 days post-inoculation (dpi). Leaf tissue for the control were harvested at 2, 4, and 6 dpi. All treatments were performed with three biological replicates per sample.

RNA preparation and RNA-seq analysis

Leaf samples for each treatment were pooled, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, ground into fine powder, and stored at −80°C. Total RNA was isolated employing the CTAB buffer method, which was adapted from Syafaah et al. (2020) with modifications as described in Supplementary Table 1. A spectrophotometer (Eppendorf BioSpectrometer Basic, Hamburg, Germany) and electrophoresis in 1% agarose gel were used to assess RNA integrity, purity, and concentration. High-quality RNA samples were used for constructing complementary DNA (cDNA) libraries, which were then sequenced on the DNBSEQ-G400 platform (BGI Genomics, Hong Kong). Three biological replicates were performed for each treatment. Fastp v0.23.4 was used to preprocess raw reads. Clean data were subsequently mapped to the Hevea brasiliensis genome (Makita et al., 2018) using STAR with default parameters (https://github.com/alexdobin/STAR, version 2.7.11a), and featureCounts v2.0.6 (Liao et al., 2014) was used to obtain the expression values. Gene count was used for gene expression calculation. Raw data of RNA-seq can be obtained as GSE310758 at the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Gene Expression Omnibus (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo).

Analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

The DESeq2 package (Love et al., 2014) in R v4.2.3 was used to analyze DEGs between pathogen-inoculation and mock samples at 2, 4, and 6 dpi. Read counts distribution was normalized via variance stabilizing transformation for principal component analysis (PCA) (Love et al., 2014). DEGs were defined as genes with |log2 fold change (FC)| of ≥ 1, and adjusted p-values of < 0.05.

Gene Ontology (GO) analysis

The functional annotations were assigned to the annotated genes using the Blast2GO pipeline according to Lau et al. (2016), and used to construct custom databases for the DEGs’ GO performed with ClusterProfiler R packages with gene length bias correction. DEGs considered GO terms, with corrected adjusted p-values of < 0.05 indicating significant enrichment.

Promoter analysis

Promoter sequences of 17,176 genes from −1,000 to −1 bp relative to TSS were prepared based on the genome sequence and TSS cluster data (Lau et al., 2016). Extraction of putative cis-elements without using transcriptome data was achieved employing the LDSS method to provide Regulatory Element Group (REG) of sequences (Yamamoto et al., 2007). This group is defined as sequences that preferentially appear from −400 to −20 bp from TSS and have a characteristic of independence on sequence direction (Yamamoto et al., 2007). The REG sequences of Arabidopsis and rice have been reported previously (Yamamoto et al., 2007). Prediction of cis-elements using transcriptome data was achieved as previously described (Yamamoto et al., 2011). As positive groups, genes with an FC of ≥ 2.5 were selected for prediction.

qRT-PCR

Seven DEGs associated with P. microspora infection in rubber leaf were selected for qRT-PCR to validate the RNA-seq results. Total RNA of 500 ng was used to synthesize first-strand cDNA using ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master mix with a gDNA Remover (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The 10 μL reverse transcription products were diluted with 90 μL of sterile water and used as templates for qRT-PCR, performed using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II FAST qPCR (TaKaRa, Otsu, Japan). Further, qRT-PCR reaction mixtures comprised 1 μL of cDNA template, 0.5 μL of each primer, 3.0 μL of dH2O, and 2xTB Green Premix master mix in a 10-μL volume. Takara Thermal Cycler Dice® Real Time System III was used for reactions. Cycling conditions were as follows: a denaturing cycle at 95°C for 30 s, followed by 40 amplification cycles (95°C for 5 s, and 60°C for 30 s), and 1 cycle (95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 30 s). Expression analysis was conducted in a 96-well plate.

Quantitation of the qRT-PCR followed the method of Bustin et al. (2009) as described by Kobayashi et al. (2014) with a standard curve from a serial dilution of cDNA to ensure precise relative quantification. Transcript levels of target genes were normalized to an internal standard (Duan et al., 2010). A no-RT control was involved to detect any possible genomic DNA contamination. Finally, the fold change of the expression was log2-transformed for comparison with RNA-seq data. The software Prime3Plus was utilized to design specific primers of the selected genes. Supplementary Table 2 presents primer sequences used in this study, and Supplementary Table 1 shows the method for RNA extraction.

Results

RNA-seq experiments

To understand the molecular mechanism of the response in rubber infected by P. microspora, the dynamics of gene expression in rubber leaves after the infection were investigated using RNA-seq. Three time points (2, 4, and 6 dpi) of infected and mock samples with biological triplicates constituted 18 samples for the analysis. The strain consistently produces necrotic lesions on detached leaves by the time of infection, accompanied by the chlorosis surrounding the lesions. This symptom is comparable to field observation in Sumatra, Indonesia (Fig. 1A). Fig. 1 illustrates the phenotype after the inoculation.

A total of 6 GB of raw sequence data was filtered out for low-quality reads, and 4 GB of clean data with ≥90% of bases scoring Q30 or above were subsequently aligned to the rubber reference genome for downstream analysis. Supplementary Table 3 presents total reads, mapped reads, and the percentage of mapping per sample.

A PCA of the normalized read counts was conducted to evaluated the influence of treatment (PI and M) and infected duration (2, 4, and 6 dpi) on variability among the samples (Fig. 2A). The first two components accounted for 88% of the total variance. PCA1 (71%) separated PI from M treatments on the first axis, whereas PCA2 (17%) distinguished the time point of infection within the pathogen-inoculation group on the second axis. These results revealed significant responses in gene expression by the inoculation, which was modulated by the infection stage.

Numbers of up- and down-regulated genes by P. microspora infection

To identify DEGs in response to P. microspora infection, we analyzed the changes in transcript amount between PI and M treatments in rubber leaves at each time point. DEGs in rubber leaves attacked with P. microspora were identified using a threshold P-value of <0.05 and |log2FC| of >1.0. Among 84,443 rubber tree genes analyzed, at the early infection stage (2 dpi), a total of 3,799 DEGs (1,573 up-regulated and 2,226 down-regulated) were identified among 23,771 expressed genes. The number of DEGs at 4 dpi increased to 7,274 (3,222 up-regulated and 4,052 down-regulated) among 22,755 expressed genes. At 6 dpi, the number of DEGs decreased from 4 dpi to 4,678 (2,445 up-regulated and 2,233 down-regulated) among 22,549 expressed genes. Fig. 2B illustrates the succession of the DEG numbers after the inoculation.

Overlap of DEGs among 2, 4, and 6 dpi was analyzed. Venn analysis of up-regulated DEGs (Fig. 2C) disclosed that 567 genes were up-regulated throughout the analysis from 2 to 6 dpi. Further, 468, 1,425, and 874 genes were transiently up-regulated at 2, 4, and 6 dpi, respectively. Additional overlaps of genes were also observed as shown in the panel.

Fig. 2D illustrates the overlap of down-regulated DEGs. Further, 1,550 genes were down-regulated throughout the analysis, and 2,565, 5,422, and 895 genes were transiently down-regulated at 2, 4, and 6 dpi, respectively. Supplementary Table 4 lists the up- and down-regulated genes.

GO analysis detected ABA and ET involvement in the pathogen response

GO enrichment analysis was conducted to evaluate the physiological aspects of DEGs, with a significance threshold of an overrepresented P-value of <0.05 for term filtering. Table 1 shows the top 15 GO terms for the up-regulation at 2, 4, and 6 dpi.

The GO terms from up-regulated genes detected chitin-related terms, including “chitinase activity,” “chitin catabolic process,” and “chitin binding,” across all the three points. This highlights the importance of fungal cell-wall recognition throughout the defense response and suggests the recognition of PAMPs, especially cell-wall components (chitin) and their degradation using pathogenesis-related proteins, including chitinases.

The detection of hypersensitive response (HR)-related terms (“regulation of plant-type hypersensitive response” and “autophagy”), which appear at 4 dpi, is consistent with the observed evolution of necrotic lesions after the P. microspora infection of the rubber clone, and supports the occurrence of HR by P. microspora infection. Interestingly, the list includes a term “negative regulation of programmed cell death” in the list, suggesting an unknown anti-HR action by the pathogen.

A term related to systemic acquired resistance (SAR), “systemic acquired resistance” also appeared at 4 dpi. The full list of the detected GO terms (Supplementary Tables 5 and 6) includes several related terms to SAR: “peroxidase activity” and “NAD(P)H oxidase H2O2-forming activity” at 2 dpi, and “peroxidase activity”, “respiratory burst involved in defense response,” “salicylic acid biosynthetic process,” and “salicylic acid mediated signaling pathway” at 4 dpi. The detection of these terms suggests induction of SAR through SA by P. microspora infection from 2 to 4 dpi.

Supplementary Tables 5, 6, and 7 present the full lists of the concentrated GO terms in the up-regulated genes as well as other terms related to pathogen response, including “defense response,” “defense response to fungus,” and “response to biotic stimulus,” at 2 and 4 dpi. The detection of these terms supports the idea of the occurrence of pathogen response by the P. microspora infection. The GO analysis demonstrates that transcriptional responses for HR and SAR is actually activated in the susceptible rubber clone.

Involvement of phytohormone-related GO terms was evaluated in the enriched GO categories listed in Table 1 and Supplementary Tables 5–7. We found involvement of two other hormones than salicylic acid (SA) as mentioned above: abscisic acid (ABA) (“abscisic acid-activated signaling pathway”) and ethylene (ET) (“response to ethylene”) at 4 dpi (Supplementary Table 6). Detection of these terms only at 4 dpi suggests a transient activation of ABA and ET responses at 4 dpi. ABA and ET are both known to initiate abscission layer formation in plant tissues; thus, the observed activation of these two phytohormone responses suggested their possible involvement in the leaf fall symptom caused by P. microspora infection.

Fig. 3 illustrates genes for phytohormone-related GO terms that are up-regulated at 4 dpi. As shown, the majority of the genes in the three lists for SA, ET, and ABA have up-regulation only at 4 dpi, but some have additional activation time point(s) at 2 and/or 6 dpi. These expression profiles are consistent with the idea that SA, ET, and ABA responses occur at a peak time of 4 dpi.

Table 2 represents the top GO terms detected from the down-regulated genes post-infection. The majority of the GO terms at 2 and 4 dpi are related to photosynthesis, that are “chloroplast thylakoid membrane,” “photosynthesis,” “chlorophyll binding,” “photosystem II,” and others. At 6 dpi, these photosynthesis-related terms disappeared from the top 15 (Table 2). Massive detection of photosynthesis-related terms at 2 and 4 dpi and their reduction at 6 dpi can be confirmed in the full list of the detected GO terms (Supplementary Tables 5–7). Overall, as the infection progresses, the down-regulated GO terms highlight a coordinated suppression of photosynthesis, energy metabolism, and cell growth.

Promoter analysis detected possible involvement of the GCC box in the pathogen response

Promoter regions play a crucial role in transcriptional regulation, including biotic stress response. The identification of cis-regulatory elements in the promoter region for DEGs provides information that is necessary for molecular understanding of plant immune response. In order to improve interpretation of the transcriptome data, we then analyzed the promoter region in the rubber genome. The information of promoter elements of the rubber genome is also useful to understand the genome polymorphism of within rubber clones which causes differential gene expression, including immune responses.

The first analysis of the rubber promoters is the global extraction of REG, which is estimated to constitute half of the cis-regulatory elements in a genome (Yamamoto et al., 2007). The information can be the basis for understanding global transcriptional regulation in the rubber genome because of the high coverage of cis-elements. The process of REG extraction does not require any expression data; thus, the function of the extracted REGs should be speculated with additional information.

This promoter analysis identified 540 rubber REGs. Comparison with Arabidopsis and rice REGs revealed common and unique REGs (Fig. 4A, full data are available in Supplementary Table 8). We found that 382 out of 540 rubber REGs were specific to rubber. In contrast, the common REGs among the three species are 55. The comparison of the REGs of three species indicates that the majority of rubber cis-elements are specific to rubber, indicating that the use of the Arabidopsis and rice sequences for detection of cis-elements from the rubber genome is limited. Therefore, the sequences of rubber REGs in Supplementary Table 8 are more helpful to understand gene regulation in the rubber genome.

The extracted REGs were then categorized into the reported subgroups, including cell cycle/meristematic expression, environmental responses (light, UV, drought, and ABA), and stress responses (Table 3). The table demonstrates high conservation of cell cycle-related cis-elements (Group 1) and acquisition of many cis-elements for environmental response in the rubber genome (Group 2).

Next promoter analysis utilized transcriptome data of the pathogen response. We evaluated the concentration of octamer sequences in the promoter region among up-regulated genes at 2, 4, and 6 dpi, which extracted 95, 853, and 194 octamers, respectively, as putative cis-elements for gene activation after the pathogen infection (Fig. 4B). The panel also shows overlap between the three groups.

Our analysis detected the TGACG motif for the SA response through TGA1 (Després et al., 2003) and the GCC box for ethylene response by ERF (Shoji et al., 2013) for up-regulation at 2 and 4 dpi (Fig. 4B). This analysis revealed possible involvement of TGA1, which actually plays a role in SAR in Arabidopsis, and the ERF family, which mediates ethylene response (Putranto et al., 2015) in the response of the rubber to infection of P. microspora. Genes containing the extracted GCC box in the promoter region among the pathogen-induced genes are listed in Table 4. Considering the role of ethylene in plants, we reveal a possibility of activation of this subset by the pathogen infection as a part of the molecular mechanism of leaf fall after P. microspora infection.

We examined whether the list of extracted putative cis-elements contains ABA-Responsive Element (ABRE) (Yamaguchi-Shinozaki and Shinozaki, 2005). Typical ABRE (CACGTGGC) was not observed in the extracted sequences; however, ACGTG-containing sequences were found in the list of 4 and 6 dpi. The absence of typical ABRE in the putative cis-elements for up-regulation after the pathogen infection indicates that gene activation through the element is minor or absent among the up-regulated genes. Therefore, the possibility of ABRE-dependent activation of ABA-related genes after pathogen infection should be low, but excluding this possibility entirely remains challenging.

Fig. 5 summarizes GO and promoter analyses. GO analysis detected chitin-related responses, HR, and SAR as reasonable pathogen responses in the up-regulated genes. In addition, two phytohormone responses to ABA and ET were also detected. Promoter analysis supports responses to SA and ET (asterisk in the figure). The down-regulated genes include photosynthesis-related genes.

Establishment of molecular markers for qRT-PCR

To assist future physiological studies of the leaf fall disease, we developed molecular markers for qRT-PCR. Fig. 6A illustrates a comparison of the expression of 7 genes determined using qRT-PCR and RNA-seq, including beta-glucosidase, lipoxygenase, PP2C, NPR3, and PAL homologs. The expression patterns observed using qRT-PCR closely replicated those obtained from transcriptome data, with an enough correlation coefficient of 0.98 (Fig. 6B).

Discussion

P. microspora is a pathogenic fungus that sometimes interacts with other pathogens to infect plant organs and leading to necrotic damage to all infected organs. Hence, the pathogen causes economic losses for various plants, including crops, fruits, and woody plants. In rubber, pathogens, such as Neopestalotiopsis spp. (Darojat et al., 2023) and Pestalotiopsis spp. (Damiri et al., 2022; Febbiyanti and Fairuzah, 2019; Kusdiana, 2020), were identified as the causal pathogen of the new leaf fall disease.

As summarized in Fig. 5, chitin-related GO terms detected from 2 to 6 dpi. This suggests continuous activation of chitin-related events in the rubber post-infection. Plants generally activate the enzymatic defense mechanism to degrade the fungal cell walls and generate chitin-derived oligosaccharides, which, as an elicitor, are recognized by PRRs (Zipfel, 2014). In our study, we observed some up-regulated genes encoding receptor kinase PRR (RK-PRR) family proteins, including chitin elicitor receptor kinase (Hb_002215_010, Hb_002812_010, and Hb_007433_020, which are all close homologs of AtCERK1). The Arabidopsis homolog, AtCERK1, triggers a downstream immune response and reinforces cell wall-associated defense (Ribeiro et al., 2021). This function is crucial to the defense in Arabidopsis and rice where chitin catabolism contributes to defense against necrotrophs and hemibiotrophs (Duan et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2016; Kumar et al., 2025).

Chitinases have been determined in the defense response of various plants, including maize (Hawkins et al., 2015), apple (Duan et al., 2022), soybean (Chen et al., 2024), and rice. Chitinase genes have been identified in rice as degrading fungal cell walls and conferring resistance to various pathogens, including Rhizoctonia solani (Datta et al., 2001; Mao et al., 2014; Prathi et al., 2024; Richa et al., 2016, 2017), Magnaporthe oryzae (Mao et al., 2014; Nishizawa et al., 1999), and Fusarium solani (Tanaka et al., 2023).

Martínez–Caballero et al. (2014) revealed that GH19 chitinase-like proteins, including HbCLP1 and HbCLP2, of rubber retain strong binding affinity for chitin and chitooligosaccharides; however, they naturally lack the glutamate residue in the catalytic domain. The restoration of these residues through site-mutagenesis reactivated their functional endochitinase and significantly inhibited Alternaria alternata growth. This finding revealed that the rubber tree against fungal pathogen depends on the GH19-mediated degradation of fungal cell-wall chitin. In this study, the activation of 19 chitinase genes, including basic chitinase, homolog of carrot EP-3 chitinase, and PR-4 homologs, by P. microspora infection is considered one of the rubber strategies against fungal infection. The activation of a set of transcripts encoding for chitin-binding type 1 was observed in the Hevea-Phytophthora interactions (Aswathy et al., 2024).

The GO analysis identified SAR and HR in the response to P. microspora, as summarized in Fig. 5. In general, HR induces SAR (Ang et al., 2024; Balint-Kurti, 2019; Cao et al., 2024), leading to improved systemic immunity to various pathogens (Grant and Lamb, 2006). Therefore, detecting SAR-related genes accompanied by HR-related genes in the analysis is reasonable.

We observed necrotic lesions of the rubber GT1 during the infection of P. microspora, and they are different from the typical HR phenotype (Fig. 1A) (Govrin and Levine, 2000). However, the detection of GO terms related to HR suggests the transcriptional activation of HR-related events after the infection. One possibility is that the HR response is partially ongoing in the rubber GT1.

The detection of transcriptional responses related to HR and SAR in the rubber after P. microspora infection implies two points. The first point is that P. microspora does not obviously suppress either HR or SAR. Some pathogens suppress the immune response of the host plants (Iakovidis et al., 2023). Hieno et al. (2019) detected strong ROS response suppression in Arabidopsis by a pathogenic bacterium, Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato DC3000 in the promoter analysis. In this study, such a stringent suppression was not observed, indicating that HR and SAR are allowed by the pathogen. The second point is that the activated defense responses in the susceptible rubber clone are not effective against disease development. To enrich our knowledge of the physiology after P. microspora infection, a comparison between susceptible and resistant rubber clones would be useful.

Herein, we identified ABA and ET responses to P. microspora infection (Fig. 5). Our promoter analysis supported the hypothesis of the involvement of the ET signaling in the pathogen response (Fig. 4). Detection of the transcriptional responses related to the two phytohormones by P. microspora infection suggests their involvement in the leaf fall symptom post-infection because of their ability to form an abscission layer. These results provide an outline to understand the molecular mechanism of the leaf fall symptom caused by the P. microspora infection. Because of the recent spread of the leaf fall disease in Southeast Asia, breeding of the rubber clones is requested, which do not reduce latex productivity under the attack of the leaf fall disease. Our results opened a new strategy for breeding, focusing on the response to the two phytohormones.

In addition to promoting abscission layer, ABA is also known to mediate an anti-infection response, which acts to prevent pathogen entry into plants by closing stomata (Hieno et al., 2016). If leaf fall is mediated by ABA and works for isolating infected leaves from the plant body, it can also account for the protective reaction of plants. However, in the case of the utilized susceptible rubber clone (GT1), leaf fall does not successfully protect the plant from the pathogen, and exhibits a big negative effect on plant growth and latex production (Matsui et al., 2025). Whether this leaf fall, without considerable positive effects, is common throughout the species or specific to the examined clone remains unclear.

Biotic stresses, including viral, fungal, and bacterial pathogens, suppress photosynthesis in plants (Bilgin et al., 2010; Cheng et al., 2016; Girija et al., 2017; Pandelova et al., 2012; Pérez-Clemente et al., 2015). This suppression is frequently caused by down regulation of photosynthetic genes in response to the biotic stress signaling including MAMP-triggered, MAPK-mediated and hormone-dependent (SA/JA/ET) immune responses (Bilgin et al., 2010; Su et al., 2018). Our case, as summarized in Fig. 5, is also consistent with these reports.

In addition to the photosynthesis-related genes, we also revealed gene suppression for primary carbon-assimilation pathways and auxin polar transport (Table 2, Supplementary Tables 5–7). These coordinated transcriptional patterns align with the growth-defense trade-off in plants; however, the mechanisms underlying these processes require further clarification.

We extracted putative cis-regulatory elements for the response to P. microspora. These data are useful for understanding molecular events in rubber trees after P. microspora infection, and apply to understanding differential gene expression between susceptible and resistant rubber clones, which have polymorphisms in the genome sequences.

Our study introduces general REGs in the rubber genome. Rubber REGs (Supplementary Table 8) are necessary to understand the function of the rubber genome and the difference in the functions among rubber clones, because they have considerable divergence from the reported Arabidopsis and rice REGs (Fig. 4A).

The method to extract rubber REGs is called LDSS (Local Distribution of Short Sequences) analysis (Yamamoto et al., 2007) and is able to detect about half of the cis-regulatory elements in a genome without intensive DNA-protein binding experiments. Because of the convenience and comprehensive genome-wide detection, it is a good method for a newly sequenced genome to add genome annotation related to transcriptional regulation.

Based on our RNA-seq data, we have developed several molecular markers that detect gene activation by P. microspora infection. The developed primers, shown in Supplementary Table 2, can be used for detailed physiological analysis of the disease using convenient qRT-PCR much easier than RNA-seq.

In conclusion, our transcriptome analysis of a rubber clone susceptible to P. microspora infection revealed the involvement of ABA and ET responses of transcription post-infection, in addition to general pathogen responses of transcription, namely HR and SAR. Our finding contributes to molecular understanding of the leaf fall symptom caused by the infection, which causes significant and global economic damage to the natural rubber industry.
