Comparative Evaluation of Field of View Across Widefield Retinal Imaging Systems
Authors: Shuibin Ni, Ringo Ng, Yakub Bayhaqi, David Sutter, Susan Ostmo, David Huang, Benjamin K. Young, John Peter Campbell, Yifan Jian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeUltra-widefield retinal imaging is critical for diagnosing peripheral retinal diseases, but discrepancies in field of view (FOV) definitions hinder system comparisons. This study aims to evaluate the FOV performance of six widefield or ultra-widefield retinal imaging systems using a simplified eye phantom model and in vivo demonstration in a healthy subject.MethodsA custom eye phantom model with concentric ring patterns and geometric markers was used to assess FOV and distortion across six imaging systems, including Nikon Optos, Natus RetCam, Intalight Dream OCT, Siloam Vision's contact and non-contact iCam-OCT, and OHSU table-top UWF OCT. The phantom model eliminated anatomic variability, enabling controlled measurements. Imaging was also performed on a healthy emmetropic adult under identical conditions for qualitative validation.ResultsThe evaluation revealed significant variations in peripheral coverage and distortion among systems. Nikon Optos and Siloam Vision contact iCam-OCT achieved the widest FOVs, whereas RetCam and Intalight Dream OCT had more limited coverage. Radial and tangential distortion increased with eccentricity, with Nikon Optos exhibiting the highest distortion. In vivo imaging confirmed phantom-based findings regarding FOV coverage and distortion across these systems.ConclusionsFOV performance varies across imaging systems, and the measured FOVs were generally consistent with the manufacturers stated specifications. Distortion increased with eccentricity and differed among devices. The phantom model provided an objective method for quantifying FOV and distortion, with findings supported by in vivo imaging.Translational RelevanceThis work offers a practical method for evaluating widefield imaging systems, supporting informed system selection, and emphasizing consistent evaluation criteria in clinical practice.

Introduction

Ultra-widefield retinal imaging has become an essential tool in ophthalmology, enabling visualization of the peripheral retina for early detection and monitoring of conditions such as diabetic retinopathy (DR), retinopathy of prematurity (ROP), retinoblastoma, etc.1–3 As clinical reliance on ultra-widefield technologies grows, the field of view (FOV) captured by imaging systems – which determines the extent of retinal coverage – has emerged as a critical performance metric, directly influencing diagnostic reach and utility. However, discrepancies in how FOV is defined and reported – whether as incident angle, visual angle, or eye angle – have led to substantial ambiguity among clinicians, researchers, and medical photographers, complicating the interpretation of imaging coverage and limiting the clarity of clinical communication and system selection.4 Further confusion arises from the use of linear units such as millimeters, particularly common in optical coherence tomography (OCT) systems, which describe lateral scan width on the retina but lack standardized angular correlation across different eye models and axial lengths. The visual angle, historically used in conventional fundus photography, is defined at the nodal point of the eye and describes how a target subtends onto the retina from an external viewpoint.5 In contrast, the eye angle, measured from the geometric center of the eyeball, is often reported by commercial ultra-widefield systems but is highly dependent on anatomic variables such as axial length and retinal curvature. These anatomic dependencies limit reproducibility and hinder consistent interpretation across imaging systems. The incident angle, relevant to retinal imaging systems with a scanning beam, such as OCT and scanning laser ophthalmoscopy (SLO), represents the angular range of the scanning beam as it enters the eye. It is intrinsic to the optical design of the imaging system and, unlike visual or eye angles, is independent of eye model assumptions, providing a device-centric, reproducible metric for comparing imaging coverage.

Whereas previous studies have explored the conversion and relationship between the visual angle and eye angle,4,6,7 the role of the incident angle remains less discussed. Both visual angle and eye angle are affected by assumptions regarding nodal point location, corneal curvature, and intraocular lens geometry. In contrast, the incident angle offers a stable reference for assessing system capability. Under certain conditions – particularly when imaging is centered on the pupil and confined to moderate field angles (up to approximately 70 degrees) – the incident angle can approximate the visual angle due to the near-linear scaling of retinal magnification.8 This approximation holds particularly well within intermediate angular ranges, where ray tracing and nodal point theory demonstrate quasi-linear angular behavior.8

In addition to the angular definition, the location of the pivot point, which refers to the location along the optical path about which the scanning beam rotates, plays a pivotal role in determining effective imaging coverage. The position of this pivot point, whether near the pupil, nodal point, or center of the eye, significantly influences the ray trajectories, thereby impacting both peripheral coverage and the risk of vignetting. In ultra-widefield imaging systems, positioning the pivot point at or near the pupil plane is particularly advantageous, as it allows scanning beams to enter the eye at wider angles without being obstructed by the iris, thus maximizing the effective FOV. Recognizing the influence of the pivot point location provides a foundation for more objective and consistent comparisons of angular performance across devices, especially when evaluated using a controlled and repeatable setup such as a phantom eye model.

To address these limitations, in this study, we conducted a comparative evaluation of six retinal imaging systems using a custom-designed simplified eye phantom model. The eye phantom model eliminates confounding anatomic factors, such as corneal curvature and lens power, allowing a controlled assessment of incident angle and its mapping to effective FOV. An interchangeable physical stop aperture placed at the iris plane defines a fixed pivot location for scanning beams, enabling consistent evaluation across systems. Although the eye phantom model does not replicate the full realism of the human eye, it provides an intuitive and repeatable model for evaluating the angular performance of imaging devices. The phantom-based findings are further validated with clinical imaging in a healthy adult subject, providing translational context on the findings and demonstrating the comparative evaluation of clinical image quality needed for diagnosing retinal diseases as well.

Materials and Methods

Ocular Geometric Modeling and Pivot Point Configuration

Figure 1 provides a conceptual diagram illustrating the angular parameters and pivot point configuration in ultra-widefield retinal imaging systems. Traditionally, imaging capabilities in fundus photography have been specified using the visual angle, which defines the extent of the imaged retinal area (see the blue arrow in Fig. 1A) relative to the nodal point of the eye (see point “N” in Fig. 1A). However, more recently, some commercial widefield systems – such as Optos (Optos Inc., USA) and RetCam (Natus, USA) – reported their FOV based on the eye angle, which defines the imaged retinal extent relative to the geometric center of the eye (see point “O” in Fig. 1A). The distinction and relationship between visual angle and eye angle have been thoroughly examined by Yao et al.4 Both are dependent on the assumed eye model.5 Given the anatomic variability among individuals, these angle-based definitions inherently vary from eye to eye. In contrast, the incident angle (or input/external angle) – defined as the angle at which light enters the system – is a property of the imaging device itself and remains independent of the eye model. Although simplified diagrams often approximate the incident angle and the visual angle as interchangeable, such representations serve only as coarse approximations for relating system geometry to retinal coverage.8 As shown in Figure 1A, the schematic illustrates a single geometric configuration that defines the relationship among incident angle, visual angle, and eye angle, thereby establishing how FOV is quantified. Because the system is rotationally symmetric, only half of the representative rays are labeled for clarity. Definitions of Angular Metrics

Of particular importance in modeling widefield imaging is the position of the pivot point – the location where scanning rays converge within the optical system. The pivot point position directly impacts both the ray tracing path and the achievable FOV. Moreover, the pivot point location in OCT retinal imaging systems also influences the optical path length across the scanning range. As demonstrated by Kolb et al.,9 positioning the pivot point closer to the center of the eye can reduce the optical path length variations, resulting in flatter B-scans – an advantageous configuration for narrow-angle scans such as 3 × 3 mm or 6 × 6 mm. This pivot point placement is particularly well-suited for imaging systems with relatively low sensitivity roll-off across the axial imaging range. However, this strategy becomes impractical for ultra-widefield applications. Placing the pivot point near the eye center or further posteriorly results in significant iris clipping of peripheral rays due to the limited pupil aperture. Alternatively, moving the pivot anteriorly near the iris plane can reduce vignetting artifacts; however, this approach introduces new challenges, as the peripheral retina may extend beyond the axial imaging range, leading to B-scan aliasing or complex conjugate artifacts that truncate retinal curvature. Although such limitations can be partially mitigated through the use of multiple reference arms10,11 or an aliasing-based data compression algorithm,12,13 this study focuses on measuring the FOV under realistic phantom model and in vivo testing conditions to ensure clinically relevant comparisons. For ultra-widefield imaging, positioning the pivot point on the pupil plane remains the most practical and effective configuration, as shown in Figure 1B. This placement enables the widest attainable FOV by reducing iris obstruction and minimizing beam vignetting, which is commonly adopted in the design of ultra-widefield imaging systems. Figures 1C and 1D illustrate how deviations in pivot point location – either anteriorly into the anterior chamber (Fig. 1C) or posteriorly into the eyeball (Fig. 1D) – can adversely affect the FOV. In both scenarios, iris vignetting becomes more pronounced, especially at extreme angles (see the dashed green circles in Figs. 1C, 1D). This limitation is a known issue in conventional OCT systems where beam wander and small pupil sizes exacerbate peripheral loss.14,15

Phantom Eye Design and Angular Transformation Analysis

To isolate imaging system performance from the variability inherent to human anatomy, a phantom eye model was developed to enable comparative evaluation of ultra-widefield retinal imaging systems available at Oregon Health and Science University (OHSU). As illustrated in Figure 2A, the eye phantom model consists of a plano-convex lens (LA1560-AB; Thorlabs Inc., USA) with an effective focal length of 25 mm, paired with a concave spherical imaging surface of 12 mm radius. This optical configuration mimics the focusing function of the human eye while eliminating intersubject variability. To replicate realistic pupil constraints, the phantom model includes a physical stop aperture (Fig. 2B), emulating typical physiological pupil diameters under imaging conditions. The CAD files of the phantom model design are included in the ZIP file in the Supplementary Materials.

The concave retinal surface is embedded with concentric ring patterns spaced at 20 degrees (eye angle) intervals (±10 degrees symmetry), enabling both FOV assessment and image clarity evaluation (see Fig. 2B). Additionally, neighboring 1 × 1 mm convex square and circle patterns (r = 1 mm) are positioned between rings to facilitate quantitative distortion analysis. Figure 2C outlines the geometric mapping between the incident angle (θ) – a system-defined input – and the phantom eye angle (β), analogous to the eye angle used in vivo (see Fig. 1A). To streamline terminology, we use “eye angle” throughout this manuscript for both phantom- and patient-based measurements, while describing the phantom model’s geometry here for completeness. The optical element is fabricated from N-BK7 glass (n = 1.51 at 1060 nm), with a posterior radius of curvature of 12.9 mm and a thickness of 3.5 mm. The lens is positioned such that the distance from its posterior surface to the center of the phantom sphere is 8.8 mm. Following geometric ray tracing and angular transformation, a mapping from system-defined incident angles to corresponding surface locations on the phantom was established (Fig. 2D). The full derivation of the equation used to calculate the angular transformation curve can be found in Supplementary Figure S1 and the accoompanying text in the Supplementary Materials. Although this model does not fully replicate the anatomic complexity of the human eye – for example, it excludes corneal curvature and internal media such as the vitreous – it provides a controlled, repeatable, and eye-model-independent approach for evaluating the angular imaging performance and distortion characteristics of ultra-widefield devices across manufacturers.

Study Subjects

A 35-year-old healthy adult with emmetropic vision (−0.20 diopter [D]) and an axial length of 23.8 mm was recruited from the Casey Eye Institute at the OHSU to participate in this pilot study. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the OHSU Institutional Review Board (IRB) and adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from the participant prior to initiating any study procedures. The participant had no known history of ocular pathology. Although some ultra-widefield systems can acquire images under non-mydriatic conditions,16–18 pharmacological pupil dilation was performed in this study to eliminate potential iris vignetting artifacts caused by pupil size variability. This approach ensured unobstructed beam entry, minimized iris-induced vignetting, and allowed for the consistent assessment of each system's maximum achievable FOV.

Results

The same eye phantom and a healthy adult were tested using the six retinal imaging systems, including Nikon Optos (P200DTx), Siloam Vision (contact iCam-OCT prototype),19 Siloam Vision (non-contact iCam-OCT prototype), Natus (RetCam), Intalight (Dream OCT), and OHSU (Table-top UWF OCT).17 All phantom and in vivo imaging were performed in a macular-centered configuration to standardize fixation across devices and ensure a consistent optical reference point for comparison. Disc-centered imaging, whereas valuable for evaluating the nasal periphery, was excluded from the primary protocol to avoid introducing anatomic asymmetry into cross-device analysis. Additional pilot disc-centered data are provided in Supplementary Figure S2 in the Supplementary Materials for reference. To emulate real-world pupil dynamics, 3 interchangeable pupil sizes – 2, 4, and 8 mm – were implemented, corresponding to typical pupil diameters under different ambient lighting levels. To guarantee the widest possible FOV, appropriate stop apertures were used to avoid iris vignetting. Comparative Evaluation of Retinal Imaging Devices With Model Eye Phantom

Using the simplified phantom eye model, a direct comparative evaluation was conducted across six ultra-widefield imaging systems to assess their relative field coverage and peripheral performance, as shown in Figure 3. Although the phantom model is not designed to replicate the full anatomic realism of the human eye, it provides a consistent and intuitive model for visualizing and comparing the lateral extent of retinal imaging across systems under identical conditions. The results revealed clear variation in each system's ability to resolve structured patterns embedded in the phantom model, particularly in the periphery.

Nikon Optos and Siloam Vision contact iCam-OCT achieved the most extensive coverage, successfully resolving peripheral FOV ring patterns with high clarity up to the 10th ring, corresponding to an eye angle of approximately 200 degrees. In contrast, systems such as the RetCam and the Intalight Dream OCT showed more restricted FOVs, with the latter also exhibiting vertical FOV truncation, which is also present in the Nikon Optos images. A comparative assessment between the contact and non-contact configurations of the Siloam Vision iCam-OCT prototypes highlighted the impact of working distance and optical coupling on imaging performance. The contact configuration yielded improved peripheral coverage. Overall, this phantom-based evaluation provides a consistent and reproducible means of comparing widefield imaging capabilities across different imaging devices, even if it does not represent full ocular realism. Radial and Tangential Distortion Quantification Across Imaging Systems

To objectively assess geometric fidelity in ultra-widefield imaging, distortion metrics were extracted from the phantom images along both radial and tangential axes, as illustrated in Figure 4. The measurements were based on well-defined structural features embedded in the phantom model – specifically, the central crosshairs and protruded square markers. As shown in the inset of Figure 3A, the crosshair, located at the image center, served as the geometric reference. Distortion was calculated by taking the ratio of the observed length of each square feature – positioned between concentric rings along radial paths – to the corresponding crosshair length. This was performed separately for the radial (Lr/Dr) and tangential (Lt/Dt) components, where Lr and Lt represent the side lengths of the square feature, and Dr and Dt denote the respective reference lengths from the central crosshair. The procedure was repeated three times independently, and the results in Figure 4 are reported as mean ± standard deviation across these measurements.

Quantitative analysis of geometric distortion in ultra-widefield imaging systems along the radial and tangential axes. (A) Radial distortion, expressed as the ratio of the observed convex square length to the centered crosshair length (Lr/Dr in pixels), plotted as a function of ring order (eccentricity) for six imaging systems. The dashed white line denotes the ideal distortion-free reference, where the lengths of the crosshair and convex square features are equal. (B) Tangential distortion, measured as the ratio Lt/Dt (in pixels), plotted similarly by ring order. Most systems demonstrate minimal distortion in the central and mid-peripheral regions, with distortion progressively increasing toward the periphery. Nikon Optos shows the highest tangential distortion across the outer rings. Error bars represent mean ± standard deviation across three independent measurements.

The dashed white line in Figure 4 represents the theoretical distortion-free reference, corresponding to a perfect match between the observed square dimensions and the central crosshair lengths. Most systems exhibited minimal distortion in the central region, but deviations became more pronounced toward the periphery. The Nikon Optos system showed the largest distortion magnitudes in both radial and tangential directions, particularly at greater eccentricities – a finding consistent with previous studies.20–22 Other systems also demonstrated increasing distortion with field angles, although to a lesser extent than the Nikon Optos.

Comparative Evaluation of Retinal Imaging Devices in a Healthy Adult Subject

For a comparative evaluation of clinical image quality needed for diagnosing retinal diseases using the aforementioned devices, the study conducted clinical testing on a healthy adult under consistent imaging conditions. As shown in Figure 5, an overlay of the retinal coverage captured by six ultra-widefield imaging systems reveals notable variation in FOV. Whereas certain imaging systems, such as Nikon Optos and Siloam Vision contact iCam-OCT prototype, achieved extended peripheral coverage reaching into the far periphery, others, including the Natus RetCam and Intalight Dream OCT, were limited to central or mid-peripheral imaging. Among these, the RetCam (fundus camera) demonstrated inherently lower contrast and resolution, attributable to the absence of confocal pinholes for rejecting scattered light. It is worth noting that Natus RetCam is specifically intended for pediatric retinal imaging. Its optical and ergonomic design supports handheld operation, ease of transport, and sufficient resolution for imaging smaller pediatric eyes. As such, its performance in this study, which involved an adult subject and emphasized extensive peripheral visualization, may not accurately represent its intended clinical application or strengths. Dashed boundary circles in Figure 5A delineate the retinal regions captured by each device, providing a visual comparison of each device's retinal coverage. It should be noted that Figure 5 is presented as a qualitative comparison of retinal coverage across systems. Quantitative in vivo FOV metrics were not reported here, as they require standardized correction for retinal curvature and device-specific distortion models prior to reliable 3D-to-2D mapping.23 To further support anatomic interpretation, Figure 6 presents a choroidal en face image acquired from the Siloam Vision contact iCam-OCT system, in which vortex vein ampullae serves as intrinsic angular reference points. The colored boundary overlays from Figure 5 are superimposed to illustrate the relative coverage of each device in terms of eye angles. The visualization of vortex veins highlights their value as robust anatomic landmarks for anchoring and comparing peripheral extent across ultra-widefield imaging studies.6

FOV coverage is particularly important in the context of retinal diseases, where peripheral retinal visualization is critical for effective diagnosis and management. Conditions such as ROP, retinal holes and tears, retinoblastoma, and retinal detachment often require assessment for the far periphery to identify disease presence, progression, or treatment response.24–30 Ultra-widefield imaging enables clinicians to evaluate these peripheral regions comprehensively, supporting early detection, accurate staging, and informed therapeutic planning. Whereas systems may vary in their design trade-offs, such as resolution, contrast, and working distance, this comparison underscores the importance of considering peripheral imaging capability when selecting imaging systems for specific diagnostic applications.

Discussion

Our systematic comparison of six ultra-widefield retinal imaging systems reveals significant variations in FOV and geometric distortion. The custom simplified eye phantom model enabled objective evaluation, demonstrating that systems like Nikon Optos and Siloam Vision contact iCam-OCT provide superior peripheral coverage compared to Natus RetCam and Intalight Dream OCT. Our findings confirm that pivot point placement at the pupil plane optimizes FOV by minimizing iris clipping, aligning with theoretical predictions. These results emphasize that peripheral imaging performance – often overlooked in system evaluations – varies substantially across different imaging systems, with important implications for clinical applications requiring adequate peripheral visualization.

The phantom model's design represents a key methodological advancement, incorporating angular patterns and structural markers to enable systematic distortion quantification. Although it does not replicate the full anatomic complexity of the human eye, this simplified approach provides an intuitive and practical means of assessing radial and tangential distortion across imaging systems. Our analysis revealed significant peripheral distortion variations, with the Nikon Optos system exhibiting pronounced peripheral geometric deviations that could compromise spatial accuracy in applications such as lesion tracking, image registration, and montage construction.31,32 These findings highlight the limitations of relying solely on central imaging performance, as peripheral fidelity varies substantially across devices. Such disparities underscore the need for integrating distortion-correction algorithms into clinical workflows, particularly for applications requiring precise peripheral measurements. By utilizing concentric rings and calibration features, the phantom model facilitates pixel-level distortion mapping, serving as a practical tool to compare geometric performance.

The study underscores the value of extended peripheral imaging for managing retinal diseases such as ROP, DR, and retinoblastoma, where comprehensive visualization of the far periphery is critical for early detection, staging, and treatment planning.3,33–35 Systems capable of achieving a wider FOV without compromising contrast or resolution could enhance diagnostic reliability in these cases. Whereas this study does not rank system performance, it highlights the importance of considering FOV alongside other factors – such as resolution, distortion, and patient tolerance (mainly for contact application) – when selecting imaging systems. Our FOV measurements, obtained using a simplified eye phantom model, revealed notable disparities among ultra-widefield retinal imaging systems. The phantom model’s angular dimensions align closely with manufacturer specifications, although its design was not intended to replicate the exact geometry of the human eye or validate commercial claims. Additionally, contact-based systems (e.g. Natus RetCam and Siloam Vision contact iCam-OCT prototype), which are commonly used in managing pediatric retinal diseases, require lubricating gel to ensure corneal coupling and prevent abrasion. Beyond FOV considerations, the contact-based approach offers improved image stability, more consistent pivot point control, and enhanced optical coupling, which together can extend peripheral visualization. However, these benefits come at the expense of increased procedural complexity and the potential patient discomfort, including a small but non-negligible risk of corneal epithelial disturbance. In contrast, non-contact systems maximize patient comfort and streamline workflow but are generally more susceptible to image degradation from motion artifacts and iris vignetting, particularly in eyes with small pupils. These trade-offs should be carefully considered alongside distortion, resolution, and disease-specific imaging requirements when selecting an appropriate modality.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the phantom model is suitable for angular benchmarking, it does not simulate optical aberrations, refractive errors, or media opacities present in real eyes. The lack of vitreous and corneal curvature may affect how light behaves at extreme angles, particularly for OCT-based systems. Future studies should incorporate diverse ocular anatomies and pathological conditions to validate broader applicability. Additionally, whereas this single subject clinical testing was sufficient for qualitative validation of phantom-derived results, it does not capture the variability present across the broader population. Disc-centered imaging was also performed in pilot testing and confirmed the expected increase in nasal coverage. Supplementary Figure S2 in the Supplementary Materials provides these disc-centered results for reference. Future work will incorporate systematic disc-centered acquisitions in a larger and more diverse cohort, including eyes with different axial lengths, refractive errors, and retinal pathologies, to characterize anisotropy in peripheral coverage and distortion. Finally, whereas the pivot point location was standardized for comparison purposes, real-world systems may use dynamic pivoting or pupil tracking strategies, which were not explored in this study.

In conclusion, this work provides a practical approach for objective performance evaluation of ultra-widefield retinal imaging systems, clarifying the relationships among FOV, pivot point configuration, and distortion among these systems. By combining phantom-based and clinical evaluation approaches, we offer a reproducible method to characterize FOV and spatial distortion, which can assist device selection for research and diagnostic applications and guide the future development of widefield imaging technologies.
