Enhanced antioxidant and anti-Mycoplasma hyopneumoniae activities of combined Piper sarmentosum and guava leaf extracts
Authors: Luli ZHOU, Jirui WANG, Maojun LIU, Guanyu HOU, Guang RONG, Renlong LV, Dingfa WANG
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Increasing antibiotic resistance in Mycoplasma hyopneumoniae (Mhp) necessitates alternative treatments. Piper sarmentosum extracts (PSE) and guava extracts (GE) show promise due to their antimicrobial and antioxidant properties. This study evaluates the combined in vitro effects of PSE and GE against Mhp and their antioxidant activities. Anti-Mhp activity was assessed using microdilution minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and synergy via fractional inhibitory concentration index (FICI), while antioxidant capacity was measured with 2,2’-Azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid (ABTS) and ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) assays for individual extracts, and a 1:1 mixture (based on the results of FICI). Phytochemical profiling used high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) to quantify main six bioactive compounds. HPLC showed PSE contained vitexin-2-O-rhamnoside (8.58 mg/g) and pellitorine (3.31 mg/g); GE had quercetin-3-O-glucuronide (6.85 mg/g), quercetin (3.72 mg/g), isoquercitrin (0.51 mg/g), and avicularin (1.37 mg/g). PSE (MIC 0.78 mg/mL) and GE (MIC 0.39 mg/mL) exhibited anti-Mhp effects, with their 1:1 combination being additive (FICI=0.75) and reducing MIC to 0.195 mg/mL. This mix also enhanced antioxidant activity by 9.0% in ABTS and 27.0% in FRAP assays. The 1:1 combination of PSE and GE demonstrated superior anti-Mhp activity (additive effect) and enhanced antioxidant properties (synergistic effect) compared to the individual extracts. These findings highlight the potential of these extracts as phytopharmaceutical agents.

INTRODUCTION

Mycoplasma hyopneumoniae (Mhp) is the pathogen behind enzootic pneumonia in pigs, leading to negative effects on animal health and welfare and causing significant economic damage. This pathogen binds to ciliated epithelial cells in the trachea, bronchi, and bronchioles through adhesins (e.g., P97 and P102), compromising mucociliary clearance mechanisms, which subsequently induces cilial stasis/shedding, epithelial cell death, and hyperactive mucin secretion, ultimately culminating in structural damage to the ciliated epithelium and the development of inflammatory pulmonary lesions [26]. Antibiotics have been extensively employed for decades in managing Mhp infections, effectively improving clinical outcomes (including symptom alleviation and pulmonary lesion resolution) and mitigating performance losses associated with porcine enzootic pneumonia. Common antimicrobials for treating Mhp in pigs are tetracyclines, macrolides, lincosamides, polypeptides, amphenicols, aminoglycosides, aminocyclitols, and fluoroquinolones [20]. According to reports, some evolving or mutant strains of Mhp have developed varying degrees of resistance to macrolides, lincosamides, fluoroquinolones, and tetracyclines [9, 25]. Therefore, the continuous development of novel alternative therapeutic agents for Mhp is essential and urgent.

The utilization of plants and plant-derived extracts for medicinal purposes, including the treatment of illnesses such as pneumonia, possesses a longstanding historical precedent and continues to be extensively practiced in developing regions. This enduring practice is attributed to the rapid efficacy, favorable safety profile, and minimal risk of inducing drug resistance associated with these natural remedies [19, 23]. Guava (Psidium guajava L.) and Piper sarmentosum, members of the Myrtaceae and Piperaceae families respectively, are notable for their dual-purpose applications as both edible and medicinal plants within tropical and subtropical regions [6, 15]. Some studies have demonstrated that extracts from guava (GE) and Piper sarmentosum (PSE) exhibit substantial broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against a variety of pathogens [18, 29]. Quantitative analyzes have identified vitexin 2-O-rhamnoside and pellitorine as the most prevalent compounds among the six characterized in PSE. In contrast, quercetin, isoquercitrin, avicularin, and quercetin-3-O-glucuronide are the predominant constituents identified in GE [32]. Furthermore, empirical evidence indicates that the synergistic integration of diverse natural plant extracts can significantly enhance pharmacological efficacy compared to the use of individual extracts, particularly in augmenting antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties [2, 24]. Our prior investigations have shown that dietary supplementation with an equimolar combination of PSE and GE markedly improved antioxidant capacity and immunomodulatory responses in weaned piglets relative to the control group [32]. These findings advocate for the further development of PSE-GE combinatorial formulations for application in functional therapeutics.

Studies have shown that Mycoplasma infection induces both inflammatory responses and oxidative stress in hosts [3, 16, 31]. The current study aims to investigate the presence of synergistic interactions between PSE and GE using in vitro anti-Mhp and antioxidant assays. This approach seeks to provide empirical evidence to support the development of combinatorial veterinary phytopharmaceuticals targeting Mhp-induced porcine pneumonia.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Preparation of plant extract

A standardized protocol was implemented for PSE and GE production to ensure batch-to-batch consistency, as detailed below: fresh stem leaves of P. sarmentosum were exclusively harvested from the Tropical Botanical Garden (Danzhou, Hainan, China; 19◦30′ 58′′N and 109◦30′30′′E) during June to August (peak growth season). All materials were subsequently dried at 60°C for 24 hr and then ground to 40–60 mesh particle size. In a similar manner, fresh guava leaves were collected from Qionghai City, Hainan, China (May to July), and subjected to the same drying and grinding process. Extracts from both plant species were prepared using the heating reflux extraction method. A total of 100 g of the dried sample were mixed with 1,000 mL of 60% ethanol (v/v) and subsequently heated to a temperature of 80°C for a duration of 4 hr. Following this, the extract solution was filtered, and the filtrates were concentrated under reduced pressure using a rotary vacuum evaporator, resulting in the production of PSE and GE. The extraction yields, calculated as the total dry weight of each extract divided by the initial 100 g of dry sample and multiplied by 100%, were determined to be 16.5% for PSE and 30.8% for GE, respectively.

Preparation of sample solutions and standard solutions

Vitexin 2‐O‐rhamnoside and pellitorine were dissolved in 60% ethanol, mixed in 10 mL of the same ethanol and diluted for a standard curve. Quercetin, isoquercitrin, quercetin‐3‐O‐glucuronide, and avicularin were dissolved in 95% ethanol, mixed in 10 mL of the same ethanol, and diluted for a standard curve. All standards were from Chengdu Must Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Sichuan, China. The mixed standards were filtered through a 0.45 µm filter for measurement.

For quantitative analysis, the sample solutions were prepared as follows: approximately 5.0 g of dried P. sarmentosum stems and leaves were reflux-extracted with 100 mL of 70% ethanol for 2 hr. After cooling, solvent was added to compensate for weight loss, and the solution was filtered through a 0.45 µm membrane for HPLC analysis. Similarly, 4.0 g of oven-dried guava leaves were reflux-extracted with 100 mL of 60% ethanol for 1 hr, cooled, replenished with solvent, and filtered for HPLC quantification. In addition, a mixed standard solution containing 0.544 mg/mL vitexin 2-O-rhamnoside and 0.487 mg/mL pellitorine was diluted with 60% ethanol to create serial working solutions. Another mixed standard solution with 0.112 mg/mL quercetin, 0.119 mg/mL isoquercitrin, 0.126 mg/mL quercetin-3-O-glucuronide, and 0.165 mg/mL avicularin was diluted with 95% ethanol for serial working solutions.

Chromatographic conditions for quantitative analysis

A Waters e2695 series high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system (Waters, Milford, MA, USA) was employed. Chromatographic separation was achieved using an Ultimate AQ-C18 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 µm). The mobile phase comprised acetonitrile (A) and 0.05% phosphoric acid in water (B), with a flow rate maintained at 1.0 mL/min and the column temperature set at 30°C. Detection was conducted at a wavelength of 254 nm, employing an injection volume of 10 µL. For PSE samples, the linear gradient was programmed as follows: 0–15 min, 17–22% A; 15–20 min, 22–50% A; 20–48 min, 50% A; 48–55 min, 50–90% A; 55–57 min, 90–17% A; and 57–65 min, 17% A. For GE samples, the gradient was: 0–35 min, 16% A; 35–45 min, 16–26% A; 45–60 min, 26% A; 60–70 min, 26–60% A; 70–72 min, 60–16% A; and 72–80 min, 16% A. Quantitative determination was executed using the external standard method, with calibration curves constructed by plotting peak areas. The primary compounds in PSE and GE were quantified using calibration curves of standard compounds, all of which exhibited correlation coefficients (r) exceeding 0.990.

Cultivation of Mhp

The Mhp (strain J) was procured from the Veterinary Research Institute of the Jiangsu Academy of Agricultural Science. Serial ten-fold dilutions of the inoculum were prepared and cultured in KM2 medium (pH 7.4) at 37°C. The cultivation process was monitored by observing the color change of the medium from pink to orange-yellow, at which point further ten-fold dilutions were conducted. The concentration of Mhp was quantified using the color-change unit (CCU) method [21].

Determination of anti-Mhp activity

Two extracts, PSE and GE, were formulated as stock solutions at a concentration of 200 mg/mL utilizing 60% ethanol, and subsequently sterilized by filtration through a 0.22 µm filter. The Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of these plant extracts against Mhp was assessed in a sterilized 96-well microplate, employing the two-fold dilution method under Mycoplasma-optimized conditions as previously established methodologies [12]. Each microwell received 100 μL of culture medium (KM2 medium, pH 7.4). Then, 100 μL of a 200 mg/mL extract was added to the first well, mixed, and 100 μL was sequentially transferred to the next well up to the 12th. From the 12th well, 100 μL was discarded. Next, 100 μL of a 103 CCU/mL Mhp solution was added to each well. The negative control received 200 μL of culture medium, while the positive control received 100 μL of culture medium and 100 μL of the Mhp solution. The microplate was sealed and incubated at 37°C for 10–15 days. The color change in the positive control from pink to orange-yellow indicated the reference point for results. The MIC value was determined as the lowest concentration of extract with no visible microbial growth, indicated by a pink medium. All experiments were conducted in triplicate independent trials.

The synergistic effect of two plant extracts against Mhp was evaluated using the checkerboard assay to determine the fractional inhibitory concentration index (FICI). A 96-well microtiter plate was employed for this purpose. In brief, 100 μL of a 103 CCU/mL Mhp solution was dispensed into each well of the plate. Subsequently, 50 μL of a serially diluted PSE solution, ranging from 4 times the MIC to 1/16 MIC, was added along the x-axis, while a similarly diluted GE solution was added along the y-axis. The FICI for each plant extract was calculated by dividing the MIC of the extract in combination by the MIC of the extract alone. The FICI was computed using the following formula: FICI=FICA (MICA in the presence of B/MICA alone) + FICB (MICB in the presence of A/MICB alone). The interpretation of the results was based on the following criteria: FICI ≤0.5 indicates a synergistic effect; 0.5 <FICI ≤1 indicates an additive effect; 1 <FICI ≤4 indicates an indifferent effect; and FICI >4 indicates an antagonistic effect [22]. The concentration ratio of the two extracts in the well corresponding to the FICI represents the optimal compatibility ratio of the two extracts.

Antioxidant activities evaluations

The in vitro total antioxidant capacity of two individual extracts, and their 1:1 mixture (the ratio is chosen based on the combined effects of the two extracts against Mhp) was assessed utilizing the 2,2’-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid (ABTS) and ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) methods, as described by Alimpic et al. [1]. The assays were conducted with kits procured from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). Butylated hydroxyanisole (BHA), butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT), and ascorbic acid, each dissolved at a concentration of 0.1 mg/mL, served as standard controls. All experiments were conducted in triplicate independent trials.

Interaction and statistical analysis

The pooled data are expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons among multiple samples were performed using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with SPSS version 23.0 (IBM-SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Tukey’s post hoc test was employed for multiple comparisons. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance. The interactions between PSE and GE, as determined by the FRAP and ABTS assays, were quantified as the difference in antioxidant activity, calculated using the following equation: Difference (%)=[(Combination ab × 100)/ (Individual a +Individual b)]-100. Here, Combination ab represents the experimentally obtained result for the 1:1 mixture of PSE and GE, while Individual a and Individual b are the values measured individually for each extract [27]. The resulting difference values (%) suggest potential synergistic effects (indicated by positive values), antagonistic effects (indicated by negative values), or additive effects (indicated by null values).

This selection allows for a comprehensive evaluation of the in vitro antioxidant capacity of GE, PSE, and their 1:1 mixture under investigation. DISCUSSION

The widespread emergence of antibiotic-resistant bacteria represents a substantial global public health challenge [17]. In response to this crisis, there is a growing focus on the investigation of medicinal plants as potential sources of novel antimicrobial agents [28]. Of particular interest are polyherbal extracts, which are synergistic formulations that combine two or more herbs. These extracts are promising due to their diverse phytochemical composition, including compounds such as polyphenols and amides, which collectively enhance antimicrobial and antioxidant activities [5].

Our prior in vivo research has demonstrated that a composite extract comprising PSE and GE in a 1:1 ratio can enhance antioxidant capacity and immune function in piglets compared to the control group. This finding suggests that the combination holds considerable promise for advancing piglet health management and veterinary clinical applications [32]. In the present in vitro study, it was established that both PSE (with a MIC of 0.79 mg/mL) and GE (with a MIC of 0.38 mg/mL) exhibited significant anti-Mhp activity. Notably, when PSE and GE were combined in a 1:1 ratio, their anti-Mhp activity was augmented, as indicated by an additive interaction with a FICI of 0.75. Under these combined conditions, the concentrations of PSE and GE required to achieve a bacteriostatic effect were reduced to 0.195 mg/mL.

Research indicates that Mhp infection could induce oxidative stress in porcine respiratory epithelium, and increase production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) that damage host cells and consequently resulting in inflammation [3, 13]. The present study demonstrated that the synergistic interaction between PSE and GE results in significant dual action (direct antimicrobial and indirect antioxidant). Research has demonstrated that the effectiveness of flavonoid compounds is largely dependent on the presence of phenolic hydroxyl groups. In contrast, amide compounds exhibit a wide range of hydrogen bond-forming capabilities due to their dipolar structure, characterized by a strong carbonyl dipole (C=O) and a weaker N-C dipole. These structural attributes account for the significant free radical scavenging properties of flavonoids and the ability of alkylamides (such as pellitorine) to disrupt microbial membrane structures, thereby enhancing their antioxidant, antibacterial, and anti-inflammatory activities [4, 8, 10, 30]. This study used HPLC analysis to identify key bioactive components in PSE and GE. PSE contains the flavonoid vitexin-2-O-rhamnoside and the alkylamide pellitorine, while GE includes the flavonoids quercetin, isoquercitrin, avicularin, and quercetin-3-O-glucuronide. Combining PSE and GE creates a blend rich in flavonoids and pellitorine, potentially leading to synergistic interactions that enhance antimicrobial and antioxidant efficacy. This synergy likely results from interactions between phenolic hydroxyl groups and amide dipolar moieties, improving pharmacological activities such as free radical scavenging and disruption of pathogen membranes.

PSE-GE phytochemicals may help prevent resistance in M. hyopneumoniae by targeting multiple pathways. Flavonoids in GE disrupt microbial membranes and inhibit efflux pumps, while alkylamides in PSE cause oxidative damage and affect energy metabolism [8, 11]. This reduces the chance of resistance through single-point mutations, unlike conventional antibiotics that target specific pathways [7, 14]. However, prolonged use of any antimicrobial can still lead to resistance [8]. To address this, we suggest alternating PSE-GE with conventional antibiotics like macrolides or tetracyclines during treatment cycles, combining the strengths of both approaches to manage antimicrobial resistance effectively [20, 25]. The present findings suggest that the synergistic effects of PSE and GE may mitigate oxidative damage induced by Mhp infection.

In conclusion, both PSE and GE demonstrated significant inhibitory effects on Mhp. When combined in a 1:1 ratio, these agents effectively enhanced anti-Mhp activity, exhibiting an additive effect, and improved antioxidant activity, indicating a synergistic effect. However, further research and validation are necessary through future animal studies.
