Management of Idiopathic Acute Pancreatitis in Europe: An International Pancreas2000 Survey
Authors: Afrodita Panaitescu-Damian, Barbara Wlodarczyk, Vytautas Kiudelis, Amer Hadi, Lucía Guilabert, Anu Aronen, Johanna Laukkarinen, Sara Regner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIdentifying the underlying cause of acute pancreatitis is fundamental for prevention of recurrence. However, no standardised algorithm for determining its aetiology exists.AimsThis survey study aimed to investigate the international variations in diagnosis, workup protocols and follow-up of acute pancreatitis.MethodsA 37-item international questionnaire was developed by a multidisciplinary team within the Pancreas 2000 program. Introduction

Acute pancreatitis (AP) is a common gastrointestinal disorder leading to hospital admissions [1]. Disease course varies from mild abdominal pain to life-threatening multi-organ failure. To be able to prevent relapse, aetiology and the possible treatment needs to be determined. Idiopathic acute pancreatitis (IAP) is defined as an AP without an identifiable aetiology. The initial evaluation should include laboratory and imaging tests [2], but comprehensive aetiologic search may reduce the rate of IAP and may thus affect the recurrence [3].

To date, no unified or validated algorithm to determine the AP aetiology exists. The use of different guidelines and workup protocols may vary between countries, centres and health care professionals. The availability of different imaging modalities and laboratory tests may also have an impact on the workup protocol. A recently published review of literature proposed an algorithm for the diagnostic workup of IAP [4].

This clinical survey aimed to evaluate the diagnostic tools and workup protocols employed in investigating the aetiology and follow-up of AP with undetermined cause. To our knowledge, clinical surveys about determining the aetiology of AP have not been previously published.

Materials and Methods

An international online survey was developed to assess the current practice of aetiological diagnostics in patients with AP. The survey was designed by a group of eight pancreatologists (AP, BW, VK, AH, AA, LG, JL and SR) within the Pancreas 2000 program [5].

This survey was aimed at physicians from different specialities who routinely manage patients with acute pancreatitis. Google Forms was used as a hosting platform. The survey consisted of 37 questions divided in 4 main sections (see supplementary material, the survey): 7 questions related to demographics and clinical experience, 7 centre-specific questions considered in-house classification and management of AP and test availability, 16 questions focused on aetiology management and IAP definition and lastly, 7 questions regarding follow-up and treatment of IAP. The standard workup in search of aetiology was defined as medical history (including clinical assessment) standard blood tests (including triglycerides and calcium) and abdominal ultrasound (US).

The survey was anonymous and respondents consented to analyses and publishing of the data obtained. The survey was launched in June 2023 during the European Pancreatic Club (EPC) annual meeting and was open until October 2023. It was distributed through the EPC newsletter, EPC booth at the United European Gastroenterology Week 2023, the national societies (Spanish Gastroenterology Association, Danish Gastroenterology Society, Finnish Society of Gastrointestinal Surgeons and Polish Gastroenterology Society) and via e-mails sent to different gastroenterology and surgical departments. Simultaneously, the survey was also distributed across social media with periodic reminders.

Data were analysed using R (R Core Team, version 4.1.2). Data were presented as frequencies with percentages for categorical data. Comparisons amongst different groups were performed using Chi square test with Fisher’s correction. A two-sided alpha < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement.

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Demographics and Characteristics of Survey Respondents

The survey had 247 respondents from 36 countries. Most of the respondents worked in European countries (96%, N = 236). Spain (16%, N = 40), Finland (13%, N = 31) and Denmark (11%, N = 28) represented the largest groups. All demographics and characteristics of the respondents and centres are described in Table 1.

Demographics, clinical experience and centre characteristics of survey respondents

Most respondents were treating patients with AP (91%, N = 225). Gastroenterologists (56%, N = 138) and surgeons (34%, N = 84) were the main specialities of respondents, and 7% (N = 17) were endoscopists. Duration of training was more than 10 years for 39% (N = 95) and less than 5 years for 35% (N = 86).

The respondents’ centres were mainly academic, either public (82%, N = 203) or private (4%, N = 10). In total, only 13% of respondents (N = 33) worked in non-academic public hospitals. Most centres were tertiary referral centres (73%, N = 181). The number of AP patients per centre was stratified into low-volume (less than 150 patients per year), medium-volume (between 150 and 300 patients per year) and high-volume (more than 300 patients per year). Thirty-seven per cent (N = 92) of the respondents’ centres treated 150–300 AP patients per year and 17% (N = 43) treated a higher number. Tertiary centres had a significantly higher number of AP patients (p-value = 0.026) when compared to non-tertiary centres.

Guidelines and Classification for AP Aetiology

Most of the respondents (83%, N = 205) classified AP severity according to the revised Atlanta Classification [6]. When treating patients with AP, 96% (N = 238) used national guidelines, 80% (N = 199) international guidelines and 28% (N = 71) internal protocols.

Of all the respondents, 64% (N = 158) reported to have national guidelines addressing the methodology to assess the aetiology of AP. Sixteen per cent (N = 39) did not have a national-agreed assessment, and of those, one fourth (N = 10) only had an internal protocol for determining the aetiology as idiopathic. Twelve per cent (N = 29) had no internal or national protocol for the aetiology. Twenty per cent (N = 50) were uncertain about the national policy.

Clinical Management of AP Aetiological Diagnosis

Abdominal US would be performed in every case of AP at index admission by the majority of respondents (80%, N = 197) in addition to medical history, and standard laboratory tests. Additionally, 69% (N = 171) would repeat abdominal US at the recurrence of AP. Registration of alcohol usage was by medical history according to most of the respondents (96%, N = 237), whereas a standard alcohol questionnaire was used by 23% (N = 56).

Additional aetiological investigations were performed during index admission by most respondents (63%, N = 156) and 31% (N = 76) would perform the tests within 1–2 months of admission. Of all investigations, endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) and genetic testing were more available for respondents from tertiary referral centres, see Tables 2 and 3. After initial standard workup, 63% (N = 158) of respondents performed magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP), 58% (N = 144) EUS, 55% (N = 136) computed tomography (CT) and 36% (N = 91) order genetic testing before classifying AP as idiopathic. When asked which test to perform first, 33% answered CT (N = 82), 29% MRCP (N = 71) and 23% EUS (N = 56), with no difference between non-tertiary and tertiary referral centres (p = 0.19). There was no difference between years of training and the minimum set of investigations used for the diagnosis of IAP (p = 0.56).

The Role of EUS Versus MRCP in Patients Without Definite Aetiology After Standard Workup

Eighty-six per cent of the respondents (N = 213) had EUS available in their respective centres; a higher rate of EUS availability in respondents from tertiary referral centres (p < 0.001) was found – Table 3. Of all respondents with EUS available in their tertiary centres, 14% (N = 26) would recommend EUS during the first admission and 39% (N = 71) would recommend EUS only when biliary aetiology was suspected.

EUS and MRCP were chosen equally (34%, N = 85) as the ideal initial diagnostic test in patients with AP without a suspected aetiology after standard workup. CT was seen as ideal by 21% (N = 52). However, 33% (N = 82) chose CT as their primary diagnostic test in actual practice, followed by MRCP at 29% (N = 71) and EUS at 23% (N = 56). Out of 206 respondents with both MRCP and EUS available, 30% (N = 61) preferred MRCP and 26% (N = 55) EUS while other tests were chosen by 44% (N = 90).

Compared to surgeons, gastroenterologists had a significantly higher use of EUS and favoured EUS as an ideal initial diagnostic test after standard workup for aetiology, 29% vs. 5% (p < 0.001) and 41% vs. 17% (p < 0.001), respectively – Table 4.

Role of Cholecystectomy for IAP

In total, 79% (N = 194) of the respondents would not perform cholecystectomy if there was no evidence of biliary aetiology, while 21% (N = 53) would refer for cholecystectomy. Recurrent AP and a history of elevated liver enzymes were the main indication (83%, N = 44) for cholecystectomy in IAP. Among respondents opting for cholecystectomy, a higher rate was observed among surgeons (40%, N = 31) compared to gastroenterologists (12%, N = 16), p < 0.0001 (Table 4). Statistical analysis revealed no difference (p = 0.57) in the decision to refer the patient for cholecystectomy across low-volume, medium-volume, and high-volume centres. Similarly, no difference was observed between centres equipped with EUS and those without (p = 1).

Follow-up Strategy for Patients Classified as IAP After the First Episode

In most patients, follow-up after the first AP episode was done by gastroenterologists (62%, N = 153) compared to surgeons (18%, N = 44), p = 0.04. Fifty-three per cent (N = 130) of respondents' centres conducted post-discharge follow-up for all patients with IAP, while 20% (N = 49) limited follow-up to moderate and severe cases of IAP. There was no significant difference between the various centres’ volumes and the follow-up (p = 0.24). In clinical practice, most of the respondents (65%, N = 160) scheduled follow-up visits for IAP cases within less than 12 weeks following the index admission.

The majority of respondents (89%, N = 220) recommended follow-up for moderate and severe cases of AP lacking an identified aetiology after initial admission. Furthermore, 60% (N = 148) recommended follow-up for mild cases as well. Gastroenterologists had a higher rate of recommending follow-up for IAP compared to surgeons, 77% (N = 106) vs. 64% (N = 54) (p = 0.04) (Table 4).

Thirty-three per cent of respondents (N = 82) supported follow-up visits every 6 months, whereas 17% (N = 42) indicated no recommendation for follow-up.

Discussion

IAP is a disease with unresolved management challenges and no validated protocol for diagnosis. The survey described in this report addressed the topic of IAP in the daily medical practice across European countries. It highlighted the lack of a structured approach and provided new insights by incorporating clinicians’ perspectives, including respondents with various clinical expertise, from tertiary as well as non-tertiary hospitals.

Determining the cause of AP is crucial to prevent recurrences, especially since IAP has a higher recurrence rate compared to AP with a known aetiology [7]. Reflecting this, 100% of respondents agreed that identifying the cause of AP is essential. However, a validated protocol for establishing the cause AP still has not been consensualized [4]. This absence of a standardised approach likely leads to variations in AP aetiology investigations across countries, and centres, as indicated by results presented in this paper. The survey assessed a structured, gradual diagnostic approach, to differentiate cases without definite aetiology after the initial evaluation from those ultimately defined as idiopathic following comprehensive assessment with advanced diagnostic tests.

Only 64% of respondents stated that their national guidelines address the approach to determine AP aetiology and more than one in ten lacked both national guidelines and an internal protocol for the aetiological diagnostics. Most respondents followed international guidelines that mainly focus AP treatment with less emphasis on aetiology [8, 9].

Abdominal US is a fundamental test for determining AP aetiology and is typically recommended as the first imaging modality in AP patients due to its low cost, safety and availability. Despite this, only 80% of respondents performed an abdominal US in every patient with AP, with only 69% using it in patients with recurrent AP. This underutilization may be due to the widespread availability of CT scans, which are often favoured for AP diagnostics, sometimes at the expense of abdominal US [2, 10, 11]. The Revised Guidelines for Acute Pancreatitis [12] recommend repeating abdominal ultrasound after discharge, if the aetiology is unknown.

Alcohol is another important etiological factor, contributing to one-fifth of global AP cases [13]. However, our survey showed that alcohol use is not routinely assessed using standardised approaches, with only 23% of respondents employing standard questionnaires for this purpose.

Identifying AP aetiology after initial laboratory tests and abdominal US can be challenging, with initial workup accuracy often below 30% [14]. In such patients, EUS and MRCP would be the diagnostic modalities of choice [15] and 34% of respondents from our survey considered both EUS and MRCP as first-line diagnostic tests. Despite this, most of the survey respondents used CT as the first investigation technique for diagnosing AP aetiology after standard workup. This choice is not solely based on test availability as most of the respondents reported having access to all the three examinations (CT, EUS and MRCP). Longer waiting times or higher cost of EUS and MRCP might also influence the diagnostic approach in clinical practice. Growing evidence indicates that EUS is more accurate in identifying the aetiology of AP [7, 16, 17].

The Revised Guidelines for Acute Pancreatitis [12] state that EUS should be used after repeated abdominal ultrasound in search of aetiology, and followed by MRI/MRCP, if the aetiology is still unknown. According to IAP/APA [11], EUS is more accurate in detecting small stones (< 5 mm) compared to MRCP and has been proven useful in discovering the aetiology in patients classified as IAP after initial workup. However, EUS also has certain limitations: it is recognized as an investigator-dependent procedure, with its diagnostic accuracy closely tied to the expertise of the operator [18]. MRCP is a more accessible and non-invasive procedure [11, 19] and may offer a more reliable alternative in settings where expert EUS practitioners are not available. While most studies evaluating EUS performance are conducted in high-expertise centres, the findings of this survey provide a pragmatic perspective, reflecting various clinical settings and their associated limitations.

In our survey, 86% of the respondents had access to EUS in their centres and 14% of physicians would perform EUS during the first episode of AP, while 39% recommended EUS if biliary aetiology of AP is suspected. A significant discrepancy was observed in this study between surgeons and gastroenterologists in the choice of EUS for initial diagnosis.

Cholecystectomy is the preferred treatment for patients with biliary AP to avoid recurrence [11]. However, some centres perform cholecystectomy in patients after single or recurrent episodes of IAP due to the possibility of undetected biliary aetiology. A multicentre study by Raty et al. [20] confirmed that performing cholecystectomy after IAP could prevent recurrences. However, patients in that study did not get an EUS before cholecystectomy to assess biliary aetiology. Therefore, the real benefit might be related to overlooked biliary stones. Similar results were obtained by Umans et al. [21], where among 524 patients, a reduced recurrence rate of IAP was observed from 35.2% to 11.1% in the cholecystectomy group. In the present survey, 21% of the respondents would perform cholecystectomy in IAP. In these cases, 83% of the respondents would perform it if AP was recurrent or there were elevated liver enzymes at some point during assessment. There is an association between elevated ALT levels and biliary AP, so elevated ALT levels in patients initially classified as having IAP strongly suggest an undetected biliary aetiology [22]. In our survey, recommendation for cholecystectomy in IAP was more common among surgeons (40%), compared to gastroenterologists—12% (p < 0.001). We did not find significant differences in performing cholecystectomy across low-volume, medium-volume and high-volume centres. Similarly, no statistical difference was found between centres with and without EUS.

In total, 89% of respondents in the survey would recommend follow-up for patients with moderate and severe IAP after their first admission and 60% would recommend follow-up for mild cases.

Nevertheless, in the study by Hallensleben et al., identifying and treating the aetiology after an AP episode without a known cause reduced recurrence rate [23].

In our survey, follow-up was mostly carried out by gastroenterologists (62%) and surgeons (18%). In total, 33% of respondents advocated for follow-up visits every 6 months, while 17% did not provide specific recommendations regarding follow-up visits. In clinical practice, the majority of respondents (65%) planned follow-up visits for IAP within less than 12 weeks from the first hospitalisation.

This study has some limitations. Respondents are mainly from European countries, leading to a possible selection bias, due to the geographical limitation and lack of homogeneity amongst answers per country. The survey was endorsed by the European Pancreatic Club and distributed via e-mail, social media and through national organisations, so, the respondents that engaged in answering the survey via those channels may have different views as opposed to potential participants without access to the survey. Moreover, the survey targeted physicians with an interest in AP, possibly accessing mainly physicians with experience in AP. This might influence answers concerning knowledge about and adherence to guidelines. While the sample size of 247 may still provide valuable insight, the findings cannot be considered generalised to all physicians treating patients with AP. Finally, all the results are to be interpreted in the context that the majority of responders came from tertiary referral centres and academic public centres.

The main strengths of our study are the fact that the respondents were from a wide array of countries, 91.1% of them were treating AP patients and 35% had over 10 years of experience. Moreover, most of the physicians were working mainly in academic/university public hospitals and tertiary referral centres, therefore, considered experienced in treating AP cases. Both gastroenterology and gastrointestinal surgery specialisations were well represented in this survey. Consequently, the results may reflect the current expert opinion regarding IAP. Considering the expertise of respondents, the obvious differences in diagnosis, treatment and follow up of IAP are even more worthy of attention.

To conclude, this survey highlights the significant variability in the approach to determining the aetiology of AP across different regions and centres, particularly in Europe. Despite consensus on the importance of identifying the cause of AP to prevent recurrences, a validated protocol for doing so is still lacking. This inconsistency can lead to differences in clinical practice and potentially impact patient outcomes.
