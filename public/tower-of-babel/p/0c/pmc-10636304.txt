A computational architecture of punishment insensitivity
Authors: Huw Jarvis, Trevor T.-J. Chong
Published: 2023

Jean-Richard-dit-Bressel et al. (1) recently distinguished three phenotypes of punishment learning: sensitive individuals (who readily avoid punishment), unaware individuals (who only avoid punishment when explicitly informed of its likelihood), and compulsive individuals (who fail to avoid punishment altogether). The authors argue that these phenotypes are evidence of a novel "cognitive" pathway to punishment insensitivity.

Here, we demonstrate that these compelling results also emerge as computational predictions of a well-established model of reinforcement learning: the opponent actor learning (OpAL) model (2–5). Extending previous work (6–9), this model proposes that reward and punishment shape choices through effects on "go" and "no-go" action weights, respectively. Consequently, this model can predict the empirical results of Jean-Richard-dit-Bressel et al. (1) by accounting for individual differences in the conversion of punishment values into inhibitory action weights.

We simulated 150 learning agents performing the tasks described by Jean-Richard-dit-Bressel et al. (1; Fig. 1A). We modeled learning as a process in which the estimated probability (P) of receiving reward or punishment was updated by a prediction error (δ) on the immediately preceding trial (t), scaled by a learning rate parameter (α).Pt+1=Pt+α×δt.

The key feature of our simulations was the inclusion of go and no-go signal gain parameters (λGo, λNoGo), which nonlinearly scaled the effect of reward and punishment probabilities (PRew, PPun) on go and no-go action weights (WGo, WNoGo; Fig. 1B).WGot=PRew(t)λGo, WNoGot=PPun(t)λNoGo.

The final action weight (A) for a given response was the difference between go and no-go weights, such that the action most likely to be implemented was associated with high reward (large WGo) and low punishment (small WNoGo).At=WGot-WNoGot.

On each trial, the choice between responses was determined by their relative action weights in a softmax function with an inverse temperature parameter (β).

We randomly sampled λGo and λNoGo from a gamma distribution (shape = 0.55, scale = 5; Fig. 1C) and fixed the other parameters at plausible values (α = 0.25, β = 2). A 10-fold increase in β was applied at the start of the final block to simulate the researchers revealing the punishment contingencies to participants.

These settings accurately reproduced behaviour across different punishment conditions (Fig. 1 D–F). Critically, the sensitive, unaware, and compulsive phenotypes could be reproduced by a tertile split of the no-go signal gain parameter (λNoGo) in each experiment (Fig. 1 G–I). In those with low λNoGo values, incremental increases in punishment contingency led to large increases in no-go weight (i.e., sensitive individuals, who rapidly avoided punishment). At the other extreme, in those with high λNoGo, large increases in punishment contingency led to incremental changes in no-go weight (i.e., compulsive individuals, who persistently risked punishment). In those with intermediate λNoGo values, punishment did not sufficiently update no-go weights until the final block (i.e., unaware individuals, who only avoided punishment when contingencies were revealed).

Together, our simulations demonstrate that these different phenotypes of punishment learning can be captured by a single parameter that scales the effect of punishment on inhibitory behaviour. We thus suggest that a critical computational (mis)step underlying punishment insensitivity may be a failure to strengthen the no-go pathway despite repeated punishment.
