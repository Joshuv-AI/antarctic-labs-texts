Context-dependent contribution of peptidyl arginine deiminase 4 (PAD4) to neutrophil extracellular trap formation and liver injury in acute and chronic hepatotoxicant challenge
Authors: Gina E Capece, Amish K Patel, Daniel Hu, Tayana Roychowdhury, Bianca Hazel, Jessica Kothapalli, Noah A Mac, Frederik Denorme, Robert A Campbell, Lauren G Poole
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeutrophils play a complex role in the pathogenesis of chronic liver disease and have been linked to both liver damage and injury resolution. Recent reports propose that neutrophils drive liver injury and fibrosis through the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs). This study tests the hypothesis that the enzyme peptidyl arginine deiminase-4 (PAD4) drives NET formation and liver fibrosis in experimental chronic liver injury. Wild-type (PAD4+/+) and PAD4-deficient (PAD4−/−) mice were chronically challenged twice weekly with carbon tetrachloride (CCl4, 1 ml/kg, i.p) or vehicle (corn oil) for 6 weeks, and samples were collected 24 h after the final challenge. In separate studies, mice were challenged once, and samples were collected 24 to 48 h later. Circulating NET biomarkers (e.g. myeloperoxidase–DNA complexes) were elevated in chronic CCl4-challenged wild-type mice compared to vehicle, though surprisingly, intrahepatic NETs were rarely observed. In contrast to our hypothesis, PAD4 deficiency did not eliminate circulating NET markers in chronic challenge. Furthermore, PAD4 deficiency did not impact liver fibrosis assessed by picrosirius red labeling or the myofibroblast marker α-smooth muscle actin but caused a modest, sex-specific decrease in hepatic collagen type I immunolabeling. Interestingly, plasma NET biomarkers and intrahepatic NETs were both increased following acute CCl4 challenge in a PAD4-dependent manner. Furthermore, PAD4 deficiency reduced coagulation activity after 24 h and decreased hepatocellular necrosis 48 h after challenge. Our studies ultimately suggest that PAD4 affects liver injury uniquely, depending on the stage of disease and that mechanisms of NET formation may occur independent of PAD4 in chronic liver injury.Keywords: liver, hepatotoxicity, hepatic, inflammation, pathology

Activation of the inflammatory response is a common feature of chronic liver disease. Hepatic parenchymal cells release damage-associated molecular patterns in response to cellular stress or cell death, which leads to activation of hepatic resident inflammatory cells (e.g. Kupffer cells) and the recruitment of circulating leukocytes such as monocytes and neutrophils (Woolbright and Jaeschke 2017). Neutrophils specifically are often considered “double-edged swords” in liver injury, where evidence indicates they may exacerbate injury but also contribute to injury resolution and repair. In multiple clinical settings of chronic liver injury, an elevated neutrophil–lymphocyte ratio is associated with worse disease outcomes (Alkhouri et al. 2012; Rice et al. 2018). In experimental settings, neutrophil depletion or inhibition has been shown to be protective in settings of non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (Zang et al. 2015) as well as experimental fibrosis induced by a high-fat–ethanol binge diet (Zhou et al. 2018). On the other hand, additional experimental evidence indicates that neutrophil depletion delays liver repair and injury resolution following challenge with hepatotoxicants such as carbon tetrachloride (CCl4) (Saijou et al. 2018; Calvente et al. 2019) or acetaminophen (Yang et al. 2019). Taken together, these studies suggest that neutrophils perform multiple functions in the injured liver that may contribute to disease pathogenesis or resolution. One critical function of neutrophils that has recently been suggested to be involved in liver disease progression is neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation.

NETs were first identified as a response to bacterial infection (Brinkmann et al. 2004), and recent research has demonstrated NETs may also form in response to many sterile stimuli, including interactions with toll-like receptor 4 or P-selectin on activated platelets (Clark et al. 2007; Caudrillier et al. 2012; Maugeri et al. 2014; Etulain et al. 2015), inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha, interleukin (IL)-8, and IL-1β (Keshari et al. 2012; Barliya et al. 2017; Meher et al. 2018), and reactive oxygen species production (Fuchs et al. 2007). The downstream signaling pathways ultimately driving NET formation are complex, but evidence suggests that this process is primarily dependent on activation of the enzyme peptidyl arginine deiminase 4 (PAD4) (Li et al. 2010; Lewis et al. 2015). PAD4 catalyzes the citrullination of histones (i.e. the conversion of an arginine residue to a citrulline residue), which weakens the histone–DNA interaction. Ultimately, the unwound DNA is expelled from the cell during the process of NET formation (NETosis). NETs are composed of extracellular DNA networks decorated with neutrophil proteins such as myeloperoxidase (MPO), neutrophil elastase (NE), and surface protein markers, as well as citrullinated histones such as citrullinated histone H3 (CitH3). Multiple methods have been developed to attempt to detect NET biomarkers in biological fluids (e.g. plasma or serum) or tissues, including immunoassays or immunolabeling approaches that detect specific NET components (e.g. CitH3, MPO, NE, extracellular DNA) or complexes of multiple NET components (e.g. MPO–DNA complexes) (Thålin et al. 2017; Li et al. 2018; Denorme et al. 2022).

Recent reports suggest that NETs may play a pathologic role in chronic liver disease. Circulating NET biomarkers, including CitH3, MPO–DNA complexes, and/or cell-free DNA, have been detected in patients with various stages of chronic liver disease, from steatohepatitis to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (van der Windt et al. 2018; Zenlander et al. 2021; Du et al. 2022). Hepatic NET biomarkers (e.g. CitH3 or co-localized MPO and CitH3) have also been detected in patients with liver disease (von Meijenfeldt et al. 2025; Xia et al. 2025). In a recent study, antibody-mediated neutrophil depletion or intraperitoneal administration of DNase reduced liver fibrosis and hepatic CitH3 levels in experimental ethanol-exacerbated metabolic-associated steatohepatitis (Babuta et al. 2024). Additionally, intraperitoneal DNase administration or whole-body PAD4 deficiency reduced liver fibrosis and hepatic CitH3 levels in experimental liver fibrosis induced by chronic Western diet feeding with CCl4 challenge (Xia et al. 2025). It has been proposed that NET components contribute to liver fibrosis by directly activating hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) (Babuta et al. 2024; Luo et al. 2025; Xia et al. 2025). Additionally, NETs have an established role in thrombosis (Fuchs et al. 2010; Savchenko et al. 2014; Xing et al. 2022), and patients with chronic liver disease often display a hypercoagulable phenotype, increasing the risk of complications such as portal vein thrombosis and venous thromboembolism (Ambrosino et al. 2017). However, the potential role of NETs in the activation of the coagulation cascade in the setting of chronic liver injury has not been explored.

Despite recent experimental reports suggesting that NETs may contribute to chronic liver injury and fibrosis, several questions remain unanswered. The mechanisms driving NET formation in experimental chronic liver injury are not completely understood due to challenges with available experimental approaches to inhibit and detect NETs. For example, neutrophil depletion disrupts all neutrophil functions, and DNase disrupts all extracellular DNA, which may accumulate in the injured liver as a result of hepatocellular necrosis, as well as NETs. Moreover, the impact of DNase administration or PAD4 deficiency on NET formation is unclear. NET inhibition is often confirmed by measuring CitH3; however, PAD4 may also drive histone citrullination in apoptotic cells (Tanikawa et al. 2012). To address these knowledge gaps, we aimed to define the role of PAD4 in NET formation in a well-characterized setting of toxicant-induced liver injury. Additionally, we sought to define the impact of PAD4 deficiency on experimental liver injury, fibrosis, and activation of the coagulation cascade. Building on prior studies, we hypothesized that PAD4 drives NET formation, liver injury, HSC activation, and fibrosis in experimental liver injury.

Materials and methods

Mice

Male and female C57BL/6J wild-type mice (#000664, henceforth described as PAD4+/+) and PAD4-deficient (B6.Cg-Padi4tm1.1Kmow/J, #030315, henceforth described as PAD4−/−) (Hemmers et al. 2011) mice were initially purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME), then were bred and maintained by crossing PAD4+/+ males with PAD+/+ females and PAD4−/− males with PAD4−/− females at Rutgers University. All studies presented in this manuscript comparing wild-type and PAD4−/− mice used age-matched cohorts of wild-type and PAD4−/− mice bred at Rutgers for 1 to 3 generations, with the exception of the female mice presented in Figs 2 and 3, in which the wild-type and PAD4−/− mice were both purchased from The Jackson Laboratory at the same time at 8 weeks of age, and then the study was initiated after a 7-day acclimation period. For all other studies, mice were used for studies in age-matched cohorts between the ages of 8 and 12 weeks. All mice were fed a standard chow diet and drinking water ad libitum and housed under a 12-h light/dark cycle. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Rutgers University.

Acute and CCl4 challenge

CCl4 (Millipore Sigma, Burlington, MA, #319961) was diluted to 10% vol/vol in corn oil (Millipore Sigma #C8267), then was administered intraperitoneally at a final concentration of 1 ml CCl4/kg bw (10 µl 10% CCl4 per g bw). Vehicle (corn oil) was administered intraperitoneally at 10 µl per g bw. Acute liver injury was induced by a single CCl4 (or vehicle) injection, and samples were collected at 2 to 96 h after injection, where indicated. Chronic liver injury and hepatic fibrosis were induced by CCl4 (or vehicle) injection twice per week (e.g. Monday and Thursday) for 6 weeks. Samples were collected 24 h after the final injection. At the conclusion of the studies, mice were placed under deep surgical isoflurane anesthesia. Whole blood was drawn from the inferior vena cava into a syringe containing sodium citrate (3.8%, Ricca Chemical Company, Arlington, VA) so that the final concentration of sodium citrate in blood was 0.38% w/v. The inferior vena cava was severed, and whole blood was collected without an anticoagulant into a separate tube. Livers were excised and rinsed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The entire left lateral lobe was fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 96 h prior to processing and paraffin embedding. Remaining liver tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and then stored at −80 °C for further analysis. Citrated whole blood was centrifuged at 4000 × g for 10 min for the collection of plasma. Non-anticoagulated whole blood was incubated at room temperature for 30 min and then centrifuged twice at 10,000 × g for 2 min for the collection of serum.

Measurement of hepatocellular necrosis and liver fibrosis

Serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) activity was measured using commercial reagents (Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, #23-666-087) according to the manufacturer’s instructions adapted for a microplate reader (Spark multimode plate reader, Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland). If necessary, serum samples were diluted in PBS. The area of hepatocellular necrosis in the entire left lateral lobe was assessed in hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) liver tissues, which were processed and stained by the Rutgers Research Pathology Services Core. The area of necrosis was measured as described previously (Pant et al. 2018) with minor modifications. Briefly, H&E-stained liver tissues were scanned with an Axioscan 7 Slide Scanner (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) with a 20× objective. Using QuPath image analysis software (v 0.4.3-0.5.1) (Bankhead et al. 2017), an evenly spaced grid (400 × 400 microns) was applied to the image, and the point counting tool was used to annotate each grid intersection on the tissue as “normal” or “necrotic.” The percent area of necrosis was calculated as [(# necrotic points)/(# normal points + # necrotic points)] × 100. To assess liver fibrosis, FFPE liver tissues were processed and stained for picrosirius red (PSR) using a commercially available kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Polysciences Inc., Warrington, PA #24901) by the Rutgers Research Pathology Services Core. In the PSR method, Sirius red binds specifically to collagen type I (Col I) and collagen type III (Col III) fibers, and PSR enhances the birefringence of collagen fibers, allowing for their detection and visualization under polarized light. PSR-stained slides were scanned with a Zeiss Axioscan 7 Slide Scanner with a 20× objective under polarized light. Analysis of the PSR-positive area was performed in QuPath using a defined pixel intensity threshold.

Immunolabeling of liver tissues

Validation for the specificity of antibodies was provided by the manufacturers. Immunofluorescent labeling for NETs was performed using snap-frozen liver tissue that was embedded in OCT and cryosectioned into 5 μm slices by the Rutgers Research Pathology Services Core. Prior to staining, slides were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA) and blocked in 10% normal goat serum (Equitech-Bio Inc., Kerrville, TX) in PBS with 0.5% Tween-20 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Tissues were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, rabbit anti-histone H3 (citrulline R8) (1 µg/ml; ab219406, Abcam, Waltham, MA) and rat anti-mouse Ly6G (2 µg/ml, BioXCell, Lebanon, NH, #BE0075-1), diluted in blocking buffer. Slides were washed in PBS, then incubated with Alexa Fluor (AF)-594-conjugated goat anti-rat IgG and AF-647-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (both at 2 µg/ml, Jackson Immunoresearch, West Grove, PA) diluted in blocking buffer. Slides were washed in PBS, then coverslipped with ProLong Gold antifade mountant with DAPI (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, #P36931). Sections were allowed to cure for at least 24 h, then imaged using a Zeiss Axioscan 7 Slide Scanner with a 20× objective. Ly6G-AF594 was pseudocolored in green, CitH3-AF647 in magenta, and DAPI in blue. Analysis was performed using QuPath. Briefly, the cell detection tool was used to detect DAPI+ nuclei within a defined region of interest. The object classifier tool was used to detect Ly6G+ cells (neutrophils) above a defined pixel intensity threshold within the previously defined cellular detections. Similarly, the object classifier tool was used to detect CitH3+ cells above a defined pixel intensity threshold within previously defined cellular detections. The percentage of neutrophils in the liver section was therefore defined as [(# Ly6G+ detections)/(# total cell detections)] × 100. The percentage of neutrophils undergoing NETosis was then defined as [(# Ly6G+/CitH3+/DAPI+ detections)/(# Ly6G+ detections)] × 100. The percentage of neutrophils undergoing NETosis was also calculated as a percentage of total cell detections as [(# Ly6G+/CitH3+/DAPI+ detections)/(# total cell detections)] × 100.

Immunohistochemistry for α-smooth muscle actin (αSMA) and Col I was performed in FFPE liver tissues (entire left lateral lobe) sectioned into 5 μm slices by the Rutgers Research Pathology Services Core. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through ethanol washes. For αSMA labeling, heat-mediated antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer (ScyTek Laboratories, Logan, UT, #CPL500), followed by endogenous peroxidase blocking in 3% hydrogen peroxide diluted at a 1:5 ratio in methanol. Sections were blocked in Super Block blocking buffer (ScyTek, #AAA125), then incubated with rabbit anti-αSMA (Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, #14395-1-AP) diluted 1:2000 in Normal Antibody Diluent (ScyTek, #ADT125) for 1 h at room temperature. Slides were washed in tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST), then incubated with Polytek anti-rabbit polymerized HRP (ScyTek, #PAR008) for 1 h at room temperature. Labeling was detected using the ImmPACT DAB substrate kit (Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA, #SK-4105), then slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and coverslipped with permanent mounting medium.

For Col I labeling, heat-mediated antigen retrieval was performed in Tris-EDTA buffer (ScyTek, #TES500) followed by endogenous peroxidase blocking in 3% hydrogen peroxide diluted at a 1:5 ratio in methanol. Sections were blocked in 10% normal goat serum in TBST, then incubated with rabbit anti-Col I (Abcam, ab270993) diluted 1:1500 in Normal Antibody Diluent overnight at 4 °C. Slides were washed in TBST, then incubated with Rabbit-on-Rodent Alkaline Phosphatase (AP) Polymer (Biocare Medical, Concord, CA, #RMR625) for 30 min at room temperature. Slides were washed in TBST, then labeling was detected using Warp Red AP substrate (Biocare, #WR806). Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and coverslipped with permanent mounting medium.

Immunolabeled slides were imaged using a Zeiss Axioscan 7 Slide Scanner with 20× objective. DAB+ area (αSMA) or fluorescent area (AF555 channel, Col I) was quantified using threshold analysis in QuPath and was normalized to the area of the entire left lateral lobe.

RNA isolation and quantitative polymerase chain reaction

Frozen liver tissue was homogenized in TRI reagent (Molecular Research Center, Cincinnati, OH, #TR118), and total RNA was extracted from the homogenate according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration was determined spectrophotometrically, and equivalent amounts of RNA were converted to cDNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit with Ribolock RNase inhibitor (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #4368813, #EO0381) according to the manufacturer’s instructions using the SimpliAmp Thermal Cycler (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA). Detection and quantification of mRNAs using the PerfeCTa SYBR Green FastMix Reaction Mix (Quantabio, Beverly, MA, #101414-288) was performed using the QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Primers from Integrated DNA Technologies were designed to measure messenger RNA expression of cytochrome P450 family 2 subfamily E member 1 (Cyp2e1), glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (Gapdh), heparin-binding growth factor (Hdgf), hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase (Hprt), and protein arginine deiminase 4 (Padi4) (Table S1). The expression of Cyp2e1 and Padi4 was normalized to the geometric mean Ct value of 3 housekeeping genes: Gapdh, Hprt, and Hgdf. Relative fold change was determined with the ΔΔCt method and normalized relative to expression in vehicle and/or wild-type controls.

Immunoassays

Plasma MPO–DNA complexes were measured using an in-house enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) as previously described (Denorme et al. 2022). Plasma levels of CitH3 nucleosomes were detected using the Volition Nu.Q H3R8Cit ELISA, and nucleosomes containing histone H3 were detected using the Volition Nu.Q H3.1 ELISA (Diapharma Group, West Chester, OH) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. If necessary, plasma samples were diluted in the provided assay buffer. Plasma thrombin–antithrombin (TAT) complexes were measured using the Enzygnost TAT Micro Kit (Siemens Healthineers, Cary, NC) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistics

Studies for key endpoints (e.g. acute CCl4 challenge) were repeated at least twice with unique cohorts of mice and analyzed together. Statistical analysis was performed using Prism (version 9, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Continuous variables are presented as values for individual mice and as mean ± SEM. Comparison of 2 groups was performed using Student’s t-test. Results were log-transformed if not normally distributed. Differences were considered significant if the P-value was 0 < 0.05.

Results

Detection of circulating and intrahepatic NET biomarkers in chronic toxicant-induced liver injury

Prior studies have reported that circulating and intrahepatic biomarkers of NETs are elevated in patients with chronic liver disease (van der Windt et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2024; von Meijenfeldt et al. 2025). Chronic challenge with the hepatotoxicant CCl4 is a well-described experimental setting that induces activation of HSCs and intrahepatic collagen deposition to drive liver fibrosis (Wu et al. 2023). Our first aim was to characterize circulating and intrahepatic NET biomarkers in this experimental setting. As anticipated, chronic CCl4 challenge caused hepatocellular necrosis and liver fibrosis in both male and female wild-type mice, indicated by elevated serum ALT activity, PSR staining (Fig. 1), and hepatic immunolabeling for Col I and αSMA (Fig. S1). Chronic CCl4 challenge also caused activation of the coagulation cascade in both male and female mice, detected by elevated plasma TAT complexes (Fig. 1B and F). Complexes of neutrophil-derived proteins (e.g. MPO) with DNA are widely considered reliable biomarkers of NET formation in vivo (Masuda et al. 2016; Donkel et al. 2021; de Diego et al. 2024). Plasma MPO–DNA complexes were significantly elevated in CCl4-challenged male and female mice compared to vehicle (Fig. 2A and I). Similarly, hepatic gene expression of Padi4 was significantly induced with CCl4 challenge (Fig. 2B and J). Building on these findings, we aimed to characterize intrahepatic NET formation in situ using immunolabeling for the neutrophil surface marker Ly6G, CitH3, and DNA. As anticipated, chronic CCl4 challenge increased neutrophils (Ly6G+ cells) in the liver in both male and female mice (Fig. 2C, D, K, and L). Surprisingly, NETs (Ly6G+/CitH3+/DAPI+) were rarely detected in both vehicle- and CCl4-challenged male and female mice (Fig. 2C, E, K, and M). When analyzed as a percentage of intrahepatic neutrophils, NETing neutrophils were not more frequently detected in CCl4-challenged mice compared to vehicle (Fig. S2).

Prior studies have suggested that NETs drive liver fibrosis in different experimental settings (Babuta et al. 2024). Correlation analysis of plasma MPO–DNA complexes with markers of liver injury and fibrosis indicated that MPO–DNA levels were not associated with serum ALT activity (Fig. 2F and N) or liver fibrosis (PSR staining) (Fig. 2G and O) in either sex. Interestingly, plasma MPO–DNA complexes had a strong positive correlation with plasma TAT complexes in male and female CCl4-challenged mice (Fig. 2H and P). Taken together, these results indicate that chronic CCl4 challenge increases circulating NET biomarkers, but intrahepatic NETs are minimally detected in this setting at this timepoint.

PAD4 deficiency has minimal impact on chronic toxicant-induced hepatocellular necrosis, coagulation activation, and liver fibrosis

Histone citrullination by PAD4 is widely recognized as a critical step in NETosis (Wang et al. 2009; Leshner et al. 2012). Therefore, to determine the role of NETs in experimental liver fibrosis, male and female wild-type (PAD4+/+) and PAD4-deficient (PAD4−/−) mice were challenged with CCl4 for 6 weeks. As anticipated, Padi4 mRNA was dramatically reduced (by >99%) in PAD4−/− mice (Fig. S3). PAD4 deficiency had no effect on chronic CCl4-induced hepatocellular necrosis in male or female mice (Fig. 3A and G). Moreover, despite a strong positive correlation between MPO–DNA and TAT, PAD4 deficiency had no effect on TAT levels (Fig. 3B and H). Surprisingly, and in contrast to prior reports (Xia et al. 2025), PAD4 deficiency did not impact CCl4-induced liver fibrosis, as detected by PSR staining, in male or female mice (Fig. 3C, D, I, and J). To increase specificity and sensitivity, we also performed immunolabeling for Col I. Using this approach, we found hepatic collagen deposition was modestly decreased in only CCl4-challenged PAD4−/− female mice compared to wild-type (Fig. 3I and K), but not in males (Fig. 3C and E). Despite decreased hepatic Col I staining in female mice, activation of HSCs, detected by immunolabeling for αSMA, was not impacted by PAD4 deficiency (Fig. 3I and L). In male mice, αSMA labeling tended to increase in CCl4-challenged PAD4−/− mice, though this effect did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 3C and F). These findings indicate that PAD4 deficiency modestly reduces CCl4-induced hepatic fibrosis in only female mice.

Plasma MPO–DNA complexes form in a PAD4-independent manner in chronic toxicant-induced liver injury

PAD4 plays a key role in the formation of NETs by catalyzing the citrullination of histones. Accordingly, the detection of citrullinated histones (e.g. CitH3) is considered a NET biomarker (Demers et al. 2012; Thålin et al. 2018, 2020). As anticipated, plasma levels of CitH3 were dramatically reduced in CCl4-challenged PAD4−/− male and female mice compared to wild-type (Fig. 4A and C). Surprisingly, plasma MPO–DNA complexes were similarly elevated in CCl4-challenged wild-type and PAD4−/− male and female mice (Fig. 4B and D). Plasma MPO–DNA complex levels (mean ± SD) in vehicle-challenged wild-type mice of the same sex from our initial studies (Fig. 2A and I) are shown in the gray shaded area for reference. In summary, specific NET biomarkers (e.g. MPO–DNA complexes) are increased in the plasma of chronic CCl4-challenged mice by a mechanism independent of PAD4-driven NETosis.

Detection of circulating and intrahepatic NET biomarkers in acute toxicant-induced liver injury

Having obtained unexpected results indicating that chronic CCl4 challenge causes the formation of MPO–DNA complexes in a potentially PAD4-independent manner, we next sought to thoroughly characterize the impact of CCl4 challenge on NET biomarkers in an acute setting. As anticipated, a single CCl4 challenge induced robust hepatocellular necrosis after 24 h, indicated by elevated serum ALT activity in both male and female mice (Fig. 5A and C), as well as activation of the coagulation cascade (Fig. 5B and D). Multiple circulating NET biomarkers, including nucleosomes (DNA-wrapped histones, Fig. 6A and H), CitH3 (Fig. 6B and I), and MPO–DNA complexes (Fig. 6C and J) were significantly elevated after 24 h in CCl4-challenged male and female mice compared to vehicle. NET biomarkers in CCl4-challenged male mice tended to be consistently higher than CCl4-challenged female mice. On a broader time course, we found that Padi4 gene expression was significantly elevated in CCl4-challenged male and female mice after 24 h (Fig. 6D and K). Similarly to chronic CCl4 challenge, acute CCl4 challenge caused intrahepatic neutrophil accumulation (Ly6G+ cells) in both male and female mice, though this effect did not reach statistical significance in female mice (Fig. 6E, F, L, and M). Interestingly, in contrast to chronic CCl4 challenge, intrahepatic NETs (Ly6G+/CitH3+/DAPI+) were more frequently detected in acute CCl4-challenged male mice (Fig. 6E and G, no primary antibody controls shown in Fig. S4). A similar trend was observed in female mice, but this effect did not reach statistical significance due to variability in vehicle-treated mice (Fig. 6L and N). Furthermore, this finding was consistent when intrahepatic NETs were analyzed as a percentage of intrahepatic neutrophils (Fig. S5). These results indicate that, similar to chronic challenge, a single CCl4 challenge causes an increase in circulating NET biomarkers; however, intrahepatic NETs are detected more frequently at 24 h following a single CCl4 challenge than at 24 h following 6 weeks of repeated challenge.

Association of circulating NET biomarkers with toxicant-induced acute hepatocellular necrosis and coagulation activation

In acute CCl4-challenged male mice, plasma MPO–DNA complexes and nucleosomes were positively correlated with serum ALT activity (Fig. 7A and C), but plasma CitH3 levels were not (Fig. 7B). Unlike chronic CCl4 challenge, plasma NET biomarkers were not associated with TAT complexes in CCl4-challenged male or female mice (Fig. 7D to F and J to L). PAD4 contributes, at least in part, to levels of circulating CitH3 and MPO–DNA complexes and intrahepatic NETs in acute toxicant-induced liver injury

Similar to our observations in chronic CCl4 challenge, PAD4 deficiency dramatically reduced plasma levels of CitH3 in acute CCl4-challenged male and female mice (Fig. 8A and F). For male mice, plasma CitH3 levels in CCl4-challenged PAD4−/− mice (8.9 ng/ml ± 1.3 SD) still tended to be higher than levels detected in vehicle-challenged mice in our initial studies (1.3 ng/ml ± 0.6 SD, Fig. 6B). In female mice, plasma CitH3 levels in CCl4-challenged PAD4−/− mice (4.6 ng/ml ± 2.7 SD) tended to be within the range of vehicle challenge (7.1 ng/ml ± 2.0 SD, Fig. 6I). It is important to note, however, that these values are very close to the reported limit of detection for the assay (∼4.63 ng/ml). Interestingly, in contrast to chronically challenged PAD4−/− mice, plasma MPO–DNA complexes were significantly reduced in acute CCl4-challenged male PAD4−/− mice compared to wild-type (Fig. 8B); however, MPO–DNA complex levels in CCl4-challenged PAD4−/− mice still tended to be slightly higher than MPO–DNA complexes detected in vehicle-challenged wild-type male mice (0.046 OD ± 0.007 SD, Fig. 6C). In female mice, PAD4 deficiency had no effect on plasma MPO–DNA complex levels after CCl4 challenge (Fig. 8G), but MPO–DNA complexes in both genotypes were not dramatically different than levels in vehicle-challenged wild-type mice (0.042 OD ± 0.002 SD, Fig. 6J). PAD4 deficiency did not affect plasma nucleosome levels after acute CCl4 challenge in either sex (Fig. 8C and H). Finally, we measured the impact of PAD4 deficiency on intrahepatic NET formation. In both sexes, acute CCl4 challenge was associated with hepatic neutrophil infiltration (Ly6G+ cells, Fig. 8D, E, I, and J), which was not affected by genotype; however, hepatic NETs (Ly6G+/CitH3+/DAPI+) were not detected in PAD4−/− mice. In contrast to chronic CCl4 challenge, PAD4 drives elevated plasma CitH3 and MPO–DNA complex levels and hepatic NET formation after a single CCl4 challenge.

PAD4 deficiency does not impact initial acute toxicant-induced hepatocellular necrosis but reduces coagulation activation

Having identified that circulating NET biomarkers associate with serum ALT activity in wild-type mice (Fig. 7) and that PAD4 deficiency reduces NET biomarkers (Fig. 8), we next sought to determine the impact of PAD4 deficiency on acute toxicant-induced liver injury. Acute CCl4 challenge caused similar hepatocellular necrosis in male and female wild-type and PAD4−/− mice, indicated by elevated serum ALT activity (Fig. 9A and E) and histology (Fig. 9D and G). In agreement with our initial studies in wild-type mice (Fig. 5), hepatocellular necrosis tended to be more pronounced in male mice at this timepoint (24 h). PAD4 deficiency had no effect on the area of necrosis (Fig. 9C). In addition, there was no difference in baseline Cyp2e1 gene expression between genotypes (Fig. S6), suggesting CCl4 metabolism is likely not altered in PAD4−/− mice. Plasma TAT complexes, indicative of coagulation cascade activation, were also elevated in CCl4-challenged male and female wild-type and PAD4−/− mice (Fig. 9B and F). Interestingly, PAD4 deficiency reduced plasma TAT levels in both sexes. In summary, PAD4 mediates, at least in part, acute CCl4-induced coagulation activation with no impact on initial hepatocellular necrosis.

PAD4 deficiency reduces peak toxicant-induced hepatocellular necrosis with no impact on HSC activation

Preliminary studies from our group (data not shown) and others (Steup et al. 1993; Giffen et al. 2003; Nakamura et al. 2004) have shown that the area of hepatocellular necrosis, assessed histologically, peaks 48 h after CCl4 challenge. Hepatocellular necrosis is then followed by liver repair and activation of HSCs to an αSMA+ myofibroblast phenotype (Karabulut et al. 2021; Cheng et al. 2022). In both male and female mice, serum ALT activity was similar in CCl4-challenged wild-type and PAD4−/− mice 48 h after challenge (Fig. 10A and G). Interestingly, the area of hepatocellular necrosis assessed in H&E-stained liver tissues was reduced in CCl4-challenged PAD4−/− mice in both sexes (Fig. 10C, D, I, and J). Plasma TAT levels in both sexes 48 h after CCl4 challenge (Fig. 10B and H) tended to be lower than 24 h after CCl4 challenge (Fig. 9B and F). At the 48 h timepoint, plasma TAT levels were similar in wild-type and PAD4−/− CCl4-challenged male and female mice (Fig. 10B and H). Finally, acute CCl4 challenge caused robust activation of HSCs after 48 h in both male and female mice, indicated by αSMA immunolabeling, which was not altered by PAD4 deficiency (Fig. 10E, F, K, and L). Taken together, these results indicate that PAD4 deficiency reduces acute toxicant-induced hepatocellular necrosis with no impact on HSC activation.

Discussion

This study tested the hypothesis that PAD4 drives NET formation in experimental chronic liver injury to promote HSC activation and liver fibrosis. Surprisingly, our results suggest that the contribution of PAD4 to NET formation and liver damage is context-dependent and varies between acute and chronic toxicant challenge. For example, circulating NET biomarkers (e.g. MPO–DNA complexes) were elevated after both acute and chronic CCl4 challenge. On the other hand, intrahepatic NETs were readily detected following acute challenge but were rare following chronic challenge. In an acute setting, circulating MPO–DNA complexes were reduced by PAD4 deficiency but, surprisingly, were not affected by PAD4 deficiency in a chronic setting. These results suggest that mechanisms of NET formation may evolve as liver injury progresses. Along these same lines, PAD4 deficiency did not impact chronic liver injury, coagulation activation, HSC activation, or fibrosis beyond a modest decrease in hepatic Col1 deposition in only female mice. On the other hand, PAD4 deficiency reduced coagulation activation and hepatocellular necrosis following a single CCl4 challenge. Taken together, our findings suggest that while PAD4 drives NET formation and disease pathology in experimental acute liver injury, mechanisms of NET formation in chronic liver injury may be PAD4-independent, contrary to our initial hypothesis. In addition, PAD4 has a modest, sex-specific impact on liver fibrosis in this setting of experimental chronic liver injury.

First, we sought to characterize NET formation in experimental chronic liver injury using multiple circulating and intrahepatic biomarkers. Elevated circulating NET biomarkers of MPO–DNA complexes (van der Windt et al. 2018; Zenlander et al. 2021), plasma CitH3–DNA complexes (Zenlander et al. 2021), and CitH3 in liver tissue (von Meijenfeldt et al. 2025) have been detected in patients with chronic liver injury. In experimental settings, NETs have been characterized by measuring serum NE, liver CitH3, and immunolabeling for co-localized Ly6G/CitH3/DAPI or NE/histone H3/DAPI (Babuta et al. 2024; Xia et al. 2025). Similarly, we detected elevated plasma MPO–DNA complexes and increased hepatic Padi4 gene expression in mice chronically challenged with CCl4. Surprisingly, intrahepatic NETs, measured by co-localization of Ly6G, CitH3, and DNA, were rare in this setting. In fact, when expressed as a percentage of intrahepatic neutrophils, NETting neutrophils were not more frequently detected in CCl4-challenged mice compared to vehicle. Hepatic Padi4 gene expression and circulating NET biomarkers (MPO–DNA complexes, CitH3, and nucleosomes) were also elevated following acute CCl4 challenge. Interestingly, intrahepatic NETs were far more frequently detected in acute CCl4-challenged mice compared to vehicle, in contrast to chronic CCl4 challenge. These findings suggest that the phenotype of hepatic neutrophils changes after multiple challenges of CCl4. A recent study documented functional changes in intrahepatic neutrophils over time following acute CCl4 challenge (Maali et al. 2024). Intrahepatic neutrophils shift from a pro-inflammatory to a pro-repair phenotype between 24 h and 72 h after a single challenge of CCl4. Interestingly, intrahepatic neutrophils at 24 h have a greater propensity to form NETs compared to intrahepatic neutrophils at 72 h. In our study, we assessed intrahepatic NET formation either 24 h after a single challenge or 24 h after the final challenge of chronic repeated exposure. Although the time point after CCl4 injection was therefore the same in both experiments, the magnitude and dynamics of liver injury likely evolve with repeated CCl4 administration, which may alter neutrophil recruitment and phenotype. It is possible that intrahepatic neutrophils in the chronic setting may more closely resemble the “pro-repair” neutrophil population, which are less likely to form NETs.

Building on these findings, we next sought to determine the role of PAD4 in NET formation in experimental liver injury. Abundant experimental evidence indicates that PAD4 plays a critical role in NET formation both in vitro (Wang et al. 2009; Li et al. 2010) and in vivo (Hemmers et al. 2011; Lewis et al. 2015). Accordingly, we anticipated that circulating NET biomarkers would be reduced in PAD4-deficient mice following chronic CCl4 challenge. Plasma CitH3 levels were dramatically reduced in CCl4-challenged PAD4-deficient mice compared to wild-type. Surprisingly, MPO–DNA complexes were similarly elevated in wild-type and PAD4-deficient mice after chronic CCl4 challenge. These results raise the intriguing possibility that NETs may form by a PAD4-independent mechanism in experimental chronic liver injury. Based on prior reports, NETs have the potential to form independently of PAD4 (Neeli and Radic 2013; Chen et al. 2018). In this mechanism of PAD4-independent NET formation, activated neutrophils induce inflammasome signaling where caspase-11 cleaves gasdermin-D (GSDMD), resulting in pore formation and perforations in the neutrophil membrane that allow for the release of MPO and DNA, which can be visualized by immunolabeling (Chen et al. 2018). Interestingly, PAD4-dependent histone citrullination can still occur in GSDMD-dependent NET formation, but it is not a required step, as LPS-stimulated neutrophils from PAD4-deficient mice still exhibited nuclear delobulation and diffuse, extracellular DNA (Chen et al. 2018). Our findings unexpectedly suggest that MPO–DNA complexes, traditionally considered a NET biomarker, form in a PAD4-independent manner in experimental chronic liver injury. These results highlight the importance of further characterization of extracellular trap formation in chronic liver injury. Indeed, the absence of intrahepatic NETs in chronic CCl4 challenge, detected by co-localization of CitH3/Ly6G/DAPI, may suggest that NETs are forming in a PAD4-independent manner, which may not be CitH3+.

Interestingly, plasma CitH3 levels, MPO–DNA complexes, and intrahepatic NETs were reduced in PAD4-deficient mice following a single CCl4 challenge. Histone H3 nucleosomes were not impacted by PAD4 deficiency. This biomarker may therefore be detecting cell-free DNA released from necrotic hepatocytes. These results, paired with our finding that CitH3/Ly6G/DAPI+ NETs are detected more frequently following acute CCl4 challenge, further suggest that the mechanism of extracellular trap formation is different between acute and chronic CCl4 challenge. Our results suggest that PAD4-dependent intrahepatic NET formation contributes to the detection of plasma NET biomarkers following a single CCl4 challenge. It is important to note, however, that CCl4 challenge can also cause tissue damage to other organs such as the kidneys (Botsoglou et al. 2008) and lungs (Pääkkö et al. 1996), and that NETs may also be forming in these tissues. These extrahepatic NETs may also therefore contribute to the plasma NET biomarker pool following acute CCl4 challenge, though at present, we are not aware of an experimental tool to selectively inhibit NETosis in a specific tissue compartment.

Furthermore, one limitation of our study is the use of whole-body PAD4−/− mice as opposed to neutrophil-selective PAD4-deficient mice. Other immune cells (e.g. monocytes, macrophages, and eosinophils) have been reported to produce DNA extracellular traps by PAD4-dependent and PAD4-independent mechanisms (Muñoz-Caro et al. 2014; Rayner et al. 2018; Barroso et al. 2021; Martinod et al. 2024). Our studies are therefore not able to delineate the specific contribution of NETs to the circulation extracellular trap biomarker pool following acute CCl4 challenge from PAD4-dependent extracellular traps derived from other immune cells. Though beyond the scope of our current study, the cellular source(s) of extracellular traps in acute and chronic liver injury remain an important target for further investigation. Previous studies have sought to clarify the specific role of neutrophil-derived PAD4 (and by extension, NETs) in various pathological contexts by crossing mice expressing a PAD4 conditional allele (Padi4flox/flox) with mice expressing Cre recombinase driven by the neutrophil-selective MRP8 promoter (MRP8-Cre) (Van Bruggen et al. 2024; Dragoni et al. 2025). However, it is critical to note that recent studies have identified that insertion of the MRP8-Cre transgene into murine chromosome 5 results in complete deletion of Serpine1 (plasminogen activator inhibitor-1, PAI-1) and partial deletion of Ap1s1 (Adaptor Related Protein Complex 1 Subunit Sigma 1) (Wang et al. 2022). As such, these mice may have very limited utility for studies in liver and metabolic disease, based on the known contribution of PAI-1 to hepatotoxicity and/or liver fibrosis in different contexts (Bajt et al. 2008; von Montfort et al. 2010; Capece et al. 2024; Kim et al. 2025). We propose that future studies seeking to determine the specific role of NETs in liver pathology will need to carefully apply other experimental approaches, e.g. bone marrow transplant or adoptive transfer of PAD4-deficient neutrophils in neutrophil-depleted wild-type mice.

Our findings that PAD4 may differentially contribute to NET formation in acute or chronic liver injury may also explain the differential impacts of PAD4 deficiency on liver pathology in these 2 settings. In our studies, we only report a modest reduction in hepatic fibrosis (by ∼20%) in response to PAD4 deficiency based on quantification of Col1 immunolabeling. We did not detect a difference in fibrosis by quantitation of PSR labeling (which labels Col I and III), indicating that the impact of PAD4 on liver fibrosis is subtle and may only be detected by more sensitive methods. The mechanisms driving this sex-specific effect remain unclear. It has been documented by our group (Fig. 5) and others (Masubuchi and Ihara 2022) that female mice are less sensitive to CCl4-induced hepatotoxicity than males. We observed that male mice tended to have higher levels of markers of hepatotoxicity and NET formation compared to female mice. Therefore, it is quite surprising that our PAD4-deficient female mice display a more significant impact on fibrosis than male mice. Our data suggest that there could be a sex-dependent effect of PAD4 on fibrosis; however, further studies are needed to identify a role of the estrous cycle in relation to PAD4-mediated fibrosis. Though subtle, our data suggest that there could be a sex-dependent effect of PAD4 on fibrosis; however, these results should be interpreted cautiously based on the assessment of multiple endpoints.

Our results contradict prior reports, which found a significant reduction in hepatic fibrosis (trichrome staining) in PAD4-deficient male mice (Xia et al. 2025). However, there were notable differences in these 2 experimental settings. In our study, mice were administered 1 µl CCl4/g, whereas the mice in the study by Xia et al. received 0.2 µl/g in addition to a Western diet. Repeated CCl4 challenge causes robust hepatocellular necrosis, which is not typically observed in settings of diet-induced chronic liver injury or chronic human disease. It is certainly possible that NET formation proceeds by different mechanisms in these 2 different experimental settings. However, it is important to note that Xia et al. used immunoblotting for CitH3 in liver homogenates as a method to confirm NET depletion in PAD4-deficient mice. While this method confirms a loss of PAD4 activity, it does not necessarily indicate that NETs are reduced. Therefore, in the setting of chronic Western diet feeding with CCl4 challenge, it is also possible that PAD4 contributes to liver fibrosis through a NET-independent mechanism. For instance, our results indicate that hepatic collagen deposition is reduced in PAD4-deficient female mice following chronic CCl4 challenge, even though there is no impact on MPO–DNA complexes. PAD4 has the capacity to citrullinate multiple proteins beyond histones, such as transcriptional regulators (Lee et al. 2005; Wang and Wang 2013), chromatin remodeling enzymes (Rebak et al. 2024), and tumor suppressors (Guo and Fast 2011). Interestingly, citrullinated extracellular matrix proteins (e.g. Col I) have been detected in human liver metastatic tumors (Yuzhalin et al. 2018). The impact of PAD4-driven citrullination of extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins in the injured liver, including their stability or interactions with cells, poses an intriguing target for further study. Though PAD4 is normally expressed primarily by granulocytes, it is possible that PAD4 may be induced in other hepatic cell types in pathological conditions. Because our studies utilized whole-body PAD4−/− mice, we are unable to discern the precise contribution of neutrophil PAD4 to acute or chronic liver injury in this setting. The cellular source(s) and potential NET-independent functions of PAD4 in liver injury should be explored in future studies.

Recent findings also suggest that NETs and NET components directly activate HSCs to a pro-fibrotic phenotype. Treatment of HSCs in vitro with NETs isolated from stimulated neutrophils (Babuta et al. 2024; Xia et al. 2025) or with NET components such as extracellular histones (Wang et al. 2020) drives HSCs to an activated phenotype. In vivo, DNAse treatment has also been shown to reduce hepatic myofibroblast markers in experimental fibrosis (Babuta et al. 2024; Xia et al. 2025). However, it is important to note that DNAse is not specific for NET-derived extracellular DNA; recent studies have demonstrated that DNAse may have other targets. For example, DNAse can hydrolyze ATP and ADP in vivo, reducing platelet activation (Carminita et al. 2021). Based on our findings, as previously discussed, the role of PAD4 in NET formation in chronic CCl4-induced liver fibrosis remains unclear. Furthermore, we did not observe any impact of PAD4 deficiency on HSC activation in this setting.

An additional aim of our study was to evaluate the impact of PAD4 deficiency on hepatocellular damage in experimental acute liver injury. Though PAD4 deficiency did not impact initial hepatocellular damage (24 h after acute CCl4 challenge), PAD4 deficiency modestly reduced the area of hepatocellular necrosis 48 h after acute CCl4 challenge. Interestingly, serum ALT activity at the 48 h timepoint was similar between wild-type and PAD4-deficient mice despite reduced area of necrosis. The utility of serum ALT as a biomarker of hepatocellular necrosis has been the subject of thorough discussion (McGill 2016; Vazquez et al. 2020). Though commonly interpreted as resulting from release from dead or dying hepatocytes, serum ALT activity reflects the sum of ALT expression, release, and clearance. Therefore, there are multiple possible explanations for the observation that serum ALT activity is similar between CCl4-challenged PAD4+/+ and PAD4−/− mice despite reduced hepatocellular necrosis in PAD4−/− mice at the 48 h time point. First, we found that hepatocellular necrosis was similar between male PAD4+/+ and PAD4−/− mice at the 24 h time point, which would likely result in equivalent initial ALT release. Additionally, CCl4 challenge is associated with increased hepatic ALT expression in rodents, which can contribute to serum ALT activity (Pappas 1980, 1986). Finally, ALT activity at the 48 h timepoint may therefore be more reflective of the clearance of ALT from circulation rather than ongoing release. Overall, we propose that serum ALT activity at the 48 h timepoint may reflect equivalent ALT induction, initial hepatocellular damage and ALT release, and similar ALT clearance in CCl4-challenged PAD4+/+ and PAD4−/− mice. Importantly, analysis of hepatocellular necrosis in liver tissues provides valuable insight into the role of PAD4 in acute liver injury, which may be missed if serum biomarkers are considered in isolation. Taken together, our results suggest that PAD4-deficient mice may exhibit a greater capacity for liver repair or an accelerated clearance of necrotic debris. Future studies may examine the impact of PAD4 deficiency on a more complete time course of liver damage and repair. As previously discussed, because we used whole-body PAD4-deficient mice, the possibility remains that PAD4 contributes to acute liver injury by a NET-independent function in this setting.

Although PAD4 deficiency reduced hepatocellular necrosis following acute CCl4 challenge, we did not find any effect of PAD4 deficiency on HSC activation at the same time point. Stellate cell activation may be influenced by multiple factors beyond hepatocyte necrosis, including activation of the inflammatory response and growth factor signaling. We did not assess these endpoints in our studies. It is therefore possible that although hepatocellular death was decreased in PAD4-deficient mice, downstream pathways mediating HSC activation remain intact. The role of PAD4-driven NET formation in HSC activation in vivo remains unclear and poses an interesting target for future investigation.

Finally, we sought to identify a potential role of PAD4-driven NET formation in the activation of the coagulation cascade in experimental liver injury. In both settings of acute and chronic CCl4 challenge, we observed activation of the blood coagulation cascade, indicated by elevated plasma TAT complexes. Multiple studies have reported a role of NETs in coagulation and thrombosis (Brill et al. 2012; McDonald et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2018; Englert et al. 2021). Interestingly, we found a strong positive correlation between plasma MPO–DNA complexes and plasma TAT levels in the setting of chronic CCl4 challenge. Because PAD4 deficiency did not impact plasma MPO–DNA complex levels in this setting, the potential contribution of PAD4-independent NETs to coagulation activation in chronic CCl4 challenge remains unknown. On the other hand, we did not find any correlation between plasma TAT complexes and any NET biomarker (MPO–DNA complexes, CitH3, or nucleosomes) following acute CCl4 challenge. Surprisingly, 24 h after acute CCl4 challenge, PAD4 deficiency reduced circulating NET biomarkers as well as plasma TAT complexes. These data suggest that PAD4-driven NET formation following acute CCl4 challenge may contribute, at least in part, to coagulation activation in this setting. The mechanisms contributing to this phenomenon remain a target for future investigation.

A final limitation of our study, which should be carefully considered, is that the PAD4+/+ and PAD4−/− mice used for these studies were maintained as separate strains as opposed to littermates. Recent recommendations highlight the importance of using littermate controls when possible to avoid potential inter-strain differences in kinetics or dynamics of hepatotoxicants (Jaeschke et al. 2021). To minimize the potential impact of confounding factors between these lines, founders for each line were both purchased from the same vendor (The Jackson Laboratory) and imported within 1 month of each other. The PAD4−/− mice used for these studies (#030315) were backcrossed onto a C57BL/6J background during development, and as such, C57BL/6J mice were used as the wild-type controls for these studies, as recommended by the vendor. Both lines were bred in the same room under identical housing and husbandry conditions at Rutgers University. Though every effort was made to minimize variability between these 2 strains of mice, potential undiscovered underlying differences beyond genetics (e.g. microbiome), which may impact hepatotoxic responses, cannot be excluded. Importantly, we detected equivalent hepatic expression of Cyp2e1 in unchallenged PAD4+/+ and PAD4−/− mice from our facility (Fig. S6). However, our findings should be interpreted in the context of this important limitation.

In conclusion, our results suggest that NET formation is driven by different mechanisms in experimental acute and chronic toxicant-induced liver injury. Furthermore, we propose that it is critical to approach NET characterization in experimental liver injury with caution, assessing multiple biomarkers in circulation and within the liver in order to fully capture PAD4-dependent and PAD4-independent NETs, as well as potential extracellular traps from other cellular sources. Finally, the contribution of PAD4, both through its role in NET formation and/or NET-independent functions, to liver pathology is likely context-dependent and warrants further investigation.
