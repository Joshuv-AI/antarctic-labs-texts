A predictive model for pathological complete response in neoadjuvant-treated breast cancer: integrating pretreatment clinicopathologic and contrast-enhanced ultrasound characteristics via interpretable machine learning
Authors: Danfeng Huang, Yijie Chen, Wanping Chen, Yaoqin Wang, Wenrong Lin, Lina Tang, Xiangyi Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe ability to predict pathological complete response (pCR) prior to neoadjuvant therapy (NAT) would inform and facilitate tailored therapeutic regimens for patients with breast cancer. This study aimed to develop and internally validate an interpretable machine-learning model for pretreatment pCR prediction using routinely available clinicopathologic variables, hematologic indices, and ultrasound (US)/contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) descriptors.MethodsThis retrospective analysis included 309 sequential breast cancer cases managed with neoadjuvant therapy followed by surgical intervention. Patients were randomly divided into training and testing cohorts at a ratio of 8:2 using stratified sampling according to pCR status. Feature selection was performed exclusively in the training cohort using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression. Five machine-learning classifiers, including CatBoost, LightGBM, XGBoost, SVM, and CART, were developed and evaluated. Model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC, with 95% CI), accuracy, specificity, sensitivity, precision, recall, F1-score, G-mean, Brier score, decision curve analysis (DCA) and calibration analysis. Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) were used for model interpretation.ResultspCR was achieved in 29.8% (92/309) patients. LASSO regression selected eight predictors, including CEA, clinical T stage, ER, PR, HER2, Ki-67 index, Hyperechoic halo on US, and Range expansion on CEUS. In the testing cohort, CatBoost achieved an AUC of 0.8295 (95% CI: 0.7166–0.9231) and showed the highest overall accuracy (0.8065), specificity (0.8182), and precision (0.6364). CatBoost also demonstrated acceptable calibration, with a Brier score of 0.1595, and favorable net benefit across clinically relevant threshold probabilities. SHAP analysis identified HER2 status, CEA, clinical T stage, and hyperechoic halo as the leading contributors to the final model.ConclusionsAn interpretable pretreatment CatBoost model showed good discrimination, acceptable calibration, and favorable clinical net benefit for predicting pCR after NAT in breast cancer. This approach may help support pretreatment risk stratification and inform multidisciplinary discussions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16452-x.Keywords: Locally advanced breast cancer, Neoadjuvant Therapy, Pathological complete response, Interpretable machine learning, Contrast-enhanced ultrasound

Background

Breast cancer has emerged as the most prevalent malignant tumor among women in China, with steadily increasing incidence and mortality, representing a major public health challenge. According to the global cancer burden data from 2020, breast cancer ranks first in incidence among female malignancies, surpassing lung cancer to become the most common cancer worldwide. Breast cancer remains a major contributor to cancer mortality in the female population worldwide [1]. Neoadjuvant therapy (NAT) is integral to the management for stage II–III breast cancer, enabling tumor downstaging and providing an in vivo readout of treatment sensitivity. Pathological complete response (pCR) was defined as no residual invasive or in situ carcinoma in the breast and axillary lymph nodes. It has long served as a significant intermediate endpoint in breast cancer research. Substantial evidence indicates that achieving pCR correlates with long-term survival, especially for patients with aggressive molecular subtypes [2–5]. These findings support the use of pCR as a valid endpoint in high-risk early-stage breast cancer. However, pCR rates vary widely by tumor biology and stage, and accurate pretreatment prediction remains challenging. A baseline ultrasound-based clinical prediction model could inform escalation strategies for patients unlikely to achieve pCR [6, 7] and, conversely, support de-escalation trials and response-adapted pathways for those with a high predicted probability.

MRI-based radiomics and deep learning methods have been extensively studied for treatment response prediction, but systematic reviews emphasize marked methodological heterogeneity and variable study quality, which limits reproducibility and generalizability [8]. Moreover, many imaging AI approaches rely on radiomics pipelines that require segmentation and substantial computational and harmonization effort, which can hinder scalability and real-world adoption. Ultrasound (US) is widely available, cost-effective, and routinely performed at baseline in many breast cancer pathways, while contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) provides additional information on tumor perfusion heterogeneity and microvascular features. Prior evidence, including a dedicated meta-analysis, supports the ability of CEUS to assess pathological response to NAT, indicating that vascular and perfusion phenotypes captured by CEUS may reflect treatment-sensitive tumor biology [9]. Moreover, recent work using pretreatment US-based deep learning radiomics has reported promising performance for predicting response to NAT, supporting the broader premise that US contains actionable baseline response information [10]. Importantly, compared with radiomics-heavy pipelines that require segmentation and extensive harmonization, standardized qualitative US/CEUS descriptors may offer a more feasible route to clinical application, particularly when combined with routinely collected clinicopathological and laboratory variables.

Peripheral blood–derived biomarkers are accessible indicators of systemic inflammation, immune response, and nutritional status, making them practical candidates for prognostic and predictive assessment in solid tumors. In patients with breast cancer undergoing neoadjuvant therapy, immune-inflammatory markers such as the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), prognostic nutritional index (PNI), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) have been reported to be associated with treatment response and pathological complete response (pCR) [11, 12].

Accordingly, the aim of this study was to develop and internally validate an interpretable baseline multimodal machine-learning (ML) model for predicting pCR of NAT by integrating routinely available clinicopathological variables, hematologic indices, and pretreatment US/CEUS descriptors. We compared multiple ML algorithms, assessed discrimination and clinical utility, and used Shapley’s Additive explanations (SHAP) to provide transparent explanations of individualized predictions, thereby supporting potential clinical application and future external validation.

Methods

Participants

We retrospectively identified 609 female breast cancer patients who received NAT at Fujian Cancer Hospital from 2016 to 2022. Among them, 367 patients who had US/CEUS examinations before NAT. No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed because of the retrospective nature of the study. Instead, all consecutive patients who met the eligibility criteria were included. All analyses were conducted using a complete-case dataset, and no statistical imputation was performed for missing data. Inclusion criteria were: (1) invasive breast cancer confirmed by US-guided biopsy before NAT; (2) complete clinicopathologic and pretreatment ultrasound data (US and CEUS); and (3) no prior anticancer therapy. Exclusion criteria were: (1) failure to complete standard NAT or proceed to surgery (n = 23); (2) distant metastasis at diagnosis (n = 21); (3) bilateral disease (n = 2); and (4) insufficient US/CEUS image quality (n = 12). After screening, 309 patients were included (Fig. 1). This retrospective observational study was not prospectively registered in any clinical trial registry. No formal written study protocol was developed prior to data collection. Data were retrospectively collected from a single hospital. No patient or public involvement was undertaken in the design, execution, analysis or manuscript preparation of this study. As no multiple clinical clusters were involved, cross-site heterogeneity assessment and related statistical adjustment were not performed in the present study. Ethical approval (No. K2023-368-01) was obtained from the Ethics Committee at Fujian Cancer Hospital, Clinical Oncology School of Fujian Medical University. The requirement for informed consent was waived because the data were gathered retrospectively. The reporting of this retrospective observational study followed the STROBE statement [13]. The development, evaluation, and reporting of the prediction models were guided by the TRIPOD + AI statement [14].

Clinicopathological data acquisition

Baseline patient data retrieved from hospital records comprised demographics (age, menopausal status, body mass index (BMI)), clinical parameters (Clinical T stage, Clinical N stage, total bilirubin (TBIL), albumin, lymphocytes, neutrophils, monocytes, platelets, CA153 (cancer antigen 153), CEA (carcinoembryonic antigen)), and pathological parameters (estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) and Ki-67 index). The systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), prognostic nutritional index (PNI), peripheral blood neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio(NLR) and the platelet to lymphocyte ratio (PLR) were calculated as follows: SII = (platelets × neutrophils) / lymphocytes, PNI = albumin + 5 × lymphocytes, NLR=neutrophils/lymphocytes, PLR=platelets /lymphocytes, with all cell counts expressed in 10³/µL and albumin in g/L.

US and CEUS data acquisition

Breast ultrasound examinations were performed using Philips (IU Elite, IU22) or Supersonic AixPlorer equipped with 5–15 MHz linear transducers. Fundamental imaging parameters, including depth, focus, and frequency, were tailored individually for each patient to ensure optimal diagnostic image quality. In contrast, the gain setting was held constant across all examinations to guarantee a homogeneous tissue appearance throughout the image, with both gain and frequency configurations kept consistent for every scan. All CEUS procedures were carried out by two experienced radiologists using designated ultrasound systems (Philips IU22 or IU Elite) and 3–9 MHz linear transducers. Following a baseline gray-scale ultrasound examination, where the largest sectional images were documented, a dose of 4.8 mL of Sono Vue was injected intravenously and flushed with 5 mL of normal saline. Upon administration of the contrast agent, the timer and dynamic image storage were initiated concurrently. The ultrasound probe was then held stationary for two minutes to monitor the dynamic enhancement within the lesion’s largest cross-sectional plane. The entire imaging sequence was documented in DICOM. The single frame demonstrating peak contrast intensity within the lesion was selected for final analysis. All ultrasound data were retrospectively extracted from the institutional breast imaging database.

The evaluated US and CEUS characteristics included: (a) largest diameter on ultrasound. (b) aspect ratio (the ratio was measured on the largest cross-sectional tumor image and defined as the anteroposterior diameter divided by the transverse diameter). (c) margin, divided into circumscribed or not circumscribed (angular, spiculated, and micro lobulated). (d) hyperechoic halo (absent or present). (e) posterior acoustic features (none, enhancement or shadowing). (f) microcalcifcation (absent or present). (g) internal vascularity, and vascularity abundance was evaluated using the Adler grading system, with grades 2 ~ 3 and grades 0 ~ 1. (h) axillary lymph node status, which was evaluated using the Ecanow method and accordingly categorized into metastatic or non-metastatic [15]. (i) enhancement intensity (iso-enhancement, hypo-enhancement or hyper-enhancement). (j) range expansion (absent, present, difficult to discern). The expansion of the enhancement area was evaluated through a comparative analysis between the tumor region visualized on contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) and the corresponding lesion area identified on conventional ultrasound. (k) enhancement defect (absent or present). A filling defect was identified if any region within the lesion demonstrated no enhancement throughout the contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) examination. (l) nutritive vessels (absent or present). A retrospective review of the ultrasound images was conducted independently by two senior sonographers who were blinded to all clinical information. Any disagreement in the assessment of imaging features was resolved through discussion.

Neoadjuvant chemotherapy and pathological assessment

Neoadjuvant therapy (NAT) was administered in accordance with the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) and the China Anti-Cancer Association (CACA) guidelines. Patients received 6 to 8 cycles of treatment, with each cycle lasting 21 days. Chemotherapy primarily incorporated anthracyclines, taxanes, or a sequential combination of both. For patients with HER2-positive disease, the regimen was supplemented with HER2-targeted therapy, specifically trastuzumab with or without pertuzumab. Surgical resection followed approximately 2–3 weeks post-NAT. The resected specimens were subjected to standard pathological analysis. The prediction outcome is determined by the pCR status. Standard pCR (ypT0 ypN0) was defined as no residual invasive or in situ disease present in either the breast or the lymph nodes upon H&E evaluation, consistent with AJCC criteria [16]. All surgical pathology specimens were evaluated by experienced breast pathologists according to institutional standards.

Data preprocessing and feature selection

The full cohort was first divided into training and testing cohorts at a ratio of 8:2 using stratified random sampling according to pCR status. To minimize information leakage, all feature selection procedures and model development steps were performed exclusively within the training cohort. The independent testing cohort was not used during feature selection, hyperparameter tuning, or model training, and was reserved only for final model evaluation.

A total of 29 variables were collected using a prespecified case report form. Age, BMI, TBIL, Albumin, CEA, CA153, SII, PNI, NLR, PLR, Ki-67 index and Largest diameter on ultrasound were treated as continuous variables, while menopausal status, Clinical T stage, Clinical N stage, ER, PR, HER-2, eleven US and CEUS features were categorized as categorical variables. Continuous variables were retained in their original continuous form whenever appropriate. Feature selection was performed in the training cohort using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression [17]. Predictors selected by LASSO regression were subsequently used to develop machine learning models, which were then evaluated in the independent testing cohort. The optimal penalty parameter λ was determined by 5-fold cross-validation. Specifically, λ.min was defined as the value of λ that minimized the mean cross-validated error.

Model development and model evaluation

The training cohort was used to develop five ML models, and the testing cohort was used for performance evaluation. Considering model diversity, overfitting robustness, and practical applicability, we constructed five machine learning models, including Support vector machine (SVM), Classification and regression tree (CART), Categorical Boosting (CatBoost), Light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM), and eXtreme gradient boosting machine (XGBoost). Hyperparameters were optimized, and early stopping was applied where supported, using five-fold cross-validation in the training cohort. Class imbalance was observed in the training cohort, with fewer pCR cases than non-pCR cases. To address class imbalance, the SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique) method was applied only within the training cohort during model development. Grid-search method was applied to fine-tune the hyperparameters of the ML models in the training cohort to identify the optimal model parameters with the goal of achieving the best model score under cross-validation. The independent testing cohort was not oversampled and was used only for final model evaluation. Predicted probabilities were subsequently recalibrated using isotonic regression, and calibration performance was evaluated using the Brier score.

A comprehensive evaluation framework was implemented. This included receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis to determine the mean area under the curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), alongside accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, recall, F1-score, G-mean and Brier score. Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) was used to evaluate the models’ clinical utility, and calibration curves were constructed to compare predictive accuracy. No dedicated fairness mitigation strategies were performed in this single-centre study. Patient demographic profiles were relatively homogeneous, formal subgroup fairness evaluation was not within the scope of the present analysis. The model outputs individual predicted risks. No categorical risk grouping or threshold definition was performed. This was a single-centre study without multiple clusters, so cross-cluster heterogeneity of model performance was not assessed.

Model interpretability

Model interpretability was achieved by using Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP), which is rooted in cooperative game theory and applicable to any model, quantifies feature contribution to explain the output of our best-performing integrated model. Variable importance in the optimal model was subsequently quantified using SHAP. SHAP analysis was further applied to explain case-level predictions, thereby improving model interpretability and supporting its potential clinical utility.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS (version 27.0) and Python (version 3.10). Categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages, with comparisons between groups conducted using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous data were summarized as mean ± standard deviation. Group differences were evaluated using the t-test for normally distributed data and the Mann–Whitney U test for skewed data. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

The whole dataset was partitioned into training and testing sets through stratified random sampling according to pCR status. All machine learning algorithms, predictive performance evaluation, and SHAP-based visualization for the CatBoost model were executed using Python (version 3.10), statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P value < 0.05. No pre-specified subgroup analyses or interaction effect assessments were performed in the present study.

Feature selection

In the training cohort, LASSO regression identified 8 predictive features from the 29 candidate variables (Fig. 2). Using the optimal shrinkage parameter (λ.min = 0.0335) obtained from cross-validation, the variables with nonzero coefficients were CEA, Clinical T stage, ER, PR, HER2, Ki-67 index, Hyperechoic halo on ultrasound and Range expansion on contrast-enhanced ultrasound. These features were then used as input variables for construction of the ML models.

Performance of different ML models

To evaluate the clinical applicability of the predictive models, DCA and calibration analysis were performed on the internal test cohort. Decision curve analysis (Fig. 5) showed that CatBoost achieved a favorable net benefit across a clinically relevant range of threshold probabilities, particularly between approximately 0.10 and 0.55. XGBoost and LightGBM showed comparable but slightly less stable net benefit, whereas CART and SVM demonstrated limited clinical utility over broader threshold ranges.

Decision curve analysis (DCA) for five ML models in the testing cohort

Calibration analysis (Fig. 6) showed that the CatBoost model had acceptable probability calibration in the test cohort, with a Brier score of 0.1595. Although the calibration curve did not perfectly align with the ideal reference line, CatBoost showed relatively balanced agreement between predicted and observed pCR probabilities compared with the other models. XGBoost yielded a slightly lower Brier score of 0.1584, however, its calibration curve showed greater fluctuation across probability intervals.

Calibration curve for five ML models in the testing cohort

Although XGBoost achieved the highest AUC in the test cohort, its AUC was nearly identical to that of CatBoost. CatBoost was selected as the final model because it achieved a comparable AUC of 0.8295 (95% CI: 0.7166–0.9231) and showed the highest overall accuracy (0.8065), specificity (0.8182), and precision (0.6364), together with favorable decision-curve performance and acceptable calibration, with a Brier score of 0.1595.

Model interpretability

SHAP analysis was performed to interpret the CatBoost model predictions. The SHAP summary plot (Fig. 7A) illustrates the contribution of each selected predictor to the output of the CatBoost model for pCR prediction. Among the eight selected variables, HER2 showed the greatest impact on model prediction, followed by CEA, Clinical T stage, Hyperechoic halo, ER, Ki-67, PR, and Range expansion. In the SHAP summary plot, positive SHAP values represented an increased predicted probability of pCR, whereas negative values represented a decreased probability. HER2 positivity and higher Ki-67 levels were generally associated with positive SHAP values, indicating favorable contributions to pCR prediction. Conversely, ER/PR positivity, higher clinical T stage, hyperechoic halo, and range expansion were mainly distributed on the negative SHAP side, suggesting that these features contributed to a lower predicted probability of pCR. The SHAP bar plot (Fig. 7B) further confirmed the ranking of global feature importance, with HER2, CEA, clinical T stage, and hyperechoic halo being the most influential predictors in the final model.

SHAP individual force plots

Individual-level SHAP force plots were used to visualize how specific predictors influenced the CatBoost model output in representative cases (Fig. 8). In the non-pCR case, HER2 negativity, ER and PR positivity, low Ki-67expression, presence of Hyperechoic halo, and elevated CEA level shifted the prediction toward a lower probability of pCR. Conversely, in the pCR case, HER2 positivity, ER and PR negativity, high Ki-67 expression, absence of hyperechoic halo, and low CEA level pushed the model output toward a higher probability of pCR.

Incremental value of US/CEUS features

To evaluate the incremental value of US/CEUS features, an ablation analysis was performed using CatBoost models based on different feature sets. The combined CatBoost model included both clinicopathologic variables and US/CEUS descriptors. The clinicopathologic CatBoost model included only clinicopathologic and hematologic variables (CatBoost 1), whereas the US/CEUS CatBoost model included only conventional US and CEUS descriptors (CatBoost 2). In the training cohort, CatBoost model and CatBoost 1 model showed comparable discrimination, with AUCs of 0.9048 and 0.9102, respectively. CatBoost 2 model showed limited predictive performance, with an AUC of 0.6057.

In the testing cohort, as presented in Fig. 9; Table 3, compared with CatBoost 1 model, CatBoost model showed a modest increase in AUC from 0.8144 to 0.8295 and improved accuracy, sensitivity, F1-score, G-mean, and Brier score. The difference in AUC between the combined model (CatBoost) and the clinicopathologic model (CatBoost 1) did not reach statistical significance by DeLong test (p = 0.1084).

Discussion

In this study, we developed and internally validated a pretreatment (baseline) multimodal machine-learning model to predict pCR after NAT using routinely available clinicopathological data, hematologic indices, and US/CEUS descriptors. Among 309 eligible patients, 92 (29.8%) achieved pCR. In the internal test cohort, the final CatBoost model demonstrated good discrimination (AUC 0.8295, 95% CI: 0.7166–0.9231) and showed favorable overall accuracy, specificity, precision, and F1-score. Decision curve analysis further showed that CatBoost provided a favorable net benefit across a clinically relevant range of threshold probabilities, particularly between approximately 0.10 and 0.55. Together with acceptable calibration, these findings suggest that the CatBoost model may have potential utility for individualized pretreatment prediction of pCR.

In the present study, LASSO regression was used to select predictors from 29 candidate variables, and eight variables were retained at the optimal shrinkage parameter (λmin = 0.0335): CEA, clinical T stage, ER, PR, HER2, Ki-67 index, Hyperechoic halo on ultrasound, and Range expansion on CEUS. LASSO regression applies L1 regularization to shrink less informative coefficients toward zero, thereby reducing model complexity and the risk of overfitting. The retained predictors were generally consistent with established determinants of response to NAT, including tumor burden, receptor status, and proliferative activity. Notably, hyperechoic halo and range expansion were also selected, suggesting that US/CEUS features may provide complementary phenotypic information when integrated with clinicopathologic predictors.

The ablation analysis further clarified the contribution of US/CEUS features to pCR prediction. The clinicopathologic model (CatBoost1) already demonstrated good predictive performance, consistent with the established role of molecular and clinical factors such as ER, PR, HER2, Ki-67, and clinical T stage in predicting response to NAT. The US/CEUS-feature model alone showed moderate predictive ability, indicating that these imaging features are insufficient as independent predictors. However, when US/CEUS features were integrated with clinicopathologic variables, the combined CatBoost model achieved the best overall performance in the testing cohort, with improvements in accuracy, sensitivity, F1-score, G-mean, and Brier score compared with the clinicopathologic model alone. These findings suggest that US/CEUS features may provide complementary information when combined with established clinicopathologic predictors. Therefore, the incremental value of US/CEUS features requires further validation in larger external cohorts.

To mitigate the “black-box” limitation of machine-learning models, we used SHAP to provide cohort-level interpretability, enabling clinicians to understand how each feature shifts the predicted probability toward pCR or non-pCR under an additive attribution framework. A recent large-scale study (N > 2,900) using machine learning with SHAP value interpretation found that ER, HER2, albumin, and inflammation index were among the most important predictors of pCR, emphasizing that baseline tumor biology and host immune-nutritional status are pivotal factors [18]. In the CatBoost model, HER2 showed the greatest impact on prediction, followed by CEA, clinical T stage, hyperechoic halo, ER, Ki-67 index, PR, and Range expansion. HER2 positivity and higher Ki-67 values tended to increase the predicted probability of pCR, whereas ER positivity, PR positivity, and higher clinical T stage tended to decrease the predicted probability.These findings are consistent with prior NAT experience and large pooled analyses showing higher pCR rates in earlier clinical T stage, HER2-positive and triple-negative disease, and in tumors with higher proliferative activity (Ki-67), which often correlates with chemosensitivity [2, 5]. Collectively, these results reinforce that baseline clinicopathologic phenotype remains a strong determinant of response across subtypes. In the SHAP analysis, higher CEA levels were associated with a lower predicted probability of pCR. Evidence regarding the relationship between baseline CEA and response to NAT remains limited and inconsistent, but some studies have suggested that higher baseline CEA portends a lower likelihood of achieving pCR [19, 20]. Biologically, this inverse relationship may reflect the aggressive tumor phenotype signaled by elevated CEA rather than direct chemoresistance. As a cell adhesion molecule, CEA has been implicated in tumor invasion, metastatic dissemination, and immune evasion. Therefore, the SHAP-identified contribution of CEA provides a plausible explanation for its role in the model, although the underlying mechanism requires further investigation.

Hyperechoic halo was retained by LASSO regression and ranked fourth among the eight selected predictors in SHAP analysis. In CatBoost model, the presence of a hyperechoic halo mainly shifted predictions toward a lower probability of pCR. Prior work has linked echogenic halo/rim-related descriptors to histopathologic features such as tumor infiltration into surrounding tissue and desmoplastic reaction, suggesting that “halo” may reflect tumor–stroma interaction and local tissue remodeling rather than a simple visual artifact [21]. Prior studies have linked echogenic halo to molecular subtype and grade—features that are strongly associated with NAT sensitivity. Invasive breast cancers of the luminal A subtype have been reported to more frequently show spiculated margins and an echogenic rim, whereas triple-negative breast cancers more commonly present with circumscribed margins and absence of an echogenic rim [22]. Therefore, hyperechoic halo may provide complementary information when combined with clinicopathologic predictors.

Range expansion on CEUS was also retained in the final feature set. This feature reflects the discrepancy between the enhanced tumor extent on CEUS and the lesion size observed on conventional ultrasound, which may indicate peritumoral microvascular involvement, infiltrative growth, or increased perfusion heterogeneity around the tumor margin [23]. In the present study, range expansion was contributed to the CatBoost model output, suggesting that CEUS-derived information related to tumor vascularity and peritumoral enhancement may capture functional characteristics about tumor perfusion and the tumor–microenvironment interface that is not fully reflected by receptor status or conventional ultrasound morphology. However, hyperechoic halo is a qualitative imaging descriptor and may be influenced by lesion size, breast composition, scanning technique, and observer interpretation. Range expansion is a semiquantitative and operator-dependent imaging feature, and its assessment may be affected by contrast timing, imaging plane selection, lesion boundary definition, and the subjective judgment of the examiner. Therefore, these imaging features should not be interpreted as an independent or definitive predictor of pCR, but rather as one component of a multimodal predictive pattern.

Numerous pretreatment models for pCR prediction of NAT have been reported. Recent work has demonstrated strong performance using large-scale multimodal systems integrating MRI radiomics/deep learning, digital pathology (pathomics), and clinicopathologic features [24, 25]. For example, Mao et al. reported a fully automated multimodal system integrating pretreatment MRI, whole-slide images, and clinical features in a multicenter cohort (n ≈ 1004), offered a favorable predictive performance in both the pooled external test set (AUC = 0.882) and the prospective test set (AUC = 0.909) and Huang et al. developed an MRI-based intratumoral heterogeneity (ITH) nomogram in a multicenter cohort of 1,448 women, reporting AUCs around 0.79–0.82 in external validation. However, despite strong predictive capability, the real-world deployment of MRI/radiomics/pathomics-heavy models is often limited by the requirements for segmentation, high-dimensional feature extraction, and infrastructure-dependent workflows. Systematic reviews of radiomics for pCR prediction likewise emphasize methodological heterogeneity and variable study quality, supporting the need for parsimonious, clinically scalable alternatives [26]. In contrast, our approach used conventional US/CEUS descriptors and routine clinical variables that are already documented in many breast imaging reports. This design creates a realistic pathway for future scaling: potentially through structured reporting and automated extraction from radiology reports without segmentation.

The discriminative performance of our model outperformed earlier baseline clinical prediction models for pCR, while it was comparable to recently published analogous baseline-based models [27–31]. The final combined CatBoost model achieved an AUC of 0.83, which is similar to the AUC reported by Fang et al. [30] and lower than the 0.89 reported by Jia et al. [31]. However, direct comparison of AUC values across studies should be interpreted with caution because of inherent heterogeneity in patient populations, neoadjuvant regimens, pCR definitions, and modeling pipelines. As one of the most widely used imaging modalities in clinical practice of breast cancer assessment, US/CEUS provides direct information on tumor morphology, local growth patterns, and vascular characteristics. To the best of our knowledge, this study is among the first to incorporate CEUS-derived qualitative descriptors into a machine-learning framework for pretreatment pCR prediction together with conventional ultrasound and clinicopathological data. Previous models have mainly relied on grayscale ultrasound morphologic features or established clinicopathological variables. They may not fully capture the angiogenic and hemodynamic characteristics that CEUS can visualize. In the present study, the final model still showed acceptable calibration and favorable clinical net benefit, supporting its potential role as a complementary tool for pretreatment pCR prediction. This study extends previous baseline prediction models and provides a framework that may be further refined through multicenter external validation and more advanced US/CEUS feature extraction methods in future studies.

From a clinical perspective, the model may serve as a pretreatment risk-stratification tool to support multidisciplinary discussions before NAT. The prediction requires manual input of clinical indicators by clinicians, and routine clinical practitioner expertise is sufficient for model application. Patients predicted to have a low probability of pCR may benefit from closer imaging follow-up, early response assessment, or consideration of treatment optimization. However, the model is not intended to replace established clinicopathologic assessment or physician judgment. The optimal decision threshold and clinical workflow should be prospectively evaluated before implementation.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, our sample size (309 patients) is modest for machine learning development, particularly given the limited number of pCR events, may increase the risk of model overfitting and reduce the precision of performance estimates. Although internal testing and cross-validation were performed, the single-center retrospective design limits the generalizability of the findings. A definitive threshold for changing management cannot be established from the current retrospective single-center dataset, the optimal decision threshold and clinical workflow should be prospectively evaluated before implementation. Therefore, the proposed model should be regarded as exploratory and requires external validation in larger multicenter cohorts. Second, variations in ultrasound equipment, imaging technique, and reader interpretation could impact the reproducibility of our feature set elsewhere. Third, we did not incorporate longitudinal response data or post-NAT imaging in the model. Our intent was to focus on baseline predictor, which is a strength in terms of early decision-making; however, it is possible that integrating early treatment changes (for example, changes in tumor size or enhancement after a few cycles of therapy) could further improve prediction accuracy [9]. Fourth, no predefined rules were established to manage missing or low-quality predictor data when this model is applied prospectively in clinical practice, which constitutes one limitation of this study. Additionally, heterogeneity in treatment regimens was present – patients received NAT according to standard protocols but with variations in chemotherapy drugs or the addition of targeted therapy/immunotherapy based on tumor subtype. We did not stratify the model performance by these treatment differences; such unaccounted variability could introduce noise. Finally, as with any observational study, there may be unmeasured confounders (e.g. genomic tumor characteristics, detailed immune microenvironment factors) that influence pCR and were not captured in our model.

Conclusion

We developed a clinically feasible baseline multimodal model for predicting pCR after NAT by integrating routinely available clinicopathologic data, hematologic indices, and US/CEUS descriptors. The CatBoost model demonstrated good discrimination and favorable net benefit in internal testing, with preliminary evidence of potential clinical utility. SHAP analysis provided model-specific interpretability and illustrated the relative contribution of selected predictors. This approach may help support pretreatment risk stratification and inform multidisciplinary discussions. However, given the retrospective single-center design, modest sample size, and lack of external validation, the findings should be interpreted cautiously and require validation in larger multicenter cohorts before clinical implementation.
