A benchmarking of genomic selection models for predicting grain‐yield related traits using haplotype‐based and genome‐wide association study‐based markers in rice
Authors: Xiankang Hu, Liu Xian, Guangping Cao, Shuoran Liu, Di Wang, Yanru Che, Weichao Huang, Yanhui Lin, Liqiang He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRice (Oryza sativa) is an important staple food, feeding more than half of the global population. A feasible improvement of rice yield is necessary to meet the ever–growing food demands. Genomic selection (GS), as an advanced breeding technique, enables the prediction of phenotypes solely based on genotypic data using a constructed genomic prediction model. A benchmarking study is required to evaluate the prediction performance of various available GS models for accelerating the breeding process. To address this concern, a diverse population containing 688 rice accessions with 66,456 haplotype‐based single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) (HA‐SNPs) were used to construct 17 GS models for grain length, grain width, and 1000‐grain weight separately. The effect of population size on the GS prediction accuracy was slightly greater than that of marker size. Among these models, ridge regression best linear unbiased prediction (RRBLUP) offered several advantages in terms of robustness, simplicity, and computational efficiency. Regarding the predictive ability, machine learning and deep learning models are also viable alternatives. Additionally, the prediction accuracy of genome‐wide association study–detected SNP based GS was significantly higher than that of HA‐SNP based GS. Furthermore, a subset including 63 selected accessions was used to construct the RRBLUP model, and followed by the evaluation of elite germplasm for practical breeding. Taken together, these findings have the potential to reduce the rice yield breeding cycle and enhance the genetic gain.

INTRODUCTION

Rice (Oryza sativa) is one of the essential staple foods, feeding more than half of the global population. It provides carbon‐hydrates, proteins, and vitamins for human consumption (Prom‐u‐thai & Rerkasem, 2020; Xing & Zhang, 2010). Currently, to meet the ever‐growing food demand worldwide, increasing grain yield remains to be the primary goal in rice genetic improvement. As a highly important and complex trait, rice grain yield can be primarily divided into three components: number of panicles, number of grains per panicle, and grain weight/1000‐grain weight (TGW) (Y. Li et al., 2023; C. Liu et al., 2024; Sakamoto & Matsuoka, 2008; Xing & Zhang, 2010; X. Lu et al., 2023). In addition to the progress achieved in the panicle number and grain number per panicle, improving the grain weight/TGW has becoming an effective approach to enhance the grain yield (X. Chen et al., 2021; Hernández‐Soto et al., 2021; Jiang et al., 2024; G. Li et al., 2021). Briefly, TGW is largely attributed to grain size/shape and the rate of grain filling. The grain length (GL), grain width (GW), and grain thickness are three determinants of the grain size/shape. Furthermore, the grain shape contributes to grain quality and commodity value, such as endosperm chalkiness, eating and cooking quality, and consumer preference (Tan et al., 2000; X. Lu et al., 2023).

With the rapid advancement of nucleotide sequencing and molecular biology, rice breeding practice was transformed from labor‐intensive phenotypic selection to the cost‐effective genomic selection (GS). Conventional cross‐breeding for the improved rice cultivars largely depends on the pedigree‐based selection and phenotypic selection of elite germplasms, which is labor‐consuming and time‐costing, especially for a large‐scale breeding program on rice grain‐yield‐related traits (J. Xu, Xing, et al., 2021; Zheng et al., 2024). Benefiting from the emergence of nucleotide sequencing technology, a limited amount of genetic information has been obtained and used for the selection in rice breeding. The valuable information provided by genetic markers enabled breeders to select superior individuals based on their genomic marker composition. However, various limitations in selection efficiency were present (Alemu et al., 2024; Crossa et al., 2017). In the last few decades, the detection and application of a large scale of genetic markers facilitated the breeding selection on genome level, such as the quantitative trait loci (QTLs) identified by the linkage mapping based on the biparental population. However, QTL mapping is constrained by low resolution, insufficient genetic variation, and the requirement to develop a biparental population (Alemu et al., 2024; Bernardo, 2008; Soller & Plotkin‐Hazan, 1977; Varshney et al., 2021). Currently, the genome‐wide association study (GWAS) is commonly used to identify the QTLs and closely linked molecular markers of target traits in a powerful and reliable manner (Tibbs Cortes et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2008). Using GWAS approach, QTLs with large and small effects underlying a complex trait are detected simultaneously (He et al., 2024; He et al., 2023; He et al., 2022; He et al., 2018; Luo, 2015; Sui et al., 2023; Tibbs Cortes et al., 2021). In practice, the major QTLs were utilized for the marker‐assisted selection, while several minor QTLs remained unstudied and unused (Jannink et al., 2010; J. Xu, Xing, et al., 2021; Zainuddin et al., 2024). As the expeditious development of advanced genome sequencing technology, the genetic variations in plants are well detected on the genomic scale. Genome‐wide single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are readily available for the GS breeding programs (J. Xu, Xing, et al., 2021). As an efficient and accurate scheme, GS relies on the genomic prediction (GP) through the statistical or machine learning (ML) model (Meuwissen et al., 2001). Using the accessions with known genotypic data and observed phenotypic data in the training set (TRS), the genomic estimated breeding values (GEBVs) are able to be predicted by GP model for the validation set, which only has known genotypic data. In contrast to other breeding methods above, previous studies have confirmed that GS combined with reproductive techniques can remarkably increase the genetic gain and dramatically reduce the breeding cycle in a low‐cost, accurate, time‐saving manner (Crossa et al., 2010; Crossa et al., 2017; Edwards et al., 2019; Y. Xu, Ma, et al., 2021).

To estimate the mathematical relationships between genotypic and phenotypic data in the TRS, the statistical methods play a pivotal role in GP model construction. Briefly, using a statistical or ML model, the data in the TRS were trained, processed, and analyzed for GP performance (Alemu et al., 2024; O. Montesinos‐Lopez et al., 2022; Montesinos Lopez et al., 2023; Tong & Nikoloski, 2021). In the application of plant GP, a major challenge is the large p small n (p >> n) phenomenon, which represents a population containing far more DNA markers (predictors or p) per individual than one observation value (n) per trait. Hence, different penalization (regularization) methods were proposed to estimate the whole genome marker effects (de Los Campos et al., 2013; Meuwissen et al., 2001; O. Montesinos‐Lopez et al., 2022). In general, according to the differences of the algorithm, these statistical models can be classified as parametric, semiparametric, and nonparametric methods (Alemu et al., 2024; O. Montesinos‐Lopez et al., 2022). Of them, parametric models assume that the relationship between independent (predictor) variables and dependent (response) variables follows a predefined functional form. These models include parsimonious linear, mixed linear, Bayesian, penalized regression models, such as partial least squares (PLS), genomic best linear unbiased prediction (GBLUP), ridge regression best linear unbiased prediction (RRBLUP), BayesA, BayesB, BayesC, least absolute shrinkage selection operator (LASSO), Bayesian LASSO, Bayesian ridge regression (BRR), and elastic net (EN) (Alemu et al., 2024; Crossa et al., 2025; O. Montesinos‐Lopez et al., 2022; Y. Xu, Ma, et al., 2021). On the contrary, nonparametric models are not based on the assumption of the function or distribution of underlying data. These models fit to several data patterns and provide more flexibility via the model structure determined by the data, which is available for treating with the data with unknown or complex relationships (Crossa et al., 2025; O. Montesinos‐Lopez et al., 2022). Nonparametric models primarily contain ML and deep learning (DL) methods, such as random forest, support vector machine, convolutional neural network (CNN), gradient boosting machine, and others (Alemu et al., 2024; Crossa et al., 2025; Y. Xu, Ma, et al., 2021). Besides, semi‐parametric models are the combined technique of parametric and nonparametric model, which contains a fraction of predictors adhere to the predetermined mathematical forms, while the rest part of predictors are not, such as reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) model (Alemu et al., 2024; Y. Xu, Ma, et al., 2021). Apart from the statistical model, the molecular marker dataset is also a considerable factor in GP. A proportion of studies adopt all the SNPs distributed in the plant genome as genomic data, while various prefer the GWAS‐detected SNPs (GWAS‐GS), which was proved to achieve a higher GP accuracy (Akutsu et al., 2023; Arenas et al., 2021; He et al., 2019; Lan et al., 2020; Y. Lu et al., 2025). Additionally, the SNPs harbored in the haplotype blocks (also known as haplotype‐based single nucleotide polymorphisms [HA‐SNPs]) across the plant genome serve as an available alternative for increasing the genetic gain in GP model as well (Ballesta et al., 2019; Bhat et al., 2021; Weber et al., 2023). However, the potential of HA‐SNPs for multi‐model GP of rice grain‐yield related traits remains unclear.

As food demand continues to grow due to the increasing global population, accelerating the rice yield breeding process in a high‐efficiency and low‐cost manner has become an important concern. To lay a theoretical foundation for real rice breeding decisions, a total of 17 methods were adopted to build GP models for grain‐yield related traits, based on a diverse population of 688 rice accessions with 66,456 HA‐SNPs. These target traits included GL, GW, and TGW. Compared to the previously reported studies, in addition to classical statistical GS models, two recently released DL methods (deep neural network for genomic prediction [DNNGP] and Cropformer) were implemented in this study (H. Wang et al., 2025; K. Wang et al., 2023). The DNNGP model has a multilayered hierarchical structure that can dynamically learns features from raw data. It avoids overfitting and improves the convergence rate by using a batch normalization layer, early stopping, and rectified linear activation functions (K. Wang et al., 2023). Cropformer, with enhanced precision and robustness, is a DL framework that combines CNNs with multiple self‐attention mechanisms to improve prediction accuracy. Its ability to predict complex phenotypic traits was extensively assessed across several traits in rice, maize, wheat, foxtail millet, and tomato (H. Wang et al., 2025). Using 10‐fold cross‐validation scheme, the prediction accuracy and computing time were obtained and compared across different models. The present study aimed to assess the potential of HA‐SNPs in GS breeding (such as HA‐GS versus GWAS‐GS), examine the GP performance influenced by distinct marker sizes and population sizes, construct a relatively realistic GS model, and further identify the elite germplasms for yield improvement in future rice breeding programs.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant sample, phenotyping, and sequencing

As described in previous study (J. Chen et al., 2024; X. Li et al., 2020), a diverse set of 705 rice accessions were planted in multiple locations across two consecutive years in China, which included 2015 in Beijing (39.90° N, 116.40° E), 2016 in Lingshui (18.48° N, 110.02° E), 2015 in Wenjiang (30.97° N, 103.81° E), and 2015 in Yangzhou (32.39° N, 119.42° E). These accessions were planted in each location with at least one season in each year. In this study, 688 accessions were selected for the assessments of the grain‐yield related traits, such as rice GL, GW, and TGW. For the phenotyping of these traits in the laboratory, three randomly adopted samples of each accession were measured with the automatic grain test instrument (Wanshen SC‐G), and their mean value was used for further analysis. In addition, the young leaf of each accession was used for the DNA extraction, followed by the 450 ± 50 bp paired‐end sequencing library construction. Raw data of whole genome sequencing were generated by Illumina Hi‐Seq sequencing system (Illumina). After removing the adaptors and low‐quality reads, approximately an average of 2.5 Gb data were retained for each accession.

Phenotypic data analysis of grain‐yield related traits

In order to account for environmental variation, using the R package lme4, the best linear unbiased prediction (BLUP) datasets were generated for the GL, GW, and TGW traits, respectively (Bates et al., 2015). Based on these three BLUP datasets of 688 rice accessions, the descriptive statistical analyses were performed through the implementation of the R package psych (v2.5.6, https://personality‐project.org/r/psych/). The correlation relationship of GL, GW, and TGW datasets was analyzed by the R package corrplot (v0.95, https://github.com/taiyun/corrplot).

The formula for the estimation of broad‐sense heritability H 2: H2=σG2σG2+σε2 where the σG2 is genotypic variance and σε2 is the residual variance.

Genotypic data analysis

As the previous study described (J. Chen et al., 2024; X. Li et al., 2020), using the MEM function of BWA software (0.7.17) (H. Li & Durbin, 2009), high‐quality sequence data were mapped to the Nipponbare rice reference genome (MSU v7.0, http://rice.uga.edu/index.shtml). SAMtools (v0.1.19) was employed to sort the BAM files, remove duplicates, and screen the rest reads with the mapping quality threshold Q30. Using the UnifiedGenotyper function of Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK, v3.4‐46) (McKenna et al., 2010), 7,048,490 high‐quality SNPs with “PASS” labels by GATK, missing rate <0.2, minor allele frequency (MAF) > 0.05 were retained for further analysis. The obtained high‐quality SNP dataset was subjected to the genotype imputation by BEAGLE software (v5.2). Haplotype blocks of rice genome were analyzed by the confidence interval algorithm of the Haploview software (v4.1). To define the pairs of SNPs in the strong linkage disequilibrium (LD), the upper limit of the 95% confidence interval of normalized disequilibrium coefficient (D') value was higher than 0.98, and the lower limit had a minimum D' value of 0.7 (Gabriel et al., 2002). A total of 66,456 HA‐SNPs in 43,147 haplotype blocks were obtained for the following analysis. The SNP density was plotted by the R package CMplot (v4.5.1). To estimate the population stratification, the principal component analysis (PCA) was implemented by the PLINK software (v1.9, https://zzz.bwh.harvard.edu/plink/) based on the high‐quality HA‐SNPs above.

GS and cross‐validation

According to the model classification reported in previous study (Alemu et al., 2024), 17 GS models were used for the comparisons of the prediction performance of the grain‐yield related traits, which included 10 parametric models (BayesA, BayesB, BayesC, BRR, LASSO, RRBLUP, sparse partial least squares [SPLS], GBLUP, ridge regression [RR] and EN), one semi‐parametric model (RKHS), four machine ML models (support vector classification [SVC], support vector regression [SVR], random forest classification [RFC], and random forest regression [RFR]), and two DL model (Cropformer and DNNGP) in the present study. Of these models, BayesA, BayesB, BayesC, BRR, LASSO, RRBLUP, SPLS, RKHS, SVC, SVR, RFC, and RFR were implemented by the R package G2P (v1.1.0, https://github.com/cma2015/G2P). The EN and GBLUP GS models were performed by the R package BWGS and BGLR, respectively (Charmet et al., 2020; Perez & de los Campos, 2014). The aforementioned methods were performed using default parameters. DNNGP and Cropformer GS models were built by the recently released python packages (H. Wang et al., 2025; K. Wang et al., 2023). The key settings for DNNGP method were used as follows: –batch_size 17 –patience 5 –lr 0.03 –dropout2 0.3 –seed 123 –epoch 100 –cv 10 –part 1 –earlystopping 10. Similarly, the major options for Cropformer method were listed here: learning_rate = 0.001, batch_size = 32, hidden_dim = 64, output_dim = 1, kernel_size = 3, patience = 5. To evaluate the prediction performance of these GS models, the optimal GS model of grain‐yield related traits was considered by the relatively high prediction accuracy with short computing time. Furthermore, the impact of marker size and population size on the prediction accuracy was investigated by various tests. Several subsets of 500, 1000, 2000, 3000, 5000, 7500, 10,000, 15,000, 30,000, and 50,000 HA‐SNP markers were randomly selected for the test of prediction accuracy by the optimal GS model above. Likewise, randomly selected subsets containing 50, 100, 200, 300, 400, 500, and 600 accessions were used for the test of prediction accuracy by the previously defined optimal GS model. In this study, a widely used cross‐validation scheme was employed for the benchmark study of GS model prediction performance. The 10‐fold cross‐validation strategy was used with 30 replicates. The Pearson correlation coefficient (also known as r) between the true breeding values and the GEBVs in the validation set was used to estimate the prediction accuracy of the certain GS model. Hence, a high Pearson correlation coefficient was accordingly indicative of strong predictive ability.

Genome‐wide association analysis

A total of 2,191,997 SNPs were filtered (‐maf 0.05 and ‐geno 0.1 settings) and used as the genotypic data for the GWAS by the PLINK software (v1.9, https://zzz.bwh.harvard.edu/plink/). Based on these high‐quality SNPs, the GWAS of the GL, GW, and TGW traits were performed by the generalized linear model (GLM) in the R package rMVP (1.1.1) (Yin et al., 2021). To determine the marker‐trait association, the Bonferroni correction was used to define the significant association signal with p‐value = 2.28 × 10−8 (p‐value = 0.05/m, where m is the number of SNPs used in the analysis). The Manhattan and QQ plots were visualized by the R package CMplot (v4.5.1).

Phenotyping of rice grain‐yield traits

In this study, a total of 688 rice accessions were analyzed for the measurement of the rice grain‐yield traits, which contained rice GL, GW, and TGW. Descriptive statistics analysis showed GL varies from 6.24 to 10.67 mm, GW is from 2.09 to 3.81 mm, and TGW ranges from 16.11 to 34.81 g, respectively. Accordingly, small coefficients of variation (CVs) were observed for GL (14.02%), GW (11.61%), and TGW (11.05%) (Table 1; Figure 1A–C). Additionally, a significantly negative correlation between GL and GW traits was observed with r = −0.77, a significantly positive correlation TGW and GL was detected with r = 0.49, and a low coefficient of correlation with 0.06 was also observed between TGW and GW traits (Figure 1D). The broad‐sense heritability of GL, GW, and TGW traits was 0.96, 0.93, and 0.83, respectively (Table 1).

Genotyping of rice population

A set of 66,456 SNPs detected from the haplotype blocks in the rice genome was used for further analysis in the present study. These SNPs were designated as HA‐SNPs hereafter. For the density and distribution of these HA‐SNPs, the number of HA‐SNPs across the 12 chromosomes in rice ranged from 4307 (chromosome 9) to 7235 (chromosome 11), and the average interval of the pairwise SNPs was from 4.00 kb (chromosome 11) to 6.53 kb (chromosome 3) (Table 2; Figure 2A). This ensured the adequate markers for the following analysis. In order to estimate the population structure, a PCA was performed on these 688 rice accessions. This population was mainly divided into four subpopulations, which contained 355 temperate japonica (accounted for 52% of 688 accessions), 291 indica (42%), 36 intermediate (5%), and 6 tropical japonica accessions (1%) (Figure 2B).

Evaluation of the prediction performance of different GS models

To evaluate the phenotypic prediction performance of GS models, a total of 17 GS models were built for grain‐yield related traits. Based on the differences of algorithm, these models were classified into three types, which contained 10 parametric models (BayesA, BayesB, BayesC, BRR, LASSO, RRBLUP, SPLS, GBLUP, RR, and EN), one semi‐parametric model (RKHS), four ML models (SVC, SVR, RFC, and RFR), and two DL models (Cropformer and DNNGP). In the present study, prediction accuracy and computing time of each GS model for each trait were investigated separately. For the GL trait, the prediction accuracy of these 17 GS models ranged from 0.7834 to 0.8979 (Figure 3A; Table 3; Table S1). For example, a highest prediction accuracy was observed in the EN model with 0.8979, while the lowest prediction accuracy was yielded by the SPLS model with 0.7834 (Figure 3A). Accordingly, the computing time of phenotypic prediction by these 17 GS models were from 0.49 to 21.93 h (Figure 3A; Table S2). The shortest computing time was observed in the SPLS model with 0.49 h, whereas the longest computing time was cost by the RFR model with 21.93 h (Figure 3A). For the GW trait, the prediction accuracy of these GS models ranged from 0.7545 to 0.8789 (Figure 3B; Table 3; Table S1). For instance, the highest prediction accuracy was observed in the EN model with 0.8789, while the lowest prediction accuracy was generated by the SPLS model with 0.7545 (Figure 3B). Additionally, the computing time of phenotypic prediction by these GS models was from 0.49 to 20.35 h (Figure 3B; Table S2). The shortest computing time was seen in the SPLS model with 0.49 h, whereas the longest computing time was cost by the RFR model with 20.35 h (Figure 3B). For the TGW trait, the prediction accuracy of these GS models varied from 0.1615 to 0.5388 (Figure 3C; Table 3; Table S1). The highest prediction accuracy was indicated in the EN model with 0.5388, while the lowest prediction accuracy was generated by the SPLS model with 0.1615 (Figure 3C). On the other side, the computing time of phenotypic prediction by these GS models ranged from 0.44 to 22.20 h (Figure 3C; Table S2). For example, the shortest computing time was found in the SPLS model with 0.44 h, whereas the longest computing time was cost by the RFR model with 22.20 h (Figure 3C). Overall, the CVs of the prediction accuracy of 17 GS models were 2.54% for GL, 3.40% for GW, and 26.21% for TGW. Besides, the CVs of the computing time of 17 GS models were 120.84% for GL, 115.92% for GW, and 116.29% for TGW. Furthermore, the RRBLUP model with relatively high prediction accuracy (0.8383 for GL, 0.8147 for GW, and 0.2985 for TGW) and relatively short computing time (0.60 h for GL, 0.66 h for GW, and 0.69 h for TGW) was selected as the optimal GS model for the following study (Figure 3A–C; Table 3).

GS prediction accuracy affected by the marker size and population size

In order to estimate the impact of marker size and population size on the GS prediction accuracy for grain‐yield related traits, rice population with 688 accessions and its subsets were used for building the RRBLUP model. In general, a consecutively increasing prediction accuracy was observed, and the prediction accuracy reached a plateau at 0.9073 in the 688 accessions with a marker size of 50,000 HA‐SNPs for GL trait (Figure 4A; Figure S1A; Table S3). Similarly, the highest prediction accuracy 0.8870 was shown in the 688 accessions with 30,000 HA‐SNPs in marker size for GW trait (Figure 4B; Figure S1B; Table S3). Additionally, the highest prediction accuracy reached up to 0.3170 was observed in the 688 accessions with 7500 HA‐SNPs in marker size for TGW trait (Figure 4C; Figure S1C; Table S3). Notably, the prediction performance of GS model improved as the population size increased. For instance, the highest prediction accuracy of GL (0.9113) and GW (0.8791) traits were both generated from the 600 accessions in population size (Figure 4D,E; Figure S1D,E; Table S4). However, the highest prediction accuracy was up to 0.5991, which was observed in the 400 accessions for TGW trait (Figure 4F; Figure S1F; Table S4).

Comparison of prediction accuracy between HA‐GS and GWAS‐GS

GWAS, as a powerful strategy for causal loci mapping, is widely applied for the genetic dissection and molecular breeding. The SNPs significantly associated with certain trait (significant SNPs detected by GWAS, denoted as GWAS‐SNP hereafter) is also used for GS model construction. In this study, a total of 10,007, 9112, and 1220 GWAS‐SNPs were identified for GL, GW, and TGW traits through the GLM model (Figure S2A–C; Table S5). Furthermore, using these GWAS‐SNPs, three RRBLUP GS models were constructed. As a result, the prediction accuracy of GL, GW, and TGW traits was 0.8918, 0.8286, and 0.5520, respectively (Table S6). Compared with the results obtained by HA‐GS, the prediction accuracies of GWAS‐GS were significantly higher than those yielded by HA‐SNP GS for GL, GW, and TGW traits (Figure 5A–C). To analyze whether the prediction performance of GS model affected by the marker size, RRBLUP models were built based on the total GWAS‐SNP dataset and its subsets for each trait. Interestingly, the prediction performance of GS model enhanced as the population size increased. Hence, the highest prediction accuracy of GL (0.8918) and TGW (0.5520) were observed in the full marker size (10,007 GWAS‐SNPs for GL and 1220 GWAS‐SNPs for TGW) (Figure 6A,C; Figure S3A,C; Table S6). However, the highest prediction accuracy (0.8380) was generated by 3000 GWAS‐SNPs in marker size for GW trait (Figure 6B; Figure S3B; Table S6).

Evaluation of the potential of the optimized GS model for breeding application

To estimate the potential the GS model for the yield breeding program in rice, a subpopulation of 688 accessions was selected. According to HA‐SNP GS results above, a subset which including 63 accessions was screened using the intersection of the optimal GS population size of GL (600 accessions), GW (600 accessions), and TGW (400 accessions) traits. These 63 rice accessions comprised of 36 temperate japonica, 24 indica, two intermediate, and one tropical japonica accessions. Correlation analysis showed that a significantly positive correlation between GL and TGW (r = 0.69) traits, a significantly negative correlation between GL and GW (r = −0.74) traits, and a low correlation between GW and TGW (r = −0.15) traits (Figure S4). Subsequently, three RRBLUP GS models were constructed. The prediction accuracy of these models was up to 0.9141 for GL, 0.7670 for GW, and 0.6566 for TGW (Table S7). Based on these models, the phenotypic prediction across grain‐yield related traits were performed. The R 2 of the linear regression carried out between the predicted GL value and the observed TGW value was 0.39, and the R 2 of the predicted GW value and the observed TGW was 0.34 (Figure 7A,B). The R 2 of the linear regression conducted between the predicted GW value and the observed GL value was 0.81, and the R 2 of the observed GL and the predicted TGW value was 0.77 (Figure 7C,D). The R 2 of the linear regression analyzed between the observed GW value and the predicted TGW value was 0.48, and the R 2 of the observed GW and the predicted GL value was 0.56 (Figure 7E,F). The R 2 of the linear regression performed between the predicted GL value and the predicted TGW value was 0.90, and the R 2of the predicted GW and the predicted TGW value was 0.79 (Figure 7G,H). Additionally, the highest R 2 value with 0.95 was observed between the predicted GW value and the predicted GL (Figure 7I). Furthermore, five elite varieties, with relatively stable and favorable phenotype across diffident environments, were screened for the molecular breeding of high‐yield rice. Of these, the 1701 and YCX369 (ID) accession are temperate japonica rice, yangzhou_199_148 is tropical japonica rice, yangzhou_199_195 is intermediate type, and L13 is indica rice (Table S8).

DISCUSSION

Due to the algorithm differences between GS models, the predictive performance for each grain‐yield related traits varies accordingly. In this study, the 17 GS models were grouped into three types, which consisted of 10 parametric models (BayesA, BayesB, BayesC, BRR, LASSO, RRBLUP, SPLS, GBLUP, RR, and EN), one semi‐parametric model (RKHS), four ML models (SVC, SVR, RFC, and RFR), and two DL models (Cropformer and DNNGP). From the prediction accuracy perspective, using 688 accessions accompanied with 66,456 HA‐SNPs, in addition to SPLS model, comparable prediction accuracies were observed across the parametric, semi‐parametric, and nonparametric models (Figure 3A–C; Table S1). Of these parametric models, the Bayesian and BLUP series methods yielded the relatively high and stable prediction accuracies, especially for RRBLUP. On the other side, the outstanding prediction accuracies were obtained by the recently released DL model Cropformer (Figure 3A–C). Among the nonparametric models, compared with the prediction accuracies achieved by ML models (RFR, RFC, SVR, and SVC), DL models were relatively unstable on the prediction accuracy when performed for different grain‐yield related traits. Previous studies reported consistent findings that the prediction accuracy of parametric model is slightly higher than nonparametric model or comparable with nonparametric model (Azodi et al., 2019; A. Montesinos‐Lopez et al., 2025; Yin et al., 2020). Additionally, ML and DL models were proposed to handle complex datasets with multiple features (Azodi et al., 2019; Bayer & Edwards, 2021; Crossa et al., 2025; A. Montesinos‐Lopez et al., 2025; Perez‐Enciso & Zingaretti, 2019; Y. Xu, Ma, et al., 2021). Consequently, it is suggested that ML and DL GS models might serve as an alternative to classical statistical models when considering scenarios involving a population of 688 rice accessions with 66,456 HA‐SNPs or datasets of similar scale. From the computing time perspective, during GS model construction and 10‐fold cross‐validation, most nonparametric and semi‐parametric models cost longer time than parametric models. Of the parametric models, RRBLUP ranked a close second generally. Among nonparametric models, the GS conducted by SVR and SVC took remarkably shorter time than other ML and DL models. However, the running time of the only semi‐parametric model RKHS was relatively long. Overall, in terms of computing time of GS, the parametric models are generally superior to nonparametric and semi‐parametric models, with the exception of SVC and SVR. Similar findings were reported in the previous studies (Crossa et al., 2025; Lourenco et al., 2024; O. Montesinos‐Lopez et al., 2021). In summary, comparable GP accuracy was demonstrated among parametric, semi‐parametric, and nonparametric models, although semi‐parametric and nonparametric models generally require longer running times than parametric ones. To comprehensively consider both prediction accuracy and running time, based on the findings this study, RRBLUP is accurate, efficient, and therefore preferred for the practical breeding programs. However, for a sustainably long‐term breeding gain, breeders need to simultaneously take the robustness, simplicity, and computational efficiency of the adopted GP/GS model into account. The prediction accuracy of GS is commonly determined by several factors, such as statistical model, marker size, population size, population structure, trait complexity, and so on (Alemu et al., 2024; Norman et al., 2018; Y. Xu, Ma, et al., 2021). Of these key factors, the impacts of marker size and population size on the prediction accuracy of GS are primarily investigated in this study. For GL, GW, and TGW traits, an improved GS prediction accuracy resulted from an increase of HA‐SNPs number when the marker size is 50,000 for GL, 30,000 for GW, and 7500 for TGW. Correspondingly, the prediction accuracy was up to 0.9073 for GL, 0.8870 for GW, and 0.3170 for TGW (Figure S1A–C; Table S3). These prediction accuracies were all higher than those with full marker size (66,456 HA‐SNPs), which indicated the 66,456 HA‐SNPs remained containing the redundant variation information in spite of the HA‐SNPs obtained by the LD screening. The combined utilization of various methods for co‐detecting HA‐SNPs may pave the way for improved prediction accuracy in future studies. Regarding the impact of population size on GS prediction accuracy, the effect of increasing population size on enhancing prediction accuracy was slightly greater than that of increasing marker size. The prediction accuracy of GL with 600 accessions, GW with 600 accessions, and TGW with 400 accessions achieved 0.9113, 0.8791, and 0.5991, respectively (Figure S1D,F,G; Table S4). It implied the representative subpopulation consisting of 600 accessions for GL, 600 accessions for GW, and 400 accessions for TGW has the potential to replace the total population (688 accessions) in improved grain yield breeding. Importantly, this pattern strongly suggested that GL (H 2 = 0.96) and GW (H 2 = 0.93) are highly heritable traits with simpler population structure, whereas TGW (H 2 = 0.83) trait is more complex. To estimate the differences of prediction accuracy caused by population structure, the component of subsets aforementioned were analyzed. This whole population included 355 temperate japonica (accounted for 52% of 688 accessions), 291 indica (42%), 36 intermediate (5%), and six tropical japonica accessions (1%). For GL, the subpopulation (600 accessions) contained 350 temperate japonica (accounted for 58% of 600 accessions), 224 indica (37%), 25 intermediate (4%), and one tropical japonica accessions (1%). For TGW, the subpopulation (400 accessions) was composed of 246 temperate japonica (62%), 132 indica (33%), 17 intermediate (4%), and five tropical japonica accessions (1%). As to GW, the subset (600 accessions) comprised of 350 temperate japonica (58%), 224 indica (37%), 25 intermediate (4%), and one tropical japonica accessions (1%). Regarding the composition of the rice population in this study, the decreasing proportion of intermediate and tropical japonica accessions had dramatically improved the prediction ability of the GS model for TGW (0.5991 for 400 accessions vs. 0.2985 for 688 accessions), GL (0.9113 for 600 accessions vs. 0.8383 for 688 accessions), and GW (0.8791 for 600 accessions vs. 0.8147 for 688 accessions) traits. For example, when the population size is 600, intermediate and tropical japonica rice accounted for 5.17% and 0.83%; when the population size is 400, they accounted for 5.00% and 1.25%; whereas the population size is 688, they accounted for 5.23% and 0.87%. The complexity of components of the given population may have a negative effect on prediction ability of GS model. Notably, consistent with the prediction accuracies obtained using the optimal population size, compared with the results from full population size (688 accessions), a significant improvement in prediction ability was also achieved in 63 selected accessions for GL (0.9141 for 63 accessions vs. 0.8383 for 688 accessions) and TGW (0.6566 for 63 accessions vs. 0.2985 for 688 accessions) traits. Several published studies confirmed the population structure partially affect the prediction accuracy of GS breeding (Gibbs et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2014; Norman et al., 2018; Werner et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2006). For instance, limitations for breeding program may be present if the same population is used for training and validation. The GP models generally influence by the population structure within the training population or between training population and breeding population. The negative or adverse impact of population structure on GP accuracy has found in crops (Isidro et al., 2015; Werner et al., 2020; Würschum et al., 2017). To take the population structure into account, a significant reduction in GP accuracy was reported in various previous studies (Callister et al., 2022; Norman et al., 2018; Werner et al., 2020). Furthermore, given the wide spectrum of genetic variants underlying the target traits, a training population with diverse genetic backgrounds ensures that GP models can precisely estimate genetic effects and generate reliable predictions (Alemu et al., 2024; Norman et al., 2018). However, when considering the training population developed for the breeding population, a negative effect on GP accuracy was reported. This is because the increasing diversity of the training population makes it genetically distant from the breeding population (Akdemir & Isidro‐Sanchez, 2019; Alemu et al., 2024; Berro et al., 2019; Crossa et al., 2014). Moreover, the TRS is a dynamic population that is necessary to be updated with the new field trial data during the breeding cycle (Akdemir & Isidro‐Sanchez, 2019; Lorenz & Smith, 2015; Pszczola & Calus, 2016). As a result, to achieve optimal prediction accuracy, the component of the GS training and breeding population is crucial and need to be fully considered in practice.

Recently, in order to accelerate the breeding process, the emerged GS/GP approach takes advantages of the genome‐wide SNP markers to capture overall genetic effects in crops. In this study, the LD screened HA‐SNPs were used for the evaluation of GS prediction performance. However, the prediction accuracies yielded by GWAS‐detected SNPs (GWAS‐SNPs) are significantly higher than those by full size HA‐SNPs across GL, GW, and TGW traits (Figure 5A–C; Table S6). Various previous studies have reported that GS prediction accuracy obtained by GWAS‐SNP surpasses those achieved by whole‐genome SNPs or SNPs simply correlated with a given trait (Crossa et al., 2025; He et al., 2019; Y. Liu et al., 2023; Y. Lu et al., 2025; Roy et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024). Leveraging the power and robustness of GWAS‐SNPs enables the improvement of GS prediction accuracy to a higher level. Although the relatively high prediction accuracy achieved by the RRBLUP model using 66,456 HA‐SNPs, the prediction ability could be further improved through the application of more advanced techniques for identifying a superior set of HA‐SNPs in future studies. To assess GWAS marker size affecting GS prediction accuracy, a number of subsets of all GWAS‐SNPs were used for performing GP. In addition to GW trait, the prediction accuracy of GL and TGW traits improved with the increasing marker size, with the highest accuracy observed using the full set of GWAS‐SNPs (Figure S3A,C). However, the highest prediction accuracy of GW trait reached up to 0.8380 with the marker size of 3000 (Figure S3B). In general, the greater the number of GWAS‐SNPs input, the higher the prediction accuracy achieved (He et al., 2019; Y. Lu et al., 2025; You et al., 2022). The slightly higher prediction accuracy yielded by 3000 GWAS‐SNPs (0.8380) compared to the full set 9112 GWAS‐SNPs (0.8286) may be attributed to the presence of some redundant SNPs located in the same loci. Theoretically, the prediction accuracy generated by GWAS‐GS represents a best‐case upper bound. However, genome‐wide HA‐SNPs is a set of screened markers through a predefined LD threshold, which eliminates a certain amount of redundant linkage information. Using HA‐GS approach, the computational burden of the GP model is more or less reduced. To pursue an accurate and fast GP, the selection of HA‐SNPs or GWAS‐SNPs also depends on factors such as the scale of breeding population, the size of SNP dataset, available computational resource and so on. Overall, to some extent, reasonably incorporating the GS model with GWAS‐SNPs or HA‐SNPs may facilitate achieving a higher prediction ability for crop breeding and further provide the methodological reference information for practical breeding.

Considering the cost and time invested in modern rice breeding, based on the obtained results in this study, a small subset screened from 688 accession was expected to improve the prediction efficiency for the practical breeding application. Hence, a subset including 63 accessions was selected using the intersection of the optimal GS population size of GL (600 accessions), GW (600 accessions), and TGW (400 accessions) traits. Subsequently, three RRBLUP models on the basis of 63 accessions with 66,456 HA‐SNPs were constructed. Interestingly, the prediction accuracy of GL (0.9141 vs. 0.8383) and TGW (0.6566 vs. 0.2985) traits were remarkably higher than that achieved using 688 accessions. In contrast, a slight decrease of prediction accuracy for GW (0.7670 vs. 0.8147) was observed (Figure 3A–C; Table S7). To study the relationship between the predicted breeding value and observed phenotypic value, nine simple linear regressions were conducted. Of note, the highest R 2 value with 0.81 was shown between predicted GW value and observed GL value (Figure 7C), followed by the R 2 value with 0.77 obtained between predicted TGW value and observed GL (Figure 7D). Additionally, the R 2 between predicted GL and observed TGW was 0.39 (Figure 7A), and the R 2 between predicted GW and observed TGW was 0.34 (Figure 7B). This indicated that these GS models have the potential for the cross‐prediction among grain‐yield related traits. Furthermore, to apply these GS models for elite germplasm evaluation, 63 accessions were analyzed using to the present models. Five elite accessions with relatively stable and favorable phenotype across different environmental conditions were identified, such as temperate japonica rice 1701 and YCX369, tropical japonica rice yangzhou_199_148, intermediate type yangzhou_199_195, and indica rice L13 (Table S8). These valuable resources hold the promise for improving grain yield through direct hybridization with modern cultivar in rice. Collectively, these new GS models based on 63 accessions provide a solid foundation for the efficient, accurate, and cost‐saving selection in rice breeding, thereby reducing the breeding cycle. In this study, the screened 63 accessions and their corresponding GS models may be utilized as an alternative strategy for parent selection, early‐generation screening, and training population optimization. To some extent, the referenced and valuable information is expected to be useful for breeders as a basis for decision‐making.
