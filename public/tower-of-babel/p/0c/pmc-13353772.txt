An evaluation of perivascular space burden and contrast enhancement on 7T MRI in multiple sclerosis
Authors: Yuxin Zeng, Austin Bertrand, Jiachen Zhuo, Daniel M. Harrison
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and Objectives:Changes to the perivascular space (PVS) may be associated with multiple sclerosis (MS) pathology. In this study, we aimed to quantify PVS burden and assess PVS contrast enhancement on 7T MRI in MS.Methods:Participants with MS (N = 100), other inflammatory neurological disorders (OIND, N = 12), and healthy controls (HC, N = 18) underwent 7T MRI. PVS segmentation was performed using an automated algorithm. Post-contrast FLAIR images were reviewed for gadolinium contrast-enhancing PVS (CE-PVS). The count, volume, and fraction of PVS and CE-PVS patterns were quantified.Results:Overall, 67% of participants were female, with the same proportion across HC, MS, and OIND groups. Median age was 41 years in HC, 46 years in MS, and 35 years in OIND. The segmentation algorithm achieved a median Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) of 0.994 compared to manually revised masks. PVS count, volume, and the proportion of PVS volume divided by total white matter (WM) volume did not differ significantly between individuals with and without MS. This finding was no different when restricting the analysis to only PVS in the large size category (> 2.65 mm3). CE-PVS were seen in 29% of MS, 11% of HC, and 8% of OIND, p = 0.431. Participants with progressive MS (PMS, N = 21) had greater PVS count (1341 (841, 1481)) than those with relapsing-remitting MS (RRMS, N = 79) (849 (571, 1283)), adjusted p = 0.037.Conclusions:7T MRI and an automated algorithm enabled accurate PVS quantification. Although trends towards increased baseline PVS burden and CE-PVS in MS were seen, these differences were not significant. An observed difference in PVS between PMS and RRMS across all time points suggests a potential biological distinction, though, which warrants further investigation.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic immune-mediated disease of the central nervous system characterized by inflammation, demyelination, and axonal loss. Autoreactive lymphocytes crossing the blood–brain barrier are thought to drive lesion formation and neurodegeneration. (Reich et al., 2018)

Perivascular spaces (PVS) are fluid-filled spaces surrounding central nervous system blood vessels. They serve important physiological functions, including clearance of metabolic waste, drainage of cerebrospinal and interstitial fluid, hosting immune cells for surveillance, and interacting with the blood–brain barrier (BBB) to regulate exchange and immune cell migration. (Carare et al., 2008; Ineichen et al., 2022)

The role of PVS in the pathogenesis of MS is an area of active research. PVS are believed to facilitate the exchange of nutrients and waste products between brain parenchyma and the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) (Carare et al., 2008; Kida et al., 1993; Bown et al., 2022). In MS, alterations in PVS could potentially disrupt these processes, contributing to the accumulation of harmful substances and exacerbating neuronal damage. (Ineichen et al., 2022; Haider et al., 2022; Vos et al., 2005) Moreover, PVS may be involved in MS pathology by serving as a site of inflammatory cell recruitment and proliferation, in addition undergoing alterations in size and shape due to neurodegeneration and alterations in glymphatic clearance. (Ineichen et al., 2022; Vos et al., 2005; Bakker et al., 2016; Carotenuto et al., 2022; Granberg et al., 2020) Pathological studies have further shown that enlarged PVS in MS are linked to vascular alterations and features of small vessel disease rather than direct immune infiltration, and that PVS changes may also relate to meningeal inflammation and cortical demyelination. (Ineichen et al., 2023; Magliozzi et al., 2025)

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a powerful tool for studying PVS in MS. The presence of enlarged perivascular spaces on MRI in some MS studies (Granberg et al., 2020) suggests the potential involvement of PVS in the disease. Despite the potential importance of PVS in MS, our understanding of these structures remains limited. This is partly due to the technical challenges associated with in vivo visualization of PVS. Their small size (ranging from 0.13–0.96 mm in diameter) (Pesce and Carli, 1988) makes it difficult to detect and accurately measure the full extent of PVS using conventional MRI techniques. The use of 7 Tesla (7T) MRI could help overcome these challenges, particularly due to improvements in signal-to-noise ratio and spatial resolution. (Zong et al., 2016) Thus, 7T MRI could be a powerful tool for quantification of PVS in MS and better characterization of their changes over time.

In recent years, a growing number of studies have attempted to quantify MRI-visible perivascular spaces (PVS) using semi-quantitative visual rating scales and, more recently, automated and AI-based segmentation methods. (GM Potter et al., 2015; Wardlaw et al., 2013; Duering et al., 2023; Sinclair et al., 2024) Traditional visual approaches such as the Potter scale assign ordinal burden scores within specific anatomical regions, typically basal ganglia and centrum semiovale, based on counted PVS per slice, (GM Potter et al., 2015) whereas consensus frameworks such as STRIVE and the updated STRIVE-2 guidelines define PVS as a marker of cerebral small vessel disease. (Wardlaw et al., 2013; Duering et al., 2023) Despite these efforts to standardize PVS evaluation, there remains considerable variation. Studies differ in the brain regions they assess, and in the numerical cut-offs used to classify PVS burden or distinguish enlarged PVS from lacunes. (GM Potter et al., 2015; Wardlaw et al., 2013; Duering et al., 2023; Adams et al., 2013; Bouvy et al., 2014) Automated and AI-based methods introduce additional variability, as they often rely on site-specific training data and use distinct preprocessing steps or network architectures, leading to quantitative estimates that are not always comparable across studies. (Sinclair et al., 2024; Boespflug et al., 2018; Dubost et al., 2019)

This methodological variability highlights the need for a validated and reproducible approach to PVS quantification that can be widely adopted and consistently interpreted. A robust, standardized framework would enhance inter-rater and inter-site reliability, enable more consistent interpretation of disease burden across studies, and links between neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration. (Adams et al., 2013; Bouvy et al., 2014; Dubost et al., 2019) With advances in MRI resolution, the need for standardization has become increasingly important. Manual identification of PVS is now prohibitively time-consuming, highly operator-dependent, and impractical for large datasets or timely analyses. (GM Potter et al., 2015; Sinclair et al., 2024; Dubost et al., 2019) Developing and validating a reliable and scalable quantification approach is therefore essential to maintain internal consistency and enable meaningful comparisons across studies in this rapidly expanding field. (Wardlaw et al., 2013; Duering et al., 2023; Sinclair et al., 2024)

In this study, we applied 7T MRI to perform a comprehensive evaluation of PVS in MS. We not only assessed the count and volume of PVS but also evaluated enhancement with a gadolinium-based contrast agent (GBCA), which could provide additional insights into vascular, lymphatic, or glymphatic clearance processes occurring within these spaces. (Taoka and Naganawa, 2018; Albayram et al., 2022)

Methods

Approvals and consents

Protocols were reviewed and approved by the institutional review boards at the University of Maryland, Baltimore and the Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine. All participants reviewed and signed written informed consent documents.

Study participants

Participants in this study included those with MS and healthy controls (HC). Participants were required to be between 18 and 65 years old at the time of enrollment. Participants with MS were recruited from the University of Maryland Center for Multiple Sclerosis Treatment and Research. Participants with MS were required to meet 2017 revised McDonald Criteria (Thompson et al., 2018) and included those with progressive forms of MS (PMS; including primary and secondary) and relapsing-remitting MS (RRMS). HC were recruited by advertisements and were approximately matched to MS participants for sex and age. Participants were excluded if they had a disease flare and/or steroid treatment within 30 days of enrollment, an allergy to gadolinium, or were unable to undergo MRI due to metallic implants or other devices. In addition, participants in the MS and HC groups were excluded if they had any other conditions that could affect the central nervous system.

Study visits

HC had only one visit, whereas participants with MS returned for annual visits. At each visit, demographics such as age, sex, as well as clinical data like treatment and disease duration were documented. Disability testing, including Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS), Timed 25-Foot Walk (T25W), Nine Hole Peg Test (NHPT), Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT), (Benedict et al., 2017) Paced Auditory Serial Addition Test (PASAT), (Rao et al., 1991) and Low Contrast Visual Acuity (LCVA), (Balcer et al., 2000) was conducted at each visit. T25FW was averaged over two trials and measured in seconds. NHPT was averaged over two trials for each hand and measured in seconds. SDMT and PASAT scores were corrected. LCVA was assessed as the number of correctly identified letters on 2.5 % contrast Sloan charts, averaged across both eyes. An MRI was acquired (details below) in addition to all disability tests on each study visit.

MRI acquisition

MRI of the brain was acquired on a 7T Phillips Achieva scanner (Phillips Healthcare, Best, The Netherlands) with an 8-channel, multitransmit and 32 channel receive head coil (NovaMedical, Wilmington, MA USA). Acquisitions included Magnetization Prepared 2 Rapid Gradient Echo (MP2RAGE) and a 3-dimensional (3D) Fluid Attenuated Inversion Recovery (FLAIR) before and after injection of gadoteridol (0.1mmol/kg). The MP2RAGE was initiated approximately 10 min after contrast injection and the FLAIR sequence was acquired approximately 23 min after contrast injection. Acquisition details are found in the Appendix.

Image processing

7T MRI images were processed to generate MP2RAGE T1-weighted (T1W) images using custom code. (Marques et al., 2010) FLAIR images underwent N4 inhomogeneity correction, (Tustison et al., 2010) and the corresponding T1W images were denoised by multiplying the image by the second inversion time image in the corresponding MP2RAGE acquisition. Denoised T1W images were used for co-registration, but the original T1W image was used for image analysis. Both the pre- and post-contrast FLAIR images were co-registered to the denoised pre-contrast T1W images following N4 inhomogeneity correction utilizing ANTs (Advanced Normalization Tools, version 1.10.14). (Avants et al., 2011; Tustison et al., 2021) Similar to our prior work on leptomeningeal enhancement (LME), (Harrison et al., 2017; Jonas et al., 2018) FLAIR subtraction maps were generated by subtracting the intensity values of the pre-contrast FLAIR image from the post-contrast image.

Image analysis

PVS were segmented using modification of an automated algorithm, which was previously validated in traumatic brain injury. (Zhuo et al., 2023) The pre-contrast MP2RAGE T1W went through sub-cortical segmentation with the FreeSurfer (version v6.0.1) followed by intensity normalization based on white-matter signal intensity using the histogram normalization module in the Quantitative Imaging Toolkit (QIT, https://github.com/cabeen/qit). Segmentation of PVS was performed utilizing modification of software and methodology described in Sepehrband et al. (2021). T1W images underwent filtration using an adaptive non-local mean filtering method, which was designed to eliminate non-structural high-frequency spatial noise. Subsequently, a Frangi filter was applied to the filtered T1W to create a vessel-likeliness map, followed by thresholding to produce PVS masks. Based on visual review of the images by authors DMH and JZ, a vesselness threshold of 0.00002 was chosen to segment the PVS voxels, striking a balance of false positive rate and false negative rate. FLAIR was used to mask out any PVS that occurred within a white matter hyperintensity (WMH), which was identified as the top 10th percentile of the FLAIR signal distribution. Then the PVS masks generated by algorithm were reviewed and manually edited by an expert reviewer (DMH), and images of poor quality were excluded from the study during the review. Any clusters of < 2 connected voxels were eliminated from analyses. See Fig. 1 for an example of PVS segmentation.

Separately, pre-contrast FLAIR, post-contrast FLAIR, and subtraction images were reviewed to generate the contrast-enhancing PVS (CE-PVS) mask. CE-PVS were defined as PVS with pre-contrast hypo-intensity on both T1W and 3D-FLAIR, but with hyperintensity in the same region on post-contrast 3D-FLAIR. CE-PVS in the deep gray, brainstem/cerebellum, and subcortical white matter regions were identified using three labels. Images of poor quality were excluded. Some examples of CE-PVS are shown in Fig. 2.

To obtain white matter lesion (WML) masks, the Lesion Prediction Algorithm (LPA) from the Lesion Segmentation Tool (LST) toolbox, (Schmidt et al., 2012) implemented in LST version 3.0.0 for SPM (www.statistical-modelling.de/lst.html), was applied to FLAIR images. Manual revisions of the algorithm-generated lesion masks were performed and WML count and volume were derived from the revised WML masks.

Statistical analysis

Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) was used to evaluate the degree of voxel overlap between automated and manually revised PVS masks. Overlap rate, defined as the ratio of PVS (defined as the cluster of voxels representing one PVS) overlapping by at least 1 voxel in both masks to PVS in the manually revised masks was also examined.

Age at each visit was calculated using the date of birth and visit date, while disease duration was computed as the difference between the year of first symptom onset and the year of the visit. Visit frequency; baseline demographics (sex, age, ethnicity); baseline clinical characteristics (treatment, disease duration); and baseline disability measures (EDSS, T25FW, NHPT, SDMT, PASAT, LCVA) were summarized as median (IQR) for continuous variables and number (percentage) for categorical variables. Group comparisons between MS and HC, and between PMS and RRMS within the MS cohort, were conducted using the Wilcoxon rank sum test for continuous variables and either Pearson’s chi-squared test (when all expected cell counts were greater than five) or Fisher’s exact test (when any expected cell count was less than five) for categorical variables. Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p-values were calculated to control the false discovery rate.

To categorize the PVS size, we determined cutoff values based on PVS cluster voxel tertiles in HC participants. This resulted in PVS size category thresholds defined as: small (≤ 1.32 mm3), medium (1.32 – 2.65 mm3), and large (> 2.65 mm3), which were used to quantify the number and volume of PVS of each category on each scan. Besides count and volume, white matter PVS fraction was calculated by dividing PVS volume by total white matter (WM) volume. For baseline white matter and PVS quantification, Wilcoxon rank sum test and Fisher’s exact test were used to compare PVS between HC and MS. False discovery rate was controlled by applying Benjamini–Hochberg correction to the p-values.

Correlation between white matter data and PVS data was tested using the Spearman correlation test with false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment. For MS participants with multiple visits, a mixed-effects model with patient ID as a random intercept was used to assess the effect of MS phenotype on PVS quantification. WM volume, WML count, and WML volume that showed significant correlations were included as covariates in the corresponding PVS models. To reduce the influence of differences in measurement scale, all continuous variables were standardized to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 prior to modeling.

Changes in PVS count, volume, and fraction were determined as the annual difference in quantification, which are the normalized count/volume/fraction difference based on the yearly difference in visit dates. To evaluate changes, Wilcoxon rank sum test and Fisher’s exact test were used to compare annualized change in PVS quantification between PMS and RRMS group. As for the change of CE-PVS existence, four scenarios were observed regarding overall and deep gray CE-PVS existence – absent or present at all visits, absent at baseline and present at subsequent visit, present at baseline and absent at subsequent visit. Scenario “present at all visits” was not observed in the brainstem/cerebellum and subcortical WM region. Fisher’s exact test was used to compare CE-PVS existence change between PMS and RRMS. P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate. To better understand which scenario led to the significant difference, mixed multinominal models were fitted with MS phenotypes as predictor and CE-PVS existence change as outcome, participant ID as a random intercept and age as a covariate.

Using all visits for MS participants, cumulative link mixed models were utilized to analyze the association between PVS and EDSS with participant ID as random intercept. Linear mixed models were used to analyze the association between PVS and other disability scales as an exploratory analysis. Co-variates in each model were selected from demographic (sex, age, ethnicity) and clinical (treatment, disease duration) data through correlation coefficient tests and Kruskal-Wallis tests. The assumptions of linear mixed models were tested by checking collinearity with Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), validating the normality of residuals with normal quantile–quantile plot (QQ Plot), and validating the homogeneity of variance with residual plot. All statistical analyses were done using R (version 4.2.2).

Results

Cohort

In this study, 244 observations were collected from 122 participants, comprising 19 HC and 103 with MS. Of the 103 MS participants, 21 had PMS, and 82 had RRMS. One scan was omitted from the study due to incomplete image acquisition; 10 scans and 7 scans were discarded due to severe motion artifact during PVS and CE-PVS review, respectively. Data from participants with both PVS and CE-PVS masks valid at each visit were used for further analysis. The final dataset consisted of 218 scans from 118 participants, including 18 HCs and 100 with MS (21 PMS, 79 RRMS).

As shown in Table 1, participants with MS were older at baseline than those in the HC groups, p = 0.3 and adjusted p = 0.335. They had a median disease duration of 11 years (IQR: 7, 18) and a median EDSS score of 3 (IQR: 2, 4), p < 0.001 and adjusted p = 0.001. Within the MS group, those with PMS were older (p < 0.001 and adjusted p = 0.001), had a longer duration of the disease (p = 0.11 and adjusted p = 0.14), and a higher EDSS score (p < 0.001 and adjusted p = 0.000) compared to those with RRMS. The percentage of females was higher in the RRMS group (72 %) compared to the PMS group (48 %), with p = 0.034 and adjusted p-value = 0.058.

Segmentation algorithm

Using the manually edited mask as a ‘gold standard’ comparator, the automated PVS segmentation algorithm output had a median DSC of 0.994 with an interquartile range (IQR) of 0.985 to 0.997. The overlap rate had a median of 0.996 with an IQR of 0.992 to 0.998.

Comparisons between MS and control groups

Baseline PVS quantification between groups is illustrated in Fig. 3 (left) and Table A1. As illustrated in Fig. 3 (left), there was no significant difference in PVS count, volume, or WM PVS fraction when comparing participants with MS to HC participants. Restricting the analysis only to “large” category PVS did not change the results for either count or volume (Table A1). CE-PVS was observed in 29 % of participants with MS, compared to 11 % of HC, but this group difference did not attain significance (Table A1, p = 0.15 and adjusted p = 428). The median count of CE-PVS in MS participants was 0, with an IQR of 0 – 2. There were no significant differences in CE-PVS count or volume between groups.

Within MS group comparisons

Table 2 shows that PVS burden was positively correlated with WM volume (adjusted p = 0.001 for PVS count; adjusted p = 0.002 for large PVS count) and with WML count (adjusted p < 0.001 for PVS count and adjusted p = 0.001 for large PVS count). Overall (adjusted p = 0.015) and deep gray (adjusted p = 0.014) CE-PVS volume were also positively correlated with WML volume. In contrast, while deep gray CE-PVS was positively associated with WML count (adjusted p = 0.037), deep gray CE-PVS count (adjusted p = 0.037) and volume (adjusted p = 0.040) showed negative correlations with WM volume. Fig. 3 (right) and Table 3 present PVS comparisons within the MS population using data from all visits available in a mixed model adjusting for visit number and inter-visit correlations. Participants with PMS had a higher PVS count (median: 1341; IQR: 841, 1481) compared to those with RRMS (median: 849; IQR: 571, 1283). PMS participants had on average a 0.47 higher value in scaled PVS count, with a p value of 0.037. Participants with PMS had a larger median PVS volume (1324.9 mm3 (IQR 868.0, 1899.0)) than those with RMS (917.8 mm3 (IQR 568.4, 1450.7)), but this difference was not significant (adjusted p = 0.300). The count and volume of “large” PVS, along with WM PVS fraction also had non-significant trends towards being higher in those with PMS. CE-PVS was seen more frequently in participants with PMS (35 %) than RMS (26 %), but this difference was not significant (adjusted p = 0.500).

Annualized change

Annualized changes in PVS are provided in Tables 4 and A2. The count and volume of PVS tended to increase in people with PMS, with a median count change of 41 (IQR: −113, 110) per year and a median volume change of 19.2 mm3 (IQR: −121.8 mm3, 172.6 mm3) per year. PVS decreased in those with RRMS, with a median count change of −2 (IQR: −120, 88) per year and a median volume change of −1.5 mm3 (IQR: −113.4 mm3, 138.6 mm3) per year, however, the differences were not significant, adjusted p = 0.637 for PVS count change and adjusted p = 0.683 for PVS volume change.

CE-PVS was present at all visits in 13 % of PMS and 18 % of RRMS (Table 4, adjusted p = 0.024). Table A.2 offers a detailed view of how MS subtypes related to the four scenarios of CE-PVS existence change. For the change of overall CE-PVS presence, the odds of CE-PVS being absent at baseline but present at subsequent visits were 78 % lower for people with RRMS (p = 0.006), and the odds of CE-PVS being present at baseline but absent at subsequent visits were 82 % lower for people with RRMS (p = 0.002). Similar results were observed in the deep gray region.

Associations with disability

There were no significant relationships found between PVS quantification and disability scores. Participants with CE-PVS completed the T25FW test 5.9 (SE 2.93) seconds faster than those without CE-PVS, p = 0.044. Conversely, the mean time for the NHPT was 21 seconds (SE 8.05) longer for participants with CE-PVS, p = 0.009. With each unit increase in the count of brainstem/cerebellum CE-PVS, the SDMT score increased by 1.5 (SE 0.661), p = 0.028. Similarly, a 1 mm3 increase in the volume of brainstem/cerebellum CE-PVS was associated with a 0.18 (SE 0.073) increase in the SDMT score, p = 0.017. On the other hand, the PASAT score decreased by 0.36 (SE 0.172) for each unit increase in the count of deep gray CE-PVS (p = 0.037) and decreased by 0.04 (SE 0.016) for every 1 mm3 increase in the volume of deep gray CE-PVS (p = 0.028). PVS quantification was not associated with LCVA. More details as to the relationship of PVS to disability testing can be found in Table 5.

Discussion

This study provides valuable insights into the quantification of PVS and CE-PVS in MS. The unique contributions of this work are the highly detailed quantification of PVS provided by 7T MRI, the validation of a PVS segmentation algorithm for use on 7T MRI images, and the evaluation of CE-PVS.

Comparison of the PVS count and volume obtained in this study to prior literature on lower field MRI confirms the marked increase in sensitivity for PVS using 7T MRI and automated quantification methods. In a recent meta-analysis of neuroimaging studies of PVS in MS, (Granberg et al., 2020) reported mean PVS counts in MS studies were in a range between 1.6 – 34.4, whereas the median (range) PVS count in this study was 927 (616, 1388) in the MS group. Similarly, the range of mean PVS volume in MS studies from the same meta-analysis was between 68.3 – 280.8 mm3, whereas median PVS volume in the MS group in our study was 1034.9 (682.8, 1703.5) mm3. This marked increase in PVS count and volume seen in this study and another small study utilizing 7T MRI for PVS quantification in MS. (George et al., 2021) The increased count and volume seen at 7T is partially explained by the increase in resolution obtainable at 7T, which is likely necessary to visualized PVS as histopathology suggests sub-millimeter diameters for most PVS. (Pesce and Carli, 1988) In this light, it is likely that prior studies evaluating PVS on lower field MRIs were only sensitive to enlarged PVS, rather than a quantification of the full spectrum of PVS in the human brain.

The segmentation algorithm used in this study demonstrated high accuracy, with a high degree of overlap and DSC when compared to the manually edited mask, indicating a strong agreement between the automated and manual segmentation of PVS. The use of this segmentation algorithm, rather than the manual identification and annotation utilized in many prior studies (Granberg et al., 2020) also likely increases the sensitivity of the approach used in our study compared to prior MS literature.

Although increased PVS burden has previously been reported in MS, no significant difference was observed in PVS quantification in this study between participants with and without MS. This calls into question the pathological specificity of enlarged PVS in MS. The increased sensitivity for all PVS noted above may contribute to differences in outcome between this report and others. In addition, prior studies using the Potter score relied on a visual rating approach that may capture subtle PVS changes but are inherently subjective, (GM Potter et al., 2015) whereas our automated algorithm applies stricter quantitative thresholds, potentially leading to different results. Differences in study populations (e.g., age, disease stage, treatment exposure) and MRI protocols (e.g., sequence type, field strength, resolution) between our study and earlier work may further contribute to the discrepancy. Notably, our findings were robust to whether all PVS were included or only those in the “large” size category. Further, it should be pointed out, that although on average most studies report a higher PVS burden in MS than those without, the veracity of this finding has been inconsistent and heterogenous in multiple reports (Harrison et al., 2011; Kilsdonk et al., 2015; Cavallari et al., 2018) – suggesting that methodology and sampling biases likely contribute to the outcomes of PVS evaluations. Within the MS group, participants with PMS had higher PVS count than those with RRMS, and the PMS subjects were more likely to have an increase in the count and volume of PVS over time. It is unclear if this finding represents confirmation of an association between the evolution and progression of MS and pathology in the PVS (Harrison et al., 2011) or known associations between PVS enlargement and age. (Bown et al., 2022) The relatively few and inconsistent relationships between PVS quantification and disability scales in this cohort, however, suggest that PVS burden may not be an indicator of pathology.

This study is the first evaluation, to our knowledge, of GBCA enhancement on post-contrast FLAIR in the PVS in MS. Although the PVS typically does not show enhancement on post-contrast T1W imaging, enhancement is reported in the PVS on post-contrast FLAIR due to the increased sensitivity to small amounts of GBCA in CSF and interstitial fluid. (Naganawa et al., 2017; Freeze et al., 2020; Mamourian et al., 2000; Zhang et al., 2023) Accumulation of GBCA in the PVS may occur due to BBB breakdown in local vessels, due to reabsorption of CSF into the PVS, or impairments in the lymphatic/glymphatic pathway. (Taoka and Naganawa, 2018; Zhang et al., 2023; Saade et al., 2018; Taoka and Naganawa, 2020; Wu et al., 2021) Each of these processes are known to be impaired in MS, (Carotenuto et al., 2022; Kermode et al., 1990) and it is therefore logical to surmise that GBCA may accumulate in the PVS to a greater degree in MS and become visible as a consequence of MS pathology. Although CE-PVS on FLAIR was first noted in studies investigating altered lymphatic clearance of GBCA in endolymphatic hydrops, (Ohashi et al., 2018) the pathologic nature of the finding is unclear given conflicting data on the presence of CE-PVS in HCs. (Naganawa et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2023; Naganawa et al., 2018) Our findings hint towards possible impairment of the clearance pathway in MS, given the higher percentage of MS participants with CE-PVS, although this difference was not significant and relationships between CE-PVS and disability scores were few and inconsistent. However, the presence of CE-PVS in the HC cohort indicates that this finding may not necessarily reflect pathological change, but rather normal metabolite clearance pathways. More exploration may be warranted, perhaps focusing on PVS in the deep gray region given that this region had the highest likelihood of GBCA enhancement in this cohort and prior work showing a propensity of basal ganglia region PVS towards GBCA enhancement on FLAIR. (Naganawa et al., 2017; Naganawa et al., 2018)

There was not a consistent pattern of relationships between enlarged PVS or CE-PVS and disability scales. These inconsistent findings are likely attributable to statistical noise and confounding, suggesting that the pathology of PVS and CE-PVS may not be directly linked to the mechanisms underlying MS-related disability.

A key limitation of this study is the unequal number of participants in the MS and HC groups, which likely reduced the statistical power and increased the potential for type II errors when assessing between-group differences. The absence of statistically significant differences observed between MS and HC should therefore be interpreted with caution, as it may result from insufficient sample size rather than a true lack of effect. Future research with larger and more evenly distributed cohorts is needed to validate these findings and to clarify whether they reflect real biological similarity or are influenced by sampling limitations.

It is possible that the lack of large differences between HC and MS may have been influenced by the removal of PVS located too close to, or within, WMLs. However, exclusion of FLAIR hyperintensities was necessary to minimize false positives, given the similar appearance of PVS and small WMLs on T1-weighted images. Another limitation of the CE-PVS analysis in this study is that the findings may be contingent on the timing of image acquisition after contrast administration (23 min in our study). Prior study using post-contrast FLAIR to study gadolinium-based glymphatic clearance has demonstrated slow accumulation of small amounts of gadolinium in CSF spaces, suggesting that some delay after injection is necessary. (Taoka and Naganawa, 2018; Wu et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2020) However, the optimal timing for visualization of CE-PVS has not been systematically investigated and should be addressed in future studies.

Furthermore, manual segmentation from scratch was not performed in comparison with the automated method. Because of the massive number of PVS visualized on each individual scan at the very high resolution of these images, such segmentation was not practical or feasible within the timeframe of this study. We acknowledge that this represents a major limitation, as an independent manual subset could have provided stronger validation; however, given the extremely time-intensive nature of such work and its limited added value relative to the comprehensive manual correction already performed, it was not pursued here. As a result, the reported DSC may be affected by confirmation bias when using manual correction of automatically generated masks. Future studies incorporating independent manual segmentations in a small subset of subjects could provide additional validation of the automated method and further strengthen the robustness of these findings.

Conclusion

This work provides detailed quantification of PVS burden on 7T MRI with an automated algorithm and provides a first evaluation of CE-PVS in MS. Although baseline comparisons with HCs did not indicate strong pathological differences, a significant difference in PVS count was observed between PMS and RRMS within MS group across all time points, suggesting potential biological distinctions. Further research is warranted to explore the underlying mechanisms and their implications for MS progression and treatment.
