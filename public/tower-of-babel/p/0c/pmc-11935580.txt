Calcium Channels, Clustering, and Single‐Amino Acid Control of Blood Pressure and Blood Flow
Authors: Lars Jørn Jensen
Published: 2024

This editorial attempts to place new findings by the study of Flores‐Tamez et al. in the current issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA )1 into physiological perspective in this vascular field with a long tradition. The regulation of vascular tone in small arteries and arterioles (“resistance vessels”) relies on Ca2+ entry via the plasma membrane through voltage‐gated Ca2+ channels (predominant role of large and long‐lasting [L‐type], minor role of T‐type), which respond to graded depolarizing or hyperpolarizing changes in the vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) membrane potential. Further Ca2+ entry elicited by hormonal, pharmacological, chemical, noxious, mechanical, and temperature stimuli, as well as Ca2+ store emptying, occurs via nonvoltage‐gated Ca2+‐permeable channels, such as those of the transient receptor potential, Orai, and Piezo type. Increases in intracellular Ca2+ concentration are crucial for excitation–contraction coupling in VSMCs through Ca2+/calmodulin complex‐mediated activation of MLCK (myosin light chain kinase), phosphorylation of the 20‐kDa regulatory subunit of MLC20 (myosin light chain), and cross‐bridge cycling. The contractile state of VSMCs is negatively controlled by dephosphorylation of MLC20 through the activity of myosin light chain phosphatase (MLCP). A process called Ca2+ sensitization, in which MLCP activity is inhibited by several signaling pathways, facilitates maintenance of resting vascular tone over time. MLCK and MLCP are the target of protein kinases and regulatory signal pathways, such as PKA (protein kinase A), PKC (protein kinase C), and Rho‐kinase. Activation of PKA elicits vasodilation through inhibition of MLCK as well as hyperpolarization through K+ channel activation. PKC activation elicits vasoconstriction through stimulation of voltage‐gated Ca2+ entry, inhibition of K+ channels (ie, depolarization), whereas PKC and primarily Rho‐kinase activation cause inhibition of MLCP activity and thus Ca2+ sensitization. Vascular tone regulation, through changes in local vascular resistance, is of paramount importance for blood flow regulation in tissues, which is compromised in serious cardiovascular and cerebrovascular conditions. VSMC activation and vasoconstriction are under direct control by noradrenaline released from perivascular sympathetic nerves and by the myogenic response responding to changes in intraluminal pressure. Whereas the myogenic response is crucial for blood flow autoregulation in most organs, the myogenic tone sets the basal tone at the prevailing arterial pressure in resistance vessels. Vascular tone regulation is also important for blood pressure regulation through changes in total peripheral resistance, but this role is much more complex in that many factors contribute to blood pressure regulation via additional control of cardiac output and renal control of extracellular volume in the body. The majority of patients with hypertension (90%–95%) suffer from essential hypertension, which is characterized by a normal cardiac output but an increased total peripheral resistance, the generic cause of which is still not resolved.

There are 10 members of the voltage‐dependent Ca2+ channel family, of which the CaV1.2 L‐type channel (pore‐forming α1C‐subunit; CAGNA1C) is responsible for excitation–contraction coupling and excitation–transcription coupling in cardiac and smooth muscle cells. The CaV1.2 channels are dihydropyridine sensitive and high‐voltage‐activated with large and long‐lasting (hence the name L‐type) whole‐cell currents. 2 , 3 A number of different splice variants of the L‐type α1C‐subunit exists, with the α1C‐b smooth muscle variant having more hyperpolarized voltage‐dependent activation threshold and higher dihydropyridine sensitivity compared with the α1C‐a cardiac variant. 3 , 4 , 5 The sigmoidal current/voltage‐activation curve rises steeply in the range from −40 mV to +10 mV in cardiac myocytes, and from −55 mV to −20 mV in smooth muscle cells, 4 , 6 which is consistent with the differential expression of splice variants. The smooth muscle variant therefore seems well suited for a role in E‐C coupling of SMCs, which have a resting membrane potential in the range −45 to −35 mV in situ in pressurized resistance vessels. 7 , 8 Moreover, graded increases in intraluminal pressure cause graded (myogenic) depolarization in intact resistance vessels, whereby more L‐type channels activate and thus contribute to the myogenic response and maintenance of myogenic tone. 7 , 9 Studies have shown an increased expression of the L‐type α1‐subunit in resistance arteries from chronic hypertensive animals but also after short‐term experimental induction of hypertension. This might be a compensatory upregulation to sustain increased myogenic constriction due to a higher intraluminal pressure. 10

L‐type channel antagonists (long‐acting dihydropyridines) have, along with renin‐angiotensin system blockers and thiazide diuretics, long been considered the first‐line antihypertensive treatment, demonstrating the crucial role for smooth muscle L‐type channels in regulating resistance artery diameter, total peripheral resistance, and blood pressure. 11 , 12 Because the global prevalence of hypertension is so high (20%–25%), and there still are conditions in which high blood pressure cannot be treated effectively (such as resistant hypertension), there is still a need for developing new targets and more effective pharmacological tools to manage hypertension. Moreover, there is a need for new antihypertensives to specifically target individual patient groups (eg, pregnancy, preeclampsia, postmenopausal age, vascular dementia, or sporadic Alzheimer's disease), or even for use in personalized medicine. An opportunity for this arises when new discoveries reveal highly specific single‐amino acid regulation of the CaV1.2 L‐type channels, which translates into clinically relevant as well as sex‐dependent effects. 1

Findings from Flores‐Tamez et al.

Flores‐Tamez et al. 1 shed important new light on the sex‐dependent role of a single amino acid, serine 1928 (S1928), on the L‐type α1C‐subunit to regulate channel activity, tissue blood flow, and blood pressure. The authors initially show, by comparing VSMC L‐type currents in wild‐type (WT) mice versus mice with a substitution of S1928 for alanine (S1928A), that phosphorylation of S1928 by angiotensin II (AngII) exposure increases calcium currents by enhancing channel open probability and cooperative gating activity in a manner that differs between males and females. Next, superresolution imaging and proximity ligation assay revealed L‐type channel clustering in a PKA‐independent manner upon AngII exposure. Again, the experiments revealed sex‐dependent differences in L‐type channel clustering mechanisms in VSMCs under basal or AngII‐stimulated conditions. Model predictions and whole‐cell patch clamp plus calcium imaging experiments showed that PKC‐dependent phosphorylation of S1928 (pS1928) is necessary for AngII‐induced intracellular Ca2+ increases and contraction of male VSMCs but is not required for VSMC depolarization per se. By preventing pS1928 using S1928A mice, the basal (myogenic) tone and the plateau tone during AngII exposure were reduced in mesenteric and cerebral arteries. The mesenteric artery blood flow was increased in parallel, and with nifedipine predominantly blocking the changes in tone and blood flow in WT, a role for L‐type channels and pS1928 in vascular tone and blood flow regulation in males is underlined. However, basal and AngII‐induced vascular tone was lower and blood flow was higher in the female mice, suggesting sex dependency. Moreover, pS1928 was not crucial for AngII induced changes in tone and blood flow in females, demonstrating important sex‐dependent differences in vivo. In male AngII‐induced hypertensive WT but not S1928A mice, open probability and gating cooperativity of L‐type single channel events were increased compared with normotensive (sham) mice, and the authors labeled this as “superclustering” behavior of the L‐type channels. Repeating the superresolution microscopy and proximity ligation assay experiments in male AngII‐induced hypertensive mice demonstrated increased channel clustering and cooperative gating activity (ie, superclustering) in hypertensive WT but not in S1928A mice, with no difference in protein levels of L‐type α1C‐subunit. In male blood pressure high genetic mice, where high blood pressure is caused by sympathetic overactivity, clustering of L‐type α1C‐subunits was increased compared with blood pressure normal mice. Furthermore, noradrenaline‐induced steady state arterial tone was increased in male WT versus S1928A mice, suggesting that PKC‐mediated phosphorylation of S1928 is also important for L‐type channel superclustering during sympathetic activation. Pressure myograph experiments revealed an increased myogenic tone in male WT AngII‐hypertensive mice compared with normotensive sham mice, but not in hypertensive S1928A mice. Finally, in the AngII‐induced hypertension model, mean arterial pressure increased more in male WT mice compared with S1928A mice with no apparent effects on cardiac function, showing a crucial role of PKC‐dependent phosphorylation of the S1928 site in blood pressure control and AngII‐induced hypertension in males. Overall, the authors propose that pS1928 is a key point for controlling vascular CaV1.2 channel function acting as a risk factor for hypertension and related diseases (eg, diabetic hyperglycemia), and making pS1928 a clinically relevant target at least in male patients.

Limitations, Current Challenges, and Perspectives

Although this study provides several new important clues to how L‐type channel function is regulated by AngII and in hypertension, there remain some points to be clarified. The study nicely shows that the vascular tone plateau response 5 minutes after AngII exposure was smaller in S1928A mice, but the peak AngII‐response was similar to WT mice. Why is the peak response not altered by pS1928 in WT mice? Undoubtedly, the phosphorylation happens faster than the 5 minutes shown for the plateau response. Although depolarization and Ca2+ influx occur rapidly during excitation–contraction coupling, Rho‐kinase mediated inhibition of MLCP and Ca2+ sensitization occur within minutes. Thus, it remains to be determined if AngII‐induced Ca2+ sensitization is changed in the S1928A mice. L‐type channels are also important for long‐term calcium influx to replenish intracellular Ca2+ stores, in addition to the direct coupling between stores and STIM (stromal interaction molecule)/Orai channels. However, what is the role of the Ca2+ stores, if any, in the ex vivo and in vivo effects seen in S1928A mice, and in AngII‐hypertensive or genetic blood pressure high mice?

The study focuses a lot on myogenic tone in relation to blood pressure and hypertension. However, the physiological role of the myogenic response and tone is not to participate in blood pressure regulation but rather in blood flow autoregulation at the prevailing arterial pressures. Inward remodeling caused by increased vascular tone is probably a leading cause for increased total peripheral resistance in hypertension, along with endothelial dysfunction, and it would be interesting to see more data on how long‐term phosphorylation of S1928 affects structural vascular remodeling. The sex‐dependent mechanisms of the L‐type channel clustering activity in isolated VSMCs is interesting, and it is important to try to understand why the study found an ex vivo/in vivo role of pS1928 in males and not females. The role played by sex hormones might be the first point to consider, but differences in expression patterns and signaling events will also need to be elucidated to understand and ultimately exploit these sex‐dependent effects. The authors discuss how the PKC‐mediated pS1928 effects on L‐type channel superclustering can be extrapolated to more clinically relevant hypertension models and human vascular cells and tissues. In order to do this, it would be important to develop compounds that can specifically prevent pS1928 to happen, without having to substitute an amino acid in the arterial L‐type channels.

This study reveals a potentially important new target to treat hypertension in nonresponders (resistant hypertension) and special patient groups, such as based on sex, metabolic/glycemic status, hypertension in pregnancy, or preeclampsia. Will it be possible to develop effective antihypertensives with an improved side effect profile over other calcium antagonists based on preventing pS1928? The authors argue that such medicine would be even more vascular‐selective, as AngII effects on CaV1.2 channels in cardiac myocytes require phosphatidylinositol biphosphate depletion and not pS1928. And perhaps in a more distant future: will it be possible to target specific channel mutations and thus develop personalized medicine?

Regardless of what the future holds in terms of overcoming the obstacles and potentially developing novel antihypertensives, the paper by Flores‐Tamez et al. 1 is scientifically very solid and highly interesting, and I hereby recommend reading and using it in your future work!
