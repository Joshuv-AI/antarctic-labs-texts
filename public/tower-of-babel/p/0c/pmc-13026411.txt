Representation learning for the prediction of length of stay in infants with congenital heart diseases undergoing cardiac surgery
Authors: Adriana Modrego, Esther Aurensanz-Clemente, Julia Meca-Santamaria, Roger Domingo-Espinós, Arnau Valls-Esteve, Joan Sanchez-de-Toledo, Bart Bijnens, Patricia Garcia-Canadilla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsCongenital heart disease is the most common birth defect and significantly impacts health, mortality, and healthcare costs. Accurately estimating intensive care unit (ICU) and hospital stays after cardiac surgery could facilitate family planning and optimal healthcare resource allocation. Although some perioperative risk factors have been associated with prolonged stays, tools to estimate the length of stay for the target population remain insufficient. This study employs explainable representation learning to enhance length of stay prediction through comprehensive spatial distribution and local learning.Methods and resultsDemographic and perioperative data from infants undergoing cardiac surgery were collected. Unsupervised multiple kernel learning was used to reduce data dimensionality and position patients by similarity. K-Means clustering was applied to identify phenogroups. Within each phenogroup, a binary classifier predicted prolonged stays, and a regressor estimated stay durations in days. Algorithms were validated using an independent cohort and compared with current clinical practices. Among 268 episodes of care (45.2% female, median age 0.6 years), 21% had prolonged ICU stay, and/or prolonged hospital stay. Key variables surgery duration, or extra corporeal circulation time were significantly correlated with latent dimensions. Classifiers reaches an AUC of 0.71 in identifying prolonged stays in the ICU and in the hospital. Regressor estimated both length of stay with a median absolute error of one day. This approach outperforms clinicians in both, classification and regression tasks.ConclusionExplainable representation learning leveraging patient similarity provides an interpretable tool for estimating the length of stay in the target population.

Introduction

Congenital heart disease (CHD) is the most common birth defect (almost 1% of births) and a major contributor to early childhood morbidity and mortality.1,2 Moreover, it is among the most expensive conditions in the healthcare (HC) system due to the need for surgery to repair heart defects, and associated in- and out-hospital morbidity.3,4 About 90% of children with CHD will need an intervention during their life, around 30% have to undergo at least one procedure during their first year of life.1,5 In addition, the most complex conditions will require multiple surgeries leading to prolonged intensive care unit (ICU) and hospital stays.

Different perioperative risk factors, associated with prolonged ICU length of stay (LOS), have been identified.6–9 However, the diversity of cardiac defects, the large number of surgical procedures, and the heterogeneity of population makes it difficult to estimate adequately LOS of children undergoing congenital heart surgery (CHS). Accurately anticipate LOS could facilitate optimal utilization of medical resources, enhance clinical decision-making, and provide valuable prognostic insights, influencing both healthcare expenses and patient satisfaction.10–12

Artificial intelligence (AI) models can analyse large amount of data to provide accurate predictions of LOS. Among the proposed models,10,13 Random Forest (RF) and Gradient Boosting (GB) have demonstrated superior performance.14–18 Most of the studies that proposed the use of AI models to predict LOS were trained on adulthood populations undergoing CHS,19–25 while few studies focused on the paediatric population.26–28

Among paediatric studies, most of them tried to identify risk factors associated with prolonged LOS.6–9,29,30 Nevertheless, Alexander et al.28 employed a multivariate logistic regression to identify patients with prolonged post-surgery ICULOS using intraoperative and clinical data. However, the most significant input variable was the Congenital Heart Surgical Stay (CheSS) score,31 which has a direct relationship with the outcome since it ranks procedures based on their geometric mean LOS.

In addition, recent efforts, such as the development of CHDmap, a patient similarity network designed for paediatric CHD patients, have demonstrated the potential of similarity-based approaches to support outcome prediction and clinical decision-making. However, that study focused on different outcomes (prediction of postoperative complications and mechanical ventilation duration).32

Hence, there is a lack of tools to estimate accurately ICULOS and hospital LOS (HLOS) for children after a CHS. Additionally, there is limited information on how proposed methods work in diverse individual healthcare settings, especially because most AI models lack clinical explainability, thus limiting universal acceptance and accountability.10

This study proposes a novel approach based on representation learning (RL) to estimate ICULOS and HLOS in CHD infants at the critical moment of ICU admission, immediately after CHS. By leveraging heterogeneous perioperative data available at this early stage, our model aims to provide clinicians and families with an informative first overview of the expected postoperative course. A key feature of our methodology is its ability to provide explainable outcomes by analysing the positioning of samples in a low-dimensional space (latent space) and its relationship with the input data. Notably, our predictions are based on local learning, allowing models to account for unique characteristics of each subpopulation.

Population

Infants with CHD, aged between 1 and 24 months, undergoing CHS in Hospital Sant Joan de Déu (HSJD) between September 2020 and November 2025, were included. All surgical episodes of care from each patient were included and considered as independent samples. This study was approved by the HSJD Research Ethics Committee (HSJD-REC number PIC-249-20: ‘Diseño de un score de riesgo cardiovascular en pacientes pediatricos con cardiopatia’ approved November, 26, 2020). Written informed consent from the participants’ legal guardian/next of kin was waived by the local Ethical Committee. In this work, all procedures followed the ethical guidelines determined by the HSJD REC and the Helsinki Declaration of 1975.

Data samples

From all data available in the electronic health record of the hospital, potential input variables for the predictive model were selected based on current literature, clinical interest, and availability, resulting in 60 clinical, demographic, and perioperative variables.

Primary outcomes, ICULOS and HLOS were calculated using ICU and hospital admission and discharge times. In patients with more than one ICU admission, only the first stay after CHS was considered. Prolonged ICULOS and HLOS were defined as stays exceeding the 75th percentile, consistent with previous studies.34 According to our LOS distributions (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1), prolonged ICU and hospital stays were those exceeding seven and fourteen days, respectively.

Data was divided into two separated sets: discovery and evaluation. The discovery dataset, used for model development, contains 75% of the samples while the evaluation dataset, used to test the entire pipeline, contains the remaining 25% of the samples. Data were split via stratified random sampling based on the outcome variable.

Data processing

From the selected variables, those with more than 50% of missing data were removed, and those with very low variability were also discarded—specifically, numeric variables with a variance below 0.01 and categorical variables where a single value accounted for more than 95% of the observations.

Variables removed due to high degree of missingness were main comorbidities, urea, gamma glutamiltransferasa, procalcitonine, troponin-I, NT pro-BNP, and reactive Protein C. Those dropped due to very low variability were: if the patient was defibrillated in the operation room (OR) and the time of heart arrest during surgery. Regarding highly correlated variables (when the correlation coefficient between variables was higher than 0.75 with a P-value < 0.05, after applying the corresponding test according to the variables datatype), we only kept the one with fewer missing values. This was the case for haematocrit (highly correlated with haemoglobin), aspartate-aminotransferase (highly correlated with alanine-aminotransferase).

To impute missing values, we used different strategies according to the variable type. More details about data imputation are provided in Supplementary Methods. The final dataset included 33 variables, with the complete list provided in Table 1.

Finally, the main diagnosis of each patient (e.g. VSD, ASD, TOF, or combinations thereof), coded in ICD10 were encoded using one-hot encoding, extracorporeal circulation (ECC) and aortic cross-clamp times were discretized, and laboratory measurements that belong to the same category (hemogram, ionogram, etc.) in standard blood tests were grouped together in a single input vector. These transformations were found to improve the performance of the MKL algorithm.

Unsupervised learning and clustering

Multiple kernel learning (MKL)35 was used to integrate all the data, calculate subjects’ similarity and reduce data dimensionality. This RL algorithm supports mixed data types (binary, categorical, continuous, and ordinal) by using dedicated base kernel for each data type, resulting in an interpretable low-dimensional output space. The MKL algorithm has shown a great potential in addressing high-dimensional low-sample size problems and it has already been used to process cardiac data.36–39 The algorithm is technically described in detail in36–38 and the source code is available online.40

The low-dimensional output space was learnt using the 33 features from the 134 samples of the discovery set. The MKL algorithm was trained by optimizing only the rotation matrix, which maps the input space to the output space, solved via a generalized eigenvalue problem. Weight optimization was omitted, as the latent space obtained with and without this step was equivalent, and skipping it significantly reduced computational cost. Therefore, all the kernels have the same weight (β = 0.056). The kernels used for each type of feature were: a probabilistic kernel for the categorical features, a frequency kernel for the binary features (rare categories induce higher distance), an ordinal kernel for the ordinal features and a gaussian kernel for the continuous features.

The number of neighbours (k) used to define the affinity matrices was set to the square root of the number of training samples so k = 11.

The values of the rotation matrix used to map the inputs to the output space were optimized while β was fixed. This optimization was solved by a generalized eigenvalue decomposition, which has an explicit solution.36

Overall, the samples of the evaluation dataset were transformed to the output space using the β vector (1 × 18) and the A matrix (134 × 133).

Once samples were positioned in the latent space, they were clustered using the K-means algorithm, a simple but effective unsupervised machine learning (ML) technique for partitioning data into a pre-defined number of clusters. K-means was implemented using the K-Means function from the Python library scikit-learn. This partitioning algorithm operates by iteratively assigning each data point to the nearest centroid and then recalculating the centroids based on the mean of the data points assigned to each cluster. This process iterated until convergence, where the centroids no longer change significantly, or after it reached 300 iterations. Additionally, to reduce the dependence of centroid positions on the initial seed selection, the algorithm was run 100 times (init = 100, n_init = k-means++), and the centroids that resulted in the lowest inertia were used as the final centroids—default behaviour of the scikit-learn implementation.

The number of clusters was determined by employing the Elbow Method rule. By computing the inertia and distortion metrics while varying the number of clusters from one to ten, the optimal number of clusters was selected as the point where the rate of decrease in these metrics significantly slows down. The Elbow plot is presented in Supplementary material online, Figure S2.

Supervised machine learning to predict length of stay

Two different supervised ML models were developed: a binary classifier to identify patients with prolonged ICULOS or HLOS, and a regression model to estimate the ICU/hospital LOS in days. In both cases, the discovery dataset was split into training (75% of samples) and testing (remaining 25%). All supervised models were trained using the first six dimensions of the latent space derived from the MKL algorithm, as these dimensions captured the majority of the population’s variance.

Both supervised ML models were trained locally, per cluster, since visual inspection showed that each cluster contained both, dense areas of samples with prolonged stays and dense areas of samples with regular stays.

For the classification step, we trained an independent RF model for each cluster. RF was selected because it has demonstrated a strong performance in LOS estimation in previous studies.27,39 Given the limited sample size within each cluster, a leave-one-out cross-validation strategy was used to train the models. Following a grid search, different combinations of hyperparameters (number of trees and maximum tree depth) were tested to optimize the performance of each model. The optimal hyperparameter values were selected separately for each cluster based on cross-validation results, using accuracy as the performance metric. Then, the selected model per cluster was trained on all cluster-specific training samples and evaluated on the cluster-specific testing subset.

The implementation of the RF algorithms was performed using the Python library scikit-learn with the ‘balanced’ mode to adjust weights inversely proportional to class frequencies in the input data.

For the regression task, a K-Nearest Neighbours (KNN)41 was implemented per cluster. Once the training samples were placed in the latent space, the LOS for each test sample was predicted as the median value of its nearest neighbours. Namely, all the training samples within the cluster and within a hypersphere of radius 0.03. This radius ensured that most of the samples have at least one neighbour.

Model evaluation

The developed solution was evaluated in two ways. First, we evaluated the algorithm's standalone performance by applying the full pipeline to the evaluation cohort. This involves projecting samples into the latent space using the learned MKL transformation matrices, and assigning each sample to the cluster with the closest precomputed centroid. Then, within each cluster, the trained RF was used to predict prolonged ICU/hospital stays, and the KNN algorithm was used to estimate ICULOS and HLOS. In addition, to contextualize the model’s performance, we also compared the performance of the regression model to a simple baseline model that outputs the median ICULOS and HLOS for all cases.

Second, we assessed whether our approach could surpass current clinical practice. Ten cases from the evaluation cohort were randomly selected and presented to 18 clinicians (from trainees to expert cardiologists) blinded to patient identities. Clinicians received the same input variables as the model, to make results comparable and to minimize the risk of patient recognition. Then, they were asked to identify patients with prolonged ICU/hospital LOS and to estimate the ICULOS and HLOS. Finally, we compared algorithm’s and clinicians’ predictions.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or as median (25th–75th percentile) depending on normality, determined by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Qualitative and ordinal variables were presented as number and percentage. Differences between groups were analysed for statistical significance using one-factor analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous normally distributed variables, Kruskal–Wallis for continuous non-normally distributed and ordinal variables, and the chi-square test for categorical variables. A P-value < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Correlations between input variables and between input variables and the first six dimensions of the latent space were computed using the Spearman correlation coefficient for continuous and ordinal variables, binary point biserial correlation coefficient for binary variables and ANOVA eta squared for categorical variables. A P-value < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

Clinical characteristics

A total of 281 episodes of care from 259 patients were initially included. Thirteen cases were discarded due to missing data. Thus, the final dataset contained 268 episodes of care. The 33 clinical and perioperative features used as input variables for the predictive models are presented in Table 1.

In the whole population, the median patient age was 0.6 [0.3, 0.9] years and females represent the 45.2%. Nearly 40% of all the patients were already hospitalized before surgery. Regarding the complexity of CHS, 78.5% of the samples have a STAT score smaller than three and none of them has a STAT score of 5. In average, surgeries have a duration of 265.5 (217.3–323.0) min with an ECC time of 74.50 (48.3–107.8) min and a clamp time of 37.00 (20.3–64.0) min.

Regarding the primary outcomes, the proportion of episodes with a prolonged ICULOS and HLOS were 21% for both cases.

The discovery subset contains 201 samples while the evaluation subset includes 67 samples. The comparison of both cohorts’ characteristics is presented in Supplementary material online, Table S1.

Exploration of the latent space

MKL positioned the subjects of the training dataset in a latent space based on data similarity. The variability of the input variables along the first six dimensions of the latent space was then explored. The number of dimensions was determined by a 95% cut-off for the correlation between patient’s neighbourhood as function of the number of dimensions. Therefore, the addition of more dimensions would not yield changes in patient’s neighbourhoods.

When evaluating the correlation between original data and the first dimensions of the latent space it was found that surgery duration, ECC and aortic cross-clamp times were highly correlated with dimension one, while pre-surgical location and pre-surgical LOS were highly correlated with dimension two (Figure 2, Supplementary material online, Figure S3). Finally, STAT score and surgery deviation time were moderately correlated with dimension three while age with dimension four (see Supplementary material online, Figure S3).

Investigation of the clusters revealed that overall, patients were stratified by perioperative complexity into distinct clusters. Clusters 3 and 4 comprise the most complex surgical profiles—longer surgeries with prolonged ECC and aortic cross-clamping times— and consequently exhibiting the longest ICU and hospital stays. Clusters 1 and 2 contained less complex cases with shorter postoperative lengths of stay. Analysing each cluster independently it is found that:

Subjects in cluster one (n = 71) were at low risk of prolonged ICULOS (15.5%) and HLOS (14.1%), the lowest across clusters. Most patients were admitted from home, and their surgical profiles indicated low-to-moderate complexity, with a predominance of STAT1/2 procedures and moderate ECC and aortic clamp times. This cluster also showed the shortest postoperative stays.

Most patients were admitted from home, and their surgical profiles indicated low-to-moderate complexity, with a predominance of STAT1/2 procedures and moderate ECC and aortic clamp times. This cluster also showed the shortest postoperative stays.

Cluster two (n = 31) shows intermediate risk of prolonged stays (ICULOS 19.35%, HLOS 19.35%). This cluster comprised the youngest patients, many of whom were already hospitalized preoperatively. Surgeries within this cluster were the least complex, characterized by the shortest duration, negligible ECC and aortic clamp time, and lowest VISmax values. Outcomes were consistent with this lower surgical burden.

Outcomes were consistent with this lower surgical burden.

Cluster three (n = 57) had the highest proportion of subjects with prolonged HLOS (33.33%) and high proportion of prolonged ICULOS (26.32%). These patients were slightly older infants and were mostly hospitalized prior to surgery. Their surgical course indicated moderate-to-high complexity, characterized by long ECC and aortic clamp times and the deepest hypothermia among clusters, with a notable proportion of higher-risk procedures (28% classified as STAT3/4). They also exhibited a distinctive laboratory profile and had longer median LOS compared with Clusters 1 and 2.

Discussion

In this paper, we provide a useful first step in demonstrating the applicability and interpretability of RL together with clustering-based ML algorithms to identify CHD infants at high risk of prolonged ICU/hospital stays following CHS. At the same time, our approach reliably estimates the expected LOS for those patients likely to experience a regular recovery.

The main contribution of this work is the integration of representation learning via MKL with clustering techniques to manage data heterogeneity and high dimensionality. This combination enables the creation of a low-dimensional and interpretable latent space, from which it is possible to extract relevant discriminative features, identify subpopulations of patients with similar characteristics, and relate them to clinical outcomes. Given the high heterogeneity of the paediatric CHD population, grouping subjects by data similarity and developing LOS predictive models for each subgroup can better capture subgroup-specific patterns than a single global model. Thus, our approach aligns with the rationale presented in the literature, which supports local modelling in heterogeneous populations.42

Beyond predictive performance, the hybrid pipeline enhances interpretability through complementary mechanisms. MKL retains the capability to project any point in the latent space back to its original feature representation, allowing precise identification of the variables that influence patient positioning within that space. Clustering further enhances clinical interpretability by grouping patients into subpopulations with comparable characteristics and linking each cluster to distinct risk profiles and outcome patterns. Finally, the latent space provides an intuitive visual representation where clinicians could infer likely clinical behaviours based on similarity to their neighbours, mirroring the logic of the KNN algorithm, which predicts outcomes by leveraging proximity. This alignment between algorithmic reasoning and clinical intuition reinforces trust in the predictions.

The MKL algorithm models complex nonlinear relationships and handles multiple data types more effectively than other methods, using specialized kernels tailored to each data type. This algorithm identifies the most relevant features, ordering individuals according to feature similarity. Hence, closer individuals in the latent space show similar clinical presentations while those far apart exhibit distinct, interpretable differences. This structure enhances interpretability and trustworthiness in predictions when new data is provided, as it allows clinicians to grasp the main clinical factors influencing the outcome and to associate predictions with clinically meaningful patient profiles. By providing insight into the patient condition, the model aligns more closely with clinical reasoning, making predictions more transparent and actionable in practice.

Exploration of the latent space revealed that intraoperative features were strongly correlated with the first dimensions of the latent space, capturing most of the population variability. These features, already recognized as risk factors for prolonged LOS,6,9,27 emerged as the most significant in distributing the samples within the latent space.

Notably, patients in clusters three and four, associated with the highest risk of prolonged ICU/hospital stays, generally underwent the longest and the most complex surgeries, characterized by extended ECC and clamp times. Conversely, patients in clusters one and two, showing the lowest risk, underwent shorter and less complex surgeries. Nevertheless, cluster two—representing the youngest cohort—exhibited actual surgical durations that systematically surpassed preoperative estimates, with a median deviation of around 25%.

This discrepancy arises because the estimated duration, defined preoperatively, is based primarily on procedural complexity and consensus, without accounting for patient-specific factors such as age or anatomical variability. As a result, the estimation process may systematically underestimate surgery time, particularly in younger patients, leading to scheduling inefficiencies and delays. This underlines the need to include additional factors such as patient age and baseline condition to estimate the surgery time.43

Regarding classification and regression tasks, the absence of a specific region containing the majority of the samples with prolonged ICU/hospital LOS, suggests the use of cluster-specific supervised predictive models. This strategy captures the intra-cluster relationships and patterns while adapting more effectively to each subpopulation's characteristics than global models.42 This aligns with recent developments such as CHDmap, which also emphasizes local modelling to predict outcomes like postoperative complications and mechanical ventilation duration.32

Our RF-based classifiers showed moderate discriminatory ability for predicting prolonged ICULOS (accuracy = 0.79, AUC = 0.71) and prolonged HLOS (accuracy = 0.82, AUC = 0.71) after CHS. Although these performance metrics may appear lower than those reported in previous studies,26,28 several key aspects related to population characteristics and feature selection must be considered.

First, the model developed by Chang Junior et al.26 achieved a higher AUC (0.856); however, their cohort was substantially larger and included both adult and paediatric patients. Recent evidence indicates that global models often fail to capture the specific physiological trajectories of paediatric populations, resulting in reduced performance when applied to narrower subpopulations.44 By exclusively focusing on a paediatric cohort, our approach addresses the limitations inherent in ‘one-size-fits-all’ models, thereby tackling a more specialized—and inherently more challenging—prediction task. Our findings (see Supplementary material online, Table S5) support the shift from broad, heterogeneous models towards paediatric-specific predictive approaches.

Second, Alexander et al.28 reported an AUC of 0.87, but their model incorporated the CheSS score as an input feature. CheSS is directly linked to LOS because it ranks procedures based on their geometric mean LOS; thus, its inclusion introduces a variable strongly collinear with the outcome. In contrast, our modelling strategy deliberately excludes features directly tied to the target outcome, making the prediction task more stringent but methodologically more robust.

Finally, direct comparisons between studies remain challenging due to substantial differences in cohort composition, feature sets, and definitions of prolonged stays. This underpersistent obstacle of model evaluation due to the absence of standardized benchmarks.

Regarding LOS estimation, the KNN models yielded a MAE of 5.3 and 7.2 days for ICULOS and HLOS, respectively, in the evaluation cohort. However, in both cases, the MedAE was 1 day. Actually, most predictions had an error between 0 and 2 days, indicating reliable performance for regular recovery trajectories. However, predictions for prolonged stays were less accurate, likely due to post-surgical events not captured by the model and the limited number of training samples above the threshold, which had highly variable and sparse LOS values. Hence, additional information about patient’s evolution after surgery may help refine predictions for prolonged LOS, particularly through dynamic updates as new clinical data becomes available. However, if the sample size for patients with long stays remains small, models may struggle to learn from these rare cases without overfitting.

Nonetheless, our ICULOS results slightly outperformed both the baseline model, which always predicts the median ICULOS, and the best-performing model presented in,26 which also predicted ICULOS for CHD patients but included both paediatric and adulthood populations. Regarding HLOS, the MedAE of our model slightly outperformed the baseline model too.

The lower MAE observed in ICULOS predictions, relative to HLOS, likely stems from the shorter forecasting horizons associated with the former. While ICULOS predictions are made closer to the actual discharge point, HLOS predictions requires forecasting over a longer time horizon, extrapolating over a longer, more uncertain timeline. As presented in the previous works, LOS prediction improves as the discharge date approaches.45

A proper estimation of the LOS at the postoperative ICU admission could help to improve patient care, reduce unnecessary hospital costs, optimizing discharge planning and patient flow, increase service efficiency, and improve communication with families prioritizing patients and families in the centre of the care process.10–12

We acknowledge that the present study has limitations. First, by focusing exclusively on perioperative factors, predictive capability diminishes after ICU discharge and the capacity of estimating the exact number of days at ICU or at hospital remains limited. The proposed approach performs well in predicting ICU stays of up to 7 days and hospital stays of up to 14 days. Acknowledging the dynamic nature of postoperative recovery and the results obtained, the incorporation of temporal data will enable more precise and individualized predictions of LOS, enhancing the performance of the proposed framework. Second, our study was conducted in a single hospital, which limited the amount of available samples and may compromise generalizability of the findings to other centres of care. Moreover, variability in data availability, protocols, and practices across hospitals, related to CHS management, further challenges the broader application of our framework. Thus, validation with multi-institutional data is necessary.

Overall, this study should be regarded as a proof of concept, highlighting the potential of integrating representation learning and clustering-based ML to support early clinical decision-making in paediatric CHD care.

Finally, it is noteworthy that our model slightly surpasses current clinical practice. Although the clinicians tested ranged from residents to expert cardiologists, the algorithm consistently outperformed the best individual performances. These results indicate that our approach can effectively leverage large datasets, select relevant variables, and make estimations more efficiently than clinicians following current clinical practice.

Conclusions

We have shown that RL based on the quantification of data similarity provides a low-dimensional distribution of patients, where new patients can be projected to describe their similarity to known individuals. Beyond generating predictions, the proposed framework enhances interpretability by enabling clinicians to visualize patient similarity in the latent space and understand risk profiles through clustering. This combination bridges advanced machine learning with clinically meaningful insights, resulting in an easily interpretable tool for predicting ICULOS and HLOS in paediatric CHD patients undergoing a CHS.
