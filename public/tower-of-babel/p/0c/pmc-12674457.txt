Uncovering chromatin factor landscapes in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Hui Cheng, Meiye Jiang, Sohyoung Kim, Arfa Moshiri, Mohd Saleem Dar, Jawad Akhtar, Vassiliki Saloura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chromatin factors, defined here as proteins that chemically modify the DNA and histones, remodel chromatin and regulate nucleosome occupancy, play central roles in the transcriptional regulation of genes and are implicated in cancer initiation and progression in multiple cancer types. To systematically investigate the genomic and expression alterations of chromatin factors in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), we utilized the molecular profiles from 530 HNSCC tumor samples in the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), and characterized the mutational, copy number and transcriptional alterations of 422 chromatin factors, as well as their correlation with the “cold” tumor phenotype in HPV-negative and HPV-positive HNSCC. Histone-lysine N-methyltransferase 2D (MLL2) was the most frequently mutated chromatin factor in both HPV-negative and HPV-positive HNSCC, with mutation frequencies of 12–17 %. Actin Like 6A (ACTL6A), a component of the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex, was the most frequently copy-number amplified chromatin factor in both HPV-negative and HPV-positive HNSCC, with amplification frequencies of 19–24 %. Double PHD Fingers 1 (DPF1), a component of the chromatin remodeling complex, and Ubiquitin Like With PHD And Ring Finger Domains 1 (UHRF1) were the most overexpressed chromatin factors in HPV-negative and HPV-positive HNSCC tumors respectively. Components of the chromatin remodeling complex, such as ACTL6A, SMARCA1 and MORF4L2, were correlated with the “cold” tumor phenotype in both HPV-negative and HPV-positive HNSCC. This brief study highlights epigenetic chromatin factors that may drive oncogenesis and immune evasion, thereby identifying novel targets for cancer therapy in clearly defined, epigenetically-driven subtypes of HPV-negative and HPV-positive HNSCC.

Introduction

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) accounts for approximately 900,000 new cases and 400,000 deaths annually [1,2]. HNSCC is etiopathogenetically classified into human papilloma virus (HPV)-positive and −negative HNSCC, which is strongly associated with smoking. The current standard of care treatment for patients with newly diagnosed HNSCC consists of a multidisciplinary approach with surgery, platinum-based chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy, with a 5-year overall survival (OS) rate of approximately 50 % in HPV-negative HNSCC and ~ 75 % in HPV-positive HNSCC [3]. In recurrent/metastatic patients, the standard of care entails platinum-based chemoimmunotherapy with pembrolizumab, an anti-programmed-death (PD)-1 antibody, or pembrolizumab monotherapy, with a median OS of approximately 13 months [4]. The above highlight the need for novel therapeutic approaches that could meaningfully increase the survival and decrease recurrences in patients with HNSCC.

Recently, a number of preclinical studies have unveiled the role of certain chromatin modifiers in the pathogenesis, development and progression of HNSCC [5]. Papillon-Cavanagh et al [6] reported a subset of HPV-negative HNSCC tumors (13 %) characterized by global DNA hypomethylation, non-overlapping recurrent K36M (p.Lys36Met) mutations and inactivating mutations in NSD1, which encodes for the protein lysine methyltransferase Nuclear Receptor Binding SET Domain Protein 1 [6,7]. Phenotypically, these tumors were enriched in cellular differentiation pathways, suggesting that NSD1 mutations may promote carcinogenesis through differentiation arrest in keratinocytes [6]. Additional studies have shown that NSD1 mutations sensitize HPV-negative HNSCC cells to cisplatin, and are associated with improved response to chemoradiotherapy and OS in HPV-negative HNSCC patients [8–10]. Furthermore, NSD1 mutations in HPV-negative HNSCC are associated with a “cold” phenotype characterized by decreased CD8 + T-cell infiltration and transcriptional downregulation of IFN-related genes mediated by reduced H3K36me2 and antagonistic occupancy of their promoters by H3K27me3 [11,12]. NSD2 and NSD3, both members of the NSD family of protein lysine methyltransferases, are overexpressed in HNSCC and they promote cell cycle progression through H3K36me2-mediated transcriptional regulation of cell cycle regulators in both HPV-positive and HPV-negative HNSCC cell lines [13,14]. Moreover, NSD2 directly monomethylates linker histone H1.4 at lysine K85, upregulating the expression of OCT4 and inducing stemness features in HPV-negative HNSCC cells [15]. NSD3 has been reported to monomethylate the epidermal growth factor receptor at lysine K721, constitutively activating the downstream ERK pathway in an EGF-independent manner and promoting DNA replication in HPV-negative HNSCC cells [16]. These, as well as additional reports in the literature [5], support that chromatin modifiers may have significant roles in the pathogenesis of certain subtypes of HNSCC.

In this study, we conducted a systematic bioinformatics-based interrogation of genetic and transcriptomic aberrations of chromatin factors in HNSCC. Herein, we define chromatin factors as proteins that chemically modify the DNA and histones, remodel chromatin and regulate nucleosome occupancy. Our findings highlight that aberrant chromatin biology may be the causal driver for specific subsets of HNSCC tumors, underlining the importance of investigating the mechanisms through which chromatin factors induce oncogenesis and cancer progression in this disease.

Materials and methods

Definition of chromatin factors and bulk RNA-seq analysis

We compiled a list of 484 chromatin factors (Supplementary Table 1) based on their function reported in the literature as modifiers, readers or erasers of specific protein or DNA modification marks, histone variants and chromatin remodelers as follows: (1) Factors related to protein methylation, including protein lysine methyltransferases (PKMTs), protein arginine methyltransferases (PRMTs), protein demethylases (PDMTs), malignant brain tumor domain proteins (MBTs), chromodomains, PHD finger domain and Tudor domain proteins, (2) factors related to protein acetylation, including histone acetyltransferases, histone deacetylases and bromodomain containing proteins, (3) factors related to sumoylation, including the SUMO family of proteins and protein desumoylases, (4) factors related to histone ubiquitination, such as histone ubiquitin ligases and histone deubiquitinating proteins, (5) factors related to DNA methylation, including DNA methyltransferases, methylcytosine oxidases (TETs), methylcytosine binding proteins (MBDs), bromo-adjacent homology proteins (BAH), (6) chromatin remodeling complexes, including the SWI/SNF family, the ISWI family (NURF, ACF and CHRAC complexes), the chromodomain helicase DNA binding (CHD) family and the inositol requiring 80 (INO80) family of proteins, (7) histones and histone variants. This functional classification is depicted in a bar graph format in Table 1.

Of these 484 factors, we excluded genes with no expression levels available in the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) HNSCC FireHose Legacy HNSCC dataset, with 422 factors remaining for the analysis. Genetic alterations and RNA-sequencing expression data were utilized from this dataset for these 422 factors. Expression changes in tumor samples (n = 530) were compared to normal squamous epithelium samples from the TCGA (n = 44). Analyses were conducted separately in HPV-negative (n = 427) and HPV-positive (n = 99) HNSCC samples. Translocations were not included. This analysis was focused predominantly on mutated, amplified or overexpressed chromatin factors.

Processing of single-cell and spatial transcriptomics datasets

For single-cell transcriptomics, our analysis was based on the dataset from Puram et al [17], with the gene expression matrix, corresponding cell and sample metadata acquired from the Curated Cancer Cell Atlas [18]. Using the R package Seurat, we processed the dataset by constructing a Seurat object and incorporating meta information. For spatial transcriptomics, our analysis utilized Seurat objects from 12 samples of the Arora et al. dataset [19]. Dominant cell types within this dataset were annotated based on the Puram et al. dataset [17]. CD8 + T cells were specifically identified as cells with CD8A expression greater than 0 and CD4 expression less than or equal to 0. Besides, the proximity between malignant cell and CD8 + T cell was defined and integrated into the Seurat object for each of the 12 samples, which subsequently served as the basis for our downstream analyses.

Statistical methods

For comparisons of gene or protein expression between paired groups, statistical significance was assessed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. For the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), adjusted p-values were calculated using the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) method to control the false discovery rate (FDR). These adjusted p-values were derived from the fgsea function in the R package fgsea. For the correlation analysis between CD8A and chromatin factor gene expression, unadjusted p-values from Student’s t-test were employed. This approach is justified given the use of the p-value threshold of 0.01, which corresponds to an estimated FDR of approximately 1 %. Genes anti-correlated with CD8A were further filtered using a correlation coefficient (rho) threshold of −0.2.

Enrichment analysis for genes anti-correlated with CD8A mRNA

Genes anti-correlated with CD8A, including both chromatin and non-chromatin factors (rho < −0.2 and p < 0.01), were ranked in ascending order based on their Pearson correlation value with CD8A mRNA. In the running enrichment score calculation, a value of + 1 was added for each chromatin factor encountered, while a penalty of −1/(length(rho) − length(gene_set)) was applied for each non-chromatin factor. The y-axis reflects the enrichment trend of anti-correlated chromatin factors.

Results

Head and neck and other squamous cell carcinomas are amongst the cancer types with the highest frequency of genetic alterations in chromatin factors

To evaluate the cumulative frequency of genetic alterations (mutations, amplifications, deletions) in HNSCC compared to other cancer types, we interrogated 42 chromatin factors with alternation frequency >=5% in 32 cancer studies within the TCGA. HNSCC was the 9th most frequently altered cancer type for these chromatin factors, with a cumulative frequency of genetic alterations at 78 % (Supplementary Fig. 1). More specifically, of 523 HNSCC tumors, approximately 32.5 % carried mutations, ~15 % had amplifications, ~3% had deep deletions and 27.5 % had multiple alterations. Bladder and lung squamous cell carcinomas were the two most frequently altered cancer types, followed by esophageal adenocarcinoma and melanoma. Cervical squamous cell carcinomas were the 11th most frequently altered cancer type. These data suggest that chromatin factors may play a particularly prominent role in the pathogenesis of squamous cell carcinomas.

Mutational landscape of chromatin factors in HNSCC

The families of protein methyltransferases (PMTs) and demethylases (PDMTs), protein acetyltransferases and bromodomain-containing proteins which “read” acetylation marks, and the family of chromatin remodelers represented the top three chromatin factor families most frequently mutated in both HPV-negative and HPV-positive HNSCC, with members affected with a frequency of approximately 3–17 % (Fig. 1, Supplementary Fig. 2).

More specifically, in HPV-negative HNSCC tumors, MLL2 (KMT2D), NSD1, MLL3 (KMT2C), EP300, PRDM9 and CREBBP were the top six most frequently mutated chromatin factors. The protein lysine methyltransferase mixed-lineage leukemia 2 (MLL2) was the most frequently mutated chromatin factor in HPV-negative HNSCC, with 17 % of patients having mutations in this enzyme (Fig. 1). MLL2 is one of the four orthologues of the mixed-lineage leukemia genes (MLL1–4), which were initially linked to cancer through their founding member MLL1 found to be involved in translocation events in childhood leukemias [20]. MLL2 is the 12th most frequently mutated gene in HPV-negative HNSCC (Supplementary Table 2). Of the mutations in MLL2, ~11 % are predicted to be inactivating and ~ 5 % missense. Additionally, MLL3 is mutated in 9 % of HPV-negative HNSCC tumors, and MLL1 (KMT2A) is mutated in 5 % of these tumors. Interestingly, the mutational patterns of these protein methyltransferases are mutually exclusive, suggesting independent and distinct roles in the pathogenesis of HPV-negative HNSCC. Together, mutations in MLL1, MLL2, and MLL3 are present in approximately 30 % of HPV-negative HNSCC tumors, highlighting the importance of these lysine methyltransferases in the pathogenesis of HPV-negative HNSCCs.

The second most frequently mutated chromatin factor and 19th most frequently mutated gene (Supplementary Table 2) in HPV-negative HNSCC tumors is NSD1, with 12 % of tumors carrying inactivating or missense mutations (Fig. 1). Other frequently mutated chromatin factors in HPV-negative HSNCC are the homologous histone acetyltransferases CREB-Binding-Protein/E1A Binding Protein (CREBBP/EP300) (14 % combined mutation frequency), which facilitate transcription through histone acetylation, and PR/SET Domain 9 (PRDM9), a protein lysine methyltransferase that trimethylates histone 3 at both lysines 4 and 36, and reorganizes nucleosomes to enable DNA double-strand breaks required for DNA exchanges during meiosis [21]. The aforementioned mutational pattern of chromatin factors was further validated in the HPV-negative HNSCC cohort of the CPTAC as well as the lung squamous cell carcinoma cohort of the TCGA (Supplementary Fig. 3A, B), which have been reported to have similar genetic backgrounds [22].

In HPV-positive HNSCC tumors, the mutational pattern of chromatin factors resembled that of HPV-negative HNSCC tumors, with CREBBP/EP300 (combined mutation frequency 18 %), MLL2 (12 %), NSD1 (11 %) and MLL5 (KMT2E) (7 %) constituting the most frequently mutated chromatin factors (Fig. 1). The MLL (MLL2, MLL3, MLL5) family of genes were the most frequently mutated chromatin factors in HPV-positive HNSCC tumors, with a cumulative frequency of 23 %. This mutational pattern was further validated in cervical squamous cell carcinomas (TCGA cohort) (Supplementary Fig. 3E), which are HPV-positive in their vast majority and have a similar genetic background as HPV-positive HNSCCs [22].

To assess the effect of the two most frequently mutated chromatin factors, NSD1 and MLL2, on overall survival (OS), Kaplan-Meier analysis was conducted using the HPV-negative and HPV-positive HNSCC cohorts of the TCGA. As previously reported, NSD1 mutations rendered a significant OS advantage in HPV-negative HNSCC tumors [8–10], which remained significant after multivariate analysis (Supplementary Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 4). In HPV-positive HNSCC tumors, NSD1 mutations were not associated with an impact on OS (HR = 1.15, log-rank p = 0.79) (Supplementary Fig. 4), however this effect was lost in multivariate analysis (Supplementary Table 4). For MLL2 mutations, while there was a significant favorable impact on OS in univariate analysis for HPV-positive HNSCC patients (HR = 0.22, log-rank p = 0.04), this was lost in multivariate analysis (Supplementary Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 4). Interestingly, in HPV-negative HNSCC patients, MLL2 mutations were associated with a trend for worse OS (HR = 1.35, log-rank p = 0.08), which was though also lost in multivariate analysis. This finding suggests distinct and differential functions of MLL2 based on the cell context (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Amplification landscape of chromatin factors in HNSCC

To evaluate the most frequently amplified chromatin factors in HNSCC, we utilized the TCGA HNSCC genomics cohort and conducted GISTIC analysis in the HPV-negative and HPV-positive patient cohorts separately. In HPV-negative HNSCC, eight significantly recurrent focal amplifications containing genes encoding chromatin factors were identified (Fig. 2A-B, Supplementary Table 3). Amplification of the 3q26.33 chromosomal locus was detected in 19 % of tumors, and included ACTL6A, which encodes for Actin-like protein 6A, a component of the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex, as well as FXR1 (19 %), which encodes for the Fragile X-Related protein 1, an RNA-binding protein with a Tudor domain that reads methylated arginine or lysine residues. In addition, SENP2 and SENP5, encoding for SUMO specific peptidases 2 and 5 respectively, enzymes that process SUMO precursors to enable sumoylation conjugation reactions, were found highly amplified in 19 % and 18 % of tumors at the 3q27.2 and 3q29 loci respectively. ATAD2, a bromodomain containing protein that reads acetylated lysines, PHF20L1, a PHD finger protein that reads methylated lysines, and CHRAC1, which encodes for the Chromatin Accessibility Complex Subunit 1, are located within 8q24 and were co-amplified at a frequency of 11 %. BRD9 (SMARCI2), a component of the SWI/SNF complex that enables binding of the complex to acetylated lysines, was also found recurrently amplified in 7 % of HPV-negative HNSCC tumors (5p15.33). 8 % of HPV-negative HNSCC tumors had recurrent amplifications in NSD3 at the 8p11.23 locus. NSD3 is a protein lysine methyltransferase which methylates H3K36 to activate its downstream targets. ASH2L (8p11.23), which recognizes methylated lysines, was also co-amplified with NSD3 (8p11.23). KMT5B (SUV420H1), a H4K20 methyltransferase, is located within 11q13 and was amplified in approximately 7 % of HPV-negative HNSCC tumors. This amplification pattern was further validated in the HPV-negative HNSCC cohort of the CPTAC as well as the lung squamous cell carcinoma cohort of the TCGA (Supplementary Fig. 3C, D).

Similar to the mutational patterns, HPV-positive HNSCC tumors showed an amplification pattern that resembled that of HPV-negative HNSCC tumors, at least for the top 4 chromatin factors ACTL6A, FXR1, SENP2 and SENP5. ACTL6A and FXR1 were co-amplified in 24 % of HPV-positive HNSCC tumors and stood out as the most frequently amplified chromatin factors. SENP2 and SENP5 were amplified with a frequency of 21 % and 20 % respectively. These four chromatin factors residing in the same 3q26 amplicon were co-amplified, signifying potential synergism or passenger alterations (Fig. 2A-B). The next most frequently amplified chromatin factor was DNMT3B (20q11.21), with an amplification frequency of 9 %. CHRAC1 was amplified in 7 % of HPV-positive HNSCC tumors. This amplification pattern was validated in the cervical squamous cell carcinoma cohort of the TCGA (Supplementary Fig. 3F).

To assess whether these amplified genes were also associated with higher mRNA and protein levels, we utilized the publicly available HNSCC cohorts of the TCGA (HPV-negative, HPV-positive) and the CPTAC proteogenomic database (HPV-negative) [23] and compared the mRNA and protein expression levels of amplified genes with a frequency of >=7% and available data in HNSCC tumors with or without the respective gene amplifications (control tumors). In HPV-negative HNSCC, all amplified genes were associated with significantly higher mRNA levels (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Fig. 5). Utilizing the CPTAC proteomic database for HPV-negative HNSCC tumors, ACTL6A, SENP2, FXR1, CHRAC1, PHF20L1, NSD3 and ASH2L protein levels reached or were significantly higher in amplified versus non-amplified tumors, though the number of available HPV-negative HSNCC tumors with the relevant amplifications were quite low (Supplementary Fig. 5). KMT5B was associated with significantly higher mRNA levels in HPV-negative HNSCC, however no protein data were available (Supplementary Fig. 5). Similarly, in HPV-positive HNSCC tumors, all amplified genes (ACTL6A, SENP2, SENP5, FXR1, DNMT3B, CHRAC1) were associated with a trend or significantly higher mRNA levels in the amplified versus non-amplified tumors (Fig. 2D, Supplementary Fig. 6).

To gain insight on the effect of the most frequently amplified chromatin modifiers on the transcriptome of HPV-negative and HPV-positive HNSCC tumors, GSEA analysis for ACTL6A, SENP2 and FXR1 was conducted using the respective cohorts of the TCGA. In HPV-negative HNSCC, pathways related to immune response (INTERFERON GAMMA and ALPHA RESPONSE), cell cycling and proliferation (E2F TARGETS, G2M CHECKPOINT, MYC TARGETS V1) were commonly enriched for all three chromatin modifiers, as expected given their common presence in the 3q amplicon (Supplementary Fig. 7A). In HPV-positive HNSCC, pathways related to metabolism (HYPOXIA, OXIDATIVE PHOSPHORYLATION), cell cycling and proliferation (G2M CHECKPOINT, MYC TARGETS V1) and epithelial/mesenchymal transition were predominantly enriched (Supplementary Fig. 7B). This difference between the pathways enriched in HPV-negative versus HPV-positive HNSCC may signify either a differential effect of these genes based on HPV-status, or may be related to the significantly lower number of HPV-positive HNSCC tumor samples.

To evaluate the impact of these amplified chromatin factors on survival, Kaplan-Meier analysis was conducted in the same datasets using mRNA or protein stratification. In HPV-negative HNSCC, patients with amplification of ACTL6A, SENP2, SENP5, FXR1, ATAD2 and KMT5B had significantly worse OS, supporting the potential importance of these chromatin factors as oncogenic drivers in HPV-negative HNSCC (Fig. 2E, Supplementary Fig. 8). Multivariate analysis for stage, age, gender and race showed that this effect remained significant (p < 0.05) for ACTL6A, SENP2, FXR1, ATAD2 and KMT5B, with a trend for significance (p = 0.06) for SENP5 (Fig. 2F, Supplementary Table 4). ACTL6A and FXR1 were further validated in the CPTAC database with protein level stratification, with a trend or a statistically significant impact on OS (Supplementary Fig. 8). NSD3 only showed significance for worse OS at the protein level (p = 0.025, CPTAC), but not at the mRNA level (TCGA) (Supplementary Fig. 8). CHRAC1, PHF20L1, BRD9 and ASH2L did not reach significance in either database, suggesting either passenger amplifications or involvement of these factors in earlier stages of HPV-negative HNSCC development. In HPV-positive HNSCC, CHRAC1 and FXR1 showed a trend or were significantly associated with worse OS in univariate analysis (Fig. 2E, Supplementary Fig. 8), with CHRAC1 remaining significant after multivariate analysis (Fig. 2F, Supplementary Table 4), but not FXR1. ACTL6A, SENP2 and SENP5 did not have an impact on OS.

A summary of the top chromatin factors most frequently altered by mutations or amplifications in HPV-negative and HPV-positive HNSCC is shown in Table 2.

The most frequently genetically altered chromatin factors are shared between HPV-positive and HPV-negative HNSCC tumors

We then assessed whether specific chromatin factors with mutations are differentially enriched in HNSCC tumors based on HPV-status. Interestingly, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) showed that both HPV-positive and HPV-negative HNSCC tumors were enriched for mutations in chromatin factors related to protein methylation, such as PKMTs, PDMTs, PHD finger and chromodomain proteins, as well as factors related to protein acetylation, such as bromodomain proteins (Supplementary Fig. 2). The MLL genes (33 % HPV-negative, 26 % HPV-positive HNSCC), NSD1 (12 % HPV-negative, 11 % HPV-positive HNSCC) and EP300/CREBBP (14 % HPV-negative, 18 % HPV-positive HNSCC) were the most frequently mutated chromatin factor genes and defined distinct, mutually exclusive subgroups of HNSCC patients. Regarding amplifications, the most frequently amplified genes in both HPV-positive and HPV-negative HNSCC were located in the 3q amplicon and included ACTL6A (19 % HPV-negative, 24 % HPV-positive HNSCC), SENP2/SENP5 (37 % HPV-negative, 41 % HPV-positive HNSCC) and FXR1 (19 % HPV-negative, 24 % HPV-positive HNSCC).

Co-occurrence of genetic alterations in chromatin and non-chromatin factor genes in HNSCC

We then sought to interrogate the co-occurrence and mutual exclusivity patterns of the top ten most frequently genetically altered chromatin and non-chromatin factor genes. In HPV-negative HNSCC, amplifications in SENP2 and ACTL6A co-occurred significantly with PIK3CA amplifications (q < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 9A). ACTL6A, SENP2, SENP5 and FXR1 also co-occurred significantly (p < 0.001, q < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 9A). Given their co-localization on the 3q amplicon and the established oncogenic role of PIK3CA in HNSCC, it would be important to decipher whether SENP2 and ACTL6A act as independent driver oncogenes, they synergize with PIK3CA or represent merely passenger amplifications (Supplementary Fig. 9A). Similarly, it will be important to determine whether the chromatin factors ACTL6A, SENP2, SENP5 and FXR1, co-amplified on the 3q amplicon, exhibit oncogenic synergy or represent passenger alterations. Another significant co-occurrence was observed between PHF20L1 and MYC (q < 0.001). Both genes reside within the 8q24 genomic locus, and given MYC’s established role as an oncogene, it would be similarly important to determine whether PHF20L1 functions as an independent oncogene, synergizes with MYC or represents a passenger amplification (Supplementary Fig. 9A). On the same amplicon, PHF20L1, ATAD2 and CHRAC1 also co-occurred significantly (q < 0.001).

In HPV-positive HNSCC, ACTL6A and SENP2 amplifications co-occurred with TP63 and PIK3CA amplifications (q < 0.001), all localized in the amplified 3q27–29 locus (Supplementary Fig. 9B). Within the same 3q amplicon, the chromatin factors ACTL6A, SENP2, SENP5 and FXR1 were also co-amplified (q <0.001), mirroring the pattern observed in HPV-negative HNSCC tumors. Interestingly, CREBBP and NSD1 mutations also exhibited significant co-occurence (q = 0.007), suggesting potential functional synergy, albeit only in a very small fraction of patients (<=3%).

Transcriptomic alterations of chromatin factors in HNSCC

We then assessed the top chromatin factors that were over- or underexpressed in HPV-negative and HPV-positive HNSCC compared to normal buccal squamous epithelium. We presumed that amongst the overexpressed chromatin factors, some may have putative oncogenic functions, while amongst the underexpressed chromatin factors, some may have putative tumor suppressive functions in HNSCC. We defined over- or underexpression as significantly increased or decreased mRNA expression of a gene by at least 30 % compared to normal squamous epithelium (Supplementary Table 4). Of these chromatin factors, the ones that were genetically altered (mutated, amplified or deleted) were excluded in order to focus on those factors that are most likely regulated through epigenetically mediated mechanisms. Table 3 shows the top twelve over- or under-expressed chromatin factors in HPV-negative and HPV-positive HNSCC (Table 3).

In HPV-negative HNSCC, the majority of the top twelve overexpressed genes were involved in processes of protein methylation as readers or writers of methylated histones. Readers of methylated histones included PHD finger proteins, such as Double PHD Fingers 1 (DPF1), the SP100 nuclear antigen and SP140 nuclear body protein, chromodomains, such as chromobox homolog 2 (CBX2), chromobox homolog 3 (CBX3) and chromodomain Y like 2 (CDYL2), and Tudor domains, such as UHRF1 which reads H3K9me3. Writers of methylated histones included SET-and-MYND-domain containing 3 (SMYD3) and PR/SET domain 13 (PRDM13). SMYD3 has already been reported to function as a major oncogenic driver in HPV-negative HNSCC with studies showing that its depletion is associated with decreased colony formation, cell cycling and invasion of HPV-negative HNSCC cells [24], as well as resistance to immunotherapy with pembrolizumab [25,26].

In HPV-positive HNSCC, similar to HPV-negative HNSCC, the majority of chromatin factors identified as overexpressed were also involved in protein methylation processes as readers, writers or erasers of methylated histones. Readers included PHD finger proteins, such as transcription factor 19 (TCF19), Bromodomain Adjacent To Zinc Finger Domain 1B (BAZ1B), Inhibitor Of Growth Family Member 1 (ING1) and Pygopus Family PHD Finger 2 (PYGO2), chromodomains, such as Chromodomain Helicase DNA Binding Protein 3 (CHD3), and Tudor domains, such as UHRF1. Writers were the protein lysine methyltransferase PRDM15, and erasers included the protein demethylase KDM3A. KDM3A, which demethylates the repressive mark H3K9me2/3, has been reported as a putative oncogene promoting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and proliferation in HPV-negative HNSCC [27,28].

Overall, these findings support a prominent role of protein methylation processes in the pathobiology of both HPV-negative and HPV-positive HNSCC.

Chromatin factors associated with “cold” HNSCC tumors

One of the main causes of resistance to immunotherapy with pembrolizumab in HNSCC is a “cold” tumor microenvironment (TME), characterized by low intratumoral CD8 + T-cell infiltration [29–31]. Certain protein methyltransferases have been associated with this phenotype, particularly in HPV-negative HNSCC [11,12,25,26,32]. Specifically, NSD1 mutations induce a “cold” TME through antagonistic occupancy and transcriptional downregulation of IFN-response genes by EZH2-mediated H3K27me3 in HPV-negative HNSCC tumors [11,12]. SMYD3, a protein lysine methyltransferase that is overexpressed in ~60–80 % of HPV-negative HNSCC tumors, is also associated with the “cold” tumor phenotype and poor response to neoadjuvant pembrolizumab through repression of cancer-cell intrinsic type I IFN responses [25,26].

To systematically evaluate the role of chromatin factors as possible biological culprits for the “cold” phenotype of HNSCC tumors, mRNA expression levels of chromatin factor genes (Supplementary Table 1 and CD8A were correlated in the HPV-negative and HPV-positive cohorts of the TCGA (Firehose Legacy). Fig. 3A-B and Supplementary Fig. 10 show the panel of chromatin factors most negatively correlated with CD8A mRNA expression (r <=−0.2, p-value < 0.01) in HPV-negative and in HPV-positive HNSCC tumors, categorized by chromatin factor family. In HPV-negative HNSCC, chromatin factors involved in protein methylation stood out as the prevalent family associated with the “cold” tumor phenotype. Compared to all chromatin factors examined (Supplementary Table 1), mRNA levels of chromodomain methylation readers such as CBX1, CBX8, CDYL, DPF1, and protein methyltransferases/demethylases such as SMYD3, SMYD2, KDM5B and KMT5B, were amongst the top 20 most strongly correlated factors with the “cold” phenotype of HPV-negative HNSCC tumors (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Fig. 10). The family of chromatin remodelers, comprising factors such as ACTL6A, SMARCA1, MORF4L2 and SS18L1, was the second most prevalent family of chromatin factors associated with the “cold” phenotype. Notably, mRNA levels of ACTL6A, which encodes a core component of the chromatin remodeling complex, demonstrated the strongest negative correlation (r ~ −0.3) with CD8A mRNA levels in HPV-negative HNSCC tumors (Fig. 3B). Chromatin factors involved in protein sumoylation, protein acetylation, ubiquitination, DNA methylation as well as the histone variant H2BC15 were less abundant but also correlated with the “cold” phenotype of HPV-negative HNSCC tumors (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Fig. 10). A number of these factors were also validated in the lung SCC TCGA cohort (FireHose Legacy, Supplementary Fig. 11). The correlations of the chromatin remodeling components ACTL6A and SMARCA1, and the methylation factors SMYD3, KDM5B, CBX8, FXR1 and CHD1 with CD8A were further validated at the protein level in the CPTAC HPV-negative cohort (Supplementary Fig.12).

In HPV-positive HNSCC tumors, a similar pattern was observed, with chromatin factors involved in protein methylation (methylation readers CDX8, CHD7, DPF1, demethylases KDM1A, KDM5B, PRDM11, PRDM12, the protein arginine methyltransferase PRMT8), chromatin remodeling (MORF4L2, SMARCA1) and also protein acetylation (BRD7, GTF3C1/2) representing the most abundant families of chromatin factors correlating with the “cold” phenotype of HPV-positive HNSCC tumors (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Fig. 10C). Interestingly, and similarly to the HPV-negative tumors, mRNA levels of a chromatin remodeler, MORF4L2, demonstrated one of the strongest negative correlations (r = −0.45) with CD8A mRNA levels (Fig. 3B).

To assess where the chromatin factor genes “fall” in terms of their magnitude of their association with CD8A mRNA levels compared to other non-chromatin factor genes in HNSCC tumors, we conducted enrichment analysis using the HPV-negative and HPV-positive cohorts of the Firehose Legacy (TCGA). In HPV-negative HNSCC, a total of 835 genes were identified as anti-correlated with CD8A mRNA levels, among which 23 were chromatin factors (Supplementary Table 5A, Supplementary Fig. 13). Notably, 13 of these 23 chromatin factors were ranked among the top 443 genes, while only 9 were positioned between ranks 646 and 835. In HPV-positive HNSCC, 720 genes were anti-correlated with CD8A mRNA levels, with 19 being chromatin factors (Supplementary Table 5B, Supplementary Fig. 13). Compared to the HPV-negative group, the chromatin factors in HPV-positive tumors showed a more uniform distribution across the ranked list, with only 7 of the 19 factors ranked within the top 200 genes. Given that intratumoral CD8 + T-cell infiltration is driven by the CD8 + T-cell attracting chemokines CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11 and CCL5, we next evaluated whether the identified chromatin factors negatively correlated with CD8A mRNA levels were also inversely correlated with these chemokines, potentially implicating them in the transcriptional repression of the respective genes. Indeed, of the 20 factors identified in HPV-negative HNSCC tumors (Fig. 3A), 18 showed significant negative correlation (r<=−0.2, p <0.01) with at least one of the aforementioned CD8 + T-cell attracting chemokines (Fig. 3C). Similarly, 18 of the 19 factors identified in HPV-positive HNSCC tumors were inversely correlated with CD8 + T-cell attracting chemokines (Fig. 3C). In HPV-negative HNSCC tumors, the chromatin methylation factors PRDM13, CBX8 and SMYD3, as we previously reported [24–26], together with the protein sumoylation factor SENP2 and the chromatin remodeler SMARCA1 showed the strongest negative correlation with these chemokines (−0.4 ≤ r ≤ −0.2) (Fig. 3C). In HPV-positive HNSCC tumors, the chromatin remodelers MORF4L2 and SMARCA1, the DNA methylation factor MTA3 and the histone ubiquitination factor RNF2 were the factors most strongly correlated with CD8 + T-cell attracting chemokines, especially with CCL5 (−0.47 ≤ r ≤ −0.28) (Fig. 3C).

We then conducted GSEA pathway analysis on all identified chromatin factors separately in HPV-negative and HPV-positive tumors (FireHose Legacy, TCGA) and found that most of the identified factors that were inversely correlated with CD8 + T-cell attracting chemokines showed significant enrichment for interferon (IFN) response-related pathways (Supplementary Fig. 14–15). Notably, the chromatin remodeling complex emerged as a key group of factors associated with the “cold“ tumor phenotype and IFN response-related pathways in both HPV-negative and HPV-positive HNSCC tumors (Fig. 3A, 3D, Supplementary Fig. 14 and 15). Within this complex, components such as ACTL6A, SMARCA1, SS18L1, DPF1 and MORF4L2, were amongst the top factors most strongly anti-correlated with the mRNA levels of CD8A and/or CD8 + T-cell attracting chemokines (Fig. 3B-C, Supplementary Fig. 10). As expected, these factors were also significantly overexpressed in “cold” compared to “hot” tumors across both HPV-negative and HPV-positive HNSCC cohorts (Fig. 3E).

MORF4L2 correlates with HLA class I gene expression in cancer cells and with activation gene signatures of proximal CD8 + T-cells in HPV-negative HNSCC tumors

Given the identified common role of certain of the chromatin remodeling complex components in “cold” phenotypes of both HPV-negative and HPV-positive HNSCC tumors (SMARCA1, MORF4L2, DPF1, ACTL6A), we decided to further interrogate the expression profiles and spatial interactions of cells expressing these components using publicly available single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics datasets [17,19]. Of these components, we focused on MORF4L2, as the expression levels of ACTL6A, SMARCA1 and DPF1 were very low and not evaluable in the spatial transcriptomics datasets. Furthermore, we focused on HPV-negative tumors given the greater availability of samples with publicly available scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics data.

To evaluate the predominant cell type(s) expressing MORF4L2, we utilized a publicly available scRNA-seq dataset of oral cavity HNSCC tumors [17] and found that MORF4L2 demonstrated the highest mRNA expression levels in cancer cells, mast cells, endothelial cells, fibroblast cells and macrophages, which have antigen presentation capacity, and the lowest levels in T-cells (Fig. 4A). Using the same dataset, we then sought to assess whether the mRNA levels of MORF4L2 correlate with the expression levels of chemokines, antigen presentation machinery and HLA class I molecules in HPV-negative HNSCC cancer cells. Due to the very low levels of CCL5, CXCL9, CXCL10 and CXCL11 mRNA levels, we could not identify correlations with these, however, cancer cells with higher MORF4L2 mRNA levels demonstrated significantly lower mRNA levels of HLA-G, HLA-DQB1, HLA-DRB1 and HLA-DRA (Fig. 4B), suggesting that MORF4L2 may have a silencing effect in the expression of these genes.

Based on this result, we hypothesized that cancer cells with lower expression of MORF4L2 have greater antigen presentation capacity and that CD8 + T-cells in proximity may have a more activated/cytotoxic phenotype compared to CD8 + T-cells in proximity to cancer cells with higher expression of MORF4L2. To explore this hypothesis, we utilized a publicly available spatial transcriptomics dataset by Arora et al for HPV-negative HNSCC tumors [19], which included 12 samples. Supplementary Table 6 presents the number of CD8 + T-cells and malignant cells available in each sample and in relation to their spatial proximity to cancer cells (defined as Euclidean distance between cancer and CD8 + T-cells < 8) with high versus low expression of MORF4L2 mRNA levels. Of these samples, we proceeded to analyze samples 1, 2, 9 and 10, based on the following criteria: (a) a clear definition of activated versus exhausted CD8 + T-cells in proximity to cancer cells using a gene signature consisting of CD8 + T-cell activation and exhaustion markers (activation markers: CD8A, PRF1, CD69, GZMB, IL2RA, IFNG; exhaustion markers: PDCD1, LAG3, ENTPD1, HAVCR2, TIGIT, TOX, CXCL12, ITGAE), (b) > 10 evaluable CD8 + T-cells and > 10 malignant cells. Fig. 4C shows the spatial localization of CD8 + T-cells in relation to cancer cells within sections of samples 2, 9 and 10. To evaluate whether the levels of MORF4L2 mRNA in cancer cells correlate with gene signatures of activation/exhaustion in CD8 + T-cells, we identified cancer cells in close proximity with CD8 + T-cells (defined as Euclidean distance between cancer and CD8 + T-cells < 8) as described above, and classified these cancer cells as low versus high MORF4L2 expressors (Fig. 4D). Interestingly, in the 3 out of the 4 examined samples (2, 9 and 10), we found that CD8 + T-cells in proximity to low expressing MORF4L2 cancer cells demonstrated higher levels of activation/exhaustion markers compared to CD8 + T-cells in proximity to high expressing MORF4L2 cancer cells (Fig. 4E). These results were further validated using the bulk RNA-seq dataset for the HPV-negative HNSCC cohort of the Firehose Legacy, where higher mRNA levels of MORF4L2 were negatively correlated with the mRNA levels of 9 of the 12 aforementioned activation/exhaustion genes (Supplementary Fig. 16). Furthermore, the number of CD8 + T-cells in proximity to cancer cells with low MORF4L2 mRNA expression was significantly higher compared to CD8 + T-cells in proximity to cancer cells with high MORF4L2 mRNA levels, possibly suggesting greater chemotaxis of CD8 + T-cells towards cancer cells with higher antigen presenting capacity (Fig. 4F, Supplementary Table 6). These findings support that lower MORF4L2 mRNA levels may be associated with higher antigen presentation capacity of HPV-negative HNSCC cancer cells.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically analyzed the genomic and transcriptomic alteration patterns of chromatin factors in HPV-negative and HPV-positive HNSCC using publicly available datasets. We found that HNSCC ranks amongst the top ten cancer types with the highest genetic alterations in chromatin factors. Notably, squamous cell carcinomas of the lung and bladder manifest the highest prevalence of such genetic alterations, signifying a prominent role for chromatin factors in the pathogenesis of squamous cell carcinomas.

HPV-negative HNSCCs frequently manifest genetic alterations in chromatin factors involved in chromatin remodeling, with amplifications in ACTL6A (19 %) and CHRAC1 (11 %), protein sumoylation, with amplifications in SENP2 (18 %) and SENP5 (19 %), protein methylation, with mutations in MLL2/3 (26 %), NSD1 (12 %) and amplifications in NSD3 (8 %), and protein acetylation, with mutations in CREBBP/EP300 (14 %). Similarly, HPV-positive HNSCCs show a comparable alteration profile, with frequent amplifications of the chromatin remodeler ACTL6A (24 %), the sumoylation enzymes SENP2 and SENP5 (20–21 %), the DNA methyltransferase DNMT3B (9 %), along with mutations found in CREBBP/EP300 (18 %), MLL2/3 (16 %) and NSD1 (11 %). These genetic alterations suggest the presence of distinct subsets of HNSCC patients with chromatin factor-driven pathobiology that warrant further investigation into the relevant mechanisms, oncogenic phenotypes and potential therapeutic vulnerabilities. Surprisingly, despite the established differences in their pathogenesis, the overlap in frequently altered chromatin factors between HPV-positive and HPV-negative HNSCCs underscores the presence of shared pathogenetic pathways linked to chromatin dysregulation in the background of squamous cell histology. This hypothesis is further supported by the similar genetic alteration pattern observed in chromatin regulators in lung squamous cell carcinomas. It would be important to determine whether these events tend to occur as early, precursor genetic drivers or, more likely, theyensue later on in the cancer progression process. Of interest, recent studies by the Whetstine group have shown that specific genetic amplifications may not be purely stochastic events, rather they may be dictated by controlled epigenetic processes that promote cancer progression [33,34]. As such, it is possible that an epigenetic regulator(s) dictating the squamous differentiation program may control the development of these amplifications in HNSCCs, regardless of HPV-status, as well as in lung squamous cell carcinomas, explaining the high similarity in the pattern of genetic alterations among these cancer types. Furthermore, amplification of the 3q locus, which harbors ACTL6A, FXR1, SENP2 and SENP5, observed in both HPV-positive and HPV-negative HNSCCs, harbors over 70 genes and the fact that key chromatin modifiers are among them underscores a possible role of epigenetic regulation in HNSCC tumorigenesis and progression. However, functional studies will be necessary to determine whether these chromatin factors, along with other co-amplified genes, function as primary oncogenic drivers or as passengers, or act synergistically to drive the development and progression of HNSCC.

In light of the importance of immunotherapy with pembrolizumab as a first-line treatment for recurrent/metastatic HNSCC patients as well as in the neoadjuvant, curative-intent setting [4,28, 35], we sought to identify chromatin factors significantly associated with the “cold” tumor phenotype that may be responsible for resistance to immunotherapy. Among the top candidates, components of the chromatin remodeling machinery were frequently enriched in HPV-negative and HPV-positive HNSCC tumors. Specifically, ACTL6A and SS18L1 ranked among the top three chromatin factors that most negatively correlated with CD8A mRNA levels in HPV-negative HNSCC tumors, while DPF1 and MORF4L2 were most prominent in HPV-positive HNSCC tumors. Importantly, in HPV-negative HNSCC tumors, ACTL6A, SS18L1 and SMARCA1 exhibited strong negative correlations with the mRNA levels of CD8 + T-cell attracting chemokines CXCL9/10/11 and CCL5. Similarly, in HPV-positive HNSCC tumors, MORF4L2, SMARCA1 and DPF1 were inversely correlated with CCL5, CXCL9 or CXCL10, with more pronounced correlations with CCL5. Our spatial transcriptomic analysis further supported the possible role of MORF4L2 in the regulation of the antigen presentation capacity of HPV-negative HNSCC cancer cells. These findings suggest that specific chromatin remodeling complexes may contribute to CD8 + T-cell exclusion, potentially contributing to immunotherapy resistance in distinct subsets of both HPV-negative and HPV-positive HNSCC patients.

Conclusion

Overall, this study highlights the presence of distinct subsets of HPV-negative and HPV-positive HNSCC tumors that may be epigenetically driven by specific chromatin factors that are either amplified or overexpressed. These chromatin factors warrant further preclinical investigation to elucidate their biological function as oncogenic drivers and evaluate their potential as novel therapeutic targets in this disease.
