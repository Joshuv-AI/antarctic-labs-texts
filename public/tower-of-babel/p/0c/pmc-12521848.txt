Donor-derived cell-free DNA in solid organ transplantation: analytical considerations, diagnostic performance, and clinical interpretation
Authors: Hyunhye Kang, Sang-Ik Cho, Eun-Jee Oh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDonor-derived cell-free DNA (dd-cfDNA) has emerged as a valuable noninvasive biomarker for detecting allograft injury in solid organ transplantation. It is released into the bloodstream from the transplanted organ as a result of cell injury and immune activation, with baseline levels influenced by organ type, tissue turnover, and posttransplant physiological changes. Several analytical platforms are available, including quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR), digital droplet PCR, and next-generation sequencing, each differing in sensitivity, throughput, and reporting format. Commercial assays have been clinically validated across multiple organs. dd-cfDNA can be quantified as a percentage of total cell-free DNA or as an absolute concentration, with diagnostic thresholds varying by platform and organ type. Although dd-cfDNA demonstrates high negative predictive value and can reduce the need for unnecessary biopsies, it is not specific to rejection and may be elevated in the setting of infection, ischemia, or inflammation. Preanalytical and technical factors can also affect test performance. Therefore, dd-cfDNA should be interpreted with careful consideration of biological variation, assay characteristics, and the patient’s clinical context. Future efforts should focus on defining organ-specific thresholds, improving interlaboratory standardization, expanding validation in underrepresented graft types, and assessing cost-effectiveness and clinical impact to support broader clinical adoption.Keywords: Donor-derived, Cell-free DNA, Transplantation, Rejection, Biomarker

INTRODUCTION

Solid organ transplantation has become a life-saving therapy for patients with end-stage organ failure [1]. Despite advances in surgical techniques and immunosuppressive regimens, long-term graft survival remains limited by immune-mediated rejection and chronic allograft dysfunction [2–6]. Accurate and timely detection of allograft injury is therefore essential for improving clinical outcomes. Traditional monitoring strategies rely on nonspecific indicators such as serum creatinine in kidney transplant recipients [7] or liver enzyme levels in liver transplant recipients [8], supplemented by imaging studies and donor-specific antibody (DSA) testing [9]. Although tissue biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosing rejection, it is invasive, carries procedural risks, and frequently yields nondiagnostic results due to sampling error [10–13]. Moreover, subclinical or early-stage rejection often escapes detection using conventional approaches.

In recent years, donor-derived cell-free DNA (dd-cfDNA) has emerged as a promising noninvasive biomarker capable of reflecting graft injury at the molecular level [14]. Circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) consists of DNA fragments released into the bloodstream during cellular apoptosis and necrosis [15]. In transplant recipients, a proportion of cfDNA originates from the donor organ. Elevated dd-cfDNA levels have been linked to active allograft injury, including acute rejection, whereas low levels are generally observed in clinically stable recipients [16–19].

Although evidence increasingly supports its clinical utility [12,17,19–21], dd-cfDNA is a complex biomarker whose diagnostic performance depends not only on the analytical platform used [9,22], but also on interpretation within the context of transplant type, time posttransplant, and concurrent clinical findings [9,22–24]. Additional confounding factors, including infection, leukopenia, mixed chimerism, and technical artifacts, may lead to false-positive or false-negative results [9,25–27].

In this review, we present a comprehensive perspective on dd-cfDNA in solid organ transplantation—addressing its biological underpinnings, measurement technologies, clinical performance, and potential pitfalls. We also highlight sources of diagnostic error and emphasize practical considerations for clinical interpretation. Our aim is to support evidence-based practice and provide direction for future research in the clinical application of dd-cfDNA.

BIOLOGICAL BASIS OF CELL-FREE DNA

cfDNA refers to short fragments of double-stranded DNA that circulate freely in the bloodstream, typically measuring 150–200 base pairs in length [15]. These fragments are primarily released from apoptotic and necrotic cells during normal cellular turnover, as well as in response to inflammation or tissue injury [28,29]. The half-life of cfDNA is estimated to range from 16 minutes to 2.5 hours, due to its rapid degradation and clearance within the body [30]. In healthy individuals, most cfDNA originates from hematopoietic cells, particularly neutrophils and monocytes, with additional contributions from other rapidly renewing tissues such as the gastrointestinal tract and vascular endothelium [31–34].

In transplant recipients, an additional fraction of circulating cfDNA is derived from the donor organ and is termed dd-cfDNA [16,35,36]. The presence of dd-cfDNA reflects ongoing cellular processes within the allograft and serves as a molecular signature of graft integrity [16,37]. Mechanistically, dd-cfDNA is released into the circulation through several pathways, including passive leakage from necrotic graft cells, active release via apoptotic vesicles, and immune-mediated cytotoxicity during rejection episodes [38–40]. Other contributing factors include inflammatory cytokines, ischemia-reperfusion injury, and endothelial injury occurring during graft damage [14,24,39,41,42].

After transplantation, dd-cfDNA levels are transiently elevated during the early postoperative phase because of surgical trauma, ischemia-reperfusion injury, and immune activation. These levels typically decline to a baseline steady state within 7 to 14 days in uncomplicated cases [24,36]. However, the baseline dd-cfDNA concentration varies by organ type [24]. For example, liver transplant recipients often exhibit higher physiological dd-cfDNA levels than heart or kidney recipients, reflecting the larger organ mass, regenerative capacity, and higher parenchymal cell turnover of the liver [16,43].

ANALYTICAL METHODS FOR MEASURING DONOR-DERIVED CELL-FREE DNA

Overview of Measurement Platforms

Several analytical platforms have been developed to quantify dd-cfDNA in transplant recipients, each differing in technological principle, sensitivity, specificity, throughput, and commercial availability. The three most widely applied methods are quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), digital droplet polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR), and next-generation sequencing (NGS). Each relies on distinct principles and offers specific advantages and limitations for dd-cfDNA quantification and interpretation (Table 1).

Real-time qPCR is a widely accessible technique that quantifies specific DNA sequences using fluorescent probes during real-time amplification. Early qPCR-based dd-cfDNA assays focused on the Y-chromosome–specific SRY gene in sex-mismatched transplant recipients [44,45]. A subsequent study by Gadi et al. [46] demonstrated that human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-mismatched alleles could be targeted to quantify dd-cfDNA in simultaneous pancreas-kidney transplant recipients. Expanding beyond single-locus detection, Dauber et al. [47] introduced insertion/deletion polymorphisms as qPCR targets, providing a genotyping-dependent alternative for kidney transplantation. Richmond et al. [48] advanced this approach with a multiplexed single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)-based qPCR method that improved assay specificity and sensitivity in both pediatric and adult heart transplant recipients. qPCR is relatively simple, rapid, and cost-effective, making it practical for resource-limited settings [39,47,49]. Nonetheless, it has notable limitations for dd-cfDNA analysis. Its sensitivity is generally lower, particularly for detecting rare donor variants against the abundant background of recipient cfDNA [50]. Furthermore, its multiplexing capacity is limited, and it usually requires prior donor genotyping to design allele-specific probes [22,50,51].

Next, ddPCR overcomes several limitations of conventional qPCR. By partitioning DNA molecules into thousands of droplets, ddPCR allows absolute quantification without the need for a standard curve [52]. Zou et al. [53] targeted eight HLA-DR alleles to identify donor-specific mismatches in lung transplant recipients, reporting dd-cfDNA as donor fraction (%). Other studies have applied multiplexed copy number variation (CNV) or SNP panels covering up to approximately 40 polymorphic loci [12,40,54,55]. These studies consistently reported dd-cfDNA as a fractional percentage, although some also proposed cutoffs based on absolute quantification. Absolute values are particularly valuable when total cfDNA levels are altered, such as in leukopenia or systemic inflammation, since these conditions can bias donor fraction results. ddPCR offers high sensitivity and precision, making it particularly useful for detecting low levels of dd-cfDNA in stable transplant recipients [23]. However, its drawbacks include limited multiplexing, the need for specialized instrumentation, and longer assay times due to droplet generation and reading [24,56,57].

NGS is the most analytically comprehensive platform, enabling parallel sequencing of numerous genomic regions and broad detection of donor-specific SNPs or epigenetic signatures [58]. NGS-based methods can interrogate hundreds of SNPs in a single run and are often designed to function without prior donor genotyping [26,59–61]. NGS also supports multiplexed sample analysis and benefits from validated commercial pipelines [16,20,21,60,62]. Two widely used commercial platforms are AlloSure (CareDx Inc.) and Prospera (Natera Inc.). AlloSure targets 266 predefined SNPs and was clinically validated in the DART study [16], which demonstrated its utility in detecting rejection in kidney recipients. More recently, a large multicenter study by Aubert et al. [63] showed that incorporating AlloSure %dd-cfDNA into conventional variables improved rejection detection, increasing the area under the curve (AUC) from 0.78 to 0.82. Prospera employs a massively multiplexed panel of >13,000 SNPs and has demonstrated high diagnostic performance in detecting rejection in heart [18], lung [64], and kidney [65] transplantation, with consistently high negative predictive value (NPV) across organs. Comparative studies of AlloSure and Prospera in heart and kidney recipients, where dd-cfDNA was measured simultaneously using both assays, showed largely similar performance [66–68]. However, Lum et al. [68] reported that AlloSure may misidentify T cell-mediated rejection (TCMR) in kidney recipients when using the manufacturer-recommended cutoff of <1%. Adjusting the threshold to <0.5% allowed both assays to successfully identify biopsy-proven cases of rejection. Despite these strengths, NGS assays remain limited by higher costs [24], longer turnaround times of 2 to 3 days from sample collection to result [36], and reliance on advanced bioinformatics for read alignment and donor fraction calculation [24,69].

Technological Advancements in Donor-Derived Cell-Free DNA Measurement

Accurate quantification of dd-cfDNA requires sensitive detection of donor-specific genetic differences [23,53,59,70–73]. Earlier studies employed genotyping-dependent methods, in which allele-specific probes targeted informative SNPs identified through prior donor and recipient genotyping, most commonly using qPCR or ddPCR platforms [74–77]. Although highly sensitive, these assays are limited by constrained multiplexing capacity and the need for customized probe design. By contrast, genotyping-independent methods, primarily based on NGS, use predefined panels of common SNPs and statistical modeling to estimate dd-cfDNA without prior donor genotyping [16,59,60]. Specifically, if the donor and recipient each carry homozygous but different genotypes at a given SNP site, the minor allele frequency detected in the recipient’s plasma indicates the presence of a donor-derived SNP. This approach enables calculation of the dd-cfDNA fraction without requiring separate donor genotyping. These platforms provide high-throughput capability, support multiplexed sample analysis, and are available as commercial kits with automated bioinformatic pipelines.

dd-cfDNA may be reported as either an absolute concentration (e.g., copies per milliliter) or as a fractional percentage of total cfDNA (%dd-cfDNA). The percentage measure is more widely used in clinical practice because it normalizes for variability in cfDNA yield and provides a dynamic view of graft-derived DNA relative to the host background. In this framework, recipient cfDNA constitutes the systemic background signal, whereas dd-cfDNA reflects injury or turnover specifically from the transplanted organ [24,78,79]. A recent study compared ddPCR assays targeting HLA-DRB1 alleles and the Y chromosome, high-throughput sequencing with unique molecular identifiers, and the commercial AlloSeq cfDNA platform for dd-cfDNA quantification. All methods demonstrated strong correlation in %dd-cfDNA, supporting consistency across platforms [70]. They also provided baseline median dd-cfDNA levels in stable recipients. In stable kidney transplant patients, the median %dd-cfDNA was 39.5% in urine (36.6 cp/μmol creatinine) and 0.19% in plasma (5.0 cp/mL), with no correlation between urine and plasma values. In stable liver recipients, the median plasma dd-cfDNA was 2.2% (120 cp/mL), while urinary levels were negligible (<0.1 cp/mL) [70].

In kidney transplantation, dd-cfDNA has been most extensively studied for early and accurate detection of rejection and for risk stratification relative to stable graft function. The multicenter DART trial demonstrated that a 1.0% cutoff distinguished any active rejection from no-rejection with moderate accuracy (AUC, 0.74) [16]. At this 1.0% threshold, specificity reached 85% and sensitivity 59%, corresponding to a PPV of 61% and an NPV of 84%. Importantly, dd-cfDNA levels below 1% virtually excluded active rejection. Subsequent studies focused on antibody-mediated rejection (ABMR). Huang et al. [20] reported that dd-cfDNA levels were significantly higher in patients with biopsy-proven ABMR compared with no-rejection controls (median, 1.35% vs. 0.38%). Using a lower cutoff of 0.74%, this study achieved 100% sensitivity and 71.8% specificity for ABMR (PPV 68.6%, NPV 100%). Similarly, Zhang et al. [80] observed that a 1.0% cutoff yielded 88.9% sensitivity, 73.7% specificity, PPV 76.2%, and NPV 87.5% for ABMR. In practice, dd-cfDNA is most effective as a rule-out test in kidney transplantation: very low levels virtually exclude active ABMR and most significant TCMR, whereas elevated values indicate possible injury [12,16,20,47,55,60,80]. In addition, dd-cfDNA-guided biopsy can enable earlier diagnosis of ABMR in patients with de novo DSA [81]. For example, Bromberg et al. [82] reported that dd-cfDNA levels may rise as early as 5 months before histological evidence of rejection, highlighting its potential as an early biomarker in kidney recipients. In patients with rising creatinine or risk factors such as DSAs, an elevated dd-cfDNA should prompt biopsy or intensified surveillance. Critically, by reliably ruling out rejection, dd-cfDNA can spare many patients from unnecessary biopsies during routine monitoring or in the setting of mild creatinine changes.

In heart transplantation, dd-cfDNA reliably rules out significant rejection, although its absolute sensitivity is modest [18,21,43,48,52,54,83,84]. Khush et al. [21] evaluated a standardized NGS-based dd-cfDNA assay in 2,164 stable heart transplant samples and 35 rejection episodes. At a 0.20% threshold, dd-cfDNA demonstrated only 44% sensitivity for any biopsy-proven acute rejection but achieved a very high NPV. In a combined pediatric and adult cohort, Böhmer et al. [52] found that a cutoff of 7.5 cp/mL provided 92% sensitivity but only 43% specificity. Clinically, the chief utility of dd-cfDNA in heart transplantation lies in its high NPV, as a low result effectively rules out significant rejection, allowing for potential reduction in surveillance biopsies and other invasive studies. Khush et al. [21] further noted that values below 0.2% essentially excluded rejection, whereas an elevated result warranted closer clinical evaluation.

In liver transplantation, dd-cfDNA has been shown to detect acute rejection with high sensitivity but requires higher diagnostic thresholds [19,40,85]. Schütz et al. [40] measured dd-cfDNA serially in 115 liver recipients, reporting a median donor fraction of 3.3% in stable patients compared with 29.6% in those with biopsy-proven acute rejection. A cutoff of 11.2% achieved an AUC of 0.97. Importantly, dd-cfDNA rose nearly 2 weeks before biopsy confirmation, highlighting its potential for detecting subclinical injury. Levitsky et al. [19] reported that a cutoff of 5.3% differentiated rejection from normal function with an AUC of 0.95, and in a validation cohort, this threshold provided 100% NPV. However, distinguishing rejection from other causes of dysfunction (e.g., ischemia, cholestasis, steatohepatitis) required substantially higher thresholds. While dd-cfDNA values correlate with histological severity, performance in detecting subclinical graft injury remains limited. For example, Baumann et al. [85] demonstrated that at a cutoff of 2.1%, dd-cfDNA had 73% sensitivity but only 52% specificity, with an NPV of 47%, underscoring the need for caution when considering dd-cfDNA as a standalone marker for tailoring immunosuppression.

In lung transplantation, dd-cfDNA has also demonstrated strong diagnostic utility [17,62,64,86,87]. Early work by De Vlaminck et al. [86] demonstrated that lung recipients with rejection exhibited markedly higher dd-cfDNA than stable controls, with an overall AUC of 0.90 for any rejection. More recently, Jang et al. [17] studied 148 lung recipients longitudinally and found dd-cfDNA levels were sixfold higher during acute rejection compared with controls. The overall AUC for rejection was 0.89, with even higher accuracy for ABMR. Jang also reported that dd-cfDNA levels below 0.5% had a 96% NPV for acute rejection, while a 1.0% cutoff still achieved a 90% NPV. These findings suggest that a threshold under 1% can reliably exclude most rejection events, potentially reducing the need for routine biopsies. Similarly, Sayah et al. [87] observed that a 0.87% cutoff yielded 73% sensitivity and 53% specificity for acute cellular rejection, with an AUC of 0.72. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that dd-cfDNA testing can guide clinical decision-making by identifying patients who may safely defer transbronchial biopsy, reducing procedural risks while maintaining sensitivity for detecting allograft injury [17]. Given the invasiveness and sampling variability associated with lung biopsies, this blood-based biomarker represents an especially promising tool for allograft monitoring.

In summary, dd-cfDNA is most clinically effective as a rule-out test across solid organ transplants, with diagnostic accuracy optimized by organ- and assay-specific thresholds. Transplant recipients remain at elevated risk for opportunistic infections, most commonly of viral origin. Such infections are often overlooked contributors to declining graft function and, in some cases, may also be associated with rejection, as illustrated by Torque teno virus (TTV) [90]. For this reason, viral detection should be considered an essential component of comprehensive graft monitoring. In this context, Ju et al. [91] combined metagenomic NGS with dd-cfDNA measurement to successfully distinguish lung allograft rejection from infectious etiologies. More recently, Sinha et al. [92] demonstrated that whole-genome sequencing can simultaneously detect dd-cfDNA and viral pathogens, enabling concurrent assessment of graft injury and infection within a single assay.

INTERPRETATION CONSIDERATIONS IN CLINICAL PRACTICE

Although dd-cfDNA is a promising biomarker for noninvasive assessment of allograft injury, its interpretation requires careful integration of biological, analytical, and contextual factors. A clear understanding of these variables is essential to avoid misinterpretation and to maximize the clinical utility of dd-cfDNA in individualized patient care [16,93].

dd-cfDNA reflects graft injury but is not rejection-specific. Levels may rise in response to infection, ischemia, or other injuries, and baseline variation poses a significant interpretive challenge. Even among stable grafts, dd-cfDNA concentrations differ by organ type, body size, and cellular turnover. For example, liver transplant recipients often display higher baseline dd-cfDNA due to the larger tissue mass and regenerative activity of the liver [40,59]. Additional systemic conditions—including inflammation, infection, or malignancy—can further elevate background cfDNA, underscoring the need to interpret dd-cfDNA in relation to each patient’s clinical course [94]. Longitudinal trends often provide more meaningful insights than single time-point measurements [60]. Time since transplantation is another important factor: elevated levels are expected in the early postoperative phase due to surgical trauma and ischemia-reperfusion injury, but they typically decline within one to two weeks [74,86]. Persistent elevation beyond this period may indicate ongoing injury, especially in deceased-donor grafts or complex clinical scenarios. One important caveat is that fractional dd-cfDNA may remain elevated in stable kidney transplant recipients between 1 and 5 years posttransplant, reflecting a time-dependent decrease in host-derived cfDNA [27]. This false-positive phenomenon does not occur with absolute quantification, making the latter more suitable for long-term monitoring [27].

Although clinical thresholds have been proposed to support rejection detection, they are not universally applicable across organs or assay platforms [75]. Absolute dd-cfDNA values are not directly comparable across laboratories due to differences in extraction methods, sample preservation, and quantification techniques. Some assays report dd-cfDNA as a fraction of total cfDNA, while others provide absolute concentrations, complicating cross-platform interpretation [11]. Moreover, rejection cutoffs vary, and platform-specific thresholds are necessary. In many cases, dynamic changes relative to a patient’s baseline are more informative than exceeding a fixed cutoff. Combining dd-cfDNA results with other biomarkers, such as DSAs, serum creatinine, or liver enzymes, can further improve diagnostic accuracy and help guide biopsy decisions [20,62].

Despite its clinical value, dd-cfDNA testing is subject to both false-positive and false-negative results. Hematologic conditions—including malignancies, mixed chimerism, or leukopenia—may alter cfDNA composition or reduce total cfDNA levels, complicating interpretation [94]. Graft infections, drug toxicity, or ischemia can also elevate dd-cfDNA in the absence of rejection, while recent escalation of immunosuppression may transiently suppress levels despite ongoing injury [46,95]. These scenarios highlight the importance of interpreting dd-cfDNA in the context of serial measurements and the broader clinical picture. Technical and preanalytical variables also influence test reliability. Delayed processing, hemolysis, or assay artifacts can compromise accuracy [96,97]. Because cfDNA is inherently unstable, specialized stabilization tubes have been developed to preserve sample integrity. These tubes, such as the PAXgene Blood ccfDNA Tube (QIAGEN) and the Cell-Free DNA BCT (Streck), contain reagents that prevent cfDNA degradation and limit leukocyte genomic DNA contamination after collection. They can maintain cfDNA stability for 7 to 10 days at room temperature (up to 25 °C), allowing greater flexibility in transport and processing compared with conventional EDTA tubes [98,99]. Although prompt plasma separation by centrifugation is still recommended, stabilization tubes enable delayed processing with minimal loss of quality. Nevertheless, inadequate cfDNA extraction, contamination, or allele dropout in SNP-based assays can yield misleading results [100]. Moreover, other clinical conditions may also introduce additional cfDNA sources that complicate interpretation [101–103]. In multiorgan transplant recipients, dd-cfDNA levels are chronically elevated, making it difficult to attribute changes to a single organ [101]. Wungnema et al. [102] reported a rare case of donor-derived posttransplant lymphoproliferative disorder in which dd-cfDNA levels were elevated despite the absence of graft dysfunction or histologic evidence of rejection. In pregnant transplant recipients, fetal cfDNA circulates in maternal plasma, necessitating separate paternal genotyping to prevent misclassification of fetal DNA as dd-cfDNA [103].

Collectively, interpretation of dd-cfDNA requires a holistic approach that incorporates biological variability, assay-specific characteristics, and clinical context (Table 3). When thoughtfully integrated with other diagnostic data, dd-cfDNA has the potential to enhance early detection of rejection and support personalized monitoring strategies in transplantation.

CONCLUSION

dd-cfDNA has emerged as a transformative biomarker in solid organ transplantation, providing a noninvasive and dynamic approach for detecting allograft injury. Evidence across multiple organ types supports its value in identifying both acute and subclinical rejection, guiding biopsy decisions, and monitoring therapeutic response. Multiple analytical platforms are available for dd-cfDNA measurement, each with distinct performance characteristics and reporting strategies.

Despite promising data, several challenges remain in achieving standardized clinical implementation. Interpretation must incorporate biological variability, time since transplantation, organ-specific thresholds, and potential confounders such as infection, inflammation, or technical artifacts. Without careful contextualization, false-positive and false-negative results may lead to misclassification and inappropriate management. Moreover, harmonization across laboratories, the development of certified reference materials, and consensus on organ-specific thresholds remain unmet needs.

Looking ahead, several research directions are critical for advancing the clinical utility of dd-cfDNA. First, standardized diagnostic thresholds must be developed that reflect the unique biological features of each transplanted organ, the time elapsed since transplantation, and relevant clinical circumstances. Establishing such thresholds will improve accuracy and consistency in interpreting dd-cfDNA results.

Second, improving interlaboratory reproducibility through assay harmonization and certified reference materials will be essential. The introduction of external quality assessment programs will further minimize technical variability and strengthen confidence in dd-cfDNA testing across institutions.

Third, additional studies are needed in underrepresented transplant types, such as intestine and pancreas grafts, where applications remain less well defined. Expanding the evidence base in these areas will broaden the clinical scope of dd-cfDNA monitoring.

Finally, evaluating the cost-effectiveness and real-world clinical impact of dd-cfDNA-guided management—including its ability to reduce invasive biopsies, improve graft survival, and optimize resource allocation—will be vital for informing health policy and supporting reimbursement.

As our understanding of dd-cfDNA biology and measurement continues to evolve, its integration into routine clinical practice has the potential to improve graft outcomes, minimize unnecessary biopsies, and enable personalized posttransplant care. Continued collaborative research, standardization efforts, and clinical validation will be essential to fully realize the promise of this biomarker in transplantation medicine.
