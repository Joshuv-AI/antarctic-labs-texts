Fungal Spore Detection of the Gray Mold Crop Disease: Imprinted-Polymer Thermometric Sensing Platform for the Real-Time Monitoring of Botrytis cinerea
Authors: Rocio Arreguin-Campos, Ramiro Marroquin-Garcia, Augusto César Parreiras de Jesus, Ulrike Zanzen, Lien Bosmans, Marlies Huysmans, Evelien Rosiers, Annelies Claes, Thomas J. Cleij, Kasper Eersels, Bart van Grinsven
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Timely identification of pathogens within the food production chain is crucial to prevent spoilage and reduce the substantial economic burden associated with crop loss. Botrytis cinerea is the causative agent of gray mold disease, which affects important crops such as tomatoes. In this study, we introduce a sensing platform for real-time monitoring of this fungus. The sensor consists of a polymeric receptor imprinted with the physical and chemical characteristics of the fungus’s spores, enabling identification. Detection is achieved using a thermal transducer, which offers a reproducible concentration-dependent signal toward the analyte. The sensor was tested for specificity with other spores (Ampelomyces quisqualis, Fusarium oxysporum, and Akanthomyces muscarius), with a significantly higher response intensity for the targeted microorganism. The sensor’s practical applicability was evaluated using B. cinerea -containing water collected from a greenhouse dedicated to tomato cultivation. These results highlight the platform’s potential as a routine monitoring tool for this plant pathogen.

Introduction

The spoilage of crops caused by fungi can lead to waste of produce and economic loss. Fungal infections account for up to 14% of crop losses annually, making them a threat to both food security and sustainability. Botrytis cinerea is recognized as one of the most common and destructive pathogens of several crops (such as grape, strawberry, and tomato), causing the well-known “gray mold”. This necrotrophic fungus destroys the tissue of diverse organs of its host (flowers, fruits, and leaves), and the infection can be easily transferred via spore contact through air, soil, and water. Notably, greenhouse production greatly increases the risk of infection with B. cinerea, given the humid and cool conditions. Currently, chemical and biological agents are the most widely applied means of controlling this disease, but the activity of many of these fungicides is being lost due to resistance from the pathogen. Reduction in usage and dependence on these substances can be achieved by implementing early infection detection strategies that allow for taking adequate measures to save intact parts of the production.

Identification of fungal infection is mostly performed through visual examination of color changes, blight, and rotting of the tissues. However, when these signs are visible, affection has already progressed. Additionally, molecular diagnosis techniques can be employed for quantifying the spores, but these can be costly. As with other microorganisms, plating and microscopy can be used to identify and estimate the concentration of these spores, but these tests usually rely on external facilities and specialized personnel to perform them. Alternative technologies remain in the research stage, but hold promise given the current challenges faced with these pathogens. Some examples for B. cinerea identification are imaging sensors and combinations of these with deep learning image techniques. A few biosensors have also been developed for the detection of airborne fungal spores, and only a limited number have focused especially on B. cinerea, which remains a target of interest.

Imprinted polymer-based sensors are biomimetic platforms that can be tailored to meet the needs of diverse applications (food safety, environmental, and health monitoring). These devices consist of a synthetic receptor layer and a transducing platform. The receptors are cross-linked polymeric matrices that possess small cavities that match the analyte in shape, size, and chemical composition, enabling binding. These cavities are created during the preparation of the materials, which can consist of bulk, precipitation, or electrochemically assisted polymerizations (to name the most common). Having a “plastic” receptor provides these sensors with beneficial robustness across diverse physicochemical conditions, which is especially desired for field applications. Despite the advantages that biomimetic sensing platforms offer, their development is limited mostly to molecular analytes (molecularly imprinted polymers) and a few microorganisms (mainly bacteria). There are known challenges for the preparation of imprinted polymers for microorganisms, and especially for large ones. These include that preparation protocols need to involve mild conditions to preserve the target, the difficulty in removing the template, and the mobility restrictions during the rebinding event. It could be related to these issues that, to our knowledge, there is scarce literature on imprinted polymers targeting fungal pathogens.

This study addresses this gap by presenting an integral approach toward the development of an imprinted polymer-based thermometric sensing device for the detection of the fungal spores of B. cinerea, going from receptor preparation to application. Research indicates that Surface-Imprinting is a successful approach for preparing polymeric receptors with advantages such as more accessible recognition sites and, therefore, higher binding capacity. This is specifically useful for large targets, as having the functional cavities on the surface facilitates target removal and binding. Nonetheless, surface-imprinting often comes with the trade-off of multistep preparation protocols that require the synthesis of polymers ab initio, compromising the reproducibility of the sensor. In this case, we opt for using polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), a commercial elastomer well-known for its applications in cell lithography. The facile functionalization of this material, based on sedimentation of the target, has been reported previously for bacteria but not for fungal spores, which possess inherent challenges given the morphological and chemical surface differences among these microorganisms. Graphene oxide powder (GOx) is employed as a thermal functional additive to achieve synergy with the transducer. Furthermore, this study aims to exemplify the potential that the sensor holds to be used in the field for the monitoring of this pathogen. Samples from the dehumidifier of a greenhouse facility for tomato crops were used to mimic the complexity of a real sample in order to assess and compare the performance of the device.

Materials and Methods

Materials and Reagents

B. cinerea (DSM-877), Ampelomyces quisqualis (DSM-4624), and Fusarium oxysporum (DSM-841) strains were purchased from DSM-Z (Braunschweig, Germany) and were handled according to the supplier’s instructions upon arrival. Akanthomyces muscarium was purchased as Mycotal product from Koppert (Koppert, Netherlands) with a concentration of 1E spores/gram. Aluminum round substrates with a diameter of 10 mm and a thickness of 0.33 mm were obtained from Raytech (Brugge, Belgium). Graphene oxide powder (GOx), potato dextrose agar (PDA), malt extract agar (MEA), phosphate buffer saline (PBS), Tween 20, lactophenol cotton blue staining agent, and anhydrous tetrahydrofuran (THF) with a minimum purity of 99.9% were used as received from Merck (Diegem, Belgium), and Polydimethylsiloxane Sylgard 184 elastomer kit was purchased from Mavom N.V. (Schelle, Belgium). Disposable hemocytometers (Millicell MDH-4N1) were employed for spore counting and were purchased from Merck (Diegem, Belgium).

Fungal Culturing and Spore Harvesting

B. cinerea

PDA was prepared following the standard protocol from the manufacturer, and sterile Petri dishes were prepared for inoculating them with fungi. The inoculated plates were kept in an incubator at 20 °C with a light bulb on to promote sporulation. After 5–7 days, conidia were harvested by pouring around 15 mL of Tween surfactant (0.01%) onto the plates and gently scraping the surface with a sterile rod to detach the fungi. The suspension was gravity-filtered employing a funnel lined with glass wool to remove the mycelium.

A. quisqualis

MEA was prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and sterile Petri dishes were inoculated with fungi. The plates were kept in an incubator at 22 °C with a light bulb on. After 15 days, conidia were harvested by pouring around 15 mL of Tween surfactant (0.01%) onto the plates and gently scraping the surface with a sterile rod to detach the fungi. The suspension was gravity-filtered employing a funnel lined with glass wool to remove the mycelium.

F. oxysporum

Mung bean broth was prepared by boiling store-bought mung beans for 30 min, followed by filtration through a gauze to remove the solids. The broth was sterilized by autoclave and inoculated with the fungi. It was kept in a shaking incubator at 30 °C for 3 days until cloudiness was observed. The suspension was filtered through a nylon filter to separate spores from mycelium.

For all the strains, the obtained spores were centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 30 min, and the pellets were resuspended in PBS in order to perform the experiments. The concentration was determined by employing a disposable counting chamber, and dilutions were prepared according to the desired concentrations.

Preparation of Synthetic Receptors for B. cinerea

A composite of PDMS with graphene oxide (PDMS/GOx) was obtained by mixing GOx with the elastomer’s base in a concentration of 0.01% using a Fisherbrand sonic dismembrator with a probe of 2 mm diameter. Afterward, this thick resin was mixed in the standard ratio of 10:1 (w/w) of base to curing agent. To decrease the viscosity of the resin, a dilution (10% w/w) in tetrahydrofuran was performed as a stock solution.

Surface-imprinted polymers were prepared and adapted for the fungal spores as follows. Circular aluminum–magnesium substrates (max. 3 wt % Mg) were spin-coated with 150 μL of PDMS-GOx stock solution at 5000 rpm for 60 s. The materials were then placed in the oven at 65 °C for 10 min to partially cure the films. Following, these were taken out of the oven and placed onto a flat surface at room temperature. A 200 μL droplet of B. cinerea (900 E3 CFU/mL) was deposited on the surface of the substrates, and these were left to sediment for 20 min. In a subsequent step, the films with the droplets on top were transferred back to the oven and left for 4 h to achieve full curing of the layers.

Synthetic Receptor Template Removal: Counter-Stamping Method

Here, we define a counter-stamp methodology for the removal of B. cinerea spores after the imprinting process (see Scheme ). Bare PDMS stamps were prepared by mixing the base and curing agent (10:1) and pouring them onto sterile plastic Petri dishes. These were degassed under vacuum and cured in an oven at 65 °C for 4 h. After cooling, the stamps were cut to an area of approximately 1 cm2 employing a sterile scalpel. Spore removal was conducted in dry conditions (no solvents/surfactants). First, the imprinted film, prepared on an aluminum substrate, was placed on a flat and clean surface. A freshly cut PDMS stamp was then gently placed on top of the imprinted film, and homogeneous pressure was applied over the entire surface using the thumb for 20 s. Finally, pressure was released, and the PDMS stamp was carefully peeled off from the imprints (see Scheme , steps 2 and 3). This procedure was repeated until maximum spore removal was achieved. Control experiments were performed with nonimprinted PDMS films to ensure that the polymer did not tear during the procedure, and results can be found in Supporting Information (Figure S1).

Optical Characterization of Polymer Imprints’ Surfaces

Imprints were prepared on glass slides (cut 1 cm2) in order to visualize the receptors before and after the target’s removal. Brightfield microscopy analysis was conducted using an Olympus BX53 microscope. The imprinted polymers were prepared on square glass substrates (1 cm2). To increase the contrast, the spores were directly stained on the films using lactophenol solution for 1 min before the analysis. Moreover, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed on a Jeol-JSM-IT200 InTouchScope, and sample preparation consisted of gold coating to enable visualization. The prepared imprinted polymers on aluminum as well as on glass slides were used in order to confirm the presence of the cavities on the surface.

Surface coverage of the imprints on glass substrates before and after removal of the template was calculated with the aid of ImageJ software by analyzing three different imprint pictures on three different areas of the imprint each. An example of how the images were processed is provided in Supporting Information (Figure S2).

Sensing Setup

The Heat-Transfer Method (HTM) has been described in literature, given the studies performed on molecules and cells. The setup consists of a microfluidic flow cell coupled to a temperature controller. The flow cell is made of polycarbonate and has an internal cylindrical chamber with a volume of 110 μL. This chamber is sealed with a rubber O-ring that delimits the surface area (28 mm2) that is in contact with the aluminum chip. The aluminum chip is pressed with the imprint facing toward the chamber, and with its back on a copper block that acts as a heating element. The copper temperature (fixed at 37 °C) is controlled with a thermocouple by modulating the voltage over the power resistor with a proportional-integral-derivative (P = 10, I = 8, D = 0) software-based controller. The temperature inside the chamber is registered by a thermocouple that is situated 1 mm above the receptor-liquid interface. Finally, the setup is equipped with a pump that allows for a controlled influx of the samples, which was fixed for this study at 2 mL/min for 1 min.

Greenhouse Samples Measurements

As an application case, water samples were collected from the dehumidifier at a greenhouse where tomato crops are grown. This sample contained solids, so the liquid was gravity-filtered once using grade 1 Whatman filter paper. In order to ensure that the sample did not contain any unknown pathogen that could interfere with the experiment, the sample was autoclaved and spiked with a known concentration of fungal spores (200 E3 CFU/mL).

Statistical Analysis

Selectivity data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10. An ordinary one-way ANOVA was performed for the different strains investigated, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. *p-value < 0.05, **p-value < 0.001.

Results and Discussion

Preparation of Synthetic Receptors for B. cinerea Spores

The imprinting process consists of a sedimentation technique where the target is deposited onto a semicured polymer film (PDMS elastomer). The spores of B. cinerea are used as mold, and they assemble by gravity at the surface of the material. Because the layers are postcured afterward, the polymer adopts the shape and size of the spores. Moreover, during the molding process, the target is incorporated into the polymer, and when it is removed, cavities are formed, and part of the microorganism’s membrane stays in the cavities, as supported by earlier studies on cell-imprinted polymers. This preparation strategy provides the PDMS with functional pockets that match the characteristics of the spores, and that will be of key importance when using them as receptors for detecting them. The imprinting results can be seen in Figure , where brightfield and scanning electron microscopy were employed to visualize the surfaces. The upper panel shows the PDMS layers before the removal of the template in brightfield and electron microscopy (see Figure A and C, respectively). It can be noticed that the spores agglomerate to a certain extent on the material and that some mycelium is present. Surface coverage was calculated as an indicator of the dispersion of the template on the material, and it was found to be 23.3% ± 3. Both the agglomeration observations and the coverage correlate closely with what has been reported for bacteria (Escherichia coli) imprints. To our knowledge, only one study has been conducted on polymer imprinting of fungal spores (F. oxysporum), in which the preparation of the materials was achieved with the aid of an electrochemical setup. To this end, the strategy reported in this work offers a simple methodology to achieve receptor preparation.

Similarly, SEM micrographs for the spore-containing (C) and the clean films (D), corroborate the incorporation of the spores onto the PDMS film and the subsequent formation of oval-like cavities after target has been removed, respectively.

As important as the assessment of the template on the surface of the polymers is the removal stage, given that the cavities need to be available for rebinding the target. Fungal spores are complex in composition, and when evaluating the washing procedures commonly used for imprinted polymers for other microorganisms, it is not straightforward to employ the same techniques. For bacteria, surfactants (such as sulfate-dodecyl sodium) or alcohol are commonly employed to burst the membrane of the cells, which are then removed by rinsing with water. In the particular case of fungal spores, these strategies could not be employed, and this study tailored a method for this specific target. A mechanical approach was adopted, in which a stamp was repeatedly used to pull out the spores from the PDMS. As can be seen in Figure B and D, the procedure was successful, but a small portion of spores remained trapped in the material (surface coverage of 4.2% ± 0.5), which means that ∼80% of the spores were removed, and indicates that the material has been cleaned and that cavities have been made available for rebinding. Additional pictures of the imprints can be found in Supporting Information (Figure S3).

B. cinerea Detection with the Heat-Transfer Method

The relevance of assessing the surface of the materials is that optical techniques can confirm the presence of cavities, and therefore that the receptors can be used for rebinding experiments. In this study, the HTM was employed as a transducing technology due to the advantages that have been previously suggested, such as simplicity and portability. In contrast to the most commonly used platforms (such as electrochemical devices), the HTM offers a cost-effective alternative while maintaining data analysis uncomplicated. To investigate the binding of the analyte to the layers, these are coupled in a microfluidic cell that monitors the temperature changes when the spores anchor to the synthetic receptors. The experiments consisted of the stabilization of the system with a PBS buffer solution, followed by the inflow of B. cinerea spore suspensions to the chamber. A concentration of 200 E3 CFU/mL was added in a stepwise manner with an automated pump that controls the rate to ensure good control of the setup. In between injections, the system was allowed to stabilize for 20 min. During the incubation period, the spores sediment and bind to the synthetic receptor, which leads to a change in thermal resistance (transduced in temperature). In our design, this thermal response is enhanced through the incorporation of GOx powder into the imprinted polymer layers. GOx is introduced as a functional additive to the PDMS matrix to increase the thermal conductivity of the composite receptor. Even small loadings have shown to transfer their outstanding physicochemical properties (including high intrinsic thermal conductance) into polymer composites, enabling more efficient heat transfer across the receptor-liquid interface and improving overall sensor performance. Figure A demonstrates a progressive decrease in temperature when increasing the target concentration. This phenomenon can be attributed to the disturbance of the heat flow when the cavities are empty, in contrast to when the spores block these microchannels (as represented in Figure B). The effects observed were fitted with OriginPro to an allometric equation with the formula y = (a × xb ). In order to attribute this effect size solely to the imprints and not to just the sedimentation of the target on the material, control polymers (without imprints) were assessed in the same way, and calibration curves for both were constructed employing 6 repetitions per concentration. From these results (Figure C), it can be confirmed that the response from the sensor stems only from the binding of the spores to the receptors and not from sedimentation on the polymers, given the signal difference between the imprinted and nonimprinted materials. The limit of detection was calculated as 170 E3 CFU/mL, making use of the 3σ level, which is calculated as the lowest concentration of the target at which the effect is higher than three times the averaged error values for all the data sets. It is important to highlight that there is not a single safe vs harmful concentration of B. cinerea spores for crop infection. The damage that occurs is related to multiple factors such as inoculum density, the susceptibility of the plant, and the environmental conditions (temperature, humidity, airflow, etc.). , In this regard, the hereby presented limit of detection could serve as an indicator of the performance of the sensor, knowing that specific applications might require further optimization.

Response of the Receptor Layer toward Other Fungal Spores

The quantitative evaluation of the binding of B. cinerea spores to the prepared receptors is as relevant as assessing whether the cavities are specific toward the imprinted strain. For this reason, the layers were exposed to different concentrations of three additional fungal spores: F. oxysporum, which is also a plant pathogen, A. muscarius and A. quisqualis, which are employed as biological pest control agents (see Figure ). The selection of these comes down to mimicking possible realistic scenarios in the practical detection of the B. cinerea. Representative raw data of these experiments are provided in Supporting Information (Figure S4).

Results for each concentration are based on 6 independent experiments. Statistical significance was determined according to ordinary one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. *p-value < 0.05, **p-value < 0.001.

The results in Figure indicate that there is no significant response for A. muscarius throughout the range of concentrations tested, and a response slightly above the noise line for F. oxysporum and A. quisqualis. In every concentration, the signal obtained from the sensor for the B. cinerea is at least twice in intensity, corroborating that the observed effect is due to the specific binding toward the imprinted analyte. Understanding the response obtained for the nonimprinted spores is complex in nature. First, it can involve nonspecific affinity between the material and the cells (electrostatic, hydrophobic, or van der Waals), which have been studied for bacteria and PDMS, but not for fungal spores. Second, there could be a certain extent of complementarity of the spores with the cavities (physical matching or certain chemical functionalities that lead to interaction). In general, this signal could be calibrated to either completely filter the contribution of other spores or to differentiate them. This could be of particular interest when aiming also at detecting other pathogens, such as the F. oxysporum. Nonetheless, this calibration would require further investigation and more advanced data analysis tools.

Greenhouse Samples Measurements

Given the applicability of the sensor hereby proposed, we aimed to compare the performance of the device in buffer (as supported by the presented results) and compare it when analyzing a real matrix. For this purpose, samples from a local greenhouse were obtained and spiked with the B. cinerea spores. Figure shows the raw temperature data obtained for both liquids (buffer and greenhouse water) and aims to depict that the temperature drops similarly in both cases. Interestingly, we observed an increased level of noise in the temperature signal for the real sample, which can be attributed to the origin of the water. This matrix was collected from a dehumidifier in the facility and given that the air that circulates in the greenhouse passes through it can carry small-sized solids (such as dust), which could potentially adhere to the spores and disturb the stabilization of the temperature. Additionally, when the water is condensed, it is contained in a piping system, which could also introduce solids. Nonetheless, when analyzing the obtained data into a dose–response curve with multiple replicates, it can be observed that, despite the slightly higher effect size exhibited by the greenhouse water samples, the response overlaps in all concentrations tested with the results obtained for the buffer liquid.

(A) Real-time temperature response of the sensor in buffer and greenhouse water at increasing concentrations of B. cinerea spores (200 E3 CFU/mL). (B) Dose–response curves of the effect sizes obtained for both matrices (6 independent experiments per concentration).

Although this result section has the goal of proving that the performance of the device can be transferred and maintained when analyzing a matrix with the complexity that would be expected for this specific application, it is important to mention that sample collection could be performed differently, given that the spores can be transported through soil, air, and water. Therefore, appropriate changes should be made, for instance, to the sample pretreatment. An example of how research can be done in this regard can be found in previously reported work where a microfluidic setup that captures and enriches airborne spores was developed. Such a type of device could be coupled to the proposed sensor.

A literature search regarding the available studies published in the last 10 years on biosensors for B. cinerea was performed, and the findings are summarized in Table , where aspects of the design of the devices as well as their performance were included. Remarkably, only three recent studies were found, which stresses the relevance of the application of biosensors in this field. Moreover, it can be noticed that most approaches focus on the detection of biomarkers rather than direct sensing, which is reflected in the different limits of detection reported. Another observation is that all the studies carried out real sample analysis, focusing on different food matrices where the infection of this fungus is relevant.

This study demonstrates the feasibility of a biomimetic sensor for the detection of B. cinerea, yielding reproducible results even in water samples collected from a greenhouse dehumidifier. The proposed platform enables direct detection using simple materials while maintaining a rapid response time. Furthermore, the findings indicate that the sensing strategy could be extended to the detection of other fungal contaminants, highlighting its broader applicability.

A limitation of this work is that the sensor was tested under controlled laboratory conditions. Consequently, further optimization and validation under real greenhouse environments are required before practical implementation. In particular, the limit of detection may need to be adjusted to meet the specific demands of individual facilities, and in situ studies should account for environmental variables such as airflow, humidity, and temperature. Future developments may include sensor miniaturization, integration of remote data acquisition, and temperature control mechanisms. Additionally, coupling the sensing layers to electrochemical transducers or incorporating sampling and preconcentration strategies could further enhance sensitivity.

Overall, the results highlight the potential of employing biomimetic, imprinted-polymer sensors for early fungal detection in agricultural applications. From a research perspective, this works expands the application scope of surface imprinting toward fungal pathogens, addressing existing challenges in microorganism imprinting and encouraging further development of field-deployable biosensing platforms. Moreover, the implementation of rapid detection technologies could support precision agriculture strategies by enabling earlier intervention, reducing unnecessary fungicide application, and contributing to more sustainable crop protection practices.
