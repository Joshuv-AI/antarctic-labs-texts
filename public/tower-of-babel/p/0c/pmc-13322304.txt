Vasoreactivity Test With Nitrate-Hydralazine by Linking Gas Exchange and Hemodynamics in PH-HFpEF
Authors: AHMED EL SHAER, ANAS ABED, MARIANA GARCIA-ARANGO, WAXNIN TU, JUSTIN JAN, MARGARET COCHRANE, ARIA KENARSARY, ARTHUR R GALLEGOS, ZAHRA MAHMOOD, MUHAMMAD USMAN, UMBISH DINO, ABDUL WAHAB ARIF, BABAK TEHRANI, KATHRYN S JONES, JAMES RUNO, FARHAN RAZA
Published: 2026

Introduction

Pulmonary vascular remodeling in pulmonary hypertension (PH) phenotypes leads to gas-exchange abnormalities, hemodynamic perturbations, and adverse clinical outcomes.1,2 The gas-exchange abnormalities, captured by peak oxygen consumption (VO2) and ventilatory efficiency slope (VE/VCO2), occur in PH phenotypes, such as heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (PH-HFpEF) and precapillary PH.1,2 Hemodynamically, patients with PH are assessed by pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR, which reflects steady load) and pulmonary arterial compliance (PAC, which reflects pulsatile load).3 Guided by at-rest PVR, PH-HFpEF trials using vasodilators have demonstrated increased harm, compared to placebo.4 These failed clinical trials likely reflect the complex and heterogeneous pathobiology of PH-HFpEF5 (compared to precapillary PH). This highlights the need for advanced protocols using provocative maneuvers (eg., exercise or vasodilator testing) to capture the dynamic nature of pulmonary vascular remodeling. A study by Sarma et al. tested rest-to-exercise hemodynamics before and after nitroglycerin in early HFpEF with exercise-induced PH.6 Although the study was not focused on assessing vasoreactivity in patients with advanced pulmonary vascular remodeling, it demonstrated the feasibility of complex protocols.6 They reported no improvement in peak VO2 and VE/VCO2 despite lowering left-heart pressures with nitroglycerin.6 While lowering left heart pressures would be expected to improve exercise capacity in PH-HFpEF, these negative results, including acute reduction in left-heart pressures, may suggest that sustained longstanding unloading is probably necessary to result in functional gains in exercise capacity. Nonetheless, there remains a clear need to develop new protocols to assess vasoreactivity in the acute setting.

The current study aims to develop a vasoreactivity test for PH-HFpEF by combining gas exchange and hemodynamic responses to vasodilation so as to assess pulmonary vascular disease independent of left-heart pressures. First, we defined hemodynamic correlates of abnormal gas exchange in a large retrospective PH cohort (n = 170) undergoing invasive cardiopulmonary exercise tests (iCPETs). These findings of closely associated hemodynamic gas-exchange metrics guided us to develop a new vasodilator test in a prospective pilot study (n = 7). The 3 specific components of this vasodilator test include: 1, testing improvements in the hemodynamic metric (which had the strongest correlation with abnormal gas exchange); 2, testing improvements in the gas-exchange metric of the oxygen-uptake efficiency slope (OUES), which is a slope-based metric better suited for a submaximal ergometer-based iCPET with repeated exercise stages (baseline and post-vasodilator); and 3, comprehensive vasodilation by combining nitroglycerin with hydralazine. This combination of nitrate and hydralazine has been shown in animal models to attenuate HFpEF,7 justifying it as a suitable combination to lower left-heart pressures.

Methods

This retrospective study included participants undergoing iCPET on a recumbent ergometer (New York Heart Association class II–II), as previously described.6 According to 2022 European Society of Cardiologists/ European Respiratory Society guidelines,2 participants were categorized based on rest and exercise hemodynamics into the following categories: PH-HFpEF (n = 91), precapillary PH (n = 54), and noncardiac dyspnea (NCD) (n = 25).

The prospective cohort included 7 participants with PH-HFpEF. After initial iCPET, participants rested for 15 minutes, and rest hemodynamics were repeated. This was followed by the administration of vasodilators (20 mg oral hydralazine and 0.4 mg sublingual nitroglycerin), and iCPET was repeated (postvasodilator rest and 25 Watts exercise). The study complies with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki.

For the retrospective part of the study, linear regression models, adjusted for patient groups with interaction, were used to assess the association of percentage between predicted peak VO2 and VE/VCO2 slope with hemodynamic predictors (PVR and PAC).5 The multivariate linear regression model was adjusted for age, sex, and BMI without interaction. All statistical analyses were performed in R studio (version 4.2.2). Formal statistical analyses were not performed for the pilot prospective study due to the small sample size.

Results

The characteristics of the 3 hemodynamic groups in the retrospective cohort are summarized in Table 1. The PH-HFpEF group included isolated postcapillary PH (IpcPH, n = 30) and combined pre- and postcapillary PH (CpcPH, n = 61). To assess the correlation with peak predicted VO2 (Fig. 1, A, B), rest PAC revealed a strong positive correlation in the PH-HFpEF (P < 0.01) and precapillary PH (P < 0.01) groups, in comparison to the NCD group (P = 0.08). Based on slopes of univariate linear regression models, an improvement of 1.0 mL/mmHg in resting PAC was associated with an increase in predicted peak VO2 by 2.6% in PH-HFpEF and 6.6% in precapillary PH. Rest PVR showed no significant correlation in 3 hemodynamic groups (P > 0.05). In multivariate linear regression models, rest PAC remained significant in precapillary PH (P = 0.01) and approached significance in PH-HFpEF (P = 0.06), while rest PVR remained statistically nonsignificant in PH-HFpEF (P = 0.60) and precapillary PH (P = 0.61). Therefore, PAC has the strongest correlation with peak predicted VO2. On univariate linear regression analyses with peak oxygen delivery (DO2), similar trends of strong correlation were noted in PH-HFpEF (nonsignificant in precapillary PH and NCD groups) with rest PAC (r2 = 0.35, slope = 0.002; P < 0.01), and there was a slightly less strong correlation with rest PVR (r2 = 0.30, slope = −0.002; P < 0.01). Notably, slopes were flat in these relationships (compared to steeper slopes with peak VO2 (Fig. 1, A, B).

Similarly, for VE/VCO2 slope, univariate linear regression models demonstrated a significant negative correlation with PAC in the PH-HFpEF (P = 0.03) and precapillary PH (P = 0.02), and a statistically nonsignificant relationship with rest PVR (Fig. 1, C, D). In multivariate linear regression models for VE/VCO2 slope, rest PAC approached significance in PH-HFpEF (P = 0.08) and precapillary PH (P = 0.05), while rest PVR was statistically non-significant in PH-HFpEF (P = 0.83) and precapillary PH (P = 0.71).

Vasoreactivity reactivity testing was performed in 7 PH-HFpEF participants: 5 IpcPH and 2 CpcPH (Fig. 1). The median (IQR) age was 67 (21) years, and the body mass index was 34.7 (8.7) kg/m2; 5 participants were female (71%). Based on the close relationship with gas-exchange metrics (peak VO2 and VE/VCO2), rest PAC was used as a key hemodynamic metric to define vasoreactivity. As a linking gas-exchange metric in the vasoreactivity protocol, OUES was used as a key gas-exchange metric to define vasoreactivity. Of the participants, 5 had improvements in rest PAC and OUES and were labeled as being vasoreactive (4 IpcPH, 1 CpcPH), and 2 were nonvasoreactive (1 IpcPH, 1 CpcPH). Among vasoreactive participants, postvasodilator changes included rest PAC increased (range: D + 0.3 to + 2.7 vs nonvasoreactive: −1.0 to −1.4), OUES increased (range: D + 0.07 to + 0.52 vs nonvasoreactive: −0.08 to −0.11), and rest PVR decreased (range: D + 0.2 to −0.6 [4/5 participants had decreases in PVR] vs nonvasoreactive: + 0.1 to + 0.3). For vasoreactive participants (n = 5), the median (IQR) pulmonary artery wedge pressure/cardiac output (PAWP/CO) slope (mmHg/L.min−1) decreased from 2.0 (1.5)–1.9 (1.0), and for nonvasoreactive participants (n = 2), the PAWP/CO slope increased from 2.5 (1.2)–3.3 (2.4).

Discussion

In this study, we report that compared to PVR, rest PAC has a stronger correlation with gas-exchange metrics among PH phenotypes, indicating that the pulsatile load is a relevant physiological hemodynamic metric in PH-HFpEF.1,3 Advanced metrics (such as characteristic impedance and wave-intensity analysis) precisely capture pulsatile load, but they are not yet able to be implemented in clinical care, making PAC an appealing, easily computed reflection of pulsatile load.1,3 We report a novel vasoreactivity testing protocol in PH-HFpEF, which lowers left-heart pressures and integrates hemodynamics (PAC) with gas exchange (OUES) to quantify comprehensively the pulmonary vascular disease burden. The vasoreactivity response in PH-HFpEF is variable and not is dictated by traditional PH phenotypes (IpcPH vs CpcPH).

In the retrospective arm of the study, correlations between hemodynamic metrics were made; peak VO2 was predicted to standardize the effects of age and sex. We acknowledge that the peak VO2 achieved with a recumbent ergometer (in this study) are lower than the peak VO2 achieved with treadmill CPET.8 However, we previously showed that this difference is consistent among PH-HFpEF, precapillary PH, and NCD groups (mean ergometer-treadmill peak VO2 difference: -6%, per Bland-Altman analysis).8 Last, fixed nitrate-hydralazine dosage may limit interpretation, because some participants may be vasoreactive at higher dosages. Future studies are needed to assess treatment effects on the PAC-gas-exchange relationship and to validate this vasoreactivity protocol.

In conclusion, compared to PVR, PAC is a better correlate of impaired gas exchange in PH. Improving PAC by 1.0 mL/mmHg may improve peak VO2 predicted (+ 2.6% in PH-HFpEF, + 6.6% in precapillary PH), and VE/VCO2 slope (−3.8% in PH-HFpEF, −2.3% in precapillary PH). The smaller benefit in PH-HFpEF likely reflects multiorgan contributors to exercise intolerance.
