From observation to ownership: a qualitative study of medical students’ learning under distant clinical supervision
Authors: Adisa Poljo, Victoria Werdecker, Bryce J M Bogie, Florian Müry, Andrea Meienberg, Christian H Nickel, Roland Bingisser, Jennifer M Klasen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis qualitative study explores the experiences of medical students involved in clinical work and learning under distant supervision, aiming to understand their adaptation, challenges and learning processes in the context of clinical uncertainty and reduced oversight.DesignThis study employed a constructivist grounded theory (CGT). CGT was chosen for its strength in examining complex social interactions and uncovering emergent themes that are not fully explained by existing theoretical frameworks. Data were collected through 13 semi-structured, in-depth interviews with medical students who actively participated in clinical care under conditions of limited supervision and high responsibility.SettingFaculty of Medicine, Switzerland.ParticipantsWe conducted interviews with 13 medical students who worked in Mobile SWAB Teams during the COVID-19 pandemic.ResultsStudents described a shift from observation to actively taking on a professional role. This experience provided a unique opportunity for medical students to apply their knowledge and skills in real-world settings, develop a sense of autonomy and foster personal growth. Acknowledging the importance of effective communication, teamwork and decision-making in providing patient care, they embraced the concept of self-regulated learning (SRL).ConclusionsCreating a supportive learning environment that promotes SRL encourages collaboration and enables medical students to take on clinical tasks with increasing autonomy. In our study, working under distant supervision promoted reflection, strengthened communication and supported both clinical development and identity formation. This approach highlights the value of integrating supported responsibility and guided reflection into future models of clinical education.

Introduction

Medical education is marked by dynamic and ongoing transitions which require trainees to adapt to new situations.1 This critical moment of professional development moves trainees from the theoretical learning environment to practical patient encounters, preparing them to become competent and confident future physicians.2

These transitions in medical education are fraught with significant adjustments and challenges, especially when they are unexpected.3 Recent experiences have shown that medical students may be called on to take on clinical roles under variable circumstances—with reduced supervision, compressed timelines and minimal preparation. Such scenarios demand flexible and adaptive learning approaches to ensure educational continuity and support professional growth.4 5 Self-regulated learning (SRL) refers to the process through which learners take active control of their own learning by setting goals, employing strategies, monitoring progress and reflecting on outcomes.6–8 It supports autonomy, metacognition and the development of professional competence, particularly in environments where external guidance is limited.9–13 Complementing SRL, self-determination theory (SDT) provides insight into the motivational aspects of learning. SDT posits that individuals are more likely to thrive when their basic psychological needs for autonomy, competence and relatedness are supported.14 In clinical learning environments, especially those marked by distant supervision, these needs can influence students’ engagement, motivation and sense of professional identity.

Understanding medical students’ experiences under limited supervision reveals challenges, learning opportunities and support needs, which can promote better training and protocols. Especially in the face of healthcare professional shortages, students can provide support while gaining experience. Recognising their contributions is key for workforce planning and improved healthcare delivery.

In one such setting, medical students from the Medical Faculty of the University of Basel served in frontline healthcare settings as members of Mobile SWAB Teams (MST) where they provided support to medical staff in various hospital wards, including the emergency department.15 These trainees were assigned clinical tasks and responsibilities under a model known as ‘distant supervision’, where they worked without direct oversight or immediate feedback.16

The aim of the current study was to explore medical students’ perceptions of working with limited supervision in frontline healthcare settings and examine the role of SRL in their professional development.

Methodology

Study design

We used a constructivist grounded theory (CGT) approach for this study, conducting semi-structured interviews to explore medical students’ perceptions of working in a clinical setting under limited supervision. CGT enabled us to explore the social interactions and learning processes that seem still not fully captured by existing theories. This approach also allowed us to both engage with and extend current research, while also remaining open to new themes that emerged during data collection and analysis. To guide our understanding of medical students’ experiences, we drew on the theoretical framework of SRL, particularly its relevance in workplace-based learning settings.

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Ethics Commission of Northwest and Central Switzerland (EKNZ, Req-2021–01299).

To ensure the rigour and trustworthiness of our qualitative research, we followed the widely recognised criteria for qualitative studies.17 First, credibility was strengthened by iterative coding, frequent comparison and team debriefing throughout the analysis. Second, transferability was promoted by offering comprehensive, thorough descriptions of the situation and individuals. Third, dependability was maintained by a transparent and methodical research approach, including a distinctive audit trail of coding decisions and thematic development. Last, confirmability was achieved by reflective memos and the maintenance of analytical notes. We describe each of these criteria in the appropriate sections in detail. In addition, this study is reported in accordance with the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research and the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research; the completed checklists are available in the submission files.

Study context

The Medical Faculty at the University of Basel follows a conventional curriculum that incorporates intermittent hands-on clinical practice throughout the initial 2 years of training. From the third year on, students engage in ‘physician-patient courses’, focusing on acquiring skills in conducting structured medical interviews and performing physical examinations. The sixth year entails a full-time, 1-year clerkship at the hospital, with all medical school-related activities conducted under careful supervision.

In the context of staffing shortages and infection control protocols, particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic, MSTs were assembled through the recruitment of medical students who previously volunteered in a triage test centre (TTC).4 These teams were responsible for taking swabs from all incoming patients on the ward, as well as those who showed new symptoms or were scheduled for outpatient surgery. Members were on call for all emergency swabs to relieve the nursing staff of a considerable workload at a time of increased system pressure. Morning and afternoon shifts consisted of four and two medical students, respectively. One group of six medical students worked for 3 days and were then replaced by the next group for 3 days. About half of the 12 students worked during the entire operating time from March through June 2021, while the rest were replaced by other medical students due to scheduling issues. Each medical student received an employment contract, insurance and compensation. During the peak of the deployment, the teams were supported by military personnel. After 1 day of supervision by hygiene experts from the test centre, the medical students were introduced into the hospital setting. The team of students was instructed to round on all wards (including the intermediate and intensive care units (IMC and ICU) and swab all patients, as assigned by the nursing staff. The trainees were subsequently allowed to work independently without direct supervision.16 The initial swab team members then assumed the responsibility of guiding and instructing the additional trainees.

Participants’ recruitment and data collection

Employing a purposive and also convenient sampling strategy, we recruited students working in frontline roles. Initial participants were contacted via WhatsApp, chosen for its accessibility and familiarity. All medical students who worked in the MST (n=20) were invited to participate in the study. Medical students who worked on the teams from their inception were invited for interviews first since we felt that they had gained more long-term and thematically rich experiences. The participants were recruited through a chat group or word-of-mouth.

This approach allowed for the efficient recruitment of a context-specific sample despite the challenges of pandemic-related restrictions. Sixteen medical students agreed to participate in the study and sampling sufficiency was reached after 13 interviews.

A total of 13 medical students participated in the study, of whom five were male and eight were female. None of the participants were in their first or second year of the medical curriculum. Three students were in their third year, nine in their fourth year and one in their fifth year; no participants were in their sixth year. The mean age of the participants was 23.9 years, with a range from 21 to 28 years.

Before each interview, participants provided written informed consent. All medical students participated voluntarily and did not receive compensation. Additionally, each participant was explicitly informed before the start of the interview that if they felt unwell, they could take a break or stop the interview at any time.

FM interviewed participants between June 2020 and February 2021 in a peer-to-peer setting in Swiss-German. This was done to create a more comfortable and communicative atmosphere. Interviews were conducted in person or via video call based on the participants’ preference. Generally, interviews lasted between 25 and 45 min and were in accordance with public health guidelines of social distancing and wearing masks. FM audio-recorded all interviews and transcribed them verbatim in Swiss German. Later, the relevant parts were translated into English. FM and JK developed the interview guide with ideas of SRL and SDT in mind to explore the mechanisms and motivations behind learning. SRL assisted with inquiries on cognitive and behavioural techniques, such as goal planning and monitoring personal progress, while SDT addressed inquiries regarding motivational requirements such as autonomy, competence and relatedness. With its combination, we aimed to gain a deeper understanding of both self-directed learning processes and the underlying motivational internal and external dynamics. We used open-ended questions to elicit in-depth responses while allowing flexibility for participants to share their experiences and perspectives freely. Being interviewed by a peer seemed to guarantee a candid conversation. We conducted two pilot interviews (P1, P2), which we included in the dataset (with participants’ consent) because of the richness of their perceptions. The interview guide was easy to follow and had a natural flow to the interview so that only questions were clarified.

The complete interview guide and field notes are available in the Appendix (see online supplemental material).

Data analysis

FM eliminated all identifying information from the transcripts. All transcripts were iteratively analysed using constructivist grounded theory methodology.18 Initially, FM, a medical student, and his supervisor, JK, conducted coding line-by-line, closely following participants' words, to generate a range of open codes that captured meaningful units of data. Using the constant comparison technique, FM, AP and JK examined data from both within and between transcripts to identify and refine into focused codes that clustered around recurring patterns and themes.18 Throughout the analysis process, FM engaged in memoing—writing reflections and theoretical notes linked to specific data excerpts. FM also used his field notes gathered during or after the interviews to help the team understand some findings and refine codes and later themes. In regular meetings with JK and AP, FM discussed additional data collection, themes and their deeper understanding and elaborated on potential improvements to the interview process, such as leaving the participants time to reflect deeper about tricky situations, especially with patients. Quirkos, a qualitative analysis software to code the transcripts and manage the data, was used to create a visual representation and comprehend the relationships between the interview data. For instance, a participant’s account of emotional detachment and fatigue was initially coded as ‘emotional distancing’ and ‘workload stress’. As analysis progressed, these were grouped under the broader theme ‘psychological impact of frontline work with patients.’

We reached data sufficiency when no new themes emerged from the interviews, and further data did not add meaningful insights.19 This was determined through ongoing team discussions and constant comparison of the data. Saturation was reached after 13 interviews, with three additional interviews conducted to confirm this.

Research team

The research team brought diverse backgrounds and expertise. FM, a medical student at the University of Basel, focused his master’s thesis on medical students’ perceptions as frontline workers during COVID-19, drawing on his involvement with the TTC and MST. His supervisor, JK, a senior surgeon, clinical supervisor and medical education researcher at the University of Basel, contributed insights shaped by her experience as a clinical educator. AP and VW, former medical students and now residents with an interest in medical education, helped analyse data and write the manuscript. AM contributed through her expertise in medical education. BB, an MD-PhD student at the University of Ottawa, brought experience in high-pressure clinical settings and qualitative research, adding an international perspective to the findings. CHN and RB shaped the study’s design, drawing on their extensive experience in developing and managing the TTC.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Below, we illustrate medical students’ perceptions concerning their learning experiences of training with distant supervision, as the transition to the clinical setting with representative quotes from the participants (P1–P13). We developed three themes from the analysed data, which are summarised in figure 1 along with their associated subthemes and descriptions, and present the relevant findings here. The first theme, teamwork, interprofessional collaboration and communication, highlights the importance of effective cooperation with nursing staff and support personnel, as well as the value of interprofessional teamwork in ensuring smooth clinical operations. While many participants described positive collaborative experiences, some also encountered logistical or interpersonal challenges that affected team dynamics. The second theme, working and learning independently, with distant supervision, reflects how the lack of direct oversight fostered autonomy, allowing students to make decisions and act independently for the first time. Subthemes included learning by doing, the necessity for self-organisation and reliance on patient feedback in the absence of immediate supervision. While many participants appreciated the freedom and found it motivating, others expressed stress and fear of making mistakes without real-time guidance. The third theme, Challenges in patient treatment and ethical considerations, encompasses ethically complex situations related to enforcing swab tests, respecting patient autonomy and managing language or cognitive barriers. Students also described emotional responses to difficult clinical cases, particularly in intensive care or emergency scenarios. Overall, these themes represent key learning opportunities that contributed to participants’ deeper understanding of patient-centred care, professional responsibility and the complexities of clinical practice in a pandemic context.

Overview of key themes and subthemes with a brief description identified from interview analysis.

Teamwork, interprofessional collaboration and communication

During their work in the MSTs, the students had the opportunity to learn important lessons about teamwork and interprofessional collaboration while working in a rapidly changing environment. One of the key lessons for successful and efficient collaboration learnt was the importance of effective communication among team members:

… cooperation and constant exchange with the nursing staff is very important. But I have always been of the opinion that nursing is a very important part of the medical system (P10). Another participant added: ”I noticed how important it is to be well understood by the nursing staff and communicate properly, because they can make things easier for you. (P2)

Other participants explained how they perceived the logistical challenges and value of nurses, support staff and administrators:

It showed what else is going on in the hospital. How many people are needed to make the processes work. I was not aware of this at all. I didn't even know how things work with the nurses. It was unclear to me that nurses spend most of their time with the patients and not physicians. I found it impressive how much knowledge the graduate nurses and ward managers have and how hard they work. They also had a lot of power and right of co-determination, which I had not been aware before (P7).

Furthermore, working together with fellow medical students and leaving the domestic circumstances was a chance to escape isolation:

I found it very pleasant with the staff as COVID-19 was still present and social limitations would have been necessary otherwise. So, it was very cool to be able to have social contact in that setting (P13).

While most students described positive experiences with working in an interprofessional team, they also had to face several challenges:

Partly, the cooperation with certain wards was very difficult. Some of the nursing staff was uncooperative. They did not keep to time agreements or were not accessible. … Partly, they did not even make any effort (P12).

Working and learning independently with distant supervision

Medical students worked with distant supervision within the MST, which represented a novel and potentially challenging working and learning environment. As a result of distant supervision, several components of traditional clinical supervision were lacking. Instead, learners relied on consistent feedback from patients, encompassing both positive and negative remarks on their performance during swab tests:

Feedback was always challenging. There was no opportunity for direct feedback because nobody was present (P1).

However, there was consistent feedback from patients, which participants perceived as both positive and negative:

… but the majority of patient feedback was positive. Statements such as ‘That wasn't as bad as ie,xpected,’ ‘Thank you for being so responsive to me’, and ‘You did a good job’, were common. Negative feedback was also received, particularly from patients who had never undergone a swab test before. (P2)

Working unsupervised provided a new unique working and learning environment. This allowed students to work in a more comfortable and relaxed setting in which they could work at their own discretion:

Compared to other work in the hospital, I took away more from this role as I was able to work more autonomously. I could decide for myself when and how to do something. It was very good as an exercise and completely different from everything else (P1).

Another participant emphasised the increased independence within the swab team, highlighting the absence of constant supervision and the subsequent learning opportunities that arose from taking on more responsibility:

In MST you had more freedom; you were much more independent. You weren't constantly protected or monitored. … You didn't have a doctor to help you afterwards. You learnt a lot more because you just had to do it (P2).

Additionally, some medical students saw this clinical situation as an opportunity to gain valuable clinical experience and develop the skills necessary to become successful healthcare professionals:

We were motivated to learn something new (P8).” and “I thought it was exciting to do something we had never done before (P11).

Another participant highlighted the importance of the transition to work independently:

Normally, you have someone with you who looks after you. We had to do everything on our own, had to organize everything on our own. Where do I get the materials, for example? Where do I find the gloves? I had to ask people, see where the patients were and organize myself how, when, and where the swab test would take place. It gave you a chance to learn from past mistakes and optimize your own work. (P8)

Over time, students noted that they had also become more self-confident and decisive in their newly set roles:

What has changed is that I can be more resolute and also make it clear that something is necessary. Also, that I simply had to fulfill a task that was not pleasant but then had to insist that it was just the way it was. I became more determined and communicated more clearly what my task was and what I was going to do now (P3).

Participants also highlighted the increased learning that occurred when faced with challenging situations and left to navigate them independently:

When you're thrown in at the deep end, you always learn faster; you're kind of forced into it. You were also more motivated because you were on your own and nobody was going to show you how to do things (P8).

Despite the lack of direct supervision, one participant emphasised their commitment to upholding high standards in their work:

I would say our team worked properly for the most part and everyone was dutiful and didn't let themselves drag. It was important to us that the work was done correctly and cleanly (P4).

At the same time, considering the first steps of a SRL cycle, some students were afraid of making technical errors due to their clinical-practical inexperience, as one participant described:

Initially, I was most afraid of sticking the stick up someone’s nose the wrong way and causing a nosebleed. I'm always afraid of triggering something in a seriously ill patient or that something will happen right this second and above all that I'll be overwhelmed in this situation and won't know what to do (P9).

Similarly, for other students, the absence of supervision was associated with stress and fear of making mistakes, including their consequences:

At first, I was afraid that I wouldn't do it right and that mistakes would happen to me. The mistakes wouldn't be noticed because no one would check it. (P2).

Another student described a similar feeling, explaining:

Especially in the first 2 weeks, this worried me a lot because I was afraid that I could take a swab inadequately and that the hospital would miss a positive patient because of my mistake (P1).

Therefore, participants often found it necessary to have debriefing sessions with their supervisors after incidents:

A lot of it was learning-by-doing, and in the beginning, we did a lot of things differently. We then had a meeting every few days to discuss how things were going and what problems we were currently struggling with. Then we adapted things and did them differently again (P12).

Overall, working in MSTs was described as ‘…a valuable experience, a century experience (P1)’. It helped the medical students to clarify their values and beliefs about healthcare and the role of the medical profession, and it also helped them to gain more confidence in their appearance:

…learnt how to work independently without someone checking behind you. You were the person who wanted something from the patient and you were not just shadowing a doctor. You did everything yourself and had to explain what to do and what you wanted. That’s where a more determined appearance was essential (P3).

Challenges in patient treatment and ethical considerations

During their work in the swab teams, the medical students repeatedly found themselves in situations with patients who refused the swab test, explaining:

Some patients felt attacked by us. All patients had to get swabbed, but certain patients vehemently resisted. It was against human rights, and we were confronted with similar statements (P5).

In some situations, medical students felt conflicted regarding patients’ autonomy:

There was always a conflict. Do you swab the patient, or do you respect the patient’s autonomy? The test was primarily for the protection of other patients and staff, which is why it was very important. It was very difficult (P9).

One participant even described that some situations escalated:

There were times when my hand was slapped away, or I was even insulted. (P2)

Further, participants also reported repeated patient encounters involving communication challenges, either because of a language barrier or because of their health condition, such as cognitive impairment or intubation. One participant shared:

There was one patient who could not express herself. She could not talk, but (she) refused to take the swab. When I then started with the nasal swab, this did not work right away, and she felt it was very bad. She did not want to take the swab under any circumstances. This made me wonder what I could do and how far I had to respect the patient’s will. … We didn't have a smear test and she had still experienced something she didn't want. (P3)

One participant also described their experience of performing a swab test on a patient in the ICU who displayed no signs of consciousness or responsiveness, causing unprecedented emotions:

When we had to do the swab test on a patient in the Intensive Care Unit (ICU), the person was unresponsive. I found it very strange to swab an unconscious person. She was alone in the room and had no symptoms (P13).

Another student shared a distressing incident that they had experienced:

Once we had to swab an unstable patient in the ICU. The patient had oxygen nasal cannula and the nurse said we could just take them off for a moment. When I took his nasal cannula off, his heart rate and oxygen saturation started to drop. I just thought to myself: ‘Oh my God’ and I panicked (P8).

Despite some negative experiences, most students reported improvements in their skills to handle difficult clinical situations:

You had a lot of contact with different patients, some of whom were troublesome, but you came into contact with all of them. It was challenging to convince patients of something unpleasant. You also got to know different types of patients and learned which approach worked better for whom (P4).

One student reflected on the positive impact of their experience regarding communication skills:

Working here gave you practice in communicating and dealing with patients' concerns. You became calmer and more relaxed. You got routine and didn't stand shivering in front of the patient’s room and being nervous (P7).

Discussion

In the current study, medical students were entrusted with independently performing clinical assessments and taking nasopharyngeal and oropharyngeal swabs. These tasks were performed under distant supervision.16 While some students recognised the importance of communication and teamwork and reported gaining knowledge and skills from these newfound responsibilities, others found the novel working and learning environment to be very stressful and overwhelming.

Medical students, while possessing valuable clinical skills and knowledge, can apply their expertise in clinical practice through low-risk diagnostic assessments. This includes clinical interviews, physical exams and basic diagnostic procedures like swabbing, as well as involvement in contact tracing and patient education.20

SRL is a crucial predictor of student academic performance in clinical settings. Medical students who engage in strategies such as self-monitoring, reflection and time management consistently perform better in both academic and practical competencies, particularly in contexts with limited direct supervision.10 Our findings align with this, suggesting that even a single, specific task like performing a swab can effectively facilitate SRL among medical students transitioning to the clinical environment. This task allows students to engage in the complete SRL cycle—setting goals, developing strategies and reflecting on their performance—thus promoting deeper learning and professional development. Despite the brevity of individual tasks, such focused experiences can significantly enhance SRL competencies, and the collected experiences support deeper learning beyond the task itself, as our study demonstrated. Medical students developed communication and collaboration skills, reflected on their performances, or even were triggered to consider ethical questions regarding patients' autonomy.

However, despite the potential for gaining crucial real-world experience, there are associated risks such as exposure to workplace hazard (ie, viral infection in the current study), burnout and challenges in balancing academic and personal commitments.21 The study also highlights the conflict between meeting training needs and respecting patient rights, particularly due to the clinical inexperience of students facing challenging situations in high-pressure clinical contexts.

As part of their work, medical students learnt to appreciate every member of the healthcare team, regardless of their role or level of experience. This meant recognising the unique skills and expertise of each team member and working together to achieve shared goals. Overall, the experience of working assuming responsibility in a clinical setting has taught the medical students important lessons about teamwork and collaboration. They faced challenges related to leadership and decision-making, particularly in situations where there was ambiguity or uncertainty.22 Effective teamwork requires clear communication and a shared understanding of roles and responsibilities, and medical students may need support and guidance in developing these skills.23 In this context, Tuckman’s model outlines the stages that teams typically go through, which resonates with the progression we observed in our students: forming, storming, norming, performing and adjourning.24 In the forming stage, team members get to know each other. Storming is characterised by conflicts and disagreements, including conflicts with patients as well. Norming sees the establishment of cohesion and trust. During this stage, our students learnt how to collaborate with various work groups in an interprofessional setting and got to know each other on a deeper level. Performing is marked by high productivity, and in the adjourning stage, the team disbands. Applying this model to medical student teams in autonomous clinical roles helps us understand their teamwork dynamics and challenges.25

In our study, medical students progressed into the clinical setting as responsible and accountable healthcare providers. This hands-on involvement not only enhanced their clinical proficiency but also nurtured a sense of accountability and independence.

Within this context, SRL emerges as a pivotal component of medical education. As our medical students assumed heightened responsibilities, their capacity to autonomously regulate and guide their learning became paramount. The degree of autonomy that medical students experience depends on a variety of factors, including their level of training, experience, the policies and procedures of their clinical settings, and the supervision and support provided by their instructors and mentors.26 At the beginning of their medical education, students may feel like they have little control over their own learning and patient care decisions. They may be closely supervised by attending physicians, residents and other senior clinicians and may have limited opportunities to make independent decisions.27 However, in the current setting, medical students were given more autonomy, which was experienced to be both exciting and challenging. By applying SDT, we tried to understand their motivations and experiences better.14 According to SDT, individuals are driven by the desire to fulfil their fundamental psychological needs for autonomy, competence and relatedness. Analysing the students’ experiences through the lens of SDT allowed us to examine how their sense of autonomy in clinical decision-making, feelings of competence in patient care tasks and relationships with healthcare team members influenced their engagement and well-being.

These findings support recent theories about learner agency. Spence et al explored how learners demonstrate agency when facing educational challenges, describing it as a context-dependent process shaped by perceived opportunities, available support and the willingness to act despite uncertainty.28 While their study focused on simulation-based learning environments, our findings extend this concept to high-responsibility, low-supervision contexts. Medical students in our study demonstrated agency through adaptive behaviours and independent decision-making under emotionally charged circumstances. They did not just follow instructions; they shaped their role within the clinical setting, assumed responsibility and made deliberate contributions, even in the absence of close supervision. Although our data were collected during a time of system strain, the underlying educational processes are not limited to crisis scenarios. Similar conditions—reduced supervision, evolving responsibilities and time-sensitive decisions—also occur in clinical learning environments. For learners, these situations can offer important opportunities to grow into new roles. At the same time, they may cause feelings of uncertainty, hesitation and deeper understanding of one’s own limitations.

The experiences described by our participants also reflect what Klasen refers to as an ‘unspoken pedagogy’ of clinical training. Failure and uncertainty, when accompanied by appropriate relational support, can serve as meaningful learning opportunities rather than threats to competence.29

However, while they frequently gained a sense of autonomy as they advanced in their training, the fear of making mistakes remained a significant worry for medical students, particularly in the early stages of their training.22 This anxiety can be overwhelming and stressful, as students bear a heightened sense of responsibility for their patients' outcomes. Moral distress may arise when medical students find themselves torn between achieving academic goals at the expense of patient care or when compelled to undertake tasks for which they lack confidence or competence.23 Coping with and learning from errors can adversely impact well-being and overall learning experiences.30

In our study, most students had no or little prior clinical experience and were confronted with technical and non-technical tasks for the first time. This lack of experience and exposure to such tasks contributed to their fear of making mistakes, especially due to the absence of supervision, leading to increased stress and anxiety. Research has shown that medical students in high-pressure environments often report elevated levels of stress, particularly when institutional structures fail to provide adequate orientation or debriefing opportunities. Klasen et al demonstrated that even under demanding conditions, students’ well-being can be maintained when their roles are perceived as purposeful and embedded in supportive conditions.20 Our findings align with this: students did not just acquire skills or perform tasks, they stepped into professional roles, navigated ethical complexity and began to understand what it means to be responsible for others. These moments contributed not only to clinical development but to the formation of a professional self, grounded in experience, reflection and accountability. Those findings emphasise learner agency in practical educational contexts and provide empirical depth to theoretical frameworks, guiding the creation of autonomy-supportive learning environments in medical education.

Another subject that the study highlights is students’ perceptions of patients’ autonomy. When medical students work with patients, they must respect the autonomy of the patient and ensure that they are involved in the decision-making process to the greatest extent possible. An observation by Gil Santos et al 31 showed that only 59% of patients who had previous experiences with medical students reported being asked for consent, raising concerns about the communication and ethical practices within clinical settings. Informed consent is a cornerstone of medical ethics, and its application in educational contexts is essential for maintaining the trust and dignity of patients. Their finding that 35% of patients would feel more comfortable without the presence of medical students underscores the importance of respecting patient preferences. Furthermore, our students had to face unique challenges, including language barriers, unstable health conditions of patients and cognitive impairments, which often complicated their decision-making process. Additionally, the fact that they were mostly unsupervised during their work further impacted their ability to make informed decisions. In such challenging situations, the students sometimes had to call for help or request debriefing sessions with their supervisors after such incidents. Striking a balance between the educational needs of students and the comfort of patients is an ongoing challenge that requires continuous evaluation and adaptation of teaching methods.

To address these challenges, medical schools and clinical settings should provide medical students with early training and support to help them communicate effectively with patients, understand cultural differences, communication issues and respect the autonomy of patients.32

Ultimately, the decision of whether medical students should assume clinical responsibility early on depends on a variety of factors. These factors include the demands of the healthcare system, the readiness and willingness of individual medical students and the measures in place to ensure their safety and well-being.33 It is important for medical schools and healthcare organisations to carefully consider these factors and make informed decisions about how to involve medical students in everyday clinical practice.

Limitations

The findings are based on context-dependent and influenced by individual experiences and perceptions, acknowledging the potential presence of self-selection among participants. However, transferability was supported through deep descriptions of the educational context, participant backgrounds and illustrative quotes from the participants. Including medical students working in frontline roles during the COVID-19 pandemic adds depth and specificity to the findings, while also reflecting challenges and strategies applicable to health professions education more broadly. Their experiences offer insight into how learners in high-stakes, high-stress environments navigate autonomy, motivation and self-regulation—making the findings particularly relevant to clinical training contexts.

While FM’s interviews fostered a peer-to-peer environment for sharing experiences, some individuals may have chosen not to discuss sensitive topics related to the unprecedented pandemic or emotionally charged situations, which could have influenced the data collected. Moreover, a peer interview approach may provide responses influenced by social desirability or may have omitted certain aspects due to memory recall limitations.

Conclusion

Integrating medical students into the clinical environment, particularly under conditions of limited supervision or high service demand, can be instrumental in ensuring continued patient support while also offering formative learning experiences. The experience of working independently in such settings offers students a unique opportunity to enhance their skills, engage in self-directed learning and begin to assume a professional role grounded in responsibility and reflection. Medical educators should therefore consider providing students with the chance to independently learn and apply both theoretical knowledge and practical skills in real-life scenarios following appropriate training. Overall, students should be supported in developing both communication skills and the ability to protect patient autonomy while also maintaining their own well-being.
