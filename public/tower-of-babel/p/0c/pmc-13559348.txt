Etiological profile and prognostic factors of airway fistula: a retrospective study
Authors: Guofeng Wu, Chi Zhang, Yinyin Qin, Xiaobo Chen, Xiaoyi Pan, Yingbo Wu, Shasha He, Shiyue Li, Zhuquan Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAirway fistula, such as tracheoesophageal fistula (TOF) and bronchopleural fistula (BPF), is a rare but life-threatening complication associated with cancer treatment. However, the etiological profile and its association with prognosis of airway fistulas remain unclear. The current study aimed to identify the etiological profile and prognostic factors of airway fistula.MethodsA total of 102 patients with TOF or BPF, recruited between January 2021 and December 2023, were analyzed. Patients’ demographics, clinical presentations, and microbiological data were collected, while survival outcomes and prognostic factors were analyzed.ResultsIn total, 67 pathogen species and 223 strains were identified, with P. aeruginosa and S. aureus among the most common. Multidrug-resistant bacteria (MDRB) constituted 61% of identified pathogens, with a higher prevalence in BPF cases. Patients with a chronic course of fistula (P=0.04) and those with Staphylococcus infections (P=0.03) had a high risk of carrying MDRB. Though TOF and BPF patients’ clinical outcomes did not significantly differ in the Fine-Gray model, MDRB infections (P<0.001) and Pseudomonas infections (P=0.02) were associated with prolonged fistula stabilization period. Cox regression identified multidrug resistance (MDR) infections (P=0.04) and Pseudomonas infections (P=0.02) as key factors associated with prolonged fistula stabilization period.ConclusionsMDRB and infection of Pseudomonas were closely associated with poor prognosis of airway fistula.

Highlight box

Key findings

• P. aeruginosa (16%) and S. aureus (5%) were the most common among the 223 pathogen strains identified.

• Multidrug-resistant bacteria (MDRB) accounted for 60% of isolates, with a higher prevalence in bronchopleural fistula (36%) than in tracheoesophageal fistula (23%).

• Chronic fistula and Staphylococcus infection were predictors of multidrug resistance (MDR) carriage.

• MDRB, Pseudomonas, and malignant fistula independently predicted poor prognosis.

What is known and what is new?

• Airway fistulas are life-threatening, often cancer-related, with high mortality from infection. Management is largely experience-based.

• This study provides the first detailed profile of pathogens and MDR burden (61%) in airway fistulas; identifies MDRB and Pseudomonas as independent prognostic factors.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection and MDRB infection in patients with airway fistulas require clinical attention, as they may indicate a poor prognosis. Staphylococcus infection and a chronic fistula course may suggest an increased probability of developing MDR isolates.

• Larger prospective studies of the association between infection and prognosis in patients with airway fistulas are warranted.

Introduction

Background

Airway fistula is a rare but life-threatening complication of long-term bronchial management. Many types of fistulas have been described, namely, tracheoesophageal fistula (TOF) (1), bronchopleural fistula (BPF) (2). Since congenital fistulas have been well described (3), this study focused on acquired fistulas. Based on their etiology, acquired fistulas can be categorized into malignant and benign types, each making up about 50% of the acquired cases (4). Most airway fistulas are treatment-induced complications from cancer therapies (5). Although the occurrence rate of fistulas is relatively low (8.3% as complications of esophageal cancer) (6), it is linked to high mortality rates and requires rapid treatment.

Rationale and knowledge gap

The median survival of patients with TOF from diagnosis is less than 3 months (1). Similarly, BPF remains one of the most severe and life-threatening complications. Postoperative BPFs are considered severe, and fewer than 20% heal spontaneously (7). The mortality rate associated with BPF ranges from 18% to 67% (8). Patient prognosis is strongly linked to disease stage and treatment modality. Studies have shown that various factors, including sex, pre-fistula tumor treatment, type of esophageal fistula, fistula treatment, and hemoglobin levels, significantly influence the prognosis of airway fistulas (9).

Patients with fistulas commonly experience severe, refractory lung infections and profound malnutrition, leading to rapid health decline and poor quality of life. The primary benign infectious causes include tuberculosis, histoplasmosis, and actinomycosis (10,11). Without prompt, aggressive intervention, patients typically succumb to the disease within weeks or months, with overwhelming lung infection being the primary cause of death in more than 90% of cases (12).

Objective

Currently, anti-infection strategies are primarily adjusted based on clinical presentation and physician experience, with limited research addressing patient drug resistance. Therefore, the current study aimed to identify the etiological profile and prognostic factors of airway fistula. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1091/rc).

Methods

This was a retrospective cohort study conducted at The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University. We defined a 3-year index period from January 2021 to December 2023 to ensure that sufficient data were available and to provide adequate follow-up for all patients (Figure 1).

Flow diagram of eligible participants. mNGS, metagenomic next-generation sequencing.

Subject enrollment

Patients with TOF or BPF were recruited from The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University between January 2021 and December 2023. Enrollment criteria included: (I) diagnosis of TOF or BPF confirmed by bronchoscopy; (II) positive pathogenic microbiological results from bacterial culture and/or metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF); and (III) treatment with fully covered metallic stenting for fistula occlusion (Figure 1). All airway stenting procedures were performed under general anesthesia using a flexible bronchoscope (BF-260, Olympus Corporation, Japan). Covered self-expandable metallic stents (Nanjing Eco, Micro-Tech, China) were implanted for fistula occlusion, with stent type and size were determined by the airway luminal diameter and the length of the fistula lesion.

Information collection

Data on demographic characteristics, high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) and bronchoscopy findings, etiological results (including bacterial culture and mNGS data), and patient outcomes were collected and analyzed. Antibiotic susceptibility profiles were also recorded. The study cohort comprised patients with airway stents and chronic airway abnormalities. To differentiate between infection and colonization, infection was defined as two consecutive BALF cultures with growth ≥104 CFU/mL accompanied by at least two clinical criteria (fever, leukocytosis, purulent sputum, or new infiltrates on imaging). Otherwise, the condition was classified as colonization and assigned to the non-infection group in this study.

Although previous studies have indicated that HRCT provides valuable insights for diagnosing bronchial fistula (13), the final diagnosis in this study was based on bronchoscopy findings. mNGS demonstrates greater sensitivity in identifying pathogens and is less influenced by previous antibiotic use (14). Therefore, we collected all available mNGS data alongside usable bacterial culture results and integrated these findings. mNGS testing was primarily indicated for patients meeting the following criteria: (I) those who remained critically ill or continued to deteriorate despite empirical anti-infective therapy; (II) those with negative conventional culture results but a high clinical suspicion of infection; and (III) those with suspected rare, unusual, or mixed pathogen infections.

Multidrug-resistant bacteria (MDRB) were defined according to the protocol of the European Antimicrobial Resistance Surveillance Network (EARS-Net) (15). Multidrug resistance (MDR) was defined as acquired non-susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories, extensively drug-resistant (XDR) as non-susceptibility to at least one agent in all but two or fewer antimicrobial categories and pandrug resistant (PDR) as non-susceptibility to all agents in all antimicrobial categories. Tigecycline references the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) standards, colistin references the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) standards, and other agents reference the 2022 Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) standards. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (No. 2021-118), and was conducted in accordance with The Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. All subjects enrolled in this study gave written informed consent.

Clinical outcomes

The primary study endpoint was fistula closure, defined as complete healing of the airway fistula. Given the high malignancy of the enrolled patients, achieving complete healing was challenging; therefore, fistula stabilization, the secondary endpoint, was considered an indicator of a favorable prognosis. Fistula stabilization was defined by the following criteria: assessed during two consecutive follow-up visits with an interval of at least 2 weeks, (I) no new erythema, swelling, tenderness, or spontaneous rupture around the external fistula opening; (II) no new branches or new fistula formation detected via bronchoscopy; and (III) no systemic signs of infection, such as fever, leukocytosis, or other manifestations indicating infection spread. All bronchoscopic images were independently reviewed by two experienced interventional pulmonologists. In cases of disagreement, a third senior specialist was consulted to reach a final consensus through discussion. When interpreting bronchoscopic findings and determining fistula stabilization or closure, the assessors had access only to the necessary imaging and laboratory data and were not provided with information regarding treatment assignment or prior medical records, in order to minimize detection bias. Each patient was evaluated by experienced clinicians, and discharge was permitted when the fistula condition showed significant improvement compared to admission and respiratory infections had ameliorated. To minimize selection bias, we adopted a consecutive enrollment strategy, whereby all TOF/BPF patients meeting the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria during the study period were sequentially enrolled without any subjective selection based on prognosis or clinical characteristics. All enrolled patients received at least 6 months of follow-up, with bronchoscopy performed at each clinical visit. Based on these criteria, time to stabilization was defined as the duration from fistula diagnosis to the achievement of either primary or secondary endpoint.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted via SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, New York) and R version 4.2.3. Missing data were addressed using complete case analysis, as the proportion of missing values was low (<5%) and assumed to be missing completely at random. To evaluate the influence of imputation on the stability of our findings, we additionally performed extreme-case scenario analyses, in which missing values were replaced with the most favorable and most unfavorable possible outcomes, respectively, in order to assess whether our conclusions remained consistent across alternative missing-data strategies. Continuous variables were analyzed via t-tests, whereas categorical variable was analyzed via chi-square test. Fine-Gray competing risk regression was used to calculate curves for the time to stabilization and post fistula survival time, as well as to determine median survival time. Prognostic factors were evaluated through univariate and multivariate analysis via Cox regression model, with P values, hazard ratios (HRs), and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) calculated for each variable. The multifactor Cox model uses forward selection as the variable selection strategy. The assessment of the proportional hazards assumption is provided in Appendix 1. A P value below 0.05 (two-tailed) was deemed statistically significant. Although the use of routinely collected healthcare data through the above methods was standardized, it remains possible that erroneous diagnoses were made (false positives) and other diagnoses were missed (false negatives), which would affect the incidence rates reported here. Future studies might increase sample sizes, adopt multicenter designs, or combine different data sources to improve case detection and reduce the potential for bias. Loss to follow-up due to various reasons resulted in missing outcome data; therefore, cases with loss to follow-up were excluded from the statistical analysis.

Results

After screening, a total of 110 patients with airway fistula complicated by infections were included in this study. 8 patients were lost to follow-up, and 102 patients were included in the analysis. 85 males and 17 females with a mean age (range) of 57 [26–86] years. The median follow-up duration was 21.7 months. Regarding fistula type, 66 patients were diagnosed with TOF, while 36 patients were diagnosed with BPF. Enrolled patients were divided into a benign fistula group (48/102) and a malignant fistula group (54/102) on the basis of the origin of the airway fistula. Benign fistulas were caused by pulmonary lobectomy (21, 44%), tracheal intubation (6, 13%), infectious diseases and trauma (12, 25%), or others (9, 19%). Malignant fistulas were caused by esophageal cancer (21, 39%), non-small cell lung cancer (23, 43%), and small cell lung cancer (10, 18%). Discussion

Key findings

This retrospective analysis investigated potential prognostic factors in patients with airway fistulas. The findings indicated that Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection, MDRB infection, and malignancy-related fistulas, were significantly associated with poor outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

The study’s strengths lie in its comprehensive etiological and prognostic analysis of airway fistulas. However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the evaluation of the impact of bacterial strains on fistulas was constrained by the limited sample size. Specifically, the sample size was insufficient to support a combined analysis of MDRB infection, Pseudomonas infection, and malignant fistula, precluding the elucidation of direct associations among these three factors. Second, etiological diagnosis relied on mNGS and/or bacterial culture rather than overall mNGS.

Comparison with similar research

Few studies have examined the relationship between infection and airway fistula. Compared with a similar study by Sun et al. (16), the present study represents one of the larger-sample analyses of the etiological profile and prognosis of airway fistulas, identifying a total of 223 pathogen strains.

Explanations of findings

Among Gram-negative bacteria, Enterobacter was the most common pathogen infecting patients with BPF. Enterobacter species are typically nosocomial pathogens (17), indicating that the lower respiratory tract is predominantly colonized by exogenous pathogens, often introduced through traumatic injuries. Risk factors for Enterobacter infection include recent exposure to broad-spectrum antibiotics, immunosuppression (e.g., cancer, diabetes), invasive procedures, and intensive care unit (ICU) admission (18,19). These factors increase susceptibility to Enterobacter colonization, potentially leading to BPF and secondary infection following pulmonary surgery. In contrast, Pseudomonas emerged as the dominant pathogen in the microbial etiology of TOF. All detected Pseudomonas species in TOF patients were identified as P. aeruginosa. The airway fistula is closely interconnected with adjacent cavities or organs, resulting in organ dysfunction and severe infection.

Candida was the most frequently detected fungus in both BPF and TOF patients, with C. albicans being the predominant species. C. albicans remained the dominant species both before and after tracheal stent placement in patients with esophageal cancer complicated by esophageal-tracheal fistula. As an opportunistic yeast, C. albicans can cause a range of infections, from superficial to life-threatening systemic disease, particularly in immunocompromised hosts (20). Furthermore, Candida infection may predispose patients to airway fistula development (21). Early Candida infection and anastomotic breakdown likely weaken the airway, thereby promoting fistula formation and progression.

Fistula origin (benign vs. malignant), MDRB infection, and Pseudomonas infection showed a statistical association with time to stabilization. TOF is a common complication of esophageal cancer, often developing advanced local stages due to the close anatomical proximity of the esophagus and airways (22). Previous research has indicated that an increased TOF risk is associated with esophageal stenting and radiotherapy (23-25). During radiation therapy, tumor shrinkage can lead to fistula formation within the lesion, creating a connection between the esophagus and nearby organs. Consequently, radiation may induce acute necrosis in cancerous tissues, inhibiting regeneration and resulting in complications such as pneumonia, abscess, hemoptysis, or dyspnea following airway fistula development. Although these factors demonstrate statistical discriminative ability, their clinical significance still needs to be comprehensively assessed in conjunction with the patient’s overall condition and treatment context.

MDRB infection was identified as a prognostic factor in multivariate Cox regression (HR =0.332, P<0.05) and the Fine-Gray model. Few studies have discussed the relationship between MDRB and airway fistula. The HR and its 95% CI were both below 1, suggesting that this exposure factor hinders the achievement of the endpoint event—namely fistula stabilization. Pseudomonas infection was found to be an independent prognostic factor for airway fistula. P. aeruginosa causes significant hemorrhagic and tissue damage, and treatment remains difficult even with sensitive antibiotics (26). Protecting the unaffected lung from pleural fluid aspiration is crucial to minimize the risk of pneumonia and respiratory failure. Given that Pseudomonas infection is negatively correlated with airway fistula prognosis (HR =0.485, P<0.05), initiating empirical treatment may be considered for patients presenting with severe signs of infection before the results of pleural effusion culture results are available. It should be noted, however, that given the retrospective single-center design and the limitations of the analysis, this conclusion still requires validation.

Implications and actions needed

The assessment of bacterial strain impact on fistulas was limited by the small sample size. Furthermore, mNGS and/or bacterial culture were conducted for etiologic diagnosis rather than comprehensive mNGS screening. Clinical features, laboratory results and follow-up data were not entirely conclusive in this retrospective study. Several clinically relevant variables were not included and may represent important confounders, including performance status, prior antibiotic exposure, immunosuppression, mechanical ventilation, and nutritional status. In this study, mNGS was used only in selected patients with severe illness, negative cultures despite high clinical suspicion, or suspected mixed/rare infections. Although not designed to systematically compare mNGS with conventional cultures, our findings suggest its potential incremental value in complex or refractory infections.

The recommendation that empiric antibiotics should cover Pseudomonas should be interpreted with caution. This conclusion may be overly strong given the retrospective, single-center design and the inherent limitations of our analysis. In clinical practice, the decision to initiate such empirical therapy should be moderated and contextualized according to local epidemiology, patient-specific risk factors (e.g., presence of airway fistula and severity of infection), and antimicrobial stewardship principles. Further research is needed to analyze the relationships between etiology characteristics and fistula prognosis.

Conclusions

Our findings highlight that MDR bacteria and Pseudomonas infections significantly prolong fistula healing time and contribute to clinical deterioration. Furthermore, a chronic fistula course and Staphylococcus infections are associated with a significantly higher risk of MDR isolates.
