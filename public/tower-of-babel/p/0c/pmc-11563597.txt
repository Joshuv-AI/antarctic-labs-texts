Factors associated with success rate of oral force-feeding ammonium chloride administration to acidify urine in goats
Authors: Phitcha Pongphitcha, Kanyarat Chuchoed, Thitiporn Thetsana, Napassorn Dachphun, Patskit Sukhong, Niorn Ratanapob
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Ammonium chloride is the most common urine acidifier used for urolithiasis treatment and prevention in goats.Aim:The objective of this study was to determine a guideline for ammonium chloride administration to acidify urine in goats.Methods:A three-period, three-treatment crossover study was conducted in three groups, with 10 female goats in each group. Three dosages of ammonium chloride were used (250, 350, or 450 mg/kg). In each experimental period, ammonium chloride was orally administered for three consecutive days or until the urine pH dropped below 6.5. Urine samples were collected to measure the pH before each administration and 1 day after the last administration. The Cox proportional hazards model was conducted to identify factors associated with the success rate in reducing the urine pH below 6.5 at a time.Results:The dosage of ammonium chloride and period of administration were significantly associated with the success rate (p < 0.001). The success rates during a unit of time for goats receiving 350 mg/kg and 450 mg/kg were greater than that of goats receiving 250 mg/kg. The second and third periods of ammonium chloride administration provided higher success rates during a unit of time compared to the first period. The success rate was also associated with the pretreatment urine pH (p = 0.044). There was a higher success rate for goats with a lower pretreatment urine pH.Conclusion:The recommended ammonium chloride dosage was 350 mg/kg. Periodic use can be considered to achieve better outcomes. The pretreatment urine pH should be measured to anticipate the clinical response.Keywords: Ammonium chloride, Goat, Success rate, Urine acidification, Urolithiasis

Introduction

Urolithiasis is the most common urinary tract problem in goats, having a direct effect on the urinary and reproductive systems that can cause economic losses and lead to death. Predominantly, the most severe cases occur in males due to the anatomical features of the urinary tract. The length and sigmoid flexure of the urethra and the presence of a urethral process with a narrow diameter in males can lead to obstructive calculi (Jones et al., 2009; Riedi et al., 2018; Ahmmed et al., 2019; Islam et al., 2021; Sharun et al., 2021). However, uroliths can be formed in females. A urolith is caused by an accumulation of minerals leading to stone formation. The most common types of urolith found in goats are calcium phosphate or apatite and magnesium ammonium phosphate or struvite (Jones et al., 2017; Falchi et al., 2023). Typically, they are formed in alkaline urine (Ewoldt et al., 2008; Mahajan et al., 2017).

Treatment options for urolithiasis include medical and surgical interventions. There are various medications that can be used to dissolve stones, while many surgical procedures have been performed to remove the blockage or to create a new opening to release urine. Variability in the success rate depends on the duration and location of obstruction, the condition of the goat, and the severity of tissue injury. The prognosis of surgical treatment is guarded to poor (Ewoldt et al., 2008; Riedi et al., 2018; Oman et al., 2019; Sickinger and Windhorst, 2022; Eicher et al., 2023); therefore, prevention of the urolith formation is preferable. To prevent urolithiasis, minerals in the diet should be controlled, with goats receiving sufficient roughage to avoid excessive concentrate intake. Adequate water consumption is also necessary to prevent stone formation. Administering anionic salts to acidify urine can be used to treat and prevent some types of uroliths that commonly are formed in alkaline urine. A urine pH below 6.5 is suitable for dissolving the stones (Jacob et al., 2001; Sprake, 2012). In addition, providing a negatively charged salt can stimulate water intake, with ammonium chloride being an anionic salt typically used to acidify urine in goats (Ewoldt et al., 2008; Jones et al., 2009; EFSA, 2012).

Because feeds mixed with ammonium chloride become less palatable (EFSA, 2012), force-feeding is sometimes used to ensure adequate intake. However, there has been no clear guideline regarding ammonium chloride force-feeding to acidify urine in goats. One study in goats reported that 400 and 500 mg/kg/day were effective in acidifying urine to the optimal pH (<6.5), while 200 mg/kg/day was insufficient; however, only one goat was used for each dosage (Mavangira et al., 2010). Sprake (2012) reported that the response to ammonium chloride administration in goats varied with individual goats. Therefore, a study with a larger sample size should provide more valid results. The effectiveness of ammonium chloride in acidifying urine may depend on the pretreatment urine pH; however, there has been no published report on this to date. Therefore, the objective of this study was to the determine factors associated with the success rate of oral force-feeding ammonium chloride administration to acidify urine in goats, considering dosage, period of ammonium chloride administration, and the pretreatment urine pH.

Materials and Methods

Experimental design and animal care

The study sample consisted of 30 female Saanen goats raised together and fed with the same concentrate and roughage. They were stratified for age and ranked according to body weight and then allocated into three groups using systematic sampling. Roughage and concentrate feeds were collected to determine the calcium and total phosphorus levels according to AOAC Official Method 927.02 and AOAC Official Method 965.17 (AOAC, 2019), respectively. The Mapada V-1200 Visible Spectrophotometer (Mapada, Shanghai, China) was used for the photometric method to determine levels of phosphorus.

A three-period crossover study design was applied, with three different dosages of ammonium chloride (250, 350, or 450 mg/kg). A 7-day washout period was used between experimental periods. Ammonium chloride was prepared based on the body weight of each goat and was mixed with flavored syrup diluted with tap water. The mixture was orally force-fed using a syringe. In each period, the goats received ammonium chloride for 3 consecutive days (d1, d2, and d3) or until the urine pH had dropped below 6.5, which was considered a successful outcome. Urine collection was performed before each ammonium chloride administration (d1, d2, and d3) and 1 day after the last administration (d4). A container was attached under the vulva to gather urine. The container was immediately removed after urination for pH measurement using the UltraBasic pH Meter UB-5 (Denver Instrument, Goettingen, Germany). Goats with a urine pH before treatment (d1) below 6.5 were not included in that experimental period.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to describe sample characteristics. Survival analysis was used for further analyses. The event of interest was a urine pH of less than 6.5. The Kaplan-Meier survival curve was conducted to analyze survival time. The Cox proportional hazards regression was used to determine the effect of the three variables of interest (dosage of ammonium chloride, period of administration, and pretreatment urine pH) on the survival time. The Efron method and robust variances estimation were used to deal with ties and clustering, respectively. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The Bonferroni correction was used to adjust for multiple comparisons. All statistical analyses were performed using the Stata version 14.0 software (StataCorp LLC; College Station, TX, USA).

The results of the Cox proportional hazards regression demonstrated that dosage of ammonium chloride (p < 0.001), period of administration (p < 0.001), and pretreatment urine pH (p = 0.044) were associated with the success rate in reducing the urine pH below 6.5. The success rates of goats receiving 350 mg/kg and 450 mg/kg were greater than those of goats receiving 250 mg/kg. There was no significant difference in the success rate of goats receiving 350 mg/kg and 450 mg/kg (p = 0.054). The second and third periods of ammonium chloride administration provided higher success rates than the first period. There was no significant difference in the success rates between the second and third periods (p = 0.981). In addition, goats having a lower pretreatment urine pH achieved a higher success rate (Table 3).

Discussion

The calcium-to-phosphorus ratio of the feeds used during the study was appropriate to prevent urolith formation. The overall mean pretreatment urine pH was 8.18, which is within the normal range of 7.5–8.5 (Parrah et al., 2013). Factors affecting the success rate of reducing the urine pH were the dosage of ammonium chloride, the period of ammonium chloride administration, and pretreatment urine pH. Goats receiving 350 mg/kg and 450 mg/kg of ammonium chloride were more likely to have their urine pH drop below 6.5 at a time than goats receiving 250 mg/kg by 99.60% and 219.14%, respectively. There was no significant difference in the success rate of goats receiving 350 mg/kg and 450 mg/kg. Therefore, either 350 or 450 mg/kg of ammonium chloride could be used to acidify urine in goats. These dosages are close to the 400–500 mg/kg recommended elsewhere (Mavangira et al., 2010; Ferreira et al., 2014; Navarro et al., 2021). However, a dosage of 500 mg/kg was reported to increase the risk of urate/uric acid crystal formation compared to a lower dosage (200 mg/kg) (Navarro et al., 2022). Therefore, the dosage of 350 mg/kg should be used for economic purposes and to avoid crystal formation complications caused by acidic urine.

The success rates of goats receiving ammonium chloride in the second and third periods were significantly greater than that of goats in the first period (1,083.76% and 860.73%, respectively). However, there was no significant difference in the success rates between the second and third periods. The faster response to ammonium chloride with a repeated dose found in this study was consistent with another study (Mavangira et al., 2010). Based on the pretreatment urine pH of each period, the 7-day washout period in the present study, which followed the protocol from Sprake (2012), was adequate for the pH to rise before the next experimental period began. Another study reported different responses among periods when the pulse dose of ammonium chloride (3 days on/4 days off) was used, with variation possibly due to discrepancies in the feed, temperature, previous dosage of ammonium chloride obtained, and the individual response to ammonium chloride in each goat (Sprake, 2012). Long-term use of ammonium chloride did not consistently reduce the urine pH, perhaps due to physiological adaptation by the goats (Stratton-Phelps and House, 2004; Sprake, 2012). Additionally, prolonged use can lead to acidosis that can impair feed intake, growth, and bone strength (Jones et al., 2009), as well as promote the formation of some urine crystals due to increased urine acidity (Navarro et al., 2022). Therefore, periodic use may be preferable to avoid such side effects, while achieving an optimal response. The better response in the second and third periods of the present study provided additional evidence supporting periodic use. Unfortunately, the original objective of the present study did not focus on periodic use; thus, the dosage used in each period was varied. Further study is required to clarify the appropriate dosage for periodic use.

In the present study, the pretreatment urine pH before ammonium chloride administration was another significant determinant of the success rate. For every additional unit of pretreatment urine pH, the probability of success in reducing the urine pH below 6.5 during a unit of time fell by 40.5%. The lower the pretreatment urine pH, the higher the success rate. Thus, measuring the pretreatment urine pH was necessary for predicting the treatment outcome.

In conclusion, the appropriate dosage of oral force-feeding ammonium chloride to acidify urine in goats was 350 mg/kg. Periodic use of ammonium chloride provided a higher success rate. The pretreatment urine pH should be determined to anticipate clinical response.
