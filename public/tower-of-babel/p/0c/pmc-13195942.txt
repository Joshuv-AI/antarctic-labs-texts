Immune and stromal signaling networks in chordoma: an evidence-weighted review of ligand–receptor interactions and therapeutic implications
Authors: Panpan Hu, Juncai Lei, Suiyong Du, Shengxin Zeng, Peilin Hua, Hua Zhou, Feng Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis scoping review aimed to systematically map reported ligand–receptor (L–R) interactions in chordoma, classify the strength of supporting evidence, and integrate mechanistic, spatial, and clinical findings to inform translational prioritization.MethodsA systematic search of PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science (2000–2024) identified original studies reporting L–R signaling in chordoma. Data on experimental models, signaling axes, functional assays, and clinical correlations were extracted using a predefined template. PD-1/PD-L1 expression showed consistent prognostic correlations but lacked mechanistic validation.ConclusionThis review provides the first evidence-weighted map of intercellular signaling in chordoma. Background

Chordoma is a rare, locally aggressive bone tumor arising from notochordal remnants and characterized by high rates of local recurrence and limited systemic involvement [1–3]. Over the past decade, research has shifted from a tumor-cell–centric view to a tumor-microenvironment (TME) perspective that recognizes stromal and immune crosstalk as key determinants of progression and therapy resistance [4–6]. Recent comprehensive reviews emphasize the complexity of the chordoma immune milieu and the need to map actionable communication axes across stromal, immune, endothelial, and tumor compartments [4, 7].

Single-cell and spatial technologies have begun to delineate the cellular architecture of chordoma TME with unprecedented resolution. Integrative scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics consistently identify heterogeneous cancer-associated fibroblast (CAF) subpopulations, including an endoplasmic-reticulum-stress (ERS)–related CAF program that associates with adverse biology and putative paracrine signaling toward tumor and immune cells [8, 9]. These datasets provide testable ligand–receptor (L–R) hypotheses rather than purely descriptive catalogs.

Emerging preclinical evidence supports several recurrent stromal–immune–tumor signaling axes. IL-6/STAT3 activation appears operative in subsets of chordoma, linking IL-6–rich CAF or tumor niches to downstream STAT3 signaling in adjacent tumor and immune cells, while its pharmacologic blockade suppresses tumor growth in model systems [4, 10–12]. Classical receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) pathways—particularly platelet-derived growth factor/receptor (PDGF/PDGFR) interactions—remain relevant through stromal–tumor crosstalk, consistent with limited disease stabilization observed in small imatinib trials [13, 14]. Single-cell and spatial analyses also highlight transforming growth factor (TGF)-β programs at tumor–stroma interfaces, linked to epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT)-like transitions and potential radioresistance [15–17]. Recent data delineate distinct CAF states, including inflammatory and ER-stress–related subtypes, which may rewire L–R networks under hypoxic or stress conditions [5, 8, 9].

Despite these advances, prior syntheses have not systematically integrated discovery-level L–R inferences (single-cell/spatial) with protein-level confirmation and functional validation, nor aligned them with the limited clinical signals seen in small phase II studies of RTK inhibition (e.g., imatinib for PDGFR, lapatinib for EGFR) [13, 18]. This gap motivates an evidence-weighted map of reported L–R axes spanning tumor, stromal, endothelial, and immune compartments, to guide translational prioritization.

Objective of this review

Building on these developments, we conducted a scoping review of reported L–R axes in chordoma, integrating evidence from single-cell/spatial inference, multiplex/protein assays, co-culture and patient-derived organoid/patient-derived xenograft (PDO/PDX)-based functional studies, and summarizing clinical correlates where available. This framework aims to define an evidence-weighted interaction map to guide translational strategies and rational combinations in chordoma.

Methods

Protocol and reporting guideline

This review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist [19, 20]. The review protocol was prospectively developed based on the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Manual for Evidence Synthesis and followed the PCC framework (Population, Concept, Context) for defining eligibility criteria [21].

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed in PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science (WoS) from January 2000 to December 2024. The search combined controlled vocabulary (MeSH/Emtree) and free-text terms related to chordoma and L-R interactions.

The following search string was applied consistently across all three databases:

Search terms

Chordoma AND (ligand OR receptor OR paracrine OR CAF OR microenvironment OR single-cell OR spatial OR CellChat OR interactome).

Detailed database-specific adaptations of this query syntax (e.g., MeSH/Emtree mapping) are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Additionally, reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were manually screened to identify further eligible articles.

Results

Study selection and general characteristics

A total of 30 reports published between 2006 and 2024 met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1; Supplementary Table S2). These reports collectively represent a cross-section of translational, preclinical, and computational research investigating L–R interactions in chordoma.

Among the included studies, fifteen were conducted in Asian centers (mainly from China and Japan), eight in Europe, and seven in North America (USA and Canada), reflecting a broad but regionally concentrated international distribution.

Overview of reported ligand–receptor axes

Across the 30 studies, about ten L–R pairs were identified (Supplementary Table S2, Table 1), spanning recurrent paracrine and autocrine signaling circuits. The most frequently investigated receptor families included receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs: EGFR, PDGFR, c-MET, VEGFR) and cytokine receptors (TGFβR, IL-6R, IFNγR), with additional data on immune checkpoints (PD-1/PD-L1) and integrin-mediated signaling (Fig. 2).

Evidence convergence and translational correlates

Across studies, EGF/EGFR and PDGF/PDGFR achieved the strongest multi-modal support (levels A–B) and have been clinically targeted with limited but measurable benefit.

VEGF/VEGFR and TGFβ/TGFβR were recurrently confirmed at the protein and transcriptomic levels, suggesting roles in angiogenesis and immune evasion.

IL-6/IL-6R/STAT3 signaling has recently emerged as a level A axis driving M2 polarization and osteolytic progression, with inhibitor studies showing functional reversal of tumor invasion.

PD-L1 expression consistently correlated with adverse outcomes, whereas targeted inhibition of EGFR or STAT3 produced disease stabilization or durable responses in selected cases.

Discussion

Overview of the evidence landscape

This scoping synthesis systematically mapped 30 reports investigating L–R interactions within the chordoma TME. The aggregated evidence reveals a narrow yet recurrent spectrum of intercellular signaling axes dominated by RTKs—particularly EGFR, PDGFR, VEGFR, and MET—and supplemented by stromal and immune pathways such as TGF-β/TGFβR, PD-1/PD-L1, and the recently defined IER2–GMFG–ITGB1 fibroblast–tumor circuit.

Quantitatively, 8 studies (26.7%) reached level A evidence, 19 (63.3%) level B, and 3 (10.0%) level C, underscoring that most data remain at the descriptive or correlative stage, with limited causal validation. Among these, the EGF → EGFR and PDGF → PDGFR axes represent the most mature signaling frameworks, validated across histologic, molecular, and clinical contexts.

Conversely, newer stromal and immune-related pathways—such as TGF-β/TGFβR and IER2–GMFG–ITGB1—are emerging as potential paracrine drivers of resistance and progression, supported by early functional assays but lacking clinical translation.

Together, these findings indicate that chordoma progression depends not only on intrinsic oncogenic signaling but also on reciprocal crosstalk among tumor, fibroblast, endothelial, and immune compartments.

Mechanistic implications for tumor–microenvironment crosstalk

The predominance of RTK-based signaling mirrors the developmental lineage of chordoma, where notochordal remnants retain high expression of growth factor receptors.

EGF/EGFR axis: a central oncogenic driver

EGFR activation, observed in up to 80% of chordoma cases, has been validated through ligand stimulation, pharmacologic inhibition, and phase II clinical testing, supporting its role as a bona fide autocrine loop. Multiple cohorts have confirmed receptor overexpression and phosphorylation [22–26, 28, 45]. Functional inhibition using erlotinib analogues and 4-anilinoquinazoline EGFR inhibitors effectively suppressed chordoma cell proliferation in vitro [24, 28, 45] and in xenograft models [26].

Translational and medicinal chemistry studies consistently demonstrate that the EGFR axis remains active in chordoma, with reproducible yet modest clinical signals. A phase II trial of lapatinib in EGFR/HER2-positive chordoma achieved disease stabilization in approximately 40% of patients [18], providing limited but direct clinical evidence of target engagement. More recently, a 2024 European multicenter phase II trial of afatinib reported durable benefit in a subset of patients, underscoring the need for predictive biomarkers to distinguish responders based on EGFR expression, activation state, spatial localization, and downstream signaling context [46].

Integrating these findings with L-R interaction evidence from this review—demonstrating that EGFR inhibition impedes proliferation and that EGFR–GAK trafficking is mechanistically coupled to oncogenic signaling—EGFR remains a high-priority, therapeutically actionable target in chordoma.

PDGF/PDGFR axis: the classical RTK loop

PDGFR signaling likewise recurs across early translational studies [30, 31, 34] and contributes to therapeutic resistance, particularly in tumors with PTEN loss or downstream PI3K/AKT activation. Tamborini et al. [30] demonstrated PDGFR-β autocrine activation and responsiveness to imatinib. Subsequent immunohistochemical series from Akhavan-Sigari et al. [32, 33] and Zhai et al. [18] confirmed high PDGFR-α/β expression and ligand co-localization in patient samples. Functional studies showed that PDGFR blockade reduced proliferation and angiogenic activity in cell and xenograft models, supporting a causal role (level A) [13, 34].

As a classical RTK circuit, PDGF/PDGFR exhibits the strongest biological and clinical consistency among the evaluated pathways. Imatinib-based trials achieved partial disease stabilization in subsets of advanced cases, aligning molecular relevance with modest therapeutic efficacy [13, 47]. These findings reaffirm PDGFR as a canonical oncogenic driver and stromal mediator of angiogenesis. However, the limited and transient clinical benefit underscores the complexity of PDGF signaling and the redundancy inherent to RTK-dependent paracrine networks. Rational integration of PDGFR inhibition with CAF- or endothelium-directed strategies may enhance therapeutic outcomes.

TGF-β/SMAD axis: EMT and immune modulation

TGF-β/SMAD signaling was detected across spatial and protein datasets, especially at tumor–stroma interfaces [10, 36, 39, 40]. Experimental activation induced EMT-like morphologies, whereas inhibition enhanced immune infiltration in xenograft models, positioning this axis between evidence Levels B and A. As in other solid tumors, context dependency remains critical: prolonged or systemic TGF-β blockade may paradoxically increase tumor aggressiveness.

Spatial and proteomic evidence links this pathway to EMT induction and immune exclusion within the chordoma microenvironment. Early clinical approaches include TGF-β–sequestering oncolytic adenoviruses (AdAPT-001), which have shown acceptable safety and biological activity in refractory solid tumors, with reported chordoma cases suggesting potential synergy with radiotherapy and immunotherapy [48, 49]. Moreover, the expanding human pharmacodynamic data from selective TGFβR1 inhibitors in advanced solid tumors further supports their rational extrapolation and combination potential in chordoma treatment [15].

IL-6/STAT3 axis: a convergent stromal–immune–tumor circuit

Evidence from independent multi-omic and preclinical studies suggests that IL-6/STAT3 signaling constitutes a key paracrine circuit connecting stromal, immune, and tumor compartments in chordoma. Recent single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses have refined this model: Zhang et al. identified endoplasmic-reticulum-stress–related CAFs (ERS-CAFs) that secrete IL-6 through the IER2/GMFG/ITGB1 cascade, thereby activating STAT3 in adjacent tumor cells and driving malignant progression [8]. Functional evidence further corroborates causation. PDO–CAF co-culture and xenograft blockade experiments confirmed that IL-6/STAT3 activation enhances tumor growth, invasion, and therapy resistance, providing level A functional validation [12]. Nevertheless, most evidence derives from small, single-center datasets, and pathway activation may vary by genomic background (e.g., 9p/10q alterations) or hypoxia-induced CAF programs [8, 50–52].

Pharmacologic inhibition of STAT3 (e.g., SD-1029, curcumin analogue FLLL32) reduced proliferation and malignant phenotypes in chordoma cell lines [10, 35], reinforcing the pivotal role of this signaling cascade. Clinically, elevated IL-6 and STAT3 activity correlate with recurrence and shorter progression-free survival across multiple solid tumors, including breast, nasopharyngeal, and pancreatic cancers [53–56]. However, corresponding clinical data in chordoma remain limited. Collectively, IL-6/STAT3 represents a biologically robust but clinically under-validated stromal–immune–tumor axis. Its reproducible functional evidence across models prioritizes it for translational testing, particularly within JAK/STAT-targeted or CAF-modulating combination strategies for chordoma.

PD-1/PD-L1: current evidence and investigational status

From an immunologic standpoint, PD-1/PD-L1 expression constitutes the most reproducible immune checkpoint finding across independent cohorts. Prognostically, PD-L1 protein is commonly expressed in chordoma and correlates with worse outcomes; IFN-γ can induce PD-L1 in U-CH1/U-CH2, supporting adaptive immune resistance biology [41, 42]. However, functional blockade or co-culture validation is lacking, and clinical responses to PD-1 inhibitors remain anecdotal. In chordoma specifically, evidence consists mainly of small series and case reports: early signals include partial responses or disease control with anti-PD-1 therapy (nivolumab/pembrolizumab), including a PBRM1-mutant responder and a pediatric SMARCB1-deficient case; a small single-arm experience with pembrolizumab reported low objective response but frequent stable disease [49, 57, 58]. Thus, PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors in sarcomas appear to benefit more in combinations (e.g., with TKIs or CTLA-4 blockade) [59–61].

Moreover, TGF-β–rich, CAF-dominated microenvironments foster immune exclusion and blunt PD-(L)1 responses across several carcinomas; overcoming primary resistance may require co-targeting TGF-β pathways, a concept with growing preclinical support and translational interest [62–64]. Overall, PD-(L)1 therapy in chordoma remains investigational [41, 42, 59].

Other and emerging axes

VEGF–VEGFR signaling, frequently detected in tissue microarrays and immunoprofiling cohorts, likely mediates angiogenic adaptation in the avascular chordoma milieu [36–38]. However, few studies employed functional inhibition, and no anti-angiogenic trials have yet demonstrated efficacy, highlighting an underexplored therapeutic avenue.

IER2–GMFG–ITGB1 paracrine program described by Zhang et al. [8, 9] expands the concept of CAF heterogeneity, defining an ERS-CAF subset capable of modulating tumor adhesion and invasion via integrin signaling. Such mechanistically validated stromal axes may serve as prototypes for integrating fibroblast biology into chordoma therapeutics, paralleling similar advances in pancreatic and colorectal cancers [5, 50–56].

Limitations of the current evidence

Despite emerging mechanistic insights, several limitations temper the strength of existing evidence. First, more than half of the available studies rely solely on IHC or in vitro expression assays, without direct demonstration of L-R binding, signaling dependency, or intercellular directionality. Second, sample sizes remain small and geographically clustered, with limited multi-institutional validation or cross-platform reproducibility. Third, functional causality—as defined by ligand blockade, receptor inhibition, or rescue experiments—is demonstrated in only a minority of reports. Even the most advanced datasets, such as those employing single-cell or spatial transcriptomics, often stop short of confirming paracrine causality experimentally. Fourth, translational studies rarely integrate molecular signaling with clinical outcomes beyond correlative associations. Only a few (e.g., Stacchiotti 2012 and 2013[13, 18]) provide interventional evidence linking pathway modulation to therapeutic response.

An additional conceptual limitation is the fragmented scope of the literature. Most studies interrogate isolated axes (e.g., EGFR or PDGFR) rather than systematically mapping the full interactome. As a result, potential compensatory loops or cross-axis redundancies—such as parallel activation of TGFβR and PDGFR under stress—remain poorly characterized. This gap underscores the need for integrative network-level approaches capable of linking multi-omic discovery to functional prioritization. Furthermore, a formal risk-of-bias assessment was not performed, as this review was designed as a scoping synthesis; however, methodological quality was partially reflected in the evidence grading system, which prioritized studies with functional and multi-modal validation.

Future directions and translational outlook

Future work should pursue three convergent priorities. First, systematic application of multi-modal single-cell and spatial profiling across larger patient cohorts could refine cellular source–target relationships and reveal spatial hierarchies of signaling dominance. Second, functional validation pipelines—combining PDOs, CAF–tumor co-cultures, and PDXs—should be used to test causality of predicted axes under defined perturbations (e.g., ligand neutralization or receptor inhibition). Third, translation to the clinic will require rational combination strategies, pairing RTK inhibitors (EGFR/PDGFR) with modulators of stromal or immune circuits (e.g., TGFβR or PD-L1 blockade). Given the redundancy of L–R networks, such combinatorial approaches are more likely to disrupt feedback loops than monotherapies.

Beyond drug targeting, mapping the functional architecture of the chordoma interactome could serve as a biomarker resource. For example, ERS-CAF–dominant tumors may exhibit distinct immune exclusion or treatment resistance phenotypes, guiding stratified therapeutic design.

Conclusion

This review establishes the first evidence-weighted map of L-R communication in chordoma. While the RTK-based EGFR and PDGFR axes remain the best-characterized, emerging fibroblast and immune circuits highlight the broader relevance of the tumor microenvironment. The predominance of level B evidence indicates that the field is transitioning from descriptive to functional investigation. Consolidating multi-omic inference with systematic validation and clinical correlation will be critical for transforming these signaling axes from biological observations into actionable therapeutic targets.
