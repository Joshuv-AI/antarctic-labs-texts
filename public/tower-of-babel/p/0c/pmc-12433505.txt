AIE-driven photothermal nanotrigger-activated multifunctional injectable hydrogel for maxillofacial infection control
Authors: Xiangxiang Huang, Xiaolong Zhu, Donglin Gan, Yuewen Yu, Xuefeng Jiang, Mingqian Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The treatment of maxillofacial infections and wounds remains highly challenging due to the risk of facial deformities and the resulting psychological trauma. Furthermore, the irregular geometry and complex anatomical structures of maxillofacial wounds hinder effective clinical intervention. Therefore, the development of controllable and multifunctional composite hydrogels is highly desirable for promoting tissue regeneration, particularly in cases involving irregularly shaped tissue defects. In this study, we developed AIE-induced photothermal nanotrigger strategy for fabricating injectable multifunctional hydrogel zwitterionic wound dressing. This antibacterial composite zwitterionic hydrogel (TSD NPs@Gel) was synthesized using sulfobetaine methacrylate (SBMA) as the monomer, N,N′-methylenebisacrylamide (MBAA) as the crosslinker, and ammonium persulfate (APS) as the initiator. By incorporating aggregation-induced emission (AIE) nanoparticles (TSD NPs), we leveraged their excellent photothermal properties to achieve a "photothermal self-catalysis (PSC)" effect. Under light irradiation, TSD NPs generated heat, which accelerated the decomposition of APS into free radicals, thereby promoting the polymerization between the monomer and crosslinker. This innovative approach eliminated the need for traditional chemical catalysts and enabled the efficient preparation of the hydrogel. This hydrogel exhibits synergistic antibacterial effects through photothermal (PTT) and photodynamic (PDT) therapies. Under 660 nm laser irradiation, TSD NPs@Gel efficiently generates PTT and PDT effects, demonstrating significant bactericidal efficacy against both Gram-positive bacteria (Staphylococcus aureus) and Gram-negative bacteria (Escherichia coli). Additionally, mouse wound experiments show that the TSD nanoparticle-loaded hydrogel can effectively adhere to the facial skin of mice. The zwitterionic properties of the hydrogel provide excellent moisturizing and immunomodulatory effects, promoting blood vessel regeneration and new tissue growth, thereby demonstrating superior healing capabilities in mouse models of maxillofacial infections and traumas.

Introduction

Maxillofacial trauma is one of the major health problems worldwide [1]. Severe oral and maxillofacial injuries caused by trauma or surgery can lead to poor healing, such as delayed wound closure and scar formation. Wound healing in the oral and maxillofacial region affects the quality of life and mental health of patients [2]. Since the oral and maxillofacial regions are involved in many daily functional activities, such as speaking, chewing, and swallowing, they are greatly affected during the process of wound healing [3]. Moreover, wound healing is a complex and slow process, and there is a risk of infection during healing, which hinders the speed of the process [4,5]. Therefore, effective treatment options are needed to promote wound healing. However, the effectiveness of traditional treatments is limited.

In the past few decades, the main means of treating bacteria-infected wounds has been the use of antibiotics [6]. However, due to the heavy use of antibiotics, many bacteria have developed resistance [7,8]. Therefore, many researchers have developed a series of new antimicrobial methods, such as the use of metal nanomaterials [9,10], photothermal therapy (PTT) [11,12], chemodynamic therapy (CDT) [13,14], sonodynamic therapy (SDT) [15,16], and photodynamic therapy (PDT) [17,18]. Among these methods, PDT is widely used in the field of antibacterial agents due to its high spatial-temporal selectivity, low side effects, low toxicity, and remarkable therapeutic effect [19]. PDT mainly uses photosensitizers (PSs) to stimulate specific light sources to produce reactive oxygen species (ROS), which damages the structure of bacteria and lead to bacterial death [20]. Under normal circumstances, PSs under light irradiation will produce type I free radicals, such as superoxide anion (·O2−) and hydroxyl radical (·OH), or type II ROS, such as singlet oxygen (1O2) [21,22]. Among them, type I ROS has low oxygen dependence, so it has high therapeutic performance in an anoxic environment. However, most PSs reported at present are prone to π-π interactions at high concentrations or aggregation, leading to fluorescence quenching (ACQ), which greatly reduces their efficiency [23,24]. PSs with aggregation-induced emission (AIE) effect will emit bright fluorescence and higher ROS production efficiency in the aggregation state due to the effect of intramolecular motility restriction (RIM) [25]. Therefore, the rational design and development of AIE can greatly promote the development of PDT in wound treatment and practical applications. However, most of the AIEgens reported to date are related to type II ROS [26]; thus, the development of AIEgens with efficient type I ROS production is of great significance.

The ideal drug treatment requires maintaining the drug in the wound area for a long time and releasing the drug as needed [27]. In recent years, hydrogels, as important biological materials, have been widely used in tissue engineering [28,29], drug delivery [30,31], wound dressing, [32,33], and so on. Among these hydrogels, Makvandi et al. [34] designed an artificial tissue model based on gel and silicone, with the hydrogel layer (simulating the active epidermis and dermis) composed of sodium chloride (NaCl), gelatin (as protein), and arabic gum (as polysaccharide). This model can serve as an in vitro alternative model for evaluating micro-needle penetration capability, drug diffusion, photothermal activity, and ultrasound imaging, offering potential for reducing animal testing and addressing ethical concerns. In their article focusing on the role of miRNAs in skin and oral wound healing and delivery systems, Deka Dey et al. [35] also mentioned that hyaluronic acid-gelatin hydrogels loaded with miRNA nanoparticles can locally release miRNAs, promoting anti-inflammatory and angiogenesis effects in diabetic wounds. Alinezhad et al. [36] incorporated poly dopamine (PDA) nanoparticles into a system crosslinked with Zn2+, consisting of hyaluronic acid (HA) and alginate (Alg). They prepared a multifunctional near-infrared (NIR) active hydrogel named Alg–HA–Zn–PDA, which exhibits high antibacterial, antioxidant, and healing properties, excellent biocompatibility, and good hemostatic performance. It is considered an efficient dressing material for wound healing applications. Among these hydrogels, zwitterionic polymethacrylate sulfobetaine (PSBMA) materials have superior hydrophilicity and ultralow biological pollution, as well as excellent biocompatibility and mechanical properties, and have been widely used in the synthesis of multifunctional hydrogel dressings [37,38].

The AIE molecule TSD here uses aniline (TPA) as an electron donor, relying on its non-planar structure and strong electron-donating ability. Multiple heteroatoms (3-(dicyanomethylene)-2,3-dihydrobenzothiophene-1, 1-dioxide (DTM) as strong acceptors, while the thiophene moiety further serves as a supplementary donor and π-bridge. This facilitates the separation of the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO), reduces valence electron repulsion, and enhances radiative decay and intersystem crossing (ISC) processes, thereby increasing fluorescence intensity and promoting reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation. The specific molecular synthesis steps are based on previous research [39]. Here, we propose the concept of photothermal self-catalysis (PSC) enabled by AIE nanomaterials. Traditionally, the gelation of sulfobetaine methacrylate (SBMA) in systems utilizing MBAA as the cross-linker and APS as the initiator required tetramethylethylenediamine (TEMED) as a catalyst. In our PSC strategy, the application of AIE photothermal materials has brought about a new revolution, successfully replacing traditional catalysts and innovating the hydrogel preparation process with their unique PSC properties. AIE photothermal materials can absorb light energy at specific wavelengths and efficiently convert it into heat, which accelerates the decomposition of APS to generate free radicals and significantly increases the polymerization rate between monomers and cross-linkers, thereby driving the gelation process. In this process, AIE photothermal materials do not need to rely on external chemical catalysts, and can self-sufficiently drive the gelation reaction with the energy generated by their own photothermal conversion, fully demonstrating the PSC characteristics. This not only streamlines the gel preparation process and avoids the risk of chemical catalyst residues, but also provides innovative ideas for the green synthesis and precise regulation of smart hydrogels. Under 660 nm laser irradiation, TSD NPs@Gel is able to produce photothermal therapy (PTT) and photodynamic therapy (PDT) effects, which work in conjunction to effectively kill bacteria infecting the wound. At the same time, the amphoteric PSBMA material itself promotes wound healing. The physicochemical properties, antimicrobial properties and biocompatibility of the TSD NPs@Gel were evaluated through a series of experiments. These studies showed that TSD NPs@Gel have great potential for the treatment of infected wounds.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of TSD NPs@Gel

The preparation scheme of TSD NPs is shown in Fig. 1A. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and dynamic light scattering (DLS) were used to analyze the morphology and size of TSD NPs (Fig. 2A and B). The average particle size of the prepared TSD NPs was approximately 70 nm, and the zeta potential value was measured to be −5.9 mV (Fig. S1). It can be seen from the UV–vis spectra that TSD NPs have an obvious absorption peak at approximately 660 nm (Fig. 2C), indicating that TSD NPs have great potential in the field of PDT. Subsequently, the TSD NPs were mixed with the hydrogel precursor solution and irradiated using a 660 nm laser, which accelerated the decomposition of the APS by the photothermal properties of the TSD NPs and contributed to the formation of the hydrogel by the cross-linking agent MBAA (Fig. 2D). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images (Fig. 2E) showed that the surface of the blank hydrogel was smooth, while the surface of the TSD NP hydrogel had subtle bumps and was uniformly dispersed, which proves that TSD NPs were successfully loaded onto the hydrogel. Furthermore, the energy dispersion profile of elements shows that there is no P element in the blank hydrogel. Mechanical properties of TSD NPs@Gel hydrogels

To achieve better therapeutic effects, hydrogels must have improved mechanical properties, adhesion properties, and fatigue resistance [40]. Excellent hydrogel wound dressings can adhere to human tissues spontaneously and will not fall off randomly with the daily activities of the human body, thus forming a good wound-healing environment [41]. As shown in Fig. 3A, the tensile strain of the blank hydrogel can reach 539 %, while the tensile strain of TSD NPs@Gel reaches 490 %, which shows that both the blank hydrogel and the nanoparticle-loaded hydrogel have good tensile properties. Meanwhile, the tensile moduli of the two hydrogels were 19.78 (±1.76) and 22.56 (±2.12) kPa, respectively, and the lower modulus hydrogel was more able to match the skin tissue elasticity (Fig. 3B). In addition, the hydrogel adhesion properties were tested using pig skin, and the results showed that the adhesion strengths of the two types of hydrogels reached 21.5 (±1.85) and 19.3 (±2.72) kPa, respectively, proving that the addition of TSD NPs did not have a large effect on the adhesion strengths of hydrogels (Fig. 3C). Moreover, after 1000 tensile and torsion fatigue tests on TSD NPs@Gel, it was found that the properties of TSD NPs@Gel had no obvious changes after long-term stretching and torsion (Fig. S2), which shows that TSD NPs@Gel had good stability and were more beneficial to wound healing in mice. Finally, we conducted in situ tensile experiments on TSD NPs@Gel and obtained a force cloud diagram of the gel during the stretching process (Fig. S3). Photodynamic analysis of TSD NPs@Gel

Electron spin resonance (ESR) was used to investigate the photodynamic properties of TSD NPs@Gel. We investigated the ability of TSD NPs and TSD NPs@Gel to produce superoxide anion (·O2−), hydroxyl radical (·OH), and singlet oxygen (1O2) under PBS, non-laser irradiation, and 660 nm laser irradiation. First, in TSD NPs, it can be observed that TSD NPs under 660 nm laser irradiation produce three ROS signals of ·O2−, ·OH, and 1O2 compared to PBS and non-laser irradiation (Fig. S4). Moreover, we were surprised to find that the signals of the three ROS, ·O2−, ·OH, and 1O2, were stronger in TSD NPs@Gel (Fig. 3D, E and F). This result may be related to the mechanism of AIE NPs. The dense structure inside the hydrogel makes AIE NPs more easily aggregate, thus enhancing the emission performance of AIE NPs [42,43].

Then, the ability of TSD NPs@Gel to produce the three ROS was further explored using ultraviolet absorption spectroscopy. First, dihydrorhodamine (DHR 123) was used as an indicator to detect the formation of ·O2−. As shown in Fig. 3G, under irradiation with a 660 nm laser at 0.4 W/cm2, the fluorescence of DHR 123 at 525 nm gradually increased with increasing irradiation time, indicating that the concentration of ·O2− increased. Next, 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethyl benzidine (TMB) was used as an indicator to detect the production of ·OH (Fig. 3H). With increasing irradiation time, the characteristic absorption peaks of TMB at 370 nm and 650 nm gradually increased. It was proven that TSD NPs@Gel can produce ·OH under 660 nm laser irradiation. Finally, 1O2 was detected using 1,3-diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF), as shown in Fig. 3I. With increasing irradiation time, the absorbance of DPBF at 410 nm gradually decreased. The above results indicate that TSD NPs@Gel has a high ROS generation capacity under laser irradiation, especially for more toxic ·O2− and ·OH.

Photothermal analysis of TSD NPs@Gel

To evaluate the photothermal properties of TSD NPs@Gel, a 660 nm laser was used to irradiate the TSD NP solution and TSD NPs@Gel solution at different concentrations at 0.4 W/cm2. The temperature rise curve of the TSD NP solution is shown in Fig. 4A. Except for the TSD NPs solution of 0 μg/mL, the solution of other concentrations has an obvious temperature rise, and the temperature change is not different, indicating that TSD NPs have a stable temperature rise effect. Among the concentrations, 100 μg/mL TSD NPs had the best temperature increase effect. The temperature rose from 28.8 °C to 55.4 °C. It can also be seen from infrared photothermal imaging that TSD NPs with different concentrations have a significant temperature increase (Fig. 4B). We also further explored the photothermal stability of TSD NPs. After several cycles of irradiation/cooling, the temperature change of the TSD NPs solution within 10 min did not show much difference (Fig. 4C), indicating that TSD NPs have excellent photothermal stability. In contrast, the temperature rise of TSD NPs@Gel solutions with different concentrations was quite different (Fig. 4D), which may be because TSD NPs coated by hydrogel can aggregate more easily with increasing concentration, thus improving the photothermal properties. Similarly, the infrared photothermal images of TSD NPs@Gel show an obvious temperature increase (Fig. 4E), and there was no significant difference in temperature in the cyclic irradiation/cooling images (Fig. 4F), indicating that TSD NPs did not weaken their PTT properties after being incorporated into the gel. In contrast, at the same concentration (100 μg/mL), the photothermal properties of the gel loaded with TSD NPs were superior to the pure TSD NPs (Fig. 4G). This result is consistent with the previously mentioned enhancement of the ESR signal in hydrogels. Furthermore, through the photothermal change in one cycle, it was calculated that the photothermal conversion efficiency of TSD NPs@Gel reached 53.52 % (Fig. 4H), which indicates that the PSBMA hydrogel can improve the PTT performance of TSD NPs without affecting the photothermal stability. Based on the good PTT properties of TSD NPs, we tested the effect of light irradiation on the hydrogel gel formation time using a rheometer. 660 nm laser was used to irradiate TSD NPs@Gel without irradiation and irradiated for 10 min, respectively, and then the storage modulus and loss modulus of the hydrogel were tested using the rheometer. The results are shown in Fig. 4I, after irradiation, the TSD NPs@Gel completed the sol-gel transition at 23 min, whereas the unirradiated group reached this transition at 58 min (Fig. S5). Biocompatibility and bacterial intracellular ROS production capacity

After it was confirmed that TSD NPs@Gel has good photothermal properties and ROS generation ability, the biocompatibility and the ability to produce ROS in bacterial cells were further studied. We verified the biocompatibility of TSD NPs@Gel. Mouse fibroblasts (L929) and macrophages were incubated with TSD NPs@Gel and stained with calcein AM. It was observed that both L929 cells and macrophages had good activity in different concentrations of TSD NPs@Gel (Fig. S6). These results show that TSD NPs@Gel has good biocompatibility.

To prove the ability of TSD NPs@Gel to produce ROS in bacteria, 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) was used as an indicator of ROS. S. aureus and E. coli were cocultured with PBS, TSD NPs@Gel, TSD NPs@Gel + 0.2 W/cm2 and TSD NPs@Gel + 0.4 W/cm2. Then, no irradiation or irradiation with a 660 nm laser for 10 min and then fluorescence staining with ROS probe DCFH-DA was used to evaluate the ROS generation capacity within bacteria. As shown in Fig. 5A, after irradiation with 660 nm laser power of 0.2 W/cm2 and 0.4 W/cm2, the generation of ROS was observed; it emitted a distinct green fluorescence, while no significant fluorescence was found in the control group, and the TSD NPs@Gel group, indicating that TSD NPs@Gel enables the significant ROS production capability under 660 nm laser irradiation.Fig. 5(A) ROS staining analysis was performed on E. coli and S. aureus after different treatments. Antibacterial effect in vitro

The treated bacteria were evaluated by the CFU plate colony counting method. A large number of colonies could be observed in the control group and the single TSD NP group, and a significant decrease in the number of colonies could be found in the 0.2 W/cm2 and 0.4 W/cm2 laser irradiation groups (Fig. 5B), the antibacterial rates of E. coli and S. aureus were 94.38 % and 92.4 %, respectively (Fig. S7). Propyridine iodide (PI) was used to stain the bacteria. Significant red fluorescence was also found in the laser irradiation group, and the results showed that TSD NPs@Gel had an excellent bactericidal effect (Fig. 5C). SEM and TEM were used to evaluate the morphological changes before and after S. aureus and E. coli treatment. As shown in Fig. 5D, the morphology of bacteria remained intact without laser irradiation; In contrast, bacteria irradiated by TSD NPs@Gel after laser irradiation produced significant rupture and collapse. In TEM images (Fig. 5E), it can be observed that the internal structure of bacteria in the control group and the TSD NPs@Gel group remained intact, while the internal structure of bacteria in the TSD NPs@Gel group was severely damaged after 660 nm laser irradiation. These results indicate that TSD NPs@Gel combined with laser irradiation can effectively destroy the structure of bacteria.

In addition, the growth of bacteria can form biofilms, which can block the delivery of drugs and are difficult to destroy, so the protection of bacteria by eliminating bacterial biofilms is an important parameter for evaluating biomedical materials [12,44,45]. Here, we evaluated the effects of TSD NPs@Gel on bacterial biofilms. E. coli and S. aureus were cultured for 2 days to form biofilms. After different treatments, the biofilms were stained with a Calcein AM/PI kit (Fig. 5F). The bacterial biofilms of the control group and TSD NPs@Gel group showed strong green fluorescence, indicating the high activity of the bacteria and the integrity of the biofilm. After 660 nm laser irradiation, strong red fluorescence was observed, indicating the destruction of the biofilm. Therefore, TSD NPs@Gel combined with 660 nm laser irradiation can effectively destroy bacterial biofilms and improve the therapeutic effect on infected wounds.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis

To further identify the mechanism of the TSD NPs@Gel + 0.4 W/cm2-mediated antibacterial effect, RNA-seq analysis of S. aureus after treatment was performed to evaluate transcriptional regulation. The Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million (FPKM) mapped reads of genes were analyzed using RSEM software to verify the expression levels (Fig. 6A). The box plot showed that there was a difference in gene expression dispersion between the control (C1-C3) and TSD NPs@Gel + 0.4 W/cm2 treatment samples (G1-G3). Pearson correlation statistics exhibited relatively high correlations on repeated samples; however, R2 values decreased between control and TSD NPs@Gel + 0.4 W/cm2 treatment samples, suggesting the occurrence of different gene expression patterns in S. aureus (Fig. 6B). Then, differential expression was analyzed by using DESeq2 to identify DEGs with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and a fold change ≥2. Volcano blot and histogram analyses showed that 219 DEGs were obtained in S. aureus after TSD NPs@Gel + 0.4 W/cm2 treatment, including 146 upregulated DEGs and 73 downregulated DEGs (Fig. 6C and D). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis suggested that the obtained DEGs were associated with biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions (Fig. 6E). Within the biological process, the main DEGs were related to the metabolic process, cellular process, and localization, especially in nitrogen utilization. The cellular component of DEGs was mainly located in the cell, cell part, membrane, protein-containing complex, membrane part, and organelle. Photothermal effect and wound healing in vivo

To better demonstrate the therapeutic efficacy of TSD NPs@Gel, we established maxillofacial trauma wounds infected with S. aureus to further investigate bacterial inhibition and wound healing ability (Fig. 7A). The mice were then divided into four groups: PBS, TSD NPs@Gel, TSD NPs@Gel + 0.2 W/cm2, and TSD NPs@Gel + 0.4 W/cm2. The temperature changes during the treatment process were recorded with thermal imaging cameras. The wound with added TSD NPs heated up rapidly in a short time after laser irradiation. However, there was no significant change in temperature in the PBS group (Fig. 7B), as seen from the temperature rise curve (Fig. S8). The temperature rise effect of TSD NPs@Gel was higher than that of TSD NPs, which is the same as the photothermal effect. The wound-healing situation is shown in Fig. 7C. After TSD NPs@Gel + laser treatment, the maxillofacial wounds of mice healed rapidly in a short time. The wound healing simulation map (Fig. 7D) and wound area map (Fig. S9) also showed that under laser irradiation (660 nm, 0.4 W/cm2), the wound area was healed by 92.5 %, which indicates that TSD NPs had a very efficient antibacterial and wound healing promotion effect under the synergistic effect of PDT and PTT. Meanwhile, the success rate of anti-infection was confirmed by culturing bacteria sampled from in vivo wounds, and the results showed that bacteria were largely removed from the wounds after treatment with TSD NPs@Gel + 0.4 W/cm2 (Fig. S10). Immunological assay of wound tissue sections

After treatment, the wound tissue was sliced and observed by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson staining to evaluate its healing efficiency, as shown in Fig. 7E and F. After TSD NPs@Gel plus laser treatment, the wound tissue produced a large amount of granulation tissue and presented a complete skin structure. However, the non-laser group still had some inflammation and a few granulations. Moreover, the collagen deposition content in the treatment group was significantly higher than that in the control group and TSD NPs@Gel group (Fig. S12). These results indicate that TSD NPs@Gel can effectively kill infected bacteria by PDT and PTT synergistic treatment under 660 nm laser irradiation, thereby promoting the healing process of infected wounds.

Moreover, macrophages play a crucial role in the overall wound healing process, with M1 macrophages promoting inflammation and M2 macrophages inhibiting inflammation and promoting tissue regeneration [51]. Changes in M1 macrophages and M2 macrophages were observed by staining the wound tissue, as shown in Fig. S13A, which is the marker CD80 of M1 macrophages. Compared with the PBS group and TSD NPs@Gel group, the colors of TSD NPs@Gel + 0.2 W/cm2 and TSD NPs@Gel + 0.4 W/cm2 showed an obvious trend of lightning, indicating a decrease in M1 macrophages. In contrast, Fig. S13B shows the marker CD206 of M2 macrophages, which shows a significant increase; quantitative statistics also confirm this (Fig. S14). Similarly, we evaluated the pro-inflammatory factors TNF-α and IL-6, as well as the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10, in wound tissue, with staining (Fig. S15) and quantitative statistics (Fig. S16) showing significant decreases in TNF-α and IL-6 and significant increases in IL-10. The results indicated that TSD NPs@Gel can inhibit the increase in inflammation and promote wound healing under 660 nm laser irradiation.

Conclusion

In summary, we designed a TSD NP capable of dual PTT and PDT effects. When these nanoparticles were incorporated into the SBMA hydrogel precursor solution, under specific laser (660 nm, 0.4 W/cm2) irradiation, activated the PSC properties of the TSD NPs, which led to the decomposition of the APS and facilitated the polymerization of the TSD NPs@Gel. The TSD NPs@Gel can effectively attach to the wound surface and produce synergistically enhanced PTT and PDT effects, resulting in highly effective antimicrobial therapy. TSD NPs@Gel showed excellent antimicrobial effects in both in vitro and in vivo experiments. The amphiphilic ions in the hydrogel have an immunomodulatory effect, which significantly accelerates the healing of infected wounds in the maxillofacial region when combined with nanoparticles. Thus, the TSD NPs@gel material has great potential in promoting wound healing and offers a new option for the treatment of maxillofacial wound infections.
