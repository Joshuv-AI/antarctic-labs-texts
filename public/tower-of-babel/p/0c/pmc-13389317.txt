An integrative workflow for lncRNA orthology detection and its application to 13 evolutionarily diverse species
Authors: Fabien Degalez, Coralie Allain, Laetitia Lagoutte, Frédéric Lecerf, Sandrine Lagarrigue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLong non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), transcripts longer than 200 nucleotides with limited protein-coding potential, are key regulators of gene expression, yet their evolutionary conservation remains poorly understood due to rapid sequence divergence. We present a flexible workflow for cross-species inference of lncRNA orthology combining two synteny-based approaches with multi-species genome alignment-derived sequence conservation. The workflow relies on standardized genome annotations and one-to-one orthologous protein-coding gene relationships, retrieved here from Ensembl resources. Applied to 13 vertebrate species spanning zebrafish, birds, and mammals, chosen to capture both broad phylogenetic distances and heterogeneous genome annotation quality, and using human (18 859 lncRNAs) as reference, the approach identified on average ∼200 putative orthologs per species under stringent criteria and up to ∼5000 under relaxed criteria. At the multi-species levels, >450 human lncRNAs were conserved in at least two species under stringent conditions, and over 10 000 in at least five species under relaxed criteria. Functional downstream analyses further revealed partial conservation of expression across 17 homologous tissues between human and chicken, as well as conserved short sequence motifs detected with LncLOOM. Together, this study provides both an adaptable workflow and a multi-species atlas to investigate lncRNA conservation and prioritize candidates for functional studies.

Introduction

Over the last decades, tens of thousands of genes encoding long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), defined as transcripts longer than 200 nucleotides with limited protein-coding potential, have been identified in genomes [1, 2]. These transcripts have emerged as key regulators of gene expression and are involved in a wide range of biological processes, although the functions of most lncRNAs remain uncharacterized [3].

Comparative genomics based on sequence conservation has proven highly effective for inferring gene function, as classically applied to protein-coding genes (PCGs). However, lncRNAs generally evolve at much faster rates than PCGs, which severely limits their detectable sequence conservation across species, even when genomic synteny (i.e. conserved genomic positioning) is maintained. Approximately 70% of lncRNAs lack detectable sequence orthologs in species that diverged >50 million years ago; for instance, only around 100 lncRNAs appear conserved between mammals and fish [4]. Consequently, unlike PCGs, lncRNA orthologs, defined as loci in different species that share a common evolutionary origin, typically identified through conserved genomic context and limited conserved sequence or functional elements, are not reported in reference databases such as Ensembl.

At the sequence level, conservation of lncRNAs is often restricted to short functional segments, typically 5–30 nucleotides long (k-mers), which are thought to mediate molecular functions [4–6]. A notable example is the human lncRNA OIP5-AS, also known as Cyrano in fish, which contains a conserved ∼300-nucleotide region in tetrapods harboring a deeply conserved 26-nucleotide motif that binds miR-7 and promotes its degradation [7]. Similarly, CHASERR has recently been shown to share two highly conserved 7-nucleotide motifs within its last exon that mediate protein binding [8].

In this context, two main strategies have been developed for the comparative genomic analysis of lncRNAs. The first relies on positional conservation (synteny), by identifying lncRNAs located in conserved genomic contexts defined by neighboring orthologous PCGs. Using this approach, several studies have reported positionally conserved lncRNAs across both closely and distantly related species [4, 9], including farm animal species and chicken, which diverged from mammals ~300 million years ago [10–12]. The second strategy focuses on the detection of conserved short sequence motifs across species, requiring alternatives to classical alignment-based methods optimized for long and highly conserved PCG sequences.

Several computational tools have been developed to investigate lncRNA conservation. SEEKR assesses functional similarity based on k-mer abundance patterns, often reflecting protein-binding signatures, and has proven effective for clustering lncRNAs with related functions within a species [13]. More recently, LncLOOM (LncRNA Linear Order cOnserved Motifs) identifies combinations of short motifs (6–12 nucleotides) conserved in sequence and relative order across putatively homologous lncRNAs from different species [8]. It operates on user-defined candidate loci and performs motif discovery without prior assumptions about functional elements, while also annotating predicted miRNA binding sites using TargetScan [14]. In contrast, the lncHOME pipeline performs genome-wide analyses but focuses on predefined functional sequence elements, such as experimentally characterized RNA-binding protein (RBP) motifs derived from curated datasets [15]. Together, these tools illustrate complementary strategies for studying lncRNA conservation, differing in analytical scale and in their reliance on prior functional knowledge.

As a complement to existing approaches, we developed a flexible workflow to infer putative orthologous lncRNAs on a genome-wide scale between user-defined species, relying exclusively on publicly available Ensembl resources. The workflow integrates three complementary strategies: two synteny-based approaches and a third approach based on conserved sequence segments identified through multiple genome alignments generated by the Mercator–Pecan algorithm [16], as implemented in Ensembl Compara. Importantly, by leveraging precomputed genome alignments and orthology relationships available through Ensembl BioMart and Compara APIs, this workflow avoids the computational burden associated with generating large-scale genome alignments locally, while remaining adaptable to other annotation sources if needed.

The workflow requires as input the genome annotation of each species and one-to-one orthologous relationships between PCGs. By default, these PCG orthology relationships are retrieved from Ensembl using the BioMart API, but alternative user-defined orthology mappings can be provided if needed. As output, it produces putative orthology relationships between lncRNAs for each species pair and for each method independently, as well as integrated summaries across methods and across all species considered. This design allows users to explore lncRNA conservation at different stringency levels and phylogenetic scales.

As a first application, we generated a catalog of putative orthologous lncRNAs conserved across diverse vertebrate lineages by applying the workflow to 13 species, including zebrafish and 12 amniotes from Boreoeutheria and Aves, which diverged ~320 million years ago. This species set includes major model organisms (e.g. human, mouse, and zebrafish) and farm or domesticated species (e.g. cow, pig, and chicken) and was intentionally selected to encompass both broad phylogenetic distances and heterogeneous genome assembly and annotation quality. This design allowed us to evaluate how phylogenetic divergence and annotation completeness influence the number and structure of inferred lncRNA orthology relationships.

Beyond orthology inference, we further explored the functional relevance of the identified lncRNAs using two optional downstream functional analyses. These analyses were intentionally developed outside the core orthology workflow because they strongly depend on the biological context, available datasets, and user-defined objectives. First, conserved short sequence motifs were analyzed using LncLOOM, illustrated by an in-depth case study of the human lncRNA OTX2-AS1 and its newly identified counterparts in other species. Second, focusing on human and chicken, we investigated the conservation of expression profiles across 17 shared tissues through cross-species co-expression analyses, as well as the enrichment of co-expressed PCGs within individual tissues. Together, these downstream analyses provide functional context for the inferred orthologous relationships and facilitate the prioritization of conserved lncRNAs for downstream experimental and mechanistic studies.

Overall, this study provides the bioinformatics community with a flexible workflow for the identification of putative orthologous lncRNAs and establishes a comprehensive multi-species atlas of candidate conserved lncRNAs across 13 vertebrate species. By combining genome context, sequence conservation, and complementary functional downstream evidence, this framework enables investigation of lncRNA conservation across species supported by Ensembl, one of the most widely used and curated genomic resources in the scientific community.

Methods

Assemblies and genomic annotations

Genomic data included annotated assemblies from 13 species of diverse taxa. Species selected included (i) model organisms such as Homo sapiens (human), Mus musculus (mouse), and Danio rerio (zebrafish) and (ii) domestic species with two clades represented: (a) Boreoeutherian with Canis lupus familiaris (dog), Bos taurus (cow), Equus caballus (horse), Sus scrofa (pig), and Capra hircus (goat), and (b) Aves with Gallus gallus (chicken), Taeniopygia guttata (zebra finch), Meleagris gallopavo (turkey), Struthio camelus australis (ostrich), and Anas platyrhynchos (duck). For each species, the genome assembly and corresponding annotation available in Ensembl version 109 were used, except for G. gallus, where a custom lncRNA-enriched annotation published by Degalez et al. in 2024 [17] was employed. Briefly, this annotation integrates data from six different resources, including the two reference annotation databases EMBL-EBI/Ensembl (v107 equivalent to the v109) and NCBI/RefSeq (v106), to provide a comprehensive atlas of lncRNA genes in chicken. Additional details regarding the specific assembly and annotation versions and availability for the 13 species are provided in Supplementary Table S12.

Identification of one-to-one orthologous PCGs

Gene orthology relationships were retrieved from Ensembl release 109 using the BioMart interface and associated API queries [18]. For each species pair, only PCGs annotated as “one-to-one orthologs” were extracted.

Analysis of N-to-N orthologous lncRNAs according to their number between both species

The systematic classification of orthology relationships between lncRNAs across species was inspired by the approach typically used for orthologous PCGs. Based on the number of lncRNAs identified in the source and target species, a categorical label was assigned (Fig. 1E). The possible categories include (i) “one-to-zero” or “many-to-zero” when a single or several lncRNA(s) in one species, respectively, have no orthologous lncRNA in the other species; (ii) “one-to-some” when a single lncRNA is observed in both species; (iii) “one-to-many” when a single lncRNA in one species are linked to multiple lncRNAs in the other species; (iv) “many-to-one” for the opposite case, and (v) many-to-many for multiple lncRNAs in both species. For the many-to-many case, an additional tag was implemented to indicate whether the number of lncRNAs was equal or different between species.

FEELnc orientation analysis of lncRNAs according to nearest PCG

LncRNA transcripts were first classified relatively to their closest PCG transcript using the “FEELnc_classifier” function of FEELnc v.0.2.1 [19] (https://github.com/tderrien/FEELnc) with a maximum window of 100 kb (default setting). The classification for gene models was performed by combining the transcript results and the “tpLevel2gnLevelClassfication” function from FEELnc. However, due to differences in genome annotation between species and since lncRNA transcripts are sometimes poorly known, e.g. an antisense lncRNA–PCG pair in one species being considered divergent in another, the FEELnc configuration nomenclature was relaxed. Therefore, as shown in Supplementary Fig. S2, four categories were defined: (i) “Conv” for tail-to-tail, (ii) “Divg” for head-to-head oriented gene, (iii) “SS.up”, and (iv) “SS.down” for upstream and downstream lncRNAs, respectively, in the same orientation as the PCG. Each category is then divided in two groups according to the distance between the lncRNA and PCG TSSs. If the distance is <5000 bp or if the lncRNA TSS is within the PCG boundaries, it is considered as genic (lncg) otherwise intergenic (linc).

Methods to infer lncRNA orthology relation

Method 1: “Orthology-by-synteny” using the two orthologous flanking PCGs

For each species, lncRNAs are associated to the nearest upstream and downstream PCG. Then, for each pair of species, lncRNAs located between pairs of PCGs with one-to-one orthology relationships were extracted. If the relative orientation and strand configurations of the lncRNAs and their flanking PCGs is concordant, i.e identical or in reverse, lncRNAs of both species are considered as putative positional orthologs (Fig. 1B). The type of relation is established as presented in the “N-to-N orthologous lncRNA analysis” section.

Method 2: “Orthology-by-synteny” using one orthologous flanking PCGs and the conserved FEELnc orientation of the lncRNA–PCG pair

For each species, lncRNAs were classified relatively to the nearest PCG and according to the relaxed FEELnc nomenclature (the “FEELnc orientation analysis of lncRNAs according to nearest PCG” section) presented previously. LncRNAs associated to PCGs with one-to-one orthology relationships and with the same FEELnc orientation were then considered as putative orthologs (Fig. 1C). The type of relation is established as presented in the “N-to-N orthologous lncRNA analysis” section.

Method 3: “Orthology-by-genome alignment” using Ensembl/Compara and the Mercator PECAN alignment of amniotes group

The Perl API provided by Ensembl was used to easily access to the Compara database [20]. For each species, the coordinates of all lncRNAs were extracted. After loading the registry associated to the last Ensembl version (here v111), adaptors associated to the species concerned were used. Genomic alignment blocks were retrieved using the “PECAN” method and for the “amniotes” group. Briefly, the Mercator–Pecan algorithm [16] include two steps: (i) Mercator defines orthologous genomic segments across species using conserved coding exons as anchors; and (ii) Pecan subsequently performs multiple sequence alignment (MSA) within these predefined blocks. The genomic coordinates of each lncRNA were used to identify overlapping Compara alignment blocks. An lncRNA was considered as aligned when its genomic region overlapped a Compara block containing a corresponding aligned segment in at least one other species of interest. No additional filtering based on sequence identity, alignment coverage, or alignment length was applied; all alignment parameters and internal thresholds correspond to those implemented in the Ensembl Compara Mercator–Pecan pipeline. For this study and using Ensembl v111, 10 species of interest were available (Canis lupus familiaris, Equus caballus, Capra hircus, Sus scrofa, Mus musculus, Homo sapiens, Taeniopygia guttata, Anas platynrhynchos, Meleagris gallopavo, Gallus Gallus) excluding Bos taurus, Struthio camelus australis, and Danio rerio from the analysis. By construction, the result of this multi-species alignment is a consensus sequence based on the 63 amniotes, but only the block alignment for our 10 species of interest is displayed here (Fig. 1D).

Conservation of short motifs (k-mers) between species sequences

For lncRNA of interest, LncLOOM v2.0 [8] (https://github.com/lncLOOM/lncLOOM) was used to identify combinations of short motifs that are conserved across different species of interest. FASTA sequences for lncRNAs exons identified as one-to-one putative orthologs by method 1 or 2 was used as input. If the lncRNAs had several transcripts, all the exonic parts were considered. The “--targetscan” option was applied to annotate motifs corresponding to conserved miRNA sites according to TargetScan [14]. The k-mers search was performed for motif sizes ranging from 20 (--startw 20) to 6 (default: --stopw 6). The enrichment analysis for the miRNA binding sites detected by TargetScan was conducted using the miRNA Enrichment Analysis Annotation Tool (miEAA) [21].

Expression data among 17 tissues (limited to chicken and human comparison)

Expression analyses were conducted exclusively between chicken and human. For human data, expression in TPM were derived from the RNA-seq expressions provided by the human GTEx Analysis v8 project [22] (https://gtexportal.org/home/downloads/adult-gtex/bulk_tissue_expression). Since the gene annotation used for the human in this study (GRCh38.p13) and the one used by GTEx (GRCh38.p10) are different, only genes with the same gene identifiers in both annotations have been kept (n = 55 525 genes; 19 214 PCGs, and 13 634 lncRNAs). Chicken expression in TPM were obtained from the chicken atlas published by Degalez et al. [17] and accessible on the GEGA user-friendly online tool (https://gega.sigenae.org/) [23]. For expression analysis, a list of 17 tissues with at least 10 samples per tissue for each species and common to both species was established (see Supplementary Table S8). A gene was considered as expressed in a tissue if its expression was >0.1 TPM for at least 80% of the samples. Note that the lack of strand specificity in some of the RNA-seq samples from both the human and chicken datasets may potentially affect the accurate analysis of antisense lncRNAs.

Co-expression indicator

All co-expression analyses were conducted using expression in TPM and the Spearman correlation (⍴). Genes were considered as co-expressed for a |⍴| ≥ 0.7 after that P-values were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method and applying a false discovery rate of 0.05.

Statistical analyses

This study is primarily based on computational analyses of publicly available genomic annotations and transcriptomic datasets. Similarity between protein-coding gene orthology sets was quantified using the Jaccard index. Gene co-expression analyses were performed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient, considering gene pairs with an absolute correlation coefficient (|ρ|) ≥ 0.7 after Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple testing with a false discovery rate threshold of 0.05. Tissue specificity was assessed using tau metrics and fold-change-based criteria calculated from median TPM values across tissues. Comparisons of proportions between orthologous and randomly selected gene sets were performed using two-sided z-tests for two proportions. Functional enrichment analyses of predicted miRNA binding sites were performed using miEAA. Human and chicken expression analyses were based on independent biological RNA-seq samples from publicly available datasets, with at least 10 biological samples available per homologous tissue in each species, as described above. Unless otherwise specified, no additional statistical hypothesis tests were performed.

Results

Overview of the workflow: inputs, methods, and outputs

The workflow developed in this study (Fig. 1A) was designed to enable cross-species inference of lncRNA orthology in a flexible and reusable manner, while requiring a minimal number of user-defined inputs. The only mandatory inputs consist of (i) genome annotation files in GTF format for each species, following the Ensembl nomenclature, and (ii) a configuration file specifying the species names using the corresponding Ensembl identifiers. One-to-one orthology relationships between PCGs, which are used as anchors for lncRNA orthology inference, are automatically retrieved through the BioMart tool. Although this automated procedure was used throughout the present study, the workflow also allows users to provide custom PCG orthology lists when needed, for instance when working with non-standard genome annotations or alternative orthology definitions.

Based on these inputs, the workflow integrates three complementary methods, detailed in the “Material and methods” section as well as in the following sections, that capture distinct and partially independent signals of lncRNA conservation (Fig. 1B–E). Each method relies on different assumptions and stringency levels, allowing users to balance sensitivity and confidence depending on the biological question, the quality of the annotations, and the evolutionary distance between species. Importantly, for each method, results are generated independently, enabling users to directly assess the contribution and behavior of each approach. The workflow then allows flexible combination of results across methods and across species, as illustrated below when we applied it to 13 vertebrate species. Users can thus construct customized sets of putative orthologs—ranging from permissive unions to stringent intersections—according to their specific objectives.

Orthology-by-synteny using two flanking one-to-one orthologous PCGs (Method 1)

Using PCG orthologs as positional anchors, the first step of the workflow is to investigate lncRNA orthology by leveraging conserved genomic context defined by two flanking and oriented one-to-one orthologous PCGs. This “orthology-by-synteny” approach relies on the hypothesis that lncRNAs located between well-conserved orthologous PCGs are more likely to share a common evolutionary origin, even in the absence of strong sequence conservation. lncRNAs identified within these conserved genomic intervals are subsequently classified using a framework analogous to that commonly applied to orthologous PCGs, enabling the identification of one-to-one as well as more complex orthology relationships (Fig. 1B and E).

Orthology-by-synteny using a single one-to-one orthologous PCG and conserved lncRNA–PCG orientation (Method 2)

To complement the previous approach, which requires two flanking one-to-one PCGs, we next implemented a more permissive synteny-based strategy that relies on a single flanking orthologous PCG combined with conservation of the lncRNA–PCG genomic configuration. This configuration is defined using a relaxed FEELnc classification that captures the relative orientation between the lncRNA and its associated PCG (convergent, divergent, or same strand), while intentionally reducing sensitivity to annotation-dependent differences in gene boundaries. Indeed, distinctions between genic and closely spaced intergenic lncRNAs can vary substantially across species due to incomplete or heterogeneous annotations, even when the underlying genomic architecture is conserved (Supplementary Figs S1F and S2). Unlike the first method, which does not impose any constraint on the distance between lncRNAs and flanking PCGs, this method applies the default FEELnc distance threshold of 100 kb between the lncRNA and its associated PCG. Orthology relationships inferred using this strategy are subsequently classified using the same framework as in Method 1, facilitating direct comparison between methods (Fig. 1C and E).

Orthology-by-genome alignment using Ensembl Compara and Mercator–Pecan multiple alignments (Method 3)

Given the rapid sequence evolution of lncRNAs, classical pairwise sequence alignment approaches are generally unsuitable for large-scale orthology inference. As an alternative, we examined conserved sequence patterns using multiple genome alignments generated by the Mercator–Pecan pipeline and provided by Ensembl Compara, which enable reconstruction of putative ancestral sequences across multiple species. By leveraging these multi-species alignments, this approach allows the detection of conserved sequence segments independently of local synteny constraints and without relying on lncRNA annotations in the target genomes. Unlike the two synteny-based approaches, this method relies exclusively on the annotation of the query genome, thereby minimizing the impact of annotation completeness in target species and providing a complementary perspective on lncRNA conservation (Fig. 1D).

A putative lncRNA orthologous atlas across 13 vertebrate species, from human to zebrafish

Having established the workflow and its individual components, we next applied it to a set of 13 vertebrate species to generate a comprehensive atlas of putative lncRNA orthology relationships spanning large evolutionary distances.

Genome annotation variability and protein-coding gene orthology across 13 vertebrates

The selected set of 13 vertebrate species (Fig. 2A) includes zebrafish and 12 amniotes from the Boreoeutheria and Aves clades, which diverged ~429 and 320 million years ago, respectively. This panel encompasses reference organisms (human and mouse), major farm, and domesticated species (horse, cow, pig, goat, dog, chicken, duck, and turkey), as well as species with more limited lncRNA annotations such as ostrich and zebra finch. The selection was deliberately designed to capture both phylogenetic distance and heterogeneity in genome assembly and annotation quality, two factors expected to influence lncRNA orthology inference.

To disentangle the respective impact of phylogenetic distance and annotation depth, four representative comparison categories are used throughout the following analyses: (i) human–mouse, representing phylogenetically close species with high-quality genome annotations; (ii) human–chicken, representing phylogenetically distant species with high-quality annotations; (iii) human–cow, representing phylogenetically close species with relatively limited lncRNA annotation; and (iv) human–ostrich, representing phylogenetically distant species with limited annotation depth.

As illustrated in Fig. 2B, the total number of annotated lncRNA loci varies widely across species, from 1480 and 4431 models for the cow and the ostrich, respectively, to 11 599 in mouse, 18 859 in human, and up to 44 428 in chicken, for which a custom gene-enriched annotation was used but converted to the Ensembl nomenclature. In contrast, the number of PCGs is comparatively stable across species, with a median of 21 343 models. Despite these large differences in lncRNA annotation depth, the overall structural features of annotated lncRNAs—such as transcript complexity and exon organization—are broadly conserved across species (Supplementary Fig. S1).

To establish a reference framework for interpreting lncRNA conservation, we next examined orthology relationships among PCGs across the 13 species. Because PCG annotations are relatively homogeneous across vertebrate genomes, differences in one-to-one PCG orthology primarily reflect phylogenetic distance rather than annotation quality. Pairwise comparisons revealed that the Jaccard index of one-to-one orthologous PCGs is extremely low between zebrafish and all other species and increases progressively with phylogenetic proximity among amniotes (Fig. 2C), reaching its highest value between human and mouse (0.64; 16 289 one-to-one PCGs). Intermediate Jaccard values are observed for more distantly related amniotes, consistent with known evolutionary relationships.

Importantly, these PCG-based orthology patterns provide a quantitative baseline for interpreting lncRNA orthology inference. Synteny-based approaches inherently rely on the conservation of neighboring PCGs, and therefore variation in PCG orthology driven by phylogenetic distance is expected to directly impact both the number and the structure of putative lncRNA orthologous relationships identified in the following sections.

Orthology inference using individual methods

We first evaluated the behavior of each orthology inference method independently, in order to characterize their respective sensitivity, limitations, and complementarity across species differing in phylogenetic distance and annotation depth.

Orthology-by-synteny using two flanking one-to-one orthologous PCGs (Method 1)

Across all pairwise species comparisons, the proportion of lncRNAs flanked by two orthologous PCGs showed substantial variation, reflecting both phylogenetic distance and differences in genome annotation quality. This proportion reached ~59% for the human–chicken comparison and 76% for the human–mouse one (Supplementary Table S1), highlighting the strong dependence of this approach on PCG conservation and annotation completeness. Applying the Method 1 to infer putative orthologous lncRNA relationships revealed marked differences across the four representative comparison categories. Using human as the reference species, we identified 5856 putative orthologous lncRNAs in mouse and 3578 in chicken (Figs 3 and 2D and E). In comparison between human and species with more limited lncRNA annotation such as cow or ostrich, fewer orthologous lncRNAs were detected (1026 and 1654, respectively). Conversely, when these species were used as the reference, 4693 (mouse), 5821 (chicken), 463 (cow), and 989 (ostrich) putative orthologous lncRNAs were identified in human (Supplementary Table S2). These non-reciprocal numbers highlight the complex orthology relationships (one-to-many or many-to-many, Fig. 1E) supported by this synteny-based approach. Therefore, for this method, strict one-to-one lncRNA orthologs represent only a small fraction of the detected cases (~15% using human as reference species). This highlights the stringent nature of requiring two flanking one-to-one orthologous PCGs and underscores that, while effective at identifying conserved genomic contexts, this method alone captures only a limited subset of highly confident one-to-one lncRNA orthologs.

Orthology-by-synteny using a single one-to-one orthologous PCG and conserved lncRNA–PCG orientation (Method 2)

We next evaluated the second synteny-based strategy implemented in Method 2, which relies on a single orthologous PCG anchor combined with conservation of the lncRNA–PCG genomic configuration. Although the default threshold concerning the distance of 100 kb between the lncRNA and its associated PCG may appear conservative, this approach successfully classified a large fraction of lncRNAs across species (Supplementary Table S3), ranging from 62% in goat (n = 1676) to 98% in chicken (n = 43 617). Among classified intergenic lncRNAs, the median distance to the nearest PCG was 12 577 bp (Supplementary Fig. S1E). The distribution of FEELnc classes further revealed differences in the proportions of genic versus intergenic lncRNAs, as well as among lncRNA–PCG configuration, likely reflecting differences in genome annotation and structure across species (Supplementary Fig. S1F).

Applying this Method 2 to infer putative orthologous lncRNA relationships again revealed marked differences across the four representative comparison categories (Fig. 3). Using human as the reference species, we identified 3178 putative orthologous lncRNAs with mouse and 5184 with chicken (Fig. 2D and E). In contrast, substantially fewer orthologous lncRNAs were detected between human and species with more limited lncRNA annotation, such as cow or ostrich (567 and 524, respectively), reflecting the combined effects of annotation depth and phylogenetic distance. Reciprocal analyses yielded 2699 (mouse), 9576 (chicken), 306 (cow), and 233 (ostrich) putative orthologous lncRNAs identified in human when these species were used as references (Supplementary Table S2). Notably, strict one-to-one orthologous lncRNAs represent a substantially larger fraction of the inferred relationships using Method 2 (~46% with human as the reference species), illustrating the increased sensitivity of this approach compared with Method 1, while still being influenced by annotation completeness.

Orthology-by-genome alignment using Ensembl Compara and Mercator–Pecan multiple alignments of amniotes (Method 3)

Finally, the orthology-by-genome alignment approach was conducted using a reference alignment set comprising 63 amniote genomes, including 10 of the 13 species analyzed in this study and excluding cow, ostrich, and zebrafish. Applying this Method 3 revealed substantial differences in the number of inferred orthologous lncRNAs across the representative comparison categories (Fig. 3). Using human as the reference species, we identified 9758 putative orthologous lncRNAs with mouse and 3854 with chicken (Fig. 2D and E). For species with more limited lncRNA annotation but included in the amniote alignment set, such as goat (phylogenetically close) and zebra finch (phylogenetically distant), we identified 8018 and 3837 orthologous lncRNAs, respectively. Results for all the species pairs are available in Supplementary Table S2. Consistent with evolutionary expectations, alignment rates varied strongly with phylogenetic distance. Median alignment rates reached 43% within Boreoeutheria and 34% within Aves, but dropped to 17% for inter-group comparisons. By relying exclusively on sequence conservation in the query genome, this approach provides an annotation-independent and complementary perspective on lncRNA conservation relative to the two synteny-based methods.

Combining orthology inference methods and species to improve reliability

Complementarity of orthology inference methods in pairwise species comparisons

Because each of the three approaches captures distinct aspects of lncRNA conservation and is subject to different methodological biases, we next assessed their complementarity using pairwise species comparisons. We first focused on the human–mouse comparison, representing phylogenetically close species with high-quality genome annotations.

When considering the union of the three methods, 12 888 human lncRNAs (68%) were identified as potentially conserved with mouse by at least one approach (Figs 2D and 3; and Supplementary Table S2). Among these, 1320 lncRNAs (7%) were supported by all three methods, representing a higher-confidence subset. When intersecting the two synteny-based approaches (Methods 1 and 2) designed to explicitly infer lncRNAs orthology relationships, we identified 2106 human lncRNAs (11%) as shared with mouse, representing 66% of Method 2 predictions (2106/3178), which is the most restrictive approach in terms of the number of orthologous lncRNAs detected. We next examined the overlap between synteny-based and genome-alignment-based approaches. Among Method 2 predictions, 61% (1936/3178) were also supported by Method 3, while 55% of Method 1 predictions (3182/5856) overlapped with genome alignment results. These results illustrate the partial but substantial concordance between synteny-based and sequence-based signals of lncRNA conservation. Focusing on strict one-to-one orthologous lncRNAs inferred by both synteny-based methods, we identified 393 human–mouse lncRNA pairs, of which 276 human lncRNAs aligned to a unique locus in the mouse genome (Fig. 3).

We next extended this analysis to the human–chicken comparison, representing high-quality genome annotations despite phylogenetically distant species. As expected, fewer potentially orthologous lncRNAs were detected. A total of 8932 human lncRNAs (47%) were identified as conserved with chicken by at least one method, and only 507 (3%) were supported by all three approaches (Figs 2E and 3; and Supplementary Table S2). When intersecting both synteny-based methods, we identified 1830 human lncRNAs (10%) as shared with chicken, corresponding to 51% of Method 1 predictions (1830/3578). Overlap with the genome-alignment-based approach was again partial: 28% of Method 2 predictions (1468/5184) and 25% of Method 1 predictions (893/3578) were also supported by Method 3. Restricting the analysis to strict one-to-one orthologous lncRNAs inferred by both synteny-based methods, we identified 214 human–chicken pairs, of which 64 human lncRNAs aligned to a unique locus in the chicken genome (Fig. 3).

Equivalent analyses for all other pairwise species comparisons, including phylogenetically close or distant species with more limited lncRNA annotation, are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Multi-species integration of orthology inference methods across 13 vertebrates

When considering all types of orthology relationships (including one-to-one, one-to-many, and many-to-many; Fig. 4A-left), the most permissive strategy [complete union; (i)] identified 14 637 human lncRNAs (78%) conserved in at least two species and 2896 (15%) conserved in eight or more species. Together, these results illustrate the expected trade-off between method stringency and the number of species in which orthologous lncRNAs can be detected, with increasingly stringent combinations yielding progressively smaller but more conservative sets of putative orthologs.

Restricting the analysis to strict one-to-one orthology relationships (Fig. 4A-right) resulted in a moderate reduction in the number of conserved lncRNAs under the union of all methods (i), with 11 841 lncRNAs (63%) conserved in at least two species and 2280 (12%) conserved in eight or more species. In contrast, when focusing exclusively on synteny-based approaches (ii), the number of strictly conserved one-to-one lncRNAs dropped sharply. Under the most stringent condition (iv), only 37 lncRNAs were conserved in at least two species, and none were conserved in more than three species. The two intermediate strategies [(ii) and (iii)] identified 2604 and 846 lncRNAs conserved in at least two species, respectively.

To further characterize patterns of multi-species conservation, we focused on the union of the three orthology detection methods (i), while considering all orthology relationship types (including one-to-one, one-to-many, and many-to-many; Fig. 4B). This analysis revealed non-uniform but phylogenetically coherent distributions of conserved human lncRNAs across species, with distinct peaks corresponding to groups of species sharing substantial numbers of conserved loci. Such structured patterns are unlikely to result from independent emergence and instead likely reflect shared ancestral regulatory architectures, suggesting that a subset of lncRNA loci has been maintained since early amniote diversification. For example, 1115 human lncRNAs were identified as conserved across nine species, including five of the six mammalian species and four of the five avian species analyzed. Samely 1355 lncRNAs were exclusively associated to five of the six Boreoeutheria. Cow and ostrich were excluded from these sets due to a combination of limited lncRNA annotation and the absence of genome-alignment-based orthology detection (Method 3) for these species. These limitations likely lead to an underestimation of the true breadth of lncRNA conservation and may explain why no single lncRNA was detected as conserved across all 12 non-human species. Consistent with this interpretation, the use of a complete and more curated lncRNA annotation for chicken compared to other avian species resulted in a higher number of detected orthologous relationships.

To assess the robustness of these patterns with respect to the choice of reference species, we performed reciprocal analyses in which each of the 13 species was successively used as the reference (Fig. 4C). Considering all the orthologous relationships (All cases) and considering the union of methods (i), 11 of the 13 reference species displayed conservation of >50% of their lncRNAs in at least two other species (Fig. 4C-left). Particularly high proportions were observed for well-annotated mammalian genomes such as human and mouse, reaching up to 78%. When the analysis was restricted to strict one-to-one orthologous relationships (Fig. 4C-right), overall conservation levels decreased across all species, as expected. Nevertheless, conservation remained substantial, with 7 of the 13 reference species still showing >50% of their lncRNAs conserved in at least two other species.

Together, these results reveal a robust and phylogenetically coherent pattern of lncRNA conservation across vertebrates. They highlight the importance of combining multiple orthology inference methods and selecting appropriate stringency criteria depending on the set of species and the specific biological question being addressed. While relaxed criteria maximize sensitivity and allow the exploration of broader sets of putative orthologous relationships, more stringent combinations provide smaller but higher-confidence subsets. Moreover, extending the analysis across multiple species further increases the likelihood of identifying true orthologous relationships, thereby enhancing confidence in conserved lncRNA candidates. Beyond methodological considerations, these results also provide biological insights into the evolutionary dynamics of lncRNAs across vertebrates. The non-uniform but phylogenetically coherent conservation patterns observed across Boreoeutheria and Aves suggest that a subset of lncRNAs has been maintained over hundreds of millions of years, despite rapid sequence divergence. Such conservation across multiple lineages is unlikely to occur by chance and supports the hypothesis that at least a fraction of lncRNAs are subjected to functional constraints. In particular, lncRNAs detected in multiple species and supported by independent orthology inference methods represent strong candidates for conserved regulatory functions.

Availability of the complete orthology resource

All tables reporting the results of the three orthology inference methods, across all species and using each species successively as the reference, are provided in the Supplementary Table S4. These tables constitute a comprehensive resource in which no filtering has been applied, neither on the orthology inference methods (Fig. 1B–D) nor on the type of orthologous relationship (one-to-one, one-to-many, or many-to-many; Fig. 1E), and no restriction has been imposed on the number of species sharing a given lncRNA (Fig. 4). This unfiltered resource is made available to the community to enable flexible downstream analyses and user-defined stringency criteria. It also served as the basis for the analyses presented in this study, including the detailed investigation of the OTX2-AS1 lncRNA orthology across species.

Application of the orthologous lncRNA atlas: the OTX2-AS1 case study

OTX2-AS1 (OTX2 antisense RNA 1) is a lncRNA antisense to the OTX2 PCG and regulated by a shared bidirectional promoter [25]. It has been implicated in eye development [25] and has recently been shown to be co-expressed with OTX2 in medulloblastoma, where it promotes tumor progression [26], although its molecular mechanisms remain largely uncharacterized.

Interrogation of the orthologous lncRNA atlas generated through this study revealed that OTX2-AS1 is potentially conserved across multiple vertebrate species up to the zebrafish (Fig. 5A; Supplementary Table S5). Using the two-PCG synteny-based approach (Method 1), OTX2-AS1 is located between EXOC5 (upstream) and OTX2 (downstream), both of which exhibit strict one-to-one orthology across most species. Based on this conserved genomic context, putative orthologs were identified in mouse, chicken, and pig, and a single candidate was detected in duck. In contrast, no ortholog was detected in dog or zebrafish using this method, due to the absence of a conserved upstream PCG directly adjacent to EXOC5 in these species.

Identification of putative lncRNA orthologs for human lncRNA OTX2-AS1 across 13 species. (A) Genomic regions and results of the three methods considered for research of potential orthologs for OTX2-AS1 (indicated by the gray top numbers). The arrow orientation corresponds to the gene strand. The number associated to the configuration corresponds to the distance between the lncRNA and the PCG. (B) Alignment details generated by the Mercator–Pecan algorithm for available species. The human sequence of OTX2-AS1 was used as input. (C) Genomic regions associated with potential OTX2-AS1 ortholog considered after manual observation. Details are provided in Supplementary Table S5.

Using the single-PCG synteny approach combined with conserved lncRNA–PCG configuration (Method 2), OTX2-AS1 was classified as a divergent genic lncRNA (lncgDivg) relative to OTX2. This approach identified a single putative ortholog in mouse, chicken, pig, duck, dog, and zebrafish. Notably, the mouse ortholog is annotated as Otx2os1, providing independent support for its orthology with human OTX2-AS1. In the other species, the corresponding lncRNAs lack assigned gene names, in accordance with HGNC recommendations for cross-species lncRNA nomenclature.

The genome-alignment–based approach (Method 3), applied to the 10 species available in the Mercator–Pecan multiple alignment, detected conserved alignment blocks for OTX2-AS1 in mouse, chicken, duck, and dog, as well as in goat, turkey, and zebra finch—species for which the synteny-based methods did not identify orthologs. Inspection of the multiple alignment revealed distinct alignment patterns between Boreoeutheria and Aves (Fig. 5B). Despite substantial sequence divergence between these clades, several short regions appear conserved, suggesting potential functional importance.

In cow and horse, no method initially detected OTX2-AS1 conservation due to the absence of annotated lncRNAs between OTX2 and EXOC5. However, manual inspection combined with updated annotations substantially revised this conclusion. In cow, the most recent ARS-UCD1.3 assembly (Ensembl v111) includes a newly annotated lncRNA in this locus, displaying a divergent intergenic (lincDivg) configuration relative to OTX2, consistent with potential orthology to OTX2-AS1 (Fig. 5C-left). Similarly, although current horse annotations lack a corresponding lncRNA, earlier Ensembl releases (v106) reported three lncRNAs in this region, including one classified as “lincDivg” (Fig. 5C-right). These observations underscore the strong dependence of lncRNA orthology inference on annotation completeness and the dynamic nature of genome annotation resources.

Taken together, integrating evidence from multiple orthology inference methods and iterative annotation updates increases the number of species supporting OTX2-AS1 conservation from 9 to 11, leaving ostrich as the only species without detectable support. This case study illustrates how the orthologous lncRNA atlas enables detailed, multi-layered investigation of lncRNA conservation that may guide future functional studies.

Functional motif conservation (k-mers) in orthologous lncRNAs revealed by LncLOOM

To further explore the functional potential of putative orthologous lncRNAs identified in the atlas, we applied LncLOOM, a tool designed to detect conserved short sequence motifs across homologous lncRNA sequences. Although this downstream analysis can be performed for any lncRNA of interest, we illustrate its use here with the OTX2-AS1 locus described above.

Exonic sequences from human OTX2-AS1 and putative orthologous lncRNAs identified across the eight species (Fig. 5) were extracted and analyzed using LncLOOM. Sequence lengths varied widely across species (Fig. 6A-left), reflecting differences in annotation depth, ranging from 549 bp in dog to over 15 kb in pig. Using the human sequence as the reference, LncLOOM identified 73 conserved motifs across species. Most of these motifs (n = 62; >85%) were shared exclusively between human and pig (Fig. 6A-right), consistent with their closer phylogenetic relationship and higher annotation completeness. Because motif discovery proceeds incrementally in LncLOOM (Supplementary Table S6), motifs absent in a given species are not searched for in more distantly related species.

Among the conserved motifs, an 8-nt core sequence (AAAAGTTG) was detected in all analyzed species and was embedded within larger conserved motifs found in subsets of species. To further assess the potential functional relevance of these conserved motifs, we examined their overlap with predicted microRNA (miRNA) binding sites using TargetScan integrated in LncLOOM. Of the 73 motifs identified using the human reference, 19 (26%) were associated with 21 putative miRNA binding sites. Most of these sites were detected only in human and pig, but several were also conserved in additional species, including duck and chicken. Enrichment analysis of miRNA-associated motifs using the MNDR (Mammal ncRNA–Disease Repository) database revealed significant over-representation of disease categories related to retinoblastoma (n = 9; P-val = 2.95e-4) and brain disorders (n = 16; P-val = 7.07e-5) (Supplementary Table S7), in agreement with the established role of OTX2 in retinal development [25] and pediatric brain tumor [26]. Notably, miR-186-5p was associated with four distinct conserved motifs and was detected in up to four species. This miRNA has been implicated in neuronal plasticity and neurodevelopmental processes [27].

Together, these results illustrate how integrating orthology inference with downstream motif-level conservation analysis enables functional prioritization of conserved lncRNAs and highlights candidate regulatory elements for downstream experimental investigation. More generally, the identification of conserved short motifs suggests preservation of functional modules, potentially mediating RNA–miRNA interactions. Such motif-level conservation, even in the absence of extensive primary sequence conservation, provides an additional layer of evidence for functional constraint.

Conserved expression patterns of orthologous lncRNAs between human and chicken

As detailed in the “Materials and methods” section, gene expression profiles were analyzed across 17 equivalent tissues (Supplementary Table S8) between human (GTEx) and chicken (enriched atlas). In human, 11 971 lncRNAs (88% of the 13 634 GTEx lncRNAs) and 18 587 PCGs (97% of GTEx PCGs) were expressed in at least one tissue (TPM ≥ 0.1), including 7054 tissue-specific lncRNAs (τ ≥ 0.9), predominantly in testis (Supplementary Table S9). Similar trends were observed in chicken, where 28 384 lncRNAs (66%) and 17 688 PCGs (87%) were expressed, and tissue-specific lncRNAs were again enriched in testis (Supplementary Fig. S3 and Supplementary Table S10).

Using the 1555 putative one-to-one orthologous lncRNA pairs identified between human and chicken by Method 2 (Fig. 3), 1120 pairs were retained for expression downstream analysis after filtering for detectable expression in at least one tissue in either species. Expression profiles of the one-to-one orthologous lncRNAs were compared tissue by tissue across the 17 shared tissues (Supplementary Table S11) and contrasted with randomly paired non-orthologous lncRNAs. As shown in Fig. 6B-(i), orthologous lncRNA pairs were significantly more likely to be expressed in both species than random pairs (25% versus 13%; z-test, P < 10−13). Applying the same analytical framework to PCGs revealed a similar but stronger enrichment, with 90% of orthologous PCG pairs showing higher cross-species expression compared to 57% for random pairs (z-test, P < 10−17). Although absolute levels differ, these parallel results indicate that orthology is associated with increased cross-species expression conservation inside a given tissue and for both gene classes, with lncRNAs displaying lower overall expression concordance, consistent with their more restricted and tissue-specific expression patterns.

We next evaluated expression concordance across the 17 shared tissues using rank-based co-expression analyses. Among the 1120 putative orthologous lncRNA pairs, 798 were expressed in at least one tissue in both species and for the 1111 pairs used as control, 443 were expressed. A significantly higher proportion of the 798 orthologous pairs displayed moderate to strong co-expression compared with the 443 non-orthologous control pairs considered [Fig. 6B-(ii)]. Specifically, 18.9% and 4.6% of orthologous lncRNA pairs exhibited correlation coefficients ≥0.5 and ≥0.7, respectively, whereas only 6.8% and 1.6% of non-orthologous pairs reached these thresholds (z-tests, P < 10−8, and p < 10−3, respectively). Therefore, orthologous lncRNAs supported by synteny-based approaches showed significantly higher proportions of conserved tissue expression compared to random gene sets, reinforcing the hypothesis that genomic positional conservation is often accompanied by preservation of regulatory roles. A similar trend was observed for PCGs. Among the 1108 orthologous PCG pairs and 835 non-orthologous pairs expressed in at least one tissue in both species, 43.1% and 14.4% of orthologous pairs showed correlation coefficients ≥0.5 and ≥0.7, respectively, compared with 11.5% and 2.5% for non-orthologous pairs (z-tests, P < 10−16, and p < 10−16, respectively). It should be noted that the magnitude of this effect is partly underestimated for lncRNAs, as a substantial fraction of non-orthologous lncRNA pairs are not expressed in one of the two species and are therefore excluded from the co-expression analysis. This limitation reduces the contrast with orthologous pairs compared with PCGs, which are more broadly and consistently expressed across tissues.

Finally, two examples illustrate the biological relevance of conserved expression patterns [Fig. 6B-(iii)]. The lncRNA OTX2-AS1 and its putative chicken ortholog show significant co-expression across tissues (ρ = 0.56) and strong tissue specificity in both species (τ = 0.96 in chicken and τ = 0.95 in human), with highest expression in the hypothalamus, consistent with the known functions of OTX2. Similarly, the human lncRNA AC099754.1 (renamed LRRC3B-AS1 in GRCh38.p14) and its chicken ortholog LOC112531717 display high co-expression (ρ = .71) and share predominant expression across several brain-related tissues. In both cases, the lncRNAs are positioned in divergent or antisense configuration relative to the conserved PCG LRRC3B, which exhibits a comparable expression profile and is involved in brain-associated processes such as cholesterol metabolisms [28] and Down syndrome [29, 30].

Together, these downstream analyses show that putative orthologous lncRNAs display higher cross-species expression overlap and co-expression than non-orthologous pairs. This provides additional support for the biological relevance of the inferred lncRNA orthology relationships.

Discussion

By combining three complementary approaches—two based on genomic synteny and one relying on multiple genome alignment— we established lists of putative conserved lncRNAs, either between pairs of species or across multiple phylogenetically distant ones.

A major limitation of this study lies in the heterogeneity and continuous evolution of genome assemblies and associated gene annotations, which can directly affect the identification and stability of inferred orthologous lncRNA relationships [31].

Regarding genome assemblies, the frequency of updates may decrease in the coming years as recent sequencing technologies enable the production of increasingly accurate and complete reference genomes. Notable examples include the telomere-to-telomere (T2T) human genome assembly published in 2022 [32], as well as recent high-quality assemblies generated for species such as chicken [33]. In parallel, large-scale initiatives such as the Earth BioGenome Project [34] and the Darwin Tree of Life project [35] aim to provide reference genomes for thousands of species, thereby substantially improving the genomic resources available for comparative analyses.

Genome annotation quality is also expected to greatly improve with the broader adoption of long-read sequencing technologies applied to transcriptomic [36], as well as targeted capture approaches designed to detect lowly expressed transcripts such as lncRNAs [37, 38]. These advances should refine isoform models, particularly for PCGs, and facilitate the identification of previously unannotated genes, including lncRNAs.

However, even when a high-quality assembly is selected as a reference, substantial discrepancies remain between gene models produced by major annotation resources such as Ensembl/EMBL and NCBI/RefSeq. While PCG annotations mainly differ at the transcript isoform level, lncRNA annotations frequently diverge at both the transcript and gene locus levels. This observation underscores the importance of integrating multiple annotation sources, as illustrated by the enriched chicken genome annotation used in this study [17]. It should be noted that, within a given reference database, successive annotation releases (e.g, Ensembl v111 versus Ensembl V109) generally introduce only limited changes, independently of gene biotype [17, 31]. Nevertheless, the inherent variability in genome assemblies and annotations remains a critical factor influencing the three orthology inference methods applied here.

The approach based on the conservation of two neighboring PCGs, referred to here as Method 1, is effective for identifying well-conserved lncRNAs but remains inherently stringent. In phylogenetically distant species, the number of conserved PCGs is often limited, which restricts the ability to reliably detect syntenic lncRNAs. In addition, because this method uses the lncRNA locus as a starting point to identify surrounding orthologous PCGs, cases in which one or both neighboring PCGs are not conserved may lead to the exclusion of otherwise relevant lncRNAs from further analysis.

To address the constraints associated with the use of two orthologous PCGs (Method 1), the second approach (Method 2) relies on the relative configuration between the nearest PCG, which needs to be conserved, and the lncRNA of interest. Although this method remains influenced by genome annotation quality and the availability of gene models, anchoring the analysis to a single PCG reduces this dependency compared with the two-PCG strategy. Nevertheless, lncRNA classification according to FEELnc standards [19] still depends strongly on the accuracy of gene models, particularly with respect to transcript isoforms. For example, distinguishing between antisense and convergent transcripts, or between genic and intergenic lncRNAs, often relies on UTR annotations that may be incomplete or poorly defined [39]. To mitigate these limitations, a relaxed classification scheme was implemented in this study. While such relaxation introduces a degree of user-dependent variability, it allows a broader inclusion of candidate orthologs. Conversely, applying more stringent classification criteria increases confidence in the inferred relationships, at the cost of reduced sensitivity.

Finally, the third approach (Method 3), which is based on MSA, is comparatively less dependent on genome annotations, but still requires the identification of lncRNAs in the source species to guide the search for orthologous genomic regions. Nevertheless, the detection of a conserved genomic region in a target species does not guarantee transcriptional activity or evolutionary retention of the corresponding lncRNA, underscoring a fundamental limitation of alignment-based approaches. As genome sequencing expands, further improvements in lncRNA annotation quality will be essential to refine orthology inference across diverse taxa.

On the other hand, constructing MSAs for large numbers of species, particularly phylogenetically distant ones, is computationally resource-intensive [40] and alternative strategies—such as annotation transfer tools (e.g. Liftoff [41]) or de novo alignment frameworks (e.g. Progressive Cactus [42] or Multiz [43])—present additional limitations. Annotation transfer is inherently pairwise, difficult to scale across many species, and highly dependent on annotation quality and parameter thresholds. Conversely, generating custom genome-wide alignments reduces reliance on gene annotations but requires substantial computational resources and would need to be repeated for each newly defined species set or generated for many species. In this context, the reliance on precomputed multi-species alignments from Ensembl Compara represents a deliberate and pragmatic choice rather than a conceptual limitation. A central objective of this study was to demonstrate that large-scale lncRNA orthology inference can be achieved using exclusively publicly available resources from established reference genome databases, thereby ensuring accessibility and reproducibility. Curated and phylogenetically broad alignment datasets, such as those provided by Ensembl, offer a practical compromise between methodological robustness and computational feasibility.

Although this study relies on Ensembl resources for standardization and reproducibility, the workflow is not intrinsically restricted to Ensembl-annotated species. Any genome annotation provided in compatible GTF format, combined with externally computed one-to-one orthologous PCG relationships (e.g. generated using OrthoFinder or OMA), could be used as input. The dependence on Ensembl in the present study reflects a choice for data consistency rather than a strict technical constraint.

Overall, while the identification of putative orthologous relationships using each individual method represents an important first step, the combined use of the three approaches provides substantially stronger support for lncRNA orthology. However, the optimal combination of methods depends on both phylogenetic distance and annotation quality. In comparative analyses involving phylogenetically distant species and/or heterogeneous genome annotations, requiring support from all three methods may prove overly stringent and lead to the exclusion of biologically relevant orthologs. In such contexts, combining one of the two synteny-based approaches with the genome-alignment-based method may represent a more appropriate compromise, as it reinforces the orthology hypothesis by integrating both positional and sequence-based evidence while preserving sufficient sensitivity. In contrast, the strict intersection of all three methods is best suited for closely related species with high-quality annotations, where maximizing confidence is prioritized over sensitivity. In this study, we established lists of potentially orthologous lncRNAs and, consistent with previous observations [4, 44, 45], found that the number of inferred orthologs is higher between well-annotated and phylogenetically close species, and progressively decreases for species that diverged earlier. Importantly, the inference of orthology does not inherently imply functional conservation, highlighting the need for additional validation layers. Nevertheless, when putative orthology is supported across multiple species—particularly across phylogenetically distant lineages—the confidence in the inferred relationship is markedly increased, as illustrated by the OTX2-AS1 case study.

To further characterize putative orthologous lncRNA pairs, downstream functional analyses can be applied Importantly, these analyses were intentionally not integrated into the core workflow because their implementation strongly depends on species-specific resources, transcriptomic datasets, annotation quality, and the biological questions being addressed. In this study, we explored two main strategies: (i) the identification of conserved functional sequence patches and (ii) the conservation of gene expression profiles across species.

Regarding the a downstream nalysis of conserved sequences (i), the LncLOOM tool [8] provides an effective framework for identifying conserved motifs, although the accuracy of the underlying gene models is a critical prerequisite. Motif discovery in LncLOOM is cumulative and depends on the reordering of sequences according to their divergence. Consequently, motifs that are not detected at a given step are not searched for in more distantly related species. The choice of the reference sequence, which defines the order of comparison, is therefore crucial, as noted by Ross et al. [8]. An alternative strategy would consist in iteratively using each putative orthologous sequence as a reference to identify all motifs conserved across species. However, inaccuracies in some gene models may introduce biases in such an approach. For this reason, restricting motif analyses to sequences derived from well-annotated species—such as human or mouse—or from species of biological interest, such as chicken in the present study, is preferable. In this context, the conservation of short functional sequence patches provides not only additional support for inferred orthology relationships but also valuable functional insights. Conserved regions may correspond to binding sites for regulatory elements, including miRNAs (identified via LncLOOM/TargetScan) or RBPs, as recently illustrated by Huang et al. [15]. Functional enrichment analyses can subsequently be performed using dedicated tools such as the miEAA [21]. Moreover, it is possible to evaluate whether the expression profiles of these regulatory elements are consistent with those of the associated lncRNAs.

For the downstream analysis of gene expression profiles (ii), the primary requirement is the availability of expression datasets covering the species of interest and including a sufficiently large number of comparable or analogous tissues. This condition is essential both to reliably compare expression profiles and to properly assess tissue specificity. Because lncRNAs are known to exhibit much higher tissue specificity than PCGs [1, 17, 46], the absence of relevant tissues in the dataset would result in the exclusion of a substantial fraction of lncRNAs from downstream analyses. Taking these constraints into account, a first level of analysis consists in determining whether two lncRNAs, considered as putative orthologs, are expressed in the same tissues. This assessment can be refined by comparing expression levels, for example by distinguishing weakly expressed genes (typically 0.1–1 TPM) from more highly expressed ones (>1 TPM). Although expression quantification may be affected by factors such as tissue panel composition, RNA-seq protocols, or sequencing depth, this initial filtering increases confidence in inferred orthologous relationships. This statement is supported in our analysis by the observation of a two-fold higher proportion of orthologous lncRNA pairs expressed in the same tissues compared with non-orthologous control pairs.

In a second step, expression profiles of each lncRNA pair can be compared across all tissues where rank-based correlations can be computed to assess the similarity of expression patterns between species. Detailed inspection of these profiles further allows the identification of lncRNA pairs sharing similar tissue specificity, providing an initial indication of functional conservation. This principle is illustrated for causal genes associated with Mendelian traits [17] and is also exemplified here by OTX2-AS1. In this case, the genomic proximity between the lncRNA and its neighboring PCG, OTX2, combined with strong expression correlation and high tissue specificity observed in both human and chicken, suggests shared regulatory mechanisms or a potential role of the lncRNA in modulating PCG expression within the same tissue context. Because these features are conserved across species, they may reflect functional constraints maintained through evolution [47]. Finally, it should be noted that tissue specificity is a relative measure that depends on multiple factors, including the metric used [48].

Finally, this work provides a versatile workflow whose main strength lies in the integration of three complementary approaches that can be applied across a diverse set of species while maintaining sufficient flexibility for users to implement customized downstream analyses adapted to their own datasets and biological questions. The ability to easily combine all or part of these methods allows both phylogenetic distance and annotation quality to be explicitly considered when inferring orthologous relationships between lncRNAs in any species. In addition, the study delivers a catalog of lncRNAs that are potentially conserved at different evolutionary scales across 13 vertebrate species, ranging from zebrafish to multiple amniotes and birds. Beyond providing a methodological framework and a multi-species resource, this work highlights that a non-negligible subset of vertebrate lncRNAs exhibits multi-level conservation—genomic, sequence, and expression—supporting their potential involvement in deeply conserved regulatory networks. By integrating complementary signals of conservation across large evolutionary distances, this framework enables the identification of high-confidence lncRNA candidates for future functional and mechanistic studies.
