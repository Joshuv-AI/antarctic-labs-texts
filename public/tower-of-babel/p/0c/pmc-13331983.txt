Biofunctionalized zinc nanoparticles from Alangium salvifolium seeds extract: a potent antibacterial and anti-Cancer therapeutic agent
Authors: Sheeba Khanam, Pooja Mishra, Faiza Siddiqui, Tabrez Faruqui, Mohd Saif, Abu Baker, Mohammed F. Hawwal, Omer M. Almarfadi, Omer I. Fantoukh, Nasir A. Siddiqui, Salman Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rise of multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens and the limitations of traditional therapies have prompted the discovery of biocompatible nanomaterials. In this study, green, one-pot synthesis of zinc oxide nanoparticles (AS-ZnO-NPs) was performed using Alangium salvifolium seed extract (aqueous) as both reducing and stabilizing agent.Nanoparticles were characterized physicochemically, showing an absorption peak at 387 nm by UV–visible spectroscopy. Dynamic light scattering produced a hydrodynamic diameter of 53.28 nm, which is consistent with a biomolecular capping layer, while transmission electron microscopy showed spherical particles with a mean core diameter of 13.2 ± 2 nm. AS-ZnO-NPs showed broad-spectrum antibacterial activity (4.2–9.96 μg/mL) (p < 0.05 compared to levofloxacin control) against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. The enhanced antibacterial effect may result from nanoparticle interactions with microbial membranes, leading to oxidative stress. AS-ZnO-NPs displayed high cytotoxicity against A549 lung carcinoma cells (IC50 = 16.38 ± 1.22 μg/mL), surpassing both the crude extract and 5-fluorouracil. Mechanistic studies using H2DCFDA fluorescence and DAPI staining indicated ROS-mediated apoptosis, as evidenced by condensed chromatin. The nanoparticles showed relatively low toxicity on normal osteoblasts. The ZnO nanoparticles synthesized from Alangiumsalvifolium show promise as antimicrobial and anticancer agents, emphasizing the need for further in vivo testing and mechanistic studies.

Introduction

The rapid rise of multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacterial pathogens has emerged as a critical global health challenge in the 21st century. The widespread and often inappropriate clinical administration of conventional antibiotics has accelerated the evolutionary selection of resistant strains, drastically reducing the efficacy of frontline antimicrobial therapies1 Concurrently, oncological malignancies, particularly lung cancer, remain a leading cause of global mortality, highlighting an urgent and unmet need for novel, biocompatible therapeutic interventions. Recent advancements in nanobiotechnology suggest that engineered metallic nanomaterials offer a promising alternative to address these dual biomedical crises due to their unique ability to generate targeted oxidative stress, disrupt microbial cell membranes, and trigger apoptotic cascades in transformed malignant lineages.1, 2 Over the past few decades, the pivotal role of oxidative stress driven by reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS) has been heavily implicated in both microbial pathogenesis and carcinogenesis.2 In cellular systems, excessive accumulation of these radical species induces severe biomolecular damage, including lipid peroxidation, protein carbonylation, and genomic instability, which subsequently aberrantly activates downstream signaling pathways involved in malignant transformation.3 In the respiratory architecture specifically, continuous exposure to environmental xenobiotics further accelerates localized pulmonary inflammation, driving the progression of human lung carcinoma (A549). Developing multifunctional therapeutic platforms capable of selectively modulating these oxidative stress pathways represents a major paradigm shift in combining effective antimicrobial action with targeted cancer cytotoxic therapies.3 Among the diverse library of inorganic nanomaterials, zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) have garnered profound scientific interest owing to their exceptional biocompatibility, low intrinsic toxicity profiles, and versatile biophysical attributes approved for biomedical applications.4, 5 Mechanistic reports indicate that structural nano-scale ZnO constructs exhibit potent broad-spectrum antimicrobial and anti-proliferative actions. These biological effects are primarily governed by the sustained release of functional zinc ions (Zn+2), surface-mediated electrostatic interactions with cellular envelopes, and the localized generation of intracellular ROS that systematically dismantle core survival pathways in both bacterial vectors and carcinoma targets.6, 7, 8 However, conventional physical and chemical top-down or bottom-up fabrication methodologies for synthesizing nano-scale zinc structures frequently necessitate substantial energy expenditure, sophisticated vacuum architectures, and the employment of hazardous chemical reducing agents like sodium borohydride or hydrazine. These protocols often leave toxic chemical residues capped onto the nanomaterial surface, severely restricting their downstream translational safety in clinical oncology and medicine.9 Conversely, the paradigm of green synthesis utilizing benign plant extracts—has emerged as an environmentally sustainable, economically viable, and highly efficient alternative. Green fabrication exploits the diverse pool of secondary plant metabolites, which simultaneously serve as non-toxic chemical reducing matrices and steric stabilizing agents, yielding structurally uniform, biofunctionalized nanoparticles with enhanced surface-dependent biological reactivity.3, 5 In recent years (2021–2026), extensive literature has documented the green biofabrication of ZnO nanostructures utilizing diverse botanical matrices. However, the exact structural performance, surface-capping chemistry, and synergistic biological potential remain heavily dependent on the specific phytochemical profile of the plant species utilized.10 While prior studies have extensively investigated crude extracts derived from the leaves, bark, or roots of various medicinal plants, the complex seed matrix of Alangium salvifolium has remained completely unexamined in the context of green nanotechnology.10 This represents a distinct scientific research gap that limits the full therapeutic exploitation of this valuable botanical assetc.11 Alangium salvifolium (commonly recognized as Ankol) is a well-documented medicinal plant indigenous to tropical and subtropical ecosystems, extensively utilized across traditional ethnomedicinal systems for managing inflammatory conditions, cutaneous infections, and metabolic disorders. Detailed phytochemical screenings reveal that the structural organs of A. salvifolium are exceptionally rich in complex bioactive principles, including specific flavonoids, polyphenolics, glycosides, and distinct structural alkaloids such as alangine, ankorine, marckine, and lamarckinine.4, 12, 13 While raw, unrefined extracts of A. salvifolium have demonstrated baseline anti-proliferative activities against select melanoma and non-melanoma lineages,9 the unique therapeutic phytochemicals concentrated within its seeds have never been deployed as a bio-reducing matrix to synthesize functionalized metallic oxide nanoparticles. To address this distinct scientific gap, the present study establishes a foundational, eco-friendly, one-pot green synthesis paradigm to fabricate biofunctionalized zinc oxide nanoparticles (AS-ZnO-NPs) specifically utilizing the aqueous seed extract of Alangium salvifolium. The structural, optical, and morphological profiles of the resulting nano-cores were systematically mapped utilizing a series of complementary physicochemical characterization approaches. Furthermore, the baseline biomedical efficacy of these newly synthesized AS-ZnO-NPs was comprehensively evaluated through broad-spectrum quantitative antimicrobial screenings against clinical bacterial pathogens and targeted in vitro cytotoxicity profiling against human lung carcinoma (A549) models. This exploratory investigation provides essential foundational insights required to develop next-generation, plant-derived, multifunctional nanotherapeutics

Materials and methods

Materials

Zinc nitrate hexahydrate [Zn(NO₃)₂·6H₂O] (≥99% purity, Sigma-Aldrich) was utilized as the primary precursor salt for the biofabrication. (St. Louis, MO, USA). Alangium salvifolium seeds were obtained from local suppliers and authenticated by botanical experts. The MTT reagent, H₂DCFDA, DAPI, and DMEM were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich.HiMedia (India) provided the agar, TSB, and Mueller-Hinton broth. Every chemical was of at least analytical grade. Throughout, 18.2 MΩ cm of ultrapure deionized water was utilized.

Cell culture and microbial strains

Gram-negative bacteria, such as Escherichia coli (MTCC 1885), and Gram-positive bacteria, such as Bacillus subtilis (MTCC 441) and Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 25923), and Klebsiella pneumoniae (ATCC 6644) were obtained from certified culture repositories. All were cultured on Mueller-Hinton agar at 37 °C. Human lung cancer A549 cells (ATCC CCL-185) and normal human primary osteoblasts (hFOB 1.19, CRL-3602) were acquired from ATCC. These cells were grown at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 in DMEM supplemented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin and 10% fetal bovine serum 14, 15.

Preparation of seed extract of AS seeds

The seeds of Alangium salvifolium were formally authenticated, and a voucher specimen (AS/SEED/2024/102) has been deposited at the institutional herbarium repository. The seeds were thoroughly cleaned with distilled water and allowed to dry completely at room temperature. Five grams of the dried seeds were then ground in 50 mL of phosphate buffer (100 mM, pH 7.4) using a sterile mortar and pestle. The grinding process was strictly performed on ice to minimize protein denaturation. The resulting homogenate was filtered through Whatman filter paper (Grade 1). The supernatant was subsequently collected after centrifugation at 6000rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. To ensure maximum clarity, 0.22 μm syringe filters were used to further filter the supernatant. The final resultant aqueous seed extract was stored at 4 °C before being utilized for downstream nanoparticle synthesis 15, 16.

Biosynthesis of biofunctionalized zinc oxide nanoparticle

Zinc oxide nanoparticles were synthesized using a green chemistry approach employing zinc nitrate [Zn(NO₃)₂] as the zinc precursor. 3 mL of aqueous Alangium salvifolium seed extract, which served as a reducing and capping agent, was combined with a 1 mM aqueous solution of zinc nitrate made in deionized water. For 48 h, the reaction mixture was kept in the dark at 40 °C. A slow shift in color from light green to pale yellow suggested that the phytochemicals in the extract were mediating the formation of nanoparticles.

To eliminate unbound macromolecules, 0.2 μm syringe filters were used to filter the resultant suspension. It was then precipitated with absolute ethanol (1:1 volume ratio). ZnO-NPs were gathered through 10 min of centrifugation at 12,000 rpm. They were washed three times using deionized water and ethanol, and then kept at 4 °C for additional examination 15.

AS-Zn-NPs: A physicochemical study

The synthesized AS-ZnO-NPs were systematically characterized using standard physicochemical techniques, including UV–Vis spectroscopy, dynamic light scattering (DLS), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) profiling, and Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, which have been consolidated here to avoid any repetition.”

UV–Vis spectroscopy validated ZnO-NPs' surface plasmon resonance band characteristic, and elemental analysis using a dual-beam spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1601 PC, Japan) operating at 1 nm resolution over 200–800 nm. The elemental composition was examined using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX). Zeta potential and dynamic light scattering (DLS) were measured in triplicate at 25 °C using a Zetasizer Nano-ZS (Malvern Instruments, UK). A Tecnai G2 Spirit TEM (FEI, USA Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis was conducted at 80 kV. Samples were made by pipetting 5 μL of AS-ZnO-NP suspension onto carbon-coated copper grids. A Perkin-Elmer Spectrum system (USA) operating in the 650–4000 cm−1 range at 4 cm−1 resolution was used to perform Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy.

Antibacterial activity and MIC determination

The broth microdilution assay and the agar well diffusion method was used to assess the antibacterial efficacy on against S. aureus, B. subtilis, E. coli, and K. pneumoniae. For the Zone of Inhibition (ZoI) test, standardized bacterial suspensions adjusted to a final inoculum density of approximately 1–2 × 108 CFU/mL (equivalent to a 0.5 McFarland standard) were uniformly spread over Mueller-Hinton agar plates. Wells with a 6 mm diameter were subsequently loaded with equal volumes (50 μL, 1 mg/mL) of aqueous seed extract, AS-ZnO-NPs, PBS (negative control), and levofloxacin (positive control). Inhibition zone diameters were measured in triplicate following an overnight incubation period at 37 °C.

The Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) was determined by the CLSI (2015) broth microdilution guidelines. In 96-well plates with TSB medium, two-fold serial dilutions of the test agents (1.5625–100 μg/mL) were made. 10 μL of standardized bacterial suspension (OD600 = 0.4) were added to each well. 2 × 101 CFU/mL) and incubated for 20 h at 37 °C. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was the lowest concentration that prevented observable bacterial growth. To guarantee reproducibility, every experiment was conducted in triplicate 3, 17.

Cytotoxicity assessment in human Cancer cells

In order to perform the MTT cell viability assay, normal human primary osteoblasts and A549 lung cancer cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 103 cells/well in 96-well culture plates, The initial seeding density was optimized at 1 × 103 cells/well to ensure linear growth and prevent premature over-confluency or contact inhibition in untreated control wells during the extended 48–72 h incubation window.and they were given a full day to adhere., cells were exposed to aqueous seed extract and AS-ZnO-NPs at serially diluted ranges between 1.5 and 100 μg/mL. The cells were incubated for a further four hours after the addition of MTT reagent (0.5 mg/mL; 10 μL per well). A microplate spectrophotometer was used to measure optical density at 570 nm (reference 655 nm) after 150 μL of DMSO was used to dissolve formazan crystals per well (18, 19). Dose-response curve fitting was used to calculate IC₀1 values. (18,19) The test was run in triplicate, and percentage inhibition values were used to calculate the results. For morphological assessment, A549 cells were seeded at 70% confluence. They were treated with AS-ZnO-NPs and seed extract at their respective IC₅₀ concentrations for 48 h at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. Inverted phase-contrast microscopy (Nikon ECLIPSE Ti—S, Japan) at 20× magnification was used to visualize gross morphological changes.

For nuclear morphological assessment (DAPI staining), treated and untreated A549 cells were stained with DAPI (1 μg/mL) in the dark for 10 min after being fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100. After three PBS washes, the cells were placed on glass slides and examined using fluorescence microscopy (excitation 405 nm, emission 461 nm) to evaluate the nuclear fragmentation and chromatin condensation that are indicative of apoptosis 18.

In order to measure reactive oxygen species (ROS), A549 cells were exposed to AS-Zn-NPs for 48 h at 37 °C at IC50 concentrations. The cells were stained with H₂DCFDA (10 μM) in PBS for 30 min in the dark. They were then washed, mounted on glass slides, and visualized by fluorescence microscopy. Quantitative analysis of mean fluorescence intensity was performed using ImageJ software on at least 50 cells per treatment condition 19.

Statistical analysis

Origin 6.0 software (USA) was used for all statistical analyses. The quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software. The mean values and standard deviations (SD) of three repeated readings for every sample and concentration are used to express the data. A p-value of less than 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Results

Physicochemical characterization and size analysis

The synthesis of AS-ZnO-NPs was confirmed by UV–Vis spectroscopy, which showed a characteristic surface plasmon resonance (SPR) peak at 387 nm (Fig. 1A), indicating successful reduction of Zn2+ ions. To evaluate the optical properties further, the optical band gap (Eg) of the synthesized AS-ZnO-NPs was determined using the Tauc relation: (αhν)2 = A(hν - Eg) where α is the absorption coefficient, hν is the photon energy, and A is a constant. By extrapolating the linear portion of the Tauc plot between (αhν)2 and photon energy (hν)to the x-axis, the optical band gap was calculated to be 3.3 eV (Fig. 1A).10 This slight blue shift compared to bulk ZnO (3.37 eV) confirms the quantum confinement effect and successful nano-dimensional fabrication. Energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) study of the metallic zinc oxide nanoparticles quantified the elemental composition as zinc (82.15%) and oxygen (17.85%). The minor deviation from the theoretical Zn:O atomic ratio is attributed to the presence of oxygen-rich biofunctional organic residues from the seed extract capping the nanoparticle surface.20 A comparatively uniform size distribution was indicated by dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements, which revealed a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.214 and an average hydrodynamic diameter of 53.28 nm (Fig. 1E). In contrast, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis (Fig. 1F) visualized significantly smaller nanoparticle cores appearing as spherical structures with no evidence of aggregation. To provide a rigorous statistical evaluation, the particle size distribution histogram (Fig. 1G) was systematically constructed by measuring the diameters of over 100 distinct nanoparticles from multiple TEM micrographs using ImageJ software. The analysis revealed a narrow size distribution profile with a well-defined Gaussian fitting, establishing the mean particle core diameter at 13.2 ± 2 nm (Mean ± SD). FTIR spectroscopy-

The chemical composition and surface functionalization of the bio-fabricated AS-ZnO-NPs were comprehensively analyzed using FTIR spectroscopy to pinpoint the precise biomolecules in charge of the stabilization and reduction process. The existence of flavonoids and phenolic compounds from the Alangium salvifolium extract is confirmed by a dominant broad peak at 3430.1 cm−1, which is a sign of stretching vibrations in O—H. By supplying electrons to convert zinc ions (Zn+2) into zinc oxide nanoparticles, these polyphenols serve as primary reducing agents. Anti-bacterial properties of aqueous seed extract and AS-ZnO-NPs

Zone of inhibition assays demonstrated potent activity of AS-ZnO-NPs against all tested bacterial pathogens (Table 1). The aqueous seed extract alone exhibited modest antibacterial activity against S. aureus, B. subtilis, E. coli, and K. pneumoniae with zones of inhibition of 8, 7, 6, and 5 mm, correspondingly. In contrast, AS-ZnO-NPs produced substantially larger inhibition zones of 26 ± 0.5, 24 ± 1, 20 ± 0.05, and 18 ± 0.5 mm against the same organisms, respectively. Levofloxacin (positive control) produced zones of 16 ± 1.5, 15 ± 1.25, 11 ± 1.5, and 10 ± 0.25 mm, proving that AS-ZnO-NPs were highly effective against these strains under the tested in vitro conditions. DAPI-stained nuclear morphology

A549 cells treated with AS-Zn-NPs (IC₅₀ value) at 37 °C for 48 h and subsequently stained with DAPI dye exhibited potent apoptotic effects characterized by condensed chromatin and nuclei with dark blue fluorescence. This phenomenon indicates increased cell membrane permeability and nuclear dysfunction compared to untreated cells.

ROS estimation

To investigate the probable mechanism underlying AS-ZnO-NP-induced cytotoxicity, intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation was evaluated in A549 cells using the oxidation-sensitive fluorescent probe H₂DCFDA (Fig. 4E–H). Quantitative analysis revealed that, in comparison to untreated control cells, AS-ZnO-NP-treated cells showed a significant, dose-dependent rise in intracellular ROS levels (Fig. 4M). One of the primary postulated ROS-associated pathways through which nanomaterials induce oxidative stress and subsequent cellular anomalies in cancer cells was thus highlighted.3, 27, 28

Fluorescence microscopy further supported these findings, as treated A549 cells exhibited intense green fluorescence along with disrupted cellular morphology, indicating oxidative stress–associated cellular damage. In contrast, control cells retained normal cellular morphology and showed minimal fluorescence intensity. Elevated intracellular ROS levels are generally believed to promote oxidative modifications, potentially contributing to downstream apoptotic pathways rather than representing a definitively proven absolute causal mechanism.29, 30 Every experiment was carried out three times, and fluorescence intensities were quantified. Furthermore, AS-ZnO-NPs may penetrate cancer cells and enhance intracellular ROS generation, which could potentially participate in the observed nuclear fragmentation and anticancer activity in A549 cells.26, 30, 31, 32

Discussion

Plant extract-based biogenic production of nanoparticles has become a viable and superior substitute for traditional chemical methods, primarily because of the “bio-organic corona” that naturally coats nanoparticle surfaces.3, 6 In the current research, the successful green synthesis of Zinc Oxide nanoparticles (AS-ZnO-NPs) was achieved using Alangium salvifolium seed extract, a plant well-recognized in traditional medicine for its diverse phytochemical profile (Fig. 5).4, 33 The physicochemical characterization via UV–Vis spectroscopy confirmed a characteristic SPR peak at 387 nm, consistent with previously reported ZnO-NPs synthesized through green routes.8, 34 The transition electron microscopy (TEM) revealed a core size of 13.2 ± 2 nm, while DLS showed a larger hydrodynamic diameter of 53.28 nm. The noticeable variation between the average hydrodynamic diameter obtained via DLS and the core size visualized through TEM is a well-documented phenomenon in biogenic nanotechnology. While TEM analysis captures the solid, high-density crystalline metallic core under dry conditions, DLS measures the fluid-state hydrodynamic volume, which includes the core along with the hydration jacket and the thick capping layer of bulky, oxygen-rich biofunctional organic residues from the Alangium salvifolium seeds.6, 34, 35Fig. 5Synthesis Mechanism of Alangium salvifolium Biofunctionalized Zinc Oxide Nanoparticles.Fig. 5

Conclusion

This study established a rapid, one-step, and sustainable green synthesis protocol for Zinc Oxide nanoparticles using Alangium salvifolium seed extract. The AS-ZnO-NPs showed broad-spectrum antibacterial activity, with MIC values much lower than those of Levofloxacin. This supports their potential as an alternative for targeting multidrug-resistant pathogens. They also showed selective cytotoxicity against A549 lung cancer cells via ROS-mediated apoptosis, while remaining safe toward normal human osteoblasts.

Combining A. salvifolium phytochemicals with ZnO enhances colloidal stability and improves therapeutic effects through strong “nano-bio” interactions. These results support the development of affordable, plant-based nanotherapeutics. Future studies should focus on molecular pathways and in vivo biocompatibility to help bring these nanoparticles into clinical use for the treatment of infectious diseases and cancer.
