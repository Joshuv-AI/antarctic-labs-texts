Trends and inequalities in the global burden of alzheimer’s disease and other dementias, 1990–2021: a Chinese perspective

Background

Dementia is a syndrome characterized by brain disease that typically leads to chronic and progressive cognitive decline, often accompanied by impairments in emotional control, social behavior, and motivation [1]. Among all causes of dementia, Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most common and leading contributor [2]. Several studies have projected that the burden of AD and other dementias (ADODs) will increase rapidly worldwide. Specifically, a recent study estimated that the global dementia population would rise from 57.4 million in 2019 to 152.8 million in 2050 [3]. In 2019, the global economic burden of dementia was approximately $2.8 trillion, with projections that it could reach $16.9 trillion in 2050 [4]. Common co-occurring symptoms of dementia such as declines in cognitive function, decreased ability to perform daily activities, and increased neuropsychiatric symptoms, impose additional burdens beyond treatment costs on families, institutions, and countries, making dementia a critical public health concern.

The burden of dementia is unequally distributed worldwide. More than two-thirds of individuals with dementia live in low- and middle-income countries [5], and by 2050, 65% of global dementia burden is estimated to be concentrated in those regions, compared to only 18% in 2019 [4]. In the last 30 years, the absolute number of ADODs cases in China has increased nearly 3.5 times [6]. A cross-sectional study estimated that 15.07 million people aged 60 and older in China had dementia, with 9.83 million diagnosed with AD [7]. However, most global dementia care expenses are concentrated in high-income countries, and dementia research is also primarily centered on these regions. This imbalance necessitates more research targeting low- and middle-income countries to ensure that the characterization of dementia fully reflects the involvement and specificities of diverse populations [8].

The Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study (GBD) is the largest and most comprehensive effort to measure health loss across different locations and over time [9]. While several studies have examined the burden of ADODs, they relied on relatively older data without accounting for the impact of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic [3, 6, 10, 11], analyzed one burden metric [3, 11], or focused solely on specific regions [10]. The latest GBD 2021 reassessed health loss for ADODs by age, sex, and year in 204 countries and territories, providing a robust basis for insights into global health trends and challenges. More importantly, GBD 2021 is one of the first studies to comprehensively assess the burden and trends of dementia during the first two years of the COVID-19 pandemic, given the profound changes in global health [12]. However, there are currently few studies on ADODs based on GBD 2021, and many lack detailed insights into temporal trends.

Given the growing global burden of ADODs and inequalities in its distribution, it is of great necessity to analyze these factors comprehensively. In this study, we utilized the latest GBD 2021 data on ADODs to evaluate (1) the epidemiology at global, regional, and national levels; (2) temporal trends from 1990 to 2021 from overall, local, and multi-dimensional perspectives; (3) spatial distribution through spatial autocorrelation analysis; (4) burden decomposition based on aging, population size, prevalence, case fatality, and disease severity; and (5) cross-country absolute and relative gradient inequalities. This study evaluates the global burden of ADODs while incorporating a Chinese perspective, offering a nuanced understanding of how China’s status quo fits into the global landscape in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Methods

Data source

All data used in this study were obtained from GBD 2021, downloaded from the GBD result tool on the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) website, University of Washington, Seattle, Washington, USA (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/) [9]. Health data from hospitals, governments, surveys, and other databases around the world were gathered. All input sources were cataloged in the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx) GBD sources tool. The collected data were analyzed using spatiotemporal Gaussian process regression, allowing for smoothing across age, time, and location for regions with incomplete datasets. To correct for data bias caused by varying case definitions and study methodologies across countries, the Meta-Regression with Bayesian priors, Regularization, and Trimming (MR-BRT) program was employed. In GBD 2021, incidence data were newly incorporated into the DisMod model of ADODs, as only prevalence data were used previously. Additionally, excess mortality rate data were also included to better inform trends in incidence and prevalence over time. The detailed methodologies applied in the GBD 2021 have been comprehensively described in other sources [13].

In this study, estimates and 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) for incidence, prevalence, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) of ADODs were extracted from GBD 2021. UIs mean the range of values that reflects the certainty of an estimate. In GBD, every estimate is calculated 1,000 times from distributions. The 95% UI is determined by the 25th and 975th value of the 1,000 values after ordering them from smallest to largest. Larger UIs can result from limited data availability, small studies, and conflicting data, while smaller UIs can result from extensive data availability, large studies, and data that are consistent across sources. DALYs measure the number of healthy years lost due to early death and disability, with one DALY indicating one lost year of healthy life. All rates were reported per 100,000 population. The Sociodemographic Index (SDI), a composite measure of income, education, and fertility, was also used to quantify a country or territory’s level of development. It includes five levels based on SDI quintiles: high, high-middle, middle, low-middle, and low. SDI values range from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater socioeconomic development.

Case definition

In GBD 2021, ADODs are defined as a progressive, degenerative, and chronic neurological disorder characterized by cognitive dysfunctions that interfere with daily functioning [9]. The GBD 2021 definition of ADODs used the codes of F00-F02.0, F02.8-F03.91, F06.2, G30-G31.1, and G31.8-G32.89 in International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision (ICD-10), and the codes of 290-290.9, 294.0-294.9, 331-331.2, 331.6-331.7, 331.82, and 331.89–331.9 in ICD-9.

Descriptive analysis

Comparisons by region, year, and sex group were performed to provide a comprehensive understanding of the burden of ADODs. The case numbers and age-standardized rates (ASRs) of incidence (ASIR), prevalence (ASPR), and DALYs (ASDR) for ADODs in both sexes from 1990 to 2021 were illustrated and compared at the global, regional (21 GBD geographical regions), and national (204 countries and territories) levels (supplementary methods), as well as across five SDI quintiles. In addition, we compared ASIR, ASPR, and ASDR between males and females at global and regional levels.

Temporal trends analysis

This study aimed to explore the trends of ADODs from both global and local perspectives, using estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) analysis, joinpoint regression analysis, and age-period-cohort analysis.

First, we used EAPC to quantify the overall trend in the burden of ADODs. Standardization is crucial when comparing groups with different age structures or when a group’s age profile changes over time. As a result, the trend in ASR, measured through EAPC, provides a more reliable metric for tracking shifts in disease patterns. The model and calculation for EAPC are detailed in supplementary methods. If both the EAPC estimate and its lower 95% confidence interval (CI) are greater than 0, the ASR is considered to be increasing. Conversely, if both the EAPC estimate and its upper 95% CI are less than 0, the ASR is deemed to be decreasing. Otherwise, the ASR is regarded as stable.

Second, we used joinpoint regression analysis to examine the local trends in the burden of ADODs. Joinpoint regression analysis breaks down the overall trend into multiple segments based on inflection points and assesses the magnitude of each epidemiological trend by calculating the annual percentage change (APC) and its 95% CI for each segment. Additionally, the average APC (AAPC) from 1990 to 2021 was calculated by weighting the regression coefficients of each segment according to the width of the intervals [14]. The Monte Carlo permutation method, using 4,499 randomly permuted datasets, was used to determine the AAPC and its 95% CI, with the overall asymptotic significance level adjusted using Bonferroni correction [15]. An increasing trend is indicated if both the APC/AAPC estimate and its lower 95% CI are greater than 0. Conversely, a decreasing trend is indicated if both the APC/AAPC estimate and its upper 95% CI are less than 0. If neither condition is met, the trend is considered stable.

Furthermore, we analyzed trends in the burden of ADODs related to age, period, and cohort effects, starting with an investigation of potential two-factor interactions among these variables. Determining the distinct effects of age, period, and cohort on incidence and prevalence risk is challenging due to their interplay. Therefore, we employed an age-period-cohort model that incorporates all three temporal dimensions to estimate their effects on incidence and prevalence risk over time [16]. In this model, age effects reflect changes in variables throughout an individual’s lifetime, period effects capture the influence of environmental factors on the entire population, and cohort effects represent variable changes among individuals who experienced similar life events due to being born in the same period. To address the inherent identification problem in APC models, we applied the intrinsic estimator (IE) method to provide a relatively efficient estimation of differing effects across the three temporal trends [17]. The age-period-cohort model with the IE method was detailed in supplementary methods. Data were recoded into consecutive 5-year age groups (40–44, 45–49,…, 90–94, 95 plus), 5-year periods from 1990 to 2019 (1990–1994, 1995–1999,…, 2010–2014, 2015–2019) and 2020–2021, and 5-year birth cohorts (1895–1899, 1900–1904,…,1975–1979, 1980–1984) to estimate net age, period, and cohort effects on incidence and prevalence risk. The estimated coefficients derived from the APC model using the IE method were then transformed into relative risks (RRs) to quantify the incidence and prevalence risks associated with specific ages, periods, or cohorts, relative to the average combined level across all ages, periods, or cohorts.

Spatial autocorrelation analysis

Spatial autocorrelation was used to evaluate the global spatial distribution of the ASIR, ASPR, and ASDR of ADODs in 1990 and 2021. The Global Moran’s I index was used to calculate spatial correlation across all relevant regions and to determine whether the factors were clustered [18]. In addition, we conducted a Hot-Cold Spots analysis, a local spatial autocorrelation statistic, to identify the presence of specific spatial regions with high or low risk of dementia [19]. A “hot spot” represents a high-risk region surrounded by other high-risk areas, while a “cold spot” represents a low-risk region surrounded by other low-risk areas.

Decomposition analysis

To better understand the factors influencing changes in DALYs due to ADODs from 1990 to 2021, we conducted a decomposition analysis to examine the contribution of aging, population size, prevalence, case fatality, and disease severity, comparing China and five SDI quintiles. The number and overall differences in DALYs from 1990 to 2021 were provided in supplementary methods.

Cross-country inequality analysis

To measure distributive inequality, two standard metrics were used to assess both absolute and relative inequality in the burden of ADODs across countries [20]: the slope index of inequality (SII) and the concentration index (CIX). The SII was computed by regressing national DALY rates for all age groups against a relative position scale linked to sociodemographic development, defined by the midpoint of the cumulative range of populations ranked by SDI. The CIX was calculated by integrating the area under the Lorenz concentration curve, based on the cumulative fraction of DALYs and the population’s cumulative distribution ranked by SDI.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and visualizations were performed using the R (v4.3.3, https://www.r-project.org/). Joinpoint regression analysis was conducted using the Joinpoint Regression Program (v4.9.1.0, Statistical Research and Applications Branch, National Cancer Institute, MD, USA). Age-period-cohort analysis and calculation of CIX were performed using STATA (v16.0, StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). The spatial autocorrelation analysis was conducted in ArcGIS (v10.8.1, ESRI, Redlands, CA, USA). A probability value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Descriptive analysis of ADODs burden at global, regional, and national levels

Globally, the number of incident cases, prevalent cases, and DALYs for ADODs showed dramatically increasing trends over time. In 2021, the number of incident cases increased to 9,837,055.84 (95% UI: 8,620,519.2 to 11,163,699.62), the number of prevalent cases increased to 56,856,688.21 (95% UI: 49,382,064.01 to 64,977,511.92), and the DALYs increased to 36,332,686.74 (95% UI: 17,237,624.04 to 76,873,276.22) for both sexes, with a noticeably higher number in females than in males (Figure S1). The ASIR, ASPR, and ASDR remained relatively stable with small fluctuation from 1990 to 2020, but exhibited significant increases from 2020 to 2021. In 2021, the ASIR, ASPR, and ASDR per 100,000 were 119.76 (95% UI: 104.96 to 135.89), 694.01 (95% UI: 602.88 to 794.08), and 450.98 (95% UI: 212.69 to 950.16) respectively (Table S1, Table S2, and Table S3).

Regionally, among five SDI quintiles in 2021, the number of incident cases, prevalent cases, and DALYs for ADODs were highest in the high SDI quintile, while the ASIR, ASPR, and ASDR were highest in the high-middle SDI quintile for both sexes. Among the 21 GBD regions in 2021, East Asia had the highest number of incident cases, prevalent cases, DALYs, ASIR, and ASPR for both sexes, while central sub-Saharan Africa had the highest ASDR (Table S1, Table S2, and Table S3). The numbers and ASR of incident cases, prevalent cases, and DALYs for males and females were provided in Table S1, Table S2, and Table S3.

At the national level, for both sexes, the incidence, prevalence, and DALYs of ADODs varied significantly across countries. In 2021, China recorded the highest number of incident cases, prevalent cases, and DALYs, as well as the highest ASIR and ASPR. Meanwhile, the highest ASDR in 2021 was found in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (Fig. 1, Table S4, Table S5, and Table S6).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to present a comprehensive evaluation of the burden, trends, and inequalities in ADODs both globally and in China. The global burden for ADODs increased from 1990 to 2021. Aging and population size were two major factors driving the changes in DALYs. Furthermore, countries with higher SDI values shouldered a disproportionately large burden of dementia, with growing inequalities among countries over time. The increasing burden and distributive inequalities globally underscored the urgent need for public health planning and policy development to control and manage ADODs.

Globally, the aging population is growing due to increasing life expectancy, and individuals with dementia are living longer after being diagnosed, which may explain the increasing numbers and ASR of incident cases, prevalent cases, and DALYs [21]. The markedly increased burden of ADODs from 2020 to 2021 may under the influence of the COVID-19 pandemic: (1) the direct health impacts of COVID-19 through the interplay of inflammation and neurobiochemical interactions, oxidative stress, and genetic factors [22], (2) the indirect health impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic, such as mental health and social isolation [23], and (3) after effects from overburdened health systems and uncoordinated policy responding to the pandemic [13]. However, despite the growing burden, the EAPCs of ASIR, ASPR, and ASDR were fairly small, likely because the increases in risk factors such as obesity, high fasting plasma glucose, and smoking were counterbalanced by improvements in educational attainment, healthcare, and decreases in case fatality and disease severity [3].

Among GBD regions, countries, and territories, the highest ASIR and ASPR in 2021, along with growing trends, were observed in East Asia, particularly in China, which were consistent with previous studies [24]. Compared to developed countries, the economic development and infrastructure construction in China started later, with a relative lack of health services especially in cognitive and mental health [25]. In addition, an increasing incidence and prevalence of dementia have been reported among the Chinese elderly, especially those with low education [26]. Although national educational attainment has improved, certain disparities still exist between rural and urban areas [27]. Increased metabolic risks, particularly diabetes, hypertension, and smoking, might also play a role [28]. Notably, a study based on GBD 2019 reported an increasing EAPC of ASDR [29], which was found to be stable in our study. This inconsistency may be attributed to updates in each GBD cycle, in which the entire time series is re-estimated using all available data to ensure the most complete and highly comparable estimates [9]. In addition, gradual improvements in China’s healthcare services, along with prevention and control strategies [30], may also slow down the rapid growth in ADODs burden: the higher proportion of years lived with disability (YLDs) in DALYs reflects less premature death from dementia and its complications as a result of the higher quality of clinical management and care [31], and the YLDs: DALYs ratio in China has gradually increased and stayed above the global average [29].

In 2021, the highest ASDR for ADODs was observed in central sub-Saharan Africa and in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. A previous study has suggested that population growth was a main contributor to the increase in dementia cases in sub-Saharan Africa [3]. Additionally, other factors such as physical inactivity, poorly ventilated kitchen, a history of central nervous system infections, and chronic headaches were associated with a higher prevalence of dementia [32]. More importantly, low education was also considered a main risk factor [32], as a considerable proportion of illiterate adults live in sub-Saharan Africa [33]. Regarding subtypes of dementia in Africa, AD, vascular dementia, and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) associated neurodegenerative disorders are leading contributors [34], and there is a disproportionately high burden of HIV-associated neurodegenerative disorders in sub-Saharan Africa [35]. However, a recent study in Kinshasa of the Democratic Republic of the Congo reported that the prevalence of dementia was comparable to that of other developing countries and central African countries [36]. We suggested that the high burden of DALYs in these regions might be a result of inadequate healthcare services and a lack of management of dementia-related risk factors. Notably, limitations such as low-quality data collection methods, inadequate data systems, and varying definitions of dementia may have influenced the estimates [37]. Considering the rapidly growing trends of ASDR in South Asia, particularly in India, these trends are likely to correlate with demographic shifts, including increased life expectancy, decreased fertility rates, and increasing prevalence of vascular dysfunction [38].

When separating the overall temporal trends into multiple subsegments, we observed generally increasing trends for ASIR, ASPR, and ASDR in China. Despite some fluctuations, these findings align with previous joinpoint analysis [25] and may be attributed to environmental changes, the accelerated pace of life, and irregular daily habits [6, 25]. The COVID-19 pandemic further posed significant challenges to healthcare systems, particularly in China. China’s pandemic policies, including strict lockdown, contact tracing, and isolation of patients with COVID-19 and their contacts [39], were effective in minimizing transmission and infections but had unintended negative consequences on mental health [40]. Studies have demonstrated that social isolation, including limiting outdoor activities, canceling events and gatherings, and physical inactivity, was correlated with accelerated cognitive decline and the exacerbation of neuropsychiatry symptoms [41]. From caregivers’ perspectives, social isolation, physical inactivity, and sleep disturbance due to lockdown policies were closely linked to increased caregivers’ burden [42], further intensifying the burden of dementia. Chinese cultural norms, particularly the values of filial piety in Confucianism, often place the responsibility of senior care on family members and relatives, leading to a preference for home care. Only childless or low-income elderly individuals seek public care, and many Chinese elderly often feel marginalized and face social stigma when considering nursing home admission [43]. Moreover, Chinese caregivers often refrain from discussing family matters with outsiders due to the stigma and fear of discrimination, limiting their access to social support [43].

In our study, the burden of ADODs was higher among females than males both globally and in China, despite the higher prevalence of vascular risk factors in males. Such disparities suggest potentially counteracting mechanisms [3]: First, females have higher life expectancies than their male counterparts [44]. Second, lower education levels among females may also play a role, as higher educational attainment is associated with better cognitive reserve and lower dementia risk [45]. Third, biological factors such as neuroinflammation, bioenergetic metabolism, sex hormones, and genetic risk factors have been identified as contributing to sex-specific differences in dementia risk [46].

Exploring the temporal trends of ADODs with a focus on age, period, and cohort effects provides a deeper understanding of disease epidemiology [47]. Our current study on age effects suggested that both relative incidence and prevalence risks increased with age. It is well-established that age is one of the most evident risk factors for dementia worldwide [48], which may be due to combined effects of cumulative lifetime exposure to different risk factors and age-related biological changes. In addition, multimorbidity is strongly associated with subsequent dementia, making it crucial to identify high-risk individuals for dementia prevention [49]. Besides, the relative incidence and prevalence risks were found to increase as periods progressed, and period effects are usually attributed to a series of environmental factors and historical events [25]. As periods progressed, lifestyle went through tremendous changes with urbanization and economic development, such as poorer sleep hygiene and unhealthy eating habits. These changes increased the risk of developing various chronic diseases, which are highly correlated with the onset of ADODs [50]. According to the 2020 report of the Lancet Commission on Dementia Prevention, Intervention, and Care, up to 45% of dementia cases could potentially be prevented through interventions targeting modifiable risk factors [51], and addressing these factors through public health interventions is a pathway towards reducing dementia prevalence [51]. Regarding the cohort effect, decreasing trends in the RRs of incidence and prevalence in later-born cohorts were observed. One possible explanation might be better educational attainment and improved awareness of health and dementia prevention among more recent generations. A study investigating the relationship between education and later-life cognitive function underscored the crucial role of education in promoting cognitive health and reducing dementia risk [52]. Another possible reason is that later-born cohorts may have benefited from improvements in dementia management compared to earlier cohorts. However, although numerous risk factors for dementia have been identified, there is a lack of convincing evidence that modifying these factors can prevent dementia [53]. Ongoing and future research will undoubtedly provide new insights into effective prevention and treatment strategies for ADODs.

As shown in our spatial autocorrelation analysis, the Middle East, North Africa, and sub-Saharan Africa were hot spots in ASIR, ASPR, and ASDR both in 1990 and 2021. The estimated dementia burden in the Middle East and North Africa was higher or comparable to the global level [10], driven by population aging, population growth [3], earlier identification of dementia as a result of recent advancements in healthcare coverage [54], and prolonged exposure to the environmental toxins [10, 55]. In contrast, while Europe and High-income North America were also identified as hot spots, these regions exhibited decreasing trends in ASIR, ASPR, and ASDR. Researchers have reported declining trends in ASIR of dementia in high-income countries [21], which may partially be explained by improved management of vascular risk factors, rising educational levels, earlier diagnosis of dementia, and substantial investments in population health over the life course [56].

The decomposition analysis of DALYs for ADODs revealed that the increase in burden was primarily driven by population growth and aging across all five SDI quintiles. Notably, population growth had a more pronounced impact in regions with lower SDI, while aging had a greater effect in higher SDI regions, indicating that demographic patterns affect ADODs burden differently depending on SDI. According to the latest United Nations reports on world population and aging, the global population is expected to continue growing for the next 50 or 60 years [57], and thus population growth will remain as a major concern for dementia management worldwide. In particular, most least-developed countries currently have a relatively slow pace of demographic transition but are expected to experience accelerated population aging in the near future [58], and aging will likely become an increasingly critical issue in countries with low and low-middle SDI. Notably, in the high SDI quintile, the prevalence component had a negative effect on DALYs in our decomposition analysis, in line with the declining trends in dementia burden observed in many developed countries [21]. In the high-middle and middle SDI quintiles, advancements in healthcare coverage and dementia control efforts may contribute to the reduction in case fatality and disease severity. The decomposition pattern in China was similar to that observed in high-middle SDI quintiles, which is consistent with China’s SDI classification in the GBD database.

According to our cross-country inequality analysis, the burden of ADODs is disproportionately concentrated in countries with high sociodemographic development levels. Developed countries are facing more severe challenges of aging, thus bearing a heavier burden of dementia. Under the shifts in demographic patterns, policymakers and public health authorities have an urgent need to develop strategies for future preparedness and healthcare planning for ADODs [12].

Comprehensive and in-depth investigations deepen our understanding of ADODs. These findings provide valuable references for policymakers in dementia prevention and control, enhance patients’ and caregivers’ understanding of the disease, and improve the diagnostic and treatment capabilities of healthcare professionals worldwide. Despite remarkable advances in dementia diagnosis and treatment in recent years, a cure remains unknown. In this context, the saying “prevention is better than cure” is more than a trite expression, but a call to action [59]. Pragmatic strategies for reducing dementia risk are essential for global health through local, national, and international efforts. For China, the Health China Action (2019–2030) includes “a decrease in the growth rate of dementia in old age among people aged 65 and above” as an outcome indicator in the health promotion action for older adult, and the National Health Commission issued the Work Plan for Exploring Specialized Services for Dementia Prevention and Treatment to instruct national and local efforts to provide comprehensive prevention and treatment of dementia [60]. Future studies can investigate the disparities on ADODs burden between rural and urban areas in China.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations should be noted. First, the trends and inequalities of burden observed in this study are limited by data source availability. The lack of high-quality civil registration and vital statistics systems in many developing countries introduces significant uncertainty in demographic estimates, which often rely heavily on modeled projections [12]. Second, the burden of ADODs may have been underestimated due to misdiagnosis and underdiagnosis, especially in less developed countries. Third, the lack of detailed dementia classification prevented further analysis for subtypes of dementia. Additionally, the time lag in GBD reporting means that the full impact of COVID-19 and other influence factors might not be fully understood for several years. Although studies have suggested the risks and excess mortality with dementia varied throughout the pandemic [61], an extended and long-term observation will be essential on ADODs worldwide.

Conclusion

In summary, ADODs constitute a significant public health concern. The global burden of ADODs has shown a generally increasing trend from 1990 to 2021. The increases in the burden of ADODs were mainly attributed to population growth and aging. Countries with high SDI face a disproportionately large burden of dementia, and the inequalities among countries based on SDI exacerbated over time. These findings underscore the significant challenges in the prevention and control of ADODs, emphasizing the need for targeted strategies to improve public health systems and reduce disparities worldwide.
