Subsonic phase front propagation in martensitic transitions of 1,2,4,5-tetrabromobenzene single crystals
Authors: Ken Takazawa, Jun-ichi Inoue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Martensitic transitions in organic crystals, which are diffusionless structural phase transitions between polymorphs, are topics of current interest. These transitions proceed by fast propagation of the phase front (phase boundary) across the crystals and induce fascinating phenomena, such as thermosalient effect and superelasticity, which have potential applications in molecular devices. Revealing the mechanism of these transitions is of crucial importance for understanding the origins of these phenomena and for their device applications. To this end, here we observe the spatio-temporal evolution of the phase front in temperature-induced martensitic transition in 1,2,4,5-tetrabromobenezen (TBB) crystals. We observed subsonic phase front propagation across the crystals with the velocity of up to ~800 m/s. The analysis using a nonlinear lattice model based on Landau’s theory of phase transitions showed that the phase front motion is described as a propagating solitary wave in a non-conservative system. Our results revealed the essential factors governing organic martensitic transitions and novel insights into the transition mechanism.

Introduction

Structural phase transitions between polymorphs of organic crystals induced by external stimuli, such as temperature and pressure, have attracted increasing attention. These transitions give rise to fascinating phenomena, such as the thermosalient effect, superelasticity, and shape memory effect, which have potential applications in organic crystal-based devices1–32. Crystallographic, thermodynamic, and spectroscopic investigations of these phase transitions have demonstrated that they are first-order diffusionless transitions that proceed with small, albeit cooperative, motion of the constituent molecules. Therefore, they are referred to as organic martensitic transitions by analogy with martensitic transitions in Fe alloys33,34. The progression of the martensitic transition is characterized by nucleation followed by the propagation of the interface between the new and original phases (the phase front). This is in contrast to transitions accompanied by diffusive atomic/molecular motions, which proceed with the nucleation of multiple small domains followed by the growth of these domains (the nucleation and growth mechanisms).

Although numerous studies on martensitic transitions in metals and alloys have been performed for many years, their organic counterparts are emerging research areas, and the mechanisms of the transitions are topics of ongoing research. A promising approach to gain crucial information for understanding the mechanisms is direct and real-time observation of the phase front propagation. The velocity, propagation direction with respect to the crystallographic axes, and width of the phase front are closely related to the thermodynamic and elastic properties of the crystal as well as to the packing and interactions of molecules in the crystal. All these properties together govern the dynamics of phase transitions. Therefore, the detailed analysis of phase front dynamics by employing an appropriate theoretical model reveals the essential factors governing the phase transitions.

However, phase front propagation has been directly observed for only a few organic martensitic crystals to date13–15,21 because of the following experimental difficulties: for most martensitic transitions, the new phase cannot be clearly distinguished from the original phase with standard optical measurements. Because the molecular structure of individual molecules remains nearly unchanged throughout the transition, the color and/or optical density of the new phase is also almost the same as that of the original phase. In addition, the martensitic transition is generally a fast process and is often accompanied by an explosive crystal motion, such as jumping and sudden cracking (the thermosalient effect)1–8,10–19. Therefore, to observe phase front propagation, a measurement technique with a high time resolution is required. For this reason, the phase front propagation has been successfully observed only for non-thermosalient crystals so far13–15,21.

Here, we developed a technique for acquiring an image of a crystal during a martensitic phase transition, where the new and original phases are clearly distinguished. By combining this technique with an ultrahigh-speed camera, we observed the phase front propagation in single crystals of 1,2,4,5-tetrabromobenzene (TBB, Fig. 1a). A TBB crystal is a typical thermosalient crystal, which exhibits martensitic transition accompanied by an explosive crystal motion1,35. We observed ballistic phase front propagation from one edge to the other across a single crystal of TBB. The velocity of the phase front reached ~800 m/s, which is 5–6 orders of magnitude higher than those previously reported for martensitic transitions of non-thermosalient crystals. The measured velocities were up to ~60% of the sound velocity (phonon velocity) in the TBB crystals, i.e., phase front propagation was subsonic. This finding indicates that the observed phenomena are not adequately described by linear elastic wave models. To account for the subsonic propagation, we employed a one-dimensional nonlinear lattice model that explicitly takes into account the fact that the transition between the two phases is first-order. Results

TBB single crystals

A single crystal of TBB was reported to transform from β- to γ-phase near room temperature, around 40 °C upon heating1,35–37. The crystal structures of both the β- and γ-phases belong to a monoclinic system with the space group P21/n. The lattice parameters are a = 10.2355(5) Å, b = 10.7193(4) Å, c = 4.0233(2) Å, β = 100.184(5)°, V = 434.47(4) Å3, and Z = 2 for the β-phase at T = 30 °C, whereas they are a = 9.8719(5) Å, b = 11.0780(5) Å, c = 4.0549(3) Å, β = 100.585(5)°, V = 435.90(4) Å3, and Z = 2 for the γ-phase at T = 40 °C37. As shown in Fig. 1b, c, the distortion of the unit cell through the β → γ transition is anisotropic, i.e., it expands along the b- and c-axes and shrinks along the a-axis. The anisotropic cell distortion is a common characteristic of organic martensitic phase transitions33. The changes in the lattice constants are −3.55%, +3.35%, and +0.79% along the a-, b-, and c-axes, respectively, whereas that in the unit cell volume is only +0.33% owing to the anisotropy. A crystal in the γ-phase transforms back to the β-phase upon cooling at ~35 °C. The temperature hysteresis of ~5 °C between the β → γ and γ → β transitions indicates that the transition is first-order.

TBB crystals were grown via slow evaporation from a saturated ethanol solution. The crystals had an elongated plate-like shape with a length along the long axis of up to ~5 mm and a thickness of a few hundred micrometers (Fig. 1d). The crystal faces were identified based on previously reported assignments38 and Bravais–Friedel–Donnay–Harke simulations (Fig. 1e)39. The primary crystal plane is (1 −1 0) and contains the crystallographic c-axis (Fig. 1f). Using a cross-polarized light microscope, the extinction direction was observed to decline by ~15° with respect to the c-axis, as previously reported (Fig. 1f and right panel of Fig. 1d)38.

Effect of phase transitions on polarization properties

The β-crystals were heated from room temperature across the phase transition temperature (~40 °C) under a polarized light microscope. We observed that the extinction direction slightly rotated upon the β → γ transition. The rotation angle (Δθ) was measured for 15 crystals and determined to be Δθ = 1.55° (s = 0.43). In the following section, we demonstrate that this rotation can be used to observe the spatio-temporal evolutions of the phase transition.

We further examined the effect of phase transition on both A(θ) and B(λ). We observed that the extinction direction rotated by Δθ ≈ 1.55° upon phase transition. Using Eq. 1, the change in A(θ) induced by this rotation ([A(θ + Δθ) − A(θ)]/A(θ)) is calculated as 0.11 and −0.11 at θ = π/8 and 3π/8, respectively. These values approximately agree with the observed changes in the intensities of the transmitted light, i.e., ~11% and ~−12% at θ = π/8 and 3π/8, respectively. This agreement implies that the changes were mostly attributable to those in A(θ). Because B(λ) provides multiple fringes in the spectra and the phase transition induces a small shift of them, the change in the averaged B(θ) over the spectral range is small. The TBB crystals investigated in this study all satisfied the conditions, where the change in B(λ) was negligible for the transmitted light intensity. A detailed analysis of the effect of phase transition on B(λ) is provided in the Supplementary Information.

Direct observation of phase front propagation

We demonstrated that the β- and γ-phases can be distinguished by orienting the crystals at specific angles. Based on this result, we recorded videos of the TBB crystals during the phase transition using a high-speed camera. Figure 3a shows snapshots of a TBB crystal with height and width of 0.6 and 3.6 mm, respectively, recorded by the camera operated at 5.4 × 105 fps with the exposure time for each frame of 1/3,811,764 s (≈262 ns) during the γ → β transition (see also Supplementary Video 1). The crystal was oriented at θ = π/8. The dark region, which corresponds to the β-phase, appeared at the right edge of the crystal and extended toward the left edge of the crystal with maintaining the clear boundary between the dark and bright regions (the phase front). The difference in intensity between the bright and dark regions was approximately 10%, which was consistent with the above analysis. These images clearly indicate that the phase transition proceeds by phase front propagation.Fig. 3Phase front propagation in the phase transition of a TBB crystal recorded with the high-speed camera.a Snapshots recorded during the phase transition. b Black curves: intensities of the transmitted light obtained by integrating the crystal images along the vertical axis of the snapshots. To measure the position of the phase front in each snapshot precisely, the image captured ~10 μs before the phase transition was subtracted from those recorded during the phase transition (insets in Fig. 3b). This procedure eliminates the inhomogeneity of the transmitted light that is unrelated to the phase transition and allows for a clear visualization of the phase front. Then, the intensity of the image was integrated along the vertical axis and plotted as a function of the horizontal position of the crystal (black curves in Fig. 3b). The positions of the phase fronts were determined by fitting a sigmoid function to the curves (red curves in Fig. 3b). The velocity of the phase front (vpf) was then evaluated from the difference of the phase front positions between those in two adjacent frames as vpf = 5.4 × 105 (xn + 1 − xn) m/s, where xn is the position of the phase front in the nth frame (note that n = 1 represents the frame, where the phase front was first captured). The evaluated vpf values for this crystal were plotted against n in the right panel of Fig. 4 (red squares) and were 300–450 m/s. The sigmoid curves in Fig. 3b show that the width of the transition region varies for each snapshot. The width can be affected by many factors, such as the tilt angle of the (0 0 1) plane with respect to the substrate, the movement of the phase front during the exposure time of the camera, and the slight tilt of the phase front with respect to the long axis of the crystal. We performed these measurements and analyses for ~30 crystals with lengths along the [0 0 1] direction ranging from 0.8 to 4.2 mm for both β → γ and γ → β transitions. The transition temperatures were 38 ± 1.5 and 35 ± 1.5 °C for the β → γ and γ → β transitions, respectively. Videos of some of them are provided in the Supplementary Video 2. The following features were observed for all the crystals: (1) the phase transition started at one of the short edges, and the phase front propagated along the [0 0 1] direction; (2) phase front was perpendicular to the [0 0 1] direction; and (3) when the phase transition started from one edge in the β → γ transition induced by heating, it started from the other in the γ → β transition induced by subsequent cooling.

The vpf values were plotted in the left (the β → γ transitions) and right (the γ → β transitions) panels of Fig. 4. For a large crystal, the phase front was captured over several frames before the entire crystal was transformed, providing several data points on the graph, whereas a small crystal provided few data points. Therefore, the number of data points is related to the crystal size. For crystals smaller than ~0.8 mm, the phase front was captured only in one frame or not captured at all. Thus, vpf for such small crystals could not be evaluated. The graph shows that vpf ranges 200–800 m/s for both the β → γ and γ → β transitions, and no clear correlation between vpf and the crystal size was found. We do not have enough data on the correlation between vpf and a crystal thickness. However, we observed a tendency that larger crystals have larger thicknesses. Therefore, the absence of correlation between vpf and the crystal size suggests that vpf does not correlate with the thickness. Because the crystals were heated by thermal contact with the substrate, a temperature gradient along the thickness of the crystals may exist and affect vpf. However, the absence of a correlation between vpf and the thickness further suggests that the effect of the temperature gradient on vpf is minimal.

The fluctuations of vpf with n observed for some crystals may be attributed to the inhomogeneity of crystal quality. The vpf values and their fluctuations are not reproducible. The phase front motions could be observed repeatedly over several heating/cooling cycles for the same crystal. However, the repeated phase transitions caused gradual destruction (cracking and disintegration) of the crystal, leading to low vpf and large fluctuations. Therefore, the vfp values presented in Fig. 4 were all measured for the first heating/cooling cycles.

The fact that the phase front is perpendicular to the [0 0 1] direction suggests that the molecular motions in the (0 0 1) sheet are significantly faster than those in the [0 0 1] direction. Dye et al. studied the anharmonicity of molecular interactions in a TBB crystal in the β-phase and showed that the barrier for molecular motions in the ab plane is lower than that along the c-axis, supporting faster motions in the (0 0 1) sheet41. This indicates that the molecular motions in the (0 0 1) sheet are cooperative and that the phase transition proceeds in a sheet-by-sheet (layer-by-layer) manner (Fig. 5a), as observed for other organic martensitic transitions21,22,42,43. On the other hand, if the cooperative motions occur in the (0 1 0) or (1 0 0) sheets, phase-front propagation should be observed along the width of the crystal (Fig. 5b). This is clearly inconsistent with our observations. This layer-by-layer progression of the phase transition allows us to model a TBB crystal as a one-dimensional chain of interacting masses, which represent the (0 0 1) sheets (Fig. 5c). We observed that the phase transition always started not in the middle but at one of the short edges of the crystals. This is likely attributed to an instability of the edges due to surface free energy. We also observed that when the phase transition started at one of the short edges in the β → γ transition by heating, it started at the other short edge in the β → γ transition by subsequent cooling. This feature is also understood in terms of the surface free energy. The surface free energy of both edges are, to a greater or lesser extent, different, and the β → γ transition started from the edge with the higher energy when the crystal is heated. Upon the phase transition, the energies of both edges lowered. However, the edge with the higher energy in the β-phase should be more stabilized than the others. As a consequence, when the crystal is subsequently cooled, the γ → β transition started at the other edge, which has the higher energy in the γ-phase.

To investigate the mechanism behind the significantly fast phase front propagation, we compare the vpf values with the sound velocity (phonon velocity) in TBB crystals. The anisotropic sound velocities of a TBB single crystal in the β-phase were investigated using Brillouin light scattering41,44. Based on the elastic constants provided in ref. 41, the sound velocities in the [0 0 1] direction were calculated as vL = 2146 m/s, vTA1 = 1283 m/s, and vTA2 = 1469 m/s, where vL and vTA1, 2 are the longitudinal and transverse velocities, respectively (Supplementary Information). Therefore, the observed vpf value of 800 m/s are approximately 40% and 60% of the longitudinal and transverse sound velocities, respectively, indicating that the phase front propagation in the TBB crystals is subsonic.

The vpf values previously measured for martensitic, but non-thermosalient crystals, were on the order of μm/s to mm/s13,21, and therefore, 5–6 orders of magnitude smaller than those observed for TBB crystals. This suggests that the fast phase front propagation is a key factor in the occurrence of the thermosalient effect. The vpf values of these non-thermosalient crystals were analyzed based on the change rates of the volume fractions of the two phases, which are governed by the free energy curve along the transition coordinate45. Yangui et al. successfully observed the phase front motion in the martensitic phase transition of organic-inorganic perovskite crystals (vpf ≈ 1.6 mm/s)46 and qualitatively analyzed the phase front propagation based on the change rates of the volume fractions47.

The vpf values as high as the sound velocity have been reported for inorganic crystals. Mariette et al. performed femtosecond time-resolved X-ray powder diffraction measurements on Ti3O5 nanoparticles during a structural phase transition induced by a short-pulsed laser irradiation48. Detailed analysis of the diffraction data showed that the phase transition is triggered by the precursor photo-induced electronic changes accompanied by cell distortions and proceeds with the motion of the phase front with sound velocity (vpf ≈ 6.5 × 103 m/s). To explain the phase front motion, they employed a one-dimensional model, where a strain wave (an acoustic wave) created by pulsed laser irradiation propagated across the crystal with the velocity of sound, leaving behind the new phase. They demonstrated that experimental observations were quantitatively reproduced using the strain wave model49.

The vpf values on the same order of magnitude as the sound velocity observed for the TBB crystals strongly suggest that the phase front propagation is also associated with the acoustic wave traveling across the crystal. However, the strain wave model is not applicable to TBB crystals because the vpf values are considerably less than the sound velocity. The solutions of a linear wave equation, including strain waves, propagate with an exact sound velocity, in contrast to the subsonic vpf of TBB crystals. Moreover, vpf for the TBB crystals was observed to differ in the range 200–800 m/s depending on the crystal, suggesting that vpf is sensitively influenced by small differences in the intrinsic and/or extrinsic properties of the individual crystals. This feature is hardly accounted for by the linear models. These observations led us to explore a suitable model for describing the phase front propagation in TBB crystals.

Analysis using a nonlinear lattice model

Here, we employ a one-dimensional nonlinear lattice model in accordance with Landau’s phase transition theory. Landau’s theory is based on free energy as a function of an order parameter. The free energy needs to be expanded up to sixth order for a first-order phase transition, whereas the fourth order for a second-order phase transition50. As stated earlier, the transition exhibited by the TBB crystals is of the first order, and the crystal can be modeled as a one-dimensional chain of coupled point masses that represent (0 0 1) sheets (Fig. 5c). Subsequently, a sixth-order on-site potential is incorporated into the mass chain model. Theoretical analyses of this type of model system, according to Lagrangian mechanics, have been conducted by several authors. We followed the work by Gooding et al.51. Quantitative validation of the nonlinear lattice model

Having confirmed the consistency of the model, next, we quantitatively analyzed the phase transition of the TBB crystal using the model. For quantitative analysis, the dimensionless order parameter ϕ must be converted into a quantity with the dimension of length. In the ϕ-space, the displacement between the metastable and stable equilibria is unity (ϕ = 0–1). In an actual crystal, on the other hand, the displacement of the (0 0 1) sheets induced by the phase transition is given by the difference in the lattice constant c between the β- and γ-phases (Δc). Considering this correspondence, it is reasonable to scale ϕ = 1 by Δc (=4.0549–4.0233 Å = 0.0316 Å). Using this conversion relation, and the previously reported thermal analysis data, including V(0) = 330 J/mol (the enthalpy of the phase transition1,55), and ΔE = 2000 J/mol (the barrier height for the transformation37,56), Eg is uniquely determined as Eg = 2.2 × 10−2 from Eqs. 5 to 7 (see Supplementary Information). To assess the validity of this analysis, we compared these results with the experimental data. In our model, γ determines energy dissipation in the system. The corresponding quantity for the actual crystals is the viscosity (damping), which is given by the imaginary part of the complex Young’s modulus, expressed as E* = E’ + iωE” (E’: Young’s modulus, ω: frequency). Danno et al. measured the E* values of several aromatic molecular crystals, such as triphenylene, 1,2,3,4-dibenzanthracene, and coronene, using forced resonance vibration methods and showed that the ωE” values of these crystals are all on the order of 107–108 N/m257. The ω0γ values calculated from our analysis are listed in Table 1. For both the limiting values of c0 = vL and vTA1, they are on the order of 107 N/m2, showing good agreement with the ωE” values of the aromatic crystals. This agreement strongly supports the validity of our analysis. The relationship between vpf and γ values in Table 1 may suggest that variation in vpf is attributed to that of γ, which can be influenced by the intrinsic and/or extrinsic properties of individual crystals. However, experimental measurements of the elastic properties of the crystals are required to verify this assumption.

Further support for the validity of the analysis is provided by examining the kink width ℓℓ. The evaluated ℓℓ is on the order of 10−12 m (Table 1). This is two orders of magnitude smaller than the lattice constant c and on the same order as the difference in the lattice constant c between the β- and γ-phases (Δc = 3.16 × 10−12 m), implying that the kink is always localized at a single site, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 7b. This indicates that the phase transition proceeds in a layer-by-layer manner, as expected from our experimental observations and the martensitic phase transitions of other organic crystals20,33. If ℓℓ is sufficiently large to extend over multiple sites, those within the kink width have intermediate displacements between the stable and metastable equilibria (Fig. 7c). This corresponds to the situation where there extends a region with intermediate crystal structures at the phase boundary, and is inconsistent with the layer-by-layer progression of the phase transition.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the martensitic phase transitions in single crystals of TBB and observed that the transitions proceeded with the subsonic phase front propagation across the crystals. We demonstrated that the nonlinear lattice model successfully explained the motion of the phase front. The analysis reveals that the phase front is described as a kink-like solitary wave, which can be formed and propagate in a non-conservative system. This indicates that the viscosity of the crystals is the crucial factor in the formation and propagation of the phase front. A number of organic martensitic crystals, including thermosalient crystals, have been discovered to date, and studies aimed at device applications of these crystals have been extensively conducted. Our results serve as a promising guideline for analyzing and understanding the phase transitions of these crystals and provide valuable information for their device applications.

Methods

Sample preparation

TBB was obtained from Wako Pure Chemical Industries and purified by recrystallization from ethanol. A saturated ethanol solution of TBB was filtered using a syringe filter with 200 nm pores and then placed in a container with a lid with holes. After the crystals were grown via slow evaporation, they were washed with ethanol and dried under ambient conditions. Microscopic observations showed that approximately half of the crystals were twinned, and only non-twinned single crystals were used for the measurements.

Optical measurement

A TBB crystal was placed on a microscope cover glass (18 mm × 18 mm) and immobilized with a small amount of mineral oil to prevent movement of the crystal at the moment of phase transition. Although we attempted to perform the measurements on crystals that were simply placed on the cover glass without immobilization, most of them hopped or broke upon the phase transitions. We could observe phase front propagation for only a few crystals that did not exhibit movement. The vpf values of these crystals were observed as ~600 m/s, suggesting that the immobilization does not largely affect vpf. The sample was then placed on a transparent thermoplate (Tokai Hit). The thermoplate was mounted on the rotational stage of a polarized light microscope (Olympus, BX51-P) equipped with a modified illumination system. A 1.25 times objective lens was used. Because the exposure time for each frame in the high-speed camera recording was short (~262 ns), high-intensity illumination was required to capture images of the crystal. We constructed an illumination unit consisting of a 100 W mercury lamp and attached it to the microscope.

High-speed videos were recorded using a high-speed camera (Photron, FASTCAM) mounted on the microscope. A video of a crystal was loop-recorded while the sample temperature was varied at ~1 °C/min. When the crystal was transformed, the loop recording was immediately stopped so that the last ~3 s of the video was saved. Because the relative orientation between the stage and the camera is fixed, we can confirm that the crystals did not rotate upon the phase transitions by reviewing the videos. This ensures that the movement of the boundary between the dark and bright regions of the crystals is solely attributed to the change in the crystal structure.
