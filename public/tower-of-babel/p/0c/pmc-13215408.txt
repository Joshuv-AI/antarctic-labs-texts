Comparative Transcriptome Analysis of Xylaria grammica Under Light and Dark Conditions Reveals Dark-Responsive Activation of a Patulin-like Locus Involved in Grammicin Biosynthesis
Authors: Yebin Nam, Yoeguang Hue, Sunmin An, Jongbum Jeon, Jinyong Kim, Sook-Young Park, Soonok Kim, Won-Jae Chi, Ki-Tae Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractXylaria grammica is an endophytic fungus that produces grammicin, a polyketide reported as a structural isomer of the mycotoxin patulin. However, the environmental regulation of grammicin production remains unclear, and the full biosynthetic pathway has not yet been completely resolved. Genome re-annotation and biosynthetic gene cluster mining revealed high secondary metabolite potential, with 92 predicted clusters spanning major biosynthetic classes. Time-series RNA sequencing under shaking dark, static dark, and continuous light conditions showed strong condition-dependent transcriptome divergence. Light-grown cultures were enriched in functions associated with stress adaptation and detoxification, including oxidoreductase- and monooxygenase-related categories, whereas static dark cultures showed broader activation of secondary metabolism-associated genes at later stages. Among the predicted biosynthetic gene clusters, a patulin-like locus in X. grammica contained homologs corresponding to most genes of the Aspergillus clavatus pat cluster from PatA to PatO, although the gene order differed. Expression profiling revealed coordinated induction of patulin-like genes after 3 days post-inoculation under dark conditions, whereas continuous light strongly repressed their transcription. Quantitative RT-PCR analysis further confirmed this darkness-dependent activation pattern. Together, with previous functional evidence showing that patK and patL are required for grammicin production, these results indicate that darkness acts as a major regulatory switch for secondary metabolism in X. grammica and support the involvement of a patulin-like locus in grammicin biosynthesis. However, the downstream tailoring steps leading to grammicin remain unresolved, and further genetic and metabolomic analyses will be required to complete pathway reconstruction.

Introduction

The fungal genus Xylaria comprises over 100 species that are widely distributed in nature and occur predominantly as endophytes and saprophytes [1]. Endophytic Xylaria species are commonly associated with both vascular and non-vascular plants and are recognized as prolific producers of structurally diverse and biologically active secondary metabolites [2]. These metabolites include volatile and nonvolatile compounds with a broad spectrum of biological activities, such as insecticidal, fungicidal, and herbicidal effects. Furthermore, antibacterial, antifungal, antimalarial, and antinematodal activities have also been reported [3].

Xylaria grammica (Mont.) Fr. is an endophytic member of the genus Xylaria and is also recognized as a versatile decomposer of deadwood. This species is frequently found on decaying wood in deciduous forests across Asia, Africa, the Americas, and Australia [4]. In natural environments, X. grammica produces cylindrical spores and forms prominent stromata, whereas stromatal development is not observed under laboratory culture conditions [5]. Interest in X. grammica has increased due to its industrial potential, particularly its ability to cause type II soft rot in wood. This decay mode involves progressive degradation of the secondary cell wall while preserving the overall integrity of cell wall cavities [4]. Accordingly, previous research has emphasized extracellular enzymes, including carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) and peroxidases, as major contributors to wood decomposition [6]. In parallel, the diversity and functions of secondary metabolites produced by X. grammica are receiving increasing attention, highlighting this fungus as a potential source of bioactive compounds.

A polyketide compound named grammicin, together with related intermediates produced by X. grammica, has been reported to exhibit strong nematicidal activity against the root-knot nematode Meloidogyne incognita [7–9]. Grammicin was initially identified from X. grammica cultures as 4-hydroxy-4H-furo[2,3-b]pyran-2(7aH)-one and was described as an isomer of the cyclic γ-lactone mycotoxin patulin [5]. Patulin is produced by several fungal genera, including Aspergillus, Byssochlamys, Paecilomyces, and Penicillium, and is among the most prevalent mycotoxins contaminating fruit-based food products [10]. In Aspergillus clavatus, patulin biosynthesis is encoded by a 15 gene cluster [11], and putative homologs of this cluster are also present in the genome of X. grammica (this study). However, the genes directly responsible for grammicin biosynthesis remain uncharacterized, and the enzymatic steps underlying its production have not yet been elucidated.

The genome of X. grammica EL0006114 was previously reported as a 54.7 Mb assembly comprising 25 scaffolds and 13,390 predicted protein‑coding genes (GenBank accession GCA_004353285.2) [12]. Building on this resource, the present study generated condition-specific transcriptomes under (i) a dark condition that induces grammicin synthesis, (ii) continuous light, and (iii) a shaking culture condition (Figure 1). Differentially expressed genes were identified across pairwise comparisons, and expression dynamics were examined across predicted secondary-metabolite biosynthetic gene clusters (SM BGCs) and metabolic pathways. The analysis uncovered 92 putative SM BGCs, including a patulin‑like locus that showed concerted up‑regulation specifically under dark culture conditions, coinciding with the onset of grammicin accumulation. Together, these findings provide a foundation for elucidating grammicin biosynthesis and for understanding how environmental cues shape secondary-metabolite regulation in X. grammica.

Materials and methods

Gene model prediction and functional annotation

To refine gene models for X. grammica, the genome was re-annotated using BRAKER3 v3.0.7 [13]. The earlier annotation was generated using Iso-Seq evidence and protein sequences from five closely related species [12], but the corresponding gene models were not available through public repositories, necessitating a new annotation for downstream analyses. The BRAKER3 pipeline integrated Iso-Seq data as transcript evidence, a protein homology set comprising 408,254 Xylaria protein sequences retrieved from the NCBI non-redundant (NR) database, and ab initio prediction with AUGUSTUS v3.5.0 [14].

Functional annotation of the predicted protein-coding genes was performed in OmicsBox v3.4. This workflow included DIAMOND searches against the NCBI NR database (release date 2025-03-05), InterProScan v5.72-103.0 for domain and family assignment, and Gene Ontology term mapping and annotation [15–17]. KEGG pathway annotation was performed using two complementary approaches. First, KEGG terms were assigned with the OmicsBox default pipeline to obtain conservative pathways. Second, KofamScan v1.3.0 was applied using relaxed thresholds (threshold ≥ 17.37, score ≥ 20.2, and P-value ≥ 2.50 × 10^-4) to improve sensitivity and recover additional candidate pathway assignments [18]. The KO identifiers assigned to each gene by KofamScan were subsequently used for KEGG reconstruction mapping with KEGG Mapper Reconstruct, enabling projection of gene-associated K numbers onto KEGG reference pathways and related network entities. SM BGCs were predicted using fungal antiSMASH v7.0.1 with relaxed strictness and all optional analyses enabled [19].

Sample preparation for RNA-seq

X. grammica strain EL000614 was obtained from the Korean Lichen and Allied Bioresources Center (Suncheon National University) and cultivated under three conditions: (i) liquid shaking culture in darkness as the control, (ii) solid culture in darkness to induce grammicin production, and (iii) solid culture under continuous light (Figure 1). For starter cultures, the fungus was grown on potato dextrose agar (PDA, Difco Laboratories, Detroit, MI, USA) at 25 °C in the dark for 7 days. Mycelia were collected using a sterile spatula, transferred to 25 mL sterile distilled water in a 50 mL tube, and homogenized for 1 min at 1,200 rpm using an IKA T10 homogenizer (IKA Co., model T10). The homogenate was inoculated into 500 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 200 mL potato dextrose (PD) broth and incubated at 25 °C in the dark with shaking at 120 rpm for 2 days. These 2-day-old mycelia were designated as the baseline sample (0 h) and used to initiate the experimental cultures.

For the control condition, the mycelia were maintained in PD broth under the same dark shaking conditions (25 °C, 120 rpm) and sampled at 2 and 7 days. For the solid culture conditions, the mycelial suspension was spread onto PDA plates overlaid with sterile cellophane to facilitate harvesting. Plates incubated in darkness (grammicin-inducing condition) were sampled at 1, 3, and 5 days, and plates exposed to continuous light were collected at the same time points. All treatments were performed with three independent biological replicates. Each sample was immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until RNA extraction.

RNA sequencing and transcriptome analyses

Total RNA was extracted using an Easy-Spin Total RNA Extraction Kit (iNtRON Biotechnology, Seoul, Korea). Strand-specific mRNA libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform at Theragen Etex (Seoul, Korea). Raw read quality was assessed using FastQC v0.11.9 [20]. Adapter sequences and low-quality bases were removed using fastp v0.24.0 [21]. Clean reads were then aligned to the X. grammica reference genome using HISAT2 v2.2.1 [22]. The resulting sequence alignment/map (SAM) files were sorted using sort tools in Samtools v1.21 [23]. Gene-level read counts were obtained using StringTie v2.2.1 [24]. Differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis was conducted with DESeq2 to identify transcriptional changes within each condition across time points and between culture conditions [25]. Genes with an adjusted P-value < 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change > 2 were considered differentially expressed.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA (5 µg) from each sample was reverse-transcribed to complementary DNA (cDNA) using the Prom-II Reverse Transcription System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The resulting cDNA was diluted to 12.5 ng/µL and stored at −20 °C until use. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed as described previously [26], with minor modifications, to validate the expression of selected patulin-like cluster genes (patC, patF, patG, patH, patI, patJ, patK, patL, patM, patN, and patO) using Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Warrington, UK) on a Bio-Rad CFX real-time PCR system. Reactions were prepared in 20 µL volumes containing 10 µL of 2× master mix, 2 µL of diluted cDNA, and 15 pmol of each primer, with nuclease-free water added to volume. The cycling program consisted of 94 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 94 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min, and a melt-curve analysis to confirm amplification specificity. Transcript abundance was normalized to elongation factor 1-alpha (EF1), and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method with the liquid shaking dark culture (control) as the calibrator. All assays were performed with three independent biological replicates, each analyzed in triplicate. Primer sequences are provided in Table S1, and the same primer sets as those used in our previous published study were applied in the present analysis [9].

Results

Re-annotation of the X. grammica genome and RNA-sequencing results

Re-annotation of the X. grammica genome predicted 10,712 protein-coding genes and 11,874 protein isoforms. To support transparency and reproducibility, the underlying Iso-Seq raw data and the updated gene and protein models were deposited in FigShare (https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.28732019). Among the predicted proteins, 11,809 (99.5%) showed significant homology to entries in the NCBI NR database, and 10,140 (85.4%) contained InterPro-defined domains. In addition, 9,145 proteins (77.0%) were assigned Gene Ontology (GO) terms, providing functional context for a large fraction of the proteome (Table S2). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that 3,630 proteins (30.6%) were mapped to metabolic pathways under stringent threshold criteria (Table S3), whereas a relaxed cutoff resulted in the mapping of 8,494 proteins (71.5%) to KEGG pathways (Table S4), indicating substantial functional diversity encoded in the X. grammica genome.

RNA-seq datasets generated from each experimental condition achieved a minimum coverage of 47.6 X. Read mapping rates to the X. grammica genome ranged from 95.75% to 99.23%, with an average of 98.82% (Figure 2A, Table S5), supporting the overall high quality of the transcriptomic data. The number of expressed genes per sample (read count > 0) ranged from 10,288 (86.6%) in the shake 0 dpi sample to 10,888 (91.7%) in the dark 5 dpi sample (Figure 2B). While the number of expressed genes remained relatively stable under continuous light, both shaking and dark conditions showed a gradual increase over time.

Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that biological replicates generally clustered, with particularly close proximity between adjacent time points such as light 3 and 5 dpi and shaking 2 and 7 dpi (Figure 2C). Moreover, samples separated clearly by culture condition, with distinct grouping of the shaking, light, and dark treatments (Figure 2D). This condition-dependent structure supports the robustness of downstream differential expression analyses and the identification of culture-specific transcriptional responses.

Functional characterization of DEGs under light and dark conditions

Differential expression analysis revealed 352 genes upregulated under light and 520 genes upregulated under dark relative to the shaking condition (Figure 3A). In the reciprocal comparisons, 320 and 489 genes were upregulated in the shaking condition relative to light and dark, respectively, with 211 genes shared between the two contrasts (Supplementary Figure 1). Direct comparison between the light and dark conditions further identified 173 genes upregulated in the dark and 252 genes upregulated under light (i.e., downregulated under dark) (Figure 3A).

To resolve condition-specific transcriptional responses, direction-specific upregulated gene sets unique to each treatment were analyzed separately. This analysis identified 323 light-specific and 397 dark-specific upregulated genes (Figure 3B). These genes displayed distinct expression patterns largely confined to their respective conditions (Figure 3C), supporting condition-dependent transcriptional responses to light and dark cues.

GO enrichment analysis of the light-specific and dark-specific upregulated genes using Fisher’s exact test (adjusted p < 0.05) revealed oxidoreductase-related functions as a common feature of both groups (Figure 3D). Light-specific upregulated genes (green) were enriched for molecular functions such as heme binding, tetrapyrrole binding, monooxygenase activity, and iron ion binding, consistent with enhanced heme-associated and metal-dependent redox functions under light conditions. By contrast, dark-specific upregulated genes (red) were enriched for biological processes including tetrapyrrole metabolic process, porphyrin-containing compound biosynthetic process, and pigment metabolic process, together with alcohol dehydrogenase (NADP+) activity, suggesting enhanced pigment- and porphyrin-associated metabolism under dark conditions. These results support the presence of distinct functional responses to light and dark environment.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis (adjusted P-value < 0.05) further supported this distinction (Figure 3E). Light-specific upregulated genes (green) were enriched in pathways associated with energy metabolism and detoxification, including glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, pyruvate metabolism, alpha-linolenic acid degradation, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and xenobiotic and drug metabolism by cytochrome P450. Dark-specific upregulated genes (red) were enriched in a broader range of pathways, including biosynthesis of cofactors, glycine, serine and threonine metabolism, steroid biosynthesis, porphyrin metabolism, fatty acid biosynthesis, nitrogen metabolism, and aflatoxin biosynthesis. Several pathways were enriched in both groups, including glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, pyruvate metabolism, xenobiotic metabolism by cytochrome P450, and naphthalene degradation, indicating shared metabolic adjustment together with condition-dependent pathway activation.

KEGG pathway analysis of light- and dark-specific upregulated genes

Using stringent KEGG mapping criteria, complete conservation of the fatty acid biosynthesis module was detected only in the dark-specific upregulated gene set. When a relaxed mapping threshold was applied to compensate for possible annotation gaps, additional modules were recovered across energy, lipid, and amino acid metabolism, as well as the biosynthesis of terpenoids, polyketides, and other secondary metabolites (Table 1). Among primary metabolic modules, assimilatory nitrate reduction was uniquely identified in the dark-specific upregulated genes, whereas C19 steroid hormone biosynthesis and proline degradation were completely conserved only in the light-specific upregulated genes. In contrast, modules related to sulfur metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, lipid metabolism, and sterol biosynthesis were conserved in both gene sets, indicating the presence of a shared metabolic backbone despite clear condition-specific differences.

Several completely conserved modules associated with secondary metabolism were also identified. Notably, the castasterone biosynthesis module was observed exclusively in the light condition, whereas modules corresponding to isoflavone biosynthesis, including daidzein and genistein, and fungal alkaloid pathways such as chanoclavine aldehyde and fumiquinazoline biosynthesis were uniquely conserved in the dark condition (Figure 4A). By contrast, modules for gibberellin biosynthesis, 3,6-dimethylsalicylyl-CoA biosynthesis in the enediyne pathway, neocarzinostatin naphthoate moiety biosynthesis, and pelargonidin biosynthesis were detected in both conditions. These results suggest that light and dark conditions are associated not only with distinct secondary metabolic signatures but also with partially overlapping biosynthetic capacities.

Among the detected modules, castasterone, daidzein, genistein, and pelargonidin biosynthesis are generally regarded as plant-associated [27], whereas gibberellin and 3,6-dimethylsalicylyl-CoA biosynthesis have been reported in both plants and fungi [28]. Likewise, the naphthoate moiety biosynthetic route has been described in both actinomycetes and fungi (Figure 4B) [29], whereas chanoclavine aldehyde and fumiquinazoline biosynthesis are considered fungal-specific pathways (Figure 4C) [30]. Together, these patterns indicate that the reconstructed KEGG modules comprise both broadly conserved cross-kingdom biosynthetic frameworks and lineage-specific fungal secondary metabolic features.

Light is known to induce photoprotective pigmentation, including carotenoids and melanin, in fungi such as Neurospora crassa and Fusarium species [31]. In X. grammica, complete KEGG modules for melanogenesis and carotenoid biosynthesis were not reconstructed (Supplementary Figure 2), However, this likely reflects the conservative nature of KEGG module completeness criteria and incomplete functional assignment for some pathway components rather than a true absence of these metabolic routes. Consistent with partial pathway representation, multiple condition-specific upregulated genes mapped to pigment-related pathways. Specifically, five light-specific and three dark-specific upregulated genes were assigned to melanogenesis, while 39 light-specific and 48 dark-specific upregulated genes were assigned to carotenoid biosynthesis. Together, these results suggest that pigment-associated metabolism is engaged under both conditions, even though these pathways do not meet the threshold for complete module reconstruction based on current annotation.

Temporal functional dynamics of DEGs under shaking, light, and dark conditions

Temporal changes in gene expression under shaking, light, and dark conditions were assessed using KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (FDR < 0.05) (Figure 5A). The largest number of upregulated DEGs was observed in the shaking condition at 7 days post inoculation (dpi), with 1,055 genes. Similarly, high numbers of upregulated DEGs were detected at 5 dpi in both the light and dark conditions when compared with the shaking 0 dpi baseline. In contrast, downregulated DEGs peaked at 1 dpi under dark conditions (1,016 genes), which was substantially higher than under light at the same time point (660 genes), indicating a stronger early-stage transcriptional repression in darkness.

Under shaking conditions, pathways enriched among upregulated DEGs over time were dominated by secondary metabolism and stress-associated processes, including enediyne-related biosynthesis, xenobiotic and drug metabolism pathways involving cytochrome P450 and other enzymes, fatty acid biosynthesis, glutathione metabolism, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and pyruvate metabolism (Figure 5B). In addition, multiple amino acid metabolic pathways were enriched, including tyrosine, phenylalanine, glycine, serine, threonine, arginine, and proline metabolism, together with fatty acid degradation, glycolysis and gluconeogenesis, and nitrogen metabolism. These patterns suggest broad metabolic remodeling during prolonged shaking culture. In contrast, porphyrin metabolism decreased over time, consistent with a shift away from tetrapyrrole-associated processes during later stages.

In the light condition, enriched pathways were largely associated with detoxification and stress responses, including xenobiotic and drug metabolism involving cytochrome P450, ether lipid metabolism, chloroalkane and chloroalkene degradation, glutathione metabolism, insect hormone biosynthesis, and limonene degradation (Figure 5C). These enrichments indicate that continuous light elicits a transcriptional program emphasizing redox balance and chemical stress management. In the dark condition, diterpenoid biosynthesis decreased over time, suggesting condition-specific reallocation of metabolic resources during dark incubation.

Comparisons between conditions further highlighted distinct metabolic reprogramming (Figures 5D–F). Relative to shaking, the light condition showed pronounced changes across xenobiotic and drug metabolism, fatty acid biosynthesis and degradation, several amino acid metabolic pathways, glycolysis and gluconeogenesis, peptidoglycan biosynthesis, and degradation pathways for aromatic environmental compounds, including naphthalene and other polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (Figure 5D). A similar pattern was observed in the shaking versus dark comparison, where pathways linked to xenobiotic and drug metabolism, amino acid and fatty acid metabolism, porphyrin metabolism, and diterpenoid biosynthesis increased, while nucleotide metabolism pathways tended to decrease (Figure 5E). Direct comparison of light and dark conditions showed that dark cultures were associated with increased enrichment of diterpenoid biosynthesis, xenobiotic and drug metabolism processes, biotin metabolism, fatty acid biosynthesis, and multiple amino acid metabolism pathways (Figure 5F). Conversely, degradation pathways for aromatic and complex compounds, including naphthalene, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, dioxins, and aflatoxins, were reduced under dark conditions. Overall, these results indicate that X. grammica deploys distinct time-dependent metabolic strategies across culture regimes, balancing pathway activation and suppression to adapt to shaking, continuous light, and darkness.

Expression patterns of SM BGCs in X. grammica under light and dark conditions

The X. grammica genome contains 92 predicted SM BGCs spanning diverse classes, including Type I/III polyketide synthases (PKSs), non-ribosomal peptide synthetases (NRPSs), terpenes, fungal ribosomally synthesized and post-translationally modified peptides (fungal-RiPPs), and indole-derived compounds (Figure 6A). Among these, nine BGCs were associated with DEGs under dark conditions, and ten were associated with DEGs under light conditions (Figure 6B). Several major clusters showed high levels of gene conservation relative to known reference clusters, including NRPS clusters related to dimethylcoprogen, AbT1, choline, and serinocyclin A/B, a T1PKS cluster related to (-)-mellein, and a terpene cluster related to clavaric acid (Figure 6C). These observations suggest that X. grammica may have the potential to produce diverse bioactive metabolites.

In addition, BGCs putatively related to the biosynthesis of swainsonine, patulin, sordaricin B, scytalone/T3HN, trichobrasilenol/xylarenic acid, betaenone C, and compaction showed more than 50% gene conservation. Among these candidates, the patulin-like locus was defined by integrating antiSMASH-based BGC prediction with manual inspection of the genomic region surrounding the core PKS gene patK. Candidate genes were assigned as cluster members based on a combination of criteria, including their localization within the predicted patulin-like biosynthetic gene cluster and sequence similarity to known patulin biosynthetic genes considering identity, query coverage, and E-value. Using this approach, BLASTp analyses identified homologs corresponding to most genes in the A. clavatus patulin cluster in X. grammica, with sequence identities ranging from 32.16% to 72.76% (Table 2 and Figure 6D). Although several core genes were conserved, the overall gene order differed from that of A. clavatus, indicating a rearranged cluster architecture. Most homologous genes were located within a contiguous genomic region, supporting their classification as part of the same biosynthetic locus. Notably, homologs of patA, patD, and patE were not detected within this region (Table 2). In addition, PatN was not initially included in the predicted cluster; however, reverse BLASTp analysis identified a putative homolog encoded by gene g2580, positioned between patM and patO. Notably, the best BLASTp hit for g2580 showed relatively high sequence identity (70.0%) but covered only a small fraction of the query sequence (∼3%), indicating that the similarity is restricted to a short region and is insufficient to support homolog assignment based on forward BLASTp alone. Despite this limitation, the reciprocal BLAST result, together with its conserved genomic position within the cluster, supports its tentative inclusion as a PatN-like gene. Overall, the combination of partial gene conservation, conserved core biosynthetic components, and local genomic clustering supports the identification of this region as a patulin-like secondary metabolite biosynthetic locus in X. grammica, despite differences in gene composition and organization.

The qRT-PCR analysis showed that patulin-homologous genes (patC and patF-patO) displayed a strong darkness-associated expression pattern in X. grammica (Figure 7). After normalization to EF1, transcript levels under dark conditions were low or close to control level at 1 dpi, suggesting limited early activation. In contrast, expression increased markedly at 3 dpi, when most tested genes were upregulated and elevated transcript levels were generally maintained at 5 dpi. Several genes, including patF, patJ, and patK, showed particularly strong induction under dark conditions.

By contrast, under continuous light, all tested genes remained strongly repressed across time points, with transcript levels consistently below the control. Overall, these qRT-PCR results were consistent with the RNA-seq expression patterns and indicate that the patulin-like locus is preferentially expressed under dark conditions, particularly after 3 dpi, whereas light is associated with transcriptional repression of this gene set. However, because these data are based on transcript abundance, they should be interpreted as evidence of condition-dependent regulation rather than as standalone proof of grammicin biosynthesis under the tested conditions. Instead, the present expression patterns are best interpreted together with our previous study, in which patK and patL were functionally validated as required for grammicin production, and patL overexpression increased grammicin yield [9]. Taken together, these observations support the relevance of this patulin-like genetic program to grammicin-related metabolism in X. grammica, while the downstream tailoring steps remain unresolved.

Discussion

Environmental signals such as light and darkness are major regulators of fungal physiology, shaping transcription, development, and secondary metabolism. In X. grammica, each culture condition produced a clearly different transcriptome. PCA separated samples by shaking, dark, and light treatments (Figure 2C–D), indicating that each condition established a different transcriptional state. Differential expression analysis also showed only partially overlap among DEG sets across conditions (Figure 3A), suggesting that X. grammica flexibly adjusts gene expression in response environment change.

Light exposure is generally known to activate secondary metabolism, detoxification and photoprotective pathways in fungi [31]. Under continuous light, the main response of X. grammica was consistent with stress protection. GO and KEGG analyses showed enrichment of functions related to heme and tetrapyrrole binding and monooxygenase activity, together with pathways involving cytochrome P450 enzymes and xenobiotic or drug metabolism (Figure 3D and E). This pattern suggests that light exposure creates oxidative or chemical stress, so the fungus increases detoxification and redox-related processes. Similar light-driven stress responses have been reported in other fungi, where light activates protective programs rather than strong secondary metabolite production [32]. In addition, pigment-related pathways were partly detected by KEGG mapping, and many DEGs were assigned to melanogenesis and carotenoid biosynthesis (Figure 4 and Supplementary Figure 2). The visible pigment accumulation in light-grown cultures (Figure 1) further supports the interpretation that X. grammica uses pigmentation as part of a photoprotective response.

By contrast, dark static culture showed a stronger shift toward secondary metabolism. Dark-specific upregulated genes were associated with a broader range of metabolic pathways, including several related to secondary metabolite production as well as lipid or cofactor metabolism (Figures 3E and 5F). This pattern is consistent with the general model in which light suppresses secondary metabolism in filamentous fungi through global regulatory systems, particularly the Velvet family network linking light sensing, development, and secondary metabolite gene expression [33]. Although the specific regulators were not tested here, the strong difference between light and dark conditions is compatible with this framework. The physical culture parameters also modulated the metabolic outcome. Dark cultures with shaking showed weaker induction of secondary metabolism than dark static cultures, even though both were incubated in darkness. This suggests that shaking may reduce cues that help promote BGC activation, possibly by maintaining a more vegetative state or altering oxygen and stress conditions. Time also played an important role as many genes were downregulated at 1 dpi in the dark, whereas secondary metabolism pathways became more active at later stages (Figure 5A, E and F). This pattern suggests that X. grammica first adjusts to the environment and subsequently reallocates resources toward secondary metabolism during later growth.

Genome mining further indicated that X. grammica has substantial biosynthetic potential, with 92 predicted secondary metabolite BGCs across major classes such as PKSs, NRPSs, terpenes, fungal RiPPs, and indole-derived clusters (Figure 6A). Only a subset of clusters was active under any one condition, which is common in fungi because many BGCs remain silent unless specific triggers are present. This condition-dependent expression supports an OSMAC (One Strain, Many Compounds) style view, in which altered culture conditions reveal otherwise hidden metabolic potential [34,35]. The high BGC diversity also agrees with reports that Xylariales species frequently harbor numerous secondary metabolite clusters, likely reflecting adaptation to diverse ecological niches [36,37]. Taken together, these results position X. grammica as a strong candidate for culture-guided and genome-guided natural product discovery.

A major finding of this work is the identification of a patulin-like BGC and its condition-dependent regulation under light and dark culture conditions. In X. grammica, most pat gene homologs were detected with moderate to high sequence identity, although gene order differed from reference clusters and several genes were not found in the same arrangement (Figure 6D and Table 2). This differs from the contiguous patA-patO clusters previously described in A. clavatus and Penicillium expansum, in which patulin biosynthesis is supported by a more canonical cluster architecture [11,38]. Despite this divergence, the X. grammica locus retains homologs to most core patulin-associated genes and exhibits coordinated transcriptional induction under static dark conditions, suggesting conservation of a substantial part of the underlying biosynthetic logic.

This interpretation is also consistent with the chemical structure of grammicin. Because grammicin is a structural isomer of patulin, the two metabolites are likely to share at least part of their early biosynthetic route. In particular, the presence of a PatK-like 6-methylsalicylic acid (6-MSA) synthase supports the idea that the pathway begins from a 6-MSA-derived core, as in canonical patulin biosynthesis [10,39]. Likewise, the presence of predicted homologs of PatH, PatI, and PatJ is consistent with conservation of early oxidative transformations that generate shared intermediates. This view is supported by our previous study, which identified two intermediates relevant to grammicin biosynthesis, 2,5-dihydroxybenzaldehyde (gentisaldehyde) and 2-(hydroxymethyl)cyclohexa-2,5-diene-1,4-dione, indicating partial overlap with early 6-MSA-derived steps of the patulin pathway while leaving downstream conversions unresolved [9].

RNA-seq and qRT-PCR both showed strong induction of this locus after 3 dpi under static dark conditions and strong repression under light, indicating tight condition-dependent regulation (Figures 6D and 7). This pattern is consistent with the idea that BGCs can undergo evolutionary rearrangement while retaining coordinated regulation. Importantly, our recently published functional study demonstrated that two genes within this locus, patK and patL, are required for grammicin production, as deletion of either gene abolished grammicin biosynthesis, complementation restored production, and constitutive overexpression of patL increased grammicin yield [9]. Thus, although the present transcriptome data alone do not establish biosynthetic function, they are consistent with and extend previous functional evidence supporting the involvement of this patulin-like locus in grammicin biosynthesis.

At the same time, the structure of grammicin indicates that the pathway cannot be identical to the canonical patulin route. Its distinct ring arrangement implies divergence at later tailoring steps, likely involving alternative oxidation, reduction, or rearrangement reactions after formation of the shared early intermediates. In this context, the absence or altered arrangement of several patulin-cluster genes in X. grammica may reflect pathway-specific modifications required to generate grammicin rather than patulin. Therefore, the predicted gene content of the patulin-like locus is chemically consistent with a model in which X. grammica uses a conserved 6-MSA-derived biosynthetic core but diverges through organism-specific downstream tailoring reactions.

In summary, X. grammica responds to light primarily by activating detoxification and pigment-related protection, whereas dark static culture promotes later activation of secondary metabolism. The large number of predicted BGCs and the dark-specific induction of a patulin-like cluster provide a useful framework for studying grammicin biosynthesis and for discovering additional metabolites under altered culture conditions. When integrated with prior functional validation of patK and patL, our results support a model in which the patulin-like locus contributes to grammicin biosynthesis, while additional downstream enzymatic steps remain to be resolved.
