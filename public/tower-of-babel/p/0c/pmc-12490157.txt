A novel nomogram based on orbital MRI parameters for early prediction of dysthyroid optic neuropathy in thyroid-associated ophthalmopathy
Authors: Xianzhi Zhao, Yongran Li, Yanling Lu, Miaozhi Liu, Zeyi Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to assess orbital magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) differences across various severity levels of thyroid-associated ophthalmopathy (TAO) and compare them to healthy controls. Additionally, it investigated the correlation and diagnostic potential of multiple MRI parameters for dysthyroid optic neuropathy (DON).MethodsPatients with TAO were classified according to the EUGOGO severity scale into mild TAO (66 orbits), moderate-to-severe TAO (59 orbits), and DON (21 orbits). A control group of 25 healthy orbits was also included. Patients underwent comprehensive ophthalmic and orbital MRI examinations. Generalized estimating equation (GEE) was used to analyze the clinical and MRI data, comparing differences across groups. Univariate and multivariate binary logistic regression identified independent risk factors for DON. The predictive value of MRI parameters for DON was further evaluated using a nomogram, receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC), and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsThe extraocular muscle area significantly differed between the control and mild TAO groups (P < 0.05), as well as between both groups and the DON group (P < 0.05). Significant variations in angle between the medial rectus and optic nerve (AMR-ON) and angle between the lateral rectus and optic nerve (ALR-ON) were observed across the control, mild TAO, and moderate-to-severe TAO groups compared to the DON group (P < 0.05). Regression analysis revealed superior rectus muscle area (SRA) and AMR-ON as independent predictors of DON (P < 0.05). The SRA + AMR-ON nomogram demonstrated high predictive performance for DON, with an AUC of 0.906 (P < 0.001), sensitivity of 90.5%, and specificity of 82.4%. DCA further confirmed that the SRA + AMR-ON model outperformed Muscle Index (MI) and Crowding Index (CI) in predictive efficacy for DON.ConclusionsA reduction in AMR-ON and ALR-ON correlates with increased TAO severity, while an enlargement in extraocular muscle area, MI, and CI reflects worsening TAO. SRA and AMR-ON serve as independent predictors for DON, and the predictive model combining these parameters is highly effective in forecasting DON onset.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-025-04375-3.Keywords: Thyroid-associated ophthalmopathy, Dysthyroid optic neuropathy, Magnetic resonance imaging, Orbit, Extraocular muscles

Background

Thyroid-associated ophthalmopathy (TAO) is the most prevalent orbital autoimmune disorder, closely associated with autoimmune thyroid diseases such as Graves’ disease [1]. Specifically, abnormal thyrotropin receptor antibodies (TRAb) produced by patients bind to receptors on orbital fibroblasts, stimulating adipocyte hypertrophy and T lymphocyte activation, which results in a cytokine-driven inflammatory cascade [2]. This infiltrative process is accompanied by glycosaminoglycan deposition, adipogenesis, and subsequent fibrotic transformation, culminating in the characteristic orbital manifestations of the disease [3].

Given the varied clinical presentations of TAO, a standardized classification system is crucial for clinical management. The European Group on Graves Orbitopathy (EUGOGO) categorizes TAO severity into three stages: mild TAO, moderate-to-severe TAO, and very severe TAO (also referred to as sight-threatening TAO) [4]. Very severe TAO is marked by dysthyroid optic neuropathy (DON) and/or corneal lesions. DON is a severe complication of TAO that can result in significant vision impairment, requiring prompt intervention. DON is a clinical diagnosis established through a comprehensive evaluation of clinical manifestations, visual function tests, and imaging studies. DON typically progresses rapidly, but it can also develop insidiously [5], with patients often unaware of their deteriorating vision. However, there is currently no gold standard for diagnosing DON, leading to ongoing debates regarding the significance of clinical signs, visual function tests, and imaging findings [6]. The prognosis of DON is closely associated with disease duration, with patients experiencing shorter disease courses generally showing a better prognosis. This underscores the importance of timely diagnosis and intervention for DON [7].

Current imaging studies of the orbits in patients with TAO predominantly focus on Doppler ultrasound, CT, and MRI [8]. Very severe TAO lacks pathognomonic features in radiological exams, with no established diagnostic criteria. While CT has been used in studies to assess apical crowding and diagnose DON via indices like muscle index, crowding index and combined parameters [9–11], it has poor soft tissue contrast and cannot directly reflect optic nerve damage.

MRI, superior to CT in visualizing the optic nerve and soft tissues, better analyzes inflammation, edema, and blood flow, aiding in disease progression prediction and tissue quantification. Existing studies have demonstrated that MRI can effectively assess extraocular muscle hypertrophy, orbital fat volume changes, and optic nerve compression in TAO patients. For instance, T2-weighted imaging and fat suppression sequences can identify edema in extraocular muscles, which is closely related to disease activity [12]. However, despite these advancements, the application of orbital MRI in the early prediction of DON remains insufficiently explored. Current research on MRI parameters for DON mainly focuses on qualitative descriptions rather than quantitative analysis, lacking specific and reliable indicators for early identification.

This study uses a GE 3.0T Signa Creator MRI scanner to quantitatively analyze orbital MRI of TAO patients. Its objectives are threefold: compare MRI differences among TAO patients of varying severities and normal controls, identify parameters related to DON onset, and evaluate MRI’s diagnostic value for DON. The lack of a unified DON diagnostic standard hinders early detection and management. By analyzing MRI variations across TAO severity levels, this study aims to develop a predictive model for identifying high - risk DON patients.

Methods

Patients

This study is a retrospective investigation conducted on 76 patients diagnosed with TAO who received treatment at the Joint Shantou International Eye Center of Shantou University and The Chinese University of Hong Kong (JSIEC) between May 2021 and March 2023. Some of the data results from this sample have been previously published [13], and this article serves as a continuation of the research conducted by the same research group. The study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics and Academic Committee of JSIEC. All data and materials collected during the study were handled with strict confidentiality.

All TAO diagnoses were confirmed through imaging and clinical examinations. The diagnosis and Severity Classification of TAO were based on the Bartley criteria, incorporating the 2021 EUGOGO European Guidelines for the Management of Thyroid-Associated Orbitopathy and the 2022 Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Thyroid-Associated Orbitopathy [4, 14]. For DON, its diagnosis was established based on the joint consensus of ATE/ETA [15], incorporating the criteria of Rezar-Dreindl [16]. The diagnosis of DON was based on at least 2 signs such as (1) Impaired visual acuity: Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) ≥ 0.3 in logMAR units, (2) Visual field (VF) defect < −5.0 decibel, (3) Impaired color vision: ≥ 2 errors on Ishihara color plates, (4) Abnormal pattern visual evoked potential (PVEP) results: Moderate or greater reduction in P100 amplitudes in any eye, (5) Optic disc swelling or presence of a relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD), (6) Radiological evidence of optic nerve compression on orbital MRI or CT. The exclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (1) Patients with very severe TAO exhibiting severe exposure keratitis; (2) Patients who received high-dose steroid pulses, immunosuppressive therapy, or radiation therapy within the past six months, or those with a history of orbital decompression surgery; (3) Patients under the age of 20; (4) Patients with a history of ocular diseases affecting vision; (5) Patients with a history of ocular trauma or intraocular surgery; (6) Patients with a history of intracranial infections, Parkinson’s syndrome, or other neurological disorders.

The inclusion criteria for control group were as follows: (1) The contralateral eye of a patient with a unilateral preoperative eyelid mass or both eyes of a healthy, medically examined population; (2) Over 20 years old; (3) Best-corrected logMAR visual acuity ≤ 0.1 and normal IOP in the enrolled eye (pneumatic tonometry measured 10–21 mmHg); (4) The enrolled eye must be asymptomatic with no signs of organic pathology; (5) Absence of systemic diseases such as diabetes, Sjögren’s syndrome, or Parkinson’s syndrome.

Measurement methods

All enrolled subjects underwent comprehensive ophthalmic examinations conducted by experienced optometrists, ophthalmologists, and medical technicians. For orbital MRI, a 3.0T MRI system (GE 3.0 Signa Creator, GE Medical System, USA) was utilized, employing the following spin-echo (SE) sequences: (1) Axial T1-weighted Imaging (T1WI): Slice thickness of 3 mm, TR: 400–600 ms, TE: 7–10 ms; (2) Axial T2-weighted Imaging with Fat Suppression (T2WI FS): Slice thickness of 3 mm, TR: 2000–3000 ms, TE: 65–70 ms; (3) Coronal T2-weighted Imaging with Fat Suppression (T2WI FS): Slice thickness of 3 mm, TR: 2000–3000 ms, TE: 65–70 ms; (4) Sagittal T1-weighted Imaging (T1WI): Slice thickness of 3 mm, TR: 400–600 ms, TE: 7–10 ms.

To minimize errors caused by extraocular muscle contraction and eye deviation, patients were instructed to gaze forward and slightly close their eyes during the scan. MRI measurements were performed using the Carestream GCRIS 2.1 imaging workstation (Carestream, USA). For the axial plane, the image slice that simultaneously maximized display of the medial rectus, lateral rectus, and optic nerve was selected. For the sagittal plane, the image slice that displayed the superior rectus, inferior rectus, and optic nerve was chosen. The following parameters were measured (Fig. 1): (1) Volume of the Medial Orbital Wall (VMW): The area enclosed by the line connecting the lateral vertex of the lacrimal bone to the medial edge of the optic canal and the medial orbital wall. A positive value indicates outward protrusion, while a negative value indicates inward depression; (2) Volume of the Lateral Orbital Wall (VLW): The area enclosed by the line connecting the medial vertex of the zygomatic bone to the lateral edge of the superior orbital fissure and the lateral orbital wall; (3) Length of the Lateral Orbital Wall (LLW): The length of the line connecting the medial vertex of the zygomatic bone to the lateral edge of the superior orbital fissure; (4) Intraorbital Optic Nerve Stretching Length (IONSL): The length from the posterior origin of the optic nerve to the orbital apex; (5) Proptosis: The vertical distance from the corneal apex to the inter-zygomatic line, defined as the line connecting the most anterior points of the bilateral zygomatic bones; (6) Medial, Lateral, Superior and Inferior Rectus Area (MRA, LRA, SRA and IRA); (7) Angle Between the Medial Rectus and Optic Nerve (AMR-ON); (8) Angle Between the Lateral Rectus and Optic Nerve (ALR-ON). (9) Muscle Index (MI): In the coronal plane at the middle-posterior one-third of the lateral orbital wall, the vertical MI was determined by summing the vertical diameters of the superior rectus-levator complex and the inferior rectus, then dividing by the vertical height of the orbit. The horizontal MI was derived by summing the horizontal diameters of the medial and lateral rectus muscles, divided by the horizontal width of the orbit. The higher value between the vertical and horizontal MI was defined as the overall MI. (10) Crowding Index (CI): Ratio of the total area of five extraocular muscle groups to the orbital area in coronal sections at the middle-posterior one-third of the lateral orbital wall.

Measurements of MRI Parameters: a Axial T1-weighted imaging (T1WI): Measurement of the angle between the medial rectus and the optic nerve, the angle between the lateral rectus and the optic nerve, and the length of the lateral orbital wall. b Axial T1-weighted imaging (T1WI): Measurement of the medial and lateral rectus area. c Axial T1-weighted imaging (T1WI): Measurement of the volume of the medial and lateral orbital walls. d Sagittal T2-weighted imaging with fat suppression (T2WI FS): Measurement of the superior and inferior rectus area. e Axial T2-weighted imaging with fat suppression (T2WI FS): Measurement of proptosis and intraorbital optic nerve stretching length. f Coronal T1-weighted imaging (T1WI): Measurement of the MI. g Coronal T1-weighted imaging (T1WI): Measurement of the CI

Statistical analysis

In this study, the affected eyes of enrolled patients were included. Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 27.0, R 4.2.0, and GraphPad Prism statistical software. Continuous variables were assessed for normality and confirmed to follow a normal distribution, with results presented as mean ± standard deviation. Categorical clinical data are reported as percentages. Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) were utilized to analyze baseline characteristics and MRI parameters, comparing differences among patients with TAO of varying severity levels and the control group. The χ2 test was applied to compare gender ratios. All MRI parameters were subjected to univariate binary logistic regression and backward stepwise multivariate binary logistic regression analyses to identify independent predictors of DON. The predictive capacity of individual parameters for DON was evaluated using ROC curve analysis. An independent predictive model was constructed using the rms package in R, with its performance further assessed through ROC curve analysis. DCA was performed utilizing the rmda package to determine the clinical utility of the model. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics

A total of 76 patients (39 males, 37 females) with TAO were included in this study, encompassing 146 eyes. The control group consisted of 22 participants (10 males, 12 females), totaling 25 eyes. Based on the EUGOGO classification, the study included 66 eyes from the mild TAO group, 59 eyes from the moderate-to-severe TAO group, and 21 eyes from the DON group. No significant differences in sex (P = 0.177) or age (P = 0.115) were observed between the four groups.

The visual acuity in the DON group was significantly lower (0.28 ± 0.15) compared to the other three groups (vs. control group, P < 0.001; vs. mild group, P < 0.001; vs. moderate-to-severe group, P < 0.001). IOP in the mild TAO group (18.88 ± 4.02) and the DON group (20.33 ± 5.52) was significantly higher than in the control group (15.92 ± 2.41) (P < 0.001). However, no significant differences in IOP were observed between the mild TAO, moderate-to-severe TAO (19.97 ± 5.88), and DON groups (P > 0.05). Regression analysis and diagnostic efficacy evaluation of MRI parameters

Univariate binary logistic regression analysis was conducted for each parameter to examine its association with DON incidence. Among the parameters, exophthalmos, IONSL, MRA, LRA, and SRA demonstrated statistically significant positive correlations with DON risk (all P < 0.05), while VMW, AMR-ON, and ALR-ON showed significant negative correlations (all P < 0.05). Multivariate backward stepwise regression identified SRA and AMR-ON as independent predictors of DON (Table 3).

A nomogram model for predicting DON was developed based on SRA and AMR-ON (Fig. 2). SRA refers to the cross-sectional area of the superior rectus muscle (mm²) with a range of 0–2.4, and AMR-ON denotes the angular relationship between the medial rectus muscle and optic nerve (degrees) ranging from 0 to 28.

Nomogram model based on independent predictive factors for DON. SRA, superior rectus area; AMR-ON, angle between the medial rectus and optic nerve

The Youden index for the SRA + AMR-ON nomogram was 0.729, which reflects the optimal balance between sensitivity and specificity of the model (Table 4). The SRA + AMR-ON nomogram yielded an AUC of 0.906 (P < 0.001), with sensitivity at 90.5% and specificity at 82.4%, reflecting strong diagnostic performance (Fig. 3). The optimal risk threshold corresponding to this Youden index for the combined model was 0.107. Comparative analysis indicated that although MI demonstrated higher specificity, the SRA + AMR-ON nomogram outperformed MI in terms of AUC and sensitivity.

Discussion

Imaging studies of TAO have consistently played a critical role in clinical evaluation. MRI provides high-resolution visualization of intraorbital tissues, offering significant advantages in predicting disease progression and conducting quantitative tissue analysis. Due to edema and hyperplasia of intraorbital tissues, soft tissues exert compressive forces on the bony walls, eyeball, and optic nerve. Among the bony structures, the medial orbital wall is most frequently affected [17]. The medial wall, being the thinnest part of the orbit and adjacent to the ethmoid sinus—supported by multiple thin trabeculae of cancellous bone—is particularly prone to remodeling and deformation under increased intraorbital pressure [18, 19]. In this study, measurements of the medial and lateral orbital walls were performed. Notably, the severity of the condition did not correlate with an increase in the area of the medial orbital wall, nor did it result in a decrease in the area of the lateral orbital wall. It is hypothesized that the thicker bony walls in moderate-to-severe TAO cases may resist bending and remodeling, thus restricting compensatory expansion of the intraorbital space. Despite the elevated intraorbital pressure, sufficient compensation or inadequate compression of the optic nerve may prevent overt visual impairment at this stage.

Bokman et al. [20] demonstrated through three-dimensional orbital reconstructions that the volume of the medial orbital wall is strongly correlated with its depression area, suggesting the depression area as a valid measurement parameter. Additionally, two cases of mild TAO were observed to present with spontaneous medial wall fractures, occurring without apparent precipitating factors and functionally resembling autogenous orbital decompression. It is speculated that incomplete development of the medial bony wall may predispose it to fracture under elevated intraorbital pressure, thus creating compensatory space. In contrast, lateral orbital wall length measurements in our study showed no statistically significant differences across severity groups, a finding that contradicts Cheng et al. [19]. This discrepancy may arise from MRI’s inferior resolution in visualizing bone structures compared to CT, where the insufficient resolution of thinner bony regions may lead to measurement inaccuracies.

Exophthalmos is a common clinical manifestation of TAO [21]. The DON group demonstrated marked proptosis (2.21 ± 0.38 cm), indicating substantial anterior displacement of the globe beyond the orbital confines. While Hertel exophthalmometry remains the clinical standard for proptosis quantification, its reliability is limited by interobserver variability stemming from measurement angles, distances, and operator experience. Our MRI-based methodology, utilizing interzygomatic line and corneal apex distance measurements, minimizes interference from cutaneous/subcutaneous tissues, enhancing reproducibility. However, inherent limitations remain, as supine positioning during MRI induces physiological enophthalmos, leading to discrepancies compared to upright clinical measurements [22, 23]. The optic nerve exhibits intrinsic tortuosity and elasticity to accommodate physiological ocular motility. In TAO, however, excessive globe protrusion leads to pathological nerve stretching, generating shear forces within the optic nerve. When tensile forces exceed compensatory mechanisms, ischemic necrosis may occur, impairing visual function [24]. This study observed that patients with higher severity grades exhibited longer optic nerve lengths, consistent with the findings of Rutkowska-Hinc et al. [25]. However, their clinical adoption remains limited due to technical challenges in standardized quantification.

Substantial clinical evidence supports that enlargement of the extraocular muscles (EOMs), particularly the medial rectus muscle, serves as a critical indicator of TAO severity and plays a pivotal role in the progression of DON [26]. Various methodologies exist for characterizing and quantifying EOM involvement, including cross-sectional dimensional analysis (length and width), cross-sectional area measurements, and three-dimensional volumetric reconstructions—each providing significant clinical insights [27, 28]. In this study, the maximum cross-sectional areas of the four EOMs were quantified in either horizontal or sagittal planes, offering operational simplicity, reduced measurement time, and enhanced clinical utility. It was found that the areas of all four EOMs increased with higher severity grades, with the medial rectus and superior rectus muscles exhibiting the largest proportional increases. These findings are consistent with the results reported by Berger et al. [29]. Epidemiological data indicate that inferior quadrant or irregular visual field defects are the most common pattern in TAO-related vision loss. Starks et al. [30] proposed that excessive thickening of the superior rectus muscle compresses the posterosuperior globe wall, disrupting retinal vascular perfusion.

Barrett et al. [9] introduced the MI in 1988 as a quantitative measure of EOM thickening, a parameter that remains extensively utilized in clinical practice. Chaganti et al. Further advancing predictive capabilities, Zou et al. [11] developed a composite model integrating MI values at the 21-mm posterior globe level with T2-weighted MRI signal intensities of the optic nerve, significantly enhancing accuracy in predicting DON progression.

In 2012, Gonçalves et al. [10] proposed the CI, a quantitative parameter for evaluating orbital apex crowding and optic nerve compression. Their study demonstrated that CI values calculated from coronal Sect. 18 mm posterior to the zygomaticofrontal suture line provided superior predictive accuracy and sensitivity for optic neuropathy. In this study, due to the thicker MRI slice thickness compared to high-resolution CT, precise localization at specific posterior distances from the zygomaticofrontal suture line proved challenging. Consequently, coronal sections closest to the posterior third of the bilateral orbital lateral walls were selected for CI measurements, in line with methodological adaptations proposed by a previous study [31]. Notably, our CI calculation incorporated the superior oblique muscle area, whereas the inferior oblique muscle was excluded due to its poor visualization on imaging and the lack of clinical evidence supporting significant inferior oblique muscle thickening in TAO. This study observed that higher severity grades in patients with TAO were associated with larger CI values, consistent with the findings of Yu et al. [32].

Due to the anatomical specificity of the orbital structure, the optic nerve traverses the narrowest bony apex of the orbit to enter the optic canal. This confined space near the orbital apex renders the nerve highly susceptible to mechanical compression by hypertrophic extraocular muscles or edematous soft tissues, highlighting the critical importance of structural analysis of the orbital apex in evaluating optic neuropathy risk. To assess whether the medial rectus and lateral rectus muscles directly contact and compress the optic nerve, AMR-ON and ALR-ON were measured [33]. Observations indicate that in some patients with DON, both the medial rectus and lateral rectus muscles directly contact the optic nerve, a finding recognized as a key diagnostic parameter for DON [14].

This study revealed that the AMR-ON angle progressively decreased with worsening disease severity. Specifically, patients with higher severity grades exhibited a reduction in the AMR-ON angle, whereas a significant decrease in the ALR-ON angle was observed exclusively in the DON group. This supports the hypothesis that the medial rectus initiates early compressive effects, although further validation is required. Anatomically, the medial rectus runs in close proximity to the optic nerve at the orbital apex, which may explain its predominant role in nerve compression. However, the viscoelastic properties of the optic nerve may partially mitigate the unidirectional pressure exerted by the medial rectus. In contrast, bidirectional compressive forces from simultaneous hypertrophy of the medial and lateral rectus muscles could generate greater mechanical stress, potentially exacerbating ischemic injury or disrupting axonal transport. This mechanistic hypothesis warrants further investigation through biomechanical modeling or histopathological studies.

However, this study has some limitations. First, this is a cross-sectional study, so we could not conduct follow-up observations on imaging changes in the same sample over time, though we still obtained accurate MRI parameters based on TAO severity staging for comparative analysis to inform follow-up research. Second, MRI slices are thicker than CT, risking loss of meaningful planes when analyzing the optic nerve and medial/lateral rectus muscles; some TAO patients with strabismus, especially those with fixed eyeballs, cannot maintain natural straight gaze during MRI, leading to image skew and potential parameter deviations; and the irregular shape of extraocular muscles on MRI may cause inaccuracies in their measurement. Third, the current study did not consider the impact of TAO activity on imaging parameters. Disease activity is a crucial factor in TAO progression, and different activity states may affect orbital tissue characteristics and MRI parameter performance, which might limit the comprehensiveness of the nomogram’s predictive basis. Fourth, the nomogram in this study is constructed based on orbital MRI parameters, while DON prediction could potentially benefit from integrating other relevant quantitative indicators (such as specific visual function indices, thyroid function-related laboratory markers, etc.). A multi-indicator combined prediction model might further improve the accuracy and robustness of early DON prediction.

In conclusion, this study identified the superior rectus area (SRA) and the AMR-ON as independent predictive factors for the development of DON. The multivariate model combining SRA and AMR-ON, established via binary logistic regression, demonstrated high predictive efficacy. MRI has emerged as a critical tool for the diagnosis, longitudinal monitoring, and risk prediction of DON. Its advantages—objectivity, visualizability, and high-resolution capabilities—allow for comprehensive assessments. The diverse MRI measurement protocols not only facilitate volumetric quantification of orbital structures but also provide groundbreaking insights into the severity of inflammatory activity, tissue edema, and ischemic changes. These advancements underscore MRI’s indispensable role in refining clinical decision-making and optimizing therapeutic strategies for DON.
