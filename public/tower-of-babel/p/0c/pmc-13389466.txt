Revealing ultrafast proton-transfer-mediated autoionization as a source of low-energy electrons in hydrogen-bonded systems
Authors: Wael Iskandar, Yi-Siang Wang, Victor A Suarez, Matthew C Rohan, Kirk A Larsen, James Zhong Manis, Travis Severt, Joshua B Williams, Itzik Ben-Itzhak, Thomas M Orlando, Joshua S Kretchmer, Daniel S Slaughter, Thorsten Weber
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIonizing radiation can trigger ultrafast proton transfer, a central mechanism in many chemical and biological functions, that in turn can enable or suppress electron relaxation processes and consequently cause abrupt changes in the reaction pathway. This study combines theory and experiment to probe ultrafast relaxation and dissociation in water dimers following inner- and outer-valence photoionization. By tracking electron and nuclear motion simultaneously, we reveal competing fragmentation pathways that produce low-energy electrons, which are key agents in radiation-induced chemistry, including DNA damage. While low-energy electrons are known to arise via intermolecular Coulombic decay, here we identify a faster relaxation mechanism gated by proton transfer following inner-valence ionization, which we call proton-transfer-mediated autoionization. Occurring within 10 femtoseconds, this process alters fragmentation outcomes, yielding either D3O+ + OD+ or D2O+ + D2O+, depending on the interplay of proton migration and hydrogen back-transfer. Our findings underscore the intricate coupling between electronic and nuclear dynamics in hydrogen-bonded systems and establish proton-transfer-mediated autoionization as a significant pathway for low-energy electron generation.Subject terms: Photochemistry, Atomic and molecular interactions with photons, Chemical physics

Introduction

The ionization of water plays a central role in a wide range of chemical and biological processes. Many reactions, particularly those in biological systems, occur in aqueous environments where water molecules act as proton donors and acceptors, driving key mechanisms such as neutralization, isomerization, charge migration, and the stabilization of intermediates1. Moreover, the generation of low-energy electrons (LEEs) during ionization can lead to direct bond cleavage, causing damage to nearby biomolecules, including DNA, proteins, and lipids2–5. As a result, ionizing radiation can initiate surprisingly complex reaction pathways in systems involving water molecules. While, on an atomistic level, electron emission during water ionization occurs almost instantaneously, the final reaction products (ionic and neutral molecular fragments) are not always formed directly. Instead, bond fission can initiate a cascade of secondary processes, leading to the formation of highly reactive and transient species such as hydroxyl radicals, hydrogen atoms, and hydrated electrons. A detailed understanding of the coupled electron-nuclear dynamics that produce these intermediates, and in particular the generation of LEEs, is essential for accurately predicting water’s response to ionizing radiation.

LEEs have been widely observed across water systems of varying structural complexity, ranging from water monomers to large clusters. In isolated water molecules, LEEs can be effectively produced through molecular autoionization (AI) of excited OH* species6,7 or atomic AI from excited O*8 following valence ionization. In water clusters, a variety of ultrafast, competing processes have been observed, primarily including intermolecular Coulombic decay (ICD) and proton transfer (PT). ICD is a non-local, ultrafast, non-radiative decay process in which, upon core or valence ionization, energy from an ionized water molecule is transferred to a neighboring molecule via a virtual photon. This causes the ejection of a LEE and results in two positively charged water molecules that repel each other9–12. Meanwhile, PT is an ultrafast charged particle motion, which can directly compete with the electron relaxation dynamics, and leads to the formation of hydronium ions (H3O+) and excited hydroxyl radicals (OH*)11,12. However, the emission of LEEs coupled with PT has not yet been seen after inner-valence ionization of water clusters. So far, only the ejection of secondary electrons from the ionized OH by the probe beam13,14 or via proton-transfer-mediated Auger(-Meitner; AM) decay11 with the emission of high-energy AM electrons from core excited OH* has been observed.

The interplay and competition between electronic decay (such as ICD and AI) and nuclear motion (such as PT) during the dissociation process are critical, complex, and challenging to track for experiment and theory alike. Yet, it is precisely this coupled dynamical behavior that governs the generation of secondary electrons and consequent fragmentation products. This is especially evident in water dimers following core ionization. In this case, ultrafast proton motion is initiated, which enables distinct electron relaxation pathways to open, dependent on the specific nuclear geometry11,15,16; this collection of processes has been termed proton-transfer-mediated charge separation (PTM-CS).

In contrast, the dynamics following inner-valence ionization are more nuanced and challenging to track, due to the comparatively lower energy available to the water dimer system. In this case, the AI process is not energetically viable at the ground-state configuration17. Consequently, early experiments by Jahnke et al.9 observed that ICD was the dominant relaxation mechanism, out-performing PT, and leading to the symmetric H2O+ + H2O+ fragmentation. However, later calculations by Richter et al.12 suggested that the ultrafast transfer of a proton, or deuteron, limits the opening for ICD to 2.9 fs in (H2O)2, and to 4 fs in (D2O)2, respectively. This significantly shorter appearance window, compared to the ICD lifetime of 36.6 fs18, is expected to reduce the ICD efficiency. More recently, theoretical work by Kumar et al.18 predicted that the location of the initial ionization, whether on the hydrogen-bonding donor (p-donor) or hydrogen-bonding acceptor (p-acceptor) molecule of the water dimer, strongly influences which process is facilitated or inhibited and thus determines the outcome of the reaction. In a study concurrent to our investigation, Zhang et al.19 reported on the effects of solvation on the ICD efficiency in water clusters and liquid water upon inner-valence ionization. They measured an efficiency below unity and hypothesized that additional non-adiabatic energy relaxation mechanisms are at play. The authors suggested that a smaller fraction of relaxation processes can be classified as local proton-transfer-mediated decay. However, a thorough and systematic exploration into the competition between PT and correlated relaxation dynamics following inner-valence ionization of water, both experimental and theoretical, is still lacking.

In this article, we address the fundamental open question: how does the coupled motion of electrons and nuclei govern the subsequent molecular dissociation of the water dimer during photo-double ionization (PDI)? Specifically, we seek to investigate the non-adiabatic coupling between PT and electronic relaxation, in order to reveal how this dynamic interplay drives the formation of LEEs and dictates the final fragmentation pathways. This is achieved through a unique combination of experimental and theoretical methods. Experimentally, we employ reaction microscopy (a.k.a. COLTRIMS)20–23 to measure the full 3D-momenta of two electrons and two fragment ions, emitted from isolated gas-phase deuterated water dimers upon irradiation with 61 eV synchrotron light. These coincident measurements enable us to distinguish between the direct double ionization (DDI) and sequential ionization (SI) pathways and to specifically investigate the emission of LEEs associated with the symmetric D2O+ + D2O+ and the asymmetric D3O+ + OD+ fragmentation channels. Theoretically, we utilize a non-adiabatic extension of our recently developed non-Hermitian real-time time-dependent density functional theory (RT-TDDFT) framework24 to directly examine the explicit dynamics that contribute to the two fragmentation channels. The key benefit of the methodology is that it allows for the simultaneous treatment of the electron relaxation dynamics and nuclear motion in real time. Through this combined experimental and theoretical approach, we illuminate how site-specific valence photoionization initiates ultrafast PT that governs subsequent electron relaxation, determining whether ICD or AI occurs and which final reaction products are generated. Our results reveal the intricate coupling between electron and nuclear motion that underlies LEE generation in hydrogen-bonded systems as well as a proton mediated decay mechanism following inner-valence ionization of water dimers, underscoring the need for employing explicitly time-dependent approaches to capture the full dynamics.

Results

We present the measured PDI yield of the D2O+ + D2O+ and D3O+ + OD+ fragmentation channels of deuterated water dimers upon absorption of a single linearly polarized 61 eV synchrotron photon as a function of the kinetic energy release (KER) of the heavy fragments and the kinetic energies Ee1 and Ee2 of either emitted electron e1 and e2 in Fig. 1(a) and Fig. 1(b), respectively, on a linear color scale. Our analysis reveals two distinct pathways leading to these fragmentation channels upon PDI: (A) direct double ionization (DDI), where two electrons are simultaneously ejected, and (B) sequential ionization (SI) involving single ionization upon absorption of the photon in the first step, followed by the subsequent ejection of another electron. Both pathways are found to contribute to the D2O+ + D2O+ and D3O+ + OD+ fragmentation channels. In DDI (A), the two indistinguishable electrons share their kinetic energy uniformly, visible as a vertical stripe in Fig. 1(a), (b). In the SI processes (B), where the electrons are emitted in subsequent steps, the firstly emitted photoelectron (high-energy feature) and the secondary electron (high-density feature at low-electron energy) share their sum energy very asymmetrically.

Direct double ionization (DDI)

After separating the DDI from the SI, we show in Fig. 1(c the yield of the DDI process for the D2O+ + D2O+ (black curve) and D3O+ + OD+ (red curve) fragmentation channels as a function of the electron energy sum (Ee1+Ee2), in order to identify the populated dication states of the D2O dimer. In this paper, we occasionally switch between deuterium (D) and hyrdogen (H), deuteron (d) and proton (p), as well as heavy water (D2O) and water (H2O) according to the respective reference or context we are discussing a specific argument, i.e., for instance we adopt the nomenclature used in the respective literature we are referring to. However, please note that, while d and D move slower than p and H according to their mass ratio (1/(Δm)), which affects the momenta, the energetics are expected to be the same.

Theoretically, it has been shown that the dissociation routes of the water dimer dication depend on which electronic state of the dication is populated25. It was concluded that the direct population of the ground state of the dimer dication leads to a H2O+ + H2O+ fragmentation, and that the direct population of the first excited state of the dimer dication leads to a H3O+ + OH+ fragmentation. The vertical double ionization potentials (VDIP) of these two dication states are estimated to be 32.9 eV and 39.2 eV25, respectively. The electron energy sum distributions for the DDI process shown in Fig. 1(c) are centered at around 22.5 eV for the D3O+ + OD+ (red line) and around 28 eV for the D2O+ + D2O+ (black line) fragmentation channels, which corresponds to VDIPs of around 38.5 eV and 33 eV, respectively, following ionization of (D2O)2 by a 61 eV photon. This experimental finding is in close agreement with the theoretical predictions in ref. 25.

Here we address the important question whether the D3O+ + OD+ fragmentation of the (D2O)22+ ion is initiated via deuterium (D) transfer within the two-site ionized (D2O+–D2O+) dimer or via deuteron (D+) transfer within the single-site doubly ionized (D2O–D2O2+) dimer (i.e., with both vacancies on the same D2O). According to previous theoretical work performed on water dimers17, the VDIP spectral lines associated with DDI with one charge on each site primarily cover the main experimental distribution of the D2O+ + D2O+ [compare the black curve with the vertical blue spectral lines in Fig. 1(c)]. However, the VDIP associated with the DDI with two charges on the donor site mainly covers the region where the D3O+ + OD+ is located [compare the red curve with the vertical green spectral lines in Fig. 1(c)]. This latter assignment requires the deuteron transfer to take place on an ultrafast timescale, outrunning the rapid fragmentation of the DOD2+ donor. This transient dication is highly unstable, with dissociation pathways to commence between 10 to 100 fs26–28. For instance, for the prevalent D2O2+ → OD+ + D+ fragmentation with an average KER of 5 eV29, we classically estimate the time to stretch a bond to its breaking point (≈ 6 a.u.) to be on the order of ≈ 15 fs. For the more complex D2O2+ → O+ + D+ + D three-body breakup, the fragmentation time was found to be ≈ 65 fs30. This result reveals the ultrafast nature of intermolecular proton transfer in the simplest aqueous environment, complementing recent investigations of more complex systems like the pyrrole-water dimer, where PT evolves within 50 to 60 fs31. Sequential ionization (SI)

For the SI events, we show the yields of the D2O+ + D2O+ (black curve) and D3O+ + OD+ (red curve) fragmentation channels of (D2O)2 upon 61 eV photon absorption as a function of the kinetic energy of either emitted electron in Fig. 1(d). The LEEs are centered around 0 eV for both fragmentation channels, and the high-energy photoelectrons peak around 24 eV for the D3O+ + OD+ (red curve) and 26.5 eV for the D2O+ + D2O+ (black curve) fragmentation channels, respectively. Note that the abscissa is broken into two sections, 0 - 4 eV and 15 - 35 eV, in order to highlight the distribution of low-energy secondary electrons (we emphasize that the two intervals use the same binning). These LEEs are emitted isotropically, while the fast photoelectrons exhibit a p-wave angular pattern with respect to the polarization vector (see Figs. 3 and 5 in ref. 32 for similar results). While this isotropic emission pattern supports the identification of SI, it does not unambiguously identify the relaxation mechanism (e.g, ICD or autoionization) generating the LEEs32.

The vertical ionization potentials (VIPs) of the inner-valence electrons of water dimers are estimated to be mainly between 29 eV and 39 eV17, corresponding to a photoelectron kinetic energy range of 22 eV to 32 eV following ionization of water dimers by a 61 eV photon. This range covers the observed photoelectron energy distribution presented in Fig. 1(d) for both fragmentation channels. Note that populating inner-valence states with the charge localized on the p-donor produces a lower photoelectron energy than in the case where the charge is localized on the p-acceptor of the dimer ion17,33. This suggests that the D2O+ + D2O+ fragmentation channel is mainly produced by inner-valence ionization of the p-acceptor, while the D3O+ + OD+ fragmentation channel is most likely produced by inner-valence ionization of the p-donor. This is in accordance with the theoretical observation that the primary production of H2O+ + H2O+ via ICD occurs after inner-valence ionization of the p-acceptor, while the production of H3O+ + OH* via PT mainly occurs after inner-valence ionization of the p-donor18.

To investigate the explicit dynamics that yield the detected fragmentation products during the SI events, we conduct an in-depth computational investigation involving three separate yet interconnected calculations. The first is a set of ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) calculations of the neutral deuterated water dimer to generate an ensemble of initial configurations. The second uses the initial configurations to perform further calculations with our recently developed RT-TDDFT methodology24, which is now combined with Ehrenfest dynamics34, to examine the ultrafast coupled electron-nuclear dynamics following inner-valence ionization of either the p-donor or p-acceptor. Lastly, the final configurations and velocities from the RT-TDDFT calculations serve as the starting points for a subsequent set of AIMD calculations on different dicationic electronic states. These are used to examine the asymptotic fragmentation products and calculate the KER of the nuclei. The results of this computational approach are detailed in the following sections, while full computational details of these calculations are provided in the Methods section.

Theory: Ultrafast SI dynamics upon ionization of the p-acceptor

We begin by examining the coupled electron-nuclear dynamics following ionization of the inner-valence 2a1 (oxygen 2s-like) molecular orbital (MO) on the p-acceptor. Fig. 2 displays results calculated using RT-TDDFT with Ehrenfest dynamics for a single representative initial configuration; all calculations from the full ensemble of initial configurations show similar behavior. Fig. 2(a) shows the time-dependent charge on the p-donor (blue) and on the p-acceptor (red). The black line corresponds to the total charge on the system and is obtained by summing the red and blue curves. The p-acceptor begins with a + 1 charge, due to the initial ionization, and remains positively charged. The p-donor starts neutral and is subsequently ionized at the same rate as the full dimer system gains charge, indicative of an ICD process. The time-scale of this process is on the order of 15 fs. This is in excellent agreement with the previous estimate of 18 fs for ICD following ionization of the p-acceptor in water dimers, which was calculated using the equation of motion coupled cluster singles and doubles method (EOM-CCSD)18. The agreement between the RT-TDDFT and the high-level EOM-CCSD results validates the accuracy of the presented methodology for modeling the electronic relaxation. Interestingly, the time-scale also matches our previous work examining the water dimer when the nuclei were frozen in place, indicating that the nuclear dynamics do not strongly influence the time-scale of the electron relaxation event24. The total charge saturates at a value below the expected final value of + 2. However, this is consistent with our previous work24.

Figures 2(b), (c) show the nuclear dynamics over the same timescale and indicate the initial dissociation of the two ionized water dimers following the ICD process. Specifically, Fig. 2(b) plots the distance between the deuterium atom participating in the hydrogen bond and either the oxygen atom on the p-donor (blue) or the p-acceptor (red), while Fig. 2(c) plots the distance between the two oxygen atoms. Fig. 2(c) exhibits a small initial oxygen-oxygen compression for the first ~ 5 fs, followed by a gradual oscillatory increase in the oxygen-oxygen distance until about 31 fs. As discussed in the Results section, we switch the analysis to the AIMD trajectories on the individual dicationic surfaces at this time. This is because the RT-TDDFT trajectories show a rapid and nearly quadratic increase in the oxygen-oxygen distance associated with the Coulomb explosion event (see Supplementary Fig. 4 in Supplementary Note 4 for more details). During this time, the trajectories should decohere onto individual Born-Oppenheimer states, but the Ehrenfest equations of motion do not properly capture this decoherence. Figure 2(b) shows that there is a limited change in the deuterium-oxygen distances until the oxygen-oxygen distance starts to increase. At this point, only the distance to the acceptor increases, implying that the deuterium stays associated with the p-donor water molecule throughout the entire ICD process. Therefore, the initial dynamics indicate that ionization of the p-acceptor of the water dimer leads to the symmetric D2O+ + D2O+ fragmentation channel.

A more complete picture of the electron decay dynamics can be observed by examining Fig. 2(d), which represents the time-dependent occupation of the valence MOs in the isolated monomer basis associated with the (D2O–D2O) dimer, as described in more detail in the Methods section. The spatial representations of these orbitals were generated using the Multiwfn program35. An initial hole is generated in the 2a1 MO of the p-acceptor (brown). This MO is repopulated by electron density relaxing from the valence p-like MOs on the p-acceptor site, indicated by the simultaneous rise of the 2a1 population (brown) and the decay of population in the 1b1 (yellow), 3a1 (orange), and 1b2 (red) MOs during the first 15 fs. Over the same timescale, the 3a1 (blue) and 1b2 (cyan) MOs on the p-donor lose population associated with ionization of the secondary electron from these orbitals. This physical picture is consistent with the ICD mechanism indicated by Fig. 2(a).

Interestingly, we additionally observe a small amount of resonant hole-transfer between the 2a1 MOs on the p-acceptor and p-donor during the ICD event. This is indicated by the out-of-phase oscillations of the brown and dark green lines in the 5 to 10 fs time interval; similar behavior was observed in real-time simulations of the (Ar–H2O) dimer24. Lastly, we should note that some trajectories do show more complicated electron dynamics after the initial ICD event, which is associated with additional non-adiabatic relaxation and resonant charge-transfer processes (see Supplementary Fig. 5 in Supplementary Note 4 for more details). These results highlight the importance of developing and utilizing simulation tools that can capture the coupled electron-nuclear dynamics during the ultrafast electron relaxation events, but do not alter the main conclusions drawn in this manuscript.

Theory: Ultrafast SI dynamics upon ionization of the p-donor

We now turn our attention to the dynamics following inner-valence ionization of the p-donor shown in Fig. 3, which exhibits more complex behavior. Fig. 3(a) depicts the time-dependent charge on the p-donor (blue) and on the p-acceptor (red) sites. The black line corresponds to the charge on the system, obtained by summing of the red and blue curves. Fig. 3(b) shows the distance between the deuterium in the hydrogen bond and the p-donor (blue) and the p-acceptor (red) associated with the left y-axis. The same panel additionally shows the oxygen-oxygen distance (black) associated with the right y-axis. Fig. 3

Fig. 3(a) shows that, after the initial ionization, the charge on the p-donor is + 1 and the p-acceptor is neutral. On the other hand, Fig. 3(b) unveils that the deuteron is initially bonded to the p-donor. Immediately, the deuteron begins to transfer from the p-donor to the p-acceptor, reaching the halfway point between the two molecules at ≈ 6 fs. At this time, we switch the assignment of the deuteron from the p-donor to the p-acceptor, which leads to the sudden switch in the charge of the two molecules in Fig. 3(a); the p-donor drops in charge from ≈ + 1.0 down to ≈ + 0.2, while the p-acceptor increases in charge from ≈ + 0.2 up to about + 1.0. The change in charge is not perfectly + 1.0, because the deuteron carries a small amount of electron density along with it in the dimer complex.

The transfer of the deuteron happens faster than ICD can set in, which is concluded from the small and gradual increase in the total charge in the dimer system before 6 fs, represented by the black line in Fig. 3(a). However, as the deuteron continues to transfer, there is a rapid increase in the total charge from around 7 to 12 fs, associated with an increase in charge on the p-donor, depicted by the blue line in Fig. 3(a). This corresponds to ionization of the p-donor, which, during this time window, is best classified as a super-excited near-neutral OD* species; the OD* is in an excited electronic state above the ionization threshold of OD. The emerging picture suggests that deuteron transfer opens up a channel for AI on the now near-neutral OD p-donor species. Interestingly, the timescales for the total charge evolution following initial ionization of the p-acceptor or p-donor are comparable; see the black lines in Figs. 2(a), 3(a). We attribute this similarity to p-donor ionization, inducing an ultrafast deuteron transfer, which outcompetes electron relaxation, followed by a delayed intramolecular AI; the latter electronic decay step is inherently faster than the intermolecular ICD process triggered by p-acceptor ionization.

Figure 3(b) indicates that, during the transfer of the deuteron and the subsequent AI process, the oxygen-oxygen distance initially compresses similarly to the ionization process of the p-acceptor, featured in Fig. 2(c). After ≈ 12 fs, the AI process is mainly complete, and the oxygen-oxygen distance begins to increase rapidly, associated with the onset of the Coulomb explosion. However, in comparison to Fig. 2, here, due to the deuteron transfer, the deuteron stays more closely bonded to the p-acceptor as the O–O distance increases, which is consistent with the asymmetric fragmentation into D3O+ + OD+.

We can elaborate on the specifics of this mechanism by examining the time-dependent MO occupations after ionization of the p-donor, which are plotted in Fig. 3(c). In comparison to Fig. 2, the plot specifically depicts the occupation of the MOs in the isolated monomer basis associated with the (D3O+–DO−) dimer, as described in more detail in the Methods section. We find this choice to be the most convenient because the electron relaxation dynamics do not begin to occur until after the initial transfer of the deuteron. However, the analogous plot in the isolated monomer basis associated with the (D2O–D2O) dimer shows effectively the same behavior. Fig. 3(c) illustrates that an initial hole is generated in the 2a1 MO of the p-donor (green). There is minimal change in the occupation of any of the MOs until 7 fs, which is after the time when the deuteron transfers from the p-donor to the p-acceptor. At this point, the 2a1 MO of the p-donor is repopulated on the same timescale as the p-like MOs (3a1, cyan and the two degenerate E states, purple) on the p-donor lose electron density associated with relaxation and ionization from these orbitals; it is not possible to distinguish which orbitals contribute to refilling the 2a1 MO versus secondary ionization. The data show that the electron dynamics occur only within orbitals associated with the p-donor. This physical picture is consistent with the deuteron transfer opening up a channel for AI on the OD p-donor species, as indicated by Fig. 3(a), (b).

To confirm the energetic feasibility of the observed mechanism predicted by the RT-TDDFT trajectory calculations, we present in Fig. 3(d) an intensity map of the relative pole strengths for the single cationic states associated with 2a1 ionization on the p-donor. These values were computed using the ADC(3) method at each geometry along the RT-TDDFT trajectory36,37. The ADC(3) method accurately captures the breakdown of the molecular-orbital picture in the water dimer, in which a multitude of single cationic states form a quasi-continuum band17,38. The energies of these single cationic states are compared with those of the lowest-energy triplet and singlet dicationic states, which are shown as solid and dashed black lines, respectively. The dicationic states are calculated using EOM-DIP-CCSD. The combination of ADC(3) and EOM-DIP-CCSD has been successfully used to examine the energetics of secondary ionization processes previously39. Single slices along the intensity map at specific points in time and an equivalent figure plotted as a function of D–O distance are additionally presented in Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7 in Supplementary Note 5. Full computational details of these calculations are provided in the Methods section. Figure 3(d) shows that, initially, the energies of the single cationic states lie well above those of the dicationic states. This indicates that ICD is energetically feasible near the minimum-energy configuration of the water dimer. As time progresses and the deuteron begins to transfer, the energies of most single cationic states drop below those of either dicationic state, rendering secondary ionization energetically unfavorable in this region. This suppression of ICD due to ultrafast PT has been observed in previous studies12,18,19. However, as the deuteron continues to transfer and time evolves further, the energies of many single cationic states rise again — this time above the triplet dicationic state. This clearly indicates an energetic gate opening for secondary ionization in configurations where the deuteron becomes more closely associated with the p-acceptor. In this regime, the secondary ionization corresponds to autoionization of the p-donor, consistent with the mechanistic picture provided by the more detailed RT-TDDFT trajectories. Similar potential energy curves were reported in the prior work by Richter et al.12 and in a concurrent work by Zhang et al.19, all in agreement with the present finding that the triplet dicationic surface lies below the single cationic surface at both short and long O-H distances.

We note that the cross-over point between the single cationic and dicationic surfaces in Fig. 3(d) does not perfectly match the onset of autoionization in the RT-TDDFT trajectories, with a discrepancy on the order of 1-2 fs. However, perfect agreement between the two types of calculations should not be expected, given the differences in the level of theory and the fact that the RT-TDDFT calculations include both explicit dynamics and non-adiabatic effects, which are not included in the static electronic structure calculations.

The trajectory presented in Fig. 3 corresponds to a single deuteron transfer event. However, a subset of the trajectories display an additional deuteron transfer back to the p-donor, as shown in Fig. 4. We note that a concurrent investigation40 predicts a transient back-and-forth motion of a proton following inner-valence ionization of the p-donor in (H2O)2. In general, a hydrogen or proton back-transfer happens when either (i) the energy landscape is nearly symmetric or only weakly stabilized after the initial proton transfer, or (ii) stabilization of the transferring particle is absent or outpaced by its motion. The following analysis details how this deuteron back-transfer process unfolds. Within this set of trajectories, the initial ≈ 15 fs show analogous behavior as observed in Fig. 3, i.e., an ultrafast initial deuteron transfer followed by an AI process of the p-donor that yields a dimer species consistent with the D3O+ + OD+ fragmentation channel. However, Fig. 4(b) shows that after 15 fs, this trajectory exhibits distinctly different behavior. The deuteron transfers back from the p-acceptor to the p-donor from 15 to 25 fs. At ≈ 19 fs, Fig. 4(a) exhibits a second sudden switch in the charge of the two molecules. However, in this case, the p-acceptor only drops in charge from ≈ + 1.0 down to ≈ + 0.5, while the p-donor increases in charge from ≈ + 0.4 up to ≈ + 0.9, which suggests that the deuteron carries significant electron density along with it. In addition, Fig. 4(d) shows that electron density is transferred from the 3a1 MO on the p-acceptor (yellow) to the 3a1 MO on the p-donor (cyan) during this time window. This leads to the observed increase of charge on the p-acceptor back to ≈ + 1.0 and the decrease of charge on the p-donor to ≈ + 0.5 in Fig. 4(a). Taken together, the results indicate that the back-transfer corresponds to an effective deuterium-atom transfer, such that the final nuclear configuration and dimer charge distribution are consistent with the symmetric D2O+ + D2O+ fragmentation channel. After the transfer event is complete, the two fragments begin to move apart from each other, as the dimer begins to dissociate.

SI fragmentation pathways and their KER

Here, we analyze the longer time dynamics associated with the fragmentation of the dicationic dimer species, following the ultrafast dynamics presented previously. As described in detail in the Methods section, the ensemble of final configurations and velocities obtained from the RT-TDDFT calculations is used to initialize a set of AIMD calculations on various dicationic potential energy surfaces (PESs) of the water dimer. We calculate the dynamics on the excited-state singlet surface (S1) and the ground- and excited-state triplet surfaces (T0 and T1, respectively) for the trajectories in which the p-acceptor was initially ionized. We only calculate the dynamics on the T0 and T1 surfaces following p-donor ionization, because the singlet surfaces are not energetically accessible following deuteron transfer12. These calculations are used to obtain the final yields of the two fragmentation channels and their KERs, which are compared to the experimental results.

In Fig. 5, we present the experimental and theoretical KER distributions for the recorded symmetric D2O+ + D2O+ and asymmetric D3O+ + OD+ fragmentation channels during the SI events. The KER spectrum is a fingerprint of the nuclear dynamics of the dimer cation, as it carries information about the internuclear distances at which ICD takes place and is, therefore, inter alia sensitive to the ratio of the electronic decay lifetime to the vibrational period.

The experimental distributions were produced while gating on the coincidence SI events that exhibit strong asymmetric energy sharing in Fig. 1 and while subtracting the contribution from the DDI in that region. The details on the separation of DDI and SI contributions are described in Supplementary Note 2. The symmetric fragmentation channel peaks at a KER of ≈ 4.2 eV and has a small tail towards lower values, matching the KER distribution in ref. 9 very well. In the asymmetric fragmentation channel, this tail is much stronger. The theoretical results accurately capture the main peaks in both fragmentation channels, with ionization of the p-donor or fragmentation on the T1 surface leading to higher average KER. The latter finding is most likely associated with a dissociation of the dication along a higher electronic state, which inherently carries more energy. However, the theoretical KER distributions struggle to capture the low-energy tails, especially the more prominent tail of the asymmetric fragmentation channel. This may result from errors in the DFT dissociation curves or the absence of nuclear quantum effects that would facilitate exploration of the region of the PES that contributes to the low-energy tail. We emphasize that the lack of nuclear quantum effects may explain the small yield of low-KER trajectories, but this does not influence the central conclusion of the present work, i.e., the importance of deuteron transfer gating the electronic decay mechanism and deuterium back-transfer that contributes to the symmetric fragmentation channel.

While it is tempting to interpret the KERs in terms of internuclear distances, the broad energy dispersion of the asymmetric OD+ + D3O+ channel compared to the symmetric D2O+ + D2O+ fragmentation channel does not reflect an onset of the Coulomb explosion at different bond lengths. This is because the timescales for ICD and the total deuteron transfer, followed by an intramolecular AI process, are comparable, both on the order of 15 fs, allowing only a 2 to 3% increase in the O–O distance (see Figs. 2, 3). Instead, we hypothesize that the low-energy tail is associated with trajectories in which deuteron transfer leads to significant vibrational and rotational excitations, which remove energy from the translational degrees of freedom. This is based on the behavior of the trajectory that results in a low-KER of ≈ 1.9 eV in Fig. 5(b), which exhibits significant vibrational and rotational excitations compared to other trajectories.

Summary of the SI dissociation pathways

Taken together, the results indicate that three distinct mechanistic pathways contribute to two final fragmentation channels during the SI process. The mechanistic pathways are determined based on the ionization of the p-donor versus p-acceptor site and the number of times a particle is transferred before the dissociation sets in. Eq. (4) corresponds to the ionization of the p-acceptor, followed by ICD and direct dissociation into the symmetric D2O+ + D2O+ fragmentation channel without deuteron transfer. This corroborates earlier work that used AIMD calculations to indicate that p-acceptor ionization does not initiate particle motion18. However, we emphasize that our simulations go beyond AIMD to include explicit non-adiabatic effects, which prior studies indicate are essential to accurately model proton transfer dynamics in valence-ionized water clusters41,42. The process in Eq. (4) is the dominant contributor to the symmetric fragmentation pathway and accounts for 50% of all possible SI pathways to two-body dissociation of the dimer dication. Following the same logic as discussed in the previous section, this number was obtained by multiplying the 100% yield of the symmetric D2O+ + D2O+ fragmentation channel observed following p-acceptor ionization (see Table 2) by 0.5, which assumes that the p-donor and p-acceptor are equally likely to be ionized.

Eqs. (5) and (6) correspond to the ionization of the p-donor. Both equations follow the same initial dynamics during which ionization initiates an ultrafast deuteron transfer that shuts off the ICD process. This aligns with previous work, showing that PT suppresses ICD in the water dimer12,18. However, here we observe that this pathway triggers an intramolecular AI process in the OD p-donor species. We term this process proton-transfer-mediated autoionization (PTM-AI) to match the analogous PTM-Auger process present in the context of core ionization events, which is one of the processes under the umbrella of PTM-CS11,15,16. Yet, this mechanism has not been observed after inner-valence ionization until now. Interestingly, the feasibility of PTM-AI is corroborated by previous high-level electronic structure calculations, which show that the dicationic triplet surface is below the energy of the single cationic surface at short and long O-H distances12. However, the possibility of this phenomenon was not discussed because the asymmetric D3O+ + OD+ fragmentation channel was not experimentally observed before.

The difference between Eq. (5) and Eq. (6) is that Eq. (5) corresponds to the dissociation into the asymmetric D3O+ + OD+ fragmentation channel directly following intramolecular AI, which accounts for 35% of all possible SI pathways discussed here. In comparison, Eq. (6) contains an additional back-transfer of a neutral deuterium atom, which then results in a symmetric D2O+ + D2O+ fragmentation. Therefore, the reaction channel described in Eq. (6) is a secondary contributor to the symmetric fragmentation channel that has not previously been discussed in the literature, but which is essential to predict the correct experimental fragmentation yield of the water dimer upon SI (see Table 2). This pathway accounts for 15% of the three SI pathways that contribute to D2O+ + D2O+ and D3O+ + OD+. In addition, the presence of the deuterium back-transfer channel described in Eq. (6) highlights that the electron energy distribution associated with the symmetric fragmentation channel (black curve) in Fig. 1d is not purely p-acceptor ionization but a combination of both p-acceptor and p-donor ionization. Consequently, we are able to subtract the PTM-AI contribution from this curve to isolate the LEEs and their correlated photoelectrons that are associated solely with the ICD process upon SI (see Supplementary Note 3 for more details).

We emphasize that the relative ratio between the three pathways computed in the present work will be sensitive to the exact time-scales predicted by the RT-TDDFT simulations, which should be taken as estimates. Specifically, the single-determinant description of the time-dependent wavefunction may not fully describe the multi-configurational nature of the inner-valence states, which could affect the precise branching ratios. However, the two important general qualitative conclusions, namely (1) PTM-AI is an energetically feasible and relevant pathway and (2) back-transfer of the deuterium introduces a clear pathway to the symmetric fragmentation channel, are robust to the limitations of the computational techniques and benefit from the ability of the presented RT-TDDFT methodology to simultaneously simulate non-adiabatic and secondary ionization dynamics. The former finding is corroborated through numerous high-level static electronic structure calculations12,19, including the results presented in Fig. 3(d). The latter finding is necessary to explain the experimentally observed relative ratio between the symmetric and asymmetric fragmentation channels.

Although the experiments were performed on D2O dimers, the same underlying physical principles govern the dissociation of (H2O)2. The electronic potential energy surfaces that dictate the available decay pathways are essentially mass-independent. However, the initial proton transfer, which drives the subsequent fragmentation dynamics, will proceed on a Δm = 2 faster timescale (with Δm denoting the mass ratio of the deuteron to the proton), driven by the higher mobility of the lighter proton. To confirm that the PTM-AI mechanism is not an artifact of deuteration, we performed additional RT-TDDFT simulations on H2O dimers (see Supplementary Note 6 for more details). While a full investigation of the more complex dynamics with ample statistics is beyond the scope of this work, our calculations confirm that PTM-AI is an intrinsic feature in (H2O)2. We find that the initial proton transfer is significantly faster than in (D2O)2, consistent with the lighter proton mass, and that the subsequent dynamics often involve multiple proton back-transfer events before dissociation. These findings confirm that the back-transfer pathway, which contributes to the symmetric fragmentation channel previously attributed exclusively to ICD, and the production of the asymmetric fragmentation channel via PTM-AI, are a general feature of the ionized water dimer.

Furthermore, we note that water dimers are unique: among water clusters, they are the only species containing a pure proton acceptor43, owing to their distinctive geometry. In contrast, larger clusters are dominated by proton donors, which our work shows favor the PTM-AI mechanism. The distinctive geometry of the dimer precludes additional PT events from other water molecules that could occur in larger clusters, and the small number of nuclear degrees of freedom in the dimer most likely limits non-radiative relaxation pathways, both of which would compete with PTM-AI. Crucially, any comparison with the efficiency values reported in ref. 12 requires careful distinction between physical quantities. The values reported in our Tables 1 and 2 represent relative branching ratios between specific, detected autoionization channels (ICD vs. PTM-AI), whereas ref. 12 reported absolute quantum efficiencies (total electron yield per ionization event), which implicitly account for example for non-radiative loss channels in the denominator. Furthermore, because the LEEs from ICD and PTM-AI are spectrally similar, previous measurements of ICD efficiency in large clusters likely represented an aggregate of both ICD and PTM-AI channels, weighted by the cluster’s specific donor/acceptor ratio. Consequently, any extrapolation of the relative probabilities reported here for the D2O+ + D2O+ and D3O+ + OD+ fragmentation channels to larger clusters should be approached with caution.

Discussion

This study combines experimental and theoretical approaches to examine the symmetric and asymmetric fragmentation of deuterated water dimers following PDI, revealing detailed insights into the role of coupled non-adiabatic nuclear and electron dynamics in LEE generation, while simultaneously identifying hitherto unknown dissociation pathways. For DDI, symmetric fragmentation into D2O+ + D2O+ occurs twice as frequently as the asymmetric D3O+ + OD+ fragmentation channel. In the latter case, a rapid deuteron transfer takes place from the double-ionized p-donor to the neutral p-acceptor of the dimer system. This deuteron transfer is ultrafast (≤ 100 fs), as it must occur before the doubly charged donor site of the water starts to fragment.

SI, an effective source of LEEs, similarly favors symmetric fragmentation, occurring twice as often as the asymmetric pathway. Our detailed theoretical investigation reveals that ICD followed by direct fragmentation into D2O+ + D2O+ occurs after ionization of the p-acceptor, while ionization of the p-donor causes ultrafast deuteron transfer, in agreement with previous studies (e.g., see ref. 12). However, here we identify and explore a relaxation pathway after ionization of the p-donor, PTM-AI, in which the ultrafast deuteron transfer initiates AI on the p-donor, i.e., on the left-behind OD* intermediate [see Fig. 6(e)]. The PTM-AI process can be considered as the inner-valence counterpart to PTM-Auger decay, which occurs after core ionization. Consequently, PTM-AI expands the umbrella of PTM-CS to now encompass inner-valence ionization in addition to core ionization events. Yet, these two processes proceed differently in time, and we reveal that the behavior in the inner-valence regime is unique. First, PT plays a crucial dual function: it not only acts as a quenching mechanism for the non-local ICD channel as previously thought12, but also serves as an ultrafast gating mechanism that enables the faster local AI pathway, thereby revealing a significant overlooked source of LEEs. Second, remarkably, the PTM-AI process does not lead to a single outcome; instead of exclusively fragmenting into D3O+ + OD+ upon completion of the deuteron transfer and subsequent AI, the dimer dication may undergo an additional ultrafast deuterium atom back-transfer to the now-charged p-donor site. This back-transfer reveals an additional layer of complexity, redirecting the fragmentation outcome to the symmetric D2O+ + D2O+ channel, a pathway previously considered exclusive to ICD. In D2O dimers, this process ultimately contributes ≈ 15% to the three possible two-body dissociation routes of (D2O)2 upon SI, and in particular, it contributes ≈ 23% to the symmetric fragmentation into D2O+ + D2O+ upon SI of the heavy water dimer. This means that around a quarter of the events yielding symmetric reaction products upon SI actually stems from deuterium back-transfer! Importantly, the presence of this pathway is essential for accurately predicting the overall experimental fragmentation yield. While our experimental isolation of these asymmetric channels was demonstrated specifically for D2O dimers, our complementary theoretical simulations strongly indicate that this PTM-AI mechanism is an intrinsic feature of H2O dimers as well. Consequently, our findings call for a reinterpretation of the origin of LEEs in hydrogen-bonded systems such as water, which have previously been attributed exclusively to ICD, and more advanced measurements of complex systems in the future. Until the present work, the existence of PTM-AI has only been hypothesized based on indirect measurements19. Here, the experimental isolation and quantification of this proton-transfer-gated decay have now been achieved, and our highly differential work disentangles and characterizes this important PTM-AI pathway [see Eqs. (5) and (6)].

In general, our study reveals how electronic states, proton migration, hydrogen back-transfer, and non-adiabatic electron-nuclear coupling shape the fragmentation of (deuterated) water dimers after PDI and drive LEE production from ICD and autoionization, which are known for their role in radiation damage. These insights pave the way for time-resolved experiments, such as X-ray pump-probe or attosecond spectroscopy, in order to capture ultrafast proton and electron dynamics and clarify the interplay between ICD and PTM-AI. Extending this approach to larger water clusters or other hydrogen-bonded systems may help generalize the mechanisms and explore the role of the environment in mediating AI, charge migration, and LEE generation, with implications for radiation chemistry, atmospheric science, and biology. Previous studies on water clusters and bulk liquid have already revealed that ultrafast proton transfer is a critical competing pathway that significantly limits the efficiency of ICD12,19. Since our work reveals that PT can also gate a faster autoionization channel, representing a significant inner-valence analogue within the PTM-CS framework, we expect that extended hydrogen-bond/proton-transfer networks will amplify this dual role of enhancing PTM-AI while suppressing ICD. Solvation effects can further stabilize charges via dielectric screening and hydrogen bonding, effectively lowering the barrier for PTM-AI and making charge-separated states longer-lived. Solvation can also introduce dynamic fluctuations (e.g., intermolecular vibration and libration), which may modulate the efficiency of charge separation and recombination. Although the rigorous, differential measurements needed to quantify PTM-AI remain challenging for larger systems, ongoing advances in electron-ion coincidence momentum imaging at ultrafast X-ray light sources may soon enable direct studies of these fundamental processes in more complex environments, ranging from bulk liquids to the critical hydration shells of biomolecules.

Methods

Experiment

The experiments were conducted at beamline 10.0.1.3 of the Advanced Light Source (ALS) synchrotron ring at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory using a reaction microscope (REMI/COLTRIMS endstation). Linearly polarized 61 eV photons were focused into a well-defined interaction region (≈ 1.0 × 0.3 × 0.3 mm3), where they intersected the adiabatically cooled D2O supersonic gas jet (≈ 50 K parallel, ≈ 15 K perpendicular) at right angles. The monochromized light had an energy resolution of ΔE ≈ 200 meV44. Electrons were detected with an 80 mm MCP chevron stack and three-layer delay-line anode, while ions were recorded with a 120 mm MCP chevron stack and two-layer delay-line anode45.

Choosing D2O as a target sample over H2O for the present experiments suppressed background from residual H2O in the main experimental chamber (≈ 1.2 × 10−8 Torr). Although the inherent (experiment-dependent) mass resolution of the REMI technique20,22,23 typically allows the separation of numerous dissociation channels via photoion-photoion coincidence (PIPICO) spectra7,8, resolving closely spaced channels within congested spectra remains challenging. The use of D2O instead of H2O enables a better separation of the reaction channels that can be produced from the jet, which is a crucial capability for the present investigation. It is highly relevant for the asymmetric fragmentation channel of interest in our studies, which becomes energetically accessible above ≈ 37 eV and should, in principle, have been observed in earlier COLTRIMS experiments on (H2O)2 at 43 eV9. However, those studies were limited by two key experimental challenges. First, the poor mass resolution between the symmetric (H2O+ + H2O+) and asymmetric (H3O+ + OH+) channels, whose fragments differ by only a single mass unit (18/18 amu vs. 17/19 amu), making them difficult to distinguish. Second, significant background contributions from warm water emitted from the apparatus in-vacuum surfaces and gas jet, leading to false coincidences. Conversely, D2O dimers provide a much higher contrast: the rare four-particle coincidence signal can be clearly distinguished from false events, and the two fragmentation channels are better isolated (20/20 amu vs. 18/22 amu). Nonetheless, as mentioned in the discussion in Supplementary Note 1, some overlap between the channels of interest remains.

For the double ionization of (D2O)2, which contributes to about 1 to 3% to the recorded events from the supersonic gas jet, we measured two electrons (≤ 30 eV) and two ionic fragments (≤ 22 eV) in coincidence within the full 4π solid angle on their respective detectors21,46. From the time-of-flights and positions of each particle, we retrieve the final-state 3D momenta and, hence, can deduce observables like kinetic energies or emission angles, in order to analyze and interpret the recorded data.

Theory

All theoretical calculations are performed using DFT with the LC-PBE* functional and the aug-cc-pVDZ basis47,48. We use a tuned value of the range-separation parameter, which showed accurate behavior in previous work24. Due to practical considerations, all calculations treat the nuclei classically, with the masses of the nuclei associated with deuterons set to 2.0 amu. AIMD calculations are performed using NWChem49, while the RT-TDDFT calculations are performed using our recently developed TiDES (Time-Dependent Electronic Structure) software package50.

The ensemble of initial configurations of the water dimer before ionization is generated from AIMD simulations on the ground-state neutral PES. The ensemble is obtained from the set of final configurations of 315 separate 5 ps constant-temperature simulations. The trajectories are run with a time-step of 1 fs and a temperature of 30 K. Thermostatting is performed using a Langevin thermostat with a friction coefficient of γ = 0.1 a.u. This procedure ensures that all configurations within the ensemble are decorrelated.

Each configuration within the constant-temperature ensemble is used to generate two separate RT-TDDFT calculations associated with the ionization of the p-donor or p-acceptor, respectively. The initial ionization event is accounted for by manually removing one electron from the 2a1 MO of the corresponding molecule from the AIMD 1-electron reduced density matrix (1RDM). We find that the 2a1 MO is well-localized for all nuclear configurations, making this choice unambiguous. Full details of this procedure follow directly from previous work24.

We note that this choice is an inherent approximation of the utilized methodology. Previous work, as also illustrated by the results in Fig. 3(d), shows a breakdown of the MO picture for the inner-valence states in water. This breakdown is associated with the presence of many single-cationic states of similar energy, all with single-particle character that resembles ionization out of the 2a1 molecular orbital17,38. Consequently, the most accurate description of the initial state should be comprised of a linear combination of the semi-continuum of 2a1 states. Our choice of manually removing one electron, which does not correspond to a stationary state of the system because we do not include orbital relaxation, can be thought of as, at least, an approximate averaging over this semi-continuum. This approximation appears to be a valid approach, as verified by the excellent agreement between the timescales of the ICD process following p-acceptor ionization calculated using our RT-TDDFT approach and EOM-CCSD calculations from previous work18.

We update the nuclear positions, forces, and electronic density matrix at every time step within the integration scheme.

The intensity map of the relative pole strengths of the single cationic states presented in Fig. 3(d) is calculated at the ADC(3)/aug-cc-pVDZ36,37 level of theory as implemented in the PySCF 2.10.0 quantum chemistry package52,53. The geometries are taken every 20 a.u. (~ 0.5 fs) along the RT-TDDFT trajectory depicted in Fig. 3. The ADC(3) spectra are convolved with Gaussian functions with a full-width at half maximum of 0.20 eV to introduce broadening into the spectrum, as has been done previously39. The energies of the dicationic states are obtained at the same geometries using the EOM-DIP-CCSD/aug-cc-pVDZ54,55 level of theory as implemented in QChem 6.2.256.

The final fragmentation yield and KER are obtained from a set of AIMD trajectories on various dicationic PESs (see Table 2). The initial configurations and velocities for the AIMD trajectories correspond to the nuclear configurations and velocities of the RT-TDDFT calculations at 31 fs. All of the AIMD trajectories are run using open-shell DFT with the appropriate spin-multiplicity and a time-step of 0.12 fs; a smaller time-step is necessary to fully resolve the Coulomb explosion event. The excited-state trajectories are calculated using LR-TDDFT under the Tamm-Dancoff approximation. An initial estimate of the KER for each PES is obtained by calculating the difference in kinetic energy of the center-of-mass of the two fragments at the end of the AIMD trajectories and at the beginning of the RT-TDDFT trajectories. The AIMD trajectories are run for a total simulation time of 360 fs to ensure that the kinetic energy has plateaued.
