Professional self-perception of hospital clinical pharmacists and its influencing factors: a systematic review
Authors: Qi Chen, Shicheng Chen, Jingru Yang, Xiao Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe global demand for clinical pharmacy services has intensified due to aging populations, increasing polypharmacy, and healthcare workforce shortages. The professional self-perception of clinical pharmacists (CPs) critically impacts service quality, retention, and patient relationships, necessitating strategic career planning.AimTo systematically evaluate hospital CPs’ professional self-perception in different countries and regions and analyze its influencing factors, providing a reference for policy optimization in low- and middle-income countries.MethodsFrom two English databases and four Chinese databases, we searched for original studies on the perception of CPs regarding their profession until January 2025. Two researchers independently screened the literature and extracted the data, and the included studies were assessed for quality according to the Let Evidence Guide Every New Decision (LEGEND) evidence evaluation tools.ResultsA total of 18 studies (17 cross-sectional studies and one prospective study) spanning 1977–2023, covering the USA, China, Sweden, and Vietnam were included. In the USA, higher levels of satisfaction are reported among pharmacists who are engaged more in direct patient care; however, challenges such as insufficient recognition and limited career advancement still remain. In China, CPs generally exhibit positive professional self-perception, although gaps in competency, income dissatisfaction, and workload challenges persist. Swedish CPs demonstrated high professional acceptance, effective interdisciplinary collaboration, and confidence in expanding their prescribing responsibilities within healthcare teams. Vietnam highlighted stress from patient care responsibilities and income disparities. The key influencing factors that affect CP job satisfaction include intrinsic factors (e.g., clinical activity involvement, autonomy), extrinsic factors (e.g., training) and other factors (e.g., level of hospital, level of skills).ConclusionsCPs’ professional self-perception exhibited notable regional differences. Given the qualitative nature of data and heterogeneity of study designs, the results still suggest that clinical activity involvement and autonomy consistently correlate with higher job satisfaction among CPs, whereas inadequate recognition, excessive workload, limited advancement opportunities and financial constraints hinder professional fulfillment.Systematic review registrationRegistered protocol at PROSPERO: CRD42024470413.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13472-4.Keywords: Clinical pharmacist, Profession, Self-perception, Influencing factor, Systematic review

Introduction

Clinical pharmacy originated at the University of Michigan in the 1960s, driven by dissatisfaction with traditional practice norms and the demand for health professionals with comprehensive knowledge of drug therapeutics [1]. In recent years, the critical need for wider adoption of clinical pharmacy services has emerged as a global imperative, due to population aging, increasing polypharmacy rates and growing shortages of healthcare professionals [2].

Self-perception is defined as the understanding and evaluation of one’s own psychology and behavior state [3]. The self-perception of clinical pharmacists (CPs) regarding their profession significantly impacts service quality, employee retention and relationships with patients, necessitating hospital pharmacy departments to prioritize career planning to advance clinical pharmacy [4]. Job satisfaction is personal satisfaction related to the work situation and is relatively detectable in quantitative studies as one of the significant parts of self-perception. In this study, we performed a systematic review on the CPs’ professional self-perception and analyzed the influencing factors, offering insights for policy optimization in low- and middle-income countries.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review conformed to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) checklist [5]. It was registered in PROSPERO under ID number CRD42024470413.

Literature search

Databases including MEDLINE, Embase, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Data, Chinese Scientific Journal Database (VIP) and Chinese Biomedical Database (CBM), were searched from inception to January 2025. The strategies developed and used for each database are presented in Table S1.

Study selection

Two authors independently screened the literature, extracted the data, and cross-checked. Discussions with a third reviewer handled all inconsistent inclusion decisions. The missing data were supplemented by contacting the authors. During the literature screening process, the title and abstract were initially reviewed, and after excluding irrelevant literature, the full texts were further read to determine whether to be included.

Quality assessment

The included studies were assessed for quality according to the Let Evidence Guide Every New Decision (LEGEND) evidence evaluation tools [6].

Perception of CPs in their profession

USA

As one of the birthplaces of clinical pharmacy, the USA has formed a relatively complete clinical pharmacy practice model, and Pharm.D. training system. Over decades of development, American CPs have consistently reported greater job satisfaction when they are engaged in more patient care; however, persistent challenges such as dissatisfaction with time allocation and high burnout rates continue to affect the development of clinical pharmacy.

A survey of hospital pharmacists in a midwestern metropolitan area in 1977 indicated that among different types of pharmacists, CPs were the most satisfied group, with a general satisfaction score of 3.00 out of 5 [7]. In 1981, Rauch surveyed pharmacists assigned to 35 U.S. Army Medical Treatment Facilities and reported that patient care pharmacists expressed a significantly greater mean level of intrinsic satisfaction (4.95) than nonpatient care pharmacists did (3.74) [8]. No significant difference in extrinsic satisfaction was found between the two groups. In 1982, Quandt et al. conducted a questionnaire survey in southeastern Michigan and reported that the job satisfaction of CPs scored 5.71 ± 1.00 out of 7, which was the highest score among different pharmacist groups (p ≤ 0.001) [9]. In 1996, Olson and Lawson reported that hospital pharmacists’ level of job satisfaction was positively associated with clinical pharmacy involvement [10]. The job satisfaction score of CPs was 3.82 ± 0.86 out of 5, the highest among those with different job titles. Quantitatively, mean job satisfaction increased as the percentage of time spent performing clinical pharmacy activities increased (p < 0.001). In 2006, Kerschen et al. performed a prospective study of inpatient pharmacists working at two hospitals [11]. The CPs reported a mean satisfaction score of 2.99 ± 0.24 out of 4, and they were significantly more satisfied than staff pharmacists. However, no statistically significant differences were found between integrated and clinical pharmacists.

According to the American College of Clinical Pharmacy (ACCP) white paper published in 2023, clinical pharmacy in the USA has undergone significant expansion in qualifications and roles, but this growth has not translated into proportional improvements in job satisfaction [23]. Many pharmacists reported dissatisfaction with time allocation for core responsibilities, with only 68.6% being satisfied with clinical activity time, 31.8% with research time, and 54.6% with teaching/mentoring time. Additionally, burnout rates remained alarmingly high (47–70%), driven by work‒life imbalance, excessive non-clinical duties, and a perceived lack of recognition of contributions. These findings underscore the need for systemic reforms in time allocation, recognition, and workload management.

China

Clinical pharmacy is currently in a rapid development period in China. Studies indicate a generally positive self-perception among CPs in mainland China. However, they are also reported to face gaps in level of skills, configuration and so on. These results suggest an urgent need for the implementation of the clinical pharmacist system after a systematic evaluation of the current states and needs of CPs.

According to a survey conducted in 30 hospitals in Shanghai in 2010, the average score of CPs’ self-perception and evaluation was 3.90 ± 0.49 out of 5, suggesting that CPs generally recognized their own work well and that the differences were small [12]. All the respondents considered that the clinical pharmacist system should be implemented in medical and health institutions. In an online questionnaire survey conducted by Zhu et al. in 2019, the average occupational well-being score of CPs was 3 points out of 5 [15]. In one survey of CPs in secondary and tertiary hospitals in Yunnan Province in 2019, the average score of most of the scale items was above 3 points out of 5, indicating that the respondents generally had good professional self-perception [17]. In 2022, Zang et al. conducted a questionnaire survey to analyze the occupational well-being of cancer CPs in Hubei Province and reported that the job satisfaction of interviewed oncology clinical pharmacists needed to be improved [22]. Consultation (81.58%) and multidisciplinary discussion (78.95%) were believed to reflect their own value. In 2023, Xu et al. investigated the current status and career development needs of CPs [24]. The competency scores of the CPs ranged from 5.93 to 7.05 out of 10.

Moreover, there are several underlying requirements of CPs in addition to relatively high job satisfaction and self-perception. In 2015, Huang et al. investigated the cognition of CPs in their profession and reported that 94.11% of the investigated subjects were willing to be CPs, but 17.65% of them considered that they were incompetent at their work [13]. In 2018, Chen et al. conducted questionnaire surveys in relevant clinical pharmacy of some hospitals in Sichuan Province and reported that CPs basically mastered the skills required for the current work and were generally competent; however, there was still room for improvement in terms of listening to others’ opinions, handling emergencies, participating in medication decisions and preventing drug abuse [14]. In 2019, Yao et al. conducted a face-to-face interview survey in secondary hospitals; 52.8% of the CPs thought that the CPs in the hospital worked smoothly, but 35.8% of the CPs thought that the configuration of the CPs in the hospital was unreasonable [16].

Sweden

In 2021, Eriksson and Melander conducted a survey targeting Swedish CPs working with patients in hospitals and in primary/community care [20]. Concerning the acceptance, role and importance of CPs, the results are very positive, and very few respondents scored less than 4 (out of 5) on the role and skills questions. For example, “Clinical pharmacist role in the care team” scored a mean value of 4.6 out of 5, and “Collaboration with others on the care team” scored 4.4 out of 5. Notably, Swedish CPs believed that they could take on greater responsibility for prescription changes without the need for further education.

Vietnam

In 2021, Nguyen-Thi et al. examined the current status of the clinical pharmacy profession in Ho Chi Minh City [18]. Overall, 74.1% of the 197 CPs surveyed were satisfied with their current job. In addition, participants were found to have moderate job stress, with an overall mean stress score of 1.5 ± 0.4 out of 4 and a stress rate of 52.8% [19]. Patient care responsibility was the main stressor, especially in public hospitals, followed by job conflicts. A lack of experience, low income, and inability to participate in clinical ward rounds also cause significant stress to CPs.

Influencing factors of job satisfaction

Job satisfaction, as a comprehensive proxy for professional self-perception, encapsulates multifaceted dimensions. According to the motivation-maintenance theory proposed by Herzberg in Work and the Nature of Man, the factors that affect job satisfaction can be categorized into intrinsic factors (motivation) and extrinsic factors (maintenance) for interpretation and measurement [25]. Intrinsic factors focus on the content and tasks involved in a job and the opportunities for self-expression that the job provides. Extrinsic factors are related to the context or work environment and are determined by external events or people. In this work, the noted factors are categorized into 4 intrinsic factors, 8 extrinsic factors and 8 other factors based on the motivation-maintenance theory and characteristics of the included studies for detailed analysis (see Table 2).

Intrinsic factors

Intrinsic factors were of greater concern in the USA before 2010. There was a consensus that involvement in clinical activities can significantly improve the satisfaction of CPs, according to the included research in the USA [10, 11]. Moreover, the greater the percentage of time spent performing clinical pharmacy activities is, the greater the degree of job satisfaction gained by the pharmacist [11]. It was subsequently verified in Vietnam that clinical activity involvement showed a statistically significant association with overall job satisfaction [18]. However, a “dose-effect relationship” has not been established, as these studies drew conclusions mainly by comparing pharmacists in different positions rather than comparing the satisfaction of CPs with different clinical working hours.

In addition to clinical activity, other qualitative intrinsic factors were also of concern. According to the research included, greater satisfaction were often linked to greater autonomy than other hospital pharmacists [7, 9]. Moreover, the greater job satisfaction perceived by CPs may have been due to the challenge and importance of their work compared with those of other hospital pharmacists [9]. Intrinsic factors were less concerned in research in China and were not mentioned in Vietnam within the included studies. A recent study in China indicated that the inability to fulfill clinical obligations significantly correlates with lower satisfaction [21].

Extrinsic factors

Extrinsic factors were mainly discussed in China and Vietnam after 2010. Professional training was the most discussed factor in all research in China, but its correlation with satisfaction was less studied [12, 21]. On the basis of the statistical results, the group that received training showed significantly greater satisfaction, as it improved their professional ability and self-confidence. However, the quality and duration of training were not considered. Policies were discussed in studies from the USA and China [7, 21]. Research in the USA suggested that institutional policies caused great variance in general satisfaction [7]. According to a study in China, a lack of pharmaceutical management rules and regulations negatively influenced satisfaction [21].

Financial support, including economic rewards and income, improved satisfaction slightly in early research in the USA [7] but significantly for the studied CPs in China and Vietnam [21]. However, the exact amounts of income and rewards were not analyzed. The opportunity for advancement and institutional policies were considered core factors for the satisfaction of CPs [7]. Advancement, supervision, and working conditions were also emphasized for their importance in the job satisfaction of CPs in a recent study in Vietnam [18]. A study in China indicated that overload could obviously decrease the satisfaction of CPs [21]. Nevertheless, the exact workload was not measured.

Other factors

In addition to intrinsic and extrinsic factors, other characteristics were discussed in most of the studies in China, but the results were more controversial. Some studies on hospital level [12, 13, 17, 21], sex [11, 12, 17, 21], age [12, 17] and education background [12, 17, 21] reported inconsistent results. Moreover, both professional background [12, 17] and length of service [12, 17, 21] were found to have no significant effect. In comparison, the level of skills [12, 13, 17] and job characteristics [17, 18] were reported to significantly influence satisfaction.

To conclude, intrinsic and extrinsic factors were more concerned in studies conducted in the USA and Vietnam, in which all the early studies in the USA focused on clinical duties. Notably, even in the latest study in the USA, which defined CP on the basis of the amount of time that pharmacists spent on clinical activities, the definition and position could still be unclarified at that stage. In comparison, research in China concentrated on diverse factors other than intrinsic and extrinsic factors. Moreover, due to the large differences in the region, time and level of hospital, the results also varied. The pattern of factors does vary among regions, but a deeper understanding of the reasons requires more support from historical and political analyses of clinical pharmacy.

Discussion

CPs’ professional self-perception varies greatly across different countries and regions. This diversity can be attributed mainly to differences in local development levels and health policies. As the economic level improves, society’s demand for modern medical teams with more refined divisions of labor continues to increase, and CPs are gradually differentiated from the pharmacist group, characterized by more clinical activity involvement and higher job satisfaction, but at the same time face challenges such as inadequate recognition, policy gaps and anxiety.

The evolution of clinical pharmacy in the USA has demonstrated a consistent trend: pharmacists engaged in direct patient care report higher job satisfaction than those in traditional roles do, yet persistent systemic challenges such as dissatisfaction with time allocation and high burnout rates continue to hinder full professional fulfillment in contemporary practice. In mainland China, clinical pharmacies generally have positive professional self-perception among CPs, but there are still gaps in terms of competency and people skills, and it is suggested that the clinical pharmacist system should be implemented. Sweden exemplifies the successful integration of CPs into care teams, where CPs have better professional self-perception. Vietnam’s emerging clinical pharmacy workforce has demonstrated strong team integration but faces developing-world challenges, including stress from patient care loads and compensation dissatisfaction. These reports highlight fundamental differences between different countries and regions in areas such as income, politics, and pharmaceutical education.

Among the included studies concerning the influencing factors of satisfaction, early studies in the USA focused more on intrinsic factors, whereas more recent Chinese studies focused more on extrinsic and other factors. All of the intrinsic and extrinsic factors except for financial support were reported to significantly influence CPs’ professional self-perception and job satisfaction. The effects of level of hospital, sex, age and education level on satisfaction are controversial in different studies. The professional background and length of service are not significantly related to the job satisfaction of CPs. Therefore, based on the requirements reflected in the influencing factors of satisfaction, policies can be adopted to improve self-motivation and major incentives for better performance of CPs and the development of clinical pharmacies. Although background or situational factors are almost impossible for artificial intervention, while most of them have a conjugated impact on intrinsic and extrinsic factors, these factors suggest recruitment and dismissal to sustain and stimulate the motivation of employees.

During the literature screening process, we excluded several studies that did not coin the term “clinical pharmacist”, but they discussed hospital pharmacists who provided clinical pharmacy services, which may also contribute to our conclusions. In 1998, Kawabata et al. surveyed pharmacists in 64 randomly selected hospitals in the Kansai Plain in Japan and reported that for pharmacists whose primary responsibility is clinical pharmacy service, their job satisfaction score was 3.8 ± 0.7 out of 5, ranking first among different pharmacists [26]. In 2011, a cross-sectional study in which a self-administered questionnaire mailed to all full-time pharmacists employed by the Hospital Authority, Hong Kong, was conducted [27]. The average job and career satisfaction of the respondents was near the neutral point. The results indicated an unmet expectation of work balance between clinical activities and drug distribution, with the majority of responding pharmacists desiring a shift in work balance from more drug distributive roles towards more clinical activities. The results also suggested that an unmet expectation in work balance affects job and career satisfaction, particularly among younger frontline pharmacists.

This study also has several limitations.

First, the inclusion of studies spanning roughly 45 years introduces historical context variability. The scope of practice, job responsibility, management policies and education system of CPs have evolved substantially over this period, which diversifies the definition of CPs. Most of the included studies did not provide the definitions of clinical pharmacists. Notably, Kerschen et al. defined clinical pharmacists as “a group of inpatient pharmacists spending greater than 61% of their time in clinical activities” to distinguish with staff and integrated pharmacists [11], which differs from the definition of CP that “practitioners who provide comprehensive medication management and related care for patients in all health care settings” by the ACCP [28]. Thus, the connotation of the term “clinical pharmacist” varies across different studies, which may obscure direct comparisons. This temporal heterogeneity, such as evolving professional expectations and healthcare system reforms may fundamentally reshape the drivers of job satisfaction.

Second, significant methodological heterogeneity exists among included studies. Variability in survey tools, rating scales, research focus and metrics of self-perception limits quantitative synthesis and cross-study comparability. While we identified recurring factors through thematic analysis, the lack of standardized outcome measures precludes meta-analysis and reduces statistical robustness. This also necessitates cautious interpretation and further standardized research.

Third, regarding the quality of the evidence, while no study was excluded due to low quality, studies rated as 2b may have introduced potential confounders, sampling bias, or measurement error, which could reduce our confidence in causal analysis and conclusions drawn from the review of their data.

Finally, most of the included studies are cross-sectional studies (17 of 18 studies) without control groups or longitudinal follow-up, which limits inferring causality and drawing more detailed conclusions, and also survey research is susceptible to factors such as recall accuracy and subjectivity.

Future research may further investigate how policy, regulatory changes or other factors impact CPs’ professional self-perception, with well-designed intervention trials, longitudinal cohort studies, and standardized quantitative research methodology. In addition, natural experiments or quasi-experiments could be added to estimate the causal impact of interventions.

Conclusions

This systematic review synthesizes evidence on CPs’ professional self-perception across diverse healthcare systems. Given the qualitative nature of data and heterogeneity of study designs, the results still suggest that clinical activity involvement and autonomy consistently correlate with higher job satisfaction among CPs, whereas inadequate recognition, excessive workload, limited advancement opportunities and financial constraints hinder professional fulfillment. This study may inform future discussions on policy approaches in low- and middle-income countries, particularly concerning the identified influencing factors.
