Administration of the Postpartum Glucose Tolerance Test During the Delivery Hospitalization Compared With 4–12 Weeks Postpartum
Authors: Hailie Ciomperlik, Megan Harper, Md Faruk Hossain, Mark A. Turrentine
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In individuals with gestational diabetes mellitus, improved completion of postpartum screening for type 2 diabetes occurs during the delivery hospitalization compared with 4–12 weeks postpartum.

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) affects between 2% and 10% of pregnancies each year in the United States.1 In individuals diagnosed with GDM, after pregnancy, the long-term risk for the development of type 2 diabetes increases linearly as the duration of follow-up increases, with estimates of 19.7% after 10 years, 29.4% after 20 years, and 39% after 30 years.2 The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) and the American Diabetes Association traditionally have recommended postpartum screening at 4–12 weeks postpartum for all who had GDM to identify individuals with glucose impairment (either impaired fasting glucose or impaired glucose tolerance) or overt diabetes.3,4 Because of the increased sensitivity for detection of impaired fasting glucose level and impaired glucose tolerance, a 75-g, 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) is recommended.3,4 However, compliance with this testing recommendation has been low and is reported to range between 16% and 34%.5

Historically, whether an OGTT is representative of normal carbohydrate response in the immediate postpartum period (2–5 days after delivery) has been debated.6 Some investigators have argued that this testing has a practical value for diagnosing diabetes.7 In contrast, others state that the testing is unreliable in diagnosing glucose intolerance.8 However, in the past decade, several studies have observed high sensitivity and specificity to estimate the accuracy of the OGTT for diagnosing diabetes in the early postpartum period compared with the OGTT at 4–12 weeks.9–13 Placental hormones responsible for the insulin resistance of pregnancy decline in the maternal circulation with delivery, and within 24–48 hours, their effects are decreased to nonpregnant levels.14 With the large portion of individuals diagnosed with GDM not undergoing assessment for persistent diabetes in the postpartum period, screening individuals with GDM after delivery and before hospital discharge could improve testing rates.

Recently, ACOG issued clinical guidance that states that screening during the delivery hospitalization in the immediate postpartum period is a reasonable alternative to the 75-g, 2-hour OGTT at 4–12 weeks postpartum.15 However, the optimal screening strategy for completion of the postpartum screening for type 2 diabetes and rates of glucose intolerance or overt diabetes in the immediate postpartum period before hospital discharge compared with screening at 4–12 weeks are not known. In individuals with GDM, missing postpartum screening has the potential to delay the identification of the development of type 2 diabetes, which could be associated with long-term adverse outcomes.4 Therefore, our primary objective was to estimate the rate of noncompletion of testing for type 2 diabetes in individuals with GDM with a fasting 75-g, 2-hour OGTT in the immediate postpartum period before hospital discharge compared with 4–12 weeks postpartum by performing a systematic review and meta-analysis. Our secondary objective was to compare the rate of detected glucose intolerance and overt diabetes in individuals with GDM in the immediate postpartum period before hospital discharge compared with 4–12 weeks postpartum.

SOURCES

This review follows the MOOSE (Meta-analysis of Observation Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines.16 The protocol was recorded with the international prospective register of systematic reviews (PROSPERO registration CRD42024530659) on April 10, 2024, before introductory searches were started. The study was judged exempt from Baylor College of Medicine IRB endorsement because it was deemed non–human subject research. An electronic search with no language restrictions using the databases of EMBASE, MEDLINE through the PubMed interface, Web of Science, CINAHL, ClinicalTrials.gov, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials was conducted to identify articles that reported individuals with GDM who were screened with a fasting 75-g, 2-hour OGTT in the immediate postpartum period before hospital discharge compared with screening at 4–12 weeks postpartum. Studies were included that offered screening to the same individuals in both periods and to individuals in either period. The primary investigation was performed from database origin through April 2024 by one author (M.A.T.). Unpublished composed articles (ie, “gray literature”) were assessed by exploring Google Scholar and the ProQuest Dissertations & Theses database. For Google Scholar searches, the initial 300 results were checked according to the assumption that the most relevant results would emerge first.17 Articles were identified with a combination of the following MeSH terms: “oral glucose tolerance test” and “postpartum” (Appendix 1, available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/D847). References from recovered articles were explored by hand for supplementary articles. The final form of the MeSH terms was used by a second author (H.C.) separately, and only one additional article was identified.

All identified articles were fully recovered and evaluated separately by two reviewers (M.A.T. and H.C.) for inclusion. Studies qualified for inclusion if they assessed individuals with GDM in the postpartum period who underwent a fasting 75-g, 2-hour OGTT within the first week after delivery in the hospital and between 4 and 12 weeks postpartum in the outpatient setting. Studies defined impaired fasting glucose or impaired glucose tolerance (fasting value of 100 mg/dL or higher or 2-hour value of 140 mg/dL or higher) and overt diabetes (fasting value of 126 mg/dL or higher or 2-hour value of 200 mg/dL or higher), as recommended by ACOG and the American Diabetes Association.3,4 Data were accepted from cohort, case–control, and cross-sectional studies. No randomized controlled trials were available. Abstracts presented at meetings were excluded because of incomplete data being available for assessment.

Two reviewers (H.C. and M.H.) independently rated the quality of the included articles. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third author (M.A.T). An intraclass correlation coefficient was calculated to estimate interrater reliability.18 The overall qualities of cohort studies were rated with a modified Newcastle–Ottawa Scale for nonrandomized studies in meta-analyses.19 These were rated as follows: low risk of bias (Newcastle–Ottawa Scale score 7–9), high risk of bias (Newcastle–Ottawa Scale score 4–6), and very high risk of bias (Newcastle–Ottawa Scale score 0–3).20

The primary outcome was the rate of noncompletion of testing for type 2 diabetes in individuals with GDM with a fasting 75-g, 2-hour OGTT in the immediate postpartum period before hospital discharge compared with 4–12 weeks postpartum, as reported by the authors of each study in the published article. Descriptive variables to be compared (if available) between those individuals who were tested in the immediate postpartum period and those who did not return for testing at 4–12 weeks were age in years, body mass index (BMI, calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared), self-reported race and ethnicity, gestational age at delivery in weeks, and mode of delivery. Planned secondary outcomes (dependent on outcomes available in identified studies) were the rates of detected glucose intolerance (either impaired fasting glucose or impaired glucose intolerance) and overt diabetes. The means and SDs were estimated from this information for studies that reported any continuous variable as the median and range.21

Two-by-two tables were generated for ordinal data, and odds ratios (ORs) with their 95% CIs were computed. The pooling of results was completed with a multilevel meta-analysis of dependent effect sizes.22 A multilevel meta-analysis model was applied because of the probable effect size dependence of included studies that screened the same group of individuals in both periods. The ORs for individuals with GDM who had a fasting 75-g, 2-hour OGTT at 4–12 weeks postpartum as the reference group were pooled separately from those with GDM who had a fasting 75-g, 2-hour OGTT in the immediate postpartum period (2–5 days postpartum), and a forest plot was constructed.

Sensitivity analyses (omitting trials to be judged as high risk of bias) and influence analysis (removing each study sequentially from the pooled estimates) were executed. For ORs, an α level of 0.05 was considered significant. Statistical heterogeneity was calculated with the Cochrane Q test and Higgins I2 statistic.23 Statistically significant heterogeneity was considered if the Cochran Q was P<.1 or Higgins I2>30% to address the low power of heterogeneity tests.23 Publication bias was assessed graphically by visually examining funnel plots and the Egger test.24,25 The meta-analysis was performed with R 4.2.3 statistical software using the metafor package.

RESULTS

Figure 1 is a flow diagram of included studies. A total of 1,395 related studies were initially identified. After elimination of duplicates, studies not pertinent to the subject of interest, and meeting abstracts, 14 studies remained and were retrieved for complete analysis.6,7,9–13,26–32 Seven studies were excluded because of the following: patients not being screened with a fasting 75-g, 2-hour OGTT in the immediate postpartum period (n=3), study results being published in a subsequent combined analysis (n=2), screening occurring between 7 days and 6 months postpartum (n=1), or a pooled analysis of the sensitivity of an immediate postpartum glucose 75-g, 2-hour OGTT for detecting type 2 diabetes (n=1).6,7,26–30 In one study, the number of individuals who had impaired fasting and glucose tolerance or overt diabetes at the 4–12 weeks postpartum follow-up was not clear.12 The authors were contacted, and patient-level data were provided to allow inclusion of the study in the pooled analysis. In total, seven studies performed at 13 hospitals with 2,254 women with GDM who were offered testing in either period were included in the review.

Studies evaluated individuals who underwent screening for type 2 diabetes in the immediate postpartum period from May 1994 through October 2021. Four studies were from the United States, and one was from Korea, Brazil, and Iran. Of the 13 hospitals that screened patients in the immediate postpartum period, the antepartum diagnosis of GDM was performed at 24–28 weeks of gestation with 10 institutions using the two-step method (eight using the Carpenter–Coustan and two using the National Diabetes Data Group criteria), two using the International Association of the Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups one-step testing method, and one initially using the Carpenter–Coustan two-step method and then changing to the International Association of the Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups one-step method.3,4,33

Six studies provided patient-level sociodemographic data on 1,477 individuals with GDM who underwent screening in the immediate postpartum period.9–11,13,31,32 Six studies reported maternal age, 32.3±0.9 years, to allow a pooled analysis. Two studies provided the type of medical insurance, with 51.6% (329/638) having Medicaid insurance and 48.4% (309/638) having private insurance. Four studies provided parity (1.6±0.5), three studies provided BMI (32.3±4.2), and four studies provided estimated gestational age at birth (38.1±0.2 weeks). Race and ethnicity were reported in 1,339 individuals, with 30.2% Asian (405/1,339), 15.6% Black (209/1,330), 33.5% White (448/1,339), 4.8% Other (64/1,339), and 15.9% (213/1,339) Hispanic.9,10,13,31,32 The Other categorization was not defined in the various studies. All six studies reported the rate of GDM requiring medication and mode of delivery.9–11,13,31,32 The mean rate of GDM requiring medication was 47.3±18.6%. The mean cesarean delivery rate was 57.0±12.7%.

Sociodemographic information could not be separated for those who completed immediate screening compared with those with glucose screening at 4–12 weeks postpartum to allow a comparison analysis. Three studies with 372 individuals provided sociodemographic and delivery data on individuals who did not return for repeat screening at 4–12 weeks postpartum.9,10,12 For those who did not return, maternal age was 31.5±1.8 years, parity was 2.0±1.2, BMI was 28.3±0.2, and estimated gestational age at birth was 37.8±0.3 weeks. Of individuals reporting race and ethnicity, 7.5% were Asian (17/372), 42.2% were Black (157/372), 42.7% were White (159/372), and 7.5% were Hispanic (28/372). The mean rate of GDM requiring medication was 51.2±28.0%. The mean cesarean delivery rate was 62.9±11.3%. Demographic and obstetric characteristics differed between patients who completed the immediate postpartum OGTT and those who did not return for the 4–12 weeks postpartum OGTT (P<.01 for all variables). Patients who completed the immediate postpartum OGTT were more likely to be Asian, to be older, to have higher BMI, and to deliver at a later gestational age. They were less likely to require medication to treat the gestational diabetes and to undergo cesarean delivery.

All seven studies were rated as having low risk of bias (mean Newcastle–Ottawa Scale score: Kim et al31 8, Nabuco et al9 8, Dinglas et al10 8, Aghdam et al11 8, Waters et al12 8, Werner et al13 8, and Ayala et al32 8). The intraclass correlation coefficient comparing total Newcastle–Ottawa Scale scores between the two reviewers for the quality of included studies noted excellent agreement (г=1.0).

All seven studies reported the noncompletion rates of screening for type 2 diabetes in the immediate postpartum period and at 4–12 weeks postpartum.9–13,31,32 The pooled proportion of individuals who did not complete immediate postpartum testing before hospital discharge was 6.6% (95% CI, 0.23–20.5%) compared with 53.0% (95% CI, 37.1–68.6%) at 4–12 weeks postpartum (Appendix 2, available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/D847).9–13,31,32 The overall rate of noncompletion of screening in the immediate postpartum partum period was lower compared with individuals who had screening in the 4 to 12–week follow-up (pooled OR 0.21, 95% CI, 0.05–0.79, I2=93%) (Fig. 2). Visual inspection of the funnel plot noted asymmetry suggesting publication bias (Fig. 3). This was confirmed with the Egger test P<.001. A third test (cumulative meta-analysis of the overall effect size) was performed for publication bias (Appendix 3, available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/D847). As additional studies were added, the effect size became more precise and stable, and it suggested no publication bias. Sensitivity analyses were completed that included removing the study that did not screen the same individuals in both the immediate postpartum and 4–12 weeks postpartum periods,32 removing each study in turn from the pooled estimates (influence analysis), excluding the two studies that collected retrospective data,12,32 and comparing the four studies performed in the United States only10–13,32 or the three studies done outside of the United States only,9,11,31 and none of these modifications substantially changed the overall pooled results.

The pooled proportion of the rates of detected glucose intolerance (either impaired fasting glucose or impaired glucose intolerance) in individuals with GDM in the immediate postpartum period was 30.4% (95% CI, 20.9–40.7%) compared with 21.9% (95% CI, 17.0–27.3%) at 4–12 weeks postpartum (Appendix 4, available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/D847). The pooled portion of the rates of detection of overt diabetes in individuals with GDM in the immediate postpartum period was 6.5% (95% CI, 5.1–8.0%) compared with 5.5% (95% CI, 2.6–9.3%) at 4–12 weeks postpartum (Appendix 5, available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/D847). A multilevel meta-analysis was planned, but, due to the notable heterogeneity between studies, a meta-analysis was precluded.

DISCUSSION

This meta-analysis demonstrates a lower noncompletion rate for screening for type 2 diabetes in individuals with GDM in the immediate postpartum period compared with traditional screening at 4–12 weeks. Furthermore, although the absolute rates of glucose intolerance or overt diabetes were greater in individuals screened in the immediate postpartum hospitalization, because of the heterogeneity between studies with the limited number of studies available, a combined analysis was not completed on the secondary outcomes.

When given the option, more individuals with GDM prefer screening for type 2 diabetes during the immediate postpartum hospitalization, which would suggest that a new strategy is needed. Significant improvement in insulin sensitivity and decreased insulin requirements have been shown to occur in the first few days after delivery in individuals whose pregnancies were complicated by GDM.34 These observed physiologic changes in maternal metabolism have been suggested to support the utility of early postpartum screening for glucose impairment in individuals diagnosed with GDM.34 In a pooled analysis of four trials with 582 women with GDM on the diagnosis of overt diabetes with screening 2–5 days after delivery compared with 4–12 weeks postpartum, a sensitivity and specificity of 74% and 56%, respectively, were calculated.30 Because of this greater sensitivity but lower specificity, concern has been expressed for the potential overdiagnosis of diabetes in the immediate postpartum period before hospital discharge compared with the traditional 4–12 week screening. However, the likelihood ratios estimated for the immediate postpartum screening were small (negative likelihood ratio 0.4, positive likelihood ratio 1.7), suggesting a minimal decrease or increase in the change in the likelihood of the disease after testing.30 In the current pooled analysis, greater proportional rates of impaired glucose or overt diabetes were shown in the immediate postpartum screening; however, a pooled analysis was not performed because of a notable moderate rate of heterogeneity between studies. A note of caution is suggested as a result of the low test completion rate at the 4–12 week postpartum period because not all patients received a confirmatory test, leading to exclusion from the analysis. This selection bias could have resulted in overestimates of impaired glucose or overt diabetes in the immediate postpartum period and limits the generalizability of these observations. Finally, secondary outcomes should be considered to be hypothesis generating.35 Additional studies will need to determine whether individuals who have GDM with either impaired glucose values or overt diabetes in the immediate postpartum period before hospital discharge should be rescreened at 4–12 weeks postpartum to confirm the diagnosis before implementing treatment interventions.

This study has several strengths, including a prospectively registered protocol, a comprehensive search strategy with no language restrictions that included populations from different parts of the world, and studies of contemporary practices for managing gestational diabetes. With the recent changes in U.S. national guidelines stating that it is reasonable to perform a 75-g, 2-hour OGTT during the hospitalization in the immediate postpartum period to improve screening for type 2 diabetes, this meta-analysis provides data that are generalizable to populations around the world and information to counsel patients with GDM on the risks and benefits of when to screen for type 2 diabetes in the postpartum period.

However, there are some important limitations to consider. Most studies were from single institutions, and external validity may be limited. For noncompletion of screening for type 2 diabetes during the delivery hospitalization, a high rate of heterogeneity with wide CIs was demonstrated, which implies a lack of precision. These findings suggest that, although immediate postpartum screening reduces noncompletion rates, the effect varies considerably within studies, necessitating careful interpretation and consideration of study-specific factors. Although inspection of the funnel plot and calculation of the Egger test suggested potential publication bias, the quantities of studies available for analysis were limited. When a meta-analysis contains fewer than 10 studies, the power of these tests is low to discriminate chance from real asymmetry. The asymmetric distribution of studies, with a tendency for more studies reporting positive ORs, indicates that studies with significant or positive results may be overrepresented. However, all studies demonstrated lower rates of noncompletion screening in the immediate postpartum hospitalization period compared with the 4–12-week postpartum timeframe. Furthermore, the cumulative meta-analysis suggests that the consistent reduction in ORs throughout the addition of studies supports the conclusion of a significant effect overall. Finally, as with all meta-analyses, the utility of the conclusion is conditional on the quality of the primary studies included. Unidentified or unmeasured effects not systematically reported could have altered the identified associations.

In summary, this meta-analysis shows improved compliance with screening for type 2 diabetes in individuals diagnosed with GDM after delivery and before hospital discharge compared with screening at 4–12 weeks postpartum. Screening for type 2 diabetes in the immediate postpartum period is a practical option in patients with GDM, which has the potential to allow interventions for individuals with an abnormal glucose result and motivate follow-up for long-term therapy, possibly reducing long-term risks such as cardiovascular disease.
