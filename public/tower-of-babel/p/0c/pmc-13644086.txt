Metabolomic profiling reveals key metabolic pathway alterations in monochorionic diamniotic twins with selective intrauterine growth restriction
Authors: Mengjie Liu, Xiaowei Zhang, Xiaona Li, Xiaoli Gong, Li Yang, Yuan Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSelective intrauterine growth restricted (sIUGR) is a major cause of stillbirth and adverse pregnancy outcomes in monochorionic diamniotic (MCDA) twins. However, the underlying pathophysiologic mechanism of sIUGR remains poorly understood. The study aims to investigate the metabolite perturbations and metabolic mechanisms of sIUGR fetuses through a metabolomics study.MethodsUmbilical cord blood was collected from 30 pairs of MCDA sIUGR twin fetuses and 11 pairs of MCDA preterm (PT) twins, followed by non-targeted metabolomics using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry. Data were further analyzed using partial least squares discriminant analysis, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis, and Spearman correlation analysis.ResultsOur results revealed significant metabolic differences in the umbilical cord blood among small for gestational age, appropriate for gestational age and PT fetuses, potentially linked to abnormalities in carbohydrate, amino acid, and lipid metabolism. A total of 156 metabolites were identified, of which 53 showed significant differences between the groups. Specifically, tryptophan levels were higher in the sIUGR group, while glutamic acid and palmitic acid levels were lower. Based on the KEGG enrichment analysis and the correlation analysis, we hypothesize that the tryptophan-indole metabolism pathway, which may impact immune regulation and neurodevelopment, requires further attention. Other key metabolic pathways, such as arginine metabolism and the citrate cycle were also altered.ConclusionsOur findings offer valuable insights into the biochemical disruptions associated with sIUGR. These results enhance our understanding of the metabolic mechanisms underlying sIUGR and pave the way for future research on potential diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.Keywords: Metabolomics, Selective intrauterine growth restriction (sIUGR), Cord blood

Introduction

Selective intrauterine growth restriction (sIUGR) is one of the most severe complications of twin pregnancies that occurs in approximately 10–15% of monochorionic diamniotic (MCDA) twins [1, 2]. In MCDA twin pregnancies, both fetuses share a single placenta and may be connected by placental vascular anastomoses, making the exchange of blood and nutrients more complex compared to singleton pregnancies [3, 4]. Due to the shared circulation, the incidence and mortality rates of serious complications are notably higher in MCDA pregnancies. Furthermore, studying MCDA twins could provide a distinct advantage by minimizing the potential confounding effects of environmental and maternal factors.

Diagnostic criteria for sIUGR are not yet uniform. Most studies define sIUGR as the estimated fetal weight (EFW) of one twin < 10th percentile and/or the EFW discordance > 25% between both twins [5–7]. This clinical condition is associated with an increased risk of adverse outcomes, including intrauterine demise, neurological impairment and prematurity [8]. Even if the twins are born alive, sIUGR may also cause behavioral disorders, childhood and adult obesity, as well as metabolic diseases, especially for the smaller twins [9]. However, there are still no effective clinical interventions for sIUGR. Therefore, early prevention, appropriate monitoring and timely termination of pregnancy are of great importance.

Metabolomics, as an emerging discipline that has been rapidly developing in recent years, is defined as the identification and quantification of small molecular weight metabolites in various sample types [10–13]. Using metabolomics platforms to assess endogenous compounds and exposure to contaminants can effectively reveal the intrauterine environment, providing critical insights for etiological research [14]. In the field of perinatal medicine, metabolomics has been employed in predicting preterm (PT) labor, preeclampsia, fetal growth restriction (FGR), and various other pregnancy complications [11, 15–17].

sIUGR is thought to be mainly caused by the inequitable sharing of placenta between twins and the presence of vascular anastomoses [18]. So far, the molecular mechanism of sIUGR remains incompletely understood. Metabolomics plays a crucial role in advancing our understanding of sIUGR, as its metabolic abnormalities are not confined to isolated pathways but involve complex networks of physiological and biochemical processes. Although an increasing number of studies suggest that sIUGR is associated with alterations in amino acid metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, and maternal nutritional deficiencies [19], current research remains limited by small sample sizes and a lack of comprehensive analyses, leading to controversial conclusions. Given the phenotypic similarities in restricted fetal growth, research on FGR could offer valuable insights into potential metabolic disturbances relevant to sIUGR. However, due to the different etiologies of the two conditions, such comparisons must be interpreted with caution. Therefore, constructing a comprehensive metabolic profile of sIUGR and further exploring its metabolic characteristics are essential for deepening our understanding and improving clinical management strategies.

Considering the convenience and non-invasiveness of collecting umbilical cord blood after delivery, as well as its ability to reflect the fetal intrauterine metabolic state [20], we utilized gas chromatography-time of flight mass spectrometry (GC-TOF-MS) to analyze the metabolic profile of umbilical cord blood from sIUGR and PT twins, aiming to identify the differential metabolites and investigate the metabolic pathways associated with sIUGR.

Methods

Chemicals and reagents

L-2-chloro-phenyalanine (≥ 98%) was purchased from Hengbo biological technology Co. (Shanghai, China). Bis(trimethylsilyl)trifluoroacetamide (with 1% trimethyl-chlorosilane (TMCS), v/v) was obtained from REGIS Technologies. Inc. (USA). Methanol (MeOH) was of LC grade and obtained from Thermo Fisher (USA).

Study participants and exclusion criteria

This study was conducted on 30 MCDA twin pregnancies with sIUGR and 11 MCDA twin pregnancies with preterm birth in the Peking University Third Hospital. The study was approved by the Ethical Committee of Peking University Third Hospital (M2022368). Informed consent was obtained from each participant.

The inclusion and exclusion criteria were listed as follows. Diagnostic criteria for MCDA: ultrasound at 6 to 9 weeks of gestation showed only one intrauterine gestational sac with two fetal poles; at 10 to 14 weeks of gestation, the amniotic membrane separated the fetuses and the placenta in a “T” sign; the number of placentas, amniotic sacs, and septal layers were examined after delivery. sIUGR was diagnosed when the EFW of any fetus in MCDA was below the 10th percentile for the corresponding gestational age. PT birth was defined as delivery before 37 completed weeks of gestation.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome (TTTS); twin anemia-polycythemia sequence (TAPS); single intrauterine fetal death; triplet pregnancy; fetal structural or chromosomal abnormalities; selective pregnancy reduction; fetal distress; severe maternal complications including preeclampsia, gestational diabetes, gestational hypertension, autoimmune diseases; metabolic abnormalities such as thyroid and adrenal diseases, and hyperlipidemia; chorioamnionitis; assisted reproductive technologies; incomplete medical records, and severe hemolysis of umbilical cord blood.

Sample collection

All the patients underwent cesarean, and cord blood (5 mL) was immediately collected from the umbilical vein into EDTA-coated blood collection tubes, then centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 10 min at 4℃. The supernatant plasma was transferred and stored at − 80℃ until analysis.

Metabolite extraction

One hundred microliter plasma from each sample was mixed with 350 µL methanol and 20 µL of L-2-Chlorophenylalanine (1 µg/mL stock solution) as the internal standard solution, vortexed for 10 s and centrifuged for 15 min at 13,000 rpm, 4℃. Then the supernatant (400 µL) was transferred into a new 2 ml GC/MS glass vial, and 10 µL from each sample was pooled as the QC sample to monitor the stability and repeatability during instrumental analysis.

Metabolite derivatization

The dried extracts were resuspended in 60 µL of methoxyamine hydrochloride (20 mg/mL in pyridine) and incubated at 80 °C for 40 min. Then, 80 µL of N, O-bis(trimethylsilyl)trifluoroacetamide (BSTFA) reagent (1% TMCS, v/v) was added to the sample aliquots, and the mixture was incubated for 1.5 h at 70 °C. After incubation, the samples were cooled to room temperature, and 8 µL of FAMEs (standard mixture of fatty acid methyl esters, C8-C16 at 1 mg/mL; C18-C24 at 0.5 mg/mL in chloroform) was added to the mixed samples.

GC-MS analysis

GC-TOF-MS analysis was performed using an Agilent 7890 gas chromatograph system coupled with a Pegasus HT time-of-flight mass spectrometer. The system was equipped with a DB-5MS capillary column coated with 5% diphenyl cross-linked with 95% dimethylpolysiloxane (30 m×250 μm inner diameter, 0.25 μm film thickness; J&W Scientific, Folsom, CA, USA). A 1 µL aliquot of the analyte was injected in splitless mode. Helium was used as the carrier gas, with the front inlet purge flow rate of 3 mL/min and the gas flow rate through the column at 1 mL/min. The temperature was initially maintained at 50 °C for 1 min, then increased at a rate of 20 °C/min to 310 °C, then held at 310 °C for 6 min. The injection, transfer line, and ion source temperatures were set at 280 °C, 270 °C, and 220 °C, respectively. The energy was − 70 eV in electron impact mode. Mass spectrometry data were collected in full-scan mode over an m/z range of 50–500 at a rate of 20 spectra per second, following a solvent delay of 4.7 min.

Metabolite identification

Most of the metabolites were identified by comparing their measured properties with the NIST database, while others were identified using in-house available library entries.

Statistical analysis

Wilcoxon test was performed in R version 4.3.3 to compare maternal and neonatal characteristics, including pregnant women’s age, height, pre-pregnancy BMI, weight gain, gestational age at delivery, birth weight and birth height, with a significance level of α = 0.05. Chroma TOF 4.3X software from LECO Corporation, along with the LECO-Fiehn Rtx5 database, was utilized for extracting raw peaks, filtering and calibrating data baselines, aligning peaks, performing deconvolution analysis, and identifying and integrating peak areas. Following preprocessing steps, including data filtering and missing value imputation, the data were imported into Metaboanalyst 5.0 for partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA). Sparse PLS-DA and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis were performed using MetaboAnalyst 6.0. Differential metabolites were identified based on the variable importance in projection (VIP) values derived from the PLS-DA model. The mean peak areas of differential metabolites were analyzed using the Wilcoxon test with a significance level of α = 0.05, performed with R version 4.3.3. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to calculate the area under the curve (AUC) based on the peak areas of the significant metabolites.

PLS-DA analysis of metabolites

A total of 385 metabolic features were detected and 156 metabolites were identified based on KEGG and the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB). Sparse PLS-DA was performed using MetaboAnalyst 6.0, and supervised PLS-DA was conducted using MetaboAnalyst 5.0 (Fig. 1). All the comparisons resulted in high performance, suggesting that metabolic signatures of sIUGR fetuses were discriminated from PT twin fetuses. Among them, the comparison between the AGA group and the PT group showed the most distinct separation and the most valid Cross-Validation with an Accuracy of 0.98, R2 of 0.95 and Q2 of 0.82. The optimal model parameters for comparing the SGA and PT groups achieved an accuracy of 0.92, R2 of 0.95, and Q2 of 0.76. In contrast, the comparison between the SGA and AGA groups yielded optimal model parameters of Accuracy of 0.88, R2 of 0.95, and Q2 of 0.55. The accuracy and fit of all three models were high. However, the predictive performance of the SGA versus AGA model requires further improvement.

Screening of differential metabolites

Differential metabolites were selected with VIP > 1, P < 0.05, and fold change (FC) > 1.3, or FC < 0.8 as the criteria. A total of 53 metabolites were identified, with all differentially expressed metabolites shown in Fig. 2a. The overlap of differential metabolites across the three groups is illustrated in Fig. 2b. In the SGA samples, we identified 25 metabolites that were differentially expressed compared to the PT group, with 10 upregulated (including tryptophan (Trp), malonic acid, etc.) and 15 downregulated (including lactose (Lac), palmitic acid (PA), tyrosine, etc.). In the AGA samples, 30 metabolites were differentially expressed compared to the PT group, with 12 upregulated (including Trp, glucose-1-phosphate (G1P), etc.) and 18 downregulated (including citrulline, glutamic acid (Glu), palmitoleic acid (POA), etc.). Compared to AGA samples, SGA had 22 differential metabolites, with 16 upregulated (including creatine, lauric acid, glycocyamine, etc.) and 6 downregulated (including Trp, galactose (Gal), etc.). Based on the VIP scores, the top five metabolites significantly contributing to the separation between SGA and PT groups were 2,4-diaminobutyric acid, gluconic lactone, talose, Trp, and G1P. The top five metabolites significantly contributing to the separation between AGA and PT groups were 2,4-diaminobutyric acid, altrose, Trp, talose and N-cyclohexylformamide. The top five metabolites significantly contributing to the separation between SGA and AGA groups were altrose, Trp, 3-phosphoglycerate, phthalic acid and glycocyamine. The peak areas of these metabolites are shown in Fig. 2c. ROC curves were generated for the four repeated metabolites. These metabolites included 2,4-diaminobutyric acid (AUC: 0.95), talose (AUC: 0.875), altrose (AUC: 0.8) and Trp (AUC: 0.85). Additionally, a multivariate ROC curve analysis incorporating these metabolites was performed, resulting in an AUC of 0.992 (Fig. 2d).

Analysis of metabolic pathways

KEGG pathway analysis was performed using MetaboAnalyst 6.0. As shown in Fig. 3a, when comparing SGA to PT, differential metabolites were primarily enriched in pentose phosphate pathway, Trp metabolism, galactose metabolism, starch and sucrose metabolism, and amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism. In the comparison between AGA and PT fetuses (Fig. 3b), the enriched metabolic pathways for differential metabolites were identified as Trp metabolism, fatty acid biosynthesis, pentose phosphate pathway, arginine and proline metabolism, and alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism. When comparing AGA to SGA fetuses in the sIUGR group (Fig. 3c), differential metabolites were predominantly enriched in Trp metabolism, fatty acid biosynthesis, arginine and proline metabolism, glycine, serine and threonine metabolism, and citrate cycle (TCA cycle). By analyzing the key amino acid and energy metabolism pathways in sIUGR and PT (Fig. 4), we found that in these pathways, sIUGR exhibited a significant upregulation of Trp, as well as the slight upregulation of glutamine, lysine and lactic acid, while other metabolites showed a downward trend. It suggests changes in the activity of multiple metabolic pathways in sIUGR.

Correlation analysis between differential metabolites and birth indicators

We conducted a Spearman correlation analysis to assess the association between metabolite abundance and fetal indicators (Fig. 5a). The results revealed significant positive correlations between birth indicators and fatty acids such as PA, elaidic acid, and POA, as well as certain inflammation-related metabolites, including 3-hydroxybutyric acid and indole-3-acetic acid. In contrast, Trp and G1P showed significant negative correlations with these indicators. Additionally, fructose, altrose, G1P, and malonic acid were correlated with most other metabolites. Notably, Trp showed a significant negative correlation with its downstream product, indole-3-acetic acid (r = -0.25, p < 0.05). Based on the results of the KEGG enrichment analysis, we hypothesize that the Trp metabolism pathway, particularly the Trp-indole pathway, requires further attention. Moreover, the primary cellular energy source, the TCA cycle, also exhibited notable alterations. Previous studies have reported decreased citrate levels in FGR [21]. Our findings demonstrated a significant positive correlation (r = 0.75, p < 0.05) between citric acid and its downstream product, aconitic acid, both of which participate in the TCA cycle, corresponding to the significant differences in the TCA cycle within the sIUGR group.

a Spearman correlation heatmap showing the associations between metabolite abundance and fetal indicators. b KEGG pathway analysis of all differentially expressed metabolites

KEGG pathway analysis was performed on 53 differential metabolites using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (Fig. 5b), revealing that they were concentrated in pathways such as fatty acid biosynthesis, galactose metabolism, and arginine and proline metabolism. These findings further confirm the hypothesis that abnormalities in amino acid metabolism and energy metabolism may affect the incidence and development of sIUGR.

Discussion

sIUGR or FGR refers to the failure of a fetus in twin or singleton pregnancies to achieve its full growth potential due to pathological factors, often resulting in reduced birth weight and underdevelopment of multiple organ systems. Despite its clinical importance, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood, and effective interventions are still lacking. In this study, we aimed to address this gap by investigating the metabolic profile of umbilical cord blood from sIUGR twins through GC-MS. Our findings reveal significant disruptions in metabolic pathways, particularly affecting amino acids (e.g., Trp, Glu) and energy metabolism, including lipids (e.g., PA, POA) and carbohydrates (e.g., Lac, Gal). These results provide critical insights into the biochemical alterations associated with this condition, advancing our understanding of its pathophysiology and paving the way for future research into potential diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Differential metabolites primarily included amino acids, sugars, organic acids, and lipids. Notably, key metabolites such as Trp, Glu, tyrosine, proline and POA have been reported to be associated with fetal development [22, 23]. Previous studies have also highlighted the importance of amino acid and lipid metabolism in the pathogenesis of sIUGR and FGR. For example, Wang L et al. found that compared to the control group, fetuses with sIUGR exhibited significant differences in several essential amino acids, such as methionine, phenylalanine, and tyrosine [19]. Miranda et al. found that the relative concentrations of lipids were higher in the cord blood of the FGR group. Further quantitative targeted analyses of lipids revealed the increase of cholesterol and triglycerides lipoproteins in the FGR group [21]. These findings emphasize the significant impact of disrupted amino acid and lipid metabolism on fetal development, supporting the importance of our identified differential metabolites in the pathogenesis of growth-restricted pregnancies. However, the metabolic environment of sIUGR twins differs from that of singletons with FGR, due to the shared circulation in MCDA twins, complicating our understanding of its unique pathophysiology.

Our study reveals notable alterations in amino acid metabolism, with significantly elevated Trp levels across the three groups, particularly in AGA fetuses. As an essential amino acid, Trp, obtained from the diet, is actively transported across the placenta to the fetus [24]. In addition to its role in protein synthesis, Trp can be degraded through the serotonin, kynurenine and indole pathways, playing a crucial role in neurodevelopment and immune regulation [25]. Our findings show that compared to the PT group, Trp levels were elevated in both the AGA and SGA groups, with AGA fetuses exhibiting higher Trp levels than SGA fetuses within the sIUGR twin pairs. This suggests that metabolic differences also exist between AGA and normal fetuses. However, there is controversy among different studies. Favretto et al. found that Trp levels were upregulated in the umbilical vein blood of FGR fetuses [26], while Cosmi et al. reported that Trp levels were downregulated in the umbilical vein blood of sIUGR fetuses [27]. This may reveal differences in the intrauterine metabolic conditions of FGR and sIUGR. The discovered differential metabolites may result from the limited sample sizes of studies and different analytical methods. Additionally, correlation analysis suggested an alteration in the Trp-indole metabolic pathway in sIUGR. The Trp-indole pathway is crucial for regulating immune responses and maintaining neural health. The observed disruption may contribute to developmental challenges associated with sIUGR, such as impaired immune function and potential neurological deficits.

Moreover, the peak area of Glu in the PT group was higher than that in the SGA and AGA groups. Glu, as the primary excitatory neurotransmitter in the nervous system, is the most abundant free amino acid in the brain. It is responsible for cognitive, motor, sensory, and autonomic nervous system functions [28]. In the developing brain, Glu plays a crucial role in neuronal differentiation, migration, and survival by promoting the transport of Ca²⁺ [29]. The adverse neurological outcomes in sIUGR fetuses may be related to the decreased levels of Glu.

Enrichment analysis of altered metabolites showed changes in arginine metabolism in the AGA group. Arginine metabolism is a key pathway for nitric oxide (NO) synthesis, which can effectively improve placental circulation and increase fetal blood supply [30]. Tranquilli et al. confirmed that NO levels in amniotic fluid are lower in mid-gestation IUGR pregnancies compared to normal pregnancies, but increase in late pregnancy due to compensatory mechanisms [31]. This may be related to changes in the activity of the arginine metabolism pathway.

In terms of energy support, both lipid and carbohydrate metabolism were altered in AGA and SGA fetuses compared to PT fetuses. Specifically, PA and POA levels were downregulated in both groups. PA is an intermediate product of fatty acid synthesis and degradation metabolism, and many key proteins related to neurodevelopment undergo palmitoylation modification and processing [32]. The downregulation of PA levels may affect neuron growth, polarization, and the establishment of neural networks. Moreover, PA can induce damage in pancreatic β-cells, leading to insulin resistance and mitochondrial dysfunction [33]. POA, as a monounsaturated fatty acid, is also essential for neurodevelopment. However, Erwin et al. found POA levels were higher in brain samples from the rabbit IUGR model [10]. For carbohydrates, SGA fetuses exhibited lower levels of Lac and Gal, possibly related to nutritional deficiencies. Ray O. Bahado-Singh et al. also found the Gal metabolism pathway was perturbed in the placenta samples from 19 FGR cases [11]. Interestingly, we also observed elevated levels of uncommon monosaccharides, such as altrose and talose, in sIUGR fetuses. This has not been reported in previous studies, and its roles remain to be further investigated.

Additionally, enrichment analysis of differential metabolites also revealed alterations in energy metabolism, particularly in the TCA cycle, within sIUGR fetuses. The TCA cycle is essential not only for providing the energy required for rapid fetal growth and development but also for supporting organ development, immune regulation, and metabolic adaptation through the generation of key metabolic intermediates. As reported in fetal sheep models, the concentration of mitochondrial TCA cycle enzymes in the skeletal muscle of IUGR fetuses was lower, adapting to hypoxemia and hypoglycemia [34].

Our study utilized monozygotic diamniotic twins as subjects to effectively eliminate confounding factors such as environmental and maternal influences, thereby aiding etiological research. Preterm infants were selected as controls based on the rationale that, although a significant gestational age difference exists between preterm and sIUGR infants, this difference is even more pronounced between term and sIUGR infants. This approach reduces gestational age-related confounding factors in metabolic comparisons. However, due to the stringent selection criteria for sIUGR fetuses and their mothers, as well as the low incidence of twin births, the sample size was relatively small. In the future, we will continue to expand our sample size and further explore the research on sIUGR.

Conclusions

In this study, we investigated the differential metabolites among AGA and SGA from MCDA-sIUGR twins and PT twins. The cord blood profile revealed the metabolic abnormalities in carbohydrate, amino acid, and lipid metabolism in sIUGR twins. Key metabolites, such as Trp and PA, were associated with birth outcomes, suggesting their roles in fetal development. In addition, arginine metabolism and the TCA cycle are essential for placental circulation and energy production. The findings enhance our understanding of the metabolic mechanisms underlying sIUGR and provide a foundation for future research into potential diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.
