Revision periacetabular osteotomy enhances joint function and activity level after failure of redirectional pelvic osteotomy at skeletal maturity
Authors: Maximilian Fischer, Andreas Nitsch, Jens Goronzy, Feras Kasabji, Andre Hofer, Georgi I. Wassilew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsThere is a paucity in the literature regarding functional outcomes of revision periacetabular osteotomy (R-PAO) after failure of previous redirectional pelvic osteotomy in a mature skeleton. This study aimed to compare the surgical and patient-reported outcome measures (PROM) between R-PAO and primary PAO.MethodsPatients undergoing R-PAO (n = 13) and primary PAO (n = 39) were matched in a 1:3 ratio for age, sex, and surgical date with a minimum follow-up of one year. PROM assessment included scores for pain, joint function (Modified Harris Hip Score -mHHS; International Hip outcome tool-12 -iHOT12), and activity level (University of California Los Angeles activity scale -UCLA). Postoperative outcomes were compared between the groups using independent-samples t-tests and Mann-Whitney U test. Multivariable linear regression was used to identify predictors of postoperative outcome improvement.ResultsPatients undergoing R-PAO had significantly worse preoperative joint function (mHHS 34.91 to 57.28, p < 0.001; iHot-12 33.17 to 46.22, p = 0.005) and activity level (UCLA 4.38 to 6.34, p = 0.004) compared to primary PAO. While achieving similar functional improvement (Δpre – post, p > 0.05) comparing both groups, postoperative mHHS (70.21 to 86.81, p = 0.002) and pain score (3.46 to 1.92, p = 0.010) remained significantly worse after R-PAO. Multivariable linear regression analysis revealed that preserved preoperative joint function (iHot-12 ß = 0.795, p < 0.001; mHHS ß = 0.696, p < 0.001) and activity level (UCLA ß = 0.360, p < 0.001), a lower BMI (iHot-12 ß = -2.011, p = 0.002; UCLA ß = -0.120, p = 0.041), and a sufficient postoperative anterior wall coverage (iHot-12 ß = 0.849, p < 0.001) are predictors of improved postoperative outcomes in both R-PAO and primary PAO.ConclusionThe failure of hip-preserving pelvic osteotomies in skeletally mature patients results in a substantial symptom burden and functional impairment. R-PAO represents a considerable treatment strategy that preserves the native hip joint and function, but postoperative outcomes after R-PAO remain inferior to those of primary PAO, due to the worse preoperative joint function.Cite this article: Bone Jt Open 2026;7(7):882–889.

Introduction

Acetabular reorientation by periacetabular osteotomy (PAO) has become an effective method to prevent the native hip joint from requiring early total hip arthroplasty (THA). Originally reserved for severe hip dysplasia (HD), the indication for PAO has been extended, due to improved understanding of pathomechanics affecting joint function, to include mild HD as well as excessive acetabular anteversion or acetabular retroversion (AR).1-3 Despite promising long-term reports on PAO with low conversion rates to THA, some patients experience inferior outcomes and persistent joint impairment. In this context, several risk factors for PAO failure have been described.4,5 For instance, advanced osteoarthritis as well as increasing patient age and BMI has been connected to poor long-term results.6 A major aspect may also untreated hip pathologies or iatrogenic acetabular overcorrection.7,8 Otherwise, it has already been shown that precise acetabular correction by minimally invasive PAO requires subsequent hip arthroscopy in less than 5% of cases.9 Therefore, the surgical accuracy is a crucial factor for excellent functional outcomes and joint preservation after PAO.10,11

However, treatment strategies after malcorrection by previous PAO or comparable redirectional pelvic osteotomies in skeletal mature patients are needed to secure not only the native joint function but also active life participation. To date, conversion THA is the most frequently used surgical procedure after pelvic osteotomy failure. This prominent therapeutic choice is supported by excellent short- and midterm data with comparable patient- reported outcomes (PROM) compared with those of primary THA.12,13 However, conversion THA after suboptimal acetabular reorientation can be challenging, and may lead to inferior functional results as well as higher complication and revision rates.14,15 Furthermore, the increased lifetime revision risk of THA in young patients must be discussed with those patients when planning early THA.16,17

Revision PAO (R-PAO) could be an alternative surgical procedure to preserve the native hip in these patients with history of redirectional pelvic osteotomy failure. Consequently, this case series aimed to compare surgical and PROM outcomes between R-PAO and primary PAO with a minimum follow-up of one year. It is hypothesized that acetabular reorientation in R-PAO and primary PAO can be performed with comparable surgical accuracy, ultimately leading to comparable improvements in postoperative PROMs.

Methods

Study design

This prospective, review board-approved cohort study included a consecutive series of patients treated with R-PAO (13 patients, 13 hips, 85% female) at our institution between February 2021 and November 2024. These patients were matched in a 1:3 ratio to a group of patients undergoing primary PAO (control group- 39 patients, 39 hips, 85% female) based on age, sex, and the date of surgical procedure (Table I).

All patients were identified from the institutional hip preservation registry and all surgical procedures were done by one fellowship-trained, high-volume surgeon (GIW). The patients gave written informed consent prior to inclusion. Ethics approval (BB099/20a) was obtained from the local independent ethics committee of the University Medicine Greifswald according to the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki.18

Surgical technique and radiological assessment

R-PAO were performed by a modified, minimally invasive Bernese PAO technique. This involved either a rectus-sparing approach with bony detachment of the sartorius from its origin or a rectus- and sartorius-sparing approach. After removal of in situ osteosynthesis material from the previous operation, new osteotomies were done under fluoroscopic control. If possible, the original skin incision was used. In cases of additional Cam morphology, a mini-open arthrotomy was performed for femoral osteochondroplasty (Figure 1).

Representative case examples of two female patients (a to c) 26-year-old; (d to f) 25-year-old)) undergoing revision periacetabular osteotomy. Pelvic radiographs of a) and d) before surgery, b) and e) immediately after surgery, and c) and f) after bone healing.

Preoperative and postoperative radiological evaluation included supine anteroposterior pelvic radiographs as well as axial and faux-profile femoral views. These were reviewed independently to analyze Tönnis osteoarthritis grade, lateral centre-edge angle (LCEA), Tönnis angle (TA), anterior- and posterior wall index (AWI/PWI), and retroversion index (RVI).19-21 Furthermore, all patients undergoing R-PAO underwent CT or MRI as part of the preoperative diagnostic evaluation.

Statistical analysis

PROM were assessed using a standardized questionnaire preoperatively and at a follow-up examination. The modified Harris Hip Score (mHHS)22 and the International Hip Outcome Tool-12 (iHOT-12)23 were used to determine hip joint function. Pain level before and after surgery were determined by numeric rating scale (NRS) with 0 (best) to 10 (worse) points. The University of California Los Angeles activity scale (UCLA)24 determined patient-reported activity level.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient characteristics, radiological and surgical parameters, as well as PROM. Continuous data are presented as mean with standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartil range (IQR). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using Shapiro-Wilk test. Depending on data distribution, independent-samples t-tests were used for normally distributed variables, and Mann-Whitney U tests were used for non-normally distributed or ordinal variables. Intergroup PROM comparisons were performed separately for the preoperative state, postoperative state, and change scores (Δ= preoperative - postoperative). Preoperative to postoperative PROM changes within the R-PAO and primary PAO group were analyzed using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test.

To identify predictors of postoperative outcome improvement, multivariable linear regression models were fitted, including patient-related and surgery-related variables. Regression assumptions (linearity, homoscedasticity, normality of residuals, multicollinearity, and influence) were evaluated using visual diagnostics and formal tests (Ramsey RESET, Breusch-Pagan, Shapiro-Wilk, variance inflation factor, and Cook’s distance). Statistical analysis was conducted using Python v. 3.11 (Python Software Foundation, USA) - pandas, scipy.stats, seaborn, and statsmodels. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Overall, 52 patients (52 hips) were included with a mean follow-up of 3.1 years (SD 1.01). Both groups were equally matched for sex and both cohorts had a comparable age and BMI at the time of surgery (Table I).

Postoperative radiological evaluation revealed a significant improvement in femoral head coverage for both groups (Table III). Acetabular reorientation led to comparable anterior, posterior, and lateral acetabular coverage between R-PAO and primary PAO group (Table III).

Discussion

The main findings of this study were that 1) failure of hip preserving pelvic osteotomies are associated with a substantial symptom burden and impaired joint function, ultimately resulting in lower postoperative outcome scores of R-PAO, despite comparable improvements in joint function and activity level compared to primary PAO; and 2) surgical accuracy, a lower BMI, and a preserved preoperative hip function and activity level are key predictors of successful postoperative outcomes in both R-PAO and primary PAO.

Revision strategies after failure of redirectional pelvic osteotomy in skeletal mature patients are limited and mainly done via hip arthroscopy to address symptomatic intra-articular pathologies or conversion THA in case of severe joint degeneration.13,14,25 However, Laboudie et al25 reported high rates of hip arthroscopy failure and limited functional improvement in patients post PAO for HD. Otherwise, conversion THA after PAO failure is a popular treatment strategy and was reported to improve postoperative PROM, even when not matching those of primary THA.14 In this respect, the findings of the present study are consistent with those studies comparing primary THA and conversion THA after PAO failure. The current results show that the native hip joint in skeletal mature patients can be successfully restored with improved joint function and preserved activity level. While the preoperative symptom burden has been shown to represent a key factor for postoperative outcomes, this could be an explanation for lower postoperative outcome scores in R-PAO compared with those of primary PAO patients. Thus, these data may influence the shared-decision making on the surgical revision strategy after pelvic osteotomy failure.

Studies on R-PAO are limited, and only data by the ANCHOR research group reported on PROMs including a cohort of adolescent patients with history of redirectional or reshaping acetabular osteotomies.26 Consequently, their study included a substantial proportion of patients with residual HD after osteotomies in childhood, whereas the present study included only patients who were suffering from iatrogenic osteotomy failure at skeletal maturity. Therefore, the present study extended the knowledge of R-PAO in impingement-driven joint morphologies. In line with the current data, the postoperative PROM in the ANCHOR research group’s study did not match those of primary PAO despite their patients having similar preoperative score for pain, joint functionality, and activity level. Accordingly, the present study with focus on an adult cohort and a high preoperative symptom burden still demonstrated significant postoperative PROM improvements, underscoring R-PAOs potential for favourable outcomes even in severely symptomatic patients.26

An important advantage of R-PAO is the potential to preserve the native hip joint and delay or ultimately avoid early conversion THA. A recent study on the information needs of patients undergoing PAO has shown that preserving their native joint is an important factor in preoperative consultations for those patients.27 However, in severe osteoarthritis post-PAO, conversion THA is indicated, even when it is potentially associated with challenges in acetabular implant positioning resulting in an increased risk of acetabular wall defects or component overhang.28,29 In this respect, conflicting results regarding complication and revision rates of conversion THA in these patient cohorts must be critically discussed. While Lemme et al30 reported significantly higher dislocation rates and inferior implant survival when patients underwent conversion THA within the first year after hip preservation surgery compared to primary THA, a recent systematic review found comparable functional outcomes of conversion THA with history of pelvic osteotomy.31 Nevertheless, conversion THA remains a technical highly demanding procedure further illustrated by longer surgical time and higher intraoperative blood.32,33 Similarly, the current study found a significantly longer surgical time in R-PAO due to technical demands. However, there were similar radiological outcomes compared with primary PAO, which is in contrast to data by Zhang et al34 reporting an increased risk for inferior radiological outcomes in patients with history of operative or conservative treatment during childhood. Thus, the potentially abnormal bony anatomy, soft-tissue remodelling, or scarring after previous pelvic osteotomy did not negatively impact acetabular reorientation in R-PAO.

The main predictors of improved postoperative outcomes in the present study were precise acetabular orientation, lower BMI, and preserved preoperative joint functionality and activity level. Several risk factors for failure of primary PAO have been described, including advanced osteoarthritis, older age, higher BMI, iatrogenic insufficient acetabular reorientation, or prior failed hip arthroscopy.2,6,10,35-37 Nevertheless, little is known about whether R-PAO itself represents a risk factor for poor outcomes. The findings of the present study indicated that it is not the revision procedure itself, but rather the higher preoperative symptom burden in patients undergoing R-PAO, that is associated with inferior overall outcomes. These findings are clinically important for preoperative patient guidance regarding postoperative outcome expectations, and the data suggest that nevertheless restoring the native hip joint by R-PAO may be a viable treatment strategy in cases of pelvic osteotomy failure.

However, there are several limitations needing further critical evaluation. First, this series, with a limited number of cases, was conducted at a single high-volume centre for hip preservation osteotomies, and all procedures were done by a single surgeon (GIW). This may limit the generalizability of the study’s results due to centre as well as operator bias, and needs examination in future multicentre studies. Next, this study lacks long-term follow-up data, which limits final conclusions especially on rates of conversion THA after R-PAO. Nevertheless, this study primarily focus on patient-reported outcome measures and found significant improvement in joint functionalality and activity level, which is a key point for patients undergoing hip preservation surgery in the short term as well as the long term. Third, since the primary pelvic osteotomy procedures in the R-PAO group were mostly done externally, baseline radiographs and PROMs were not available for all patients to evaluate the initial joint functionality and symptom burden. Consequently, there is a risk of residual confounding due to different baseline characteristics when comparing a revision and a primary surgical procedure. Ultimately, ongoing research on this topic should include a control group of patients undergoing conversion THA to reveal differences in functional outcomes and improve evidence for treatment recommendations after pelvic osteotomy failure.

In summary, failure of redirectional pelvic osteotomies leads to a high symptom burden and impaired joint function. R-PAO is a suitable therapeutic option to preserve the native hip joints with significant improvement in activity and hip function. A maintained preoperative joint function, a lower BMI, and a sufficient postoperative anterior wall coverage are the main predictors of successful postoperative outcomes in both primary and revision PAO.

Take home message

- Revision periacabular osteotomy (R-PAO) significantly relieves pain and improves patient-reported outcomes.

- Patient-reported outcomes of primary periacetabular osteotomy are higher compared to R-PAO.
