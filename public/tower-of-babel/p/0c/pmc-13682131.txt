Assessment of oxidative stress markers in multi-episode schizophrenia: a comparative study before and after electroconvulsive therapy and their correlation with clinical improvement
Authors: Man Yang, Qing Tian, Xue Yuan, Dongliang Liu, Qiang Shi, Haidong Yang, Xiaobin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study examined oxidative stress (OS) markers in acute relapse schizophrenia, assessed the impact of electroconvulsive therapy (ECT), and explored correlations with clinical improvement.Methods110 Han Chinese patients and 55 healthy controls were enrolled. All enrolled patients had multi-episode schizophrenia with a mean illness duration of 11.15 ± 9.11 years. Patients received 8–12 bilateral ECT sessions plus antipsychotics. Serum levels of GSH-Px, MnSOD, CuZnSOD, and CAT were measured before and after ECT. Symptoms were assessed using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS).ResultsAt baseline, schizophrenia patients showed significantly lower serum levels of GSH-Px, MnSOD, and CAT, while the MnSOD/CuZnSOD ratio was decreased, CuZnSOD levels showed a non‑significant trend as compared to healthy controls. GSH-Px levels were negatively correlated with the PANSS positive subscale scores (p = 0.007). Following ECT, all PANSS scores significantly decreased (all p < 0.001). Serum levels of GSH-Px, TSOD, CuZnSOD, and CAT increased significantly, while MnSOD levels and the MnSOD/CuZnSOD ratio significantly decreased (all p < 0.01). In responders, changes to OS markers were significant (all p < 0.01). Increases in GSH-Px levels were positively correlated to reductions in the PANSS total score (Bonferroni corrected p = 0.024) and general subscale scores (Bonferroni corrected p = 0.008). Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified the baseline GSH-Px level as an independent potential indicator of clinical improvement (p = 0.001).ConclusionAcute relapse schizophrenia shows significant redox imbalance. ECT in combination with antipsychotics modulates antioxidant enzymes, and GSH-Px dynamics correlate with symptom improvement, suggesting its potential as a predictive and monitoring biomarker for ECT outcomes.Clinical trialNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-026-08437-8.

Introduction

Schizophrenia is a severe mental disorder with a global prevalence of approximately 1%. It is characterized by positive symptoms, negative symptoms, and cognitive impairment, which significantly diminish quality of life while imposing substantial burdens on families and society [1–3]. Despite decades of intensive research, the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying the onset and progression of schizophrenia—recognized not only as a disease linked with changes to central nervous system neurotransmitters [4] but also involving disruptions to multiple systems, including pro/antioxidant imbalance—remain incompletely understood, particularly regarding the biological basis of acute relapse [5]. Accumulating evidence suggests that multiple pathological processes, including oxidative stress (OS) (an imbalance between pro-oxidant and antioxidant molecules), are associated with the progression of schizophrenia [6, 7].

Increased production of reactive oxygen species (ROS)—frequently observed in pathological states, such as schizophrenia—can overwhelm cellular antioxidant defense systems, leading to oxidative damage of biomolecules (e.g., lipids, proteins, enzymes, DNA) [8, 9]. Key components of the OS response include the antioxidant enzymes catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), which inhibit ROS-induced damage [8, 10]. Notably, manganese SOD (MnSOD) in the mitochondria and copper/zinc-containing SOD (CuZnSOD) in the cytoplasm play fundamental roles in the disproportionation of superoxide radicals, rapidly converting O2− to H2O2 [11, 12]. Mn-SOD alone scavenges ~ 95% of the ROS and superoxide anions generated by mitochondrial oxygen consumption [13]. Meanwhile, CuZn-SOD, which constitutes > 90% of cytoplasmic SOD enzymes, contributes more to total cellular SOD activity than other isoform [14]. Collectively, these antioxidant enzymes protect against free radicals and maintain intracellular redox balance.

Reduced activities of the major antioxidant defense enzymes (i.e., SOD, CAT, and GSH-Px) have been reported in schizophrenia patients as compared to controls [15, 16]. Most studies assessing OS in schizophrenia populations indicate increased toxic damage due to both elevated levels of pro-oxidants and diminished levels of antioxidants [17, 18]. Beyond redox imbalance, growing evidence links OS to immune-inflammatory pathways and composite hematological indices, which have been shown to provide additional explanatory power in schizophrenia pathophysiology and treatment response [19, 20]. In schizophrenia, OS has been documented in diverse biological systems: cerebrospinal fluid and the prefrontal cortex in vivo [21], the anterior cingulate cortex via postmortem studies [22], and in peripheral tissues and plasma [12, 23]. However, significant controversy persists regarding the activity levels of antioxidant enzymes (e.g., SOD, CAT, GSH-Px) in schizophrenia, as research findings show notable heterogeneity: some studies report decreased activity (suggesting heightened OS), others observed increased levels (potentially reflecting compensatory mechanisms), and yet some reported no significant alterations relative to healthy controls [11, 17, 22–26].

Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) has been used to treat major psychiatric disorders for more than 80 years and is considered safe even for high-risk populations [24–26]. In modern practice, a modified form of ECT is administered under anesthesia. ECT involves three phases: (1) induction, where an electrical current is employed to stimulate brain tissue; (2) seizure, ideally producing controlled seizure activity (20–50 s); and (3) recovery. Notably, both the induction and seizure phases can trigger neurochemical changes [25], as the electrical current used in ECT relies on electron flow, which may theoretically interact with biomolecules to generate oxidant compounds. This aligns with epilepsy models where electrical stimuli and chemical agents (e.g., pilocarpine) induce similar patterns of OS [27]. While repeated ECT sessions can improve psychiatric symptoms—particularly the positive and affective domains—correlating with restored redox balance [5], the potential impact on oxidative pathways remains incompletely characterized. In vitro studies show that electrical stimuli can trigger mitochondrial autophagy via OS induction [28], yet whether OS compounds are increased in human patients undergoing ECT is unclear. Although ECT regulates nitrosative stress and glutathione homeostasis [5, 29], the impact on oxidative DNA damage is controversial, with studies reporting either aggravation [5] or no significant change [30]. To resolve these inconsistencies, the aims of the present study were to (1) quantify major antioxidant enzymes (SOD, CAT, GSH-Px) in schizophrenia patients post-ECT, (2) analyze relationships between psychiatric symptoms and antioxidant levels at baseline/follow-up, and (3) assess potential correlations of enzymatic changes with clinical symptom trajectories.

Methods

Subjects

A total of 132 consecutive Han Chinese inpatients with acute schizophrenia relapse at the Fourth People’s Hospital of Lianyungang were screened for study inclusion. Of these, 22 were not enrolled (15 did not meet inclusion criteria, 7 declined to participate). The remaining 110 patients were included and received routine clinical care, which consisted of 8–12 sessions of bilateral ECT combined with antipsychotic medications. All patients had discontinued antipsychotic medication for a minimum of 4 weeks prior to enrollment, leading to acute symptom exacerbation. After enrollment and at the start of the ECT course, antipsychotic medications were initiated and maintained at stable doses throughout the study period.

Patient inclusion required: (1) DSM-IV schizophrenia diagnosis confirmed via Structured Clinical Interview; (2) age 18–65 years; (3) completion of at least primary education; (4) documented schizophrenia history with acute relapse following ≥ 4 weeks of medication discontinuation; (5) no anti-inflammatory or antibiotic use within 4 weeks preceding enrollment. Exclusion criteria for all participants encompassed neurological comorbidities (intellectual disability, dementia, epilepsy, neurodegenerative conditions, traumatic brain injury), endocrine disorders (thyroid dysfunction, diabetes mellitus), alcohol/substance dependence, or recent anti-inflammatory/antibiotic exposure.

A total of 55 healthy subjects matched for age and sex were recruited through community advertisements. The inclusion criteria for healthy controls were: (1) age 18–65 years; (2) absence of any current or lifetime DSM-IV Axis I disorder, as confirmed by the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV (non-patient edition); and (3) no family history of psychiatric disorders in first-degree relatives.

The exclusion criteria for healthy controls were identical to those for patients, including: (1) epilepsy or other neurological disorders (e.g., neurodegenerative diseases, history of traumatic brain injury); (2) endocrine system diseases (e.g., thyroid dysfunction, diabetes mellitus); (3) alcohol or substance dependence; and (4) use of anti-inflammatory or antibiotic medications within 4 weeks prior to enrollment.

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fourth People’s Hospital of Lianyungang and conducted in accordance with the ethical principles for medical research involving human subjects described in the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to inclusion in this study, written informed consent was obtained from all participants, as well as from their legal guardians, due to the potential impairment of the participants’ capacity to consent resulting from their medical condition.

ECT

Participants underwent 8–12 bilateral ECT sessions scheduled on alternate mornings (07:00–09:00) following overnight fasting. Each session followed a standardized anesthetic protocol: intravenous atropine sulfate (0.05 mg) for secretion control, propofol (0.8–1.2 mg/kg) for anesthesia induction, and succinylcholine (1.0 mg/kg) for muscle relaxation. A Thymatron® System IV device (Somatics LLC, Lake Bluff, IL, USA) delivered stimulation through bilaterally positioned frontotemporal electrodes. Treatment parameters remained consistent throughout: maximum charge 504 mC, current 0.9 A, frequency range 10–70 Hz, pulse width 0.5 ms, and maximum stimulus duration 8 s. Clinical staff continuously monitored motor manifestations, cardiac activity, and electroencephalographic patterns during each procedure. The total study duration for each participant was approximately 4 weeks, from baseline assessment (within 24 h before the first ECT session) to post-treatment assessment (within 2 h after the final ECT session).

Clinical symptom assessment

Two experienced psychiatrists independently evaluated symptom severity using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) on blood collection days [31], with an interrater correlation coefficient greater than 0.8 throughout the study. Treatment response categorization occurred after the 4-week ECT course based on PANSS total score reduction: responders demonstrated > 25% improvement from baseline, while non-responders showed ≤ 25% reduction [24]. All antipsychotic medications were converted to chlorpromazine equivalent doses for standardized comparison [32].

Measurements of oxidative stress levels

Venous blood samples were collected at two critical timepoints: within 24 h preceding initial ECT (baseline) and within 2 h following final ECT session completion. The post-treatment collection window was specifically selected to capture cumulative effects of the complete ECT course while minimizing acute procedural influences. Morning fasting samples were obtained between baseline and post-treatment (07:00–09:00). Blood was collected in procoagulant tubes, allowed to clot for 30 min at ambient temperature, then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min. Serum aliquots were immediately prepared and cryopreserved at -80 °C until analysis by laboratory personnel masked to clinical data.

Serum concentrations of GSH-Px, total SOD (TSOD), MnSOD, CuZnSOD, and CAT were quantified using microplate methodology with commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) following manufacturer specifications. All specimens were analyzed in duplicate, yielding intra-assay coefficients of variation of 1.2–5.8% and inter-assay coefficients of 3.4–6.7%. Results are presented as µmol/L.

Statistical analysis

Data distribution normality was initially examined using Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests. For normally distributed continuous variables, between-group comparisons employed independent samples t-tests with results expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Non-normally distributed continuous data were analyzed via Mann-Whitney U tests and reported as median with interquartile range. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests and presented as frequencies and percentages. Within-subject comparisons of PANSS scores and antioxidant enzyme levels before versus after ECT were conducted using paired t-tests. Analysis of covariance was implemented to adjust for potential confounders including age, educational attainment, sex, illness onset age, and disease duration. Correlation analyses between antioxidant enzyme levels and psychopathological measures utilized Pearson’s or Spearman’s coefficients based on data distribution characteristics. To identify independent predictors of symptom domains, multiple linear regression models were constructed incorporating GSH-Px, TSOD, MnSOD, CuZnSOD, and CAT as independent variables while controlling for demographic and clinical covariates. Statistical power calculations and effect size estimation (Cohen’s d) were performed using G*Power software (version 3.1; available at https://www.psychologie.hhu.de/arbeitsgruppen/allgemeine-psychologie-und-arbeitspsychologie/gpower). Two-tailed tests with α = 0.05 defined statistical significance throughout. All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 22.0 for Windows (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Table 1 presents the demographic and clinical profiles of participants enrolled in this study. The patient group and healthy control group were comparable with respect to age, sex distribution, educational attainment, smoking habits, and body mass index (all p > 0.05), indicating successful matching between groups. Among the 110 inpatients experiencing acute schizophrenia relapse, the mean age at disease onset was 24.90 ± 5.56 years, with an average illness duration of 11.15 ± 9.11 years. A family history of psychiatric disorders was documented in 26 patients (23.6% of the cohort). Throughout the study period, all patients received concurrent antipsychotic pharmacotherapy alongside ECT, with a mean chlorpromazine-equivalent daily dose of 764.32 ± 554.98 mg administered during the treatment course.

Association of OS markers with psychopathological symptoms

In acute relapsing schizophrenia patients, GSH-Px levels exhibited a significant negative correlation with the PANSS positive subscale score (r = -0.255, df = 108, p = 0.007), a finding mirrored by TSOD levels (r = -0.232, df = 108, p = 0.015), as detailed in Table 2. However, after group-wise Bonferroni correction (α = 0.0125 per OS marker), only GSH Px levels remained significantly negatively correlated with the PANSS positive subscale score (corrected p = 0.028) [33]. Conversely, statistical analyses showed no significant associations between MnSOD levels, CuZnSOD levels, the MnSOD/CuZnSOD ratio, CAT levels, or the PANSS total score and any other PANSS subscale scores (all p > 0.05). Further, stepwise multiple regression analyses were performed with the PANSS positive subscale score as a dependent variable and serum GSH-Px levels, TSOD levels, and GSH-Px* TSOD as independent variables, while controlling for covariates of age, sex, smoking status, education level, chlorpromazine equivalent dose, age of onset, and duration of illness. The results revealed negative associations between serum GSH-Px levels and PANSS positive subscale scores (β = −0.246, t = − 2.736, p = 0.007, Fig. 2). Age of onset (β = 0.366, t = 3.171, p = 0.002) and disease duration (β = -0.258, t = -2.230, p = 0.028) were identified as confounding factors in the relationships of GSH-Px levels with the PANSS positive subscale scores.

Changes in OS markers within the responder subgroup and comparisons with healthy controls

Based on a reduction rate of ≥ 25% in the PANSS total score after ECT as the criterion for clinical improvement, patients were classified as responders or non-responders. Overall, 89 patients (80.9%) met the improvement criterion, while 21 (19.1%) did not. In the responder group, serum levels of GSH-Px (225.42 ± 38.61 vs. 259.19 ± 62.61 U/ml, t = -4.090, p < 0.001), TSOD (68.98 ± 3.35 vs. 70.18 ± 4.21 U/ml, t = -3.054, p = 0.003), CuZnSOD (43.24 ± 8.05 vs. 49.84 ± 6.96 U/ml, t = -5.733, p < 0.001), and CAT (0.32 ± 0.08 vs. 0.37 ± 0.10 U/ml, t = -3.894, p < 0.001) were significantly increased after ECT, while serum MnSOD levels (25.74 ± 8.10 vs. 20.34 ± 6.28 U/ml, t = 4.931, p < 0.001) and the MnSOD/CuZnSOD ratio (0.65 ± 0.31 vs. 0.43 ± 0.19, t = 5.516, p < 0.001) were significantly decreased. No significant differences in OS markers were observed in the non-responder group.

One-way analysis of variance comparing pre- and post-ECT levels to healthy controls revealed significant differences in serum levels of GSH-Px (F = 15.008, p < 0.001), MnSOD (F = 27.427, p < 0.001), CuZnSOD (F = 28.149, p < 0.001), and CAT (F = 14.241, p < 0.001), and the MnSOD/CuZnSOD ratio (F = 31.126, p < 0.001). Bonferroni post-hoc analysis showed that for GSH-Px, pre-ECT levels were significantly lower as compared to the healthy controls (p < 0.001), while post-ECT levels were significantly increased as compared to pre-ECT levels (P < 0.001) but showed no significant difference from the healthy controls (p = 1.000, Fig. 3A). For MnSOD, pre-ECT levels were significantly lower than the healthy controls (p = 0.019) and post-ECT levels were further reduced as compared to both the healthy controls (p < 0.001) and pre-ECT levels (p < 0.001, Fig. 3B). Regarding CuZnSOD, post-ECT levels were significantly higher than both the healthy controls (p < 0.001) and pre-ECT levels (p < 0.001), while there was no significant difference between pre-ECT levels and the healthy controls (p = 0.105, Fig. 3C). For the MnSOD/CuZnSOD ratio, both pre-ECT (p = 0.009) and post-ECT (p < 0.001) levels were significantly lower than for the healthy controls. Additionally, post-ECT levels were significantly lower than pre-ECT levels (p < 0.001, Fig. 3D). Regarding serum CAT, pre-ECT (p < 0.001) and post-ECT levels were lower than for the healthy controls (p = 0.035), but post-ECT levels were higher than the pre-ECT levels (p = 0.006, Fig. 3E). Further, in the responder group, Spearman’s correlation analysis revealed significant negative correlations of changes to serum GSH-Px levels with the PANSS total score (r = -0.291, Bonferroni corrected p = 0.024, Fig. 4A) and PANSS general subscale scores (r = -0.320, Bonferroni corrected p = 0.008, Fig. 4B) following ECT. Sensitivity analyses confirmed that the significant pre-to-post ECT changes in all OS markers remained robust after adjusting for antipsychotic dose (CPZeq), and Spearman correlation analyses further revealed that ΔGSH-Px was significantly negatively correlated with improvements in PANSS positive, general, and total scores in responders (all detailed results in Tables S1–S2).

Baseline factors associated with treatment response

As shown in Table S3, baseline GSH-Px levels were significantly higher in responders than in non responders (p = 0.001), while no other significant differences were observed in demographic characteristics, clinical variables, or other OS markers between the two groups. To identify independent predictors of clinical improvement following ECT, we performed binary logistic regression analysis with treatment response (responder = 1, non-responder = 0) as the dependent variable. The model incorporated demographic factors (age, sex, education level), clinical parameters (BMI, smoking status, chlorpromazine-equivalent dose, illness duration, age at onset), baseline psychopathological severity (PANSS total score), and pre-treatment OS indicators (GSH-Px, TSOD, MnSOD, CuZnSOD, CAT, and MnSOD/CuZnSOD ratio). Multivariate logistic analysis revealed that GSH-Px was a favorable predictor of the outcome (B = -0.031, odds ratio (OR): 0.969, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.951–0.988, p = 0.001), while age (B = 0.040, OR: 1.041, 95% CI༚0.939–1.154], p = 0.448), sex (B = -0.391, OR: 0.677, 95% CI༚0.198–2.317, p = 0.534), education level (B = 0.079, OR: 1.082, 95% CI: 0.897–1.304, p = 0.410), BMI (B = -0.108, OR: 0.898, 95% CI: 0.781–1.033, p = 0.133), smoking status (B = 0.966, OR: 2.628 [95% CI: 0.720–9.592, p = 0.144), chlorpromazine equivalent dose (B = 0.001, OR: 1.001, 95% CI: 1.000–1.001, p = 0.285), duration of illness (B = 0.008, OR: 1.008, 95% CI: 0.887–1.145, p = 0.907), and baseline PANSS total score (B = -0.001, OR: 0.999, 95% CI: 0.960–1.040, p = 0.975) were not significant predictors of the outcome.

Discussion

The present study revealed several key findings: (1) as compared to the healthy controls, acute relapse of schizophrenia was associated with significantly decreased serum GSH-Px, MnSOD, and CAT levels and MnSOD/CuZnSOD ratios after Bonferroni correction for six comparisons. CuZnSOD levels showed a trend toward elevation that did not survive correction, and TSOD did not differ between groups; (2) reduced serum GSH-Px levels were significantly negatively correlated with the PANSS positive symptom score; (3) after ECT, serum GSH-Px, CuZnSOD, and CAT levels were significantly increased and serum MnSOD levels and the MnSOD/CuZnSOD ratio were significantly decreased; (4) in treatment responders, changes to serum GSH-Px levels were positively correlated with reductions in both the PANSS total score and general symptom score, and (5) GSH-Px may be a potential indicator of improvement to clinical symptoms. While previous studies have investigated various OS markers in ECT-treated patients with schizophrenia [24, 34], this is the first investigation of SOD isoenzyme dynamics—specifically, SOD activity levels and the MnSOD/CuZnSOD ratio—in a Chinese schizophrenia cohort post-ECT.

Our study demonstrated that acute relapse of schizophrenia was associated with significantly decreased serum levels of GSH-Px, MnSOD, and CAT. Yang et al. reported significantly lower plasma levels of Mn-SOD, CuZn-SOD, TSOD, and GSH-Px, and higher CAT levels in clinically stable medicated male schizophrenia patients [35]. Zhang et al. observed a significant decrease in the activities of SOD and GSH-Px in patients with chronic schizophrenia [36]. An et al. reported lower serum levels of SOD and GSH-Px levels in the observation group, and higher serum CAT levels in patients with early-onset schizophrenia [37]. Similarly, melatonin significantly ameliorated the impairment of passive avoidance memory, place navigation, and spatial probe ability of mice induced by aluminum chloride. Melatonin was found to significantly prevent the decline of TSOD, CuZn-SOD and GSH-Px activities in the mouse cortex and hippocampus induced by aluminum chloride [38]. These discrepancies might be attributed to several factors, including differences in blood sample sources, medication status, disease state, sample processing methods, sample size, and the heterogeneity of OS responses across different stages of schizophrenia. These findings highlight the central role of OS in the pathophysiology of schizophrenia, particularly through dysregulation of the antioxidant enzyme system. GSH-Px, MnSOD, and CAT are key enzymes for scavenging free radicals, and their reduced activity directly impairs defenses against oxidative damage [39]. In the present study, the MnSOD/CuZnSOD ratio was reduced in schizophrenia patients experiencing acute relapse after Bonferroni correction. CuZnSOD levels showed a non-significant trend toward elevation. This pattern could reflect a disruption to mitochondrial redox homeostasis, while the trend for CuZnSOD might suggest a compensatory response that did not reach statistical significance. MnSOD is primarily localized in mitochondria, whereas CuZnSOD is in the cytoplasm. The decreased MnSOD: CuZnSOD ratio may indicate more pronounced mitochondrial OS, consistent with reports of mitochondrial dysfunction in schizophrenia [3, 40]. The non‑significant trend toward higher CuZnSOD levels, together with the unchanged TSOD levels, might reflect a compensatory attempt that was insufficient to reverse the overall imbalance of the antioxidant defense system; however, this interpretation is exploratory and requires validation in larger studies. The precise molecular mechanisms underlying these alterations, particularly the relationship between the imbalance in SOD isoenzymes and acute exacerbation of psychotic symptoms, require further investigation.

Our study found a significant negative correlation between GSH-Px levels and the PANSS positive symptom score, further supporting the association between alterations to the redox system and disease severity. This finding aligns with previous studies that have documented associations between various OS markers and psychiatric symptoms. For instance, a study of antipsychotic-naïve first-episode patients revealed that baseline GSH-Px activity was associated with symptom improvement in non-smoking schizophrenia patients [41], while Yang et al. observed a positive correlation between GSH-Px levels and PANSS general psychopathology scores in patients with chronic schizophrenia [35]. Mechanistically, GSH-Px alleviates neuronal membrane damage and excessive activation of the dopaminergic system (dopaminergic system hypothesis) by scavenging H2O2 and lipid peroxides [42, 43]. This finding aligns with previous studies linking OS markers (e.g., MDA) to positive symptoms [44] and further establishes GSH-Px as a potential symptom-related biomarker [36]. However, the relationship between GSH-Px activity and clinical manifestations of schizophrenia requires cautious interpretation due to the potential influences of multiple underlying mechanisms, such as neuroinflammatory processes, overall OS balance, and pathophysiological differences across clinical stages. Some recent studies suggest that combining oxidative, inflammatory, and metabolic markers into composite indices may be more informative than using single markers. For example, Rahman et al. [45] found that atherogenic indices predict cardiovascular risk better than any single lipid measure. Following this idea, we plan to build composite panels that include multiple oxidative markers, such as GSH-Px-to better predict treatment response in future studies.

Schizophrenia patients consistently demonstrate a state of OS, featuring impaired antioxidant defenses and aberrant free radical metabolism. This imbalance is strongly implicated in the mechanisms that underlie the development and progression of this disorder [46]. After ECT treatment, the total oxidant status and calculated OS index values were significantly decreased in schizophrenia patients as compared to baseline measurements [47]. The impact of ECT on OS markers has been explored in both human and animal studies, although the findings were inconsistent [48]. In this study, following ECT treatment in patients with acute relapsed schizophrenia, the significant increase in serum GSH-Px, CuZnSOD, and CAT levels, coupled with the decrease in MnSOD levels and the MnSOD/CuZnSOD ratio, suggests that ECT plus antipsychotics may ameliorate symptoms by improving OS conditions, with these indicators (like GSH-Px, and CAT levels) approaching levels comparable to those of healthy controls. Barichello et al. showed that a single electroconvulsive shock (ECS) and multiple ECS administrations could downregulate lipid peroxidation levels, but upregulated SOD and CAT levels in various brain domains of rats [49]. In psychiatric patients, Mahmut et al. reported that ECT significantly lowered serum total oxidant status and raised total antioxidant status, consistent with the alleviation of OS [50]. Notably, the observed reduction in MnSOD levels and the MnSOD/CuZnSOD ratio is not indicative of a generalized failure of antioxidant defense, but rather can be interpreted as an adaptive response to a fundamental shift in the cellular redox landscape. This interpretation is consistent with recent findings highlighting heterogeneous and sometimes paradoxical oxidative responses across psychiatric populations [51]. Evidence from animal models indicates that ECS intricately regulates mitochondria, characterized by altered fission-fusion dynamics, attenuated respiratory function, and a consequent reduction in mitochondrial superoxide generation [52, 53]. This diminishment of the primary substrate for MnSOD logically leads to decreased demand, providing a mechanistic basis for the observed adaptive downregulation of the enzyme in our study. This phenomenon likely signifies a strategic shift in the cellular redox landscape rather than a mere diminution of overall antioxidant capacity. Based on our results, the increase in GSH-Px and CAT appears to be treatment-related adaptive changes, as they were associated with symptom improvement and approached healthy control levels. In contrast, the decrease in MnSOD likely reflects an adaptive downregulation due to reduced mitochondrial superoxide production, rather than a sign of increased OS. The non-significant trend for CuZnSOD elevation is exploratory and may represent a compensatory attempt that was insufficient to reverse the overall redox imbalance. These interpretations may help explain some inconsistencies in the literature, where antioxidant enzyme activities have been reported as increased, decreased, or unchanged across different studies - differences possibly due to illness stage, medication status, sample size, or assay methods.

Simultaneously, the robust upregulation of cytosolic antioxidants likely occurs in response to significant oxidative challenges associated with ECT-augmented treatment originating outside the mitochondria, which are potentially linked to pro-oxidant processes that underpin neuroplastic remodeling, such as heightened neural activity, neurotransmitter turnover, neurogenesis, synaptogenesis, angiogenesis, or gliogenesis, as supported by both animal and human studies [48, 54]. Therefore, the therapeutic efficacy of ECT-augmented treatment for schizophrenia patients may not stem from blanket suppression of OS, but rather sophisticated recalibration of the redox environment [50]. This novel perspective positions the dynamic interplay between the mitochondrial and cytosolic antioxidant pathways as a central mechanism in the capacity of ECT to ameliorate the oxidative pathophysiology of schizophrenia. This rebalancing of SOD isoenzymes, with the systemic antioxidant status (GSH-Px, CAT) normalizing towards healthy control levels, strongly points to global reduction in OS and improved management of superoxide radicals across cellular compartments.

Furthermore, the convergence of OS indicators with those of healthy controls suggests that ECT may play a role in regulating redox balance, potentially helping to correct the pre-existing OS imbalance in schizophrenia patients [55]. Our results contribute to this narrative by demonstrating that ECT-augmented treatment is associated with differential modulation of peripheral OS markers: GSH-Px and CAT levels moved toward healthy control values, whereas MnSOD decreased and CuZnSOD increased, suggesting an adaptive recalibration rather than a uniform normalization. This convergence of biomarker levels positions OS as a modifiable element of schizophrenia pathophysiology and provides a plausible peripheral biological correlate for clinical improvement. Although a direct causal link remains to be established, these parallel findings invite the hypothesis that the reduction of systemic OS may constitute one of the mechanisms through which ECT‑augmented treatment fosters a plastic brain state conducive to recovery.

In this study, the significant correlation between rising GSH-Px levels and reductions in both PANSS total and general symptom scores among ECT responders suggests that this antioxidant enzyme could predict therapeutic efficacy, thereby identifying a novel biomarker to monitor the treatment responses of schizophrenia patients. We further examined the relationships between changes in OS markers and changes in PANSS subscores in the responder group. After stringent multiple-comparison correction, only ΔGSH-Px showed significant and consistent negative correlations with improvements in positive, general, and total symptom scores, whereas no significant associations were observed with negative symptoms. These domain-specific findings reinforce the specificity of GSH-Px dynamics as a correlate of symptom improvement, particularly in the positive and general psychopathology domains. The lack of correlation between ΔGSH-Px and Δnegative symptoms aligns with the well-known limited efficacy of ECT on negative symptoms [56], suggesting that GSH-Px may track treatment-responsive symptom dimensions rather than global pathology. The mechanisms by which ECT modulates OS may involve at least two interconnected pathways. One potential mechanism involves neuroinflammation, whereby ECS-induced microglial activation and the subsequent upregulation of proinflammatory cytokines can amplify OS responses [57]. Activated microglia generate ROS and reactive nitrogen species, which may exert dual effects, either causing neuronal damage or promoting neuroplasticity via specific signaling pathways [58]. A second, non-exclusive pathway may operate through glutamate receptor signaling. Dong et al. reported that ECT increases NMDA receptor expression while lowering its stimulation threshold in the rat brain [59]. Crucially, the NMDA receptor and neuroinflammation pathways engage in a reciprocal interaction with the OS system [60]. Moreover, moderate OS may exert compensatory protective effects by inducing upregulation of antioxidant defense systems and enhancing cellular tolerance to injury. These findings from preclinical studies, combined with the observed changes to peripheral GSH-Px levels during ECT, suggest a possible link between oxidative responses associated with ECT-augmented treatment and therapeutic effects. However, further research is needed.

An interesting finding of this study is that higher GSH-Px levels may serve as a potential indicator of improved clinical symptoms of schizophrenia patients. This predictive value remained significant after controlling for baseline symptom severity, indicating GSH-Px as an independent biological marker of treatment response. The observed elevation in GSH-Px levels reflects a critical compensatory adjustment within the redox system, suggesting a potential self-protective mechanism that may play a crucial role in mitigating the underlying pathophysiology of schizophrenia and facilitating clinical recovery [61, 62]. Higher GSH-Px levels suggest enhanced antioxidant capacity and improved cellular metabolic function, which may contribute to neuronal protection and functional recovery [63]. However, the specific regulatory mechanisms of GSH-Px in the pathophysiological process of schizophrenia warrant further investigation.

Based on these findings, we propose a possible framework to explain how ECT combined with antipsychotics might lead to clinical improvement. This framework involves three interconnected pathways. First, mitochondrial modulation: ECT-induced seizures may reduce mitochondrial superoxide production and oxidative burden, leading to adaptive downregulation of MnSOD and improved mitochondrial function [52, 53]; Second, neuroinflammatory regulation: ECT may reduce proinflammatory cytokine release and microglial activation, thereby decreasing systemic and central OS [57, 58]; Third, synaptic plasticity enhancement: Lower oxidative damage and inflammation may create conditions that favor neuroplasticity, supporting synaptic remodeling and functional recovery [54]. GSH-Px may play a role in linking these pathways, as it scavenges reactive oxygen species and can influence inflammatory signaling and neuronal protection. This perspective is consistent with earlier reviews on ECT mechanisms, which noted broad effects on neurogenesis, immune regulation, and the hypothalamic-pituitary-endocrine axis [48].

Several limitations in this study should be addressed. First, although we observed temporal changes to specific OS markers (e.g., GSH-Px, MnSOD) and clinical symptoms during ECT treatment, a definitive causal relationship between these changes could not be established due to the observational nature of our study. Second, the levels of OS enzymes (GSH-Px, MnSOD, CuZnSOD, and CAT) were only measured at two time points (before and after ECT), which may not fully capture the dynamic changes to the redox state throughout the entire treatment process. Third, this was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, thus requiring validation through larger-scale multicenter studies in the future. Fourth, we only measured peripheral OS markers. Although evidence supports a peripheral-central correlation, future studies incorporating cerebrospinal fluid analysis or neuroimaging techniques would provide more direct evidence of brain OS changes during ECT. Fifth, as antipsychotic medications were administered concurrently with ECT following standard clinical practice, it was not possible to disentangle the individual effects of ECT from those of antipsychotics on the OS markers. Although our sensitivity analysis adjusting for CPZeq confirmed that the observed biomarker changes persisted after accounting for antipsychotic dosage, this does not fully eliminate the possibility of synergistic or interactive effects. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as reflecting the overall effect of ECT-augmented treatment, not ECT alone. Sixth, the healthy control group (n = 55) was relatively small compared to the patient group. Although the observed effect sizes were acceptable (Cohen’s d = 0.18–0.54) and group matching was successful, a larger control sample would have provided more precise estimates of normal reference ranges. Future studies with larger control cohorts are warranted to confirm our findings. Seventh, the small number of non‑responders (n = 21) precludes robust predictive modeling; therefore, our finding regarding baseline GSH‑Px as a potential indicator of treatment response should be considered exploratory and requires validation in larger independent cohorts. Eighth, our study exclusively included patients with multi-episode schizophrenia. Therefore, the findings may not be directly generalizable to first-episode or drug-naïve patients. Future studies should examine whether similar OS dynamics occur in earlier stages of the illness. Ninth, the response criterion of ≥ 25% reduction in PANSS total score is commonly used in ECT studies but represents a relatively low threshold; therefore, our responder group may include patients with only modest symptom improvement. Future studies should consider using more stringent criteria (e.g., ≥ 50% reduction) to define response. Tenth, we did not measure systemic inflammatory markers or composite immune-inflammatory indices. Future studies incorporating these measures are warranted to explore the interactions between OS and inflammation in the context of ECT-augmented treatment.

Conclusion

The present investigation demonstrates that acute exacerbation of schizophrenia is associated with profound disturbances in systemic redox homeostasis. Specifically, we observed diminished serum concentrations of GSH-Px, MnSOD, and CAT, alongside an elevated MnSOD/CuZnSOD ratio and a non-significant trend toward increased CuZnSOD levels in patients experiencing acute relapse compared to healthy controls. These findings point to a complex dysregulation spanning both mitochondrial and cytosolic antioxidant defense mechanisms during active disease phases. Then following a course of electroconvulsive therapy combined with antipsychotics, we documented significant restoration of redox equilibrium. This normalization was characterized not only by elevated GSH-Px, CuZnSOD, and CAT concentrations but also by a selective reduction in MnSOD levels and the MnSOD/CuZnSOD ratio. The compartment-specific modulation of antioxidant enzymes—particularly the attenuation of mitochondrial MnSOD relative to its cytosolic counterpart—suggests that ECT‑augmented treatment may trigger adaptive responses tailored to alleviate oxidative burden within distinct subcellular compartments. Such refined regulation implies that therapeutic effects extend beyond generalized antioxidant enhancement to encompass targeted recalibration of superoxide metabolism. Notably, the magnitude of GSH-Px elevation following treatment correlated significantly with symptomatic improvement among patients who met response criteria, whereas non-responders exhibited minimal changes in redox markers. This differential pattern underscores the potential utility of GSH-Px as a dynamic indicator of treatment efficacy. Furthermore, baseline GSH-Px level showed a potential association with clinical improvement in this exploratory analysis, retaining statistical significance after adjustment for initial symptom severity. This predictive capacity positions GSH-Px as a promising biomarker for identifying patients likely to benefit from ECT, potentially guiding personalized treatment decisions.

The coordinated shifts observed across mitochondrial (MnSOD) and cytosolic (GSH-Px, CuZnSOD) antioxidant systems support the concept that the therapeutic mechanism of ECT-augmented treatment mechanism involves systemic recalibration of oxidative stress responses rather than isolated effects on individual enzymes. Such integrated modulation may contribute to the restoration of cellular redox balance and subsequent clinical improvement.

Collectively, these findings advance our understanding of the pathophysiological processes underlying schizophrenia and highlight the relevance of markers in monitoring disease activity and treatment response. Future investigations should expand the panel of OS indicators examined and explore how different therapeutic modalities influence redox status, with the ultimate goal of optimizing clinical outcomes through mechanism-informed treatment strategies.
