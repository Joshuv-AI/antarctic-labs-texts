Multisite Randomized Controlled Trial of CareSTEPS: A Supportive Care Intervention for the Family Caregivers of Patients With Advanced Lung Cancer
Authors: Maria L Rangel, Kathrin Milbury, Karen Kayser, Robert Taylor Ripley, Elizabeth Kvale, Hoda Badr
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesPatients with advanced lung cancer (LC) face significant physical, psychological, and functional challenges, increasing their reliance on caregivers for practical and emotional support. This study evaluates the efficacy of CareSTEPS (Self-Care, Stress management, Symptom management, Effective communication, Problem-solving, and Social support), a 6-week telephone-delivered intervention designed to improve psychological functioning (depression and anxiety symptoms) and reduce caregiver burden among family caregivers of patients with advanced LC.MethodsIn this multisite, open-label, parallel-group randomized controlled trial, 174 caregivers (74.1% female individuals, 40.2% from a racial or ethnic minority group) of patients with stage IIIB or IV NSCLC or extensive-stage SCLC completed baseline surveys and were randomly assigned to CareSTEPS (n = 87) or usual care (n = 87) using stratified block randomization. Caregivers in the CareSTEPS arm received a manual with information on self-care, stress management, symptom management, effective communication, problem-solving, and social support. They also participated in six weekly telephone sessions with Masters-level trained interventionists with a mental health background, who provided psychoeducation, skills training, and support. Eight weeks after baseline, caregivers completed a follow-up survey.ResultsUsing an intent-to-treat framework, analyses of covariance were conducted to assess the efficacy of CareSTEPS, with p values lower than 0.05 indicating significant differences. Caregivers who received CareSTEPS reported improved psychological functioning and lower burden compared with those who received usual care, with effect sizes ranging from small to large (depression d = −0.55, anxiety d = −0.81, burden d = −0.37).ConclusionsThe CareSTEPS intervention reports great promise in meeting the critical support needs of the family caregivers of patients with advanced LC, significantly improving caregivers' psychological functioning and reducing their burden. Overall, the findings emphasize the importance of incorporating caregiver support into the comprehensive management of advanced LC.Clinical Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT02414672.Keywords: Lung cancer, Caregivers, Intervention, Supportive care, Palliative care

Introduction

Lung cancer (LC), is the second most prevalent cancer and leading cause of cancer death in the United States, posing significant challenges for patients and their family caregivers. LC primarily manifests as NSCLC and, to a lesser extent, SCLC,1 and is frequently diagnosed at advanced stages, so treatment and management are primarily palliative.2 Despite advances in treatment, such as immunotherapy, which have increased survival rates for some patients, the prognosis remains poor and most patients will not survive.3 Patients receiving treatment for advanced LC frequently report significant symptom burdens, such as diminished physical functioning, shortness of breath, and pain.4 They are also more likely than patients with other types of cancer to have anxiety and depression symptoms.5 These progressive symptoms cause increased reliance on caregivers, exacerbating the suffering of families, who play an important role in providing care and emotional support.6

The early integration of palliative care with standard oncologic care has been reported to improve quality of life and extend survival in advanced LC.7 Leading clinical practice guidelines also recommend concurrent palliative and standard oncologic care from the time of diagnosis.8 Although family support is deeply ingrained in palliative care philosophy,9 real-world support for caregivers is frequently lacking, even when acknowledged.10 Caregivers of patients with LC report feeling unprepared, lacking caregiving resources,11,12 having low self-efficacy for managing patient symptoms, experiencing significant emotional distress,13, 14, 15 and receiving minimal psychosocial care.6,16,17 This contributes to caregiver burden18 and poor mental health,19 which can adversely affect the caregiver’s ability to provide quality care to the patient. This discrepancy between the philosophical emphasis on family support and the actual support received by caregivers stems from systemic issues like inadequate caregiver training, limited support services, and poor integration of informal caregiving into the patient care plan.20

While numerous interventions have been tested for cancer caregivers, interventions specifically addressing the needs of advanced LC caregivers in the outpatient setting remain limited.21 This highlights a significant gap in interventions tailored to the unique challenges faced by this group, who are managing palliative treatment rather than end-of-life care. Likewise, most educational resources in palliative care are designed for patients, despite caregivers' preference for materials tailored to their specific needs.22,23 Targeted interventions that provide caregivers with tools for effective caregiving is a more comprehensive approach that has great potential for improving caregiver mental health and enhancing supportive care for patients with advanced LC.

Building on our previous work in LC,24,25 we developed CareSTEPS—an acronym for six domains that are central to the caregiving role (Self-Care, Stress management, Symptom management, Effective communication, Problem-solving, and Social support), a 6-week psychoeducational intervention for the caregivers of advanced patients with LC. CareSTEPS26,27—is manualized and delivered weekly over the phone to enhance acceptability.28 The program is grounded by Self-Determination Theory,29 which holds that meeting people’s needs for competence (self-efficacy), autonomy (choice and volition), and relatedness (belonging and connection) is critical for psychological health and well-being. Accordingly, the intervention aims to (1) enhance caregivers' competence by teaching self-care skills and strategies for coping with cancer and symptom management at home, (2) support caregivers' autonomy by offering a variety of choices and explaining the rationale behind recommendations to encourage informed decision-making, and (3) foster a sense of relatedness through teaching effective communication techniques, problem-solving strategies, and ways to sustain supportive relationships.

The purpose of this study was to assess the efficacy of the CareSTEPS intervention on caregivers’ psychological functioning and the burden of caregiving. Specifically, we hypothesized that caregivers receiving CareSTEPS would have fewer depression and anxiety symptoms and lower caregiver burden at an eight-week follow-up relative to those receiving usual care (UC). In addition, supplementary program evaluation data were collected to enhance understanding of the CareSTEPS intervention’s practical implementation and its potential for broader application.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Overview

This multisite, open-label, individually randomized trial, approved by the Baylor College of Medicine Institutional Review Board and registered with clinicaltrials.gov (NCT02414672) was conducted between 2015 and 2021. The study used a parallel-group design across four comprehensive cancer centers in the Northeastern, Midwestern, and Southwestern regions of the United States Sites were chosen on the basis of the diversity of patient populations and catchment areas served. Owing to the nature of the intervention involving psychoeducational and behavioral components delivered by interventionists, blinding of participants and caregivers was not feasible. Participants were aware of their group assignments as the interventions required active engagement and self-reporting of outcomes. Nevertheless, to maintain the integrity of the study results, the data analysts were blinded to group assignments. Caregivers completed baseline assessments (T1) and were randomly assigned to either CareSTEPS or UC. Randomization was stratified by histologic type (NSCLC versus SCLC), disease stage (IIIB versus IV or advanced), and type of patient-caregiver relationship (spouse or partner versus other family members) using a computer-generated schedule with random block sizes of two and four, maintaining a 1:1 allocation ratio within each stratum. Follow-up surveys were conducted 8 weeks after baseline (T2).

Participants

Caregivers were eligible if they were (1) a spouse/partner or family member of a patient with advanced LC (stage IIIB or IV NSCLC or extensive-stage SCLC) who was within 1 month of treatment initiation and had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status of 2 or less, (2) aged 18 years or older, (3) able to read and understand English, and (4) capable of providing informed consent.

Procedures

Patients who met this low-risk supportive care study’s disease, stage, and treatment criteria were identified by means (M) of electronic health record review and approached by research staff during their routine clinic visits. In instances where multiple caregivers were present, patients were asked to identify their primary caregiver. If the caregiver was present during the visit, they were asked to participate; otherwise, research staff attempted to contact and recruit them at a subsequent visit. Interested caregivers gave informed consent, completed self-report baseline surveys, and were randomly assigned to either the six-week CareSTEPS intervention or UC. Follow-up self-report surveys were either mailed with a postage-paid return envelope or given to caregivers to complete during routine clinic visits. Caregivers received $10 gift cards for their time/effort completing the baseline survey and $20 for the eight-week follow-up.

Measures

Primary Outcome

Psychological functioning was assessed using the six-item Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) short-form for depression, which measures negative mood and views of the self,30 and the six-item PROMIS short-form for anxiety, which measures fear, anxious misery (e.g., worry), and hyperarousal.30 Both measures apply a seven-day time frame, with responses from 1 (never) to 5 (always). Scores are summed and converted to T-scores, with a M of 50 and SD of 10. T-scores of 60 or more indicate moderate depression/anxiety in need for further psychological evaluation.31, 32, 33 In this study, internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s α) scores for depression, αT1 and αT2, were 0.90 and 0.91, respectively and the scores for anxiety, αT1 and αT2, were 0.89 and 0.90, respectively.

Secondary Outcome

Caregiver burden. The 12-item short form of the Zarit Burden interview34 assesses personal and role strain. Items are rated on a five-point Likert-type scale from 0 (never) to 4 (nearly always). The scores 0 to 10 indicated no to mild burden, 11 to 20 indicated moderate burden, and higher than 20 indicated high burden. Cronbach’s α scores, αT1 and αT2, were 0.90 and 0.91, respectively.

Sociodemographic and Medical Variables

Caregivers reported on their relationship to the patient, cohabitation status, age, race/ethnicity, education, employment, and marital status. Patient cancer type, disease stage, and diagnosis duration were confirmed by Ms of electronic health records.

Study Conditions

UC

UC included standard oncologic and primary palliative care for advanced LC diagnosis, with the patient’s oncologist managing pain, symptoms like depression and anxiety, and conducting basic discussions on prognosis and treatment goals. Specialty palliative care consultations were available on the oncologist's assessment. Caregivers could attend oncology/palliative care visits, but their presence was not required.

CareSTEPS

In addition to UC, CareSTEPS caregivers participated in a structured six-week intervention that included a manual and weekly 60-minute phone calls with a Masters-level mental health-trained interventionist (e.g., licensed social worker or counselor), who had undergone 16 hours of specialized training in the intervention protocol. The interventionists' training focused on the delivery of the CareSTEPS program, the intervention’s theoretical foundations, the psychoeducational content, and the practical application of each of the six core skill domains outlined in the manual.

The role of the interventionists was multifaceted, primarily centered around guiding caregivers through the intervention process. This included delivering psychoeducational content and facilitating skill-building through in-session activities. For example, interventionists led caregivers in stress management techniques such as guided relaxation exercises and cognitive reframing tasks, and in effective communication through role-playing exercises that simulated challenging caregiving scenarios.

Homework assignments were integral to the intervention, extending the learning process beyond the sessions. These tasks were directly linked to the skills covered in each session and were designed to promote continuous engagement and application of the skills. Caregivers were tasked with activities such as implementing a self-care action plan, maintaining a daily journal to document and reflect on stressors and their coping responses, and practicing communication techniques with family members. During the weekly sessions, the interventionist reviewed the homework submissions, providing personalized feedback and support. This review process was standardized across the program through a rubric that assessed skill application.

The scope of practice for interventionists was defined strictly within the parameters of the CareSTEPS protocol, ensuring that their interactions remained focused on educational and supportive functions rather than clinical psychological counseling or crisis management. Their role was to empower caregivers with knowledge and practical skills and enhance their efficacy in the caregiving role while maintaining a clear boundary to ensure the program's focus remained educational and supportive.

Program Evaluation and Treatment Integrity

At follow-up, caregivers in the CareSTEPS arm evaluated the sessions, manual, content, and interventionist on an 11-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating stronger approval. The time commitment to manual review, homework, and sessions were rated on a three-point scale (too long, too short, or just right). An open-ended question also asked about suggestions for improvement.

For each interventionist, a random subset of 25% of digitally recorded sessions were rated for fidelity by two trained coders. The fidelity checklist assessed whether session topics were covered and whether in-session exercises and homework were completed. The fidelity score was the number of topics divided by the fidelity criteria.

Sample Size Calculation

The sample size was determined for testing the hypothesis that CareSTEPS is superior to UC regarding the reduction of depression and anxiety symptoms. A sample size recalculation was conducted after 100 caregivers had been randomized (50 in each arm) using actual attrition rates rather than pretrial estimates and without examining treatment effects. On the basis of this, it was determined that a minimum of 87 caregivers per arm (n = 174) is required at baseline to detect an effect size of 0.46 with a two-tailed test, given a power of 0.80 and an alpha of 0.05. This target effect size is further justified by existing meta-analyses on caregiver interventions in cancer settings, which have reported effect sizes ranging from small (d = 0.20) to moderate (d = 0.50) for outcomes including depression and anxiety.35,36

Data Analysis

Descriptive analyses assessed feasibility, acceptability, recruitment rates, and baseline (T1) and follow-up (T2) Ms for both study arms. Pearson correlations were conducted for continuous variables. T-tests and Chi-square tests compared baseline sociodemographic characteristics across study arms to ensure balanced randomization and differentiated non-completers from completers.

Analyses of covariance were conducted to assess the efficacy of CareSTEPS, with T1 scores as model covariates and T2 scores as outcomes. Missing data were handled using multiple imputation methods. To address potential baseline group imbalance,37 sociodemographic and medical variables were examined. Model covariates were selected on the basis of their association with the study outcomes. Continuous variables were included as covariates if they reported a significant association with the outcomes at a p value lower than 0.05. Similarly, categorical variables were incorporated as covariates when M differences in the outcomes were statistically significant (p < 0.05). An intent-to-treat framework was implemented, and a two-sided p value of less than 0.05 was considered significant. We also calculated the effect size (Cohen’s d) for significant findings, interpreting effects as small (d = 0.20), medium (d = 0.50), and large (d = 0.80).38 Finally, a post hoc, observed power analysis was conducted. All statistical analyses were performed in IBM SPSS Statistics 29.1 (IBM).

To gauge the clinical significance of the treatment effects, we identified caregivers in the CareSTEPS and UC groups with high levels of depression and anxiety (T-scores > 60 on the PROMIS measures) and moderate to high levels of caregiver burden (Zarit-12 scores > 11). The proportion of caregivers in the CareSTEPS and UC groups whose scores moved below the case threshold versus those who remained stable or worsened from T1 to T2 was then examined using chi-square analysis or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate.

Results

Study Enrollment and Participation

The CONSORT diagram (Fig. 1) shows that 412 patients were screened. Seventy-eight were excluded because they did not meet the inclusion criteria, primarily owing to the lack of a caregiver or the research team’s inability to contact the caregiver to determine interest and eligibility. Of the 334 eligible caregivers, 187 (56%) consented. Sociodemographic characteristics did not differ significantly between caregiver participants and refusers. Refusals were primarily owing to a lack of caregiver interest or availability. Thirteen consenting caregivers dropped out before returning to the baseline survey (passive refusal), leaving 174 who were randomized (87 to CareSTEPS, 87 to UC). Telephone session attendance was 67% (four of six sessions) on average. In most cases, missed sessions were caused by conflicting demands or poor patient health. Make-ups were scheduled whenever possible. The eight-week follow-up was completed by 62 (71%) caregivers in the CareSTEPS arm and 56 (64%) caregivers in the UC arm. In most cases, the lack of follow-up completion was caused by the patient entering hospice/dying (n = 15 of 34; 44%), the caregiver being too busy (n = 10; 29%), or the patient’s poor health/death. On average, caregivers completed 2.5 homework assignments (range 0–5). Table 1 reports no significant differences in sociodemographic/medical characteristics or baseline study variables between groups. The chi-square and t tests found no significant differences in baseline demographic, medical, and outcome measures between study completers and non-completers.

Participant Characteristics

Table 1 describes the sociodemographic characteristics of the study sample. Caregivers were mostly female individuals (74.3%), lived with the patient (73.7%), were middle-aged (X¯ = 55.10, SD = 13.20), and employed (62%); 55.4% were spouses/partners, and the rest were adult sons/daughters or other close family members (e.g., siblings, son/daughter-in-law of the patient). No significant baseline differences by study arm were found. Independent-sample t-tests and analysis of variance of baseline data revealed that caregivers who lived with the patient reported greater caregiver burden (M = 15.69, SD = 8.60) than those who did not live with the patient (M = 11.78, SD = 8.28; t = 2.67, p = 0.01), employed caregivers reported greater depression (M = 12.77, SD = 4.65) than those who were not employed (M = 11.35, SD = 4.46) (t = 1.97, p = 0.05), and spouses/partners (M = 15.69, SD = 8.40) reported greater caregiver burden than sons/daughters (M = 14.71, SD = 9.28) and other family members (M = 11.44, SD = 8.00) (F = 2.97, p = 0.05). Also at baseline, 27 (15.5%) and 60 (34.5%) caregivers exceeded the cut-offs for the PROMIS depression and anxiety measures, respectively; and 114 (65.5%) reported moderate to high caregiver burden. As Table 2 reports, correlations between study outcomes were of moderate magnitude and in the expected direction. Likewise, there were no significant group differences regarding baseline participant characteristics.

Primary Outcomes

Depression

At T2 there was a significant difference in depression (p < 0.0001), with the CareSTEPS group having lower M scores (less depressive symptomatology) than the UC group. The effect size for this difference (Cohen’s d) was −0.55, which is a medium effect,39 with an observed power of 0.91.

Anxiety

At T2 there was a significant difference in anxiety (p < 0.0001), with the CareSTEPS group having lower M scores (less anxiety) than the UC group. The effect size (d) was −0.81, which is a large effect, with an observed power of 0.99.

Secondary Outcome

Caregiver Burden

At T1 there was a significant difference in caregiver burden (p < 0.0001), with the CareSTEPS group having lower M scores (less caregiver burden) than the UC group. The effect size was −0.37, which was a small to medium effect with an observed power of 0.91.

Clinical Significance

For caregivers meeting PROMIS depression criteria at baseline (N = 27), six of the 15 (40%) in UC improved versus 10 of the 12 (83.3%) in CareSTEPS, showing a significant difference (p = 0.047, Fisher’s exact test). In those meeting PROMIS anxiety criteria (N = 60), improvements were seen in 34.4% (11 out of 32) in UC versus 82.1% (23 of 28) in CareSTEPS (p < 0.001, Fisher’s exact test). Among caregivers with high burden at baseline (N = 43), 5.6% (one of 18) in UC improved compared with 56% (14 of 25) in CareSTEPS (chi-square = 11.72, p < 0.001).

Treatment Fidelity and Program Evaluation

CareSTEPS interventionists averaged 97% fidelity. Table 4 reports that participants liked the program's convenience, usefulness, and relevance and praised the interventionists. Most caregivers enjoyed their participation, but some wanted more personalized sessions. Others suggested choosing modules that addressed their caregiving challenges rather than completing the program to improve relevance and effectiveness.

Discussion

This study found that the CareSTEPS intervention reduces depression (d = −0.55), anxiety (d = −0.81), and burden (d = −0.37) in advanced LC caregivers relative to UC. These effect sizes are comparable to and exceed those reported in the broader literature on cancer caregivers,21,40 and suggest that CareSTEPS is a promising approach for supporting caregivers in the setting of advanced LC. Findings also provide compelling evidence for incorporating interventions that address caregiver needs into standard care protocols, thereby improving the well-being of those who play a critical role in the advanced cancer care ecosystem.

Recruitment and sustained participation in CareSTEPS highlight the challenges of engaging advanced cancer caregivers. Many caregivers refused owing to busy schedules, with 56% consenting. This highlights the significant demands on caregivers' time and suggests that traditional clinic-based strategies may be insufficient. Furthermore, focusing on accessible community-based outreach and caregiver mental health may help to increase reach and acceptance. Despite positive evaluations, the 67% attendance and 50% homework completion rates reveal that caregivers struggled to consistently participate in the multisession intervention. Caregivers' busy schedules may make self-directed homework less effective than phone calls with a trained professional who listens and coaches them. Small to large effect sizes were achieved despite suboptimal engagement rates, demonstrating the effectiveness of the program’s key components. These treatment effects imply the program could be shortened without losing impact. Streamlining CareSTEPS—reducing homework and introducing more flexible, potentially asynchronous, delivery methods—would better align with caregivers' complex realities. Simplifying the program in this manner would better meet caregivers' immediate needs and increase its feasibility and acceptability.

This study revealed that caregiver experiences vary by relationship, cohabitation, and employment status. Employed caregivers experienced more depression, likely from juggling work and caregiving. Spouses/partners bore a heavier burden, likely owing to closer emotional ties and greater responsibility for patient care. Those living with the patient may have experienced more intense demands, emphasizing the continuous nature of the caregiving role. These insights stress the need to tailor cancer support programs to the diverse challenges of different caregiver groups.

This study had several strengths, including its caregiver-centric and theory-grounded approach, and methodological rigor achieved through the use of standardized, validated measures and evidence-based strategies. The telephone-based delivery model provided convenient and accessible support, expanding its reach beyond clinical settings. Geographic diversity enhanced generalizability. Limitations included a lack of extended follow-up, which is often complicated in the advanced cancer setting owing to poor patient prognosis and increased caregiving demands.41,42 The predominance of female caregivers, which is consistent with broader caregiver demographics, may limit generalizability to male caregivers, though no statistically significant gender-based differences in distress were discovered. Limited racial/ethnic minority inclusion, partially owing to English-only delivery suggests future iterations of CareSTEPS may benefit from cultural and linguistic adaptations to appeal to a broader caregiver demographic.

Given that we compared CareSTEPS to UC rather than an attention control condition, it is possible that the observed improvements were due to nonspecific effects. At the same time, if the goal of a trial is to see if an intervention is superior to existing practices (in this case, UC), it must be compared with them.43 Because the most important factor in selecting a control condition is how well it fits the purpose of the trial,44 and the goal of this trial was to push the envelope of efficacy to achieve better outcomes for caregivers (rather than differentiate between specific and nonspecific effects), we believe that UC was a reasonable and appropriate comparison condition. In the future, we would like to investigate whether comparable outcomes can be achieved with a less intensive intervention, such as that used in attention control. That, nevertheless, is a comparative effectiveness question that first requires a demonstration that CareSTEPS is superior to UC.

In conclusion, the CareSTEPS intervention significantly reduced caregiver depression, anxiety, and burden, relative to UC. These findings emphasize the importance of accessible caregiver-focused interventions in advanced LC care and support the notion that incorporating caregiver-focused interventions early in advanced cancer care is critical for improving caregiver well-being. Although caring for someone with advanced cancer is always difficult for family caregivers, the CareSTEPS intervention is a promising approach that has the potential to significantly improve cancer care quality, promote caregiver well-being, and inspire a shift toward a more holistic model that addresses both caregiver and patient needs. Given the positive outcomes observed, future research should explore the potential applicability of the CareSTEPS intervention to caregivers of patients with other chronic or terminal conditions. Such studies could investigate necessary adaptations, including tailoring support to the specific symptom management and progression stages of different diseases, and developing culturally sensitive materials to engage more diverse caregivers.
