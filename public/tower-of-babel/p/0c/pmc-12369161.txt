Mapping the interactions of critical factors in China’s first-contact healthcare systems using causal loop diagrams
Authors: Wenjun He, Yusupujiang Tuersun, Tao Yang, Yuting Wan, Minji Yong, Zizhen Huang, Zhiyong Xiao, Menglin Luo, Yujia Zhang, Yujie Liu, Tao Yang, Yi Qian, Dong Roman Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrimary healthcare plays a pivotal role in optimizing healthcare resource allocation and ensuring equitable access to services. In China, first-contact care within the tiered medical care system is essential for alleviating pressure on large hospitals and enhancing healthcare equity. However, challenges such as uneven resource distribution and low patient trust in primary institutions limit the effectiveness of first-contact care. This study aims to explore the dynamic interactions of key factors influencing first-contact effectiveness and provide evidence-based policy recommendations.MethodsA systematic approach was employed, integrating a literature review, qualitative interviews, and MICMAC structural analysis. Using Causal Loop Diagram (CLD) methodology, this study mapped the causal pathways and feedback mechanisms among factors such as health insurance awareness, medical consortium development, and tiered healthcare policy guidance. MICMAC analysis quantified the influence and dependence of these variables to identify key drivers within the system.ResultsThe study revealed that primary healthcare service quality, patient trust, and healthcare-seeking behaviors are are vital determinants for improving first-contact effectiveness. Key driving factors include health insurance awareness, medical consortium construction, and tiered healthcare policy guidance, which form reinforcing feedback loops to enhance system efficiency. The findings highlight the importance of resource sharing, trust-building, and policy support in optimizing the tiered medical care system.ConclusionsThis study provides a comprehensive framework for understanding the complexities of first-contact care within China’s healthcare system. The insights gained emphasize the need for targeted interventions, including promoting health insurance awareness, strengthening medical consortiums, and improving policy incentives. These recommendations can inform policy reforms aimed at achieving equitable and efficient healthcare systems globally.Trial registrationNot applicable, as this study does not involve a healthcare intervention on human participants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-23884-w.

Background

Primary healthcare is the first and most versatile level of care, responsible for prevention, treatment, rehabilitation and health promotion. Globally, robust primary healthcare systems have proven instrumental in improving population health outcomes, as seen in countries such as Brazil and the United Kingdom, where family health teams or general practitioner (GP) models [1, 2] help ensure first-contact care. International experiences highlight the pivotal role of strong primary care gatekeeping—backed by financial support, continuous professional training, and public trust—in reducing unnecessary hospital admissions and improving equity. According to statistics from the World Health Organization (WHO), primary healthcare services can meet approximately 80% of essential health needs, typically delivered through institutions such as community health service centers, township health centers, and village clinics [3].China’s “Healthy China 2030” blueprint highlights high‑quality primary healthcare as fundamental to universal coverage and system sustainability [4].

As the initial point of contact for residents accessing healthcare services, primary first-contact care plays a crucial role in disease screening, prevention, and primary diagnosis and treatment. Core functions include triage, early intervention and easing tertiary‑hospital congestion [5]. Ongoing reform embeds first‑contact primary care at the foundation of the national tiered system. This approach aims to achieve the rational allocation of medical resources and comprehensive healthcare service coverage, aligning closely with the overarching goals of the “Healthy China 2030” strategy [6]. Within this framework, enhancing the quality of primary healthcare services is crucial to ensuring the effectiveness of first-contact care. High-quality primary healthcare services not only improve residents’ first-contact experiences and trust but also strengthen the critical role of primary healthcare institutions in disease prevention and health management [7]. High-quality primary care improves triage and enables timely detection and intervention, thereby optimizing resource use [8, 9].

Despite strong policy and financial support, primary institutions have yet to fulfil the intended ‘gatekeeper’ role, as many patients still bypass them for large hospitals [10, 11]. The key reason for this issue lies in the shortage of professional medical personnel in primary healthcare institutions, particularly their limited capabilities in critical areas such as diagnosis, treatment, and health management. Studies have shown that the quantity and quality of healthcare workers are among the decisive factors influencing residents’ reluctance to prioritize primary healthcare institutions for their initial consultations [12]. For example, a nationwide survey revealed that less than 10% of personnel in primary healthcare institutions hold general practitioner qualifications, with most staff receiving only brief professional training, limiting them to providing basic health services. This lack of professional competence directly undermines residents’ trust in the diagnostic and treatment capabilities of primary healthcare institutions, prompting them to bypass these facilities and seek care at comprehensive hospitals instead [13]. Moreover, due to insufficient professional capabilities, primary healthcare institutions fail to effectively fulfill their roles in disease screening and chronic disease management, resulting in inefficiencies in resident health management. This mismatch in healthcare resource utilization not only exacerbates the resource overload at large hospitals but also significantly undermines the overall efficiency of the healthcare system [14].

In addition, the “policy intention–practice gap” between policy design and actual implementation outcomes further underscores the necessity of this research [15]. While the overarching goal is to establish a tiered medical care system centered on primary healthcare, improving the service capacity and accessibility of primary healthcare institutions remains a key focus. The Plan for the Construction of Primary Healthcare Service Systems (2015–2020) explicitly proposed optimizing resource allocation and strengthening the capacity of primary healthcare institutions to achieve the overall goal of “first-contact care at the primary level and tiered medical services.” The Healthy China 2030 blueprint further emphasized deepening healthcare system reform, enhancing the capacities of county-level hospitals and township health centers, and accelerating the redistribution of medical resources toward primary care to improve the accessibility and coverage of primary healthcare services. However, significant challenges persist during implementation, such as insufficient trust from residents in primary healthcare institutions, uneven distribution of medical resources, and resource shortages in staffing and equipment at the primary care level [16]. These factors collectively impact the actual effectiveness of the primary first-contact system, resulting in a deviation between policy implementation outcomes and original intentions. This misalignment between policy design and practical execution not only undermines the efficiency of the first-contact system but also reveals deeper systemic contradictions within the healthcare service framework [17].

Although existing studies have identified several key factors influencing residents’ primary first-contact decisions—such as the quality of primary healthcare services, patient behaviors, policy environment, and resource allocation—systematic analyses of how these factors interact to shape first-contact effectiveness remain insufficient. Most current research is limited to descriptive statistics and single-factor analyses, failing to fully uncover the causal pathways and dynamic feedback mechanisms between primary healthcare service quality and first-contact choices. The prevailing literature tends to examine these variables in isolation, often overlooking the complex interactions among them [18, 19]. For example, although primary healthcare service quality is widely recognized as having a significant impact on first-contact choices, existing studies often lack in-depth and systematic quantitative analysis when evaluating its interactions with other factors such as patient behaviors, policy environment, and resource allocation [20]. This methodological limitation makes it difficult to accurately assess the relative importance and underlying mechanisms of each factor in shaping first-contact effectiveness.

Building on the gaps identified in existing literature, this study adopts the CLD methodology to comprehensively evaluate multiple key factors influencing primary first-contact care and their interactions. As a systems thinking tool, CLD is well-suited to uncovering the causal relationships and dynamic feedback mechanisms among factors within complex healthcare systems [21]. CLD has achieved notable success in various public health fields. For instance, a study conducted in Australia mapped critical domains such as physical activity, dietary habits, and social influences [22]. A study in the Netherlands highlighted subsystems with fundamental objectives, such as online environments and profit maximization [23]. CLD has also been extensively applied to mental health issues, including analyzing addiction to substances such as alcohol and tobacco [24]. It has elucidated critical feedback mechanisms. The application of CLD is not limited to specific contexts [24, 25]. For example, it has also been used to analyze multi-sectoral systems influencing health promotion policies and practices. Thus, CLD can play a significant role in both scientific research and practical applications in public health [26].

Therefore, this study employs the CLD methodology to comprehensively construct and optimize a model that systematically analyzes the dynamic interactions among multiple key factors influencing primary first-contact care. Specifically, the study develops a CLD model to identify and uncover the causal feedback loops among multidimensional factors such as primary healthcare service quality, patient behaviors, policy environment, and resource allocation, further clarifying how these factors collectively affect the effectiveness of the primary first-contact system.Through this analytical framework, the study not only deepens the understanding of the mechanisms influencing first-contact effectiveness but also provides data support and practical guidance for policy optimization, thereby promoting the efficient operation of the first-contact system.

Methods

To comprehensively analyze the multiple key factors influencing first-contact effectiveness and their interactions, this study adopted a step-by-step, structured research approach to ensure the model’s comprehensiveness, validity, and criticality.

First, to ensure comprehensiveness, a systematic literature review was conducted to identify and integrate key variables—such as primary healthcare service quality, patient behaviors, policy environment, and resource allocation—and their documented interrelations in existing studies. This provided a theoretical foundation for constructing the initial version of the CLD. Next, to ensure the model’s practical applicability and contextual relevance, qualitative interviews were conducted with health policy makers, primary healthcare practitioners, and patients. These interviews collected firsthand data to refine and validate the initial CLD content. Furthermore, for precision and criticality, the CFIR (Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research) was utilized to explore barriers and facilitators in the implementation of first-contact policies. Through expert and key stakeholder interviews and scoring, the most significant factors influencing first-contact effectiveness were identified and incorporated into a revised CLD. Finally, the MICMAC (Matrice d’Impacts Croisés Multiplication Appliquée à un Classement) structural analysis method was applied to quantitatively assess the direct and indirect influence of these factors. This process completed the final version of the CLD, which systematically illustrates how multiple factors, including primary healthcare service quality, interact in complex ways to affect first-contact effectiveness. We chose CLD because it excels in revealing feedback mechanisms in healthcare systems, thereby highlighting how policy shifts or resource reallocations can produce cascading effects on first-contact decisions. MICMAC was selected to complement CLD by providing a structured means to quantify the relative impact of each identified factor. Such a hybrid approach ensures both the discovery of complex interdependencies (via CLD) and the systematic prioritization of influential variables (via MICMAC). This robust analytical framework provides a solid foundation for evidence-based healthcare policy optimization and practical improvements.

Systematic literature review

Literature search

This study developed a literature search strategy for constructing a CLD of the primary first-contact care system. The PICOS framework was used to define the key points of the search (detailed in Supplementary Table 1). Both Chinese and English databases were included: CNKI, Wanfang Data, PubMed, and Web of Science (SCI-E)-from 1 st January 2000 to 28th February 2023.

By refining the relevant dimensions of PICOS during preliminary searches and continuous optimization, this study constructed three keyword dimensions during the initial trial search. The first dimension is “Primary First-Contact Care”, which restricts the search scope to studies specifically in this domain. The second dimension is “Healthcare System”, which ensures the discussion remains within the healthcare system framework and excludes content related to primary first-contact care but not belonging to the healthcare system domain. The third dimension is “System Dynamics or Causal Loop Diagram”, focusing on the theme of causal relationship research (detailed in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3).

Literature screening and extraction

During the literature screening phase, all retrieved articles were imported into Zotero software for deduplication. Clear inclusion and exclusion criteria were established for each article (detailed in Supplementary Table 5).

After completing the literature screening, the research team used Excel to organize and summarize the information (template provided in Supplementary Table 6). During the data extraction phase, the Excel sheet was divided into six subsystems, aligned with the six dimensions of WHO’s Building Blocks framework [27]. The collected factors influencing primary first-contact care and their sources were organized into the Excel sheet.

Subsequently, researchers used the six subsystems in the Excel sheet to construct the CLD. In the initial stage, collaborative construction was carried out using an online shared PowerPoint, while in the later stage, detailed depiction was performed using Vensim software. Once the construction of each subsystem was completed, they were integrated into a comprehensive CLD.

Qualitative interviews and identification of key factors

This study adopted a structured qualitative interview approach to systematically explore the multiple key factors influencing first-contact effectiveness and their interactions. The interviews had two main objectives: first, to evaluate the initially constructed CLD, which was based on a systematic literature review. This evaluation aimed to ensure (a) the rationality of the causal relationships in the diagram, (b) the identification of any missing key variables, and (c) the removal of redundant or unnecessary causal pathways. By integrating stakeholders’ feedback, this step improved the accuracy and applicability of the diagram. Second, the interviews aimed to uncover the major barriers and facilitators impacting first-contact services.

The interview guide was designed using the CFIR [28] to comprehensively cover CFIR’s five key domains: intervention characteristics, outer setting, inner setting, characteristics of individuals, and the implementation process. The official CFIR Interview Guide Tool was employed to generate exploratory questions to guide respondents in sharing their experiences, perspectives, and insights, ensuring systematic and comprehensive data collection.

After drafting the preliminary interview guide, the research team conducted mock interviews to test the clarity and logic of the questions and made adjustments based on feedback. Supplementary questions and prompts were also prepared to accommodate varied responses from participants. The final guide was peer-reviewed by a senior qualitative research expert and a healthcare management expert with extensive experience in first-contact services to further enhance its content, structure, and applicability (see Supplementary Material Appendix 1 for details).

Interview participants

To achieve the research objectives, this study targeted stakeholders in the healthcare system closely related to tiered medical care. These stakeholders included, but were not limited to, healthcare service managers, policymakers, first-contact service providers, and practitioners in primary healthcare institutions. The selection of participants was based on the WHO Building‑Blocks framework, ensuring representation across all six dimensions (service delivery, health workforce, information systems, access to essential medicines, financing, and leadership/governance). Each dimension was represented by at least 1–2 stakeholders with extensive practical experience. Participants were selected based on their in-depth understanding of primary first-contact policies, while exclusion criteria included individuals unable to participate in the entire interview, those with weak relevance to the research field, or those lacking practical experience (see Supplementary Tables 6 and Supplementary Fig. 1).

Interview process

The interviews began with closed-ended questions to gather basic information about the participants, followed by open-ended questions exploring macro- and micro-level aspects of policy implementation across the five dimensions of the CFIR framework. This approach aimed to comprehensively understand the complexities of policy execution. Participants were invited to validate the causal relationships in the existing CLD, supplement missing elements, and propose revisions. This process improved the accuracy of the study while obtaining firsthand information on specific challenges encountered during the implementation of primary first-contact policies.

The interviews were led by the principal investigator with the participation of at least one team member to ensure a thorough understanding of responses. Digital recording and verbatim transcription were employed to maintain data integrity. Credibility was enhanced through analyst triangulation and member‑checking; dependability via an audit trail; transferability by providing thick contextual description; and confirmability through reflexive memos that documented how researchers’ prior experience with China’s tiered‑care reform might shape data interpretation. This process captured critical dimensions of primary first-contact policy implementation, providing an empirical foundation for policy optimization. Participants were encouraged to share their experiences candidly, yielding authentic and profound insights.

Cross-Impact Matrix Multiplication Applied to Classification (MICMAC)

In this study, we employed the Cross-Impact Matrix Multiplication Applied to a Classification (MICMAC) approach—an established method in complex systems research—to systematically measure both direct and indirect relationships among key variables. Specifically, we began by identifying fewer than 30 variables (derived from our literature review and expert consultations) that potentially influence the primary first-contact care system. We then constructed a direct impact matrix in which experts and stakeholders scored the impact of each variable on every other variable on a scale of 0 (no impact) to 3 (strong impact). This matrix-based scoring allowed us to calculate a ‘driving strength’ (the sum of each variable’s row) and a ‘dependence strength’ (the sum of each variable’s column), revealing how strongly each variable influences or depends on others.

The MICMAC technique further categorizes variables into four groups within an influence-dependence map: determinant variables (high influence, low dependence), relay variables (high influence, high dependence), dependent variables (low influence, high dependence), and autonomous variables (low influence, low dependence). Determinant variables represent key levers within the system, while dependent variables serve as principal outputs most sensitive to changes elsewhere in the system. Relay variables exhibit both substantial driving and dependence, underscoring their pivotal “linking” roles. By quantitatively identifying such patterns, MICMAC highlights the feedback loops that may reinforce or hinder first-contact system effectiveness, guiding which factors warrant priority interventions. Finally, the research team and stakeholders jointly reviewed the preliminary classification to ensure consistency and to refine the final set of variables used in the CLD construction.

Data analysis

The data analysis in this study employed multiple methods and tools to ensure a comprehensive understanding and quantitative support of the causal relationships between factors such as primary healthcare quality and first-contact effectiveness. A three-step approach to integrate and synthesize the interview data with the literature-based CLD. First, during the systematic literature review, the research team used Zotero software for literature management, extracting and organizing key variables and causal relationships. Next, two researchers independently coded all interview transcripts using NVivo, employing both deductive (CFIR-based) and inductive (emergent theme) coding procedures. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached, thus ensuring inter-coder reliability. we systematically mapped each emergent theme (e.g., “lack of referral standards,” “specialist allocation in medical consortiums,” “patient trust in primary care”) to the relevant loops or feedback pathways in our initial CLD. Where a theme did not match existing causal structures, we revised the CLD to add or remove variables or to modify causal directions. Third, to further validate and quantify the relationships among variables, we implemented MICMAC structural analysis using the Micmac Analyse Structure online tool. This step quantified both direct and indirect influences and dependencies, guiding the final prioritization of key drivers (e.g., “Medical Consortium Construction,” “Health Insurance Awareness”) and dependent variables (e.g., “Willingness to Seek Care at Primary Facilities”). This process identified the roles of each variable within the system, optimizing and constructing the final CLD. Through this combined qualitative–quantitative approach, we ensured that stakeholder insights were robustly integrated into the final CLD, making the model both theoretically grounded and empirically informed.

Results

Findings from literature analysis

In the data analysis process, multiple methods and software tools were employed to ensure a comprehensive understanding and quantitative support for the causal relationships between primary healthcare quality and first-contact effectiveness. Through a systematic literature review, the research team conducted searches in PubMed, Web of Science, CNKI, and Wanfang databases, initially identifying 5347 relevant articles. After removing 14 duplicates in Zotero, 5,333 unique references remained. Title/abstract screening and outcome indicators screening excluded 5,262 records leaving 71 articles for full‑text review. Ultimately, 63 articles were included in the study (Fig. 1). These articles provided critical information on the policy implementation outcomes, barriers, and facilitators of the primary first-contact system, laying a solid foundation for constructing the CLD. Construction of the Initial Causal Loop Diagram (Version 1)

Initial Causal Loop Diagram (CLD)

In the initial version of the primary first-contact care CLD constructed in this study, the structure is divided into three components—main framework, internal factors, and external factors—distinguished by different colors: the main framework is marked in red, internal factors in brown, and external factors in blue.The main framework, located at the center of the diagram, highlights the core logic chain: “Primary Healthcare Service Quality → Willingness to Seek Care at Primary Facilities (or Hospital Care Willingness) → Number of Primary Care Visits.”

Internal factors include core elements of primary healthcare that influence the main framework, further categorized into three groups: (1) Factors affecting “Primary Healthcare Service Quality”, such as “Primary Medical Technology Level,” “Medical Staff Competence,” “Service Attitude,” “Telemedicine,” and “Medical Facility Conditions.” (2) Factors influencing “Willingness to Seek Care at Primary Facilities”, such as “Patients’ Economic Status,” “Health Conditions,” “Satisfaction with Primary Care,” and “Trust in Primary Healthcare Institutions.” (3) Factors related to “Number of Primary Care Visits”, such as “Healthcare Institution Revenue,” “Workload,” and “Healthcare Workers’ Salaries.”

For example, improving “Service Attitude” can enhance “Service Quality”, thereby increasing “Willingness to Seek Care” and subsequently raising the “Number of Visits.” At the same time, an increase in “Number of Visits” may lead to a higher “Workload,” potentially reducing “Healthcare Workers’ Motivation,” which might negatively impact “Service Attitude.” External factors primarily include policy-related elements that regulate the main framework and internal factors. These involve “Healthcare System Development,” “Family Doctor Contracting,” “Health Insurance Reimbursement Levels,” “Primary Care Collaboration Teams,” and “Financial Investment.” For instance, an increase in “Number of Visits” might drive enhanced policy support and financial investment, thereby improving the implementation of supportive policies such as higher health insurance reimbursement levels.

Qualitative interview optimization of the causal loop diagram

In this study, participants in the interviews anonymously completed an interview effectiveness evaluation questionnaire (see Supplementary Material Appendix 2). A total of 12 questionnaires were distributed, with 10 completed and returned.

In the evaluation of overall interview experiences, 8 participants (80%) strongly agreed that the interview process was enjoyable, 9 participants (90%) strongly agreed that they felt highly engaged during the interviews, and 7 participants (70%) agreed that they were satisfied with the perspectives presented. All participants strongly agreed that they gained new knowledge from the interviews.

Regarding the achievement of interview objectives, all participants agreed that the perspectives raised during the interviews were suitable for discussion, and 7 participants (70%) recognized the existence of diverse perspectives.

In terms of negative factors, 7 participants (70%) strongly agreed that the interview objectives were clear, and all participants agreed that their expectations during the interviews were well-defined. Participants widely acknowledged that the interviews achieved their goals, their views were fully heard, and their participation felt free and open. The interviews were perceived as smooth and cooperative, with no participants feeling pressured, and all were confident that no important points were overlooked.

The updated CLD retained its original structure, including the main framework and two subsystems: internal environment and external environment, distinguished by red, brown, and blue, respectively.

In the internal environment, core elements remained largely unchanged, covering key variables influencing “Primary Healthcare Service Quality” (e.g., primary medical technology, healthcare staff competence, service attitude, telemedicine, and infrastructure conditions), variables affecting “Willingness to Seek Care at Primary Facilities” (e.g., patients’ economic and health status, satisfaction with care, and trust), and factors influenced by “Number of Primary Care Visits” (e.g., healthcare institution revenue, workload, and healthcare staff salaries). For example, improving service attitudes enhances healthcare quality, which in turn boosts willingness to seek care, ultimately increasing the number of visits. However, as visit numbers rise, increased workload may reduce staff motivation, potentially impacting service attitudes.

In the external environment, core elements primarily influenced the main framework and internal environment through regulatory effects, including healthcare system development, family doctor contracting, health insurance reimbursement levels, primary care collaboration teams, and financial support. For instance, an increase in visit numbers may strengthen policy support, leading to increased financial investment in primary care, thereby raising total insurance payouts and ultimately improving reimbursement levels.

MICMAC analysis

Direct influence matrix of causal elements

The optimized CLD, comprising 44 factors, underwent analysis to identify barriers and facilitators for implementing primary first-contact policies. Given that excessive variables in MICMAC structural analysis can impose a significant burden on matrix construction, this study filtered the variables based on insights from the CLD and analysis of barriers and facilitators. A total of 28 factors were selected (see Supplementary Table 8) to construct the Direct Influence Matrix (results in Supplementary Fig. 8). As MICMAC software does not support Chinese and has a character length limitation for English, the 28 factors were translated from Chinese to English (see Supplementary Table 9).

Ranking of driving and dependence strengths

Using the “Micmac Analyse Structure” online tool, the driving and dependence strengths of variables were calculated. The results indicated that top-ranked drivers included “Health Insurance Awareness (InsAware),” “Medical Consortium Construction (HCCBuild),” and “Hospital Tiered Referral Guidance (HospGuide),” highlighting their importance as potential policy intervention points. In contrast, “Number of Primary Care Visits (PCNum)” and “Willingness to Seek Care at Primary Facilities (PCWilling)” exhibited high dependence, representing key system output variables. Detailed rankings of driving and dependence strengths are presented in Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10.

Driving-dependence map

The driving-dependence map generated by MICMAC (Fig. 4) visualizes the driving and dependence strengths of the 28 variables. In the map, the size of the points represents the intensity of influence, the horizontal axis indicates dependence, and the vertical axis indicates driving strength, categorizing variables into four quadrants: (1) Relay Variables (First Quadrant): Variables such as “Health Insurance Awareness,” “Medical Consortium Construction,” and “Hospital Tiered Referral Guidance” exhibit high driving and dependence strengths. (2) Determinant Variables (Second Quadrant): These have high driving strength but low dependence. No such variables were identified in this study. (3) Autonomous Variables (Third Quadrant): These have low driving and dependence strengths, such as “Restrictions on Hospital Selection for Referrals” and “Lack of Referral Standards.” (4) Dependent Variables (Fourth Quadrant): These are system outputs highly influenced by other variables, including “Number of Primary Care Visits” and “Willingness to Seek Care at Primary Facilities.”.

Discussion

Using a multi‑method design, we treated the CLD not merely as a descriptive map but as an analytical lens to interpret how first‑contact reforms can improve equity and efficiency in mixed public–private health‑care settings. The model was successively strengthened—via qualitative stakeholder insights, barrier/facilitator analysis and MICMAC mapping—to ensure that causality, rather than simple association, underpinned the key feedback structures. In addition to drawing upon China’s healthcare reforms, the analysis also resonates with international experiences where primary healthcare stands as the gateway for integrated care, such as in Canada’s and Australia’s GP-oriented systems [29, 30]. The synergy between local policy settings and recognized global frameworks—like the WHO Building Blocks and the CFIR—emphasizes the universal relevance of reinforcing trust, quality, and financial accessibility for effective first-contact care. After identifying a range of possible influencing factors, the CLD was iteratively refined through qualitative interviews, barrier and facilitator analysis, and MICMAC structural analysis to ensure the validity of the causal relationships and the accurate identification of key variables. The final core CLD revealed that primary healthcare service quality, willingness to seek care, and number of visits are key indicators for evaluating the effectiveness of first-contact policies. Within this system, health insurance awareness, medical consortium construction, and the guiding strength of tiered medical care policies emerged as significant drivers. Specifically, improved health insurance awareness not only enhanced residents’ trust in primary healthcare institutions but also strengthened their first-contact choices by improving economic accessibility. Medical consortium construction acted as a critical “relay” mechanism through resource sharing and support for bidirectional referrals, boosting the service capacity and credibility of primary institutions. Tiered medical care policies further reinforced the positive effects of health insurance awareness and medical consortium construction through resource allocation and promotional mechanisms. These multi-layered feedback pathways collectively provided systemic support and empirical evidence for optimizing first-contact systems and tiered medical care policies.

This study, using CLD, highlighted the core driving roles of health insurance awareness, medical consortium construction, and tiered medical care policies in improving the effectiveness of primary first-contact care, adding a dynamic, multi-factor perspective to the existing literature. Previous studies often focused on the direct impact of individual variables, rarely considering multi-level feedback within the system. For example, while existing research has shown the positive role of health insurance policies in enhancing residents’ willingness to seek care at primary institutions, they typically regarded it as a unidirectional process [16, 17].

This study’s CLD demonstrated that health insurance awareness not only directly increases trust in primary institutions but also indirectly strengthens first-contact choices by reducing economic burden, forming a dual positive feedback loop of “trust-economic burden-choice.” This mechanism expands the understanding of health insurance awareness in the existing literature [31, 32]. Furthermore, the “relay” role of medical consortium construction has been underexplored in prior research, which primarily emphasized its role in resource allocation [14]. This study showed that medical consortiums not only improve service quality through resource sharing but also enhance trust through increased referral efficiency, significantly improving primary first-contact outcomes. The positive feedback loop of “resources-referrals-trust” within medical consortiums is a critical factor encouraging residents to choose primary care, providing a richer explanatory framework for existing studies [33].

Qualitative interviews also revealed details such as referral standards and resource allocation, supplementing the understanding of policy barriers in the existing literature. For example, previous studies have noted that the lack of clear referral standards hinders referral efficiency, but these factors were classified as autonomous variables with relatively limited impact in the MICMAC analysis [34, 35]. MICMAC analysis further identified health insurance awareness, medical consortium construction, and the guiding strength of tiered medical care policies as the main drivers of first-contact effectiveness. Notably, the guiding strength of tiered medical care policies, rarely mentioned in the literature, was found in this study’s interviews to significantly influence the attractiveness of primary institutions and doctors’ salary incentives [36–38], Therefore, policies should enhance salary incentives while strengthening the positive effects of medical consortiums and health insurance policies to achieve rational resource allocation and utilization.

This study identified three key pathways for optimizing primary first-contact policies from a complex systems perspective: improving residents’ health insurance awareness, strengthening the “relay” role of medical consortiums in resource sharing and referral support, and establishing effective promotional mechanisms for tiered medical care. First, enhancing health insurance awareness. This not only augments patients’ trust in primary care but also alleviates economic pressures, thereby creating a dual positive feedback loop of ‘trust–accessibility.’ Policy measures could include targeted insurance education campaigns, particularly in underdeveloped areas, to ensure that residents understand both their coverage options and the cost advantages of seeking initial care at the primary level. Second, Strengthening Medical Consortiums as “Relay” Hubs. By simplifying bidirectional referral processes and facilitating resource-sharing between primary and higher-level institutions, consortiums can substantially enhance frontline service capacity and patient confidence. Policymakers might implement standardized referral protocols and shared electronic health record systems to further streamline communication and reduce duplication of diagnostic tests. Finally, Promoting Tiered Medical Care Mechanisms., Tiered medical care policies can form a reinforcing feedback loop of policy incentives, resource allocation, and patient choices. Examples include offering preferential reimbursement rates for primary-level visits or incentivizing high-level hospitals to transfer stable patients back to community clinics for follow-up, ensuring more effective utilization of overall health resources.

These pathways provided specific implications for policy and practice, Firstly, stakeholders at national, provincial, and local levels should collaborate to harmonize regulations, ensuring that financial incentives, referral policies, and human resource initiatives converge to strengthen primary care as the first point of contact. Secondly, encouraging general practitioners to develop specialized skills (e.g., chronic disease management) could further boost patient trust. Medical consortiums should provide regular staff exchanges and training to elevate the clinical competencies of primary healthcare workers. Thirdly, To address the persistent distrust in primary care settings, more robust community outreach—through health education drives, mobile clinics, or nurse-led home visits—would help familiarize residents with primary care services and highlight the potential cost savings and convenience.

In summary, the empirical findings of this study provide theoretical support and practical guidance for policymakers in optimizing primary first-contact systems. These pathways underscore the need for a multifaceted approach that integrates policy-level reforms with ground-level capacity-building. Future policy reforms should focus on coordinated interventions among insurers, healthcare institutions, and local governments to reinforce dynamic feedback loops promoting first-contact care. By doing so, the tiered medical care system can better realize its long-term goals of efficiency, equity, and sustainability.

Innovation and limitations

This study introduced several methodological innovations. First, the integration of the WHO Building Blocks framework with the CFIR to construct the CLD represents a major innovation. The WHO Building Blocks framework has been widely recognized in healthcare system research, such as in multi-sectoral health promotion studies in Australia, where it was used to construct causal pathways, demonstrating its broad applicability and effectiveness [39–41]. By employing this interdisciplinary approach, the study not only provides a comprehensive framework for analyzing the complex causal relationships in the primary first-contact system but also enhances the systemic understanding of policy implementation barriers and facilitators, supporting more effective policy optimization and implementation. Second, the innovative combination of qualitative interviews with MICMAC structural analysis enabled a multi-dimensional analysis of causal relationships. Originally derived from engineering, MICMAC has shown great potential in complex systems but is rarely applied in healthcare system research [42]. In this study, its application not only strengthened the empirical foundation of the model, making the findings more robust but also quantified the driving and dependence strengths of key factors, providing a quantitative basis for healthcare policy decision-making. By analyzing key factors such as health insurance awareness, medical consortium construction, and tiered medical care policies, this study directly supports health policy decision-making and proposes actionable recommendations, particularly emphasizing the importance of enhancing health insurance promotion and optimizing resource allocation in underdeveloped areas.

Despite using the WHO framework and CFIR to provide solid theoretical support, the construction of the CLD relied on existing literature and stakeholder judgments, which may not fully capture the true system dynamics. Additionally, as the study primarily relied on qualitative data, it may have introduced subjectivity and bias. The study did not extensively use quantitative data for empirical validation of the model, which could limit the generalizability and accuracy of the findings. Moreover, although MICMAC scores each variable’s influence and dependence, it does not quantify the magnitude or statistical significance of causal effects, limiting the method’s predictive precision. Further, while MICMAC can reveal the relative positioning (drivers vs. dependent variables), it cannot capture all real-world complexities such as time delays and non-linear feedback loops. Finally, because our search did not include Google Scholar or dedicated grey‑literature sources, unpublished programme reports might have been missed; however, the very high yield from four major databases suggests that key peer‑reviewed evidence was captured.

Future research should adopt advanced statistical methods such as structural equation modeling or system dynamics modeling to validate and refine the relationships in the CLD and further quantify interactions among key factors. Additionally, the model should be tested in broader regions and different healthcare system contexts to enhance its generalizability and applicability.

Conclusions

This study combined a systematic literature review, qualitative stakeholder interviews and MICMAC structural analysis to build an empirically grounded CLD of China’s first‑contact primary‑care system. The diagram highlights three high‑leverage drivers—health‑insurance awareness, medical‑consortium construction and tiered‑referral guidance—that reinforce service quality, patient trust and utilisation of primary facilities through multiple positive feedback loops.

Policy makers should therefore:

(i)intensify health‑insurance promotion to strengthen economic accessibility and trust;(ii)institutionalise resource‑sharing within medical consortia to facilitate bi‑directional referral and capacity‑building; and(iii)harmonise financial and performance incentives with tiered‑referral policies.
