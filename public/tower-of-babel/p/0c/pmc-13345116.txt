Investigating Image Distortion and Its Impact on Material Quantification in Photon-counting Computed Tomography
Authors: Rayhan Uddin, Marzieh Anjomrouz, Neryda Duncan, Anthony Butler, Philip Butler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Photon-counting computed tomography (PCCT) enables simultaneous spatial and spectral imaging for improved material discrimination. However, inaccuracies in scanner geometric calibration can introduce reconstruction distortions that degrade both image quality and spectral fidelity, thereby affecting quantitative material analysis.Aims and Objectives:This study investigated the impact of scanner-induced geometric distortions on material quantification in the Medipix All Resolution System scanner and examined the relationship between spatial image degradation and spectral signal integrity in photon-counting CT.Materials and Methods:Multiple phantoms were scanned using a geometrically calibrated system. Geometric reconstruction parameters were then systematically perturbed to generate intentionally distorted reconstructions and create a spectral image library for visual assessment. Distortion patterns, including edge blurring, shading artifacts, and ring artifacts, were analyzed alongside changes in spectral signatures and material reconstruction performance. A comprehensive geometric calibration framework was subsequently developed to mitigate spatial distortions and improve spectral consistency.Results:Geometric perturbations produced significant spatial distortions accompanied by measurable degradation in spectral response. Distorted reconstructions showed reduced material discrimination accuracy and altered spectral signatures. The proposed calibration approach effectively reduced reconstruction artifacts and improved both spatial image quality and spectral reliability.Conclusion:Spatial and spectral degradations in photon-counting CT are intrinsically interconnected. Accurate geometric calibration is therefore essential for reliable material quantification and optimal spectral reconstruction performance in photon-counting CT systems.

INTRODUCTION

Image artifacts are a common issue in medical imaging that lead to severe problems in diagnostic imaging.[12] An artifact is distortion in an image that results in errors in reconstructed images. This image distortion can be produced from different sources such as geometric misalignment, inconsistency in measurements, uncalibrated detector elements during the reconstruction process, and any malfunction of the scanner components.[3] There are different types of artifacts that produce image distortion in the reconstructed computed tomography (CT) images. Ring artifacts are caused by malfunctions or calibration errors in the detector.[4] Object motion results in shading and streak artifacts due to the misregistration of the object between projections. Cupping, shading, and streak artifacts can also be caused by beam hardening. Scanner-based artifacts result from imperfections in CT scanner function such as the geometric misalignment of different scanner components.[5678]

In an ideal cone-beam CT geometry, the X-ray source, the center of rotation, and the center of the detector should be aligned along a single straight line. In addition, the central ray should be perpendicular to the detector surface at its center. Any misalignment in this geometry can lead to image distortion in the reconstructed CT images. If there is any misalignment in the system, the CT image will suffer from image distortion.[91011]

In contrast, in the photon-counting spectral CT scanner, the X-ray beam that passes through the object is measured in multiple views by multiple pixelated detector elements. Therefore, a small misalignment between the X-ray beam, objects, and the camera can cause an error in the reconstruction software that produces severe image distortion such as edge blurring and shading. Such image distortion can degrade the capability of spectral CT in material identification and quantification, resulting in the obscure diagnosis of disease.[12]

The Medipix All Resolution System (MARS) is a quantitative color X-ray imaging modality that uses a multichip Medipix3RX photon-counting detector which enables simultaneous identification and quantification of different materials. The key features of the Medipix3RX detector are to measure and count the energy of the individual X-ray photon at multiple energy bins over the entire X-ray spectrum (20–120 keV) with a spatial resolution of 110 μm.[13]

The current small-bore MARS scanner has contributed to preclinical areas such as monitoring the biomarker for cancer imaging, identifying unstable atherosclerosis plaque and its treatment, characterizing cartilage to identify osteoarthritis, and applications in the meat industry.[14]

We identified three types of image errors in photon-counting spectral CT scanners: spatial image errors, spectral image errors, and spectral image contamination. The main source of spatial image error is produced from inaccurate geometric parameters. The source of spectral image error is pulse pileup, charge sharing, inaccurate energy response, incomplete charge collection, and threshold dispersion in spectral CT. Spectral image contamination occurs when information in the energy domain is distorted by errors in the spatial information. This includes effect such as charge steering in the detector, where incident photon interaction is measured in different positions to the actual interaction position.

In this study, we investigate the sources of spatial image errors in photon-counting CT and their impact on material quantification. To reduce spatial image errors, a comprehensive geometric calibration method was developed to estimate various geometric parameters from projection images acquired using a line phantom.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The MARS scanner was used to characterize spatial image distortion using multiple phantoms. The system is equipped with a 2-mm-thick CdZnTe sensor bump-bonded to a Medipix3RX ASIC with a pixel pitch of 110 µm. A polychromatic X-ray spectrum ranging from 18 keV to 120 keV was employed for all scans. Data acquisitions were performed in spectroscopic configuration using charge summing mode. Four energy bins were defined as 18–30 keV, 30–45 keV, 45–75 keV, and 75–120 keV to enable identification of K-edge materials. An external bias voltage of − 600 V was applied across the sensor. For all measurements, the lower energy threshold was set just above the noise floor so that all photons exceeding this threshold were counted. To conduct this study, we produced distorted images by perturbing geometric offset parameters to observe the spatial error in the reconstructed images. The range of geometric perturbations applied in this study was 0 to ± 1 mm, with no repeated scans performed within this range. The perturbations were implemented in 0.2 mm increments, resulting in a total of 11 discrete positions (0, ±0.2 mm, ±0.4 mm, ±0.6 mm, ±0.8 mm, and ± 1 mm) for evaluation. This step size was chosen to systematically assess the effect of small geometric deviations on the reconstructed image quality and the corresponding signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) measurements.

Geometric calibration

Geometric calibration is essential for minimizing image distortion and ensuring high image quality.[1516171819] A systematic and accurate calibration procedure reduces imaging artifacts and consequently enhances the capability of the MARS scanner for reliable material identification and quantification. To address distortion in photon-counting CT scanners, a geometric calibration method was developed to measure offsets in key geometric parameters and incorporate these corrections during image reconstruction, thereby improving spatial fidelity and spectral accuracy.

The novel contribution of this geometric calibration method over existing approaches in photon-counting CT lies in its integrated correction of multiple geometric misalignment parameters that simultaneously influence spatial accuracy and spectral fidelity. Unlike conventional techniques that focus mainly on basic alignment or rely on simplified assumptions, the proposed method quantitatively models and compensates for tangential offsets and associated distortions across energy bins. This enables preservation of voxel-level spectral information, improves material decomposition (MD), and enhances CNR and edge definition. Consequently, it establishes a more robust and application-ready calibration framework for high-precision spectral CT imaging. A calibration phantom was designed to calculate geometric offset parameters from projection images. The calibration phantom is a plastic circuit board (PCB), 1.5 mm × 89.8 mm × 120 mm in size, with copper tracks of varying diameters arranged in vertical, horizontal, and diagonal patterns. The track centers are 500 μm apart, as shown in Figure 3. The vertical and horizontal arrays of copper tracks were used to calculate different geometric offsets. Calculation of geometric offsets

To measure the geometrical parameters using the line phantom, several steps are followed as: (1) measurement of the angular offset between the gantry and line phantom; (2) measurement of sample-x offset; (3) measurement of radial offset of the X-ray tube and detector; and (4) measurement of camera/X-ray tube axial and tangential offsets, chip X and chip Y.

Step 1: Measurement of the angular offset between gantry and line phantom

The phantom surface must be aligned completely parallel to the detector, meaning it is perpendicular to the incident X-ray beam. To achieve this, the line phantom is mounted on the sample holder, and the gantry is rotated according to the following procedure. The gantry is first rotated by 90°, and then, its angle is adjusted by small increments while observing the shadow of the phantom edge in the projection image. At a specific gantry angle, the shadow of the line phantom exhibits minimum thickness; this occurs when the gantry is perpendicular to the phantom surface, as shown in Figure 5. This angle represents the relative offset between the current sample orientation and the gantry and is denoted as θp.

A projection image showing the top view of the line phantom. The shadow of the line phantom exhibits minimum thickness when the gantry is perpendicular to the phantom surface

Step 2: Measurement of sample-x offset

The sample-x parameter in the Medipix All Resolution System needs to be adjusted through the graphical user interface (GUI) to ensure that the sample (line phantom) is positioned at the center of the rotational axis. To achieve this, the gantry is rotated once to θp + 90° and once to θp − 90°. Ideally, the projection image of the copper edge acquired at +90° gantry rotation should overlap with the shadow of the same copper edge acquired at −90°, as illustrated in the right panel of Figure 6. However, if the sample-x position is not properly adjusted, the copper edge images at +90° and −90° will appear separated. The distance between these two edges in the projection images must be measured and used to correct the sample-x offset. The corrected alignment is demonstrated in Figure 7.

Uncertainty in geometric calibration

The accuracy of geometric calibration of the MARS scanner depends on uncertainties in its geometric parameters. Therefore, other sources of variation should be minimized so that only geometric variations are considered. To investigate parameter uncertainty, potential sources of variation related to system geometry must be identified. These variations may arise from the X-ray source, the detector system, or the scanned object. As discussed in the previous section, the phantom contains a specialized pattern consisting of copper tracks with different diameters. Due to manufacturing tolerances of this precision phantom, variations in detected counts may occur at different pixel indices. To evaluate the physical uncertainty of the copper tracks, quantum noise was measured at different SOD values while maintaining constant exposure time. Quantum noise is influenced primarily by the number of frames and counts per pixel. Measurements showed that the maximum SOD produced higher quantum noise than the minimum SOD. Projection image analysis indicated typical quantum noise levels of approximately 2000 counts per pixel for a single exposure. A series of 1000 projection images acquired with and without the PCB yielded total counts of about 2,000,000 and a corresponding quantum noise of approximately 0.07%. The attenuation difference between track and nontrack regions was observed to be about ±5%.

Regarding magnification, the middle detector chip exhibited a lower standard deviation compared with the top and bottom chips. Sources of uncertainty in magnification include variations in maximum and minimum counts between PCB substrate and copper tracks, inter-pixel response variation, chip-to-chip differences (Y-direction), and detector chip gaps.

DISCUSSION

These results demonstrate that different types of image distortion can influence material quantification. The study further shows how a novel geometric calibration method can reduce image distortion and thereby improve the material quantification capability of photon-counting CT. Systematic experiments were conducted to evaluate the effects of geometric misalignment on reconstructed images and variations in HU values. The impact of geometric misalignment was investigated by perturbing different geometric offset parameters to assess spatial image errors. Initially, parameters such as CTO were used to demonstrate the concept.

To generate a spectral image library, a multi-contrast phantom was scanned using both calibrated and uncalibrated system configurations, producing paired normal and distorted images for each perturbation level. Camera axial offset and CTO were varied systematically to examine how spectral signals from different materials degrade under geometric perturbations. The SNR decreased in the uncalibrated system compared with the calibrated system. Edge blurring was observed as a result of inaccurate CTO and was quantified by plotting line profiles across the central vial of the phantom.

The CNR of each material decreased in the distorted images due to spatial errors, whereas higher CNR values were observed in the corresponding normal images, highlighting the impact of geometric perturbations on image quality. Image differencing and subtraction techniques were applied to highlight variations between normal and distorted images. The linear attenuation response within each voxel of an ROI was analyzed and found to be skewed in distorted images, while remaining symmetric in normal images.

It was also observed that spatial errors degrade energy information by spreading the spectral content of one voxel into neighboring voxels. A key finding of this investigation is the relationship between geometric accuracy and energy-domain information, and its implications for material reconstruction. An important concept explored in this study is that the energy-domain information in spectral CT images is intrinsically linked to the spatial domain. Consequently, spatial artifacts in reconstructed images inevitably produce corresponding distortions in the energy information. Spatial information may be interpreted as the total linear attenuation within a given voxel, which is the primary information provided by conventional CT scanners. The effect of spatial distortion on material characterization is clearly demonstrated in Figure 18, where the material information is shown to be almost completely degraded due to geometric error.

Circular ring artifacts are produced due to gaps between chips, faulty chips or pixels, and miscalibration of detector elements. Edge blurring was reduced by correcting the CTO. Ring artifacts were also minimized by setting the arbitration counter above the noise floor, applying proper pixel masking, and performing energy calibration. Seven geometrical parameters including the angular offset between the gantry and line phantom, sample-x offset, radial offset of the X-ray tube and detector, camera, source axial offset, tangential offsets, as well as chip-x and chip-y were estimated using the line phantom. The vertical positions of the middle and bottom chips were found to be offset by 113 μm and 344 μm, respectively. The tangential and axial offsets of all three chips need to be adjusted by 1.085 mm and 2.86 mm, respectively. The geometric calibration greatly enhances the accuracy and reliability of material measurements in a photon-counting CT scanner.

For future work, spectral image quality can be investigated by scanning larger objects as part of the continued development of photon-counting CT scanners. It is necessary to examine how the size of reconstructed data relates to coverage length, memory storage, voxel size, pitch, scan time, and gantry rotation speed. Additionally, the effects of detector instability, temperature variation, inter-pixel variation, and threshold drifting on reconstructed images should be studied.

CONCLUSION

This study investigates the sources of spatial distortion in reconstructed images that affect material quantification in photon-counting spectral CT. By analyzing errors associated with scanner parameters, it develops a library of artifact-affected spectral CT images to better understand how different factors influence image quality. The results show that geometric calibration reduces RMSE across all tested materials including lipid, water, gold, gadolinium, iodine, and calcium, thereby improving quantitative accuracy and enhancing the reliability of photon-counting CT-based MD over a wide range of concentrations.
