Genome and transcriptome sequencing reveal pathogenic activation of a pseudoexon in PKD1 via a de novo–common variant complex allele
Authors: Sangwoo T. Han, Atteeq U. Rehman, Sowmya Thirumalai Srinivasa, Alicia Lam, Michael Ficurilli, Vanessa Felice, Priya Krithivasan, Shruti Phadke, Avinash Abhyankar, Katherine Bergstrom, Jennifer A. Bassetti, Vaidehi Jobanputra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeDespite improvements in and access to clinical genetic testing, many patients with classic phenotypes remain without a molecular diagnosis. Such cases may reflect variants in noncoding regions or complex alleles made up of disparate sequence variants. In this article, we report a patient with autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease caused by a deep intronic de novo single-nucleotide substitution forming a dinucleotide variant, with an adjacent common single-nucleotide variant (formerly single-nucleotide polymorphism) inherited from an unaffected parent. Although neither variant alone was predicted to be pathogenic, together as a dinucleotide variant, they created a novel splice donor site that activated a pseudoexon within intron 16 of the PKD1 gene.MethodsGenome sequencing was paired with transcriptome sequencing to evaluate the dinucleotide variant within intron 16 of PKD1.ResultsEvaluation of RNA from patient cells confirmed the inclusion of a 114-bp pseudoexon and an in-frame premature termination codon into the PKD1 messenger RNA transcript. This case identified a rare scenario in which the combination of 2 independently predicted benign nucleotide substitutions immediately adjacent to one another generated a pathogenic allele.ConclusionThis case highlights the diagnostic utility of genome sequencing coupled with RNA sequencing to clarify the effects of a deep intronic variant, confirming a molecular diagnosis for a patient with a clear clinical diagnosis who had undergone years of inconclusive testing. Furthermore, this case cautions that similar events may underlie unsolved cases across other genes and disorders.

Introduction

Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) (MIM 173900) is the most common inherited kidney disorder worldwide.1 ADPKD is an adult-onset disorder characterized by the development of bilateral renal cysts and hepatic cysts and an increased risk of intracranial aneurysms.2 Most cases of ADPKD are caused by pathogenic variants in the PKD1 (HGNC:9008) and PKD2 (HGNC:9009) genes, which collectively account for >90% of ADPKD cases, with PKD1 accounting for the majority of those cases.3 Historically, diagnostic testing for ADPKD began with targeted testing of these 2 genes, although larger panels have become preferred in recent years. Despite sequencing and copy number analysis of all ADPKD-associated genes, approximately 5% of patients lack a molecular diagnosis.3 Although there may be additional APDKD genes yet to be discovered, some portions of the undiagnosed 5% likely harbor variants in regions that are not routinely evaluated.

Sequencing of the PKD1 locus (NM_001009944.3) presents technical challenges because of the presence of highly homologous sequences across chromosome 16. These sequences are the products of segmental duplications of exons 1 to 32 of PKD1 and have approximately 98% homology to the PKD1 gene,4 raising the possibility of missing or miscalling pathogenic variants in PKD1. This necessitates developing diagnostic assays that are highly specific to the true PKD1 gene,5 which increases the cost of PKD1 sequencing. Here, we report a case of ADPKD solved through genome sequencing (GS) paired with RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) after multiple rounds of genetic testing, all of which returned negative results. The pathogenic variant identified through GS was a de novo deep intronic substitution in PKD1 located immediately adjacent to an inherited sequence variant that is common in the general population, forming a NM_001009944.3:c.7066-131_7066-130delinsAG variant. The de novo substitution itself is not predicted to affect PKD1 splicing; however, when paired with the adjacent single-nucleotide variant (SNV) as a dinucleotide variant, it activated a pseudoexon. This study highlights the utility of interrogating the introns of all genes associated with ADPKD and suggests that GS should be considered for cases with a clear clinical diagnosis.

Materials and Methods

DNA was extracted from whole blood from the proband and his parents. Trio clinical GS was performed on these samples using Illumina NovaSeq 6000. Data analysis was performed using a proprietary analytical pipeline and aligned to the GRCh38.p14 reference genome. Total RNA was extracted from skin fibroblasts. RNA libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA kit (Illumina) and sequenced to a target depth of 100 million reads on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000. All sequencing was performed using 150-bp paired-end reads.

Results

The proband was initially referred to genetics for evaluation of bilateral renal cysts identified on abdominal magnetic resonance imaging performed at the age of 26 years. The patient’s medical history was notable for HFE-associated hemochromatosis and hypertension. There was no reported family history of kidney disease in his extended family (Figure 1A). Initial imaging revealed bilateral renal cysts without hepatic cysts, raising suspicion for an inherited cystic kidney disorder. He has been followed by Nephrology for chronic kidney disease, Mayo Class 1C. Echocardiogram showed no aortic dilation. Genetic evaluation began with targeted testing of the coding exons of PKD1 and PKD2 by Sanger sequencing and multiplex ligation–dependent probe amplification analysis, which returned a negative result. Given the ongoing concern for hereditary renal disease, further testing using next-generation sequencing–based sequencing panels was pursued 5 years later. A 6-gene polycystic kidney disease panel was sent and revealed a variant of uncertain significance (VUS) in PKHD1, but this was judged not to explain the full phenotype because PKHD1 is associated with autosomal recessive disease or very mild disease in some monoallelic cases.6 Subsequent testing by a 385-gene kidney disease panel revealed VUS in 3 additional genes (including CREBBP, NPHP3, and RPGRIP1L), none of which were thought to be diagnostic. Separate testing for DNAJB11 was also sent at this time and had a negative result (Figure 1B). Ultimately, trio GS identified a de novo deep intronic C>T substitution, NM_001009944.3:c.7066-131G>A, within intron 16 of PKD1 (Figure 1C). Although the presence of high homology between PKD1 and several pseudogene regions on chromosome 16 complicates sequence analysis of this region, this variant was confirmed by Sanger sequencing using PKD1-specific polymerase chain reaction and sequencing primers, which differentiated the functional PKD1 gene from highly similar pseudogene sequences as well as from the PKD2 gene (Supplemental Figure 1). Additional findings that were unrelated to the patient’s presenting phenotype of ADPKD were also identified through GS. The patient was homozygous for the common NM_000410.3:c.845G>A p.(Cys282Tyr) pathogenic variant in HFE (HGNC:4886), consistent with his known diagnosis of HFE-related hereditary hemochromatosis, and this was reported as an American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) secondary finding. In addition, an secondary finding of a heterozygous pathogenic variant NM_152594.3:c.305C>A p.(Thr102Lys) was also identified in SPRED1 (HGNC:20249). Pathogenic variants in SPRED1 are associated with Legius syndrome (MIM 611431), a rare autosomal dominant condition primarily characterized by multiple café-au-lait macules without neurofibromas or tumor predisposition. On follow-up reverse phenotyping, the patient reported having several café-au-lait macules, which was consistent with this diagnosis.

The de novo NM_001009944.3:c.7066-131G>A variant in PKD1 is absent from the gnomAD v4.1.0 (https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/) and All of Us (https://databrowser.researchallofus.org/) population databases, as well as Human Gene Mutation Database (https://hgmd.cf.ac.uk) and the ADPKD Variant Database (https://pkdb.mayo.edu/). This variant has weak in silico scores by spliceAI (https://spliceailookup.broadinstitute.org/), with a 0.12 acceptor gain score and a 0.06 donor gain score, and is classified as a VUS following the ACMG/Association for Molecular Pathology variant interpretation guidelines7 in combination with recommendations from the ClinGen Sequence Variant Interpretation Splicing Subgroup.8 This variant is immediately adjacent to a paternally inherited T>C sequence variant (rs3874658; NC_000016.10:g.2107078T>C) and forms the NM_001009944.3:c.7066-131_7066-130delinsAG dinucleotide variant. This variant has been submitted once in ClinVar as a VUS (ClinVar ID: 4072221), raising the possibility that this might be a recurrent variant. When evaluated as a dinucleotide variant, SpliceAI predicted activation of a pseudoexon with a donor gain score of 0.78 at the site of the variant and an acceptor gain score of 0.41 located 114 bp upstream (Figure 2A). SpliceAI predicted that both the common SNV and the de novo variant individually would be insufficient to cause aberrant splicing, but when combined as a dinucleotide variant, these could activate a 114-bp pseudoexon (Figure 2B). We evaluated RNA from skin fibroblasts of the proband and his unaffected father who carried the common SNV. Although ADPKD does not present with skin findings, PKD1 (NM_001009944.3) is expressed in skin fibroblasts at levels sufficient for evaluation by RNA-seq. Additionally, we found no evidence that PKD1 pseudogene sequences were expressed in RNA because sequence reads were clean and did not contain mismatches at known pseudogene points of differentiation. We observed approximately 50× coverage of exons 16 and 17 by RNA-seq in the proband and father. The presence of the predicted pseudoexon was confirmed in the proband at a depth of approximately 22× and was absent in the father’s RNA. Splice junctions were observed connecting the pseudoexon to exons 16 and 17 (Figure 2C). Importantly, all sequence reads that spanned the novel donor site contained the dinucleotide variant, demonstrating that the variant was required for the activation of this pseudoexon (Figure 2D). The pseudoexon sequence carried a UGA stop codon in the native reading frame of PKD1, which is predicted to cause loss of function of that allele. These RNA data were sufficient to upgrade the variant classification to pathogenic by ACMG/Association for Molecular Pathology standards with the application of PVS1 Pathogenic Very Strong criteria9 (Supplemental Table 1). Based on the clinical and molecular findings, a diagnosis of ADPKD because of a pathogenic variant in PKD1 was established.

Discussion

This report highlights the utility of genome and RNA-seq and the importance of evaluating adjacent variants as complex alleles. Despite knowing that PKD1 and PKD2 account for most ADPKD cases, conventional testing often stops short of full gene sequencing. Approximately 75% of ADPKD cases are caused by pathogenic variants in PKD1,10 whereas approximately 5% of ADPKD cases are unsolved following sequencing of the coding regions of all known genes associated with ADPKD.11 The likely explanations for those cases include technical errors because of PKD1 pseudogene homology or technical limitations for identifying structural variants, noncoding variants, or undiscovered disease genes. Each of these limitations can be addressed by GS, and so, when feasible, GS should be considered as a first-tier test even when the clinical diagnosis is clear.

The de novo substitution created an AG dinucleotide adjacent to a GT dinucleotide, which together form a splice donor site that was confirmed by RNA-seq. Confirmation of this finding suggests that the additional sequence elements required for splicing (eg, splice acceptor, branch site, splicing enhancer) are already present at this locus, a finding that has been observed across variants associated with aberrant splicing.12 This pseudoexon was not present in the paternal RNA despite carrying all the same sequences as the proband, suggesting that the de novo substitution was required to activate splicing of this pseudoexon. Interestingly, significant sequence reads corresponding to the pseudoexon were observed despite the presence of an in-frame UGA stop codon. Incorporation of a termination codon into the PKD1 transcript would result in either nonsense-mediated mRNA decay or protein truncation and is expected to cause a loss of function and is consistent with the phenotype of the proband. Alternatively, it is possible that the UGA stop codon could be read through13 and incorporate arginine, cysteine, or tryptophan amino acids14 at this position, in addition to the rest of the 114 bp pseudoexon being translated into protein. These excess amino acids would be inserted into the REJ domain, which is required for proper cleavage of the PKD1 protein,15 and multiple missense variants in this region of the protein have been identified in individuals with ADPKD. Although the exact consequence on protein code remains ambiguous, all likely possibilities are expected to result in disrupted PKD1 protein function.

Identification of the pathogenic variant requires full gene sequencing because it is located outside the regions evaluated by other methods. This variant is further complicated due to its location immediately adjacent to an SNV and requires evaluation of these variants as a single allele to correctly assess its pathogenicity. This scenario is diagnostically challenging because the rs3874658 SNV would be filtered out by most clinical pipelines as benign, thus leaving the de novo variant as an SNV instead of a dinucleotide variant. As an SNV, it has benign in silico predictions; however, as a dinucleotide, it is predicted to impact messenger RNA splicing. Human Genome Variation Society recommends classification of this variant as a deletion-insertion (NM_001009944.3:c.7066-131_7066-130delinsAG), because of the higher likelihood of a dinucleotide variant arising from a single substitution event rather than 2 separate events. However, the high frequency of that SNV in the general population is strongly suggestive of this variant arising by 2 separate substitution events even if the parental states are unknown. This distinction is significant for diagnostic pipelines because classifying this variant as a single deletion-insertion event would likely yield a correct in silico prediction, whereas classifying this variant as 2 separate events would not. Complex alleles, those that are pathogenic only when they carry multiple variants in cis, have been documented for some genes (eg, G6PD,16 APOL1,17 and CFTR18); however, these are well-established haplotypes that have been observed in numerous affected individuals. Predicting whether multiple variants of a haplotype have additive pathogenic properties remains a challenge for future genomic medicine. However, considering adjacent variants as a single variant is the simplest case for re-evaluation and should be treated as a single variant, as recommended by Human Genome Variation Society (https://hgvs-nomenclature.org/stable/recommendations/DNA/delins/), even if it required multiple substitution events. Although rare, ensuring that these events are properly annotated will increase the accuracy of molecular tests.

This case underscores the diagnostic utility of GS even for conditions with low genetic heterogeneity. Excluding PKD1 and PKD2, the remaining genes associated with ADPKD explain <5% of cases11; therefore, reflexing from sequencing PKD1 and PKD2 coding regions to full gene sequencing may have a similar diagnostic yield as larger panels. We propose that GS should be considered as a first-tier test for ADPKD because coding variants, noncoding variants, copy number variants, and structural variants can be evaluated in a single assay while being less affected by pseudogene sequences than capture-based (panel) sequencing.19 Although cost and reimbursement are barriers to GS uptake, the benefits of a single assay will become apparent in the future. Cases with negative GS results may require other assays such as long-read sequencing to resolve complex structural variants or gene conversion events,20 but that is also true of cases negative by current standard testing. Use of GS will increase the identification of noncoding variants with pathogenic potential, which will, in turn, necessitate increased use of RNA-seq in clinical testing.21 Incorporation of RNA-seq into diagnostics will resolve the functional consequences of many noncoding variants and improve the interpretation of such variants.22 Updating diagnostic methods with current technologies will shorten the diagnostic odysseys experienced by many patients, even those with clear clinical diagnoses.
