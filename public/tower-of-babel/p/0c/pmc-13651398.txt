Application of CT-guided microcoil localization in thoracoscopic resection of small pulmonary nodules
Authors: Duwang Qiu, Wei Jiang, Defu Chen, Lei Su, Zhenjia Li, Xiaomei Luan, Fei Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the clinical value of CT-guided microcoil localization for video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) in patients with small pulmonary nodules.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted in 100 patients with small pulmonary nodules who underwent VATS. The patients were divided into a localization group (n = 50), who underwent CT-guided microcoil localization followed by VATS resection, and a non-localization group (n = 50), who underwent direct VATS resection without preoperative localization.The localization success rate, localization time, and complication rate of the localization group were observed, and the VATS operation time, intraoperative bleeding, conversion to thoracotomy, postoperative drainage, hospital stay, and hospitalization costs were compared between the two groups.ResultsIn the localization group, the localization success rate was 98% (49/50), with minor pneumothorax occurring in 6 patients (12%, 6/50) and low-grade pulmonary hemorrhage in 8 patients (16%, 8/50). The average localization time was (15.7 ± 3.5) min. Compared with the non-localization group, the localization group showed significantly shorter operation time (P < 0.001), lower postoperative drainage volume (P < 0.001), and lower hospitalization costs (P = 0.005).ConclusionCT-guided microcoil localization is a feasible and safe technique for the surgical management of small pulmonary nodules. In this retrospective cohort, its application was associated with shorter operative times, lower postoperative drainage volumes, and reduced hospitalization costs. These findings suggest that the technique may serve as a valuable ancillary tool in facilitating precise and minimally invasive resection of small pulmonary nodules, especially those that are difficult to identify intraoperatively.

Introduction

Lung cancer, a disease with the highest incidence and mortality rates among all malignant tumors in China for an extended period, has shown a continuous upward trend in prevalence [1, 2]. With the widespread use of low-dose CT screening and artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted imaging analysis, the detection rate of suspicious pulmonary nodules has increased, and some of these nodules require surgical intervention. Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS), known for its minimal invasiveness and rapid postoperative recovery, has become the preferred treatment method [3, 4]. However, small pulmonary nodules are difficult to accurately locate through visual observation or tactile perception during VATS [5, 6]. CT-guided localization can help thoracic surgeons accurately identify pulmonary nodules intraoperatively. Currently, commonly used location methods include the implantation of metallic markers, medical glue, lipiodol, and tracer marking, among others. Micro-coils can be left in the body for an extended period without significant tissue reactions, demonstrating good patient tolerance and no risk of pulmonary embolism. Hookwires are prone to displacement or even detachment, which may lead to complications such as pain, pneumothorax, or hemorrhage. Medical glue exhibits strong irritancy, often causing pain and irritative coughing. Iodized oil carries a risk of peroneal artery embolism, while tracer markers pose radiation exposure risks. Their operability, safety, and effectiveness remain key concerns [6–8], with limited literature reported on these aspects. This study explores the effectiveness and safety of CT-guided microcoil localization technology in the thoracoscopic resection of small pulmonary nodules and summarizes relevant localization techniques.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

Clinical data from 100 patients with small pulmonary nodules who underwent video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery in Jinan Zhangqiu District People’s Hospital between January 2022 and December 2024 were retrospectively analyzed. Because this was a retrospective observational study, no a priori sample size calculation was performed; instead, all eligible patients treated during the study period were consecutively included. In routine clinical practice, preoperative CT-guided microcoil localization was generally considered for nodules that were expected to be difficult to identify intraoperatively during VATS, particularly small, relatively deep-seated lesions or lesions with limited tactile feedback, provided that a safe puncture route could be established. This indication was not limited to ground-glass nodules, but could also apply to subsolid nodules and selected small solid nodules when intraoperative localization was expected to be difficult. Due to the retrospective and observational nature of this study, treatment allocation was not randomized; instead, patients were assigned to groups based on real-world clinical decisions and technical indications. To control for confounding and improve reliability, the inclusion criteria for suspicious malignant nodules were tightly specified. Specifically, for solid or subsolid nodules, the decision to proceed with surgical resection was strictly guided by multidisciplinary consensus based on: (1) persistence or volume growth over a minimum of 3 to 6 months of CT follow-up, (2) an increase in the density or solid component of a subsolid nodule, or (3) highly suspicious morphological features (e.g., spiculation, lobulation, or pleural indentation). Preoperative microcoil localization was selectively indicated for lesions predicted to pose high intraoperative identification failure risks, defined as non-peripheral ground-glass nodules (GGNs) or small subsolid nodules located > 10 mm deep from the visceral pleura, or those lacking sufficient tactile feedback for manual palpation, provided a safe percutaneous puncture window existed. Patients meeting these specific anatomical criteria underwent microcoil localization, while those with more superficial, easily palpable, or solid lesions underwent direct VATS. Specifically, patients who underwent VATS after CT-guided microcoil localization were included in the microcoil localization group ( n=50), whereas those who underwent direct VATS resection without preoperative localization were included in the non-localization group ( n=50).

The inclusion criteria were as follows: ①Suspicious malignant pulmonary nodules requiring surgical intervention. In particular, for nodules measuring approximately 6 mm, the decision to proceed with surgery was not based on size alone, but on a comprehensive assessment of malignancy risk, including persistence on follow-up CT, interval growth, an increase in the solid component, suspicious morphological CT features, as well as the patient’s operative tolerance and willingness to undergo surgery; ②No antiplatelet or anticoagulant drugs had been used within 1 week before surgery. The exclusion criteria were as follows: ①Severe cachexia; ②Coagulation dysfunction; ③Severe cough or asthma with no relief after symptomatic treatment; ④Contraindications of CT and thoracoscopic surgery, adjacent to critical structures such as the mediastinal pleura, major vessels, main pulmonary artery, or heart.

This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (ethics batch number: 202208). All subjects signed written informed consent. This study received no financial support.

Instruments and methods (localization of microspring coils)

Chest CT scans were performed using a Philips Brilliance 16-slice large-bore CT scanner with the following parameters: 120 kV tube voltage, 92 mAs tube current, and 5.0 mm slice thickness with equal interval spacing. Thin-section reconstructions were performed when clinically indicated [9]. An 18G interventional puncture needle, Tornado-shaped microcoils (MWCE-18 S-7/3-TORNADO, COOK, United States), a customized localization grid, and a disposable interventional procedure kit were used.

The patient was positioned appropriately, and the customized localization grid was placed at the predetermined puncture site. Chest CT scanning was performed to identify the optimal puncture trajectory, with preference given to the shortest possible path while maintaining a needle trajectory perpendicular to the pleural surface. Following standard skin disinfection, draping, and local anesthesia, an 18-gauge introducer needle was advanced stepwise under CT guidance along the predetermined path until the needle tip was within 1 cm of the target nodule. The microcoil delivery sheath was then inserted through the introducer needle, and the microcoil was deployed at the periphery of the pulmonary nodule using the pusher wire. The entire assembly (introducer needle and sheath) was subsequently withdrawn to the extrapleural space to release the remaining microcoil segment (Fig. 1). A CT scan was performed immediately after localization to confirm the position of the microcoil, assess its spatial relationship to the lesion, and evaluate procedure-related complications such as pneumothorax and hemorrhage. After the localization was completed, the patient returned to the ward to await thoracoscopic surgery.

To control for confounding and improve reliability, the inclusion criteria for suspicious malignant nodules were tightly specified. Specifically, for solid or subsolid nodules, the decision to proceed with surgical resection was strictly guided by multidisciplinary consensus based on authoritative international guidelines and national expert consensus, including the Fleischner Society guidelines [10] and the Chinese Experts Consensus on Artificial Intelligence Assisted Management for Pulmonary Nodule [11]. According to these consensus statements, surgical intervention is strongly indicated based on: (1) persistence or volume growth over a minimum of 3 to 6 months of CT follow-up, (2) an increase in the density or solid component of a subsolid nodule, or (3) highly suspicious malignant morphological features (e.g., spiculation, lobulation, or pleural indentation).

Instruments and methods (thoracoscopic surgery)

The STORZ full HD thoracoscopic system was utilized for the procedure. After general anesthesia induction, the patient was placed in the lateral decubitus position with the unaffected side down, followed by routine disinfection and sterile draping. A single incision approximately 3 cm in length was made at the fourth to sixth intercostal space along the axillary line on the affected side. The skin, subcutaneous and muscular layers were dissected, the thoracoscope was then inserted to identify nodule localization (In the localization group, the microcoil position guided nodule identification; In the non-localization group, nodules were identified through visual inspection and manual palpation). A disposable linear stapler was used to perform wedge resection of the nodule and the surrounding lung tissue, with a margin of approximately 2 cm from the edge of the nodule. The resected specimen was immediately sent for intraoperative frozen section analysis. Subsequent surgical management (extended resection or lymph node dissection) was determined based on the pathological findings. After the operation, a closed thoracic drainage tube was routinely placed and the chest was closed by layer-by-layer suture. This was followed by transfer to the intensive care unit, and postoperative drainage volume was continuously monitored.

To minimize operator bias, all localization procedures were performed by two interventional radiologists with over 5 years of experience in CT-guided lung punctures, and all VATS resections were completed by the same surgical team consisting of two senior thoracic surgeons. This consistency helps control for confounding variations in technical proficiency.

Observation indicators

The basic data of the two groups were recorded, including gender, age, nodule location (upper lobes and right middle lobe were classified as upper lung, lower lobes were classified as lower lung), maximum diameter, nodule type (solid or subsolid), the shortest distance from nodule to pleura, thoracoscopic operation time, intraoperative blood loss, whether to convert to thoracotomy, number of lymph node dissection, postoperative drainage, hospitalization time, hospitalization cost, and postoperative pathological data Materials and so on. Nodule location was categorized as upper lung or lower lung according to anatomical and surgical considerations [12, 13], in order to provide a simplified baseline assessment of lesion distribution that might influence CT-guided localization and thoracoscopic resection. The localization-related outcomes recorded in the localization group included: Localization success rate (The criteria for localization success and accuracy were defined prior to the study based on two sequential clinical technical targets: 1) Technical/Radiological success: post-procedural CT scans confirmed that the distal end of the microcoil was placed within ≤ 10 mm from the margin of the target nodule, and the proximal tail successfully anchored across the visceral pleura; 2) Surgical success: intraoperative thoracoscopic visualization of the microcoil tail without dislodgement, allowing the surgeon to achieve a complete wedge resection with a negative margin ≥ 20 mm based on the marker’s guidance), localization time (time interval from the first CT image to the last CT image), pneumothorax (small, medium and large pneumothorax were defined by 30% and 60% of lung compression volume), hemorrhage (bleeding grading evaluation [14]), severe pleural reaction, whether the microcoil was dislocated or not, radiation dose during localization, etc.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0, IBM Corp). Before inferential analysis, the distribution of continuous variables was assessed for approximate normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of Q-Q plots, and homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene’s test. Continuous variables with an approximately normal distribution and homogeneous variances were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using the Student’s t-test. Continuous variables that did not meet these assumptions were presented as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test when the assumptions for expected cell counts were met; otherwise, Fisher’s exact test was used. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Comparison of baseline characteristics between groups

No significant differences were observed between the two groups in baseline clinical characteristics, including sex, age, nodule location, nodule type, pathological type, and the shortest distance from the nodule to the pleura (all P > 0.005), as shown in Table 1. The maximum nodule diameter was significantly smaller in the localization group (1.15 ± 0.4 cm vs. 1.38 ± 0.4 cm, P = 0.008; Hedges’ g = 0.57), indicating a moderate effect size.

Microcoil localization and associated complications

In the localization group, the success rate of localization was 98% (49/50), and one case of microcoil migration into the extrapleural space was identified during thoracoscopy. The average positioning time was (15.7 ± 3.5) minutes. Minor pneumothorax occurred in 6 patients (6/50, 12%), and none required closed thoracic drainage. There were 8 patients of low-grade hemorrhage (8/50, 16%), and the bleeding range was within 2 cm from the needle tract. No patients of significant intrapulmonary hemorrhage or severe pleural reaction were observed.

Comparative analysis of postoperative outcomes between groups

There were no significant differences between the two groups in intraoperative blood loss (P = 0.065), number of harvested lymph nodes (P = 0.343), postoperative hospital stay (P = 0.313) and conversion to thoracotomy (P = 1.000). One patient in the non-localization group was converted to thoracotomy because the target nodule could not be reliably identified during VATS by visual inspection and manual palpation alone, and thoracotomy was required to ensure complete resection and surgical safety. In contrast, the localization group showed a significantly shorter operative time [62 (50, 95) vs. 105 (73, 136) min, P < 0.001; effect size r = 0.41], lower postoperative drainage volume [200 (100, 270) vs. 300 (200, 500) mL, P < 0.001; effect size r = 0.42], and lower hospitalization costs [(3.69 ± 0.42) vs. (3.97 ± 0.48) ×10,000 yuan, P = 0.005; Hedges’ g = 0.62], suggesting moderate between-group differences for these key perioperative outcomes. Discussion

Accurate preoperative localization of small pulmonary nodules remains clinically significant, as these lesions are often difficult to visualize or palpate during VATS procedures. Effective localization techniques enable thoracic surgeons to precisely identify target nodules, thereby facilitating successful wedge resections. At present, CT-guided percutaneous localization is the most common preoperative localization method. The commonly used markers include metal markers (such as Hook-wire positioning needle, metal spring coil, etc.), liquid markers (medical glue, lipiodic oil, tracer, methylene blue, indocyanine green, etc.). These methods are still the focus of attention in terms of operability, safety and effectiveness [7, 8]. Previous studies have shown that Hook-wire positioning needle may cause obvious discomfort or pain in patients and carries a risk of dislodgement [15, 16]. Liquid markers may cause irritating cough in patients, and lipiodiol may penetrate into blood vessels and cause pulmonary embolism. Methylene blue and indocyanine green need VATS surgery within 3 h due to rapid diffusion. The fusion of medical glue with pulmonary nodules may affect the accuracy of postoperative pathological results. In contrast, microcoils have been widely used in vascular embolization because of their good histocompatibility and flexibility. A high-quality systematic review and meta-analysis comparing various preoperative lung localization modalities has further validated that microcoil deployment achieves a technical success rate comparable to traditional metallic anchors, while significantly reducing the incidence of procedure-related complications such as severe pneumothorax and marker dislodgement [17]. After implantation, they are less likely to cause substantial injury to the lung parenchyma or pleura and are less prone to dislodgement. The microcoil localization technique demonstrated good operational feasibility in our study, with a mean procedural time of only 15.7 ± 3.5 min - significantly shorter than that reported by Kha et al. and Mayo et al. [18, 19].

The placement operation was typically completed using 3–4 low-dose localized CT scans. The puncture technique is summarized as follows: after making sure that the needle reaches within cm of the edge of the small pulmonary nodule (pay attention to avoid puncture into the nodule to prevent tumor dissemination and postoperative imaging pathology [20]), release the distal coil (generally more than 3 cm), and directly withdraw the needle outside the pleura (measure the skin-to-pleura distance) to release the proximal coil. Finally, the needle and coil insertion device were withdrawn simultaneously, and no additional CT scan was required to minimize radiation exposure to the patient. At the end of the localization, CT was reviewed to confirm that the microcoil marked both the nodule and the pleura. In addition, the puncture path should be designed as perpendicular to the pleura as possible, and the trachea, blood vessels and interlobar pleura should be avoided to reduce the risk of complications.

The artificial fibrous filaments coating the microcoil surface enhance frictional properties, enabling secure anchoring within the pulmonary parenchyma and effectively reducing risks of migration or dislodgement. The localization success rate in the 50 patients was 98%, and all patients in the localization group underwent VATS on the same day. Thoracoscopic examination revealed one case (2%) of microcoil displacement into the extrapleural space, which was retrospectively attributed to insufficient deployment depth (only 10 mm from the pleura). The distal end of the microcoil should be developed at an adequate depth to ensure its stability, and the interval between localization and VATS should be kept as short as possible [7]. The microcoil has good flexibility and high safety. CT scan after positioning in this study showed a small amount of pneumothorax (6/50, 12%) and bleeding (8/50, 16%), which were similar to those reported in the literature [7, 8], and no further treatment was needed for pneumothorax and bleeding. No clinically significant pain or pleural reactions were observed in any patients. While our study recorded only minor, self-limiting complications (12% pneumothorax and 16% low-grade hemorrhage), it is critical to acknowledge that the relatively small sample size (n = 50 in the localization group) may lack the statistical power to capture rare but catastrophic complications. Potential severe adverse events associated with percutaneous lung punctures include systemic air embolism, massive hemothorax, tension pneumothorax, and microcoil migration into the pulmonary veins leading to systemic arterial embolization. Although no such severe events occurred in our cohort, clinicians must remain vigilant, maintain meticulous technique—such as instructing patients to avoid coughing and ensuring the needle tip does not enter major pulmonary vessels—and be prepared for emergency protocols.

In this study, the maximum nodule diameter was smaller in the microcoil localization group than in the non-localization group. Smaller nodules are more difficult to indentify during VATS, underscoring the importance of preoperative localization. Our results showed that the localization group exhibited a shorter operative time, lower postoperative drainage volume, and lower hospitalization costs. However, given the retrospective and non-randomized design of this study, these perioperative advantages should be interpreted as a positive clinical correlation rather than a direct causal relationship, as confounding variables such as baseline nodule size may also contribute to these outcomes. Nonetheless, these findings suggest that preoperative microcoil localization holds a strong clinical association with optimized procedural efficiency and could be considered a supportive factor in reducing the perioperative and economic management footprint for selected patients. The reduced postoperative drainage volume in the localization group may be related to more accurate intraoperative identification of the target nodule after CT-guided microcoil localization, which may allow more precise wedge resection and reduce unnecessary manipulation or traction of the surrounding lung tissue. In addition, the lower hospitalization costs in the localization group may be associated with the shorter operation time and improved procedural efficiency, which could help reduce the use of intraoperative and perioperative medical resources. These findings suggest that preoperative microcoil localization may provide not only technical support for VATS, but also potential benefits in perioperative management and reduction of the economic burden on patients. Notably, this technique may facilitate precise lung tissue resection and maximize preservation of pulmonary function while ensuring complete nodule removal with adequate surgical margins. No statistically significant intergroup differences were observed in the length of hospital stay and intraoperative blood loss. One patient in the non-localization group was converted to thoracotomy due to unsuccessful nodule identification, a difference not reaching statistical significance (P = 1.000), potentially reflecting the study’s limited sample size.

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective single-center study, it is subject to potential selection bias, information bias, and residual confounding. Because patient grouping was not randomized, baseline differences between the two groups may have influenced the observed outcomes. For example, the nodules in the localization group were significantly smaller than those in the non-localization group, which may have affected procedural difficulty and surgical efficiency. Second, the sample size was relatively limited, which inherently restricts the generalizability of our safety profile and may mask the true incidence of rare clinical complications. Future large-scale prospective multicenter studies are warranted to further validate these findings, and additional investigations may explore the value of combining microcoil localization with other localization or image-guided techniques to further optimize the surgical management of small pulmonary nodules. Third, there was a baseline imbalance in lesion size between the two cohorts, which introduces a major confounding variable. In our study, the maximum nodule diameter was significantly smaller in the microcoil localization group than in the non-localization group (1.15 ± 0.4 cm vs. 1.38 ± 0.4 cm, P = 0.008). While smaller and subsolid lesions inherently justify the clinical decision for preoperative localization due to the challenge they pose for tactile palpation during VATS, this discrepancy limits our ability to evaluate the outcomes independently. Smaller nodules may naturally require less extensive wedge resection margins and minimal tissue handling, which could partially account for the observed reductions in operative time, postoperative drainage, and hospitalization costs. Because advanced statistical adjustments—such as propensity score matching or multivariable regression—were not performed due to our restricted sample size, these perioperative advantages cannot be entirely decoupled from the smaller baseline lesion size, and the observed benefits must be interpreted with caution.
