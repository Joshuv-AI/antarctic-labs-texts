Temporal dynamics of Purkinje cell-intrinsic excitability governs cerebellar systems consolidation
Authors: Jewoo Seo, Yong Gyu Kim, Yong-Seok Lee, Sang Jeong Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummarySystems consolidation reorganizes newly encoded memories from cortical networks into downstream circuits. Although synaptic mechanisms are extensively characterized, the role of neuronal intrinsic excitability (IE) in this process remains relatively unexplored. Here, we utilized a cerebellum-dependent learning model to investigate the causal link between neuronal IE and memory consolidation. The optogenetic manipulation of Purkinje cells (PCs), the sole output of the cerebellar cortex, identified a crucial 90-min post-learning window during which PC-IE undergoes transient depression. Disrupting this decline by elevating PC-IE hindered long-term memory, whereas interventions outside this window did not. Elevated PC-IE spared basal ocular reflexes, indicating its role is specific to adaptive consolidation states. Moreover, increased PC-IE abolished learning-induced intrinsic plasticity in flocculus-targeting neurons (FTNs) of the medial vestibular nucleus (MVN), a downstream locus for persisting memory storage. These findings underscore the precise temporal dynamics of IE as a critical mechanism in systems consolidation, emphasizing its vulnerability to disruptions.Subject areas: Neuroscience, Behavioral neuroscience, Sensory neuroscience

Introduction

Systems memory consolidation, the process by which memories become stable and permanent over time, has been extensively characterized in cortical-hippocampal circuits,1 where memories initially encoded within the hippocampal-entorhinal cortex are gradually consolidated in the neocortex for long-term storage.2 In the cerebellum, short-term memories initially acquired through motor learning, driven by sensory inputs from the external environment, undergo a maturation process leading to long-term storage.3,4,5 This consolidation process critically involves coordinated activity between GABAergic inhibitory Purkinje cells (PCs)6 in the flocculus, a lobe-like structure responsible for the ocular reflex,7,8,9,10 and its prominent downstream circuit, flocculus-targeting neurons (FTNs) in the medial vestibular nuclei (MVN).11,12,13,14 Cerebellar systems consolidation accounts for the enhancement of compensatory eye movements, such as the optokinetic reflex (OKR),15,16 which relies on various sensory modalities, including visual inputs, to ameliorate the stability of vision.17

The canonical view of cerebellar learning and memory has long been centered on bidirectional modifications in synaptic transmission, particularly long-term potentiation and long-term depression.18,19,20,21 Empirical pieces of evidence suggest that the attenuation of synaptic strength between the parallel fibers (PFs) and PCs plays an important role in the acquisition of motor memory, allowing for the adaptation of cerebellum-dependent behaviors.20,21,22,23

However, a growing body of evidence has demonstrated that the understanding of memory consolidation extends beyond synaptic mechanisms and involves complementary non-synaptic mechanisms, such as intrinsic excitability (IE), to achieve intact learning capabilities.24,25 Indeed, plasticity of IE manifests bidirectional plasticity that parallels synaptic modifications.26,27,28 This indicates homeostatic maintenance within the neural circuitry29 and supports the notion that multiple mechanisms operate within the cerebellar memory system.4 Notably, OKR learning and eye-blink conditioning are accompanied by the down-regulation and up-regulation of PC intrinsic excitability (PC-IE), respectively.30,31 Transgenic mouse models with intact synaptic transmission but deficient intrinsic plasticity show impaired consolidation despite the successful acquisition of short-term memory.32,33 In different regions, such as the hippocampus, increased cortical excitability has been observed in response to eye-blink and fear conditioning.34,35 Although numerous studies have documented a robust association between neuronal IE and learning outcomes, direct evidence demonstrating its causal contribution to long-term memory formation remains scarce. This knowledge gap is particularly pronounced in the cerebellum, where the impact of manipulating PC-IE on memory consolidation remains unexplored. While a previous study32 has shown that PC-IE fluctuates in a time-dependent manner following motor learning, the precise temporal dynamics underlying its contribution to memory consolidation remain undefined. Therefore, establishing a causal link between PC-IE dynamics and systems consolidation and identifying the critical time window for effective long-term memory storage is imperative.

Effective inter-regional communication between the cerebellar cortex and its target nuclei is fundamental to systems consolidation.9,36 This process is thought to be mediated by synaptic plasticity and IE within the PC-FTN circuit.32,37 Building on these observations, it became plausible for us to speculate that PC-IE is a decisive neural substrate for coordinating inter-regional communication and can dictate the intrinsic plasticity of FTNs in the medial vestibular nucleus during memory consolidation.

In the present study, we hypothesized that the precise temporal regulation of PC-IE is critical for the maturation of motor memories and for establishing effective neural communication between the cortex and its target nuclei during the consolidation period. Using neuron-specific optogenetic manipulations in conjunction with the optokinetic reflex (OKR) paradigm, we demonstrate that artificially upregulating cortical PC-IE within a defined early phase of the post-learning window disrupts memory consolidation, while innate or non-adaptive basal ocular performance is not affected. This emphasizes the specialized role of PC-IE during the adaptive state of systems consolidation. Moreover, our data reveal that such manipulation impairs the intrinsic plasticity of FTNs in the MVN, underscoring the dependency of the PC-FTN circuit on the natural regulation of PC-IE. Collectively, these findings offer a new perspective on the non-synaptic mechanisms underlying systems consolidation in the cerebellar circuit, broadening our understanding beyond the classical focus on synaptic plasticity.

Results

Optogenetic manipulation of Purkinje cells-intrinsic excitability selectively impairs adaptive states of memory consolidation

Recent studies have demonstrated that the intrinsic excitability of PCs is strongly associated with cerebellar motor learning,30,31,32 yet the actual causality between PC-IE and motor memory consolidation through cell-type specific manipulation remains unexplored. Hence, we employed an appropriate approach for the selective manipulation of PC-IE in the flocculus. To achieve this, we employed optogenetics using the stabilized step-function opsin (SSFO).38 It promptly elicits depolarization and a stable excitatory state in neurons in response to optostimulation. The experimental design involved the bilateral injection of AAV1-Ef1a-DIO hChR2(C128S/D156A)-EYFP or AAV1-Ef1a-DIO EYFP into the flocculi of Pcp2-cre (L7-Cre) mice, which allowed specific recombinase activity in the floccular PC layer (Figure 1A). Following the surgery, an in-vitro whole-cell patch clamp recording of PCs was conducted (Figure 1B). We validated that in response to 473 nm optostimulation, which activates the opsin, the spontaneous firing of SSFO-expressing PCs was significantly elevated (Figure 1C). Specifically, the average firing rate of SSFO-expressing PCs was robustly enhanced, whereas EYFP-expressing PCs did not show any changes (Figure 1D). Furthermore, upon 593 nm optostimulation, which deactivates the opsin, the elevated firing rate of SSFO-expressing PCs reverted to the basal condition, and no change was detected in EYFP-expressing PCs, as expected (Figures 1C and D). These results establish the efficacy of the proposed approach for specifically manipulating PC-IE during the consolidation period, providing a promising foundation for investigating the causal relationship between PC-IE and cerebellar memory consolidation.

To understand the contribution of IE to memory consolidation, we employed optogenetics during the behavioral assessment. Specifically, we utilized the OKR protocol to train mice to adapt their ocular reflex in response to visual stimulation. To manipulate the floccular PC-IE in vivo during the consolidation period, we bilaterally implanted an optic cannula into the flocculi of mice that had undergone surgery for SSFO/EYFP expression. One mouse underwent 2 days of training. On the first day, it was trained for 50 min and placed back into the home-cage in a completely dark condition to rest without any visual interruption (Figure 2A, top panel). During the training session, an animal was subjected to continuous oscillation (±5° peak-to-peak motion at 0.5 Hz) of the optokinetic screen designed with vertical stripe patterns (Figure 2B). Once training was completed, bilateral 473 nm optostimulation was administered at 0 min post-learning to activate the SSFO, and another set of bilateral 593 nm optostimulation was provided at 24 h post-learning to deactivate the SSFO (Figure 1E, bottom panel). Proper training reduced the retinal slip, which directly reflected the accomplishment of motor learning in both the SSFO and EYFP groups (Figure 2C). When mice were exposed to continuous sinusoidal screen oscillation before training, the gain, a value representing how closely the eye motion overlaps with the rotation of a screen, ranged between 0.22 and 0.58 (EYFP = 0.235–0.576; SSFO = 0.217–0.557); after training, the gains were increased ranging between 0.38 and 0.84 (EYFP = 0.571–0.746; SSFO = 0.381–0.838) (Figure 2D). Improved oculomotor performance was observed in both groups, indicating intact learning capability (Figure 2E). After confirming the learning results, we activated the SSFO during the initial phase of the consolidation period. Thus, we were able to instantly disrupt the learning-induced depression of PC-IE that is reported to be promptly exhibited immediately after cerebellar motor learning.30,32 As a result, long-term memory was normally formed in the EYFP group after 24 h of learning, but was abolished in the SSFO group due to aberrant conditions in the neural circuitry. Specifically, the consolidation percentage of motor memory in the SSFO group was significantly lower than that in the control group (Figure 2E). To examine whether the mere expression of SSFO disrupts memory consolidation, we performed an additional OKR learning experiment using SSFO-expressing mice. Following an initial round of optogenetic manipulation, which impaired consolidation, these mice were given a recovery period of 2–3 weeks. Mice that still exhibited high gains prior to the second learning session, suggestive of lingering effects from the first training, were excluded from further analysis. In SSFO-expressing mice that were trained and then allowed a consolidation period without further optogenetic interference (Figures S1A and B), long-term memory was consistently retained (Figures S1C–E). These results underscore the importance of the proper regulation of PC-IE for successful memory consolidation.

Optogenetic manipulation of Purkinje cells-intrinsic excitability does not affect non-adaptive, basal ocular performance and learning capability

Building on our finding that proper PC-IE is critical for the adaptive phase of memory consolidation, we next investigated whether PC-IE also underpins the basal, non-adaptive state of the ocular reflex and acquisition during the learning phase. To address this, we first quantified basal ocular performance, a robust measure of innate, unmodulated ocular reflex, using a series of behavioral assays designed to isolate distinct sensory modalities. Specifically, we used the OKR to assess visual processing, the vestibulo-ocular reflex in dark (dVOR) to evaluate vestibular input in the absence of visual cues, and the vestibulo-ocular reflex in light (lVOR) to capture the integrated processing of both visual and vestibular signals (Figures S2A, C, and E). Ocular gains were recorded at various stimulus frequencies (0.1, 0.25, 0.5, and 1.0 Hz) before and after SSFO activation to determine the impact of PC-IE manipulation on non-adaptive, basal performance. Notably, our results indicate that baseline ocular gains remained consistent across all tested frequencies, regardless of optogenetic manipulation. Both EYFP- and SSFO-expressing groups demonstrated robust preservation of sensory-driven, basal oculomotor function.

Next, to determine whether elevated PC-IE affects the ability to learn an ocular reflex, we conducted a phase-restricted activation experiment. In this setup, the SSFO effect was engaged exclusively during the entire 50-min training session and stopped immediately afterward (Figure S3A). Under these conditions, both EYFP and SSFO groups showed similar increases in OKR gain (average gain increase of EYFP = 102.9%; SSFO = 109.8%), indicating that elevated PC-IE does not hinder gain acquisition. The extent of gain change after OKR learning was comparable between the groups, and initial and final gains were also not significantly different (Figures S3B–D).

These findings substantiate that our targeted optogenetic intervention selectively disrupts the adaptive processes involved in memory consolidation, mediated by PC-IE, without affecting the essential sensory processing required for basal ocular performance and learning capability for acquisition. These findings not only validate the specificity of our optogenetic approach but also underscore a fundamental dissociation between the mechanisms governing adaptive versus non-adaptive neural processes in the cerebellum.

Purkinje cells-intrinsic excitability contributes to the formation of long-term memory during the early phase of the consolidation period

In addition to the OKR, long-term memories of other cerebellum-dependent learning, such as VOR and eye-blink conditioning, are susceptible to interference within specific periods of consolidation. For instance, disruption of intracortical activity between 45 and 90 min after eye-blink conditioning in rabbits prevented the proper expression of long-term memory.39 Similarly, following VOR training, the continuous interruption of visual input during the first hour of post-training in cats elicited a reversion of learning outcomes.40 In addition, the pharmacological suppression of G-protein coupled receptors implicated in learning within the first hour of post-training has been shown to inhibit memory consolidation.41

Based on previous findings suggesting that memory consolidation operates within a critical time window, the primary objective of this part of the study was to ascertain whether this window exists during which PC-IE exerts a decisive and highly effective role in memory consolidation. Given our observation that optogenetic manipulation throughout the entire consolidation period resulted in the loss of long-term memory, we designed two distinct behavioral schemes involving different time points of optogenetic manipulation. In Scheme #1, mice received 473 nm optostimulation immediately after learning, 0 min post-learning (Figure 3A). After a recovery period of 2–3 weeks, allowing for the extinction of previously acquired motor memory, these same mice underwent a second learning trial with optostimulation administered at 90 min post-learning (Scheme #2, Figure 3A). The order of manipulation was counterbalanced to control for potential bias.

Consistent with our previous findings, EYFP-expressing control mice robustly retained long-term memory, as evidenced by high consolidation percentages in both protocols. (Figures 3B and C). In contrast, the SSFO group exhibited timing-dependent outcomes. When SSFO was activated to increase PC-IE at 0 min post-learning, the previously enhanced gain levels were reversed, resulting in a relatively low consolidation percentage (Figures 3D [left] and 3E). By contrast, when the manipulation was administered 90 min after learning, the increased gain was maintained, resulting in a high consolidation percentage (Figures 3D [right] and 3E). Moreover, when optogenetic manipulation was confined exclusively to the 90-min post-learning window, EYFP-expressing mice continued to demonstrate intact memory consolidation (Figures 3F [left] and 3G), whereas SSFO-expressing mice failed to retain long-term memory (Figures 3F [right] and 3G). Collectively, these results provide compelling evidence that PC-IE exerts a critical, temporally specific influence on memory consolidation: aberrant increases in PC-IE within the first 90 min post-learning is sufficient to disrupt the consolidation process, while manipulations outside this critical window do not significantly affect long-term memory formation. This underscores the notion that proper PC-IE regulation is essential for stabilizing and retaining cerebellum-dependent motor memories.

Dynamics of Purkinje cells-intrinsic excitability within the critical temporal window following motor learning

Under general circumstances, PC-IE is maintained at a constant level, but according to a previous study, VOR learning induces the bidirectional modulation of PC-IE during consolidation, initially reducing excitability immediately after motor learning and later restoring it.30,32 In particular, Kim et al. (2020) reported that the OKR learning leads to an immediate reduction in PC-IE,30 yet the detailed temporal dynamics within the critical window that we found remain insufficiently characterized. To address this question, we investigated the underlying cellular mechanisms that establish a critical time window. Once the mice achieved stable performance in the OKR task, we prepared acute brain slices to perform whole-cell patch clamp recordings from floccular PCs at two defined post-learning periods: 0 min post-learning and 90 min post-learning (Figure 4A). To evaluate PC-IE, we provided current injections from +0 pA to +900 pA (100 pA increment per injection, 500 ms long) in the presence of NBQX (2,3-dihydroxy-6-nitro-7-sulfamoyl-benzo[f]quinoxaline) and picrotoxin to block excitatory and inhibitory synaptic inputs, respectively. The results demonstrated that the mean firing rate of floccular PCs was significantly reduced compared with that of naive control conditions, but after 90 min post-learning, the rate recovered to that of the naive condition (Figures 4B and 4C). This indicates that the depression of PC-IE did not last for more than 90 min.

Furthermore, we analyzed the changes in action potential (AP) properties to explain the depression of PC-IE. We measured four AP parameters: the rheobase current, AP threshold, AP amplitude, and afterhyperpolarization (AHP) amplitude. Notably, only the rheobase current exhibited significant changes, with a marked increase immediately after learning that returned to baseline by 90 min (Figure 4B and C). This suggests that the transient decrease in PC-IE is primarily mediated by an elevated current threshold required for AP generation. Besides the rheobase current, other properties were not altered following the OKR learning (Figures 4D–F).

Furthermore, ex vivo comparisons between EYFP-expressing control mice and SSFO-expressing mice revealed that, following motor learning, SSFO-expressing mice exhibited significantly higher firing rates (Figures S2A and B). This heightened activity indicates that our optogenetic manipulation effectively abolished the expected learning-induced depression in PC-IE, correlating with observed impairments in long-term memory retention. These results suggest that the transient depression of PC-IE immediately post-learning is crucial for proper memory consolidation, and that disrupting this process through optogenetic intervention adversely affects learning outcomes.

Consequently, these findings provide compelling evidence for a critical, temporally confined window, within the first 90 min following motor learning, during which the transient depression of PC-IE is essential for effective memory consolidation. This highlights the importance of the precise temporal regulation of PC-IE in facilitating adaptive neural processes associated with memory consolidation.

Disrupted plasticity of intrinsic excitability of flocculus-targeting neurons in the medial vestibular nucleus

Postsynaptic circuitry of cortical PCs is predominantly organized by neurons in the MVN, characterized by two distinct types: flocculus-targeting neurons (FTNs) and non-FTNs.14,42 Among these two populations, FTNs play an active role in memory consolidation, as they serve as the principal recipients for the transfer of memory traces from the cerebellar cortex.9,13,36,43 The ability of FTNs to maintain and express long-term memory is supplemented by both synaptic and intrinsic plasticities.37,44 A previous study reported that not only was the intrinsic plasticity of PC elicited following motor learning, but also a potentiation of intrinsic excitability was induced in MVN neurons.32 To determine whether the optogenetic manipulation of PC-IE directly influences the intrinsic plasticity of FTNs, we used Pcp2-Cre (L7-Cre) mice, which received injections of SSFO or EYFP into the flocculus. Following OKR training, mice were subjected to continuous optostimulation throughout the entire consolidation period, and acute brain slices were prepared 24 h post-learning for whole-cell patch clamp recordings (Figure 5A, top). The robust expression of SSFO/EYFP in floccular PCs enabled the clear morphological identification of FTNs and nonFTNs in the MVN, as FTNs were distinguished by axon terminals with multiple boutons en passant (Figures 5A [bottom] and 5B).

We then assessed whether learning-induced intrinsic plasticity occurred in FTNs by measuring mean firing rates in both EYFP- and SSFO-expressing mice under naive and post-learning conditions. Comparisons revealed distinct patterns. In EYFP-expressing mice, intact memory consolidation post-learning was accompanied by the potentiation of intrinsic excitability in in-vitro (Figures 5C and D). Conversely, in SSFO-expressing mice, the abnormal increase in PC-IE resulted in impaired memory consolidation and the absence of intrinsic plasticity in FTNs (Figures 5E and F). Upon the completion of the learning and subsequent consolidation period, significant disparities in mean firing rates between EYFP-expressing and SSFO-expressing FTNs were particularly evident during 100 pA and 150 pA stimulations (Figure 5G).

Turning our attention to nonFTN circuitry, which remains largely segregated from the cortical learning circuit and lacks adjacent floccular PC terminals (Figure S3B), we speculated that motor learning would not induce significant intrinsic plasticity in nonFTNs (Figure S3A). As anticipated, motor learning did not elicit any discernible changes in IE of nonFTNs in either EYFP- or SSFO-expressing groups, as evidenced by similar mean firing rates (Figures S3C–F). Unlike FTNs, nonFTNs in both EYFP and SSFO groups maintained comparable excitability following motor learning (Figure S3G).

Overall, these findings strongly underscore that the proper maintenance of long-term memory is critically dependent on the precise expression of intrinsic plasticity in both PCs and FTNs. This dual expression of intrinsic plasticity not only facilitates the encoding and stabilization of memory traces within the cerebellar cortex but also ensures their effective transfer to downstream targets in the medial vestibular nuclei. The coordinated modulation of PC and FTN excitability is therefore a fundamental component of systems consolidation, enabling adaptive neural networks to communicate and integrate memory-related signals across regions. In essence, any disruption in this finely tuned plasticity cascade could compromise inter-regional communication and, ultimately, impair the stabilization of long-term memory, highlighting the indispensable role of intrinsic plasticity in the broader context of memory consolidation.

Discussion

Conventional theories of learning and memory have predominantly focused on bidirectional changes in synaptic efficacy at multiple sites within the cerebellar circuitry.4 Our findings, however, challenge this traditional view by revealing a crucial role for PC-IE during systems consolidation. IE, which reflects a neuron’s inherent ability to generate action potentials independent of synaptic input,24 has largely been overlooked in the context of cerebellar memory. However, recent work shows that intrinsic excitability in PCs can itself be modified during learning,31,45 and that similar learning-related increases in excitability occur in cerebellar nuclei neurons,46 suggesting a multi-site role for IE in consolidating motor memories. Motivated by these insights, we employed a comprehensive approach that integrates optogenetics, behavioral assays, and electrophysiological recordings. Our optogenetic manipulation of PC-IE induced an aberrant increase in neuronal excitability and spontaneous firing, which in turn resulted in a marked impairment in the consolidation of motor memory. These findings redefine our understanding of cerebellar memory circuitry, emphasizing that memory consolidation is not solely dependent on synaptic plasticity but also on the precise regulation of intrinsic neuronal properties. Nevertheless, the stability of non-adaptive, innate basal ocular performance was unchanged by aberrant PC-IE. Prior studies further support this conclusion: It was demonstrated that cerebellar inactivation in goldfish primarily impairs the adaptive modulation of the VOR, suggesting that intact cerebellar function is essential for fine-tuning motor responses in dynamic environments,47 while others showed that the reversible inactivation of the monkey flocculus compromises VOR adaptation retention without significantly altering baseline performance, highlighting that adaptive processes rely on specialized cerebellar circuits while the fundamental sensory-driven reflex remains stable.48 Moreover, evidence for a transsynaptic shift of the motor memory trace from the cerebellar cortex to the deep nuclei was provided, underscoring the specialized role of cortical circuits in adaptive consolidation.9

Building on our behavioral findings, we propose that distinct cerebellar mechanisms govern acquisition and consolidation. Specifically, during acquisition, the learning may be driven by climbing-fiber (CF) driven synaptic modifications, which are made to be context-dependent, such that modest, sustained elevations in PC-IE induced by SSFO have a negligible impact on initial learning. Mechanistically, we hypothesize that CF input provides the dominant instructive signal during acquisition, overpowering the relatively subtle contributions of increased PC-IE. Supporting this, our recent study demonstrated that the brief optogenetic suppression of CF transmission within the 0–50 min training window abolishes OKR learning, whereas suppression after training has no effect.49 This phase-specific requirement underscores the preeminence of CF activity in driving acquisition-phase plasticity. While it was shown that brief, phasic PC activation evokes eye movement,50 our SSFO protocol used a single 473 nm pulse to produce a tonic, moderate increase in PC-IE, rather than repeated pulses that synchronize spikes. Moreover, CF-evoked error signals are dynamically gated by local circuit mechanisms in a behaviorally relevant manner, such that only appropriately timed CF bursts induce learning.51 This model is consistent with classic work demonstrating that CF bursts evoke robust dendritic Ca2+ transients that gate synapse-specific LTD and LTP52,53 and whose efficacy is dynamically modulated by molecular-layer interneurons.54 By contrast, when PC-IE is elevated only during consolidation, we observe a selective blockade of long-term retention, mirroring the phenotype of PC-specific STIM1KO mice, which learn normally but fail to consolidate due to impaired intrinsic plasticity.32,33 Together, these data delineate a clear dissociation: CF-dependent synaptic mechanisms underpin acquisition, whereas intrinsic-excitability-dependent processes regulate offline memory stabilization. Collectively, these results emphasize that while PC-IE is critical for the consolidation of adaptive memory, its disruption does not compromise the inherent stability of the ocular reflex, thereby validating the specificity of our manipulation and elucidating the distinct contributions of PC-IE to adaptive versus non-adaptive, basal neural processes.

These findings suggest that PC-IE is neither dispensable nor global, but must be precisely regulated during consolidation to support memory stabilization without disrupting other cerebellar functions. We propose that the transient reduction in PC-IE observed after learning creates a permissive environment for the stabilization of memory-relevant synaptic modifications. Although we did not directly measure PF-PC synaptic plasticity in this study, the preservation of normal acquisition alongside impaired long-term memory expression suggests that synaptic changes were likely induced but failed to consolidate. This dissociation is supported by prior work from our group, where LTD of intrinsic excitability (LTD-IE) in PCs was shown to be essential for memory consolidation, even in the presence of intact PF-PC synaptic LTD.32 SSFO-induced elevation of PC-IE during the consolidation window would be expected to prevent this physiological decrease in excitability, thereby uncoupling synaptic modifications from their functional stabilization. Furthermore, another study demonstrated that in the developing visual system that newly formed synaptic changes can be reversed by excessive spontaneous activity during consolidation.55 By analogy, sustained PC firing in our SSFO condition may have interfered with synaptic stabilization by altering the temporal structure of post-learning activity. Together, these findings support the view that intrinsic plasticity operates as a permissive mechanism that enables proper consolidation, acting in concert with but independently from synaptic plasticity. Moreover, SSFO-induced elevation of PC-IE abolished the learning-associated intrinsic plasticity in FTNs, underscoring that proper PC gating is required for downstream excitability changes. Hence, future work measuring PF–PC and MF–FTN synaptic efficacy under altered PC-IE may explain how intrinsic and synaptic mechanisms interact during consolidation.

In particular, our temporal analysis revealed that cerebellar learning elicits sustained, time-dependent fluctuations in PC-IE that extend well beyond the immediate post-learning phase. Contrary to earlier reports describing only a transient decrease in PC-IE shortly after the motor learning,30,32 our detailed examination uncovered a prolonged phase during which a pronounced depression in PC-IE occurs, a phase that appears to be critical for the stabilization of motor memory. The existence of a critical 90-min window during which PC-IE is dynamically regulated highlights a mechanism for the transformation of short-term memory into long-term memory. Our electrophysiological recordings demonstrated that immediately after learning (0 min post-training), there is a significant reduction in PC-IE, as evidenced by an elevated rheobase current. By 90 min post-learning, this depression in excitability is largely reversed, suggesting a tightly orchestrated process in which transient alterations in intrinsic properties are essential for the consolidation process. Zhou et al. (2003) demonstrated that activity-induced synaptic modifications are highly susceptible to reversal by spontaneous or random activity unless stabilized by specific, appropriately timed patterns of input.55 This principle of temporally constrained stabilization may extend to the cerebellar system, where the transient depression in PC-IE likely functions as a gating mechanism. By selectively regulating PC output, this mechanism could protect newly formed synaptic modifications from interference, allowing the selective transfer and stabilization of memory traces in downstream structures. Given that PC firing serves as the primary output of the cerebellar cortex, encoding both temporal and sensory information, these fluctuations in PC-IE likely function as a gating mechanism, permitting the selective transfer of memory traces to downstream structures. Moreover, the precise timing regulation of PC-IE that we have identified suggests that the initial consolidation phase is a crucial period for effectively stabilizing and integrating memory traces. This aligns with findings in hippocampal systems consolidation, where early stages of synaptic plasticity prepare the ground for long-term storage in cortical circuits.56,57

Importantly, our findings indicate that these dynamic changes in IE are not restricted to the cerebellar cortex but extend to downstream targets, notably FTNs in the MVN. FTNs are the principal recipients for the transfer of memory traces from the cerebellar cortex, and their capacity to undergo intrinsic plasticity is essential for the expression of long-term memory. Our data reveal that the abnormal increase in PC-IE, induced by optogenetic manipulation, prevents the induction of intrinsic plasticity in FTNs, thereby disrupting inter-regional communication within the memory circuit. In contrast, nonFTNs, which are largely segregated from the cortical learning circuit, present no significant learning-induced changes in intrinsic excitability. This dissociation further supports the notion that the adaptive component of memory consolidation is mediated by the selective regulation of PC-IE and its downstream effects on FTNs, rather than a global alteration in neuronal excitability. This concept aligns with the hypothesis that intrinsic plasticity may perform as a trigger or conductor of systems consolidation within the circuits involving the medial prefrontal cortex-basolateral amygdala, and hippocampus.58,59

The involvement of intrinsic neuronal excitability in memory consolidation has been observed in other memory systems.34,35 While hippocampal and amygdala neurons typically exhibit increased excitability associated with the consolidation of contextual and fear memories,60 our data reveal a distinct cerebellar mechanism characterized by an initial depression of PC-IE following motor learning. This divergence highlights the specialized, region-specific mechanisms that govern memory consolidation across different circuits. Intrinsic plasticity, however, has been recognized as a ubiquitous and highly versatile process, enabling neuronal networks to integrate synaptic and intrinsic modifications in a task-specific manner.61,62,63 In the cerebellum, the transient reduction in PC-IE observed in our study may function as a gating mechanism, fine-tuning Purkinje cell output to optimize the downstream stabilization of motor memories. This underscores the unique role of intrinsic excitability in the cerebellum as compared to other brain regions. The optogenetic manipulation used in this study, which artificially increased PC-IE during the critical post-learning phase, may have disrupted this cerebellar-specific process, emphasizing the incompatibility of uniform plasticity paradigms across brain regions.

It is widely recognized that memory consolidation requires de novo protein synthesis, and the temporal aspect of protein synthesis is of paramount importance for long-term storage.64,65 Long-term contextual fear memory formation in the hippocampus, for instance, relies on transcription factors and associated signaling cascades, such as the cyclic AMP response element binding protein and the Ras/Erk pathway, both of which are essential for protein synthesis.66,67,68 Intriguingly, cerebellum-dependent motor learning is also associated with certain modifications in protein expression, though the specific proteins involved differ depending on whether they are linked to short- or long-term memory.69 Our data emphasize the existence of a critical time window for memory consolidation regulated by intrinsic excitability. Nonetheless, we acknowledge that we have not yet provided further insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying this phenomenon. As such, the involvement of protein synthesis in establishing this critical time window and its relationship with fluctuations in PC-IE are areas that require further investigation.

Similar to the systems consolidation processes observed in the hippocampus, where remote memories are gradually established in the neocortex, the cerebellar cortex-to-nuclei network appears to undergo analogous mechanisms. Previous studies suggest that hippocampal and neocortical synaptic plasticity share conserved molecular substrates and conditioned pathways,70 and that hippocampal modifications, such as dendritic spine reorganization and suppression of neuronal output, directly influence cortical representations,71,72,73 but unlike studies on the hippocampus, there is limited understanding of the neural properties underlying cerebellar systems consolidation. Therefore, we further investigated the post-circuitry of floccular PCs, which primarily interface with FTNs in the MVN. Our findings reveal that PC excitability and the intrinsic excitability of FTNs in the MVN play an important role in the construction of cerebellum-dependent long-term memory. The disruption of PC-IE was also accompanied by the impaired plasticity of the intrinsic excitability of FTNs, indicating a direct coupling of intrinsic plasticities across distinct regions within a unified memory circuit. Our data show that the intrinsic plasticity of PCs provides guidance to drive the induction of intrinsic plasticity in FTNs, which aligns with previous models proposing that PC activity modulates IE of FTNs.32,37 Additionally, our analysis of nonFTNs, neurons that lack synaptic connections with floccular PCs, revealed that alterations in PC-IE did not affect their intrinsic excitability. This specificity further underscores the unique role of the PC-FTN pathway in cerebellar memory consolidation.

PCs are a rare and exclusive type of neuron found solely within the cerebellar cortex, and have long been associated with motor coordination and movement.74,75 Overall, our study demonstrated that the ability to fine-tune firing patterns in a time-dependent manner by modulating intrinsic excitability plays an essential role in memory consolidation. This mechanism is crucial for the precise refinement of motor memory, prompting us to explore the fundamental question of why PCs employ such sophisticated regulatory strategies. The cerebellum’s capacity to process vast amounts of sensory input via a single output pathway, the PCs, raises intriguing evolutionary and functional questions regarding the significance of IE in this context.76 Despite receiving abundant sensory inputs, the cerebellar cortex conveys output exclusively through PCs, which exert extensive influence over various regions in the cerebral cortex to modulate both motor and non-motor behaviors.77,78,79 Although PCs in different cerebellar lobules exhibit no gross morphological differences, compelling evidence demonstrates that each lobule encodes specific sensory modalities,80,81,82 resulting in distinct firing patterns and membrane properties according to their location.83 More recent in vivo work shows that discrete PC microzones encode specific kinematic parameters via unique simple-spike tuning,84 and that during saccades, PC populations exhibit phase-locked synchrony signaling deceleration.85 In light of these findings and our results, we speculate that PCs have evolved finely tuned excitability profiles shaped in part by IE regulation, to match output dynamics to behavioral demands, thereby optimizing neural performance and enhancing motor-memory consolidation.

In summary, our investigation represents a paradigm shift in cerebellar memory consolidation by demonstrating that long-term memory formation depends not only on synaptic plasticity but also on the precise temporal regulation of intrinsic neuronal excitability within a critical 90-min window. These findings have far-reaching implications for understanding memory processes throughout the brain and suggest therapeutic targets for disorders associated with cerebellar dysfunction.86,87,88,89

Limitations of the study

We demonstrated that a transient 90-min reduction in PC-IE is necessary for the consolidation of motor memory. However, we did not directly measure PF-PC or mossy fiber-FTN synaptic changes or the molecular pathways that might link IE dynamics to durable storage, and the generality of the 90-min window across other tasks, species, and perturbation modalities remains to be tested.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

C57BL/6J male mice were used for all experiments. Mice were 6- to 10-week-old at the start of behavioral training. Animals were housed under a 12 h light–dark cycle, with ad libitum access to food and water, at 20–24°C and 40–60% relative humidity. All procedures were approved by the Seoul National University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (SNU-21031907-1 and SNU-230109-2-4). The influence of sex on the results was not systematically examined; therefore, potential sex-dependent differences in memory consolidation represent a limitation of this study.

Method details

Virus injection and optic cannula implantation

The experimental procedures were approved by the Seoul National University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and performed the guidelines of the National Institutes of Health. In this study, 6- to 10-week-old PCP2-Cre male mice (Jackson Laboratory, ME, USA), a driver line with high selectivity for Cre activity in PCs, were anesthetized with intraperitoneal injections of Zoletil/Rompun mixture (30 mg/kg and 10 mg/kg, respectively) and then they were placed on a stereotaxic frame (Narishige, Tokyo, Japan) for virus injection. AAV-Ef1a-DIO hChR2(C128S/D156A)-EYFP (SSFO) and AAV-EF1A-DIO EYFP were manually injected at a rate of 3 nL/sec into the bilateral flocculi using Picopump. The glass pipette was set at a 34° angle and the targeted coordinates for the virus injection were at 5.48 mm dorsoventral (DV) and ±0.10 mm mediolateral (ML). To achieve robust, bilateral coverage of the flocculus, with particular emphasis on the middle zone, referred to as the h-zone, which is instrumental in mediating horizontal ocular reflexes, an injection of 1.0 μL of SSFO was administered per flocculus at a titer of 2.0 × 1012 vg/mL. Although this volume inevitably dispersed into adjacent caudal and rostral regions, it successfully facilitated a dense expression throughout the middle (h) zone. After the mice were fully recovered (approximately 1 week) from the first surgery of virus injection, the second operation of optic cannula implantation (Ø1.25 mm Ceramic Ferrule, L=3.0 mm) near the cerebellar flocculus and head fixation were conducted. Unlike the fiber optic cannula, the head fixation components were hand-made (personalized design and production). Prior to the surgery, the mice were anesthetized using intraperitoneal injection of a mixture of Zoletil 50 (Virbac, 15mg/kg) and xylazine (Rompun, Bayer, 15mg/kg). Again, the mice were placed on placed on a stereotaxic frame (Narishige, Tokyo, Japan), and optic cannulae were implanted on both sides of the flocculi at ±2.80 mm ML.

Confirmation of viral expression

After behavioral experiments, brains were collected from all animals. Sampling was performed using cardiac perfusion. The brain was then extracted, and coronal sections were made at 30 μm intervals. Images were acquired and processed using a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM 7 MP, Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany) and Zen software (Zeiss). The anatomical location of virus expression was confirmed by comparison with Paxinos mouse brain atlas.90 Injection and implant coordinates, AAV serotype/titer, and fiber specifications are listed above. While we observed modest anterior-posterior variation in expression across mice, SSFO/EYFP expression and optic cannula were consistently located within the flocculus in all animals. We also did not observe cases of expression confined exclusively to specific zones.

Slice preparation and electrophysiology recording

Acute slices were prepared from 6- to 10-week-old mice. Mice were anesthetized using isoflurane and decapitated. To acquire the records of floccular PCs, 250 μm thick coronal slices of the cerebellar flocculus were obtained from mice using a vibratome (VT1200S, Leica). However, to acquire FTNs data, 200 μm thick coronal slices of the cerebellar MVN were obtained. Every slice was prepared in ice-cold cutting solution containing 75 mM sucrose, 75 mM of NaCl, 2.5 mM of KCl, 7 mM of MgCl2, 0.5 mM of CaCl2, 1.25 mM of NaH2PO4, 26 mM of NaHCO3, and 25 mM of glucose, with bubbled 95% O2 and 5% CO2. Then slices were immediately transferred to the artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) containing 125 Mm of NaCl, 2.5 Mm of KCl, 1 mM of MgCl2, 2 mM of CaCl2, 1.25 mM of NaH2PO4, 26 mM of NaHCO3, and 10 mM of glucose, with bubbled 95 % O2 and 5 % CO2. Subsequently, they were recovered at 32 °C for 30 min and at room temperature for 1 hr before recording. This 1 hr incubation “freezes” the in vivo neural state by halting further physiological changes, preserving intrinsic and synaptic properties. As long as the slices remain oxygenated and are maintained at physiological temperature, this recovery period sustains healthy neuronal activity without introducing artifacts. Finally, slices were placed in a submerged chamber perfused with ACSF for at least 10 min, and whole-cell recordings were performed at 29.5-30°C. We used recording pipettes (3-4 MΩ) filled with 9 mM of KCl, 10 mM of KOH, 120 mM of K-gluconate, 3.48 mM of MgCl2, 10 mM of HEPES, 4 mM of NaCl, 4 mM of Na2ATP, 0.4 mM of Na3GTP, and 17.5 mM of sucrose (pH 7.25). Electrophysiological data were acquired using an EPC9 patch-clamp amplifier (HEKA Elektronik) and PatchMaster software (HEKA Elektronik) at a sampling frequency of 20 kHz, and the signals were filtered at 2 kHz. All electrophysiological recordings of the PCs were acquired in the flocculus, which is a lobe-like structure of the cerebellum. The spontaneous firing rate was analyzed using Igor Pro (WaveMetrics) and normalized to the average firing rate during the baseline period. Whole-cell recordings of FTNs were acquired from the MVN that exhibited EYFP-expressing boutons en passant from floccular PCs. Inhibitory synaptic inputs were completely blocked using 100 mM of picrotoxin (Sigma Millipore) during PC recordings, and strychnine (1 mM) was added to block the glycinergic input in FTN recordings. Patch pipettes (3-4 MΩ) were pulled from borosilicate glass and filled with an internal solution (pH 7.25) containing 9 mM of KCl, 10 mM of KOH, 120 mM of K-gluconate, 3.48 mM of MgCl2, 10 mM of HEPES, 4 mM of NaCl, 4 mM of Na2ATP, 0.4 mM of Na3GTP, and 17.5 mM of sucrose for testing in vitro recordings, ex vivo PC excitability, and ex vivo FTN recordings. To examine the excitability of cerebellar PCs, we provided a series of square-waved current injections that ranged from 100-900 pA with increments of 100 pA for 500 ms. To investigate the excitability of FTNs in the MVN, we also provided current injections from -150 pA to 150 pA with increments of 50 pA for 1000 ms.

Optokinetic reflex behavior test

Three acclimation sessions were carried out first. During each session, the mouse was restrained in a custom-made restrainer for 30 minutes, with and without light, to habituate it to the recording environment. Following acclimation, calibration was conducted. In each video frame, both the pupil center and the corneal reflection were detected as landmarks for eye position. With the head fixed, the camera was moved systematically across known horizontal positions. From the relative displacement of the pupil versus the corneal reflection during calibration, we estimated the geometric distance between the pupil and the center of the cornea, which serves as a reference for converting pupil movements into angular rotations. During experiments, the pupil displacement observed in each video frame was measured relative to this reference, and for small displacements, these linear shifts were used to calculate eye rotation angles, which are approximately proportional to the measured pupil movement. This method accounts for the depth difference between the pupil and the corneal surface, improving the precision of eye angle estimation.91 Next, prior to oculomotor training, three basal oculomotor performances including the optokinetic reflex (OKR; visual only), vestibulo-ocular reflex in the dark (dVOR; vestibular only), and vestibulo-ocular reflex in the light (lVOR; combined), were measured. During the recording of eye movements, it was necessary to control the pupil dilation that was induced by the absence of light in the dark condition. Thus, physostigmine salicylate solution (eserine; Sigma Millipore) was given to mice under brief isoflurane anesthesia. The concentration of eserine solution was increased from 0.1%, 0.15% and 0.2% based on the pupil size. Basal oculomotor performances were described in gains. For the actual learning protocol, we adopted the OKR, and it consisted of five training sessions (10 min per session), six checkup points, and 24 h consolidation period. During the training sessions, the mice were visually stimulated with a sinusoidally rotating drum (±5 degrees). After the training, mice were placed back into their home cage and stored in complete darkness during the consolidation period to eliminate potential visual stimuli that could activate optokinetic reflex pathways. This protocol minimized external sensory inputs that might interfere with intrinsic mechanisms underlying memory stabilization, ensuring that observed changes in OKR gain were attributable to cerebellar processes, including PC-IE and downstream MVN activity. To assess the impact of optogenetic manipulation during the acquisition period, the training consisted of three sessions, each lasting 15 minutes.

Optogenetic manipulation during basal oculomotor performance and consolidation phase following optokinetic reflex

SSFO-expressing and EYFP-expressing mice underwent in-vivo optogenetic manipulation using a 473 nm blue laser (10.5 mW, 2 s pulse) to activate SSFO and a 593 nm yellow laser (10.5 mW, 2 s pulse) to deactivate SSFO. All behavioral sessions were carried out under head-fixed conditions, with the optic cannula targeting the flocculus.

Prior to any learning, mice were assessed in three basal reflex paradigms, OKR, dVOR, and lVOR, across four stimulus frequencies (0.1, 0.25, 0.5, and 1.0 Hz). To investigate any effect of manipulating PC-IE on basal oculomotor performance, we recorded two consecutive blocks of gain measurements: first, Pre-Optostim, in which SSFO remained OFF (no 473 nm light) to capture true basal performance; immediately thereafter, Post-Optostim, during which a single 473 nm pulse was delivered to activate SSFO, and neurons were recorded continuously under the sustained SSFO-ON state until yellow-light deactivation. For in-vivo optogenetic manipulation, SSFO-expressing mice were stimulated by a 473nm blue laser and a 593nm yellow laser for activation and deactivation, respectively. Each optostimulation was briefly given for 2 seconds at 10.5 mW. During the behavior assessment to determine the critical time window of memory consolidation, both SSFO and EYFP-expressing mice were subjected to optogenetic manipulation during the OKR (optokinetic reflex) training. The training was conducted twice, with each manipulation occurring at a different time point. Following the first training session, a break period of 2 to 3 weeks was implemented to reset the learning conditions before the commencement of the second training.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Gain analysis

Basal oculomotor performance and optokinetic reflex (OKR) learning gains were calculated as the ratio of evoked eye movement to the movement of the visual stimulus (screen) or vestibular stimulus (turn-table). All calculations were performed using a custom-built LabVIEW (National Instruments) analysis tool, VOG Analyzer.

Intrinsic excitability analysis

Neuronal firing properties were assessed from whole-cell patch-clamp recordings of PCs and FTNs. Action potential responses to step current injections were recorded, and intrinsic excitability parameters, such as rheobase current and firing frequencies, were quantified. Analysis was performed using a custom-built LabVIEW tool, Intrinsic VIEW Analysis, which allows automated extraction of electrophysiological features from raw voltage traces. Only neurons meeting established quality criteria, stable resting membrane potential, low series resistance, and adequate seal were included.

Statistical analysis

Graph plotting and statistical analysis were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software Inc, CA, USA). The hypothesis was tested by using paired and unpaired t-tests between sample pairs using GraphPad Prism. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Asterisks denoted in the graph indicate the statistical significance. ∗ indicates p < 0.05, ∗∗ < 0.01, ∗∗∗ < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗ < 0.0001. The test name and statistical values are presented in each figure legend. Data are shown as mean ± SEM and statistical evaluations were performed using, independent t test, one-way repeated-measured (RM) ANOVA and two-way RM ANOVA with post hoc Tukey’s test and Sidak’s test and were performed.
