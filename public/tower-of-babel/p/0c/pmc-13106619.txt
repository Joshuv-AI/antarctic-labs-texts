The fungistatic mechanism of benzaldehyde against the nematophagous fungus Arthrobotrys oligospora suggests a method for manipulating soil fungistasis
Authors: Li-Xue Tan, Ying-Ying Zhang, Zhi-Jian Liu, Yi Cao, Jian-Jin Liu, Ming-He Mo, Tong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Germination and growth of biocontrol microorganisms in soil are often inhibited by soil fungistasis (SF), resulting in unsatisfactory control efficacy. Therefore, exploring mechanisms underlying SF is important for developing efficient biocontrol agents. Benzaldehyde has a strong fungistatic effect against the nematophagous fungus Arthrobotrys oligospora. Our transcriptome analysis suggested a fungistatic model by which benzaldehyde induces reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to energy deficiency and in turn activating the AMPK-mTOR pathway. The ROS-inducing compound retinol enhanced benzaldehyde fungistasis (BF), whereas the antioxidant substance N-acetyl cysteine reduced ROS production and enhanced BF resistance in A. oligospora. Inhibiting the glutathione antioxidant system by blocking the supply of NADPH decreased BF resistance in A. oligospora. Furthermore, the AMPK activator acadesine bolstered BF resistance in A. oligospora, while the AMPK inhibitor dorsomorphin dihydrochloride or knocking out the AMPK gene had the opposite effect. These results strongly support a fungistatic mechanism of action for benzaldehyde. Finally, we found that the fungistatic mechanism of benzaldehyde similarly underlies SF, with the AMPK activators acadesine or metformin effectively increasing the SF resistance of A. oligospora. This study suggests a mechanism by which soil inhibits fungi and offers a potential method for improving SF resistance of fungal biocontrol agents.

Introduction

Soil-borne pathogens such as fungi, oomycetes, and parasitic nematodes are known to colonize plant roots. This can also occur with certain bacteria and other microbes. These pathogens to rot, damping-off, and vascular wilt in a wide range of cereal, vegetable, and fiber crops1. These pathogens and pests cause large annual crop losses worldwide and pose a significant threat to agricultural sustainability. Moreover, these pathogens can survive in the soil for long periods through the formation of specialized resistance structures, including sclerotia2, chlamydospores3, oospores4, eggs, and cysts5. Consequently, controlling these pathogens presents a significant challenge. The long-term excessive use of classic chemicals such as chloropicrin and azodrin for soil treatments raises serious concerns regarding human health, environmental pollution, and pesticide resistance6. Management of soil-borne pathogens requires the combined use of multiple control strategies, including crop rotation, biological control, the use of resistant varieties, and physical control techniques1.

Microorganisms are the primary resources used for the biological control of soil-borne pathogens. Fungi from the Trichoderma genus7 and bacteria from the Bacillus genus8 are commonly used to control fungal pathogens such as Fusarium spp. and Rhizoctonia spp., as well as oomycete pathogens such as Phytophthora spp. and Pythium spp. Biocontrol fungi9, such as Arthrobotrys oligospora, Pochonia chlamydosporia, and Purpureocillium lilacinum, and bacteria, such as Pseudomonas fluorescens and Bacillus subtilis10, are the main biocontrol resources for plant-parasitic nematodes. Although many microbial resources against soil-borne pathogens have been isolated, and management mechanisms have been revealed in the past decades, the low and unstable control efficiency of microbial biocontrol agents in soils has become a bottleneck problem in this field.

Regardless of how biocontrol agents control soil-borne pathogens, the precondition for biocontrol agents to exert control effects is that they can germinate, grow well in the soil, and establish a sufficient population density. However, the germination and growth of biocontrol agents in soils are inhibited to varying degrees; this phenomenon is called soil fungistasis (SF; also known as mycostasis) and occurs in almost every soil11. The fungistasis intensity of 287 agricultural soil samples collected from 26 provinces in China was evaluated in our laboratory, and all tested soils showed more than 95% inhibition of spore germination of three Trichoderma species (T. harzianum, T. viride, and T. hamatum) and P. lilacinum12. In addition, the average inhibition rate of all tested soil samples on spore germination of Clonostachys rosea and P. chlamydosporia was more than 70%. Therefore, the inhibition of fungal biocontrol agents by SF is an important reason for the low and unstable efficiency of biocontrol agents.

After nearly 70 years of research, a basic consensus has been reached on SF mechanism, involving the combined actions of soil nutrient deficiency, abiotic factors related to soil physical and chemical properties (e.g., pH and heavy metal ions), and microbial origin compounds in soils12–14. Among these, volatile compounds produced by soil microorganisms play key roles in the inhibition of biocontrol agents, and many fungistatic compounds of microbial origin have been identified, including ammonia15, benzaldehyde14,16, and sulfur-containing compounds14. Although the causes of SF are known, the molecular mechanisms by which SF suppresses biocontrol agents have not yet been elucidated. Understanding the fungistatic mechanisms is necessary to find methods to improve the efficiency of biocontrol agents in soils. However, owing to the complex and variable composition of soil, elucidating a universal fungistatic mechanism is challenging, with limited existing studies on this topic. In contrast, studying the fungistatic mechanisms of specific compounds is comparatively straightforward. For example, we revealed an endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress-related fungistatic mechanism of ammonia that inhibits conidial germination of A. oligospora, a nematode-trapping fungus17,18.

Previous studies have shown that benzaldehyde is a common fungistatic factor in soil. One of the metabolic pathways that produces benzaldehyde in soils is the microbial breakdown of phenylalanine and phenylvaleric acid19, which may be derived from the degradation of plant tissue. Besides, benzaldehyde is a derivative of benzoic acid that is commonly used as an antibacterial and antifungal preservative and flavoring agent in food, cosmetic, hygiene, and pharmaceutical products19. The widespread production and utilization of benzoic acid and its derivatives inevitably leads to the anthropogenic leakage of benzoic acid into the environment (primarily water and soils). Moreover, leaked benzoic acid can be reduced to benzaldehyde by soil microorganisms. Taken together, benzaldehyde may be a representative fungistatic factor in soil. In the present study, we investigated the fungistatic mechanisms of benzaldehyde.

Results

Glucose and glutamate enhanced benzaldehyde fungistasis (BF)

SF inhibits the germination and growth of biocontrol fungi, and benzaldehyde is a common fungistatic factor produced by many soil bacteria. The benzaldehyde fungistasis was tested in a two-compartment Petri dish (Fig. 1A). With the increase of benzaldehyde dose, the conidial germination rates of A. oligospora decreased gradually, with 0.8 µL benzaldehyde inhibiting the germination of ~90% conidia (Fig. 1B). These results indicated that benzaldehyde had a strong fungistatic effect against A. oligospora. It was reported that soil supplementation with nutrients can relieve SF20,21. Therefore, the effect of nutrients on relieving BF was also assessed in this study. The results showed that a low concentration of glucose (1%) slightly relieved BF, but high concentrations of glucose (5%) enhanced BF (Fig.1C). Furthermore, 1% glutamate had almost no effect on benzaldehyde fungistatic activity, whereas 3% glutamate unexpectedly enhanced the fungistatic effect of benzaldehyde (Fig. 1D). These results suggested that the addition of nutrients was not always beneficial in relieving BF. Transcriptome characteristics of benzaldehyde fungistatic conidia

To elucidate the fungistatic mechanism of benzaldehyde, the transcriptomes of fresh, germinated, and benzaldehyde fungistatic conidia were analyzed. The transcriptome data showed that 2613 and 1588 genes were upregulated and downregulated in fungistatic conidia, while in germinated conidia, 2120 and 1056 genes were upregulated and downregulated (Fig. 2A). Additionally, 667 genes were commonly upregulated, and 437 genes were commonly downregulated in both germinated and fungistatic conidia. Excluding these common genes, the KEGG pathways analysis was conducted on 1946 upregulated genes and 1151 downregulated genes in fungistatic conidia. The analyses showed that the upregulated genes were mainly distributed across 12 pathways, with spliceosome and ribosome biogenesis exhibiting the highest number of upregulated genes (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, 29 genes associated with autophagy and mitophagy were found to be upregulated (Fig. 2C), suggesting that mitochondrial stress may occur under benzaldehyde-fungistatic stress. Additionally, 17 upregulated genes were associated with protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum (ER) (Fig. 2D), specifically related to the processing of misfolded protein, including those encoding PERK and IRE1, which are key regulators of ER stress-induced unfolded protein response (UPRER). Transcriptome data revealed a putative fungistatic mechanism of benzaldehyde

The greatest clue revealed by the transcriptome is that ER stress and mitochondrial stress might occur in benzaldehyde-fungistatic conidia. It remains unclear how benzaldehyde induces ER stress and mitochondrial stress. It was reported that benzaldehyde could induce ROS formation in rat synaptosomal fractions22. We found that several antioxidant genes were upregulated in fungistatic conidia, including genes related to glutathione synthesis (glutamate-cysteine ligase and glutathione synthase), peroxiredoxin gene, glutaredoxin gene, and yeast activator protein YAP (Fig.3A). These data suggested benzaldehyde might induce oxidative stress in fungistatic conidia. ROS-mediated oxidative damage may be one of the causes of ER stress and mitochondrial stress. In response to mitochondrial stress, eukaryotic cells can activate mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPRmt) to rescue mitochondrial dysfunction. The UPRmt includes the upregulation of mitochondrial protease and molecular chaperones23, which promote mitochondrial protein quality control, and downregulation of oxidative phosphorylation (OXPH) genes to reduce oxidative stress24. Three mitochondrial protease encoding genes (Lon, ATP23, and AFG3) and three mitochondrial chaperone encoding genes were upregulated in fungistatic conidia (Fig.3B). The UPRmt-induced downregulation of oxidative phosphorylation (OXPH) genes was also observed in fungistatic conidia (Fig.2F). A stronger mitochondrial response to mitochondrial stress is mitophagy, which was also upregulated in the fungistatic conidia. For UPRER, the PERK pathway attenuates protein synthesis by phosphorylating eIF2α, and the IRE pathway promotes chaperone protein expression and protein degradation through the proteasome. The transcriptome data showed that the proteasome-encoding genes were globally upregulated in fungistatic conidia, although most genes did not reach the 2-fold difference screening standard for differentially expressed genes (Supplementary Fig. S1A). In addition, there was an overall upregulation of cytoplasm and endoplasmic chaperones in the fungistatic conidia (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Based on the analyses of transcriptome data, a fungistatic model of benzaldehyde was proposed (Fig. 3D) to explain the major transcriptomic changes in fungistatic conidia, including UPRmt and UPRER. Mitochondrial stress should result in energy deficiency in conidia, and energy deficiency would activate the AMPK-mTOR pathway, which can promote autophagy and inhibit protein synthesis25,26. This would help alleviate mitochondrial stress and ER stress. We speculate that AMPK might be a key target for regulating spores’ tolerance to benzaldehyde-induced fungistatic stress.

ROS regulation determines the fungistatic effectiveness of benzaldehyde

We found that benzaldehyde induced ROS formation in the fungistatic conidia of A. oligospora. As shown in Fig. 4A, under the fungistasis induced by 0.4 μL benzaldehyde, no evident ROS was detected in the conidia. When the added benzaldehyde increased to 0.6 and 0.8 μL, ROS was detected, with ROS levels increasing with higher benzaldehyde content (Fig. 4B, C). In addition, the antioxidants NAC, a known inhibitor of ROS27, and retinol, which can induce ROS28, were used to regulate ROS formation in fungistatic conidia. The results showed that 1% NAC reduced ROS formation in fungistatic conidia treated with 0.8 μL benzaldehyde (Fig. 4D). However, 5% NAC resulted in evident ROS in conidia treated with 0.4 μL benzaldehyde (Fig. 4E). Moreover, 1% retinol also enhanced ROS formation in conidia treated with 0.4 μL benzaldehyde (Fig. 4F). In addition, 1% NAC, which reduced ROS formation in fungistatic conidia, improved the conidial germination rate under benzaldehyde fungistatic conditions, whereas 3% and 5% NAC reduced the conidial germination rate under benzaldehyde fungistatic conditions (Fig. 4G). Retinol (1% and 3%) also reduced the conidial germination rate under benzaldehyde fungistatic conditions (Fig.4H). Low glucose concentration relieved the benzaldehyde fungistasis by providing NADPH

Based on the fungistatic mechanism of benzaldehyde, we attempted to determine why low concentrations of glucose relieved the fungistatic effect of benzaldehyde. The gene AOL_s00078g131 encoding glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) catalyzes glucose metabolism to produce nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH). And most importantly, it is the major source of NADPH in cells29. It is known that reduced glutathione plays an important role in combating the oxidative stress caused by ROS, with NADPH being necessary for the conversion of oxidized glutathione to reduced glutathione. Therefore, we hypothesized that NADPH produced by glucose metabolism may be necessary for glucose to relieve BF. To prove it, AOL_s00078g131 was knocked out in A. oligospora, and four knockout mutants were obtained (Fig. 5A, B). Under normal condition, conidial germination of the gene knockout mutant (△g131) at various time points was comparable to that of the wild-type strain (Fig. 5C). However, under BF conditions, the conidial germination rate of mutant strains was significantly lower than that of the wild-type strain (Fig. 5D), with even as little as 0.1 µL of benzaldehyde greatly inhibiting spore germination of the mutant strains. Moreover, low concentration glucose (1%) could not relieve BF against the △g131 mutant (Fig. 5E), whereas high concentration glucose (5%) still enhanced BF against the △g131 mutant. AMPK is the key target for regulating ROS and the fungistatic effect of benzaldehyde

As shown in the putative fungistatic model of benzaldehyde (Fig. 3D), the AMPK protein may be important for the response of conidia to BF. To confirm this, the AMPK activator (acadesine) and the AMPK inhibitor (dorsomorphin dihydrochloride) were used to test their function in regulating the fungistatic effects of benzaldehyde. Acadesine significantly improved the conidial germination rate under benzaldehyde fungistatic conditions, indicating that acadesine could relieve the fungistatic effect of benzaldehyde, with 2 mM acadesine having the greatest effect (Fig.6A). In contrast, dorsomorphin dihydrochloride, an AMPK inhibitor, significantly reduced the conidial germination rate under benzaldehyde fungistatic conditions, indicating that it enhanced the fungistatic effect of benzaldehyde (Fig. 6B). After adding 2 mM of acadesine to the WA medium, no evident ROS was observed in fungistatic conidia treated with 0.8 μL benzaldehyde (Fig. 6C). This dose of benzaldehyde could induce ROS formation in the absence of acadesine (Fig.4C). The A. oligospora AMPK deletion strain (△173g192)30 was further used to assess the role of AMPK in regulating the fungistatic effect of benzaldehyde. By measuring the colony radius, it was found that wild-type A. oligospora and the △173g192 strain had no significant difference in growth rate under the inhibition of 2 μL of benzaldehyde, and AMPK activator acadesine promoted hyphal growth of wild-type A. oligospora. However, it was found that wild-type A. oligospora continued to grow under the inhibition of 3 μL of benzaldehyde, while hyphal growth in the △173g192 strain was severely impeded (Fig. 6E). The AMPK activator acadesine promoted more hyphal growth of wild-type A. oligospora under the fungistasis stress induced by 3 μL of benzaldehyde. However, acadesine could not improve hyphal growth of the AMPK deletion strain △173g192 (Fig. 6E, F). These results further demonstrate the important role of AMPK in regulating the fungistatic effects of benzaldehyde.

SF alleviation through AMPK activator

Benzaldehyde represents just one of many fungistatic factors present in soils, and it remains uncertain whether the mechanism of BF is applicable to SF. Therefore, we tested whether SF could be relieved by the AMPK activator acadesine. As shown in Fig. 7A, the germination rate of A. oligospora in the soil suspension without acadesine was 48.3%. However, with the addition of acadesine at a concentration of 1.5 mM, the germination rate of A. oligospora in the soil suspension significantly improved to 81.7%, surpassing that of the control group. However, acadesine, being a costly AMPK activator, may not be economically viable for widespread field applications. Therefore, we explored the use of a more cost-effective alternative. Metformin, a known AMPK activator31, was considered because of its affordability. The results showed that metformin improved the germination rate of conidia under BF. Specifically, in the concentration range of 1–4 mM, the germination rate increased with increasing metformin concentration (Fig. 7B). This indicated that metformin could relieve BF. We further investigated whether metformin could alleviate SF, and our findings revealed that metformin significantly enhanced conidial germination rates at concentrations of 2, 4, and 5 mM in the soil suspension (Fig. 7C). Moreover, a germination rate of 71% was observed in a soil suspension containing 5 mM metformin. These results indicated that metformin could increase the SF resistance of A. oligospora. Similar results were obtained for the other two soil samples (Supplementary Fig. S2), indicating that AMPK is a key target for relieving SF.

Discussion

Compared with chemical pesticides, biocontrol of soil-borne pathogens offers several advantages, including environmental friendliness and minimal development of drug resistance. However, the germination and growth of microbial agents used for biocontrol are often inhibited in soils owing to SF. The discovery of a fungistatic mechanism is important for developing methods to enhance SF resistance in microbial agents, which is necessary for microbial agents to thrive in soils. Our previous study revealed that ammonia, an important fungistatic factor in agricultural soils, plays a fungistatic role by inducing ER stress18. In this study, we identified a fungistatic mechanism for benzaldehyde, a common soil component. As shown in Fig.3C, benzaldehyde plays a fungistatic role mainly by inducing oxidative stress and energy deficiency. The observations of ROS, upregulation of antioxidant genes in transcriptome data, and BF alleviation by antioxidants NAC support the existence of oxidative stress in fungistatic conidia. However, the mechanism of benzaldehyde-induced oxidative stress in A. oligospora is unclear, and further research is needed to reveal the exact mechanism. Based on the reported results, there might be two possible mechanisms. Benzaldehyde can impair the antioxidant system by inactivating glutathione peroxidase22. Besides, benzaldehyde can be converted to benzoic acid, which can inhibit the biosynthesis of coenzyme Q in yeast, which is an essential component of the electron transport system32.

Based on the consideration of providing nutrients and enhancing tolerance to soil fungistasis, nutrients are often used as additives in the production of biocontrol agents to help them survive and colonize in soils. Our results show that under stress conditions, excessive nutrients are likely to inhibit spore germination of biocontrol fungi. Specifically, glucose (1%) enhanced the BF resistance of A. oligospora. However, 5% glucose resulted in the unexpected decrease in BF resistance. Although it is not known yet whether this inhibition is temporary or lethal, this inhibitory effect may delay the rapid function of biocontrol fungal agents in soils. Therefore, more work needs to be done to assess the positive and negative effects of nutrients on fungal agents in soils. Under stress conditions, like BF or SF, excess nutrients (e.g., 5% glucose) or specific nutrients (e.g., retinol) may induce ROS formation during enhanced oxidative phosphorylation27,33. So, in order to obtain the best control effect of fungal agents, it is necessary to determine the appropriate kinds and amounts of nutrients used in biocontrol agents.

Interestingly, our study found that the activation of the AMPK helps A. oligospora resist both BF and SF. It suggested that the fungistatic mechanism of benzaldehyde might be common to soil to some extent. There are three possible reasons why AMPK activators are able to mediate BF and SF alleviation. First, activating the energy sensor AMPK increases cellular energy supply. Second, AMPK can activate autophagy through the AMPK-mTOR pathway, and the autophagy helps cope with oxidative stress. Finally, activation of AMPK would inhibit protein synthesis which will alleviate protein misfolding in the endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria and help alleviate mitochondrial stress and ER stress.

In the transcriptome data, we also found that dozens of genes involved in spliceosome and ribosome biogenesis in fungistatic conidia (Fig. 2B). It is not clear whether these two pathways are directly related to the benzaldehyde fungistasis. A large-scale protein synthesis is involved in the early stage of spore germination, and the biogenesis of ribosomes and RNA splicing are necessary conditions for protein synthesis. Upregulation of the spliceosome and ribosome biogenesis may be a transcriptional response caused by the spore germination process. Besides, 22 genes related to the peroxisome were also upregulated in fungistatic conidia (Supplementary Fig. S3), including genes related to peroxisome biogenesis and lipid metabolism. It is well known that mitochondria and peroxisomes are functional core partners in lipid metabolism. Research also showed that peroxisomes are juxtaposed physically to strategic sites on mitochondria34, and this contact is crucial for the homeostasis of lipids within cells. Impaired mitochondrial function (e.g., mitochondrial stress in the fungistatic conidia) may feed back to induce peroxisome activity or biogenesis to meet the cell’s demand for lipid metabolism.

This article not only reveals the fungistatic mechanism of benzaldehyde, but also, more importantly, our results suggest that AMPK activators may be used as adjuvants for fungal biocontrol agents, and may enhance their efficacy in field applications. However, we only tested a few soil samples, and results show that the AMPK activator can enhance the resistance to these soils in A. oligospora. It is unclear if this applies to most farmland soils on Earth. Moreover, the effect of the AMPK activator on the control efficiency of fungal biocontrol agents also needs to be evaluated experimentally. Besides, compared with using AMPK activators as an additive to help biocontrol fungi resist SF, constructing an engineered fungal agent with AMPK controllably expressed or screening biocontrol strains that are insensitive to SF may be a more perfect method, because AMPK activators may also enhance SF resistance in plant pathogenic fungi and help them colonize in soils.

Methods

Determination of the transcriptome

The nematode-trapping A. oligospora fungal strain ATCC 24927 was used for studying the fungistatic mechanism of benzaldehyde, aimed at biocontrolling soil-borne, plant-parasitic nematodes. To analyze the transcriptome, three kinds of conidial samples were prepared using two-compartment Petri dishes35. After cultivation on corn meal agar plates at 28 °C for 14 d, the conidia of A. oligospora strain ATCC 24927 were harvested and used as fresh conidia. The fresh conidia were used as a control. Approximately 0.3 g of fresh conidia were allowed to germinate on water agar (WA) medium for 24 h at 28 °C and used as germinated conidia. Meanwhile, for preparing fungistatic conidia, 0.3 g of fresh conidia were spread on WA medium in one compartment of the two-compartment Petri dish (diameter = 9 cm), while a tampon was placed in another compartment, and 2.0 µL benzaldehyde (99% purity) was dropped on the tampon. Plates were then wrapped with Parafilm (Bemis, USA) and incubated for 24 h at 28 °C. The conidia were recycled and used as benzaldehyde fungistatic conidia. To recycle conidia, the surface of the WA medium was covered with cellophane. The fresh, germinated, and fungistatic conidia had three biological replicates each and were sent to Shanghai Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) for determining the transcriptome.

The total RNA of the conidial sample was extracted using the Magnetic Hi-Plant RNA kit (TIANGEN BIOTECH, Beijing, China), and the mRNA was enriched using magnetic beads with Oligo(dT) in this kit. The Illumina® Stranded mRNA Prep-Ligation kit (Illumina, USA) was used to construct the cDNA library according to the following process. The cleaved mRNA fragments were used as templates to synthesize the first strand of cDNA with random hexamers as primers. The first strand of cDNA was used as a template to synthesize the second strand of cDNA by DNA polymerase I. The resulting cDNA was purified, followed by sticky-end repair, addition of an ‘A’ base to the 3’ ends, and ligation with a barcode adapter. After the selection of fragment size, cDNA was amplified by PCR to construct a cDNA library. Finally, cDNA library quality was checked using Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and ABI StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System, and the qualified cDNA library was used for sequencing on the Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform. The obtained raw transcriptome data were deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive (no. CRA039124).

Transcriptome analysis

According to a previously reported method36, raw reads of the transcriptome data were filtered to obtain clean reads using fastp (Version 0.23.4, parameters:--paired-end\--thread 16\--qualified quality phred 20\--length required 50 \--auto detect adapter for paired-end RNA-seq reads filtering), which were mapped to the genome37 of A. oligospora strain ATCC 24927 in GenBank using HiSAT2 (Version 2.2.1, parameters:--dta\--p16 for non-strand-specific paired-end RNA-seq), and the gene expression levels (TPM values) were calculated using RSEM (Version 1.3.3, parameters:--bowtie2\--paired-end\-p16\--forward-prob 0.5\--no-bam-output\)38. To screen for differentially regulated genes in the transcriptome, the average TPM values of genes in germinated and fungistatic conidia were compared to those in fresh conidia using DESeq2 (Version 1.42.0). The genes with fold change ≥2 and false discovery rate <0.05 were selected as upregulated or downregulated genes. All the differentially regulated genes in benzaldehyde fungistatic conidia were compared with those in germinated conidia using a Venn diagram (https://hiplot.com.cn/basic/venn). The gene sets from the Venn diagram results were annotated using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) online service tool KEGG Automatic Annotation Server and mapped to the KEGG pathway database using the KEGG mapper. The pathway distribution of differentially regulated genes was counted.

Evaluating nutrient relief effects on benzaldehyde-induced fungistatic stress

The fungistatic role of benzaldehyde was assessed following the method of preparing fungistatic conidia. Five milliliters of 2% WA medium were poured into one side of a two-compartment Petri dish (diameter = 9 cm), and approximately 2000–3000 fresh conidia were spread onto the medium surface. A tampon containing different volumes of benzaldehyde (0–1.2 µL) was placed in another side of the dish. The plates were then wrapped with Parafilm (Bemis) and cultured at 28 °C for 24 h. Next, the conidial germination rate was determined under a microscope by observing the formation of the germ tube. All treatments were conducted in triplicate, three microscopic fields were observed for each plate, and about 300 conidia were assessed. The fungistatic stress of benzaldehyde was assessed by comparing conidial germination rates.

To examine the alleviating effects of certain compounds on the fungistatic stress induced by benzaldehyde, 5 ml of 2% WA medium containing various concentrations of glutamate, N-acetyl cysteine (NAC), retinol, metformin, acadesine (an AMPK activator39, MedChemExpress, USA), and dorsomorphin dihydrochloride (an AMPK inhibitor39, MedChemExpress, USA) were added to one side of a two-compartment Petri dish. According to the method described above, conidia were spread, different volumes of benzaldehyde were added, and the conidial germination rate at 24 h was determined. All treatments were conducted in triplicate, and three microscopic fields were observed for each plate.

Investigation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels

Benzaldehyde induces ROS formation in the central nervous system of rats22. To investigate whether benzaldehyde treatment induces the formation of ROS in conidia, the fresh conidia ( ~ 104) were treated for 24 h with different volumes of benzaldehyde (0.4, 0.6, and 0.8 µL) in a two-compartment Petri dish (diameter = 9 cm). Then the conidia were recycled from the surface of the cellophane that covered the WA medium. These conidial samples were treated using the fungal/yeast intracellular ROS primary fluorescence assay kit (GMS10016.8; Shanghai Branch of GENMED Scientifics Inc., USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, ROS in these conidial samples were assayed using a fluorescence microscope. In order to evaluate the effect of nutrient addition (glutamate, NAC, retinol, acadesine, and dorsomorphin dihydrochloride) on ROS formation in fungistatic conidia, the fungistatic conidia were prepared according to the same method, except that different concentrations of nutrients were added into the WA medium. Then the conidia were recycled and used for testing ROS according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Assessing the alleviating effects of acadesine and metformin on SF

Fungistatic stress of soil and the relief effects of acadesine and metformin on SF were assessed by testing the conidial germination rate in soil suspensions according to a previously reported method18,36. Soil samples were collected from Yunnan Province, China, and deionized water was added to a beaker at a mass ratio of 1:1 to produce fungistatic soil suspensions. 1 mL conidia suspension containing about 10,000 fresh conidia was placed into a dialysis bag (300 kDa; Spectrum, USA), and the dialysis bag was then dropped into 100 mL soil suspensions and cultured at 28°C. After culturing for 24 h, 100 µL of the conidia suspension was removed from the dialysis bag and used to determine germination rates. Three biological replicates were set. To test the relief effects of acadesine and metformin on the fungistatic stress of the soil, acadesine or metformin solution was also added to the dialysis bags with conidia, and the total acadesine or metformin in the soil suspension reached a range of different concentrations.

Assessing the alleviating effects of acadesine on BF against the AMPK knockout strain

Acadesine is an AMPK activator. To further study the function of AMPK to resist BF, the alleviating effects of acadesine on BF were compared using wild-type A. oligospora and the AMPK knockout strain △173g192, in which the catalytic α-subunit gene was knocked out. Because the △173g192 strain produces only a few conidia, we assessed the alleviating effects of acadesine on BF by evaluating the mycelial growth rate on TG medium (10 g/L tryptone and 10 g/L glucose). Hypha plugs (0.3 cm in diameter) from the wild-type or mutant strains were inoculated at the center of TG medium located in one compartment of a two-compartment Petri dish (diameter = 9 cm), while benzaldehyde (99% purity), contained in a tampon, was located in another compartment. Acadesine was added to the TG medium of the experimental group, while no acadesine was added to the TG medium of the control group. Three biological replicates were set for each group. The hyphae were cultured at 28°C for 3 days, and the radius of the hyphae was measured and compared.

The disruption vector of gene AOL_s00078g131 was constructed through fragment ligation based on homologous recombination in yeast18,36. The primers 5 f (5’-GTAACGCCAGGGTTTTCCCAGTCACGACGTTCGGACGAGTTCACCAAG-3’) and 5r (5’-ATCCACTTAACGTTACTGAAATCTCCAACTGTGCTACCCAATT-

CAGGAG-3’) were used for PCR amplification of the upstream homologous fragments of AOL_s00078g131. And the primers 3 f (5’-CTCCTTCAATATCATCTTC

TGTCTCCGACTACAACACCTGTCGGGTCTGC-3’) and 3r (5’-GCGGATAACAA

TTTCACACAGGAAACAGCGGAGCACCTACCAGCATTGAC-3’) were used for PCR amplification of the downstream homologous fragments of AOL_s00078g131. The primers hphF (5’-GTCGGAGACAGAAGATGATATTGAAGGAGC-3’) and hphR (5’-GTTGGAGATTTCAGTAACGTTAAGTGGAT-3’) were used to amplify the hygromycin cassette from the vector pCSN44. The upstream and downstream homologous fragments, the hygromycin cassette fragment, and the gapped yeast shuttle vector pRS426 were co-transformed into the yeast strain FY834. They were ligated to produce the disruption vectors pRS426-g78 by the homologous recombination in yeast. The disruption vector pRS426-g78 was extracted from yeast and transformed into E. coli DH5a.

Using the plasmid pRS426-g78 as a template, the DNA fragment was PCR-amplified with the primers 5 f and 3r, and purified through DNA gel recovery. The resulted DNA fragment was then transformed into the protoplast of A. oligospora, and the resulted protoplast was cultured on PDASS medium containing 200 μg/ml hygromycin B. After the hygromycin-resistant transformants grew on hygromycin resistance plates, they were selected and used to identify double crossing-over homologous recombinants by PCR (Supplementary Fig. S4). The primers 78 F (5’-CCAAACATCCCATTCTAACG-3’) and 78 R (5’-CTATTTCCTGTTCCTGCCTTC-3’) were used to identify the double crossover mutants of genes AOL_s00078g131. The positive transformants were subcultured for five generations and confirmed by PCR.

Analysis of gene transcription by real-time qPCR

To confirm the RNAseq data by qPCR, the total RNA was extracted from the same batch of conidia used for transcriptome determination (fresh conidia, germinated conidia, and fungistatic conidia) using the SteadyPure Plant RNA Extraction Kit (Accurate Biotechnology, China). After RNA quality analysis by using an Ultramicro-spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Lite, Thermo Scientific, USA), the RNA is converted into cDNA using the FastKing RT Kit (Tiangen, China). The cDNA was used as the amplification template for real-time qPCR. The gene AOL_s00006g327, encoding 3,5-bisphosphate phosphatidylinositol 5-phosphatase, had similar transcriptional levels in the transcriptomic data of three conidia samples, and was used as the reference gene. Several downregulated or upregulated genes in the fungistatic conidia were selected for qPCR confirmation. The CDS sequences of these genes were downloaded from NCBI for primer design (Supplementary Table S1), and primer specificity was verified using melt curves. qPCR was performed following the procedure: pre-denaturation at 95°C for 2 min, followed by 40 amplification cycles (95°C for 30 s, 50°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 15 s), and all experiments were performed with three technical replicates. Using the cycle threshold (Ct) data, the relative expression levels of genes were calculated by the 2-ΔCT method. The resulted values of gene relative expression levels in fresh, germinated, and fungistatic conidia were compared to those in fresh conidia, and the fold change values were obtained.

Statistics and reproducibility

In this study, every experiment group contained three biological replicates. The significance of the differences between groups was analyzed using software GraphPad Prism 8.0.1 (GraphPad Software, China). First, a two-way ANOVA was performed to test if treatments affect the outcome, and the testing results were provided in supplementary materials (Supplementary Table S2). Second, multiple comparisons were performed, and the Tukey correction for multiple comparisons was used. If there was only one factor in the experiment, one-way ANOVA with post hoc Tukey HSD test was performed using the software SPSS 16.0 (SPSS lnc., Chicago, IL, USA).
