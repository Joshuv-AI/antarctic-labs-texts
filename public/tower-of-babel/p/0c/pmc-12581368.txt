NPRL2 gene expression as a predictor of biochemical recurrence after robot-assisted radical prostatectomy
Authors: Sedat Yahsi, Pınar Öksüz, Kazım Ceviz, Cavit Ceylan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the potential prognostic role of nitrogen permease regulator-like 2 (NPRL2) gene expression in predicting biochemical recurrence (BCR) following robot-assisted radical prostatectomy (RARP).Materials and methodsThis single-center retrospective study included patients who underwent RARP performed by three different surgeons. Clinicopathological data and NPRL2 expression in tumor tissues were evaluated immunohistochemically (IHC). The level of NPRL2 staining was quantitatively assessed using an IHC score based on staining intensity. Factors associated with BCR were analyzed using multivariable logistic regression.ResultsA preoperative PSA level > 10 ng/mL (OR: 4.10, 95% CI: 1.38–12.20, p = 0.011), an International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) grade group > 3 (OR: 4.39, 95% CI: 1.45–13.29, p = 0.009), and high NPRL2 expression (IHC score > 4) (OR: 3.82, 95% CI: 1.07–13.62, p = 0.039) were identified as independent risk factors for BCR.ConclusionHigh NPRL2 expression is associated with an increased risk of BCR following RARP. These findings suggest a potential prognostic value of NPRL2; however, further validation studies are needed due to the small sample size and the subjective nature of the assessment.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12894-025-01820-9.

Introduction

Prostate cancer is one of the most common malignancies among men, and radical prostatectomy is widely preferred for the treatment of localized disease. Due to its minimally invasive advantages, robot-assisted radical prostatectomy (RARP) has become the standard surgical approach in recent years [1, 2]. However, despite curative treatment, approximately 20–40% of patients experience biochemical recurrence (BCR) [3, 4].

BCR is defined as a prostate-specific antigen (PSA) level of ≥ 0.2 ng/mL detected in two consecutive measurements. Although it does not always indicate clinical recurrence, it is considered an important marker for metastatic disease and cancer-specific mortality [5]. In the literature, classical prognostic parameters such as initial PSA level, Gleason score, surgical margin status, and tumor stage have been shown to be associated with BCR [5, 6]. However, nomograms and risk classification systems developed based on these factors may fail to accurately predict recurrence risk in some patients and can lead to heterogeneous outcomes. This uncertainty in clinical decision-making highlights the need for novel molecular biomarkers.

NPRL2 is a tumor suppressor involved in DNA repair, cell cycle regulation, and apoptosis mechanisms [7]. Decreased NPRL2 expression has been associated with tumor progression in lung, breast, liver, and brain cancers [8–12]. On the other hand, there are only a limited number of studies investigating the relationship between NPRL2 expression and disease progression in prostate cancer. Current evidence suggests that NPRL2 may play a role in the biology of prostate cancer, particularly through its influence on the mTORC1 pathway [13–15].

Although NPRL2 has been investigated primarily at the gene level in preclinical studies, this study specifically evaluates NPRL2 protein expression using immunohistochemistry (IHC), which may be more practical for clinical applications.

This study aimed to investigate whether NPRL2 protein expression (assessed via IHC) is associated with biochemical recurrence in patients undergoing RARP and to evaluate the potential prognostic value of NPRL2.

Materials and methods

This retrospective cohort study was conducted among patients who underwent RARP at our center between February 1, 2022, and October 31, 2022. Hospital records were reviewed retrospectively, and 75 patients were identified. Three patients who had received neoadjuvant/adjuvant hormonal therapy or radiotherapy, four patients with insufficient follow-up data, and two patients without appropriate tissue samples for NPRL2 IHC analysis were excluded from the study. All patients were followed postoperatively with PSA monitoring every three months. BCR was defined as two consecutive PSA measurements ≥ 0.2 ng/mL [3]. Based on this criterion, patients were divided into two groups: BCR (+) and BCR (–). The BCR (–) group served as the statistical control group for comparison purposes.

Clinical and pathological data collection

The following data were collected for all patients: age, baseline PSA level, D’Amico risk classification according to biopsy pathology, and radical prostatectomy pathology findings, including pathological T stage (pT), ISUP grade group, presence of lymph node metastasis (LNmet), extracapsular extension, perineural invasion, lymphovascular invasion, seminal vesicle invasion, and surgical margin status (R stage). The R stage (positive/negative surgical margin) was included in the analyses.

Due to the retrospective nature of the pathology reports, detailed features such as extent of positive surgical margins or ISUP grade at the margin could not be consistently evaluated and were therefore not included in the analysis.

NPRL2 immunohistochemical analysis

All IHC evaluations were performed by a single pathologist (P.O.). Hematoxylin-eosin sections of the included cases were re-examined. A paraffin block containing the most representative tumor area with the highest Gleason score was selected according to the 2014 ISUP consensus [16, 17]. For IHC analysis, the NPRL2 antibody (PA6-116091: 1 mg/mL; Invitrogen, NPRL2 Polyclonal Antibody, Rockford, IL, USA) was used. The antibody concentration (1:200) and staining conditions were optimized based on previous studies evaluating NPRL2 expression in various malignancies and preliminary staining tests performed in our laboratory [13].

Due to the heterogeneous NPRL2 staining in tumor tissue, 10 different fields were evaluated, and the mean score was calculated. NPRL2 expression was assessed using an IHC score derived from the product of staining percentage and staining intensity. The scoring for staining percentage was as follows: 0% (0 points), 1–25% (1 point), 26–50% (2 points), 51–75% (3 points), and 76–100% (4 points). Staining intensity was scored as: no staining (0 points), weak (1 point), moderate (2 points), and strong (3 points) (Fig. 1). The IHC score was calculated by multiplying the percentage score by the intensity score. The IHC score was then categorized into four groups: 0 (–), 0–4 (+), 4–8 (++), and 8–12 (+++). Since the semi-quantitative IHC scoring method inherently involves some variability, patients were grouped into two categories for statistical analysis:–/+ (0–4 points) and ++/+++ (5–12 points), and their association with BCR was evaluated.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 24 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Non-normally distributed data were presented as median (minimum–maximum) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, and comparisons were made using the Chi-square test.

To identify factors associated with BCR, univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed. Variables with a p-value < 0.10 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate model. The dependent variable was the presence of BCR (yes/no). Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF); variables with VIF > 10 were excluded from the model. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Biochemical recurrence-free survival (BRFS) was analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method. Survival tables showing the number of patients at risk were generated, and group comparisons were made using the log-rank test.

There was no significant difference in age between the two groups (BCR–: 67.2 ± 6.0 years; BCR+: 67.7 ± 6.5 years, p = 0.624). However, the baseline PSA level was significantly higher in the BCR + group (BCR–: 16.0 ± 23.6 ng/mL; BCR+: 18.0 ± 14.2 ng/mL, p = 0.043).

Several parameters, including pT stage, ISUP grade, seminal vesicle invasion, and lymphovascular invasion, were significantly higher in the BCR + group (p < 0.05, Table 1).

Positive surgical margins were observed in 11 patients (36.6%) in the BCR – group and 19 patients (52.8%) in the BCR + group. Although margin positivity was numerically higher in the BCR + group, the difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.167). Due to the retrospective design, the extent and ISUP grade of the margins could not be reliably assessed and were not included in the analysis. Gleason scores (ISUP grades) were available for all patients based on radical prostatectomy specimens, and these were used in all comparative and regression analyses.

Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated that high NPRL2 expression was associated with shorter BRFS (p = 0.02, Log-rank test) (Fig. 2). The number of recurrence-free patients and log-rank test results were provided in the figure legend.

Discussion

The primary rationale for post-treatment surveillance in prostate cancer is the early detection of potential recurrences. In clinical practice, nomograms based on preoperative variables have been developed to predict biochemical recurrence (BCR) [18]. These tools provide risk estimations solely based on preoperative factors. Postoperative pathological features such as ISUP score, pT stage, surgical margin status, extraprostatic extension, lymph node metastasis (LNmet), and cribriform morphology have also been identified as BCR risk factors [6, 17]. In this study, the association between NPRL2 gene expression and BCR in patients who underwent robot-assisted radical prostatectomy (RARP) for prostate cancer was evaluated and compared with conventional pathological parameters.

Previous studies have reported that pathological stage pT3 or higher is a strong indicator of BCR [7, 19]. Although a significant difference in pT stage was observed between BCR + and BCR − groups in this study, it was not identified as an independent risk factor in multivariate analysis (p > 0.05). The ISUP grade group is a well-known predictor in D’Amico risk classification. Here, an ISUP grade > 3 was associated with a 4.3-fold increased risk of BCR (p < 0.05). Some nomograms aim to enhance predictive accuracy by including pathological features such as the proportion of Gleason pattern 4 and/or 5 and intraductal carcinoma [20]. A similar study from our clinic also demonstrated that cribriform architecture was an independent predictor of BCR and should be included in predictive models [18]. However, due to the limited number of patients in the current study and the retrospective nature of pathology reports, detailed pathological patterns such as Gleason pattern ratios or cribriform morphology could not be consistently evaluated.

Positive surgical margins are known to be a risk factor for BCR, particularly when associated with high Gleason scores or extensive margin involvement [17]. In this study, no significant association was found between positive surgical margins and BCR. However, it should be noted that surgical specimens were evaluated retrospectively by different pathologists, and details regarding margin extent and ISUP grade at the margin were not systematically reported. This may have limited our ability to detect the true impact of margin positivity.

LNmet is a well-established prognostic marker for BCR in the literature. According to the European Association of Urology (EAU) guidelines, adjuvant therapy is recommended for patients with N1 disease [21]. However, selected patients with postoperative PSA < 0.1 ng/mL and ≤ 2 positive lymph nodes may be suitable for active surveillance. In this study, patients with LNmet were those who did not receive adjuvant treatment and were considered appropriate for follow-up. Although LNmet was more frequent in the BCR + group, the difference was not statistically significant, possibly due to the limited sample size and short follow-up period.

Initially identified as a tumor suppressor gene, NPRL2 has demonstrated such a role in various cancers [13, 14]; however, some in vitro studies have shown that NPRL2 may also act as a tumor promoter in cancer cell lines [15]. NPRL2 may exert different effects by binding to distinct target proteins in different cell types [15, 22]. It has been suggested that inactive mTORC1 can activate autophagy under stress conditions such as chemotherapy or radiotherapy, thereby promoting survival of prostate cancer cells [23].

To date, studies have particularly emphasized the adverse impact of NPRL2 expression on systemic therapy in castration-resistant metastatic prostate cancer. NPRL2 has been shown to reduce the efficacy of PARP inhibitors (such as olaparib and niraparib) and taxane-based chemotherapy (such as docetaxel) [22, 24]. Furthermore, a study conducted in non-metastatic patients found significantly higher NPRL2 expression in tumor tissue compared to non-tumor prostate tissue, which was associated with higher pT stage, LNmet, and shorter survival [25]. Consistently, our study showed that high NPRL2 expression (via IHC scoring) was associated with an increased risk of BCR.

Although the term “gene expression” is used in the title, it refers to protein expression determined through immunohistochemical methods. This should be considered a limitation in terms of terminology accuracy. Another limitation is that the IHC evaluation was performed by a single pathologist, and no blinded or repeated scoring was conducted to assess intraobserver or interobserver variability. This introduces the potential for subjective bias in NPRL2 scoring.

Among new prostate cancer biomarkers, the Decipher test is a genomic classifier including 22 genes, designed to provide better insights into diagnosis and prognosis. It can be applied to both biopsy and radical prostatectomy specimens and demonstrates superior AUC values in predicting BCR or metastasis compared to traditional risk classifications. Unlike NPRL2, which evaluates a single gene, the Decipher test covers 22 genes, potentially offering more comprehensive information [26, 27]. While no direct comparison has been made between NPRL2 and Decipher, NPRL2 could be considered a cost-effective, single-gene-based alternative. Nevertheless, NPRL2 may present as a cost-effective and accessible alternative to multigene panels, particularly in resource-limited settings.

Another point of interest is whether NPRL2 can be detected in non-invasive samples such as serum or urine. Although this study focused solely on tissue-based IHC analysis, future research should explore the feasibility of NPRL2 measurement in body fluids, which could enable its use as a non-invasive biomarker for recurrence surveillance.

Considering its potential prognostic value, integration of NPRL2 into clinical practice may strengthen BCR prediction models. Although NPRL2 was identified as an independent predictor in this study, its predictive power should be further assessed in comparison with PSA and ISUP scores. Longer-term follow-up is particularly important, as the current mean follow-up duration (24 months) may not fully capture late recurrences.

A major limitation of this study is the absence of a formal power analysis. Due to the retrospective nature and the fixed number of eligible patients, it was not feasible to perform either an a priori or post-hoc power calculation. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution, and validation in larger prospective cohorts is necessary.

In summary, while the results are promising, further multicenter studies with longer follow-up and standardized, preferably blinded, IHC scoring protocols are required to determine whether NPRL2 can be routinely incorporated into risk stratification and treatment planning for prostate cancer patients.

This study demonstrates that NPRL2 protein expression, as assessed by immunohistochemistry, is significantly associated with biochemical recurrence (BCR) following robot-assisted radical prostatectomy (RARP). High NPRL2 expression was identified as an independent risk factor for BCR, alongside elevated preoperative PSA and high ISUP grade. These findings suggest that NPRL2 may serve as a valuable prognostic biomarker in prostate cancer.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. No formal power analysis was performed due to the retrospective design and fixed sample size, which may affect the strength and generalizability of the results. Additionally, immunohistochemical evaluation was performed by a single pathologist without repeated or blinded assessment, introducing the possibility of subjective interpretation bias.

The relatively short mean follow-up period (24 months) may also have limited detection of late biochemical recurrences. Despite these limitations, the consistent association between high NPRL2 expression and BCR supports its potential role in improving current prognostic models.

Future studies should focus on validating NPRL2’s prognostic value in prospective, multicenter cohorts with longer follow-up. It would also be beneficial to explore whether NPRL2 can be measured in serum or urine samples to enable non-invasive risk assessment.

In conclusion, NPRL2 holds promise as a cost-effective and accessible biomarker for predicting BCR in prostate cancer patients undergoing RARP, but further validation is required before it can be adopted in routine clinical practice.
