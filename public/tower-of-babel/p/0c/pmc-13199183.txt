Causal relationships between insomnia and blood metabolome: a Mendelian randomization study with mouse experimental validation
Authors: Hsien-Yu Fan, Cheng-Chia Hsieh, Tsung-Po Chen, Po-Jui Lai, Hsin-Yin Hsu, Chiu-Li Yeh, Changwei W Wu, Kuo-Liong Chien, Hui-Yu Huang, Yu-Tang Tung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe two-way relationship between metabolites and insomnia is not yet fully understood.ObjectiveWe investigated the bidirectional relationship between metabolites and insomnia using human genome-wide association study and mouse models to identify potential biomarkers.MethodsWe analyzed data from two large genome-wide association studies: one on insomnia (336,965 participants) and one on metabolites (7,824 participants). Genetic variants related to metabolites and insomnia were examined. In the animal study, 12 mice were divided into control and chronic sleep deprivation groups (n = 6 each). After 32 days, blood and fecal metabolites were analyzed.ResultsHigher oleate levels were linked to lower insomnia risk (β: -5.99, p = 0.01), but insomnia did not alter oleate levels. Insomnia increased glucose (β: 0.18, p = 0.002), mannose (β: 0.11, p = 0.02), and influenced six lipids and four amino acids. In mice, sleep deprivation lowered corticosterone (p = 0.03), raised glucose and mannose (p = 0.001), and reduced blood (p = 0.007) and fecal oleate (p = 0.05). Fecal 4-aminobutyric acid also decreased (p = 0.01).ConclusionsInsomnia and metabolites exhibited a bidirectional relationship, with notable metabolic and hormonal changes in the animal model. Introduction

Insomnia, a common sleep disorder characterized by difficulty falling or staying asleep, has been observed in relation to various blood metabolites. Chronic insomnia was widespread, with around 10% of adults having an insomnia disorder and another 20% experiencing insomnia symptoms (Morin & Jarrin, 2022). A recent study has identified several blood metabolites linked to sleep-related disorders, particularly focusing on how changes in these metabolites might correlate with sleep-related disorders (Lv et al., 2024). For example, fatty acids like α-linolenic acid have been found to influence sleep duration (Liu & Shan, 2022). Another human genetic study identified specific metabolites that affected insomnia, including 2-hydroxypalmitate, methyl-cysteine, and salicylate (Lv et al., 2024). Understanding these metabolic links provides an opportunity for primary prevention, where interventions aimed at maintaining or altering these metabolite levels could potentially reduce the incidence of insomnia in the general population. Additionally, animal studies suggest that dietary factors, such as high-fructose intake, may disrupt sleep and alter metabolic indicators (Byun et al., 2018; Franco-Pérez et al., 2018), indicating that nutritional interventions could serve as a preventive strategy against sleep disturbances.

In contrast, studies have established connections between sleep disturbances and specific blood metabolites, including amino acids and fatty acids (Lv et al., 2024), which are associated with conditions like sleep-related disorders. For example, sleep deprivation has been shown to alter metabolites involved in glucose metabolism and stress hormones such as cortisol and adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) (Hirotsu et al., 2015). Early identification of these metabolic changes could facilitate secondary prevention efforts, allowing for the early detection and management of sleep disorders before they progress into more severe conditions. Additionally, sleep timing has been connected with metabolic pathways, including γ-glutamyl amino acid metabolism, where metabolites like erythrulose and circulating 3-carboxy-4-methyl-5-propyl-2-furanpropanoic acid (CMPF) exhibited significant associations (Gordon-Dseagu et al., 2019). By monitoring these biomarkers, healthcare providers could intervene earlier in patients with sleep disturbances, potentially preventing the escalation of metabolic and sleep-related disorders.

Observational studies may face challenges such as confounding bias, and randomizing sleep-related disorders or metabolite levels in clinical settings is often not ethical or feasible. To address these challenges, we used genetic instrumental variables (IVs) derived from human genome-wide association studies (GWAS) to perform a bidirectional analysis (Bowden et al., 2015; Burgess et al., 2013, 2015; Lawlor et al., 2008; Smith & Ebrahim, 2003). By using genetic variants as IVs, we explored the causal relationships between insomnia and blood metabolites. This approach allowed us to assess the potential for primary prevention by identifying metabolites that influence the risk of insomnia, which could inform early interventions to prevent sleep disorders. Conversely, we also investigated whether insomnia itself causally affects various human blood metabolites, which may provide insights into secondary prevention strategies. By integrating human genetic data with animal model results, our study aimed to gain a deeper understanding of how sleep-related disorders affect metabolic health and to potentially identify biomarkers or therapeutic targets for sleep-related disorders, ultimately contributing to more effective public health interventions.

Methods

Human blood metabolomes

Study design

This study aimed to unravel the bidirectional causal links between insomnia and blood metabolites by leveraging genetic IVs from human GWAS. The genetic instruments were selected to satisfy the critical assumptions of relevance, independence from confounders, and exclusion restriction (Glymour et al., 2012; Lousdal, 2018). We first investigated whether specific blood metabolites could influence the risk of developing insomnia and then examined whether insomnia, in turn, could alter blood metabolite levels. Data were sourced from two extensive human GWAS: one dedicated to insomnia, and the other to blood metabolites. The genetic variants associated with metabolites were derived from a GWAS involving European participants (Shin et al., 2014), while those related to insomnia were drawn from a multi-ancestry meta-analysis involving European participants. Ethics approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Taipei Medical University (approval number: N202405131).

Human GWAS summary datasets

For the metabolite data (Table S1), datasets from two European human studies were used: the Kooperative Gesundheitsforschung in der Region Augsburg (KORA) and the TwinsUK cohort (Shin et al., 2014). The KORA dataset included 1,768 participants aged 32 to 77 years, while the TwinsUK dataset comprised 6,054 participants aged 18 to 102 years, resulting in a combined sample size of 7,821 individuals. The analysis covered 2,545,661 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) based on the HG19/GRCh37 build, with metabolite concentrations measured in log10 units. Of the 529 identified metabolites, 452 met quality control criteria and were included in the analysis. Detailed GWAS methods were described in publications by Wichmann et al. (Wichmann et al., 2005), Moayyeri et al. (Moayyeri et al., 2013), and Shin et al. (Shin et al., 2014). The insomnia-related genetic data were obtained from the Neale Lab, specifically identified as GWAS ID: ukb-a-13 (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/datasets/ukb-a-13/) (Jiang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). This dataset, published in 2017, includes 336,965 individuals of European descent and contains 10,894,596 SNPs, based on the HG19/GRCh37 genome build.

Bidirectional causal analysis

Circulating metabolite levels were measured using relative quantification and were standardized prior to genetic association analyses. Various SNPs associated with metabolites were used as IVs to explore their causal effect on insomnia. The number of SNPs for each metabolite ranged from 1 to 18 (Table S2). Statistical values () were extracted and compared with () from the insomnia GWAS. Causal effects were estimated using the ratio method. This approach relied on the key assumptions: Relevance (SNPs were strongly associated with the metabolites), Independence from confounders (assuming SNPs were not affected by confounding factors), and Exclusion restriction (assuming SNPs affected insomnia solely through the metabolites). Although these assumptions were theoretical and cannot be directly tested, they are essential for drawing causal inferences within this methodological framework.

Similarly, we then selected 21 SNPs related to insomnia as instrumental variables and extracted the statistical values for each SNP () (Table S3). These SNPs were then used to obtain corresponding effect estimates () from the metabolite GWAS. We used the ratio method to estimate causal effects, where indicateed the effect of SNPs on metabolites and indicated the effect of SNPs on insomnia. In this context, the assumptions of relevance (SNPs being strongly associated with insomnia), Independence from confounders (assuming no confounding variables affecting both insomnia and metabolites), and exclusion restriction (assuming SNPs influenced metabolites only through insomnia) were considered. While these assumptions are theoretical and cannot be directly validated, they were critical for making causal inferences using this approach. Candidate SNPs were considered using the genome-wide significance threshold (p < 5 × 10⁻⁸), which reflected Bonferroni correction for multiple testing across the genome. When SNPs from the exposure dataset were unavailable in the outcome dataset, proxy SNPs were identified following linkage disequilibrium clumping using the TwoSampleMR package (window size = 10,000 kb; r² < 0.001). For single SNP instruments, the Wald ratio method was used (Smith & Ebrahim, 2003). When two or more SNPs were available, the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method was used as the major MR analysis (Burgess et al., 2013). For analyses involving three or more SNPs, additional MR methods, including weighted median, mode-based estimators, and MR-Egger, were performed as sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of the findings.(Bowden et al., 2015; Hartwig et al., 2017) These methods were chosen to address potential violations of the key instrumental variable assumptions—relevance, independence from confounders, and exclusion restriction—thereby enhancing the robustness and reliability of our bidirectional assessments between insomnia and blood metabolites. The statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1, with the TwoSampleMR package employed for bidirectional analysis, and the metafor and dplyr packages used for data manipulation.

Animals and procedures

Study design

C57BL/6J male mice, 5 weeks old, were obtained from the National Laboratory Animal Center, Taipei, Taiwan. They were accommodated at the Laboratory Animal Center of National Chung Hsing University, maintained in controlled conditions with a 12-hour light/dark cycle, temperatures at 20 ± 2 °C, and relative humidity around 50 ± 10%. The mice were fed a standard lab diet and given unlimited access to water. Prior to the start of chronic sleep deprivation (CSD) experiments, the mice were allowed a week for acclimation to their new conditions. A computer-generated random number sequence was used to allocate the mice into their respective groups. For the study, 12 mice were divided evenly into two groups: control (CN) and sleep deprivation (SD), with 6 mice in each. ach animal was treated as a single experimental unit. After the completion of 32 days, fecal samples were collected immediately. At the end of the experiment, the mice were humanely euthanized to collect blood. Blood samples were collected through retro-orbital bleeding using heparinized microhematocrit capillary tubes. These samples were then centrifuged at 4 °C with a force of 3000g for 15 min. Following centrifugation, the serum was preserved at − 80 °C for metabolite analysis. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with ethical guidelines and were approved by the Ethics Committee of National Chung Hsing University (approval number: 111–150R).

Sleep deprivation

In the present study, sleep was disrupted in 32 day-CSD experiments using a device from Lafayette Instrument Company, Lafayette, IN, USA. The detailed procedures and device specifications were adapted from previously published methods with slight modifications. (Cabrera-Aguilera et al., 2019) The disruption mechanism involved a nearly silent mechanical motor equipped with a horizontal bar that swept just above the floor of a standard mouse cage, moving from one side to the other. This automatic system operated without human involvement, minimizing stress to the animals. Disruptions occurred at 4 times per minute from 1 p.m. to 9 a.m. (totaling 20 h), and this schedule was maintained over a continuous 32-day period. The detailed methods were described in SUPPLEMENTAL METHODS.

Statistical analyses

The area under the curve (AUC) for each metabolite was calculated to quantify overall exposure levels. The differences in AUC between the SD and CN groups were estimated by using the Mann–Whitney U test. All results were showed as the mean ± standard error of the mean. P-values < 0.05 denoted statistically significant differences.

Seven well-named metabolites were identified as being associated with the risk of insomnia, including 1 amino acid, 4 lipids, and 1 energy metabolite (Table 1). Additionally, three unnamed metabolites were identified and are listed in Table S4. Higher levels of N-acetylalanine (amino acid) were positively associated with insomnia risk (β: 12.5; 95% CI: 0.4 to 152.1; p = 0.04). Among xenobiotics, pro-hydroxy-pro also showed a positive association with insomnia risk (β: 7.4; 95% CI: 1.9 to 40.8; p = 0.008). Genetical associations of insomnia with metabolite levels based on Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis in a human study

Genetically predicted metabolite levels were associated with insomnia risk based on MR analysis (Fig. 2). Fifteen well-named metabolites affected by insomnia were identified, including 5 amino acids, 3 carbohydrates, and 7 lipids (Table 2). Additionally, fifteen unnamed metabolites were identified and are listed in Table S4. Among the amino acids, 4-hydroxyhippurate showed a significant decrease (β = −0.40, standard error [SE] = 0.17, P = 0.02), N-acetylalanine also decreased (β = −0.07, SE = 0.03, P = 0.02), and pyroglutamine exhibited a notable reduction (β = −0.25, SE = 0.08, P = 0.002). Blood metabolites in a sleep deprivation animal model

Since our human study found that insomnia was associated with the levels of 6 lipids, 4 amino acids, and 3 carbohydrates, we specifically analyzed these target metabolites in the animal model. As shown in Fig. 3, metabolite analysis of the serum indicated that the AUC for glucose and mannose in the serum of the SD group was significantly higher compared to the CN group (p = 0.001). The AUC for oleic acid in the serum of the SD group was also significantly lower compared to the CN group (p = 0.008). Additionally, the AUC for corticosterone in the serum of the SD group was significantly lower compared to the CN group (p = 0.03). In addition to metabolites showing concordant directional changes with the human findings, the animal experiments identified a broader range of metabolic alterations (Table S10). These included changes in steroid and hormone metabolites (such as cortisol 21-acetic acid, deoxycorticosterone acetate, androsterone, and canrenone), nucleosides and nucleotides (including inosine, guanosine, thymidine, deoxycytidine, and deoxyuridine monophosphate), amino acids and polyamines (including phenylalanine, putrescine, and glutamine), as well as xenobiotics and drug-related compounds (including oseltamivir phosphate, chlorpromazine, and valproic acid).

Discussions

Main findings

In the human study, we observed that the relationship between metabolites and insomnia was bidirectional. The levels of seven metabolites (primarily lipids) were associated with the risk of insomnia. In contrast, insomnia may affect the levels of 6 lipids, 4 amino acids, and 3 carbohydrates. In the animal model, sleep deprivation led to lower blood corticosterone levels, while glucose and mannose levels in blood were higher. Additionally, oleic acid levels in both blood and feces were lower, and the concentration of 4-aminobutyric acid in feces decreased.

Compared with previous studies

Previous studies showed the association between dietary macronutrients and sleep disturbances, revealing that low intake of protein was associated with difficulties in initiating sleep and poor sleep quality, while high intake of protein was linked to challenges in maintaining sleep (Tanaka et al., 2013). Low intake of carbohydrate was also associated with sleep maintenance issues (Tanaka et al., 2013). However, these studies did not delve into specific metabolites influencing the risk of insomnia. Our study built on this existing research by investigating the association between blood metabolite levels and insomnia, focusing on how these metabolites are linked to the condition. Recognizing the possibility of a bidirectional relationship, we also explored the pathway from insomnia to metabolites. These findings were then compared to previous studies based on the type of macronutrient: carbohydrates, amino acids, and lipids. In terms of carbohydrate metabolites, our study found that fructose levels were negatively affected by insomnia, whereas glucose and mannose levels were positively affected, suggesting that sleep quality may influence different sugars. However, we did not identify any sugar that could predict the risk of insomnia, and evidence remains limited. An animal study found that a high-fructose diet altered sleep patterns, increasing wakefulness and decreasing non-rapid eye movement sleep, along with changes in metabolic indicators (Franco-Pérez et al., 2018). Among amino acids, only N-acetylalanine was associated with insomnia risk, with higher levels correlating to increased risk. A randomized controlled trial indicated that GABA from unpolished rice improved both subjective and objective sleep quality, (Byun et al., 2018) while tyrosine supplementation enhanced cognitive functions following sleep deprivation (Magill et al., 2003). Interestingly, unlike our findings in adults, where specific amino acids showed distinct associations with insomnia, previous studies demonstrated that higher levels of branched-chain amino acids in children were linked to shorter sleep duration, delayed sleep timing, and more frequent reports of trouble sleeping (Matricciani et al., 2023). Our study also found that two unsaturated fatty acids (oleate and 10-nonadecenoate) and two saturated fatty acids (2-stearoylglycerophosphocholine and 1-stearoylglycerol) were negatively associated with the risk of insomnia. Interestingly, hyodeoxycholate was associated with a reduced risk of insomnia, suggesting a protective effect. In contrast, National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data suggested that higher intake of α-linolenic acid was linked to very short sleep durations (Liu & Shan, 2022), and another NHANES study identified a positive association between omega-6 fatty acid consumption and the omega-6:omega-3 ratio with sleep disorders, with omega-3 fatty acids negatively impacting sleep quality in men only (Luo et al., 2021). Additionally, the Kuopio Ischaemic Heart Disease cohort study showed that higher serum linoleate levels were associated with lower odds of long sleep durations (Murphy et al., 2022).

Previous studies have shown that sleep deprivation may alter blood metabolites, particularly those involved in glucose metabolism (Hirotsu et al., 2015; Yi et al., 2023) and stress hormones such as cortisol and ACTH (Hirotsu et al., 2015). Some studies had further demonstrated associations between sleep timing and specific metabolic pathways, such as γ-glutamyl amino acid metabolism, with significant links to various metabolites, including erythrulose and CMPF.(Gordon-Dseagu et al., 2019) We also found that several amino acids—4-hydroxyhippurate, N-acetylalanine, pyroglutamine, and threonine—were negatively affected by insomnia, while serine was positively affected. Poor sleep efficiency and later bedtime routines have been reported to be associated with more pronounced postprandial glycaemic responses to breakfast the following morning (Tsereteli et al., 2022). Additionally, we observed that higher levels of omega-6-related lipids—including 1-arachidonoylglycerophosphoinositol, 1-linoleoylglycerol, adrenate, dihomo-linoleate, and linoleate—were affected by insomnia, highlighting the complex role of omega-6 fatty acids in sleep regulation. Furthermore, 5-Dodecenoate (12:1n7) was also identified, suggesting additional metabolic pathways may be involved in the interplay between sleep and metabolism. Additionally, insomnia was associated with significantly lower levels of Hyodeoxycholate, indicating a potential alteration in bile acid metabolism in response to sleep disturbances.

Possible mechanisms

The bidirectional relationship between insomnia and blood metabolites was underpinned by complex biological mechanisms. Insomnia, a condition characterized by persistent difficulty in initiating or maintaining sleep, may lead to various metabolic disturbances. One of the primary mechanisms involves the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, which regulates the stress response (Vgontzas et al., 2022). Chronic insomnia has been shown to alter the HPA axis function, resulting in dysregulated cortisol secretion (Vargas et al., 2018). Our study found that long-term sleep deprivation led to significantly lower blood corticosterone levels in mice, suggesting that chronic insomnia may blunt the HPA axis response, which could, in turn, impact metabolic processes.

Additionally, sleep deprivation has been linked to alterations in glucose metabolism (Yi et al., 2023). Our findings demonstrated increased levels of glucose and mannose in both blood and feces of sleep-deprived mice, indicating that insomnia might impair insulin sensitivity and glucose homeostasis. This disruption could be mediated by hormonal imbalances such as decreased insulin secretion or increased counter-regulatory hormones like glucagon and adrenaline (Koopman et al., 2020). The elevated glucose levels observed in insomnia could also promote the accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), contributing to oxidative stress and inflammation, further exacerbating metabolic dysregulation (Goldin et al., 2006).

The changes in lipid metabolism observed in our study, particularly the decrease in oleate levels, may be attributed to altered enzymatic activity and fatty acid oxidation due to sleep deprivation (Aho et al., 2016). Lipid metabolism is closely regulated by circadian rhythms, and insomnia can disrupt these rhythms, leading to imbalances in lipid synthesis and breakdown (Petrenko et al., 2023). The decrease in oleate, a monounsaturated fatty acid, might reflect an adaptive response to preserve energy stores under conditions of chronic sleep loss.

Moreover, amino acid metabolism was also affected by insomnia. Our study showed significant alterations in the levels of several amino acids, such as serine, threonine, and N-acetylalanine. These changes could be related to disrupted protein synthesis and degradation pathways, as well as altered neurotransmitter production, given that amino acids are precursors for neurotransmitters involved in sleep regulation.

Clinical and public health implications

The bidirectional relationship between blood metabolites and insomnia observed in this study carries significant clinical and public health implications. Clinically, the identification of specific metabolites associated with insomnia highlights the potential for these molecules to serve as biomarkers for early detection and monitoring of insomnia-related metabolic disturbances. For example, the increased levels of glucose and mannose, coupled with decreased oleic acid levels observed in sleep-deprived mice, suggest these metabolites could reflect the metabolic impact of chronic insomnia, potentially aiding in the diagnosis and personalization of treatment strategies (Humer et al., 2020).

Incorporating sleep assessments into routine clinical practice could be an essential step toward secondary prevention. Screening for sleep disturbances, particularly in patients with metabolic syndrome or type 2 diabetes, should become standard practice, allowing for early intervention in cases where insomnia may exacerbate metabolic dysfunction. Cognitive-behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) has been suggested to improve the quality of sleep and might also help normalize disrupted metabolic profiles associated with sleep deprivation (Trauer et al., 2015). Pharmacological interventions that target neurotransmitter systems involved in both sleep and metabolic regulation could provide dual therapeutic benefits for patients suffering from both insomnia and metabolic disorders.

Moreover, our findings suggest that integrated care approaches may be crucial in managing the interplay between insomnia and metabolic disorders. Multidisciplinary care teams—comprising sleep specialists, endocrinologists, and dietitians—could collaborate to provide comprehensive management plans for patients with co-existing conditions. Routine sleep assessments in patients with metabolic syndrome could lead to early identification of sleep disturbances, facilitating timely and targeted interventions that may prevent further metabolic deterioration.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study was the use of bidirectional two-sample MR. This method may enable the assessment of causal direction, allowing our study to determine whether specific metabolites affect the risk of insomnia or were altered as a consequence of insomnia. In addition, the two-sample MR design uses independent datasets for exposure and outcome, which may help reduce bias from reverse causation, confounding, and sample overlap. However, several limitations should be noted. First, after applying the Benjamini–Yekutieli (BY) correction for multiple testing, no robust associations were observed between metabolites and insomnia in either direction. Second, although we applied a conventionalgenome-wide significance threshold (p < 5 × 10⁻⁸), some metabolites had no or insufficient SNPs available as valid instrumental variables, limiting the ability to perform MR analyses. Third, our MR analyses were based on summary-level data rather than individual-level data, which limited our ability to perform more detailed analyses, such as assessing the correlation structure among metabolites. However, to partially address this limitation, we complemented our findings with animal experiments to provide additional biological support.

Conclusion

Our study revealed a bidirectional relationship between metabolites and insomnia, with specific metabolites, particularly lipids, being associated with an increased risk of insomnia. Conversely, insomnia appears to affect the levels of various lipids, amino acids, and carbohydrates. The animal model further supported these findings, demonstrating that sleep deprivation leads to hormonal imbalances, as evidenced by lower blood corticosterone levels and altered glucose and oleic acid levels in both blood and feces.
