Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s 2026 Nutrition Report Presents Data for 131 Biomarkers from the Cross-Sectional National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES 1999–August 2023)☆
Authors: Christine M Pfeiffer, Maya R Sternberg, Carissa D Powers, Cynthia L Ogden, Maria Elena D Jefferds, Krista S Crider, WenYen Juan, Jaime J Gahche, Michael E Rybak, Zia Fazili, Ming Zhang, Ekaterina M Mineva, Hubert W Vesper, Enada Archibold, Jeffery M Jarrett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s ‘National Report on Biochemical Indicators of Diet and Nutrition in the US Population’ provides nutritional status assessment by presenting blood and urine concentrations of diet and nutrition biomarkers in a representative sample of the US population ≥1 y participating in the NHANES. Earlier reports contained information for 27 (1999–2002) and 58 (2003–2006) biomarkers. We aimed to provide an overview of the 2026 report, which covers data for ≤131 biomarkers since 1999, with 50 biomarkers from August 2021 to August 2023. Concentrations over time by cycle are stratified by dietary supplement use, reference interval concentrations focus on tail percentiles, and concentrations grouped by age, sex, and/or race and Hispanic origin demonstrate demographic differences. The report is intended for agencies and organizations involved in developing nutrition policies and monitoring their effects. It provides easily accessible, comprehensive nutritional biomarker data for the US population during the last 24 y.

Introduction

Nationally representative data on the health and nutritional status of a country’s population are of great public health importance: they identify health and nutrition problems, inform the development of public health programs and priorities, and determine their impact and effectiveness. The NHANES collects key data on the nutritional status of the US population, including nutrient intake assessments from foods, beverages, and dietary supplements, and the measurement of nutritional biomarkers. The survey provides comprehensive information about the health of individuals and is the most comprehensive nationally representative survey that collects biological specimens to generate reference information for nutritional biomarkers [[1], [2], [3]].

Given the large volume of NHANES data and the complexity of analysis of NHANES data files, it is important to have access to straightforward and standardized descriptive statistics that allow for comparisons across demographic subgroups and indicators. “What We Eat In America” provides descriptive data tables for nutrient intakes from food/beverages and—since NHANES 2007–2008—dietary supplements [4]. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)’s "National Report on Biochemical Indicators of Diet and Nutrition in the US Population” (CDC Nutrition Report) provides information on nutritional biomarkers. Two earlier publications of the CDC Nutrition Report contained reference information for 27 (NHANES 1999–2002) and 58 (NHANES 2003–2006) biomarkers for which CDC’s Division of Laboratory Sciences at the National Center for Environmental Health conducted the biomarker laboratory analyses as part of the NHANES protocol [5].

The 2026 “CDC Nutrition Report,” to be released in June 2026, covers all publicly available data for ≤131 nutritional biomarkers from the inception of the continuous NHANES in 1999 to the most recent cycle conducted from August 2021 to August 2023, which included 50 biomarkers [6]. The goal of this article is to review the main features of this comprehensive report and to serve as a portal for the extensive report.

Methods

Basic methods information is provided below, with additional information presented in Supplementary Text 1.

Participants, survey design, and laboratory methods

NHANES is a series of cross-sectional surveys of the civilian, noninstitutionalized US population designed to collect data on the health and nutritional status of Americans. In 1999, NHANES became a continuous survey. Participation eligibility starts at birth, and public data are released in 2-y cycles [3]. It includes health interviews and examinations, including detailed information on prescription medications and dietary supplements, and the collection of biological specimens in a mobile examination center (MEC) (blood and urine for participants ≥1 y and ≥3 y, respectively). Information is collected on a variety of health issues through questionnaires (e.g., from allergies to weight history) and through examinations at the MEC (e.g., from audiometry to vision tests) [7]. Interview and examination response rates for each cycle are publicly available [8]. Participants aged ≥18 y consented, parents provided consent for those aged <18 y, and youth aged 7–17 y assented. The National Center for Health Statistics Ethics Review Board approved NHANES [9]. CDC’s Division of Laboratory Sciences analyzed biological specimens for 131 nutritional biomarkers as part of the NHANES protocol (Supplementary Table 1) [10].

Study variables

The biomarkers included in the 2026 report are categorized into 4 main groups: water-soluble, fat-soluble, trace element, and bioactive compounds (Supplementary Table 2), with each row representing biomarker(s) measured by a laboratory method. For most biomarkers, data were not available continuously for the 24-y period, nor were data available for the same age groups across cycles. When the eligible sample population changed across cycles, we used a consistent age range for overall, sex-specific, and race and Hispanic origin-specific estimates for comparability across cycles. We grouped data by age, sex (male and female), race and Hispanic origin categories (Mexican American, non-Hispanic Black, and non-Hispanic White for 1999–2010; all Hispanic, non-Hispanic Asian, non-Hispanic Black, and non-Hispanic White for 2011–2018 and August 2021–August 2023), and supplement use (dichotomized as yes/no based on self-reported consumption of any dietary supplements in the past 30 d).

Statistical analysis

Analyses accounted for the NHANES complex survey design by applying Taylor series linearization to obtain variance estimates that incorporate stratification and clustering features of the design (SAS for Windows, version 9.4, SAS Institute Inc; and SAS-callable SUDAAN version 11.0.4, RTI International). Data from all publicly available 2-y cycles from 1999 to August 2023 were included. NHANES August 2021–August 2023 data available at the time of report generation were included.

Two-year MEC weights were applied to account for unequal probabilities of selection and nonresponse. When applicable, specialized weights were used: fasting weights or subsample weights for biomarkers measured in a random subset of eligible participants (e.g., phytoestrogens, caffeine and metabolites, trans-fatty acids, etc.) or phlebotomy weights for the August 2021–August 2023 cycle due to notable nonresponse differences by age group in the phlebotomy component of the MEC [8].

We reported biomarker concentrations as weighted geometric means with 95% confidence intervals. When the distribution was approximately symmetric (e.g., vitamin C, 25-hydroxyvitamin D, and body iron index), we reported the arithmetic mean. For urine measurements, we presented data for both the measured concentrations and after adjustment for urine flow effects (e.g., creatinine-normalized concentrations for iodine and phytoestrogens or excretion rates for caffeine and metabolites). We calculated percentile estimates using linear interpolation and confidence intervals for percentiles by the Woodruff method [11].

Results

Features of the 2026 CDC Nutrition Report

The 2026 report includes all publicly available data for ≤131 nutritional biomarkers from NHANES 1999 to August 2023, with 50 biomarkers from August 2021–August 2023. This covers 69 fat- and 22 water-soluble vitamins, nutrients, and related compounds, 7 iron indicators and 4 trace elements, 6 phytoestrogens, 15 caffeine and caffeine metabolites, as well as 2 acrylamide adducts (plus the ratio of acrylamide to glycidamide) and 4 trans-fatty acids (plus the sum of the 4), biomarkers typically associated with the consumption of certain highly processed foods. New biomarkers in the 2026 CDC Nutrition Report

The new report includes several biomarkers not available in the earlier “First (NHANES 1999–2002)” or “Second CDC Nutrition Report (NHANES 2003–2006)”: 1 biomarker for vitamin B-12 status (combined indicator cB-12, composed of vitamin B-12, methylmalonic acid, and total homocysteine), 1 biomarker for heart health [omega-3 index, calculated as the sum of eicosapentaenoic acid and docosahexaenoic acid as a percentage of total fatty acids in red blood cells (RBC)], 2 trace elements (serum copper and zinc); 4 trans-fatty acids (plus the sum of the 4); 6 serum folate forms; 6 RBC folate forms (plus RBC total folate); 15 caffeine and caffeine metabolites; and 21 RBC fatty acids.

Types of summary statistics in the 2026 CDC Nutrition Report

The report presents 3 types of summary statistics (Figure 2): concentrations over time by cycle presenting the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentile along with the geometric mean and sample size; reference interval concentrations focusing on tail percentiles (2.5th, 5th, 95th, and 97.5th) after combining 2 cycles (where available) to provide sufficient sample size; and multi-level stratified concentrations after again combining 2 cycles (where available) and presenting concentrations for specific age, sex, and race and Hispanic origin subgroups to allow for a deeper examination of demographic differences. The report contains, for the first time, data stratified by any dietary supplement use as part of the trend tables, showing concentrations over time separately for supplement users and nonusers in addition to the overall population. Discussion

The “2026 CDC Nutrition Report” presents standardized data tables and graphs that facilitate visualization for 131 nutritional biomarkers measured in the US population as part of the nationally representative NHANES from 1999 to August 2023 [6].

Few countries beyond the US operate national nutrition surveys that include nutritional biomarkers as part of their health assessment. Examples are the Australian National Health Measures Survey [12], Canadian Health Measures Survey [13], China Nutrition and Health Survey [14], Guatemala Epidemiological Health and Nutrition Surveillance System [15], Irish National Adult Nutrition Survey [16], Korea National Health and Nutrition Survey [17], New Zealand Adult Nutrition Survey [18], Philippine National Nutrition Survey [19], and UK National Diet and Nutrition Survey [20]. Other surveys that assess biochemical status limit their focus to clinical indicators of disease risk such as blood glucose, hemoglobin A1c, and lipid status [21].

With over 50 y in the field, the US NHANES is the longest-standing and most comprehensive of nationally representative health and nutrition surveys and provides gold standard scientific data suitable for guideline development and to validate advanced data science research. In 1971, NHANES was created by incorporating nutritional surveillance at the national level into the National Health Examination Survey, and 4 nutritional biomarkers were introduced to assess iron and vitamin A status, with an additional 5 biomarkers added in 1976 to assess a few trace elements, more iron status indicators, and vitamin C and folate status [22]. Over time, particularly with the introduction of the continuous NHANES in 1999, the number of nutritional biomarkers increased >10-fold. The availability of such a broad array of nutritional biomarker data is unique in and of itself. The linkage of these data to additional information collected from each NHANES participant adds further value by enabling the examination of associations with sociodemographic, lifestyle, anthropometric, and other biochemical data, as well as information from health conditions, health examinations, and dietary intake. Challenges and lessons learned in generating and interpreting NHANES nutritional biomarker data have been reviewed earlier [1].

The uses of NHANES data are manyfold: they inform on the health and nutritional situation of the population and thus provide the foundation to develop nutrition recommendations and establish policy regulations; they allow policy makers to evaluate the impact of public health programs or assess the need for new interventions; they enable clinicians to develop national reference intervals; and they empower scientists to formulate research hypotheses on the relationship between nutritional status, health status, and lifestyle or behavioral factors.

The “CDC Nutrition Report” provides complex data in a user-friendly format to obtain a quick overview of people’s nutritional status. It presents descriptive data from univariate analysis without adjusting for demographic variables or other influential variables (e.g., dietary intake, supplement use, smoking, and BMI). It provides data users with an overview of the distribution of concentrations grouped by the main demographic variables age, sex, and race and Hispanic origin. Its consistent layout allows comparisons across biomarkers, among demographic subgroups, and over time. The 2026 edition also provides nutritional biomarker data stratified by dietary supplement use to account for this highly influential variable. The ‘CDC Nutrition Report’ is an easily accessible and comprehensive tool presenting nutritional biomarker data for the US population.
