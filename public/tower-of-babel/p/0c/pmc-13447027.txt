Glioblastoma in a Patient With MELAS Syndrome: A Case Report
Authors: Seung Min Chang, Hun Ho Park, Ji Hyun Park, Ji-Hoon Na, Hyunjoo Lee, Young-Mok Lee, Jihwan Yoo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
MELAS (Mitochondrial Encephalomyopathy, Lactic Acidosis, and Stroke-like episodes) syndrome is a multi-systemic genetic disorder characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction, often presenting with diverse clinical phenotypes. We report a rare case of glioblastoma (GBM) in a 60-year-old female whose MELAS manifestations were primarily metabolic and neuro-sensory. Her clinical course began at age 40 with diabetes mellitus, bilateral sensorineural hearing loss, and left ophthalmoplegia. She was diagnosed with MELAS syndrome by molecular genetic testing at age 56 following her son’s diagnosis. Despite the absence of previous stroke-like episodes, she presented with an acute 2-day history of headache, vomiting and altered mental status. Brain MRI revealed a mass lesion, and subsequent gross total resection was performed. Histopathological and molecular analysis confirmed a GBM, IDH-wildtype (CNS WHO grade 4). Despite surgical resection, the patient failed to recover neurologically, remaining in a stuporous state with eye opening but inability to follow commands. She subsequently received the Stupp protocol; however, massive local recurrence with increased relative cerebral blood volume was observed within one month of treatment initiation, indicating rapid and aggressive tumor progression. This case underscores that in MELAS patients, primary brain malignancies can arise independently of typical stroke-like episodes. In addition, the unusually aggressive clinical course and rapid recurrence despite standard Stupp protocol therapy raise the possibility that altered mitochondrial metabolism may influence tumor behavior or treatment response in certain patients.

INTRODUCTION

MELAS (Mitochondrial Encephalomyopathy, Lactic Acidosis, and Stroke-like episodes) syndrome is a rare maternally inherited multi-systemic disorder caused by mutations in mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), most commonly the m.3243A>G mutation in the MT-TL1 gene [12]. This genetic defect leads to impaired mitochondrial protein synthesis and defective oxidative phosphorylation, primarily affecting high-energy demanding organs such as the brain, skeletal muscle, and endocrine system [1]. Clinical manifestations vary widely, ranging from diabetes mellitus and sensorineural hearing loss to acute neurological deficits known as stroke-like episodes.

While the neurological complications of MELAS are well-documented, primary central nervous system malignancies such as glioblastoma (GBM) have rarely been described in affected patients [3]. Recent evidence suggests that mitochondrial dysfunction may play a crucial role in cancer development and progression [4]. Chronic mitochondrial stress leads to the overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can induce genomic instability and activate oncogenic signaling pathways [45]. Despite these theoretical links, clinical reports of GBM arising in the background of MELAS are scarce, and the clinicopathological characteristics of such cases remain poorly understood.

In this report, we describe, to our knowledge, the first reported case of IDH-wildtype GBM (CNS WHO grade 4) arising in a 60-year-old woman with genetically confirmed MELAS syndrome. Notably, the patient’s MELAS manifestations were characterized predominantly by metabolic and neuro-sensory abnormalities—including diabetes mellitus, sensorineural hearing loss, and ophthalmoplegia—without prior stroke-like episodes. This case provides an opportunity to describe the unusually aggressive clinical course and treatment response of IDH-wildtype GBM arising in a patient with MELAS syndrome, while also highlighting the potential relevance of mitochondrial dysfunction to tumor behavior and therapeutic response.

CASE REPORT

A 60-year-old female presented to the emergency department with a 2-day history of progressive headache, nausea, vomiting, and altered mental status. On admission, her Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score was E4V4M6, and she demonstrated decreased responsiveness without definite focal motor weakness. Her medical history was significant for multi-systemic complications associated with mitochondrial dysfunction. At age 40, she was diagnosed with diabetes mellitus and had since been managed with insulin therapy. Over the following years, she developed progressive bilateral sensorineural hearing loss and chronic left ophthalmoplegia. At age 56, she was formally diagnosed with MELAS syndrome via genetic testing following her son’s diagnosis. Genetic analysis revealed the m.3243A>G mutation in the MT-TL1 gene with a blood heteroplasmy level of 16.8%. Her affected son showed a higher heteroplasmy burden (35.1%), while another family member carried a similar low-level heteroplasmy without overt neurological symptoms, consistent with maternal inheritance and phenotypic variability (Fig. 1).

Brain MRI and magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) performed four years prior (at age 56) provided an important metabolic baseline. MRI revealed multiple T2/fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) hyperintensities in both cerebral white matter and periventricular areas (Fazekas grade I), consistent with leukoaraiosis or gliosis (Fig. 2A). MRS demonstrated increased choline and decreased N-acetylaspartate (NAA) peaks in both frontal lobes and the right basal ganglia, suggesting chronic metabolic stress without evidence of focal mass lesion or abnormal enhancement (Fig. 2B and C). Upon the current admission, an initial non-contrast brain CT revealed a high-attenuated intra-axial lesion in the right basal ganglia with significant mass effect (Fig. 3A). Subsequent preoperative MRI demonstrated a heterogeneously enhancing mass involving the right frontal lobe, temporal lobe, and insula, with extensive surrounding T2/FLAIR abnormality and mass effect. The maximal longitudinal diameter of the lesion measured approximately 6.5 cm on sagittal imaging. The lesion caused significant peritumoral edema and leftward midline shift with compression of the right lateral ventricle (Fig. 3B and C). The mass was closely adjacent to the right M1 and M2 segments of the middle cerebral artery, compressing the right lateral and third ventricles.

Given the radiographic findings of a high-grade glioma, the patient underwent urgent craniotomy under general anesthesia with neuro-navigation guidance. A Dandy incision was performed, and the M2 branches were secured via Sylvian dissection. Gross total resection was achieved through sequential resection of the middle and inferior temporal gyri, temporal pole lobectomy, and wide resection of the inferior frontal gyrus and insular cortex, while carefully preserving the M2 branches. Histopathological and molecular analysis confirmed the diagnosis of GBM, IDH-wildtype (CNS WHO grade 4) (Fig. 4).

Postoperatively, the patient failed to recover neurologically. Postoperative MRI demonstrated newly developed infarctions involving the right frontal lobe, temporal lobe, and corpus callosum. During the postoperative period, the patient developed acute kidney injury requiring continuous renal replacement therapy. Despite supportive management, meaningful neurological recovery was not achieved. Although she maintained spontaneous eye opening, she was unable to follow commands, with a postoperative GCS score of E4V1M4, consistent with a stuporous mental status. With a few weeks of inpatient rehabilitation, she proceeded to receive the Stupp protocol. However, follow-up MRI with dynamic susceptibility contrast perfusion imaging performed after one month of treatment demonstrated massive local recurrence with markedly increased relative cerebral blood volume, indicating early and aggressive tumor progression despite standard-of-care treatment.

DISCUSSION

The clinical association between MELAS syndrome and the development of GBM remains an extremely rare phenomenon. To our knowledge, this represents the first reported case of GBM arising in a patient with genetically confirmed MELAS syndrome. Although mitochondrial dysfunction may theoretically contribute to tumorigenesis through chronic oxidative stress, the present case alone does not establish a causal relationship between MELAS syndrome and GBM development [6]. This case is particularly notable because it documents the occurrence of IDH-wildtype GBM in a patient with genetically confirmed MELAS syndrome who had no prior history of stroke-like episodes. Notably, the MRI/MRS obtained four years prior to tumor presentation demonstrated only mild, nonspecific white-matter hyperintensities and a metabolic profile characterized by elevated choline and reduced NAA, without focal mass effect or abnormal enhancement [7]. These findings may suggest that the subsequent lesion represented a de novo neoplastic event rather than a misinterpretation of MELAS-related stroke-like pathology.

MELAS is characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction driven by mtDNA mutations that impair oxidative phosphorylation and promote excessive production of ROS, leading to oxidative stress and cellular damage [1]. This chronic oxidative stress is a well-established contributor to genomic instability and tumorigenesis [5]. Despite this theoretical predisposition, GBM occurring in patients with MELAS syndrome has rarely been described in the literature and, to our knowledge, reports of IDH-wildtype GBM in genetically confirmed MELAS remain extremely limited. This discrepancy may be explained by several factors, including the relatively limited life expectancy of MELAS patients and the predominance of acute metabolic crises, which may favor neuronal loss over neoplastic transformation [1]. In contrast, our patient exhibited a prolonged disease course characterized by chronic, sublethal metabolic stress without overt stroke-like episodes. Although speculative, such a clinical background may have provided conditions permissive for the gradual accumulation of molecular alterations associated with tumorigenesis [6].

Serial neuroimaging findings obtained several years before tumor presentation may provide additional observational context regarding the patient’s underlying metabolic abnormalities. Four years prior to tumor development, MRS demonstrated an elevated choline-to-NAA ratio in the bilateral frontal lobes and right basal ganglia without focal mass effect or contrast enhancement [7]. Such findings are commonly observed in mitochondrial disorders and are generally considered nonspecific manifestations of neuronal loss and reactive gliosis [8]. In the present case, interpretation of these metabolic abnormalities in relation to subsequent GBM development should be approached with caution, particularly given the spatial discordance between the earlier MRS abnormalities and the eventual tumor location in the right frontotemporal and insular regions. Therefore, these retrospective observations should be regarded as hypothesis-generating only, and the possibility of confirmation bias must be acknowledged. Further molecular and longitudinal studies are required to clarify whether chronic mitochondrial dysfunction has any contributory role in glioma development [6]. Chronic mitochondrial dysfunction leads to excessive ROS production, resulting in oxidative DNA damage and impaired genomic integrity [6]. Nevertheless, chronic mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress remain biologically plausible mechanisms that may influence cellular vulnerability and genomic instability. Further molecular and experimental studies are required to clarify whether such metabolic alterations have any role in glioma development [4]. The genetic heterogeneity of MELAS, including variable mutation load, may also influence disease expression and progression [1]. Higher mutation loads have been associated with earlier disease onset and broader systemic involvement, although their relationship with disease progression remains unclear [9]. This genetic and phenotypic variability may partly explain why tumor development has not been consistently observed across MELAS patients [9]. It is also conceivable that certain mitochondrial or metabolic backgrounds could influence individual susceptibility to neoplastic transformation, although this remains entirely speculative [4].

Another distinctive aspect of the present case is the patient’s relatively late clinical presentation. MELAS syndrome is typically diagnosed before the age of 20 years in most patients, whereas disease onset or diagnosis after the age of 40 years is relatively uncommon [1]. In the present case, the patient exhibited a comparatively mild and slowly progressive phenotype characterized primarily by diabetes mellitus, hearing loss, and ophthalmoplegia without overt stroke-like episodes. In addition, the blood heteroplasmy level was relatively low (16.8%), which may partly explain the delayed and less fulminant clinical manifestation. This atypical clinical course differs from that of classical early-onset MELAS and may reflect substantial phenotypic heterogeneity associated with mitochondrial mutation burden [9]. Whether late-onset and relatively indolent mitochondrial dysfunction influences tumor biology differently from classical MELAS phenotypes remains entirely unknown.

The patient’s persistent postoperative impaired consciousness was considered multifactorial in origin. Although MELAS-associated mitochondrial vulnerability may have contributed to impaired neurological recovery under perioperative metabolic stress, there was no definite postoperative elevation of serum lactate to suggest overt metabolic decompensation [10]. However, the postoperative course was complicated by acute kidney injury requiring continuous renal replacement therapy, indicating substantial systemic physiological stress.

In addition, postoperative MRI demonstrated newly developed infarctions involving the right frontal lobe, temporal lobe, and corpus callosum. These structural injuries, together with the severe preoperative mass effect and edema involving deep cerebral structures, may have contributed to persistent dysfunction of widespread cortical-subcortical networks associated with arousal and consciousness. Although thalamic compression was suspected preoperatively because of the marked mass effect, no definite thalamic infarction was identified on postoperative diffusion-weighted imaging. Therefore, the patient’s poor neurological recovery was considered more likely attributable to the combined effects of postoperative ischemic injury, diffuse cerebral dysfunction, systemic physiological stress, and underlying mitochondrial vulnerability rather than to a single isolated mechanism.

From a therapeutic perspective, this case raises important considerations. Patients with MELAS have impaired mitochondrial function, which may render normal brain tissue more susceptible to treatment-related toxicity from therapies that induce mitochondrial stress, including radiotherapy and alkylating agents such as temozolomide [11]. However, in this case, the tumor did not demonstrate a dramatic therapeutic response and instead exhibited treatment resistance. This observation suggests that tumor cells may adapt to mitochondrial stress through metabolic reprogramming, thereby reducing their dependence on oxidative phosphorylation and diminishing treatment sensitivity [12]. Further experimental and clinical studies are warranted to elucidate the impact of mitochondrial dysfunction on treatment response in GBM and to explore potential metabolic-targeted therapeutic strategies.

In conclusion, this case describes an unusually aggressive IDH-wildtype GBM arising in a patient with genetically confirmed MELAS syndrome. Despite surgical resection and standard Stupp protocol therapy, the tumor demonstrated rapid progression and early massive recurrence. Although a direct pathogenic relationship between MELAS syndrome and GBM cannot be established from a single case, this report highlights the potential clinical relevance of mitochondrial dysfunction in tumor behavior and treatment response. Further molecular and clinical investigations are warranted to clarify whether altered mitochondrial metabolism contributes to glioma biology or therapeutic resistance.
