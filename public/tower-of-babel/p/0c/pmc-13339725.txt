Preoperative Pollen Exposure and Chronic Rhinosinusitis Outcomes After Endoscopic Sinus Surgery: A Pilot Investigation
Authors: Benton Tullis, Jess C Mace, Conner Massey, Stuart Batterman, Vijay R Ramakrishnan, Daniel M Beswick, Zachary M Soler, Timothy L Smith, Jeremiah A Alt, Amarbir S Gill
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAlthough particulate matter is an environmental factor that may increase the risk of developing chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS), the impact of pollen on CRS outcomes is understudied. Here, we analyzed the effects of preoperative pollen exposure on disease‐specific and general quality‐of‐life (QOL) outcomes after endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS).MethodsProspectively enrolled patients with medically refractory CRS, who self‐selected ESS, completed the 22‐item SinoNasal Outcome Test (SNOT‐22) and Medical Outcomes Study Questionnaire Short‐Form 6‐D (SF‐6D) surveys at enrollment and follow up. Preoperative pollen counts were obtained from the National Allergy Bureau using residence zip codes and total pollen was divided into tree, ragweed, and grass/weed subcategories. Spearman's correlation coefficients (r s) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated and compared.ResultsSeventy‐nine patients met inclusion criteria with a mean follow‐up of 8 months. Higher average total pollen and tree pollen exposure 1 week before ESS were associated with less postoperative improvement in SNOT‐22 scores at follow up (r s = −0.26; 95% CI: −0.46, −0.03; p = 0.021 and r s = −0.27; 95% CI: −0.47, −0.04; p = 0.017, respectively). Pollen exposure did not attenuate improvement of SF‐6D scores.ConclusionIn this pilot investigation, increased preoperative pollen exposure attenuated improvement in SNOT‐22 scores after ESS.Keywords: chronic rhinosinusitis, endoscopic sinus surgery, environment, multi‐institution, particulate matter, pollen, preoperative exposure, quality of life, SNOT‐22, surgical outcomes

Introduction

Airborne pollen is a common aeroallergen that is mainly attributable to plants using wind pollination [1]. Pollen exerts its effects by inducing T helper type 2 (Th2) and IgE‐mediated immune responses in susceptible IgE sensitized individuals [2]. This occurs when aerosolized allergens, such as pollen, penetrate the nasal and paranasal mucosa and interact with naïve T cells. These cells can then differentiate into Th2 cells which produce IL‐4 and IL‐13, inducing the production of IgE from B cells. The circulating IgE sensitizes individuals, and when reencountered with the allergen, mast cells and basophils are activated via the IgE [3]. Partially due to the significant physiological effects and ubiquity of airborne pollen, the American Academy of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology (AAAAI) created the National Allergy Bureau (NAB) to coordinate the measurement and reporting of pollen and mold spores [4, 5]. This resource allows researchers to make progress on understanding the relationship between this ubiquitous environmental exposure and human health.

Chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) is characterized by inflammatory dysregulation of the sinonasal mucosal epithelium [6, 7, 8]. Up to 50% of patients with CRS may be recalcitrant to medical management and require endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS) [9]. Of those undergoing ESS, 18% may require a revision [10, 11]. Although our understanding of factors impacting the success of surgery is limited, recent data indicate that environmental factors, specifically particulate matter, may play an important role in the pathophysiology of CRS and negatively impact symptom improvement after ESS [12, 13, 14, 15].

Although the impact of poor air quality on CRS is an active area of research with many projects focusing on pollutants [12, 14, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20], the relationship between pollen and CRS outcomes remains understudied. For example, some studies have shown that allergic rhinitis and/or aeroallergen sensitization are more prominent in individuals with CRS, while others find no significant evidence for an increased prevalence of allergic disease in CRS [21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26]. The heterogenicity of the study methods makes comparing and extrapolating conclusions difficult when evaluating the role of allergy in CRS [13, 22, 23]. Studies have investigated the use of immunotherapy in CRS but with limited and weak evidence to support its use [26, 27].

No studies to date have examined the relationship between pollen concentrations and disease‐specific or general quality‐of‐life (QOL) outcomes after ESS for CRS, and the current literature is inconclusive on the role of allergy in CRS. Furthermore, climate change is expected to increase pollen counts, further supporting the need to investigate the potentially deleterious effects of pollen on sinonasal health [28]. To fill this knowledge gap, the present pilot investigation employed a multi‐institutional cohort study to examine the potential associations between preoperative pollen exposure and post‐operative outcomes in CRS. We hypothesized that increasing pollen exposure at the time of ESS will lead to impaired post‐operative sinonasal QOL improvement.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Sample Population

A single‐arm, multi‐center, longitudinal outcomes study design was utilized. Rhinology practices across four academic medical centers prospectively enrolled study participants. The study and clinical data safety protocols and annual reviews were approved by each institution's Institutional Review Board. Study participants provided written informed consent for the duration of the study.

Environmental Pollen Determination

Pollen data was requested through the American Academy of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology (AAAAI) from counties in the states where the four academic medical centers from which patients were recruited are located [4]. These stations are all certified by the AAAAI and sampling was conducted in accordance with the standard procedures of the NAB. Briefly, pollen grains were manually measured after 24‐h periods of aeroallergen sampling with a volumetric air sampler, and counts were converted into air concentrations (pollen grains per cubic meter [m3] of air) [4]. In order to understand the potential impact of acute pollen exposure, average pollen exposure during the one‐week period before ESS was collected. We chose to examine a 7‐day average given prior data looking at the impact of pollen on allergic rhinitis (AR) showing that this exposure window had greater association with AR health outcomes compared to same‐day measures [29]. Pollen was grouped into four categories, namely ragweed, grass/weed, tree, and total pollen. Total pollen was a conglomerate of ragweed, grass/weed, and tree pollen, in addition to “unidentified pollen spores.” This breakdown was chosen a priori based upon studies in the literature highlighting the significance of these pollen types in other airway diseases [30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35]. The 9‐digit zip code affiliated with the subject's primary residence was confirmed to be located in a county with a pollen measurement station.

CRS Diagnosis and Inclusion Criteria

The inclusion criteria consisted of adult participants (> 18 years of age) diagnosed with CRS, with or without nasal polyposis (NP), by a practicing rhinologist in accordance with the American Academy of Otolaryngology‐Head and Neck Surgery (AAO‐HNS) criteria [36]. No vulnerable populations were considered for inclusion. Further exclusion criteria included patients with cystic fibrosis, primary ciliary dyskinesia, sinonasal tumors, odontogenic sinusitis, immunodeficiency, or active treatment with immunomodulators. Patients self‐selected ESS as a subsequent treatment modality due to continued, recalcitrant symptoms after appropriate medical therapy.

Clinical Measures of Disease Severity

Sinonasal endoscopic examination staging (Lund‐Kennedy, LK), computed tomography (CT) image staging (Lund‐Mackay, LM) [37], the 22‐item SinoNasal Outcomes Test (SNOT‐22; 2006; Washington University, St. Louis, MO.) survey scores [38] and SF‐6D health utility value (HUV) survey scores were obtained from study participants at the time of enrollment [39] and at follow‐up visits. Postoperative CT sinus scans were not available per the standard of care. Given the small number of available postoperative nasal endoscopy scores, these were not analyzed. Atopy status was assigned by participants designating on a questionnaire that they had a history of positive allergy testing.

Data Management and Biostatistical Analyses

Participant data was secured in a central database (Access; Microsoft Corporation; Redmond, WA) where all protected health information was removed prior to transfer and participant data was assigned unique identification numbers. Data analysis was conducted with GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 (GraphPad Software; San Diego, CA) and SPSS 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Normal Q–Q plots and Shapiro–Wilk testing were utilized to assess data normality. Descriptive characteristics are presented using means, standard deviation [±SD] values, and range limits. Two‐tailed, bivariate Spearman's rank correlation coefficients (r s) were used in conjunction with Bonett & Wright 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to explore associations between pollen concentration values and disease severity measures at unique time intervals. Kruskall–Wallis testing was used to identify significant omnibus differences in pollen volume between enrolling institutions. Fisher's r‐to‐z transformations were also used to identify significant differences between Spearmans' rank correlation coefficients via stratification between all independent subgroups of interest. Type‐I error rates (p‐values) are provided for each statistical association.

Pollen Exposure and Post‐ESS QOL Outcomes

High total pollen exposure 1 week prior to ESS attenuated improvement in within‐subject SNOT‐22 scores (r s = −0.26; 95% CI: −0.46, −0.03; p = 0.021; Table 3). This association was primarily driven by the tree pollen subcategory (r s = −0.27; 95% CI: −0.47, −0.04; p = 0.017), as the ragweed and grass/weed subcategories had no effect on within‐subject improvement in SNOT‐22 scores (r s = −0.05; 95% CI: −0.17, 0.28; p = 0.64, and r s = −0.09; 95% CI: −0.31, 0.14; p = 0.448, respectively). There was no significant association between individual or total pollen concentration levels and improvement in SF‐6D scores (Table 3). Despite the negative impact of pollen exposure on postoperative SNOT‐22 outcomes, 92.4% (73/79) of patients demonstrated a within‐subjective improvement after surgery (mean improvement 27.9, SD 18.4), and 79.7% (63/79) achieved a minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of 9 or greater [40].

Stratification Analysis

Stratification analysis for each of the significant bivariate associations identified between pollen exposure variables and postoperative outcome differences was performed using R‐to‐Z transformations to understand the potential for confounding due to key sociodemographic variables and comorbidities listed in Table 1, with a particular focus on race, ethnicity, household/family income, history of NP, and history of atopy. Household income of greater than $75,000 was noted to be a potential confounder for the association between ragweed exposure and baseline LK endoscopy scores (Z = −2.21, p = 0.027). Otherwise, there was no evidence of significant confounding by socioeconomic variables after stratification analysis. Furthermore, history of NP or atopy did not show any evidence of potential confounding. Accurate multivariate analyses were not further possible due to data limitations/restricted sample sizes/violations of modeling assumptions.

Discussion

Aeroallergens can lead to differentiation of Th2 cells from naïve T cells, which leads to sensitization and production of IgE from B cells, preparing the body to react to the specific allergen upon re‐exposure [41]. When IgE antibodies bind to an allergen, mast cells and basophils are activated with the release of bioactive mediators and can induce rhinorrhea, congestion, and pruritus [42]. This is followed by the infiltration of inflammatory cells (eosinophils, T cells, and NK cells), which continue to release inflammatory mediators that disrupt the epithelial barrier [43]. In this way, chronic allergen exposure can lead to mucosal hypersensitivity and hypertrophy [42, 43, 44].

Several studies have investigated the link between allergy and CRS with mixed results [21, 22, 23]. Systematic reviews by Tantilipikorn et al. [23] and Wilson et al. [22] investigated the role of systemic allergy on the prevalence of CRS. Included studies examined co‐morbid allergy status through various modalities, including skin prick, immunoglobulins, intradermal, in vitro, and tissue homogenate testing. Allergy status was then tested for associations with a diagnosis of CRS. Neither group of authors was able to draw definitive conclusions.

The heterogeneity of results may partially be due to the fact that CRS is increasingly understood as a spectrum from type 2 to non‐type 2 mediated disease, and overlap in inflammatory response from different endotypes may be muddling findings [23, 24]. One may also consider that multimorbidity could be contributing to conflicting results, as AR and CRS commonly are comorbid [22, 45, 46]. To date, no study has examined the relationship between pollen exposure and ESS outcomes in CRS—a knowledge gap addressed by the present pilot investigation.

Our data suggest that preoperative aeroallergen exposure may negatively impact CRS‐specific QOL outcomes after ESS. Higher concentrations of total pollen were correlated with a statistically significant attenuation of within‐subject improvement in SNOT‐22 scores. These findings were mostly attributable to tree pollen, which is highest during the spring season in North America [47]. Prior data show that in AR, tree and weed pollen increase the relative number of medication fills, while other types of pollen do not [29]. Our work adds support for the negative impact of tree pollen on upper airway inflammatory disease. Perhaps, counterintuitively, higher ragweed counts at 1‐week pre‐op weakly correlated with lower/better baseline LK scores. However, the trend on the negative R value is skewed by the number of subjects with 0 pollen counts at 1 week pre‐op (i.e., 55 of 79 subjects had 0 counts for pollen at 1 week pre‐op) for all LK score types. Furthermore, it is well established that objective measures of CRS, including LK nasal endoscopy scores do not correlate well with patient reported outcome measures, including the SNOT‐22 [48, 49]. For these reasons, it is hard to draw any definitive conclusions about the relationship between pre‐op ragweed and baseline LK nasal endoscopy score.

Although aeroallergen exposure negatively impacted SNOT‐22 scores in our study, there was no significant association for within‐subject improvement of SF‐6D HUV scores. It is possible that the general QOL questions on the SF‐6D HUV survey are too non‐specific to demonstrate the impact that allergy has on CRS‐specific subjective symptoms targeted by the SNOT‐22 survey.

These data suggest that pollen exposure may play a role in these associations, but the true magnitudes of association and their clinical relevance require additional, appropriately powered research that will allow execution of accurate multivariate analyses. As we seek to achieve precision medicine, such studies may allow us to better understand whether certain seasons of the year may represent better windows for operative intervention in particular individuals.

The QOL outcomes observed in this investigation may be, in part, driven by the type 2 pro‐inflammatory response commonly induced by pollen. It is also possible the impact of pollen exposure is greatest for type 2 CRS disease and/or those with a comorbid history of atopy. Indeed, prior investigations have demonstrated that the presence of comorbid AR can negatively impact baseline SNOT‐22 scores [50]. Although the limited sample size precluded definitive multivariate regression analysis to account for all potential confounding factors, R‐to‐Z transformations were adopted to screen and identify potential confounding associations, with a particular focus on NP and allergy status. We found that NP and allergy status did not significantly confound these results. Nevertheless, these results may be affected by participants who had positive allergies but had not yet undergone allergy testing. Ideally, formal testing would be completed for all participants to fully elucidate atopic differences between groups of patients. Future larger cohort studies with formal allergy testing and robust endotyping are needed to more definitively comment on the complex relationship between CRS, comorbid allergy and NP, and preoperative pollen exposure.

The present study used the country of primary residence as an estimate for airborne pollen exposure. This may not reflect an absolute exposure, as patients may not spend all their time within their county of residence. Further, pollen levels at their respective monitoring station may not be representative and/or may lag or lead levels at the patient's residence, predisposing to inaccuracies as a pollen exposure representation, a limitation that should be considered when interpreting the results of this study. Nevertheless, this methodology has been used in previous studies investigating the impact of aeroallergens on asthma disease severity [33, 34]. Our study also did not control for differences in the postoperative medical regimen for participants. Moreover, our participants are limited to those that accessed academic medical centers, thereby potentially limiting generalizability to other populations. Finally, a sample size of 79 is on the smaller size, making it difficult to accurately account for other potential confounding environmental exposures, such as temperature, humidity, and pollutants. Moreover, the small sample size could impact the ability to detect smaller but clinically relevant effects of pollen on postoperative outcomes. Additionally, due to sample size limitations, a distributed lag analysis approach, which might better identify the time windows and time dependent patterns that drive results, was not utilized. Due to these limitations the pilot data presented herein should be considered hypothesis‐generating rather than definitive, with a plan for further multi‐institutional work to validate pollen as an independent predictor of ESS outcomes.

Conclusion

The present multi‐institutional pilot investigation suggests that preoperative pollen exposure may negatively impact improvement in disease‐specific QOL outcomes after ESS in patients with CRS. Future studies with a larger sample size and robust multivariate analyses are required to confirm these results.
