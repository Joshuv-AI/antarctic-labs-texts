Automated quantification of lung pathology on micro-CT in diverse disease models using deep learning
Authors: Flore Belmans, Laura Seldeslachts, Eliane Vanhoffelen, Birger Tielemans, Wim Vos, Frederik Maes, Greetje Vande Velde
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundMicro-CT significantly enhances the efficiency, predictive power and translatability of animal studies to human clinical trials for respiratory diseases. However, the analysis of large micro-CT datasets remains a bottleneck.MethodsWe developed a generic deep learning (DL)-based lung segmentation model using longitudinal micro-CT images from studies of Down syndrome, viral and fungal infections, and exacerbation with variable lung pathology and degree of disease burden. 2D models were trained with cross-validation on axial, coronal and sagittal slices. Predictions from these single-orientation models were combined to create a 2.5D model using majority voting or probability averaging. The generalisability of these models to other studies (COVID-19, lung inflammation and fibrosis), scanner configurations and rodent species (rats, hamsters, degus) was tested, including a publicly available database.FindingsOn the internal validation data, the highest mean Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) was found for the 2.5D probability averaging model (0.953 ± 0.023), further improving the output of the 2D models by removing erroneous voxels outside the lung region. The models demonstrated good generalisability with average DSC values ranging from 0.89 to 0.94 across different lung pathologies and scanner configurations. The biomarkers extracted from manual and automated segmentations are well in agreement and proved that our proposed solution effectively monitors longitudinal lung pathology development and response to treatment in real-world preclinical studies.InterpretationOur DL-based pipeline for lung pathology quantification offers efficient analysis of large micro-CT datasets, is widely applicable across rodent disease models and acquisition protocols and enables real-time insights into therapy efficacy.FundingThis research was supported by the Service Public de Wallonie (AEROVID grant to FB, WV) and The Flemish Research Foundation (FWO, doctoral mandate 1SF2224N to EV and 1186121N/1186123N to LS, infrastructure grant I006524N to GVV).Keywords: Lung disease, Small animal models, Micro-computed tomography, Imaging biomarkers, Segmentation, Deep learning

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We conducted a comprehensive PubMed search without date restrictions using the query: “micro-CT AND (rodent OR mice OR mouse) AND lung AND segmentation AND (automated OR automatic),” which returned 30 results. Additional relevant studies were identified through supplementary searches. From these, 6 studies reported on the development of a deep learning-based method to automate the quantification of lung pathology on micro-CT of animal models. However, these methods were developed and validated exclusively in mice and within specific studies and disease models, suggesting that their applicability may be limited to those particular contexts.

Added value of this study

We have developed a robust, deep learning–based pipeline for lung segmentation and quantification, using heterogeneous murine datasets with varying degrees of lung pathology. We have demonstrated its generalisability across a wide range of rodent micro-CT datasets, including different disease models, scanner configurations and rodent species. Biomarkers extracted manually and automatically showed strong agreement, indicating that our proposed solution reliably monitors longitudinal lung pathology development and response to treatment in real-world preclinical studies.

Implications of all the available evidence

This generic, deep learning-based pipeline addresses a critical bottleneck in preclinical lung imaging by enabling fast, reliable, and fully automated analysis of lung pathology on micro-CT. It can be applied reliably off-the-shelf across rodent disease models and acquisition protocols, delivering real-time insights into therapy efficacy. By integrating AI-powered longitudinal micro-CT into lung disease research, this approach has the potential to enhance the translatability of preclinical drug candidate screening through direct quantification of disease burden.

Introduction

Diseases of the respiratory system remain a prominent public health issue with conditions such as infections, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and cancer ranked among the leading causes of mortality by the World Health Organization (WHO).1 It is therefore crucial to continue research directed at unravelling disease mechanisms, investigating new therapeutics and evaluating the efficacy of candidate drugs. In vivo animal studies are widely used in the experimental phase of the drug development cycle and a crucial step towards initiating clinical studies in humans. The most common animal models used in respiratory disease research are rodents such as mice, rats, hamsters, but also rabbits and guinea pigs.2

Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) is increasingly being used in experimental lung research to evaluate disease burden in animal models.3, 4, 5, 6, 7 In vivo micro-CT has been shown to be essential to complement terminal measurements such as respiratory system mechanics, histopathological and biochemical markers, which alone provide only a partial view of the disease pattern.8, 9, 10, 11 Moreover, parameters derived from micro-CT, such as non-aerated lung volume (NALV), correlate with classical terminal readouts such as histology.12 Micro-CT enables non-invasive, longitudinal monitoring of disease progression and therapeutic response over time, which closely reflects clinical practices using high-resolution computed tomography in patients. This compatibility narrows the methodological gap between experimental models and clinical studies, overcoming at least partially the issues when translating drug efficacy studies from bench to bedside.

Despite the potential of using micro-CT in lung research, analysis of large batches of imaging data in preclinical studies remains a bottleneck. Micro-CT analysis usually starts with the identification, also referred to as segmentation, of the volume of interest (VOI), i.e. the lung volume when studying lung disease models. From the VOI, quantitative measurements describing volume, image intensity (reflecting tissue density) or other regional properties, can be extracted and used as imaging biomarkers or as radiomic features to assess disease progression or response to treatment. Lung segmentation on micro-CT is still often performed manually or requires manual corrections, to incorporate dense pathological regions with unclear boundaries from surrounding tissues.3, 4, 5, 6, 7 This makes the process highly resource-intensive and prone to inter- and intra-reader variability.

Building on the success of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in automating segmentation across various clinical and biomedical imaging applications, particularly through Deep Learning (DL) approaches that leverage Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs),13 DL has been applied to automate lung segmentation in micro-CT images. Wang et al.,14 Schoppe et al.15 and Malimban et al.16 reported on the development of different DL models to segment multiple organs in micro-CT of mice, including the lungs. However, these models were trained and tested on images of healthy mice only and therefore have limited applicability for experimental studies involving lung diseases that significantly affect the appearance of the lung parenchyma. Sforazzini et al.17 trained a 2D U-Net based DL model on CT images, acquired on a clinical PET/CT scanner, of both healthy mice and mice with different levels of lung fibrosis originating from irradiation and subsequently fine-tuned their model for segmentation of high-resolution micro-CT images, but again of healthy mice only. Vincenzi et al.18 developed a 2.5D approach, combining the output of different 2D U-Net models trained on either axial, sagittal or coronal slices, for lung segmentation of micro-CT images of healthy and bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrotic mice. Ferl et al.19 developed a 3D U-net model for the segmentation of both lungs and lung tumours on murine micro-CT, using data from healthy subjects, lung cancer studies using a genetically engineered mouse model and bleomycin-induced lung fibrosis studies. In other animal model systems used in respiratory research, very few studies exist describing lung segmentation on micro-CT and none of them are fully automated using DL.7,20, 21, 22, 23, 24

While different DL models can be created ad hoc for specific studies and disease models, as in the works mentioned above, their applicability will be limited to those specific contexts. Moreover, the segmentation behaviour of such specific models will be biased by protocol and observer-dependent differences in the way the manual segmentations used to train these models were created, which in turn may also introduce bias in the imaging biomarkers quantified from these segmentations. Hence, preclinical lung research could benefit from the availability of a single, more generic lung segmentation model that can be applied reliably off-the-shelf in different studies, disease models and scanner configurations, ensuring consistent segmentation performance in different datasets of both healthy and diseased lungs in various lung research lines including unseen models and conditions, thereby eliminating potential quantification bias and enhancing comparability between different studies.

In this study, we have developed and validated such a generic DL-based segmentation model by training it on multiple murine micro-CT datasets from diverse lung disease models with variable degree of disease burden. We evaluated our model for fully automated quantification of imaging biomarkers of lung disease, demonstrating its usability for preclinical micro-CT studies. In addition, we show that the method can be applied in rodents other than mice, further extending its applicability in preclinical lung research.

Methods

Datasets

Disease models

To train and evaluate a DL model for lung segmentation on micro-CT, datasets of lung micro-CT were retrospectively collected from different experimental studies ran in our lab at KU Leuven which are related to active research lines within our group, including various disease models. An overview of the datasets can be found in Table 1. For model training, murine datasets were used which were obtained from longitudinal experimental studies in mouse models of Down syndrome (DS),25 Cryptococcus infection (CRYPTO1-2-3),26,33 and influenza-associated pulmonary aspergillosis (IAPA),4 including a wide spectrum of lung disease types and severities ranging from healthy baseline and wild-type data, over mild lung disease towards severe pathology. The resulting lung segmentation model was subsequently evaluated in other murine disease models, namely of COVID-19 (COVID) and of bleomycin-induced lung fibrosis (BLEO),8 on the non-contrast enhanced subset of a publicly available database of segmented murine micro-CTs (ROSENHAIN)27,28 to test robustness to differences in centre, scanner and resolution, and on a different rodent species, namely a monocrotaline-induced pulmonary hypertension model in rats (RAT). In addition, performance was qualitatively assessed in a COVID-19 model in hamsters (HAMSTER)30, 31, 32 and healthy degus (DEGU). The model was also evaluated for longitudinal quantification of lung disease burden in a different murine micro-CT dataset of IAPA (IAPA2).29

Micro-CT acquisition & annotation

The scan parameters for each micro-CT dataset used in this study are listed in Table S1.

The DS, CRYPTO1-2-3, IAPA1-2, COVID, BLEO, and RAT datasets were acquired with a dedicated, properly calibrated, whole-body small animal micro-CT scanner (either SkyScan1278 or Skyscan1076, Bruker, Kontich, Belgium). The animals were imaged in a free-breathing anesthetised state in supine position. Anaesthesia was applied by inhalation of 1.5–3% isoflurane in 100% oxygen during scanning. The images were reconstructed using NRecon, visualised using DataViewer, and delineated using CTan software, provided by the manufacturer. Each acquisition resulted in a respiratory weighted reconstructed 3D dataset with isotropic voxel size of 35 μm (BLEO) or 51 μm (all other). The VOI, which consisted of the entire lung field, was manually delineated on axial slices with automatic interpolation between slices, by experienced biomedical scientists.

The ROSENHAIN dataset was acquired on a dedicated preclinical μCT scanner (Tomoscope Duo, CT Imaging GmbH, Erlangen, Germany), reconstructed at an isotropic voxel size of 280 μm and segmented using the Imalytics Preclinical software.34 The mice were anesthetised using 2.5% isoflurane vaporised in 1.0 l/min of oxygen gas using a dedicated vaporiser.27 Acquisition details and annotation protocols for this dataset can be found in Table S1 and in the relevant publications.27,28 The mouse bed was removed from the images using the bed segmentation provided with each scan. Three out of 140 non-contrast enhanced micro-CT scans did not have the mouse bed segmented on the 280 μm scan and were therefore discarded.

Intensity normalisation

Micro-CT is known to be less standardised than clinical CT with variable acquisition and reconstruction protocols used by different labs. Therefore, intensity normalisation of all the images was applied to equalise the appearance of images of different datasets (Figure S1A). All micro-CT scans in all datasets were converted from grey values (GV) to Hounsfield Units (HU) by linearly scaling each image, mapping the GV of air and of water to HU = −1000 and HU = 0 respectively. The GV of air and water is conventionally determined by scanning a phantom. To avoid the dependency on phantom scans, an algorithm was developed that automatically extracts this information from the intensity histogram. Using a peak detection algorithm, the GV of air and the GV of tissue, approximating the GV of water, was extracted from the image directly, enabling an approximate HU scaling without additional effort.

Disease burden

The inclusion of heterogeneous datasets associated with different research lines contributes to the generalisability of the final model towards different disease models and pathology severity. Dataset diversity was quantified by assessing the disease burden for each scan across all datasets using one consistent method. The total lung volume (TLV) was separated into non-aerated lung volume (NALV) and aerated lung volume (ALV) by applying a fixed threshold (−178 HU) on the pre-processed images, which was determined empirically by histogram analysis (Figure S1B). Lung disease burden was defined by the percentage of NALV relative to TLV. The lung disease burden was assessed for each scan based on the manual lung segmentations and varied between different datasets used in this study, as indicated in Table 1.

Lung segmentation model

Image pre-processing

For the images used for model training, the body region was identified in each scan using the Otsu thresholding algorithm35 and the resulting mask was used to remove background noise and possible embedded annotations. The images were then cropped to the identical size of 448 × 448 x 448 voxels, axially centred around the body region and covering the entire chest region. The image intensities were clipped according to the lung window (level: −600 HU, window: 1500 HU), and the image intensities were normalised to have zero mean and unit variance.

Model training

The training data, composed of 345 scans of 130 animals obtained from 5 different micro-CT datasets (Table 1), was split into 5 sets for 5-fold cross-validation. The split was performed on the subject level such that all longitudinal scans of the same subject were included in the same fold. In addition, the split was stratified on the disease model and the severity of induced lung disease to ensure a similar distribution over the different folds. The severity of induced lung disease was assessed for each animal by averaging the disease burden over all scans of that animal, with an average disease burden of 15% considered as severe. The number of subjects and scans for each fold is listed in Fig. 1. As adjacent slices were very similar and therefore redundant for training, only a subset of the slices was used for training (1 out of 5, on average ∼4000 2D slices per fold).

Model development pipeline. Workflow showing data splitting for five-fold cross-validation, 2D model training in axial, coronal and sagittal plane, and 2.5D combination strategies.

Three models with identical 2D U-Net architecture13 and input size of 448 × 448 were trained for each fold using either axial, coronal or sagittal slices, which was facilitated by the identical isotropic voxel size of all training images (51 μm). Specifically, the model architecture consisted of a contracting encoder path and an expansive decoder path, connected via symmetric skip connections to preserve spatial context. The encoder path consisted of the repeated application of two convolutional layers with a 3 × 3 kernel, a stride of 1 and zero padding to maintain the spatial dimensions of the output feature map equal to those of the input. Each convolutional layer was followed by batch normalisation and a Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function. After each pair of convolutions, a 2 × 2 max-pooling operation with a stride of 2 was applied for downsampling. The number of filters increased with depth, starting from 64 and doubling at each stage up to 1024. In the decoder path, feature maps were upsampled via 2 × 2 nearest-neighbour interpolation, concatenated with their encoder counterparts, and refined with two subsequent convolutional layers including batch normalisation and ReLU activation. At the end of the network, a 1 × 1 convolution with stride 1 followed by a sigmoid activation layer was applied to produce the output lung segmentation prediction. A detailed schematic overview of the model architecture is presented in Figure S2. The model was implemented in Keras (version 2.3.1) using TensorFlow (version 2.3.0) and trained on a NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2080 Ti GPU with 11 GB memory size. The model weights were optimised with respect to both soft Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) and binary cross-entropy using the Adam optimiser at a learning rate of 0.001, which was reduced upon learning stagnation. A batch size of 6 was used. The training was terminated when the validation loss did not improve during 3 consecutive epochs.

To make the model less sensitive to the exact HU calibration, random intensity variations were added during training. Other data augmentation methods included affine transformations, lateral and vertical flipping, addition of noise and image smoothing. Additionally, to account for variations between different studies in the axial extent of the cylindrical field of view in which the images were reconstructed, specific data augmentation was applied during training of the axial model, masking out image intensities outside a circular region defined around the body's centre, with a radius ranging from 160 to 170 pixels.

Model prediction

In total, 15 2D segmentation models resulted from 5-fold cross-validation training using slices along each of the 3 orientations. For prediction with each individual 2D model, the images were resampled to a voxel size of 51 μm when the reconstructed voxel size was different. For the RAT dataset, the scans were downsampled by a factor of 2 along each axis, to account for the larger body size of rats compared to mice. The same pre-processing steps as used for model training were applied. Predictions were generated for each slice in the respective plane and stacked to form a 3D volume. To enhance performance on whole-body micro-CT scans, the segmentation from the axial model was first predicted and used to identify the chest region. The cropped scan was then used to predict the segmentation with the coronal and sagittal models. Probability maps were binarised using a threshold of 0.5.

An ensemble 2D model, combining the 5 individual axial, coronal or sagittal model predictions, was created for each orientation by averaging the probability maps of the respective models, followed by binarisation. Two strategies for creating a 2.5D model, combining the individual or ensembled axial, coronal and sagittal model predictions, were implemented. In the first approach, the binarised predictions were combined using majority voting, meaning that each voxel was assigned the label agreed upon by 2 of the 3 models. In the second approach, the predicted probability maps were first averaged before binarisation.

All resulting segmentations were post-processed with connected component analysis to retain only the largest connected 3D component.

Evaluation

Segmentation performance

Internal validation was performed for the models obtained for each of the 5 folds by evaluating the axial, coronal and sagittal 2D models and the 2.5D models resulting from majority voting or probability averaging on the held-out validation fold. To assess the generalisability of these models to other disease models, scanners, resolution and rodents, external validation was performed by evaluating the ensembled 2D and 2.5D models on the COVID, BLEO, ROSENHAIN and RAT datasets that were not used for training. Lung segmentation performance was evaluated by comparing the automated segmentations against the corresponding manual segmentations using the DSC and the relative absolute volume difference (RAVD), both widely accepted and complementary metrics for evaluating segmentation performance in medical imaging.36 DSC measures the spatial overlap of two segmentations, ranging from 0 (no overlap) to 1 (perfect overlap). RAVD measures the relative error in the segmented volume, which is particularly relevant when the segmentations are being used to extract volumetric biomarkers, and was computed as: (|volumeprediction—volumemanual|/volumemanual) ∗ 100%. All scores are reported by the mean and standard deviation over the respective samples.

Accuracy of imaging biomarker quantification

Model performance was further evaluated by comparing the results of biomarker extraction from the segmented lungs. The impact of segmentation accuracy measured by DSC and dataset diversity measured by disease burden, on downstream biomarker quantification measured by (relative) absolute disease burden difference, was assessed for the 2.5D probability averaging model on the internal validation dataset. Furthermore Bland–Altman analysis37,38 was conducted on the external COVID dataset to assess the agreement between manually and automatically extracted biomarkers. The biomarkers investigated are the TLV, ALV and NALV, quantified from either the manual or the automated segmentation. The mean difference (bias) and 95% limits of agreement as ±1.96 standard deviations of the differences were calculated.

Longitudinal monitoring of lung disease burden and treatment response

We evaluated whether our proposed method can be used to monitor the development of lung disease burden and response to treatment in a real-world longitudinal study and whether it can discriminate effectively different behaviours between groups that received different treatments. For this purpose, we used a previously published study investigating the therapeutic effect of the antiviral drug baloxavir marboxil in a murine model for IAPA (IAPA2 dataset in Table 1).29 Immunocompetent mice received intranasal instillation of influenza A followed by orotracheal inoculation with Aspergillus fumigatus 4 days later. Administration of baloxavir marboxil or sham was started at day 0, day 2 or day 4, resulting in 4 different treatment groups. Each group included 10 mice. Mice were monitored with micro-CT on day 0, day 2, day 4, day 5, day 6 and day 7. The resulting 240 scans were automatically analysed, including segmentation and biomarker extraction.

Statistics

Statistics were performed in Python using the SciPy (version 1.13.1) and scikit-posthocs (version 0.11.2) packages. The non-parametric Friedman test for matched samples was performed to test the null hypothesis that there are no significant differences between the different 2D and 2.5D segmentation approaches. When the test resulted in rejection of the null hypothesis, pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Nemenyi post-hoc test and the resulting p-values were reported in a heatmap. For the analysis of the longitudinal monitoring experiment, a mixed-design ANOVA with time as within-subject factor and group as between-subject factor on the NALV biomarker followed by a Tukey's post-hoc test to compare differences between groups, was conducted from day 0 until day 4 (influenza infection) and day 4 until day 7 (influenza-Aspergillus co-infection). A significance level of 0.05 was used to determine statistically significant differences. For correlations, Spearman's correlation test was used.

Internal validation

On the internal validation set, the highest mean DSC was obtained with the 2.5D probability averaging model (0.953 ± 0.023). The difference in DSC was statistically significant between this model and all others (Fig. 2a and b). The 2D axial model (0.951 ± 0.025) and 2.5D majority voting model (0.952 ± 0.023) also showed a statistically significant difference in DSC with the 2D coronal (0.946 ± 0.025) and 2D sagittal (0.946 ± 0.026) models. No statistically significant differences were found between the models in terms of RAVD (Table S2 and Figure S3). The lowest mean aggregated RAVD was obtained for the 2.5D majority voting model (2.623% ± 3.024%). Disease burden was found to have a modest influence on model performance (Table S3). Scans with a disease burden <15% yielded a mean DSC of 0.957, whereas those with ≥30% achieved a slightly lower mean DSC of 0.921. This performance drop is expected, as scans with extensive disease involvement are more challenging to segment accurately. Even manual segmentations in such cases often only approximate true lung boundaries. Including heterogeneous datasets during training has enhanced the model's generalisability to severe disease, though caution remains warranted for cases with the highest disease burden. 3D visualisations of the manual and model-based (2D and 2.5D) segmentations showed that, while the manual segmentation appeared axially layered and non-smooth, all model segmentations exhibited a smooth outer surface, suggesting more accurate segmentation of the lung borders compared to the manual segmentation (Fig. 2c). The high mean DSC for the 2D axial model could be explained by the fact that the manual segmentations were performed in the axial plane without concurrent visualisation in the other orientations. This case also illustrated that the 2.5D models can improve the output of the 2D models by removing erroneous voxels outside the lung region and removing the layered nature in any of the three orientations. Accuracy of imaging biomarker quantification

To study the impact of segmentation accuracy and dataset diversity on downstream biomarker quantification, we performed a sub analysis on the results on the internal validation set for the 2.5D probability averaging model (Table S3). Segmentation performance as assessed by DSC slightly decreased with higher disease burden, leading to an increased absolute difference between the automated and manual quantification of disease burden for these cases. However, we did not observe an increase in the relative accuracy of automated disease burden quantification with higher disease burden.

To evaluate agreement between manually and automatically extracted TLV, ALV and NALV biomarkers, we generated Bland–Altman plots showing the mean difference and limits of agreement and assessed the Pearson correlation on the COVID dataset (Fig. 4). An example of a micro-CT scan from the COVID dataset with automated lung segmentation separated in aerated (blue) and non-aerated compartments (orange) can be found in Figure S6. The mean differences between the manual and automated methods were −11.87 mm3 for TLV, −1.36 mm3 for ALV, and −10.52 mm3 for NALV, with nearly all data points falling within the limits of agreement. Pearson correlation coefficients of 0.98, 0.99, and 0.84 for TLV, ALV, and NALV, respectively, further support the strong agreement between manual and model-derived biomarkers.

Longitudinal monitoring of lung disease burden and treatment response

To evaluate whether our proposed method can be used effectively to longitudinally monitor lung disease burden development and response to treatment, we automatically segmented all micro-CT scans in a real-world longitudinal study (Fig. 5a). Micro-CT quantification showed a longitudinal, treatment group-dependent increase of the NALV biomarker, extracted from the automated lung segmentations for the different treatment groups (no baloxavir, baloxavir at day 0, baloxavir at day 2 and baloxavir at day 4) (Fig. 5b), consistent with progressive pneumonia and antiviral treatment effects. Statistical analysis revealed statistically significant differences in NALV from day 0 until day 4 between baloxavir d0 and baloxavir d4, and between baloxavir d0 and no baloxavir groups. Statistically significant differences in NALV were also found from day 4 until day 7 between baloxavir d0 and baloxavir d2, baloxavir d0 and baloxavir d4, and between baloxavir d0 and no baloxavir groups. These observations agree with the manually analysed data that was published before, confirming a time-dependent reduction in viral and viral-fungal lung disease burden during baloxavir marboxil treatment.29 This result suggests that the proposed DL-based pipeline can detect longitudinal changes in lung disease burden and can effectively discriminate different behaviours between groups that received different treatments.

Proof of concept for pan-rodent applicability: hamster and degu

As a proof of concept, we investigated the feasibility of applying our ensembled 2.5D probability averaging model, developed on murine micro-CT scans and validated on rat micro-CT, to yet other rodents, i.e. hamster and degu micro-CT, motivated by the importance of these rodents in modelling human disease.30, 31, 32,39,40 The automated lung segmentation results appeared visually accurate and consistent across rodent species for n = 59 hamsters and n = 4 degus (Fig. 6). These findings suggest that our proposed pipeline can be effectively applied to both species, further expanding its potential applicability to a wider range of experimental models and research studies. These findings are preliminary and need to be further validated in a larger cohort of micro-CT scans acquired from hamster and degu models.

Discussion

In this paper, we described the development and validation of a generic DL-based pipeline for automated quantification of lung pathology on micro-CT. Different 2D U-Net based segmentation models were trained in axial, coronal and sagittal planes and the outputs generated in the three orientations were combined resulting in a 2.5D approach. Although the single-orientation models, especially the axial model, already showed good performance, we found that the 2.5D approach further smoothed the outer surface and improved single-orientation predictions by removing erroneous pixels. We demonstrated the generalisability of our method to images from different disease models in mice, but also to images acquired at different resolution, on a different scanner and in a different centre. Additionally, the approach demonstrated good objective evaluation results in a rat model, supporting its generalisability to other rodent species. This was further supported by the proof-of-concept we provided for applicability of our pipeline in hamster and degu models. In summary, this approach enables automated lung pathology quantification on micro-CT and is broadly applicable across various disease models, rodent species, and acquisition protocols.

The decision to develop a segmentation model that relies on a 2.5D ensemble based on 2D U-Nets was based on several considerations. 2D models require significantly less GPU memory compared to 3D architectures. This allows for the use of larger batch sizes during training, which contributes to more stable optimisation and better generalisation. In addition, the trained 2D models have lower memory requirements during inference, making them easier to implement in experimental labs, where computational resources are often limited. Although 3D models can incorporate valuable spatial information, training 3D models often involves working with low batch sizes, and patches or downsampled volumes to fit memory limitations. Our 2.5D setup offers a compromise by incorporating spatial context through the aggregation of multiple 2D planes, while maintaining the training simplicity and efficiency of 2D models. Additionally, the 2D models could be trained in the full, original resolution.

Compared to existing methods,14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19 our pipeline demonstrated both strong performance and broad applicability. For instance, Wang et al.,14 Schoppe et al.,15 and Malimban et al.16 reported DSC values of 0.92, 0.93 and 0.97 respectively, but these were achieved exclusively on healthy mice. The studies by Sforazzini et al. (DSC = 0.905),17 Vincenzi et al. (DSC = 0.967),18 and Ferl et al. (DSC = 0.920)19 demonstrated applicability to only one or two specific pathologies. Conversely, our approach was developed on data from different longitudinal experimental studies, investigating cryptococcosis, Down syndrome and IAPA, resulting in a rich dataset ranging from healthy baseline and wild-type data, over mild lung disease towards severe pathology. Furthermore, its performance was evaluated in a wide range of research settings, including evaluation on the open-source dataset published by Rosenhain et al.,27 and DSC scores between 0.98 and 0.94 were achieved, demonstrating robustness across disease types, rodent species, and scanner configurations. This broader validation distinguishes our method from prior work and underscores its potential as a general-purpose segmentation tool for preclinical lung micro-CT. The slight drop in average DSC to 0.89 for the open-source dataset can be attributed to a difference in segmentation style and image resolution. Although made freely available to the scientific community, none of the previous studies has benchmarked their methods on this public dataset. However, benchmarking of methods is becoming increasingly important with the exponential rise of published AI models and allows for a more objective comparison between different approaches.

By normalizing the micro-CT images before model prediction, we ensured a robust pipeline that accounts for the limited standardisation of micro-CT over different scanners and labs. Before model prediction, images are calibrated to HU units, using a fully automated algorithm that extracts the relevant info from the histogram, and stripped of background labels. Additionally, our pipeline can be used on chest-restricted or whole-body scans.

Once an acceptable lung segmentation is obtained, quantitative radiomic features or imaging biomarkers, such as total, aerated and non-aerated lung volume and densities within these volumes, are extracted and examined for differences over time and between experimental groups. While metrics such as DSC and RAVD provide insight into segmentation accuracy, they do not directly assess the clinical or biological relevance of the model output. Therefore, we also directly investigated the accuracy of imaging biomarkers extracted from automated segmentations. We have observed that segmentation errors can somewhat propagate into downstream biomarker quantification, especially for cases with high disease burden, with a mean absolute error in automated disease burden quantification of 3.7% for cases with manually defined disease burden ≥30%. Given that the manually defined disease burden is also not the exact truth, the question remains whether the manual or the automated analysis is responsible for this difference. Nevertheless, we found that the biomarkers TLV, ALV and NALV extracted from manual and automated segmentations are well in agreement, showing Pearson correlations close to 1. The Pearson correlation for the non-aerated lung volume biomarker was slightly lower (0.84), suggesting that the manual and automated segmentations may exhibit more differences in non-aerated than in aerated regions. This can be attributed to the relatively higher impact that differences may have on the typically smaller volume of non-aerated compared to aerated lung regions, or to the less distinct boundary between lung parenchyma and surrounding soft tissue in the presence of severe lung pathology. This finding is consistent with the experience of biomedical experts performing manual segmentations, who note that severe lung pathology complicates finding the anatomical lung borders and achieving consistent segmentation. However, while manual and automated lung segmentations may show slight differences in the presence of disease, this study did not investigate whether the segmentation of one method is preferable over the other. While we showed that automated segmentation resulted in smoother lung borders, an in-silico trial could be conducted to objectively assess the preference for either approach, where blinded segmentations are presented to scientists for qualitative scoring. Beyond segmentation accuracy, a well-known advantage of automated over manual segmentation is its ability to reduce both inter- and intra-observer variability, thereby ensuring more consistent and repeatable measurements.

To prove the usability of our fully automated pipeline, the micro-CT data of a real-world preclinical, longitudinal study, investigating efficacy of different antiviral treatment regimens in IAPA, was analysed. Our pipeline was able to reproduce the statistically significant differences in treatment regimen efficacy found during manual analysis. Additionally, our fully automated pipeline decreases substantially the man hours spent on image quantification, being the most time-consuming step from acquisition to results, allowing scientists to reallocate valuable resources to tasks such as data interpretation.

This efficiency and generic applicability are especially critical during pandemics, such as the past COVID-19 pandemic, where we have seen that rapid data analysis of an unseen lung disease modelled in a non-murine rodent was essential to accelerate drug and vaccine development. Given its versatility for animal models and lung diseases, the DL model developed in this study is highly likely to perform well for any lung disease in any rodent animal model, as it has proven to work for rats and shown potential applicability in hamsters and degus. This DL-based pipeline is therefore an essential tool in our armamentarium for preclinical lung research and pandemic preparedness, as it allows us to provide virtually real-time answers on in vivo vaccine and therapy efficacy against lung diseases in rodent models. With refinement, this pipeline could be generalised to other, non-rodent animal models used for lung disease research, such as non-human primates.41

A limitation of this study is the reliance on a single manual segmentation as ground truth for model development, a common limitation in segmentation model development due to the resource-intensive nature of manual segmentation. However, manual segmentation is subject to both inter- and intra-observer variability, making it an approximation rather than an absolute ground truth. In addition, manual segmentations were performed in the axial plane and with interpolation between slices to speed up the segmentation process. This inherent limitation of the manual segmentations influences the quantitative performance scores, with the axial models consistently scoring better than the coronal and sagittal models.

In conclusion, our proposed DL-based pipeline enables efficient analysis of large micro-CT datasets, overcoming a key bottleneck in preclinical lung imaging studies. By integrating AI-powered longitudinal micro-CT in lung disease research, we can potentially improve the translatability of drug candidate screening in rodent models through direct quantification of lung disease burden.
