Efficacy of TDF, TAF, TMF, and TDF-to-TAF switch in chronic hepatitis B: a network meta-analysis
Authors: Shuqi Yang, Yijie Lin, Huatang Zhang, Yanlan Liang, Wenjin Yuan, Xing Wang, Wenwu Lin, Wencong Hong, Zhijun Su, Dawu Zeng, Xueping Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveWe compared the efficacy of four treatment strategies for chronic hepatitis B (CHB): tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF), tenofovir alafenamide (TAF), tenofovir amibufenamide (TMF), and TDF-to-TAF switch strategies strategy. We conducted a network meta-analysis to provide evidence-based clinical guidance.MethodsWe systematically retrieved PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Embase, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) databases up to April 2025 for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and cohort studies. We assessed literature quality using the Cochrane Risk of Bias (ROB) tool and Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). Stata 17 was employed for network meta-analysis, focusing on virological response rate, hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) clearance rate, hepatitis B e antigen (HBeAg) clearance rate, and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) normalization rate.ResultsVirological response did not differ between TMF monotherapy and TDF-to-TAF switch (OR = -0.03; 95% CI -0.42 to 0.36). HBsAg clearance was similar between TAF and TMF (95% CI: -1.17-1.14). For the rate of HBeAg loss, both TAF and TMF exhibited favorable therapeutic effects. For ALT normalization rate, TMF monotherapy was the most effective, with an average effect size of 0.11 (95% CI: -0.14-0.37), while TDF monotherapy was generally inferior to the other three treatment strategies.ConclusionOur findings may help guide the selection of individualized CHB treatment strategies. However, given the limited evidence and potential bias, these results are preliminary and must be interpreted with caution. For regimens such as TMF that lack robust data, well-established alternatives should be preferred. Long-term, multi-endpoint studies are required to confirm both efficacy and safety before routine clinical adoption.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04289-w.

Introduction

CHB, caused by the persistent infection of the Hepatitis B Virus (HBV), is a major global cause of liver cirrhosis, liver failure, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [1]. The goal is sustained viral suppression to prevent cirrhosis, HCC and improve prognosis [2].

Nucleos(t)ide analogues (NAs) are a primary treatment for CHB, effectively inhibiting viral replication by targeting the HBV DNA polymerase. TDF achieves potent viral suppression with a high genetic barrier, but long-term TDF causes dose-dependent nephrotoxicity and bone loss, including declining estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and bone mineral density (BMD) [3]. TAF, a tenofovir prodrug with hepatocyte-targeted delivery, maintains antiviral potency while minimizing renal and bone toxicity relative to TDF [4, 5]. TMF, another novel prodrug, concentrates in hepatocytes and demonstrates comparable antiviral efficacy to TAF with no excess renal or bone toxicity in early-phase studies [6, 7].

Furthermore, the TDF-to-TAF switch strategy has gained clinical attention for its potential advantages in improving renal function and bone density. This network meta-analysis compared the efficacy of TDF, TAF, TMF, and the TDF-to-TAF switch strategy in CHB treatment. We evaluated four prespecified efficacy endpoints aligned with international CHB guidelines.

These indicators are clinically critical: virological response reflects sustained viral suppression, fundamental for reducing liver inflammation and disease progression; HBsAg clearance is a hallmark of functional cure, associated with improved long-term prognosis; HBeAg clearance indicates immune control, particularly relevant in HBeAg-positive patients; and ALT normalization directly mirrors reduced hepatic inflammation, all aligned with core endpoints in international CHB management guidelines.

Methods

This study adheres to the PRISMA guidelines for systematic reviews and meta-analyses and has been registered with PROSPERO (registration number: CRD420251000965). The study protocol was pre-designed and registered to ensure transparency and traceability.

Study design and literature retrieval

This network meta-analysis evaluates the efficacy of different treatment strategies for CHB patients. We systematically searched PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Embase, and CNKI up to April 2025. Chinese search terms included “Hepatitis B”,“Tenofovir Alafenamide”,“Tenofovir Amibufenamide” etc. English search terms included “Hepatitis B,” “Tenofovir Disoproxil Fumarate,” “Vemlidy,” “Tenofovir Alafenamide,” “Tenofovir Amibufenamide,” “emtricitabine,” “TDF switching,” “TMF efficacy”. To ensure comprehensiveness, we also searched clinical trial registries—specifically, searches of ClinicalTrials.gov, WHO-ICTRP, conference abstracts, dissertations, and sponsor correspondence up to April 2025 yielded no eligible unpublished studies. Additionally, we manually searched the reference lists of relevant reviews and original studies to ensure the inclusion of all relevant studies.

Study selection and data extraction

Inclusion criteria: (1) RCTs or cohort studies; (2) participants with diagnosed CHB; (3) comparison of at least one of the following regimens: TDF, TAF, TMF, or TDF-to-TAF switch; (4) reporting of at least one primary outcome—virological response (HBV DNA < 60 IU/mL), HBsAg loss, or ALT normalization (local laboratory or APASL criteria). Exclusion criteria: (1) case reports, reviews, or non-original studies; (2) incomplete or unextractable data; (3) low-quality studies or those not reporting any primary outcome.

Endnote software was used to deduplicate initially retrieved literature. Subsequently, two researchers independently screened the literature, first identifying potentially relevant studies by reviewing titles and abstracts. For these potentially relevant studies, researchers further read the full text to make final inclusion decisions and extracted necessary data: (1) Basic study information, including author names, publication dates, sample sizes, participant gender, age distribution, and interventions; (2) Study outcome indicators; (3) Information required for bias risk assessment. When available, we extracted drug originator status; 30 of 41 studies (73%) reported originator formulations. When necessary, attempts were made to contact the original authors to clarify ambiguous information; if clarification could not be obtained, the relevant subgroups were analyzed separately to avoid bias. To address potential biases from missing data, sensitivity analyses used intention-to-treat (ITT) (11 RCTs) or complete-case (8 RCTs) approaches. For cohort studies, 2 used multiple imputation, and 20 had unspecified methods. All studies had less than 10% loss-to-follow-up. Excluding studies with high loss-to-follow-up altered pooled estimates by < 3%, confirming robustness to missing-data handling (Table S17). To minimize bias from inconsistent outcome measurements, core outcomes were standardized: HBsAg clearance was uniformly defined as undetectable serum HBsAg (< 0.05 IU/mL), and HBeAg loss as seroconversion from positive to negative (both consistent across studies). For virological response (HBV DNA < 29 IU/mL vs. < 60 IU/mL) and ALT normalization (international [APASL, ALT < 40 U/L] vs. local standards), potential measurement variations were noted for subsequent analysis.

Quality assessment

In this study, two independent researchers followed the Cochrane Handbook’s guidelines and used the ROB tool to assess the quality of RCTs. This tool evaluates studies across six dimensions: selection, implementation, measurement, follow-up, reporting, and other potential biases.

For non-randomized cohort studies, the NOS was used for scoring. The NOS scale quantifies the bias risk of cohort studies by assessing three aspects: selection, comparability, and outcome, with a total score of 9. Based on the total score, study quality was divided into three levels: high quality (scores of 7–9), medium quality (scores of 4–6), and low quality (scores < 4).

We used CINeMA [8, 9] to grade evidence quality. CINeMA grades network-meta-analysis evidence across six domains (within-study bias, across-study bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity, inconsistency) and assigns overall ratings of high, moderate, low or very low. After the above two assessments, the two researchers cross-checked the results. In case of discrepancies, a third party was consulted to determine the final result.

Statistical analysis

All missing 95% CIs were back-calculated. Heterogeneity was considered low if I² ≤ 50% and P ≥ 0.05; otherwise, we explored sources through subgroup or sensitivity analyses.

Pre-specified subgroups were HBeAg status, treatment experience, and baseline viral load (Table S7). Additional subgroups examined outcome definitions: virological response at HBV DNA < 29 IU/mL versus < 60 IU/mL, and ALT normalization by international (e.g., APASL) versus local criteria.

Risk-of-bias assessment and conventional meta-analyses used RevMan 5.4. We tested whether study design (RCT vs. cohort) modified treatment effects with interaction terms; when heterogeneity remained high, only qualitative summaries were provided.

For sensitivity analyses, influential studies were predefined as those whose exclusion reduced I² by ≥ 50% or altered the effect size by ≥ 10%, or those with unusual baseline characteristics or non-standard outcomes. Stratifying follow-up duration (TDF vs. TAF ≤ 48 vs. > 48 weeks; switch therapy ≤ 96, 96–144, >144 weeks) changed estimates by < 5% with overlapping 95% CIs, confirming temporal stability (Tables S13–S15).

Network geometry and “comparison-adjusted” funnel plots were generated in StataMP 17. Node size reflects sample size, line thickness indicates the number of direct comparisons. When closed loops existed, node-splitting tested consistency; P ≥ 0.05 supported use of a consistency model.

Comparison-adjusted funnel plots assessed publication bias, and SUCRA (Surface Under the Cumulative Ranking) probabilities were generated in StataMP 17. Higher SUCRA values suggest better relative performance, but statistical significance (P < 0.05) remained decisive. Evidence was weighted by sample size and 95% CI precision: the 19 RCTs averaged 678 participants per arm versus 365 in cohort studies, and their narrower CIs carried greater weight in the pooled estimates.

Handling of missing safety data

For the safety outcomes (BMD and eGFR), data were available in 15 of 41 studies. Measurements were harmonised (DXA at femoral neck and L1–L4; eGFR recalculated with CKD-EPI 2021) (Tables S19 and S20), timing dichotomised at 144 weeks, and thresholds set at ≥ 25% eGFR decline or ≥ 2.5% BMD loss (with sensitivity analyses at 30%/3%). Baseline comparability suggested data were missing at random; we therefore analysed the 15 complete studies and tested a worst-case scenario assuming a 20% higher event rate in the 26 studies without data. Even under this assumption TAF remained superior to TDF (MD − 0.46, 95% CI − 0.60 to − 0.32). Multiple imputation was not attempted because > 60% of data were missing and external validation was unavailable.

Results

Literature search and screening results

An initial search of various databases yielded 14 110 relevant articles. After deduplication and title/abstract screening, 13 765 records were removed. 345 articles were further screened by titles and abstracts, with 41 study records (40 journal articles plus one unpublished conference abstract [Seto 2016]) finally included after full-text review[10–49]. The literature screening process is shown in Figure S1.

Characteristics of included studies

We identified 41 eligible studies: 19 RCTs (n = 12 882) and 22 cohort studies (n = 6 875). Together they covered four direct comparisons: TDF vs. TAF, TMF vs. TAF, TDF-to-TAF switch vs. TAF, and switch vs. continued TDF (Table 1 ).

Patients were recruited from treatment-naïve, treatment-experienced, cirrhotic (compensated or decompensated), high-viral-load (> 4 log10 IU/mL), or multi-drug-resistant subgroups. Across the dataset, treatment-naïve individuals predominated, mirroring real-world referral patterns. Baseline demographics differed between RCTs (younger, fewer comorbidities) and cohorts (Table S4).

TMF evidence was the scarcest: only two RCTs (n = 666) and five cohorts (n = 1 123) compared TMF with TAF; none compared TMF directly with the TDF-to-TAF switch. Consequently, TMF’s apparent benefits in HBeAg loss and ALT normalisation rely largely on indirect comparisons, which CINeMA rated as “moderate” quality owing to single-sponsor data and wide CIs (e.g., HBsAg loss OR 0.13, 95% CI − 1.18–1.45).

Detailed baseline parameters—including viral load, cirrhosis status, outcome definitions and follow-up durations—are provided in Supplementary Tables S2, S4 and S7, where subgroup analyses show consistent effect directions (ΔOR < 10%). Risk-of-bias assessments and CINeMA evidence profiles are reported under each pairwise comparison.

Heterogeneity analysis

Only the direct comparison “TDF-to-TAF switch vs TAF monotherapy” exhibited high heterogeneity for virological response (I² = 83.7%). Meta-regression identified multi-drug resistance as the main driver (P = 0.017); excluding these studies abolished heterogeneity (I² = 0%). ALT normalisation, another head-to-head comparison between conversion therapy and TAF, initially showed moderate heterogeneity (I² = 74.3%) before applying the heterogeneity-exploration strategy. Stratifying follow-up duration at 96 weeks removed most inconsistency (≤ 96 weeks: I² = 0%; >96 weeks: I² = 28.2%). Heterogeneous ALT definitions across studies (e.g., hospital-specific criteria in Highleyman 2018) further inflated variability; adopting a uniform cut-off (< 40 U/L) reduced I² to nearly zero. Sensitivity and subgroup analyses consistently altered pooled estimates by < 3%, indicating that the observed variability is attributable to methodological rather than intrinsic differences in treatment efficacy (Tables S7, S15, S21).

Global inconsistency tests (χ²) and loop-specific node-splitting indicated no significant discrepancy between direct and indirect evidence for any outcome (all P > 0.05; Table S6). Leave-one-out sensitivity produced < 10% fluctuation in pooled estimates. Stratified analyses across study design (RCT vs. cohort) (Table S8), drug originator status (Table S10), baseline viral load, cirrhosis and follow-up duration yielded consistent effect directions and non-significant interaction terms (all P > 0.05), confirming transitivity and external validity.

Efficacy

Virological response rate

Network meta-analysis showed no significant differences in virological response among TDF, TAF, and TMF monotherapies. Notably, the TDF-to-TAF switch strategy initially appeared superior to TAF monotherapy (OR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.04–1.39) (Table 2), but this was driven by two studies (Byrne 2018, Chan 2017) that enrolled multi-drug-resistant patients. After excluding these studies, heterogeneity resolved (I²=0%) and the effect became non-significant (OR = 1.04, 95% CI: 0.87–1.24). Stratification by HBV DNA thresholds (< 29 vs. < 60 IU/mL) did not materially alter findings (Table S7).

Traditional meta-analysis of direct comparison

Virological response rate

TDF vs. TAF

Clearance rate of HBsAg

Comparisons of HBsAg clearance rates across treatment regimens (TDF vs. TAF monotherapy, TMF vs. TAF monotherapy, and TDF-to-TAF switch vs. continued TAF monotherapy) revealed no statistically significant differences. These findings indicate that TAF monotherapy did not outperform the other three regimens in terms of HBsAg clearance (Table 2).

The rate of hbeag loss

Among direct comparisons, TAF monotherapy achieved significantly higher HBeAg loss rates than both TDF monotherapy (OR = 1.50, 95% CI: 1.22–1.85) and the TDF-to-TAF switch strategy (OR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.02–1.41). Head-to-head data showed no significant difference between TMF monotherapy and TAF monotherapy (OR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.62–1.37, P > 0.05) (Table 2).

ALT normalization rate

ALT normalization rates differed across regimens. TAF monotherapy outperformed TDF (OR = 0.64, 95% CI 0.57–0.72) and the TDF-to-TAF switch (OR = 0.63, 95% CI 0.56–0.71). TMF showed no significant difference versus TAF (OR = 1.26, 95% CI 0.91–1.76) (Table 2).

Heterogeneity for TDF-to-TAF versus TAF was high (I² = 74.3%). Stratifying by treatment duration (≤ 96 vs. > 96 weeks) reduced I² to 0% and 28.2%, respectively (Table S9); stratifying by ALT criteria left the TAF vs. TDF OR unchanged (international 0.64, local 0.62) (Table S7). Meta-regression confirmed that both duration and ALT definition independently contributed to heterogeneity (P < 0.05).

Sensitivity analyses excluding studies using HBV DNA < 29 IU/mL shifted the TMF vs. TAF OR minimally from 1.23 (0.93–1.63) to 0.97 (0.57–1.46) with I² remaining 0% (Table S7). TMF’s ALT-normalization benefit is supported up to 96 weeks; longer-term data are not yet available.

Cumulative ranking probability analysis (SUCRA)

SUCRA rankings indicated that the TDF-to-TAF switch strategy achieved the highest probability of superior virological response (84.0%), followed by TMF monotherapy (76.8%), although the between-group difference was not statistically significant (OR = − 0.03; 95% CI − 0.42–0.36). For HBsAg clearance, TAF monotherapy ranked highest (63.2%), but overall clearance rates remained < 5%, limiting clinical relevance. For HBeAg loss, TAF (85.1%) and TMF (79.5%) demonstrated comparable efficacy (OR = 0.00; 95% CI − 0.25–0.25) (Fig. 2), both outperforming TDF. Notably, TMF monotherapy ranked highest for ALT normalization (SUCRA 93.2%), with a clinically meaningful effect size (OR = 0.51; 95% CI 0.25–0.78) meeting the EASL threshold (< 40 U/L). Safety outcomes

Across 15 studies with harmonised bone-mineral density (BMD) and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) data, TDF consistently produced greater losses than TAF. At 48 weeks, TDF reduced hip BMD by − 0.57% (95% CI − 0.70% to − 0.43%) and eGFR by − 4.8 mL/min/1.73 m²; these deficits persisted at 96 weeks (Table S11). Switching from TDF to TAF attenuated bone loss through 144 weeks (hip BMD − 0.78% vs. − 1.53% for continued TDF) (Table S12). Total cholesterol fell slightly more with TDF (− 1.17 mg/dL); serious adverse events occurred in < 5% of participants across regimens. Hepatic or drug-interaction events were not extractable.

Trim-and-fill was performed but did not impute any studies, leaving pooled estimates unchanged (Figure S4). A sensitivity analysis assuming a 20% higher adverse-event rate among the 26 studies lacking safety data did not overturn TAF’s renal or bone benefit.

Three registered but unpublished TMF safety trials were identified; none reported renal or BMD outcomes. Consequently, no direct comparison of TMF vs. TAF (or any other regimen) on renal or bone safety is currently feasible, highlighting a critical evidence gap. Formal funnel-plot or Egger/Begg tests remained under-powered (< 10 studies per comparison). A safety-focused update will be registered once ≥ 10 studies per comparison are available.

Small-sample effect assessment

Comparison-adjusted funnel plots for all efficacy outcomes—virological response, HBsAg clearance, HBeAg loss, and ALT normalization—showed broadly symmetric distributions around the midline, indicating a low risk of publication bias. Some scatter was observed, likely attributable to variable sample sizes and study quality, but overall symmetry was preserved (Fig. 4). Begg’s test detected no significant publication bias (Table S5 and Table S18). Trim-and-fill imputation, Orwin’s fail-safe N (> 60 negative studies required), and comprehensive registry and sponsor checks further confirmed robustness against potential industry sponsorship. Taken together, these findings support the reliability of the efficacy results.

Discussion

This network meta-analysis compared the efficacy and safety of four CHB treatment strategies—TDF, TAF, TMF, and TDF-to-TAF switch—across four key endpoints: virological response, HBsAg loss, HBeAg loss, and ALT normalization. TAF consistently outperformed TDF in HBeAg loss (OR = 1.50, 95% CI 1.22–1.85) and showed a more favourable renal and bone safety profile, with significantly less hip and spine BMD loss (hip−0.57% vs−0.34%, p < 0.01) and minimal eGFR decline (< 0.2 mL/min/1.73 m²), supporting its use as a long-term option, especially in patients with baseline renal or bone risk [50]. In contrast, TDF-to-TAF switch achieved the highest SUCRA (84.0%) for virological response, but this advantage disappeared after excluding studies with multidrug-resistant patients (OR = 1.04, 95% CI 0.87–1.24), underscoring the importance of stratifying patients by resistance status.

TMF demonstrated the highest SUCRA value (93.2%) for ALT normalization, suggesting a potential benefit in reducing hepatic inflammation [51]. However, the direct comparison with TAF did not reach statistical significance (OR = 0.11, 95% CI−0.14–0.37), and all TMF studies were conducted in Chinese populations with genotypes B/C, used a single manufacturer’s formulation, and had limited follow-up (92.86% ≤48 weeks, maximum 96 weeks). None reported serial BMD or eGFR measurements beyond 48 weeks, precluding any meaningful assessment of long-term bone or renal safety. Until multicentre, ≥ 96-week data become available, TMF should be considered only for a short, closely monitored trial in patients with persistently elevated ALT and preserved renal function.

Based on these findings, we propose a practical, risk-guided approach. Patients with baseline renal impairment (eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m²) or accelerated bone loss (> 2% per year) should start or switch to TAF [52]. In those with normal renal function and refractory ALT elevation (≥ 2× ULN), a 48-week trial of TMF may be reasonable, provided eGFR and hip BMD are reassessed every 12 weeks; treatment should be converted to TAF if eGFR falls by ≥ 10% or BMD loss exceeds 2% annually. For anticipated treatment durations beyond 96 weeks, TAF remains the default choice given its established long-term safety record. TDF can still be used in treatment-naïve patients without renal or bone risk factors, especially where cost is a major concern.

Real-world evidence further supports these recommendations. In cohort studies, TDF-treated patients who switched to TAF showed rapid improvement in eGFR and stabilization of BMD, particularly among those with baseline renal impairment or osteoporosis. Conversely, early virological response rates were numerically higher with TDF than with TAF in healthy, treatment-naïve patients, suggesting that TDF can still be justified when short-term cost savings are critical and renal or bone risk is negligible. Pharmacoeconomic analyses from China and South Korea indicate that TMF is currently priced between TDF and TAF; formal cost-effectiveness studies are now warranted to clarify its role in resource-limited settings. Until such data are available, clinicians should prioritize regimens with the most mature safety profiles for lifelong therapy.

Several Limitations should be acknowledged. First, TMF evidence is confined to short-term, single-manufacturer studies in Chinese patients, Limiting generalisability to other ethnicities and genotypes. Second, long-term comparative safety data, particularly on bone and renal outcomes, are lacking for TMF and for TDF-to-TAF switch beyond 144 weeks. Third, only a minority of studies systematically reported cardiovascular or drug-interaction adverse events. Finally, the absence of head-to-head RCTs comparing TMF with TDF-to-TAF switch introduces potential indirect-comparison bias. Sensitivity analyses across HBV genotype, comorbidity status, outcome definition, and study quality (exclusion of four high-risk studies, Table S16 and S17) all yielded stable pooled estimates. Multiregional, independently funded trials with ≥ 3-year follow-up are urgently needed to validate these preliminary findings and to refine genotype-specific treatment algorithms.
