Global burden of skin cancers and trends from 1990 to 2021: An observational study based on secondary analysis of GBD 2021
Authors: Ruosu Zhang, Yike Zhao, Yanling Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Skin cancer has emerged as a major global health challenge, with persistent variations in the burden across countries and regions. The objective of this study was to evaluate the global, regional, and national burden of malignant melanoma (MM), squamous cell carcinoma, and basal cell carcinoma from 1990 to 2021, and to analyze their temporal trends and associations with the Socio-demographic Index. We selected data on cancer incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) from 204 countries and regions between 1990 and 2021, using the Global Burden of Disease 2021 study. Temporal trends were quantified by calculating the incidence, mortality, and DALY rates as well as their percentage changes, while also analyzing the association between DALYs and Socio-demographic Index. Globally in 2021, 2.3 million cases of illness, 61.5 thousand deaths, and 1678.8 thousand DALYs were attributed to MM; squamous cell carcinoma caused 2.8 million prevalent cases, 56.9 thousand deaths, and 1210.9 thousand DALYs; and basal cell carcinoma accounted for 2.2 million epidemic cases and 2 thousand DALYs. Epidemic cases, deaths, DALYs counts, and age-standardized point rates are rising, but MM-induced deaths and DALYs rates have declined over the past 3 decades. The burden of skin cancer is higher in Australasia and High-income North America than in other regions. Under the Global Burden of Disease regional classifications, Australasia comprises Australia, New Zealand, and surrounding territories, while High-income North America encompasses the United States and Canada. At the national level, the burden is higher in Australia, New Zealand, and the United States; the burden is generally higher in men than in women, and is higher in older adults than in young people. Our study indicates that skin cancer remains an important public health issue worldwide. High-income regions such as Australasia and North America will continue to bear the greatest burden. Simultaneously, the burden on East Asia, Latin America, and Africa is rapidly increasing. This study also showed that the disease burden was greater in men and older adults. Therefore, countries and regions should attach great importance to the prevention and treatment of skin cancers, and develop and improve preventive measures.

1. Introduction

Skin cancer is currently one of the most prevalent cancers in the world, with patients affected in all regions and countries, predominantly affecting adult populations.[1–3] It is mainly categorized as malignant melanoma (MM) and non-melanoma skin cancers (NMSCs), the latter of which includes basal cell carcinoma (BCC) and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) as the major histologic subtypes.[4] The incidence of skin cancer is increasing annually; for example, the cancer with the highest incidence in 2017 was NMSC.[1,5] MM is not only the third most prevalent skin cancer but is also highly aggressive and highly prone to metastasis.[4,6] This epidemiological trend is expected to occur in the near future due to enhanced diagnosis and increased life expectancy. This makes it a preeminent threat to public health, resulting in substantial economic costs.[7]

According to previous studies, ultraviolet (UV) radiation is an important risk factor for skin cancer.[8,9] Different regions, races, ages, and living standards also affect the prevalence.[1,9,10] Caucasians seem to have more skin cancers than people of color.[11] Addressing inequalities in skin cancer incidence across regions and countries is key to optimizing resource allocation. In line with this need, there is currently little comprehensive analysis of global and regional transnational burden inequalities in skin cancer and its subtypes.

The Global Burden of Diseases (GBD), Injuries, and Risk Factors Study 2021 includes mortality and prevalence in 204 countries and territories and subnational estimates for 21 countries and territories. While several studies have explored the epidemiology of skin cancers, a comprehensive comparison of the long-term global trends using the most recent data remains limited. Therefore, the objective of this study was to perform a systematic analysis of the global, regional, and national burden of MM, SCC, and BCC from 1990 to 2021 using data from the GBD 2021 study. Specifically, we aimed to evaluate temporal trends in prevalence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs); compare the disease patterns across different geographic regions; and examine the associations between these burdens and the Socio-demographic Index (SDI). These findings are intended to provide evidence-based insights for optimizing public health strategies and resource allocation for skin cancer prevention and management.

2. Methods

This study is an observational, cross-sectional analysis using secondary data from the GBD 2021 study. The GBD 2021 study estimated the burden of 3 diseases and injuries from 1990 to 2021 in 204 countries, territories, and 21 regions. This analysis focused on 3 major skin cancers: MM, BCC, and SCC. The data are accessible through the Global Health Data Exchange query tool (https://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-results-tool) and categorized by region, gender, and country.[12]

All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing), with a P < .05.

This study adheres to the GATHER (Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting) statement to ensure the quality and transparency of health estimates reporting. A completed GATHER checklist is provided as a supplementary file.

2.1. Data sources

The data sources of the analysis focused on the GBD database. All data are de-identified and publicly available. The GBD database offers detailed information on the burden of skin cancers worldwide, including the prevalence, death rate, and DALYs. The analysis focused on data related to MM, BCC, and SCC from 1990 to 2021, covering 204 countries, territories, and 21 regions globally. The GBD calculated essential statistics for 20 different age categories ranging from the time of birth to 95 years and older, covering males, females, and all sexes. In our study, the prevalence, death rate, and DALYs of MM, BCC, and SCC were assessed.[12]

Data were included based on the following criteria: Disease classifications: Cases formally diagnosed as MM, SCC, or BCC according to the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10); Timeframe: All available annual data from 1990 to 2021; Geography: All 204 countries and territories across 21 GBD regions; and Demographics: Both sexes and all age groups (from birth to 95+ years).

We also excluded other rare histological subtypes of skin cancer that were not separately categorized under MM, SCC, or BCC in the GBD 2021 database, and data entries with missing or logically inconsistent geographical or temporal identifiers.

The sample size of this study encompasses the entire global population-level data aggregated by the GBD 2021 study across 204 countries and 21 regions. Specifically, the analysis included estimates derived from thousands of primary data sources, including vital registration, cancer registries, and representative surveys, covering the global burden of skin cancer over a 32-year period.

2.2. Acquisition

The data were acquired through the official GBD database platform and exported in CSV format. In the R environment, data were loaded using the read.csv function to ensure data accuracy and integrity.

2.3. Data preprocessing

Before conducting the analysis, the raw data were preprocessed to ensure data quality and meet the requirements for analysis.

For data cleaning, variables related to MM, BCC, and SCC were extracted, including region, year, sex, and age. Data were formatted to ensure that the categorical and numerical variables were correctly specified.

For data integration and grouping, data for MM, BCC, and SCC were extracted and grouped by region, year, and sex separately. For multidimensional data, the dplyr package was used for integration and grouping.

2.4. Data analysis

Descriptive statistical analyses for prevalence, death rate, and DALYs of MM, BCC, and SCC were calculated using the dplyr package, including means, medians, standard deviations, and interquartile ranges. The descriptive statistical analysis mainly included the prevalence number, prevalence rate, age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR), change in ASPR, death number, age-standardized death rate (ASDR), change in ASDR, number of DALYs, age-standardized DALYs rate, and changes in age-standardized DALYs rate. Stratified analysis was conducted by region, sex, and age group to assess the distribution characteristics of MM, BCC, and SCC.

Descriptive statistical analysis for the prevalence of MM, BCC, and SCC, death rate of MM and SCC in 2021, and percentage change in ASPR of MM, BCC, and SCC were calculated using tidyverse and readr packages.

A Geographic Information System was employed in combination with the ggmap, maps, rgdal, dplyr, cowplot, patchwork, purrr, stringr, maptools, sf, and tmaptools packages to create global, regional, and national maps of the prevalence, death rate, and DALYs of MM, BCC, and SCC in 2021.

Linear regression models were fitted to the trends, and slopes with confidence intervals were calculated to evaluate the upward or downward trends based on the prevalence, death rate, and DALYs rate in MM, BCC, and SCC by sex and age in 2021.

ASPR and age-standardized DALYs rates were visualized using the tidyverse, ggplot2, and scales packages to analyze the differences in GBD regions by sex in MM, BCC, and SCC in 2021. ASDR was visualized using packages to compare the differences in GBD regions by sex between MM and SCC in 2021. The change in ASPR was also used to analyze the changes in GBD regions by sex in MM, BCC, and SCC from 1990 to 2021.

The SDI was used to demonstrate variations in the burden of MM, BCC, and SCC attributed to different levels of socioeconomic development. In 2021, the SDI ranged from 0 (low SDI) to 100 (high SDI), classifying countries into 5 groups based on quintiles: high SDI, high-middle SDI, middle SDI, low-middle SDI, and low SDI.[13]

2.5. Ethical considerations

The GBD study uses de-identified, publicly available data sources. As this study was a secondary analysis of aggregated, anonymous data from the GBD 2021 study, institutional review board approval and patient informed consent were not required.

3. Results

3.1. Global level

Globally, in 2021, for all ages and both sexes combined, the number of prevalent cases of the 3 skin cancers was 2.3 million for MM, 2.8 million for SCC, and 2.2 million for BCC. During the same period, the global number of deaths was 61.5 thousand for MM and 56.9 thousand for SCC, while the number of DALYs was 1678.8 thousand for MM, 1210.9 thousand for SCC, and 2.0 thousand for BCC (Table 1).

4. Discussion

4.1. Findings and comparison with other studies

Based on data from the GBD, we comprehensively analyzed the time and spatial trends in the prevalence, death, and DALYs of skin cancers at the global, regional, and national levels by age and sex from 1990 to 2021. Globally, by 2021, MM accounted for 2.3 million prevalent cases, 61.5 thousand deaths, and 1678.8 thousand DALYs, while SCC accounted for 2.8 million prevalent cases, 56.9 thousand deaths, and 1210.9 thousand DALYs, and BCC accounted for 2.2 million prevalent cases and 2.0 thousand DALYs.

Prevalent cases, deaths, DALYs counts, and age-standardized point rates are on the rise, but the mortality and DALYs rates due to MM have decreased over the past 3 decades. This may be due to population growth, aging, and advances in medical care.

In this study, the global prevalent cases of MM in 2021 (2.3 million) were notably comparable to those of SCC (2.8 million) and BCC (2.2 million). This observation warrants careful interpretation, given that MM is significantly more aggressive than NMSCs. First, this parity may stem from the inherent reporting structures of the GBD database; while MM is strictly monitored in most oncology registries due to its metastatic potential, BCC and SCC are frequently treated as minor outpatient procedures and may not be consistently captured in long-term prevalence statistics. Second, there is a recognized underreporting of NMSCs in low- and middle-income regions, where medical infrastructure for histopathological confirmation is often lacking. Consequently, the reported prevalence of BCC and SCC likely represents only a fraction of the actual global burden. Finally, the GBD estimation model for prevalence incorporates various data sources that may prioritize mortality-heavy diseases like MM for exhaustive tracking, whereas the relatively lower mortality of BCC may lead to more conservative prevalence estimations in certain territories.

A study in 2021 found that, although there was a slight decrease in the number of deaths and DALYs, the global prevalence of MM dramatically increased from 1990 to 2019. Also, there was a remarkable increase in these 3 indicators of BCC and SCC. Another study in 2021 showed that the incidence, prevalence, and DALYs of skin cancers are increasing disproportionately among different demographic groups.[14] There are many other studies showed similar phenomena, which is basically consistent with this study results that skin cancer has become a rapidly evolving disease that humans urgently need to be dealt with.[15–17]

Central Europe, Eastern Europe, Central Asia, Latin America, the Caribbean, and high-income countries had consistently higher DALYs for skin cancers than the global average between 1990 and 2017.[14] Australasia, High‐income North America, and Europe, in particular, continue to be the areas most burdened by skin cancers. The prevalence and death rates of skin cancers have shown a significant increasing trend in East Asia, Latin America, and Sub-Saharan Africa, partly due to their increase in population density and inadequate preventive and control measures.

Studies on risk factors were lacking in this study, but based on previous studies, UV is a well-established risk factor for skin cancer.[18–21] Previous evidence has suggested that occupational exposure to UV is common and causes substantial, inequitable, and growing attributable burden of NMSC.[21,22] So governments have a duty to protect outdoor workers from hazardous exposure to UV and attributable NMSC burden and inequalities. However, some studies have suggested that UV is only a risk factor for light-colored people; it may not be an important risk factor for skin cancers in colored people.[23] Some ubiquitous, naturally occurring metalloids also pose a significant human cancer risk. A previous quantitative risk assessment study showed that consumption of arsenic in food increases the incidence of skin cancers.[24] A prospective study of male in 2021 found that light eye color was associated with higher risks of SCC and BCC, but not MM.[25] Moreover, immunosuppressed patients were at a significantly increased risk of developing cutaneous malignancies.[26]

Combined with this study and some previous studies, we found poor agreement between point prevalence and deaths due to skin cancer when ranking these regions. For example, despite the low prevalence of MM and SCC in Africa, death rates are very high globally.[1,14] The same situation also occurs with SCC data in China.[27,28] Studies have shown that Asian melanoma patients typically present with more advanced disease and low 5-year survival rates, suggesting that skin cancer may be underreported in Asian countries.[29] This may be related to a variety of factors, such as the backwardness of medical care and the growing population. Population aging contributed to global cancer-related DALYs, revealing a bell-shaped pattern when stratified by socioeconomic development, affecting middle-SDI countries the most.[30] In response to an increasingly aging population and reducing cancer-related DALYs, it is necessary to allocate health care resources to older adults and prioritize interventions for them.[30,31]

There are also some relationships between data on skin cancers and the SDI. The incidence is usually higher in areas with high SDI than in areas with low SDI.[2] And DALYs were also higher in skin cancers in areas with high SDI, especially in Australasia and high-income North America. The rising burden of cancer in low- and middle-income countries puts pressure on already weak healthcare and economic infrastructure. Therefore, cancer screening and early diagnosis in low- and middle-income countries are very important and urgently require development and construction.[32,33]

A study in EU states showed that the prevalence and death rates of MM were greater in males than in females from 1990 to 2019.[34] According to another study, males have a higher incidence, prevalence, DALY, and mortality rates from MM and NMSC every year since 1990 in the United States.[35] This study also found that males have a higher susceptibility than females.

4.2. Advantages and limitations

One advantage of this study is that we provided the most recent available estimates and comprehensive estimates of the incidence, mortality, and DALY levels and trends associated with skin cancer at the global, regional, and national levels from 1990 to 2021. However, this study has some limitations. First, the reliance on modeled data from GBD introduces uncertainties due to missing raw data in many regions and nations, which may affect the accuracy of the study. This phenomenon may be due to the imperfect health system in low-income areas that meets the requirements of collecting and recording disease cases. Second, the GBD database does not include data on histological subtypes, risk factors, and BCC deaths associated with skin cancer. Therefore, this study did not estimate the spatial and temporal trends in incidence, death, or DALYs stratified of these skin cancers by these factors. Third, the quality of GBD skin cancer data cannot be guaranteed. For example, underreporting in low-income regions or overestimation in areas with better diagnostic facilities could result in errors. Moreover, health systems, disease surveillance, and reporting standards vary across regions, which can lead to errors.

5. Conclusions

In conclusion, this study fulfilled its objective of providing a comprehensive evaluation of the global skin cancer burden from 1990 to 2021. Our findings demonstrate that skin cancer remains a significant and evolving public health challenge worldwide. While death and DALY rates for MM have shown a declining trend over the past 3 decades, the overall burden of SCC and BCC continues to rise, particularly in terms of prevalence. Regarding our research questions on geographic and SDI disparities, high-income regions such as Australasia and North America remain the most heavily burdened; however, the rapid increase in disease burden in East Asia, Latin America, and Africa highlights an urgent need for shifting global health priorities. Furthermore, our analysis confirms that the burden is disproportionately higher among males and older populations. Therefore, countries should prioritize the implementation of targeted preventive measures, such as enhanced UV protection and accessible screening programs, to mitigate the growing global impact of these malignancies.
