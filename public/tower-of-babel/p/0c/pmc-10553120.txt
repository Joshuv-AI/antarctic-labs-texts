Machine learning-based metabolism-related genes signature, single-cell RNA sequencing, and experimental validation in hypersensitivity pneumonitis
Authors: Jie He, Bo Wang, Meifeng Chen, Lingmeng Song, Hezhi Li
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Metabolism is involved in the pathogenesis of hypersensitivity pneumonitis. To identify diagnostic feature biomarkers based on metabolism-related genes (MRGs) and determine the correlation between MRGs and M2 macrophages in patients with hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP). We retrieved the gene expression matrix from the Gene Expression Omnibus database. The differentially expressed MRGs (DE-MRGs) between healthy control (HC) and patients with HP were identified using the “DESeq2” R package. The “clusterProfiler” R package was used to perform “Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway enrichment analyses” on DE-MRGs. We used machine learning algorithms for screening diagnostic feature biomarkers for HP. The “receiver operating characteristic curve” was used to evaluate diagnostic feature biomarkers’ discriminating ability. Next, we used the “Cell-type Identification by Estimating Relative Subsets of RNA Transcripts” algorithm to determine the infiltration status of 22 types of immune cells in the HC and HP groups. Single-cell sequencing and qRT-PCR were used to validate the diagnostic feature biomarkers. Furthermore, the status of macrophage polarization in the peripheral blood of patients with HP was determined using flow cytometry. Finally, the correlation between the proportion of M2 macrophages in peripheral blood and the diagnostic biomarker expression profile in HP patients was determined using Spearman analysis. We identified a total of 311 DE-MRGs. Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway enrichment analysis showed that DE-MRGs were primarily enriched in processes like steroid hormone biosynthesis, drug metabolism, retinol metabolism, etc. Finally, we identified NPR3, GPX3, and SULF1 as diagnostic feature biomarkers for HP using machine learning algorithms. The bioinformatic results were validated using the experimental results. The CIERSORT algorithm and flow cytometry showed a significant difference in the proportion of M2 macrophages in the HC and HP groups. The expression of SULF1 was positively correlated with the proportion of M2-type macrophages. In addition, a positive correlation was observed between SULF1 expression and M2 macrophage proportion. Finally, we identified NPR3, GPX3, and SULF1 as diagnostic feature biomarkers for HP. Further, a correlation between SULF1 and M2 macrophages was observed, providing a novel perspective for treating patients with HP and future studies.

1. Introduction

Hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP) is an interstitial lung disease (ILD) caused by immune reactions in response to recurrent exposure to environmental allergens, such as bacteria, mycobacteria, low-molecular compounds, and other substances in susceptible individuals.[1] The prevalence of HP varies based on climate, occupational exposure, and regional differences in environmental exposure; however, only a few cohort studies on HP are available. In the USA, the prevalence of HP is 1.67 to 2.71/100,000 individuals, and the cumulative annual incidence of HP is 1.28 to 1.94/100,000 individuals.[2] However, the prevalence of HP could be significantly higher than reported numbers. In India, 47.3% of all patients with ILD suffer from HP.[3] Based on a study conducted between 1980 and 2002, the age-adjusted death rate in patients with HP was 0.29/million in the USA, and the age-adjusted death rate was higher in farmers.[4] Immunomodulatory responses induced after environmental exposure are associated with the onset and progression of HP. However, no specific stimulatory antigens have been identified as the cause of HP in nearly 60% of patients,[5] thereby increasing the complexity of HP diagnosis. The definitive HP diagnosis requires a multidisciplinary discussion between clinicians, radiologists, and pathologists. In addition, the rapid progression of pulmonary parenchymal fibrosis, especially chronic HP (cHP), may present clinical symptoms and imaging features similar to other ILDs.[6]

HP onset and progression are complex and multifactorial. It involves multiple mediators and pathways. Interestingly, the involvement of metabolic disorders in HP has gained attention. Dasgupta et al[3] screened the metabolic profiles in serum, exhaled air condensate, and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid of the patients with HP using proton nuclear magnetic resonance. The results showed abnormalities in patients’ lactate, pyruvate, and proline metabolism. D’Alessandro et al[7] showed significant differences in lipid metabolites in patients with HP and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis in the alveolar lavage fluid. Excess accumulation of lipids causes changes in cellular homeostasis and activates the glycogen-genic and lipogenic signaling pathways.

Furthermore, the quantity and quality of lipids contribute to lipotoxicity-induced alveolar injury by inducing inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and cell death.[8] Statins regulating lipid metabolism inhibit pulmonary fibrosis.[9] Together, this shows the critical involvement of various metabolites in the progression of HP. Hence, additional studies should be conducted to identify metabolism-related mechanisms in HP onset and progression, which could aid in designing therapeutic strategies for HP in the future.

Various studies have used bioinformatic tools and sequencing technology to identify disease-specific biomarkers. This study used multiple bioinformatics algorithms to determine the role of metabolism-related genes (MRGs) in HP pathogenesis. We identified 3 MRGs as diagnostic feature biomarkers for HP and validated these results using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and clinical samples. Furthermore, we determined the correlation between the proportion of M2 macrophages and diagnostic features biomarkers. In summary, our results shed light on new perspectives and insights for treating patients with HP.

The GSE150910 dataset contains data on lung tissues from 103 normal samples and 82 patients with cHP. The GSE47460-GPL6480 dataset contains data on lung tissues from 17 normal samples and 9 patients with HP. The GSE47460-GPL14550 dataset contains data on lung tissues from 91 normal samples and 21 patients with HP. Moreover, the GSE184316 dataset contains data on lung tissues from 24 normal samples and 36 patients with fibrotic HP. The details of these 4 datasets are shown in Table 1. We used Perl scripts for extracting the gene expression matrix, and the platform annotation file was used to annotate probes.

2.2. Identification and differential expression analysis of MRGs

Differential expression analysis was performed on 2577 MRGs identified from previous studies.[12] The Perl scripts were used to extract MRGs from the gene expression matrix. The differential expressed MRGs (DE-MRGs) in healthy controls (HC) and patients with HP from GSE150910 were identified using the “DESeq2” R package. The thresholds for determining DE-MRGs were “|log2Fold Change|>1” and “false discovery rate < 0.05.” In addition, principal component analysis (PCA) was used to validate the reproducibility of the GSE150910 data.

2.3. Functional enrichment analysis

The “clusterprofiler” R package was used to perform Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses for identifying potential functions and pathways enriched by MRGs. We used “Gene Set enrichment analysis (GSEA)” for identifying KEGG terms enriched in HCs and patients in the HP group. Statistical significance was set at a P < .05.

2.4. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

The “WGCNA” R package was used to perform WGCNA. First, for WGNCA, we selected DE-MRGs with variances > all quartile variances. It is necessary to eliminate outliers before constructing the network since they influence the results of the network module analysis. We used the inter-array correlation between chips to evaluate microarray data distribution and eliminate the outliers with significantly lower mean inter-array correlation values. The gene co-expression network confirmed no scale distribution; in other words, it conforms to a power law distribution. Consistent with the scale-free network distribution, the results were obtained using weighted coefficients, which were screened using WGCNA. Finally, we identified key genes with potential biological significance in the network using genes and gene modules associated with the clinical information. The gene significance of any gene was relative to a specific dependent variable. The module significance relative to a particular dependent variable in a module was the correlation coefficient between its characteristic gene and the level of the dependent variable. The module membership of a gene in a module is the correlation coefficient between the gene and the characteristic gene of this module.

2.5. Identification of diagnostic feature biomarkers based on DE-MRGs

We used multiple machine-learning algorithms to identify diagnostic feature biomarkers. We used algorithms like “least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis” and penalty functions for constructing a refined model. LASSO regression analysis reduces the set of indicators by setting the coefficients of some indicators to zero. In machine learning, an algorithm like “support vector machine (SVM)” is a supervised learning model and a discrimination method widely used for classification, pattern recognition, and regression analysis. The random forest (RF) algorithm also eliminated overfitting, achieved reliable accuracy, and identified optimal genes from the training cohort. Hence, we used the RF algorithm for screening appropriate diagnostic feature biomarkers. In the RF algorithm, the number of trees was set to 500, and the Gini index was the measure of importance. Genes with the “MeanDecreaseGini” values > 1 were retained. Finally, the overlapping genes screened by “WGCNA,” “LASSO,” “SVM,” and “RF” algorithms were considered diagnostic feature biomarkers. We validated the expression profiles of these candidate genes in the validation cohort. The “receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves” measured the accuracy of diagnostic feature biomarkers for determining dichotomous outcomes.

2.6. scRNA-seq analysis

The GSE135893[13] dataset containing scRNA-seq data of 10 HCs and 3 patients with cHP was retrieved from the GEO database.

The “Seurat” R package was used for performing computational analyses on GSE193782. The “Seurat” R package was used for performing quality control assessment on both datasets (200 < no. of feature RNA < 5000; percent ribosomal genes > 3; % of mitochondrial gene < 20, the proportion of erythrocyte genes < 0.1). PCA was performed using the “RunPCA” tool of the “Seurat” R package. The scRNA-seq data was normalized using the “NormalizeData” functions of the “Seurat.” The “IntegrateData” and “FindIntegrationAnchors” algorithms of RPCA-based Seurat were used for merging data from different samples. Universal manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) were used for dimensionality reduction. Louvain clusters and UMAP were determined using 30 primary principal components with the Seurat “FindClusters” and “RunUMAP” functions, respectively, and a resolution of 0.8. The “FindAllMarkers” tool of the “Seurat” R package was used to identify each cluster’s markers. The “CellMarker,” “PanglaoDB,” and original research articles included in both datasets were used to determine the cell types based on the markers in each cluster. The distribution of genes and their expression profiles were visualized using the “VlnPlot,” “FeaturePlot,” and “DotPlot” functions of the “Seurat” R package. The “select” function of the R package was used to identify the diagnostic feature biomarkers from the GSE135893 dataset.

2.7. Analyzing the landscape of infiltrating immune cells

We used the “Cell-type Identification by Estimating Relative Subsets of RNA Transcripts (CIBERSORT)” R package for analyzing the landscape of immune cells infiltrating in patients with HP and HCs. The expression profile of immune cell-related genes was based on “CIBERSORT R script v1.03,” and the algorithm was run using the LM22 signature for 1000 permutations. The difference in immune cells infiltrating in HCs and patients with HP was determined using the “ggplot” R package.

2.8. Patients with HP and HCs

A total of 107 patients underwent treatment for ILD at our department between August 2021 and December 2022. We diagnosed cHP in patients based on their clinical records, radiology data, and histology criteria.[14,15] To determine and validate the bird-related HP diagnosis, we performed the inhalation provocation test ad an avian antigen on the patients.[16] Based on the inhalation provocation test data, we diagnosed 10 patients with chronic bird-related HP based. The patients with a history of autoimmune or pulmonary disease, acute coronary syndrome, chronic kidney disease, severe heart failure (NYHA III and above), HIV infection, and any previously diagnosed psychiatric disease were excluded from the study. In this study, the patients with chronic bird-related HP had no previous medical history of bronchial asthma, atopic dermatitis, or undergoing any medical treatment at the time of diagnosis. Ten healthy individuals as HCs were enrolled at the hospital’s health examination center.

2.9. RNA extraction and real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

The Ficoll solution (Solarbio Life Sciences, Beijing, China) was used to extract peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from the patient’s blood. We extracted total RNA from PBMCs using the RNA extraction kit (Omega, Guangzhou, China). RNA was reverse transcribed with the PrimeScript RT Master Mix Kit (Takara, Dalian, China). We measured gene expression using TB Green PreMix Ex Taq Kit (Takara). The sequences of primers used in this study are listed in Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/J736. The 2−ΔΔCt method determined the relative gene expression normalized to GAPDH.

2.10. Flow cytometry

For identifying immune cells, 5 mL of peripheral venous blood was collected in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid anticoagulation tubes. Then, the blood samples were stored at room temperature (20–30°C) for 6 hours before processing. For analyzing the mononuclear cell phenotypes, we used fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-CD14, allophycocyanin-CD86, and phycoerythrin-cyanine7-CD163 monoclonal antibodies (eBioscience, San Diego, CA). CD14-FITC and CD86-allophycocyanin were used for double-staining M1 macrophages, whereas CD14-FITC and CD163-PE were used for double-staining M2 macrophages. The detailed steps were as follows. 20 μL of antibodies were added to 100 µL of anticoagulated whole blood, gently mixed, and incubated for 30 minutes at room temperature; 1 mL hemolysin was added and incubated in the dark for 10 minutes, centrifuged at 1600 rpm for 5 minutes, and the supernatant was discarded; next, we added 2 mL PBS to each tube at 4°C, vortexed, mixed, centrifuged at 1600 rpm for 5 minutes. Again, the supernatant was discarded; and the cells were fixed in 1% paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes at 4°C, stored at 4°C, and analyzed within 24 hours. NovoCyte Flow Cytometry (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA) was used for compensating and analyzing data using the appropriate gating setting for cells. Finally, the proportion of all cell types was measured using Flow Jo software, and the data were analyzed and visualized using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad Prism Software Inc., La Jolla, CA).

2.11. Statistical analysis

R software (version 4.1.0, R Statistical Software, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), GraphPad Prism (version 8.0), and Perl scripts were used to perform statistical analyses. Spearman’s Rank correlation tested the correlation between variables. P < .05 was considered significantly difficult. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to determine the differential functions between the 2 groups. Statistical significance was set at a P < .05. We used the MedCalc 20.1 software (MedCalc Software Ltd., Ostend, Belgium) for data analysis and generated 3 ROC curves.

We identified 3 diagnostic feature biomarkers using a 4-machine learning algorithm (Fig. 5A). Interestingly, a high SULF1 expression level was observed in patients in the HP group (Fig. 5B). 3.8. Correlation between diagnostic feature biomarkers and proportion of M2 macrophages

qRT-PCR and flow cytometry was performed on clinical samples to determine the correlation between NPR3, GPX3, and SULF1 expression, which were screened using bioinformatics, and the proportion of M2 macrophages. Spearman correlation results showed no significant correlation between NPR3 and GPX3 expression and the proportion of M2 macrophages (r = −0.15, P = .687; r = −0.12, P = .759; Fig. 9E and F). However, a positive correlation between SULF1 expression and the proportion of M2 macrophages (r = 0.75, P = .015; Fig. 9G).

4. Discussion

The involvement of metabolic disorders in HP progression is gaining widespread attention. Dasgupta et al[3] analyzed metabolomic and transcriptomic data, and the results showed significant abnormalities in lactate, pyruvate, and proline metabolism in patients with HP. In this study, we identified 311 DE-MRGs from GSE150910, which had the largest sample size, suggesting metabolic abnormalities in patients with HP, consistent with previous analyses. GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses showed that DE-MRGs were enriched in multiple metabolic pathways and metabolism-related biological functions. GSEA showed dysregulation in multiple metabolism-related pathways in patients with HP compared with HCs. In this study, multiple bioinformatics algorithms were used to investigate the role of metabolism-related genes in the pathogenesis of HP. Three metabolism-related genes, including NPR3, GPX3, and SULF1, were identified as diagnostic feature biomarkers for HP, with AUC values greater than 0.6. These genes had high diagnostic significance, thereby suggesting significant involvement of these MRGs in the pathogenesis of HP.

A significant decrease in NPR3 expression level was observed in patients with HP. NPR3 is one of the 3 HP-related genes we identified, and its role in HP has not been reported. Studies have shown the involvement of NPR3 in the onset and progression of diabetes and atherosclerosis.[17,18] NPR3 is a single-stranded transmembrane and natriuretic peptide receptor abundantly expressed by the vascular smooth muscle, endothelium, kidney, and major endocrine glands.[19] NPR3 binds to all natriuretic peptides, removes circulating natriuretic peptides, and performs biological functions via the guanine nucleotide regulatory protein signaling pathway.[20] Moghtadaei et al[21] showed that NPR3 deletion causes sinus node dysfunction and induces atrial remodeling and fibrosis. Pulmonary fibrosis caused by cHP shares common pathological features with atrial fibrosis. Fibrosis occurs in both diseases, which could be associated with NPR3. A study has shown the involvement of the NPR3 signaling pathway in pyroptosis.[22] cANF4-23, an NPR3-specific agonist, inhibits nitric oxide synthase and nitric oxide levels in cells.[23] In addition, NPR3 reduces interleukin-1β secreted by macrophages by inhibiting oxidative stress.[24] Acute and chronic hypoxia reduces NPR3 expression without affecting other NPR genes.[25,26] HP is an ILD, and hypoxia is an important pathophysiological characteristic of HP. Our bioinformatics analysis and qRT-PCR results showed low NPR3 expression in patients with bird-related HP compared with HC. scRNA-seq showed that NPR3 was primarily expressed by endothelial cells. Moreover, low NPR3 expression was observed in endothelial cells of patients with HP compared to HCs, consistent with previous studies.

GPX3, a member of the GPX family, is an extracellular glycosylase encoded by the GPX3 gene and one of the vital antioxidant enzymes against damage caused to host cells by oxidative stress.[27] In humans, GPX3 contains 5 exons and is located on chromosome 5q32. A study has shown that GPX3 is the only known antioxidant in the extracellular matrix, and its active site contains selenocysteines.[28] GPX3 degrades hydroperoxides via multiple donors like glutathione, thioredoxin, and glutaredoxin[29,30] and scavenges free radicals. GPX3 is a major antioxidant enzyme found in plasma. It catalyzes H2O2 and lipid peroxide and reduces organic peroxides via reduced glutathione to scavenge H2O2, thereby protecting cells from reactive oxygen species-induced DNA and cellular damage.[31] In addition, an increase in H2O2 production by macrophages was observed in a guinea pig model of exogenous HP.[32] Therefore, GPX3 could confer protection to patients with HP.

Bioinformatics analysis showed a reduction in GPX3 expression levels in patients with HP lung tissues compared with HCs. GPX3 was expressed mainly by endothelial cells and, to a lesser extent, by macrophages and fibroblasts. Schamberger et al[33] showed that primarily bronchial epithelial cells and mesenchymal fibroblasts expressed GPX3. It is localized in the basement membrane and the extracellular matrix of the lung. Furthermore, they found high GPX3 expression levels in some alveolar lavage fluid from patients with HP in specimens with ILD. Bioinformatics analysis showed decreased GPX3 expression in the lung tissues of patients with HP.

Further, our results showed that low GPX3 expression was observed in the PMBCs of patients with HP, which differs from previous studies results. The following reasons could lead to the differences in results: differences in patients included. Notably, previous studies have enrolled patients with end-stage HP, whereas, in our study, we primarily enrolled patients with cHP, specifically patients with bird-related HP. Therefore, the difference in the severity of the disease could lead to differences in GPX3 expression; the different sample types. Consequently, previous studies have used alveolar lavage fluid. However, in our research, we have used PBMCs. Patients with HP suffer from varying degrees of hypoxia and cannot tolerate fiberoptic bronchoscopy. Hence, alveolar lavage fluid was no longer collected, and patients preferred blood sampling and CT examination; the sample size of previous studies and our study was small, which influenced the reliability of the results. Therefore, the role of GPX3 in HP should be further validated using a larger sample size.

SULF1 is a newly discovered member of the sulfatase family, secreted by the Golgi apparatus and subsequently localized to the cell surface. It alters the sulfated state of heparan sulfate proteoglycan core protein (HSPG) and participates in cell signaling, thereby regulating various physiological and pathological processes.[34] HSPG, a glycoprotein, comprises multiple cell surface proteins and is characterized by the covalent attachment of one or more glycan chains like heparan sulfate (HS).[35] In addition, SULF1 selectively hydrolyzes the 6-O-sulfate group of the HS chain, thereby altering the sulfated state of HSPG. This modulates the ability of HSPG to bind to various protein ligands, inhibiting the formation of complexes and participating in different physiological and pathological processes.[36,37] Our results showed increased SULF1 expression in the lung tissues of patients with HP. SULF1 was expressed primarily by fibroblasts and macrophages of the lungs. Perkins et al[38] demonstrated that an increase in SULF1 expression level enhances the silica-induced proliferation of rat lung fibroblasts, the expression of lung fibrosis-related genes, and collagen production. Our results are consistent with previous studies. We obtained tissues of patients with HP; however, the pathological features of pulmonary fibrosis were presented by patients with cHP. Therefore, it is likely that SULF1 is significantly involved in the process of pulmonary fibrosis in cHP. SULF1 regulates the activities of various growth factors binding to HS and morphogens like fibroblast growth factor-2.[39] Yue et al[40] used qRT-PCR to demonstrate a TGF-beta1-induced increase in SULF1 expression in primary lung fibroblasts in a time- and dose-dependent manner in humans.

Patients with HP have metabolic abnormalities and disorders of immune function. Therefore, the proportion of 22 immune cell types was determined using the “CIBERSORT” algorithm.[41] A significant difference in the proportion of multiple immune cells was observed between patients with HP and HCs, predominantly M2 macrophages. Macrophages are involved in the pathogenesis of pulmonary fibrosis. Macrophages are widely distributed in various tissues and organs and are significantly involved in maintaining physiological homeostasis and metabolism in humans.[42] Alveolar macrophages, differentiated from monocytes, play a significant role in HP, promote inflammation, and induce immune responses in response to stress induced by gases, suspended particles, and microscopic particles. In addition, they are involved in lung fibrosis and granuloma formation, thereby causing the remodeling of airways and lung dysfunction.[43] Alveolar macrophages differentiate into M1 and M2 macrophages. The M1/M2 macrophage polarization balance determines the inflammation status and lung tissue regression on exposure to external stimuli.[44] During severe infection or inflammation in the lungs, the macrophages are polarized to the M1-phenotype and eliminate pathogens by secreting inflammatory factors like IL-1β, TNF-α, and iNOS.[45–47] However, the long-term presence of M1 macrophages causes tissue damage; hence the levels of M1 macrophages gradually decrease during the late inflammatory response. In addition, intra-tissue macrophages are polarized toward M2-phenotype to reduce inflammation and promote tissue repair and reconstruction by secreting large amounts of cytokines like TGF-β1.[48] However, excess M2 macrophages could cause abnormal repair after alveolar injury, induce epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and form pulmonary fibrosis.[49] Our bioinformatics results showed an increased proportion of M2 macrophages in patients with cHP compared with HCs. These bioinformatics results were validated by flow cytometry.

M2 polarization may occur in fibrotic lung disorders. A study has shown a correlation between an increase in the Th2 pathway and CCL-18 (marker of M2) level in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid and the degree of fibrosis in ILD.[50] In addition, studies have shown that macrophages regulate T cell activation and differentiation, thereby mediating Th1 or Th2 response.[51] Pechkovsky et al[52] performed flow cytometry using an anti-CD206 antibody for phenotyping M2 macrophages based on cytokine secreted, and the results revealed M2 macrophage polarization in fibrotic lung disease. We used the CD163 antibody for the M2 phenotype. CD163 is a transmembrane protein expressed by monocyte/macrophages. An increase in CD163 expression was observed during the resolution of inflammation and wound healing.[53] This could be the underlying immune responses involved in the onset and progression of ILD.

In this study, we investigated the correlation between M2 macrophage proportion and diagnostic feature biomarkers. Our results showed no correlation between NPR3 and the proportion of M2 macrophages. Further, no significant correlation was observed between GPX3 and M2 macrophage proportion. Interestingly, a positive correlation was observed between SULF1 and the proportion of M2 macrophages. No previous study has reported the correlation between NPR3, GPX3, and macrophages. The effect of NPR3 and GPX3 on macrophage polarization in patients with HP requires further experimental validation. Brasil da Costa et al[54] showed a correlation between SULF1 and M2 macrophages. In prostate cancer, a correlation was observed between cancer-associated fibroblasts and macrophages.

Furthermore, M2 macrophages enhance SULF1 expression in fibroblasts. Kim et al[55] suggested that SULF regulates macrophages’ antigen presentation and phagocytic activities. Hence, inhibiting or overexpressing SULF expression could be a novel immunomodulator of macrophages’ antigen presentation and phagocytic activity. Based on previous studies and our results, it is likely that reciprocal promotion of SULF1 and M2 macrophages in HP (Fig. 10), thereby inducing the onset and progression of pulmonary fibrosis in patients with cHP. Therefore, exploring the effect of SULF1 on macrophage phenotype may play an essential role in designing therapeutics for patients with HP.

Despite the study’s numerous contributions, some limitations remain to be recognized. First, further experiments, particularly microscopic analyses, need to prove whether SULF1 regulates M2 macrophage function in HP. Second, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis as an ILD resembles chronic HP clinically and radiographically. More IPF subjects should be collected in future studies to verify our bioinformatic results. Finally, HP is an uncommon disease in our region; we should consider increasing the sample size to confirm our current findings due to the small size of the clinical samples.

5. Conclusion

In this study, we applied multiple bioinformatics algorithms to investigate the role of MRGs in HP onset and progression. In addition, we identified 3 MRGs as diagnostic biomarkers of HP, which were validated in clinical samples. Moreover, the correlation between M2 macrophages and diagnostic biomarkers was preliminarily explored. Finally, the correlation between metabolism and the immune microenvironment was analyzed, which provides new perspectives and insights for designing therapeutic strategies for treating patients with HP in the future.
