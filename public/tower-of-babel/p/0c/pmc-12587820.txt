Discovery of flavonoids as potent inhibitors of CYP1A to alleviate cellular inflammation and oxidative stress induced by benzo[a]pyrene-induced high CYP1A expression
Authors: Xiao-Dong Li, Nan-Shan Ma, Ci-Ye He, Li-Wei Zou, Jing Zhang, Zhen-Hua Ni, Yang Ling
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe environmental pollutant benzo[a]pyrene (BaP) can induce cytochrome P450 family 1 subfamily A (CYP1A) or generate metabolic products that disrupt the balance of oxidative stress, triggering inflammatory responses in the lungs and leading to tissue damage. Flavonoids, known for their natural anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, are potential targets for intervention. This study used phenacetin, a specific substrate probe for CYP1A, to evaluate the inhibitory effects of 40 flavonoids on CYP1A. Structure-activity relationship analysis revealed that introducing hydroxyl groups at positions 3- and 6-enhances CYP1A inhibition. Notably, 3,6-dihydroxyflavone (DHF) emerged as a significant inhibitor of CYP1A. In vitro experiments confirmed that DHF effectively inhibits BaP-induced cytochrome P450 family 1 subfamily A member 1 (CYP1A1) in airway epithelial cells and shows dose-dependent inhibition of intracellular and mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. In summary, DHF is a promising CYP1A inhibitor and a potential anti-inflammatory candidate for preventing and treating CYP1A-mediated lung diseases.Keywords: Flavonoids, CYP1A, benzo[a]pyrene, lung injury

Introduction

CYP1A is a critical member of the P450 protein family, including CYP1A1 and CYP1A2. CYP1A1 is primarily expressed in the intestines1, lungs2,3, and kidneys4,5 of mammals, while CYP1A2 is predominantly found in the liver6,7. CYP1A plays a significant role in metabolising various endogenous8 and exogenous substances9, such as fatty acids and steroids, as well as exogenous compounds, including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) and heterocyclic amines.

During the metabolism of these organic pollutants, such as polychlorinated biphenyls, BaP, and aflatoxin B1, CYP1A generates highly electrophilic reactive intermediates (like chlorinated phenols, epoxides of BaP, and aflatoxin B1)10,11. These intermediates can covalently bind to DNA and proteins, leading to toxicity and potentially triggering various cancers, including prostate, breast, and lung cancers12–15. Notably, PAHs, represented by BaP, play a key role in inducing CYP1A1 overexpression16. Increased CYP1A levels are often associated with heightened uncoupling reactions, releasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) that induce oxidative stress, inflammation, and tissue damage17. This can induce oxidative stress18, resulting in inflammation and damage in various organs. PAHs are commonly found in various air pollutants, including cigarette smoke and smoke from charcoal19,20. This study aims to further explore the potential of flavonoids in reversing organ damage, particularly in the context of CYP1A overexpression induced by environmental toxins.

Numerous recent studies have shown that natural products, particularly flavonoids, effectively inhibit CYP1A, delaying its metabolic activation and reversing organ damage induced by environmental toxins18,21. Flavonoids, commonly found in vegetables, fruits, and herbs, have a basic core structure of 2-phenylchromone and have demonstrated significant roles in cardiovascular protection22, immune regulation23, antioxidant activity24, and anti-inflammatory effects25,26. However, there has been no analysis of the structure-activity relationship (SAR) regarding the inhibitory effects of different kinds of flavonoids on CYP1A, nor research on whether flavonoids can reverse lung damage induced by the overexpression of CYP1A induced by environmental toxins through CYP1A inhibition.

This study utilised the highly specific and sensitive substrate probe phenacetin27,28, employing human liver microsomes (HLMs) as the enzyme source to measure the rate of acetaminophen production from phenacetin metabolites via liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC–MS/MS) 29. By comparing the changes in the production rate of phenacetin metabolites before and after the addition of inhibitors, we evaluated the inhibitory capacities of 40 different flavonoids. We analysed SAR, ultimately identifying DHF as an effective CYP1A inhibitor. Since BaP was primarily metabolised by CYP1A1, with CYP1A2 contributing relatively little to its metabolism30,31, we further employed airway epithelial cell lines32–34, specifically 16HBE cells35,36, to validate that DHF effectively reversed the CYP1A expression induced by BaP, as well as the associated increase in ROS levels. In summary, this study provided new insights into the potential of flavonoids in preventing and treating CYP1A-related pulmonary diseases.

Chemical structure of natural flavonoids.

CYP1A inhibition assays

The measurement of CYP1A activity is based on the dealkylation of the specific substrate phenacetin to produce acetaminophen. The inhibitory effects of 40 flavonoids on CYP1A were assessed by evaluating the residual activity fraction. The assay was conducted using a SCIEX 4500 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer coupled with a Shimadzu LC20A liquid chromatography system, with a total incubation volume of 200 μL. The reaction mixture, containing 20 μL 1 mM NADPH in 176 μL buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM CaCl2, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4), was incubated with 1 μL of the inhibitor (final concentration of 10 μM) and 2 μL HLMs (final concentration of 0.2 mg/mL) at 37 °C for 30 min. The reaction was initiated by adding the substrate after a 3-min pre-warming step, followed by the addition of 1 μL phenacetin (final concentration of 50 μM) to start the dealkylation reaction. A positive control was established using alizarin (IC50 = 5.5 μM for CYP1A1, (IC50 = 2.7 μM for CYP1A2) 37. The inhibitory effect was expressed as the percentage of the peak area of the reaction products from the different inhibitor groups relative to the peak area of the internal standard (lansoprazole).

Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS)

Chromatographic separation was achieved on a Shim-pack GIST-HP C18 column (2.1 mm × 50 mm, 3 µm) using a flow rate of 0.4 ml/min at 40 °C. The mobile phases consisted of water with 0.1% formic acid (A) and acetonitrile (B), employing the following gradient: 0–0.5 min, 2% B; 0.5–1.0 min, 2%-85% B; 1.0–2.0 min, 85% B; 2.0–3.0 min, 85%-2% B. Acetaminophen metabolite measurements were performed using LC-MS/MS in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode. Electrospray ionisation was used in positive mode (5500 V). MS/MS parameters were as follows: curtain gas, 25 psi; ion source gas 1, 50 psi; ion source gas 2, 55 psi; temperature, 500 °C.

Molecular docking simulations

To investigate the binding mechanisms of DHF and alizarin to CYP1A, molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina (Version 1.1.2) based on the Lamarckian genetic algorithm. The CYP1A1 structures (PDB: 4I8V) and CYP1A2 structures (PDB: 2HI4) were downloaded from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/) and used as receptors, while DHF served as the ligand. Hydrogen atoms were added, and Kollman charges were assigned to the receptor. The dimensions of the grid box were set to 80 × 80 × 80 Å³ with a spacing of 0.4 Å. These steps were performed to ensure a favourable binding process, enabling the identification of the lowest binding energy for subsequent calculations.

Cell culture

Human airway epithelial 16HBE cells were provided from Bogoo Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Human airway epithelial NCI-H292 were provided from The Cell Bank of Chinese Academy of Sciences (the Institute of Cell Biology, Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% foetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 1.0 μg/mL streptomycin (ThermoFisher, USA) in an incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Cells were then harvested and treated with varying concentrations of BaP (10 μM) plus DHF (10,15, and 25 μM), or alizarin (25 μM) for the indicated times in serum-free RPMI 1640.

Immunocytochemistry (ICC)

Following drug treatment, cells were subjected to immunocytochemical staining. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 10 min. After fixation, cells were permeabilized and washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.3% Triton X-100. To block non-specific binding, cells were incubated with a blocking buffer containing 10% goat serum at room temperature for 30 min. Rabbit CYP1A1 antibody (Proteintech, Cat No. 13241–1-AP, 1:400 dilution) was applied and incubated at 4 °C overnight. The following day, cells were washed three times with PBST. Cells were then incubated with Goat Anti-Rabbit Cy3-conjugated secondary antibodies (Abcam, Cat No. ab6939, 1:400 dilution) for 2 h at room temperature. After incubation, Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using a fluorescence microscope.

Fluorescence microscopy analysis of intracellular ROS levels

Airway epithelial cells were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with the designated compounds for 24 h. Following treatment, the cells were incubated with the fluorescent probe H2DCFDA (AbMole, Shanghai, China) for 20 min at 37 °C under 5% CO2 to detect intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS). After washing with PBS to remove excess probe, fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope. The fluorescence intensity was quantified using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software to assess the levels of ROS.

Flow cytometric analysis of intracellular total and mitochondrial ROS

After drug treatment, cells were washed once with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and collected by centrifugation. Total ROS and mitochondrial ROS detection kits (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) were used for flow cytometry analysis. Intracellular total ROS were stained with DCFH-DA, while mitochondrial ROS were labelled using MitoSO Red. Cells were incubated with the respective probes at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. Following incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS to remove excess dye. Fluorescence intensity was then analysed by flow cytometry to quantify the changes in total and mitochondrial ROS levels.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cultured airway epithelial cells using the EZB RNA Extraction Kit (EZBioscience, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, 1 μg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the EZB Reverse Transcription Kit (EZBioscience, USA). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using EZB SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (EZBioscience, USA) on Real-Time PCR Detection System. The cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, annealing and extension at 60 °C for 30 s. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^–ΔΔCt method, with β-actin serving as the internal control. The sequences of the qPCR primers used in this study were listed in Table 1.

Western blot analysis of cytoplasmic aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) protein expression

To evaluate the cytoplasmic expression of AhR protein following BaP stimulation, airway epithelial cells were treated with BaP or BaP + DHF for the indicated time periods. After treatment, cells were washed with cold PBS and cytoplasmic proteins were extracted using the Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Equal amounts of protein samples were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA) for immunoblotting. Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA in TBST and then incubated with primary antibodies against rabbit AhR (Cell Signalling Technology, Cat No. 83200, 1:1000 dilution) and rabbit Actin (Cell Signalling Technology, Cat No. 4967, 1:1000 dilution) overnight at 4 °C. After washing, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cell Signalling Technology, Cat No. 7074, 1:1000 dilution) for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualised using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents and quantified using ImageJ software.

Cell viability assay

The effect of DHF on cell viability was evaluated in 16HBE cells and NCI-H292 cells using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Dojindo, Japan). Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at an appropriate density and allowed to adhere overnight. Subsequently, cells were treated with DHF at final concentrations of 10, 15, and 25 μM for 24 h. At the end of treatment, CCK-8 solution was added to each well. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. Cell viability was expressed as a percentage relative to the untreated control group.

Data analysis

All experimental results are presented as mean ± standard deviation, based on triplicate assays. Statistical significance (p < 0.05) was assessed using one-way ANOVA with Sidak’s multiple comparisons test, conducted with GraphPad Prism 10 software.

Results

Screening of CYP1A inhibitors from flavonoids

In this study, we screened forty flavonoids for their inhibitory activity against CYP1A (see Figure 2). As shown in Table S1, flavonoids F1, F2, F5 (DHF), F8, F12, F14, and F16 demonstrated strong inhibition of CYP1A, with residual activity below 15%. Notably, DHF demonstrated superior inhibitory potency compared to the known CYP1A inhibitor alizarin. To further investigate the inhibitory effects of the functional groups at the 3- and 6-positions of DHF, we evaluated the dose-dependent inhibition of CYP1A by eight related flavonoids. The IC50 values for these flavonoids are presented in Table 2 and Figure 3, indicating that the catalytic activities of CYP1A were inhibited in a dose-dependent manner by each tested flavonoid. Remarkably, DHF displayed potent inhibition of CYP1A, with an IC50 value of 0.81 μM. These findings suggest that the hydroxyl groups at both the 3- and 6-positions of DHF significantly contribute to its strong to moderate inhibitory effect on CYP1A, prompting further investigation into the potential role of DHF in combating cellular inflammation.

Structure-activity relationship analysis

Based on the structure of flavonoids, variations in hydroxyl groups at different positions exhibit distinct inhibitory effects. Analysing the structure-activity relationship (SAR) and summarising the findings can provide insights for modifying compounds to enhance their biological inhibitory effects. Compared to flavonoid F2, the methylation of the hydroxyl group at the 3-position of flavonoid F1 enhances its inhibitory capacity against CYP1A, indicating that the 3-position hydroxyl of flavonoids can enhance F1’s inhibition. Using a similar approach, we examined flavonoid F3 and F4 and found that the hydroxyl group at the 6-position of F3 also enhances its inhibitory capacity, although not as effectively as the 3-position hydroxyl of F1. Furthermore, when comparing F5 (DHF), F6, and F7, we discovered that the hydroxyl groups at the 3- and 6-positions of F5 (DHF) worked synergistically to enhance its inhibition of CYP1A (Figure 4).

SAR summary of flavonoids inhibiting CYP1A.

We also further compared flavonoid F1 and F8 and found that the hydroxyl group at the 7-position weakens the inhibitory effect of flavonoid F1. Continuing our analysis of the inhibitory capacities of flavonoids F8, F9, F10, F11, and F12, we confirmed that introducing a hydroxyl group at the 3-position enhances inhibition. However, we also observed that a methoxy group at the 4′-position of flavonoid B weakens its inhibitory effect on CYP1A.

Incorporating a methoxy or hydroxyl group at the 5-position of flavonoid A enhances the inhibitory effect of F9, which is more pronounced with the hydroxyl group. When comparing flavonoids F13, F14, F15, F16, F17, and F18, we found that a hydroxyl group at the 5′-position of flavonoid B enhances the inhibition of F14, while a hydroxyl group at the 2′-position weakens the inhibition of F13. Methylation of the 5′-position hydroxyl further enhances F14’s inhibitory capacity. When the hydroxyl at the 3-position of flavonoid C is converted to a glycoside, it weakens the inhibition of F14 and F15.

We also studied flavonoids F19-F40 and found that introducing a hydroxyl group at the 8-position of flavonoid A enhances inhibition against CYP1A. However, methylation of the hydroxyl group on flavonoid A does not improve its inhibitory capacity. We did not observe significant inhibitory effects on CYP1A from isoflavones, four types of biflavonoids, or various flavonoid glycosides.

Docking simulation

We conducted molecular docking simulations to comprehend the interaction between CYP1A and the inhibitor DHF. As shown in Figure 5 and Table S2, the binding energy of DHF with CYP1A1 was −11.2 kcal/mol. The main interactions involved key amino acid residues PHE224, GLY316, and PHE123, primarily through π-π stacking, which directs towards the solvent and could impact substrate entry. In contrast, the binding energy of DHF with CYP1A2 was −11.7 kcal/mol, with significant interactions involving key residues PHE226, GLY316, and THR124, characterised by π-π stacking and a hydrogen bond with THR124 (Figure S3). Molecular docking analyses revealed that the 3- and 6-position hydroxyl groups of compound DHF significantly affect the activity of CYP1A enzymes through a variety of interactions with the enzyme. Specifically, the 3- and 6-position hydroxyl groups modulate the electron density of the A and C rings of flavonoids through electronic effects, thereby affecting hydrogen bonding and Pi-Pi stacking with aromatic amino acid residues. The interaction pattern of DHF with key amino acid residues of the CYP1A enzyme is similar to that of alizarin, as detailed in Figure S4. In addition, specific hydroxyl substituent groups have an effect on the spatial structure of the A and C ring orientations, which in turn change the strength of their interactions with key amino acid residues in the CYP1A enzyme. These changes in interaction strength ultimately determine the suitability of the compounds in the CYP1A enzyme binding pocket.

DHF suppressed BaP-induced CYP1A1 overexpression in airway epithelial cells

CYP1A1, a critical downstream effector of the AhR pathway, mediates BaP-induced cytotoxicity in airway epithelial cells. We therefore examined the effects of DHF on CYP1A1 expression in 16HBE cells. DHF exhibited only minor effects on cell viability at concentrations of 10–15 μM (Figure S5), but significantly suppressed BaP-induced upregulation of CYP1A1 (Figure 6a), indicating that the effect of DHF on regulating CYP1A1 expression and may interfere with AhR signalling. To further characterise DHF’s AhR-antagonistic properties, we compared its efficacy with CH-223191, a well-established AhR antagonist. At equivalent concentrations, DHF exhibited comparable inhibition of BaP-induced CYP1A expression in 16HBE cells (Figure 6b,c). Importantly, DHF also attenuated CYP1A induction by ITE in 16HBE cells, an endogenous AhR agonist (Figure 6d,e), reinforcing its broad AhR-inhibitory activity. Additionally, DHF restored cytoplasmic AhR protein levels, which were depleted upon BaP exposure (Figure 6f). Consistently, DHF treatment inhibited BaP- or ITE-induced CYP1A1 overexpression in NCI-H292 airway epithelial cells (Figure S6). Taken together, these results indicated that DHF effectively attenuated BaP-induced CYP1A1 overexpression in airway epithelial cells, thereby highlighting its potential as a therapeutic candidate for mitigating AhR-mediated cytotoxicity.

DHF inhibited BaP-induced ROS generation in airway epithelial cells

ROS generation is a key mediator of BaP-induced cytotoxicity, a process driven in part by CYP1A1-mediated metabolic activation. To evaluate the antioxidant potential of DHF, we examined its effects on BaP-induced ROS accumulation in airway epithelial cells. BaP exposure induced a significant increase in intracellular ROS levels compared to untreated controls. However, co-treatment with DHF (10–25 μM) markedly suppressed BaP-dependent ROS generation (Figure 7a) in 16HBE cells. Flow cytometric analysis further confirmed that both DHF and pharmacological CYP1A1 inhibitors significantly attenuated BaP-induced total intracellular ROS accumulation in 16HBE cells (Figure 7b). Consistent with these findings, DHF and CYP1A1 inhibitors similarly reduced mitochondrial ROS overproduction triggered by BaP in 16HBE cells (Figure 7c). In addition, DHF treatment significantly suppressed BaP-induced intracellular and mitochondrial ROS production in NCI-H292 airway epithelial cells (Figure S7). Together, these results demonstrate that DHF effectively mitigates BaP-induced oxidative stress by suppressing CYP1A1 activity, thereby implicating its potential as a therapeutic agent for chemical-induced cellular damage.

DHF inhibited BaP-induced inflammation response in airway epithelial cells

Previous studies had shown that BaP and other AhR ligands, by activating the AhR pathway, transcriptionally up-regulate a spectrum of pro-inflammatory cytokines and C-X-C motif chemokine ligands (CXCLs); this sequential induction amplifies downstream inflammatory signalling and ultimately exacerbates the overall inflammatory response38,39. Based on this evidence, we sought to characterise the anti-inflammatory properties of compound DHF in BaP-exposed airway epithelial cells. Our findings demonstrated that BaP exposure significantly upregulated mRNA expression of key inflammatory mediators, including the pro-inflammatory cytokine interleukin-6 (IL-6) and the C-X-C motif chemokine ligands CXCL1, CXCL2, and CXCL3 (Figure 8a–d). Consistently, DHF significantly inhibited BaP-induced expression of IL6, CXCL1, CXCL2, and CXCL3 in NCI-H292 airway epithelial cells (Figure S8). Notably, DHF co-treatment dose-dependently attenuated this inflammatory response, with maximal inhibition observed at the highest tested concentrations. These results indicate that DHF exhibits anti-inflammatory activity by suppressing BaP-induced inflammatory gene expression in airway epithelial cells.

Discussion

Lung diseases induced by organic pollutants such as PAHs are a significant concern40,41. These pollutants are metabolised by CYP1A, leading to adverse effects such as oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, carcinogenicity, endocrine disruption, and immune system disorders41–43. Additionally, these compounds can induce the overexpression of CYP1A1, further accelerating the metabolism of pollutants in the lungs and resulting in more severe toxic effects44. Notably, CYP1A expression and activity are higher in Asian populations, making this enzyme a crucial factor in influencing susceptibility to lung diseases, including lung cancer45,46. Therefore, identifying safe and effective CYP1A inhibitors is essential for preventing and reversing CYP1A-mediated toxic responses.

Previous studies have demonstrated the inhibitory role of flavonoids in CYP1A activity. For example, Shirin Fallahi et al. reported that β-naphthoflavone inhibited CYP1A enzymes in fish47. However, unlike the flavonoids examined in the present study, β-naphthoflavone lacked phenolic hydroxyl groups, and thus its activity did not provide insight into the influence of hydroxyl substitutions at different positions. Furthermore, interspecies differences between fish and human CYP1A enzymes raised uncertainty about whether its inhibitory effect could be directly extrapolated to humans. Complementarily, Rebeca et al. showed that Phe224 and other aromatic residues in the human CYP1A1 active site played a critical role in flavonoid–CYP interactions through π–π stacking with the flavonoid C-ring48. These studies highlighted how structural diversity among flavonoids contributed to their distinct interactions with CYP1A and their potential protective mechanisms against CYP1A-mediated toxic responses. Building on this knowledge, we systematically screened 40 representative flavonoids and identified DHF as the most potent inhibitor. DHF, a naturally occurring compound found in plants such as Sphagneticola trilobata, was also recognised for its diverse pharmacological activities, including anticancer49, antibacterial50, and antiviral51 properties.

To further analyse the functions of the hydroxyl groups at the 3- and 6-positions of DHF, we also assessed the inhibitory effects of seven structurally similar flavonoids (F1–F8) on CYP1A. The results showed significant differences in the inhibitory effects of the eight flavonoids, with DHF showing the strongest inhibition (IC50 = 0.81 μM). F1 (IC50 = 1.37 μM), F2 (IC50 = 1.02 μM), and F8 (IC50 = 1.67 μM) also demonstrated strong inhibitory capacities, while F3 (IC50 = 2.90 μM), F4 (IC50 = 15.69 μM), and F6 (IC50 = 2.92 μM) showed relatively weaker inhibition. To more obviously compare the inhibitory effects of the 3-hydroxyl group, we chemically synthesised a compound: 3,6-dimethoxyflavone (F7, The structural data of the 3,6-dimethoxyflavone is detailed in Figures S1 and S2), in which the hydroxyl groups at both the 3- and 6-positions of DHF were methylated, resulting in an IC50 of 5.55 μM. Overall, these results highlight the importance of the hydroxyl groups at the 3- and 6-positions of DHF in inhibiting CYP1A. Molecular docking studies also indicated that DHF can form π-π stacking interactions and hydrogen bonds with key amino acid residues of CYP1A (see Figure 5).

To further explore the potential of DHF in preventing and treating lung diseases caused by organic pollutants, this study focused on its effects on BaP-induced ROS generation. As a reference, alizarin—a flavonoid compound previously reported as a strong CYP1A inhibitor—was used as a positive control. Consistent with its known inhibitory activity, alizarin significantly suppressed BaP-induced ROS production, supporting the rationale for comparing its effects with those of DHF. This parallel not only underscores the relevance of flavonoid-based compounds in modulating oxidative stress responses by inhibiting CYP1A activities, but also positions the study of DHF as a meaningful extension and complement to earlier research on alizarin. However, we should also acknowledge that CYP1A1 plays a dual role in the metabolism of BaP. On the one hand, CYP1A1 contributes to detoxification by converting BaP into more water-soluble and less harmful metabolites, thereby facilitating its clearance. On the other hand, CYP1A1 can also bioactivate BaP into highly reactive intermediates that form DNA adducts and promote carcinogenesis. Previous studies have reported that CYP1A1 knockout mice exhibit increased susceptibility to BaP toxicity compared with wild-type controls52, highlighting the critical role of CYP1A1 in BaP detoxification in vivo. Therefore, the application of DHF in preventing BaP-induced toxicity should take dosage into careful consideration, as excessive inhibition of CYP1A1 may interfere with its detoxification function.

The AhR is a well-known ligand-activated transcription factor that primarily regulates responses to environmental pollutants45,46. When AhR binds to its ligands, such as BaP and other polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, it translocates to the nucleus, binding to specific DNA sequences and activating the transcription of downstream genes, including CYP1A53. Our results showed that DHF significantly inhibited BaP-induced CYP1A1 expression, suggesting that DHF may alleviate BaP-induced cytotoxicity by suppressing the activation of the AhR signalling pathway. Furthermore, comparisons with the known AHR antagonist CH-22319154 indicated that DHF exhibits similar inhibitory effects on BaP-induced CYP1A1 expression at the same concentration, further supporting its potential as an AhR antagonist. Additionally, DHF reduced CYP1A expression induced by the endogenous AhR agonist ITE55, indicating that it may have a broad AhR antagonistic effect. However, our study has several limitations. The substrate specificity, catalytic activity, and regulatory mechanisms of CYP1A1 differ substantially between humans and mice, making it difficult to directly extrapolate findings from animal studies to human physiological and pathological processes. Therefore, animal experiments were not included in the present study. Future research employing humanised CYP1A transgenic mouse models would be valuable to further investigate the in vivo effects of DHF. Moreover, since CYP1A enzymes are also involved in the metabolism of multiple toxic components of air pollution, it remains to be clarified whether DHF could attenuate the toxicity of other pollutants besides BaP, which warrants further studies.

Conclusion

In summary, we screened 40 flavonoids and identified DHF as a potent inhibitor of CYP1A, with an IC50 value of 0.81 μM. This demonstrates significant inhibitory effects due to hydroxyl groups at the 3- and 6-positions. Molecular docking analysis confirmed that DHF can form hydrogen bonds and pi-pi stacking interactions with key residues of CYP1A, thereby enhancing its inhibitory effect.

Additionally, DHF demonstrates significant anti-inflammatory effects by suppressing BaP-induced inflammation in airway epithelial cells, notably reducing the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines in a dose-dependent manner. It also significantly inhibits BaP-induced CYP1A1 expression, likely mitigating cytotoxicity by suppressing the AhR signalling pathway. Comparisons with a known AhR antagonist further support DHF’s potential as an AHR antagonist. Additionally, DHF reduces CYP1A expression induced by the endogenous AhR agonist ITE, highlighting its broad AhR antagonistic effects.

These findings serve as a pivotal stepping stone for the development of novel anti-inflammatory drugs. This is particularly significant for diseases associated with environmental pollutants. The substantial inhibitory and anti-inflammatory effects of DHF hint at its potential as a novel drug candidate for diseases involving the AhR signalling pathway. Future studies could delve into the mechanisms of DHF, optimise its structure for enhanced bioactivity, and evaluate its clinical potential. This could potentially open up new avenues for tackling CYP1A-related health problems, inspiring further research and development in this area.
