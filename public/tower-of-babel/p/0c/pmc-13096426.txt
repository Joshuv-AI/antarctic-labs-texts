The chromosome-scale genome assembly and annotation of Rosa bracteata (Macartney Rose)
Authors: Runhui Li, Yanhong He, Fayun Xiang, Jian Wang, Wei Zhang, Yuanyuan Yang, Guogui Ning
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRosa bracteata is the only wild species in the section Bracteatae of the genus Rosa, and no genome assembly exists for this section. It has been used as a paternal donor in modern rose breeding and biological research due to its exceptional heat tolerance. Here, we present a high-quality chromosome-scale genome assembly of R. bracteata, totaling 540.63 Mb with a contig N50 of 35.97 Mb, anchored into seven pseudochromosomes. A total of 42,789 protein-coding genes were predicted, with 38,394 functionally annotated using public databases. This high-quality genome provides a valuable resource for investigating genetic evolution, molecular basis of adaptive traits, and facilitating molecular breeding for Rosa species.Subject terms: Evolution, Plant sciences

Background & Summary

The genus Rosa, belonging to the family Rosaceae, comprises approximately 200 species that are widely distributed across the Northern Hemisphere1,2. Among these, modern roses, often referred to as the “Queen of Flowers,” have originated from a complex process of interspecific hybridization. They currently represent approximately 30% of the global cut flower trade annually3. Various Rosa species native to China have made significant historical contributions to the formation and development of modern roses1–3. Rosa bracteata Wendl. is the only wild species within the section Bracteatae of the genus Rosa, found in southern China at elevations ranging from 0 to 300 meters, and it exhibits excellent heat tolerance1,2. After its introduction to the United Kingdom in 1794, R. bracteata became widely recognized as the Macartney rose. Subsequently, it was used as a male parent in hybridization with tea-scented rose cultivars. Through selection of the resulting seedlings, a new cultivar named ‘Maria Leonida’ was developed in 18294. Although F1 hybrids are readily obtained, these progeny are often sterile or exhibit very low fertility. Currently, the number of hybrids derived from R. bracteata is limited, yet their impact has been profound. The most renowned example is the cultivar ‘Mermaid’, a hybrid between R. bracteata and a tea-scented rose cultivar, which is celebrated for its exceptional disease resistance, vigorous growth, and large, beautiful single-petaled flowers. This and other hybrids demonstrate the potential of R. bracteata to contribute valuable traits, particularly heat tolerance, disease resistance, evergreen foliage, and large single-petaled flower morphology, to modern rose breeding programs aimed at developing more resilient cultivars5.

Unlike other species within the genus Rosa, R. bracteata is characterized as an evergreen, creeping climber. Its young shoots exhibit a brown coloration and are densely covered with fine hairs, while paired, sharp reddish-brown prickles can be found beneath the petioles. The plant features several large bracts that are alternately arranged and adorned with soft pubescence. Additionally, it bears large white flowers that emit a distinctive fruity fragrance1,2 (Fig. 1). In summary, R. bracteata holds substantial research value in genetic evolution and the molecular basis of adaptive traits within the genus Rosa.

The assembly of multiple rose genomes has shed light on the genetic origins of modern roses and established a foundational framework for exploring the molecular mechanisms underlying key agronomic and ornamental traits. Previous studies have predominantly focused on horticultural varieties6–13, while the vast majority of wild Rosa species resources remain inadequately investigated3,14–19. In this study, we present a high-quality, chromosome-scale genome assembly of R. bracteata, achieved through the application of PacBio single-molecule real-time (SMRT) sequencing20 and high-throughput chromatin conformation capture (Hi-C) technologies21. The assembled genome encompasses 540.63 Mb with a contig N50 of 35.97 Mb. A total of 501.06 Mb has been anchored onto seven pseudochromosomes. The assembly exhibits exceptional completeness and accuracy, as evidenced by a BUSCO score of 98.5%, an LTR Assembly Index (LAI) of 22.86, a Quality Value (QV) of 61.17, and a mapping rate of Illumina paired-end reads reaching 99.75%. The genome consists of 57.45% repetitive sequences. Gene annotation was conducted through a combination of ab initio prediction, homology-based methods, and RNA-seq data derived from various tissues (root, stem, leaf, and flower). A total of 42,789 protein-coding genes were predicted, among which 38,394 (89.73%) received functional annotations using public databases (GO, KEGG, NR, Swiss-Prot, Trembl, and eggNOG). The high-quality genome of R. bracteata provides a valuable foundation for research on the genetic evolution, molecular basis of adaptive traits, and breeding improvement of Rosa species. In the context of comparative genomics, the genome of Bracteata section fills a crucial phylogenetic gap in the genus Rosa, facilitating studies on its evolution and enabling the discovery of key genes underlying specific traits such as disease resistance, heat tolerance, evergreen habit, and unique bract characteristics. The functional genes and molecular markers identified through these efforts can be introgressed into modern rose cultivars to enhance their resilience to climate change.

Methods

Plant materials and polyploidy estimation

The plant material used for genome sequencing was a clonal progeny of a R. bracteata specimen collected from Yuxi City, Yunnan Province, China. This plant has been maintained in the greenhouse at Huazhong Agricultural University (latitude 30.474852°N, longitude 114.356769°E; altitude 78 m) in Hubei Province, China.

A living duplicate of this sequenced accession is preserved and available for research purposes upon request.

We collected the young sprouting stem apices of R. bracteata between 08:00 and 10:00 for chromosome number observation. The stem apices were sliced into 2–5 mm segments and immersed in 2 mM aqueous 8-hydroxyquinoline solution at 4 °C for 4 h. The samples were then fixed in Carnoy’s solution at 4 °C in the dark for 20 h, macerated in 5 M HCl at 37 °C for 12 min, and subsequently squashed in Carbol fuchsin for 2–10 h. Cells in the metaphase stage of mitosis were observed and photographed using a DM6B microscope. The result showed that R. bracteata is diploid with 14 chromosomes (Fig. 2a).

Genome assembly prediction of R. bracteata. (a) Chromosome observation (2n = 14). (b) K-mer analysis based on the 21-mer frequency distribution.

DNA extraction and sequencing

Genomic DNA (gDNA) for PacBio HiFi long-read sequencing was extracted from fresh young leaves of R. bracteata using the CTAB method. After assessing DNA quality and concentration using agarose gel electrophoresis and a Qubit fluorometer (Invitrogen, USA), the gDNA was sheared and size-selected using the BluePippin system (Sage Science, USA) to construct ~15 kb sequencing libraries. The libraries were sequenced on the PacBio Sequel II platform (Pacific Biosciences, CA, USA) based on their effective concentration and target data output. Raw sequencing data were preprocessed using SMRT Link (https://www.pacb.com/products-and-services/analytical-software), and 32.42 Gb filtered data of HiFi reads (Table 1) were generated using the CCS tool (https://github.com/PacificBiosciences/ccs). For Hi-C sequencing, genomic DNA was digested with the MboI restriction enzyme, and Hi-C libraries were constructed using the BGI PE150 sequencing strategy. Following A-tailing, biotin pull-down, and adapter ligation, the libraries were sequenced on the BGI DNBSEQ-T7 platform, generating 96.03 Gb Hi-C data (Table 1).

RNA extraction and sequencing

For transcriptome sequencing, fresh tissues, including roots, stems, leaves, and whole floral from three developmental stages (bud, partial-opening, and full-bloom, refer to Fig. 1c), collected from a single R. bracteata individual. All samples were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and transported to the laboratory, where they were stored at −80 °C until RNA extraction. Total RNA was extracted using the RN09 EASYspin RNA Plant Mini Kit (Aidlab, Beijing, China) and the Hipure HP Plant RNA Mini Kit (Magen, Guangzhou, China). RNA quality and concentration were assessed using three methods: (1) agarose gel electrophoresis for RNA purity and integrity; (2) a NanoDrop spectrophotometer for concentration and purity (OD260/280 and OD260/230 ratios); and (3) an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer for accurate RNA integrity analysis. Paired-end cDNA libraries were then constructed from poly (A) + mRNA enriched using Oligo (dT) magnetic beads, fragmented, circularized, and sequenced on the BGI DNBSEQ-T7 platform in PE150 mode. Three biological replicates were performed for each of the four tissues (root, stem, leaf, and flower), yielding a total of 12 RNA-seq libraries. Each sample produced more than 10 Gb of data, with a total output of 187.53 Gb (Table 1).

Genome survey and assembly

K-mer analysis was conducted using Jellyfish v2.2.1022 and GenomeScope2.023 with a k-mer size of 21, based on PacBio HiFi reads. The estimated genome size of R. bracteata was approximately 422.55 Mb, with a heterozygosity rate of 0.604% (Fig. 2b). For de novo genome assembly, raw HiFi data were converted to FASTQ format using the bam2fastq tool (https://github.com/PacificBiosciences/pbtk#bam2fastx). Given the low heterozygosity, we generated a primary, pseudo-haploid assembly using Hifiasm with default parameters (integrating Hi-C data for scaffolding)24. This approach collapses heterozygous regions into a single consensus sequence, resulting in one representative sequence per chromosome pair and generating 517 contigs totaling 540.62 Mb, with a contig N50 of 35.97 Mb and a GC content of 38.87% (Table 2). Hi-C reads filtered by Fastp v0.21.025 were aligned to the draft genome using BWA v.0.7.1726 through the Juicer v1.627. The 3D-DNA v18092228 was employed to anchor contigs onto pseudochromosomes, followed by manual curation and visualization of Hi-C contact maps using Juicebox v1.11.0829. We also perform a phased assembly using Hifiasm, which generated two haplotypes (hap1 and hap2). A comprehensive comparison of the primary and haploid assemblies (Table 2) shows that the primary assembly exhibits superior contiguity, making it a more robust and valuable resource for the community. The high similarity between haplotypes was further confirmed by whole-genome alignment using Minimap2 v2.1730 and SyRi v1.7.031, which revealed an extremely high degree of collinearity and sequence identity (Fig. 3).

In conclusion, our analysis reveals an exceptionally high degree of identity between the two haplotype assemblies of R. bracteata, consistent with the low heterozygosity estimated from our initial survey. Given that the primary assembly achieved higher contiguity and completeness, we elected to use this pseudo-haploid representation for all subsequent work, including gene annotation and comparative genomics. Finally, a total of 501.06 Mb of the assembled genome was anchored to seven pseudochromosomes, which ranged from 50.29 Mb to 89.97 Mb in length and contained a total of 19 gaps (Table 3). The pseudochromosome sequences were aligned to the published Rosa genome using MUMmer v4.0.032, and chromosome orientations and numbering were adjusted according to conserved synteny (Figs. 3, 5).

Identification and characterization of repetitive elements

Repetitive sequences were annotated using a combination of homology-based and de novo prediction approaches. For homology-based identification, RepeatMasker v4.1.033 was employed to search the genome for sequences homologous to known repeats in the RepBase database34. De novo transposable element (TE) prediction was performed using the EDTA v2.1.035 and DeepTE v2.036.

EDTA first integrates the outputs of several specialized tools to generate an initial transposable element annotation, including LTRharvest37, LTR_FINDER38, LTR_retriever39, TIR-Learner40, and HelitronScanner41. To ensure structural completeness, candidate TEs were stringently filtered based on the presence of characteristic terminal and internal features. For LTR retrotransposons, intact TG-CA terminal motifs and internal protein domains were required and verified against the Pfam database. TIR and MITE candidates were retained only if both terminal inverted repeats and target site duplications (TSDs) were identified, with a maximum TSD length deviation of ≤5 bp, as determined by TIR-Learner. Helitron elements were required to possess the canonical 5′-TC/CTRR-3′ and 3′-R-5′ termini. To improve detection of non-LTR elements, a custom curated library of SINE and LINE sequences was incorporated via the --curatedlib option. In addition, the pipeline was executed with parameters optimized for non-model species (--species others) and the --sensitive 1 parameter to improve annotation completeness. Remaining unannotated repetitive sequences were subjected to de novo prediction using RepeatModeler, and the results incorporated into the custom TE library. Second, DeepTE36 was employed with the plant-specific model (-sp P) and LTR family setting (-fam LTR) to classify previously unannotated LTR candidates at the superfamily level. Finally, EDTA35 was re-run in integration mode to merge classification results and remove low-confidence elements (defined as those with coverage <80% or sequence identity <80%), thereby generating a high-confidence, non-redundant, genome-wide TE annotation library. The final annotation set comprised 325.67 Mb of repetitive elements, accounting for approximately 60.24% of the genome (Table 4). The LTR Assembly Index (LAI) value of 22.86, as calculated by LTR_retriever39, further supported the high assembly quality of the genome (Table 2).

Gene prediction and functional annotation

Protein-coding genes were annotated in the repeat-masked genome by integrating transcriptome evidence, ab initio predictions, and homology-based approaches using self-training gene prediction tools. RNA-seq data from fresh tissues, including roots, stems, leaves, and floral organs at various developmental stages, were filtered and trimmed using fastp. The resulting clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 v2.1.042, and assembled into full-length transcript sequences with Cufflinks v2.2.143. PASA vr2013090744 was then employed to predict open reading frames (ORFs) and filter high-quality transcripts. Ab initio gene prediction was performed using AUGUSTUS v3.2.145, GeneMark-ES46 Suite v4, and SNAP vr2013021647. For homology-based prediction, amino acid sequences from related species (Rosa chinensis6, Rosa wichuraiana8, Rosa multiflora9, Rosa rugosa8,15, Rubus occidentalis48, Fragaria vesca49, Prunus persica50, and Malus domestica51) were filtered with CD-HIT v4.8.152 (parameters: -c 0.7) and aligned to the genome. Finally, MAKER v3.01.0353 was employed to integrate transcriptome-based, ab initio, and homology-based evidence, resulting in the prediction of 42,789 protein-coding genes with an average gene length of 2,905.4 bp (Table 5). The distribution of gene density, GC content, and repetitive sequences across each chromosome was visualized using Circos54 (Fig. 4).

Functional annotation of protein-coding genes was performed by aligning sequences to the NR55, GO56, KEGG57, EggNOG58, SwissProt and TrEMBL59 databases using DIAMOND v2.0.4.1425960 with an E-value threshold of 1e-5. A total of 38,394 protein-coding genes (89.73%) were successfully annotated with at least one functional term (Table 5).

Noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs) were annotated using multiple approaches. Ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs) were identified with RNAmmer v1.261, and transfer RNAs (tRNAs) were predicted using tRNAscan-SE v1.3.162. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) and small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs) were detected by aligning sequences to the Rfam database63 using the cmscan module of Infernal v1.1.264. In total, 1,347 ncRNAs were predicted in seven chromosomes, comprising 818 tRNAs, 162 rRNAs, 237 miRNAs, and 130 snRNAs (Table 5).

Phylogenetic relationships among R. bracteata and related Rosa species were reconstructed using Fragaria vesca as an outgroup. A total of 7,282 single-copy orthologous genes were identified using OrthoFinder v2.5.465 and individually aligned to build gene trees with RAxMLv.8.2.1066. The species tree was inferred using the ASTRAL v5.7.167 coalescent-based method by integrating these gene trees. To investigate genome structural conservation, syntenic blocks were identified based on the longest protein isoforms using JCVI v1.1.1168 (Fig. 5).

Technical Validation

We evaluated completeness quality of the assembled genome using four methods. BUSCO74 analysis with the embryophyte_odb10 database indicated that 98.5% of expected conserved genes were present in the assembly. The consensus base accuracy, estimated using Merqury v1.375 with HiFi reads, yielded a quality value (QV) of 61.17. The LTR Assembly Index (LAI) score, calculated as 22.86, further supported the high continuity and completeness of the assembly. To assess read-level support, Illumina short reads76 and PacBio HiFi long reads were mapped back to the final assembly using BWA v0.7.1726 and Minimap2 v2.1730, achieving mapping rates of 99.75% and 99.86%, respectively (Table 1). To assess the usability of the transcriptome data, all RNA-seq reads from each of the 12 samples were mapped back to the genome assembly using HISAT2 v2.1.042. As shown in Table 6, the mapping rates for all samples ranged from 83.2% to 95.8%, with more than 10 Gb of clean bases successfully mapped per sample. These high mapping rates demonstrate the high quality of both the RNA-seq data and the genome assembly, and confirm the suitability of the transcriptome data for gene annotation and future expression studies. Furthermore, the annotated protein-coding gene set exhibited high completeness, with 94.4% of embryophyte BUSCOs identified (Table 5). Our phylogenetic inference aligns well with previously reported evolutionary relationships among Rosa species. The identified syntenic blocks among Rosa genomes indicate a high degree of genome conservation within the genus. Overall, the results validate the high-quality assembly and annotation of the R. bracteata genome (Fig. 5).
