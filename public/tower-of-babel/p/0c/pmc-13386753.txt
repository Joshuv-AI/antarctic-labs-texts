Improving clinician knowledge, comfort, and adherence to standard of care for suspected child abuse
Authors: Mercie J. DiGangi, Sara Stewart, Evan Taragano, Jerry Cheng, Lee Barton, Sonya Negriff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThis quality improvement project aimed to improve clinician knowledge, comfort, and adherence to standard of care for suspected cases of child abuse and neglect.MethodsA multi-pronged child abuse identification QI program was implemented within a large integrated healthcare system in Southern California to educate physicians on the detection and appropriate work-up for suspected child abuse and neglect. This included educational modules, webinars, and updated clinical support tools. A survey was given to clinicians who completed the educational modules regarding their comfort and knowledge with child abuse protocols. Use of skeletal surveys, the standard of care for suspicious injuries, was examined for patients < 12 months old presenting with skeletal fractures, sentinel injuries, and head trauma.ResultsFor clinicians who completed the educational modules, 97% reported increased confidence in identifying child abuse cases, while 95% reported increased confidence in managing such cases (p<.01). For children < 6 months old with skeletal fractures there was a significant increase in skeletal surveys from 4% to 17% and for children 6-12mos an increase from 6 to 14% (ps<0.01). For children < 6 months old with sentinel injuries, the rate in skeletal surveys increased from 2% to 8% and for those with sentinel injuries or fracture the rate of skeletal surveys increased from 3% to 10% (ps<0.01). For children < = 12months old with head trauma, the rate of skeletal surveys increased from 8%-23%(ps<0.01).ConclusionOverall, the findings suggest the QI program contributed to substantial improvement in adherence to standard of care for suspected child abuse.

Experience of child abuse and neglect, collectively child maltreatment, is a significant and costly social issue [1]. In 2023, 546,159 children (0.7% of the total child US population) were victims of abuse or neglect [2], although self-reported data suggests that the true prevalence is much higher, with 12.5% of individuals experiencing maltreatment by age 18 [3]. Yet, child maltreatment is only documented in medical records in less than 1% of pediatric hospitalizations or emergency department visits, and only 0.02% in primary care visits among youth aged 0–21 years old [4]. While these data may suggest substantial under-documentation, child maltreatment is a complex issue that is subject to both under- and over-reporting. Lack of clinician education and clinical support may lend to under-reporting, leaving children in harmful environments and potential lifelong physical and psychological trauma. On the other hand, overreporting, often driven by implicit bias, can result in unnecessary investigations and emotional stress for children and their families [5–10].

Clinician knowledge and comfort with the identification and appropriate work-up of suspected abuse and neglect in pediatric medical settings can be hampered by a number of factors [6, 11]. Clinicians may have inconsistent, or incomplete training and education on how to identify abusive injuries or the appropriate assessment when encountering a suspicious injury. Sentinel injuries (i.e., poorly explained, medically minor injuries such as bruising in a non-ambulatory infant) in themselves are likely to be overlooked or unrecognized by a clinician [12]. This contributes to a ‘grey zone’ of probable abuse that relies on clinical judgement as to whether a report to child protective services is necessary. This may be more challenging for clinicians with minimal child abuse education or support tools to guide the assessment of suspicious injuries. There may also be reticence to report suspected abuse knowing the tumult that this may initiate for the child and family. Lastly, clinicians may lack clinical decision support tools that aid them in the appropriate and legally necessary steps for documentation or resources to deal with suspected abuse.

Physical abuse has a particular set of challenges for identification, as many injuries may appear minor and require further evaluation to determine nonaccidental injury. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) has set forth a standard of care to guide the assessment of suspected child physical abuse [13]. This includes the recommendation to obtain a skeletal survey (SS) in all patients aged 24 months or younger with any one of the following: obvious abusive injuries, suspicious injury (inconsistent history, injury in nonambulatory infants), or unexplained intracranial injury. While a skeletal survey is used to detect occult fractures that may be due to nonaccidental injury, a negative skeletal survey cannot rule out suspected abuse. In the absence of occult injury on imaging, the determination of nonaccidental injury again, falls to clinical judgement which incorporates the assessment of the nature and location of the injury, further physical examination, unexplained injuries, or injuries inconsistent with the explanation [13]. The decision-making around nonaccidental abuse can be aided by the availability to consult with child abuse pediatricians, particularly for difficult cases [14, 15]. Unfortunately, this support may not be available in all pediatric practices, but could substantially improve the accurate assessment of suspected abuse.

This quality improvement program aimed to address many of these challenges and improve clinician knowledge, comfort, and adherence to the recommended standard of care for suspected child abuse within a large integrated healthcare system. Figure 1 demonstrates the primary and secondary drivers targeted by this program. By increasing clinician comfort with early and appropriate identification and work-up while ensuring compliance with national standards of care, this quality improvement (QI) program had the goal of improving use of the standard of care for children with red flags for suspected abuse, thus preventing further harm to vulnerable pediatric populations. The aims of the QI project were to (1) assess improvement in clinician knowledge and comfort with identifying and assessing suspected abuse, and (2) determine the change in utilization of skeletal surveys among children presenting with injuries suspicious for physical abuse. Our SMART goals were 70% completion for clinician education, significant increase in clinician knowledge and comfort, and 10% increase in skeletal survey usage among children with sentinel injuries.

Methods

The QI program consisted of multiple components which were implemented between January, 2021 and December 31, 2024 at Kaiser Permanente Southern California, a large integrated healthcare system in Southern California. This healthcare system serves approximately 1 million pediatric members (0–18 years old) across 15 medical centers with hospitals and emergency departments, and urgent care and 233 clinics that house Pediatrics and urgent care. Healthcare is coordinated through an integrated electronic health record (EHR) system that captures comprehensive information on the healthcare patients receive at owned and contracting facilities.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The data were gathered as part of a quality improvement project in our healthcare system between 2021 and 2024 and were determined to be non-human subjects research by the Kaiser Permanente Southern California Institutional Review Board (KPSCIRB). Because of the non-human subject research determination, informed consent was waived by the KPSCIRB. Use of human subjects data was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Interventions

The multi-faceted QI program was comprised of interventions in three primary areas: (1) clinician education; (2) clinical decision support; and (3) child abuse specialist support. See Fig. 2 for timeline of components.

Clinician education

In 2021, we hosted three educational webinars, each with over 200 attendees, addressing how microaggressions and racism impact child abuse reporting. In June 2022, we launched easy-to-use, web-based educational modules focused on national standards for identifying and working up injuries that are highly suspicious for abuse in specific age groups as outlined by the Committee of Child Abuse and Neglect [13]. These include sentinel injuries such as bruising, burns, bites, and oral injuries including frenulum tears which are often minor and may be missed at the initial presentation, leading to delayed diagnosis and further harm to the child. Other more serious injuries such as long bone or skull fractures are not always identified as non-accidental and need further evaluation. Information about injuries identified as a risk for abuse were included with guidance on how to evaluate and complete a clinical work up. These modules also addressed social determinants of health and implicit bias as child abuse cases are frequently subject to implicit bias in reporting. The educational modules were initially mandated as training for Pediatricians and later for Emergency Department, and Urgent Care clinicians. In March 2022, we launched a Sharepoint site to house all standard guidelines, operational instructions, and resources for reporting suspected abuse cases.

Clinical decision support

In February 2022, a child abuse guidelines dotphrase was created (.childabuseguidelines), a feature within the EHR system. This dotphrase inserts data and text into a clinical note, providing clinicians with quick access to key considerations for non-accidental trauma evaluation guidelines. In addition, SmartSets and OrderSets were given a major update in July 2023. These are workflow efficiency tools that streamline the process of placing orders. The updates codified the standardization of appropriate imaging, lab, medication, and referral orders specific to Suspected Child Abuse or Neglect (SCAN). This ensured the use of specific orders that are medico-legally accepted for use in child welfare court and standardized the use for follow-up radiology procedures, particularly skeletal surveys as recommended by the AAP in clinical guidelines.

Child abuse specialist support

In April 2024, we formalized a partnership with two County Child Abuse Board-certified physicians to serve as per diem consultants for child abuse cases at two medical centers. The consultants managed 65 cases between April 2024 and May 2025, coordinating care between County Medical Hub clinics and our healthcare system, utilizing the EHR to follow up on imaging and leave notes for referring clinicians. Standardized telehealth workflows were implemented across the Emergency Department, Urgent Care, and Inpatient and Outpatient Pediatric departments at both medical centers, with work underway to expand this program to the entire coverage area. Due to the restricted availability of the Child Abuse Specialists and the focus of this program for regional improvement we do not present any analyses on the impact of this component.

Evaluation of the QI Program

Improvements after implementation of the QI components on clinician education and clinical support tools were based on clinician report on a pre-and post-survey as well as use of the skeletal survey procedure code pre-and post- QI implementation (pre: January 2018-June 2022; post: January 2023-June 2024).

Clinician surveys

All clinicians that completed the online education modules were asked to complete a survey before they began and then immediately after they finished the modules. The pre-training survey contained 4 questions: (1) What is your comfort level with identifying child abuse and neglect (not at all, somewhat, very); (2) What is your comfort level with doing a child abuse and neglect work-up (not at all, somewhat, very); (3) What is your comfort level with reporting child abuse and neglect (not at all, somewhat, very); (4) Do you know where to find information on [Healthcare System] guidelines regarding child abuse and neglect identification, work-up, and reporting? (yes, no). The post-training survey contained 4 questions: (1) Did the training improve your comfort level with identifying child abuse and neglect (yes, no); (2) Did the training improve your comfort level with doing a child abuse and neglect work-up (yes, no); (3) Do you know where to find information on [Healthcare System] guidelines regarding child abuse and neglect identification, work-up, and reporting (yes, no); (4) Did this training help you in your clinical practice when evaluating child abuse and neglect (yes, no).

Skeletal survey usage

Skeletal surveys are recommended by the AAP in the evaluation of suspected abuse and a key component of child abuse workups, particularly for children under 2 year old [13, 16, 17]. We were interested in testing particular presenting injuries that should trigger a skeletal survey according to the AAP standard of care for suspicious injuries. All injuries were captured using ICD-10 diagnosis codes and categories of injury were validated using King et al. [18] and Berger et al. [19] to indicate possible child maltreatment, and physical abuse in particular. We tested the rate of skeletal survey imaging for several key presenting injuries and age groups: 1) presenting injury of any type of skeletal fracture for infants (a) 7 days-<6months old) and (b) 6-12months old; 2) presenting injury of bruising, frenulum tear or subconjunctival hemorrhage (excluding subconjunctival hemorrhage in newborns < 14 days old) among infants < 6 months old; 3) presenting injury of bruising, frenulum tear, subconjunctival hemorrhage, or skeletal fracture among infants < 6 months old; 4) presenting injury of head trauma (i.e., intracranial hemorrhage, traumatic brain injuries, skull fracture) among infants < 12 months old. We excluded head contusions and closed head injury for 6–12 month olds. We focused on children < 12 months old because this is the age period with less uncertainty about suspicious injuries.

Data analyses

Reponses from the pre- and post-training surveys were examined using frequencies. We then selected those clinicians who did not feel comfortable with identification of child abuse on the pre-survey and used the McNemar test to determine the proportion that felt comfortable at the post-training survey.

For the rates of skeletal surveys for each of the four initial injury categories, we used Poisson regression to compare the pre-intervention period (January 2018-June 2022) to the post-intervention period (January 2023-June 2024). All analyses were completed using SAS 9.4.

Results

Clinician surveys

A total of 798 pediatric primary care clinicians (99% physicians, > 1% advanced practice clinicians; 71% of all pediatric clinicians) completed a web-based training comprised of 4 educational modules between June 2022 and December 2022. Of those, 590 also completed pre- and post-training surveys assessing their comfort level in identifying and managing suspected child abuse.

Among 725 clinicians that completed the pre-training survey, 4.6% did not feel comfortable identifying child abuse and neglect, 69.2% felt somewhat comfortable, and 26.2% felt very comfortable. For comfort doing a child abuse work up, 19.3% did not feel comfortable, 62.6% felt somewhat comfortable, and 18.1% felt very comfortable. Regarding comfort with reporting procedures, 9% did not feel comfortable, 49.2% felt somewhat comfortable, and 41.8% felt very comfortable. Finally, 43.9% did not know where to find information on guidelines for identification, work-up, and reporting, whereas 56.1% did.

Among those clinicians that completed both the pre-and post-training surveys (n = 590), pre-training only 25% of respondents reported being very comfortable identifying child abuse and neglect. Post-training, 97% of those who initially felt “not comfortable” reported improved comfort (McNemar test, p<.01). Similarly, pre-training, only 18% of respondents were very comfortable completing a work-up for child abuse and neglect, with 95% of those initially “not comfortable” reporting improved comfort post-training (McNemar test, p<.001) (see Table 1).

Skeletal survey rates

Results showed statistically significant increases in skeletal survey rates across all types of initial injuries (see Tables 2 and 3). For children < 6 months old with skeletal fractures there was an increase in skeletal survey rates from 4% to 17% (RR = 3.90; 95%CI 2.40, 6.34; p<.001) and for children 6-12 months the rate increased from 6 to 14% (RR = 2.22; 95%CI 1.43, 3.46; p<.001). For children < 6 months old with sentinel injuries, the rate in skeletal surveys increased from 2% to 8% (RR = 3.85; 95%CI 2.08, 7.14; p<.001), for those with sentinel injuries or fracture the rate of skeletal surveys increased from 3% to 10% (RR = 3.76; 95%CI 22.50, 5.65; p<.00). For children < = 12months old with head trauma, the rate of skeletal surveys increased from 8% to 23% (RR = 2.86; 95%CI 1.96, 4.17; p<.001).

Discussion

This multi-component QI program sought to improve clinician knowledge, comfort and adherence to standard of care for suspected child abuse and neglect. Children who are victims of one episode of child abuse have a 10% mortality risk when returned to an abusive environment, while victims of recurrent abuse have a 25% mortality risk [20]. Physicians hold a critical role as professionals with the opportunity to intervene and change the trajectory of these children’s lives [21]. The results of this evaluation indicated a significant increase in clinician comfort with the identification and work-up of suspected child abuse and neglect as well and in the usage of skeletal surveys for young children (< 12 months) who presented with suspicious injuries.

Among clinicians that initially reported that they did not feel comfortable identifying or completing a work-up for suspected child abuse, nearly all reported an improvement in their comfort level. In a survey of 520 randomly selected AAP members, the majority had little experience evaluating and reporting suspected abuse [22]. In conjunction with our findings, this demonstrates that clinician education is a critical component of supporting clinicians in making these important clinical decisions. Other QI projects have also shown improvements in risk assessment with standardized toolkits for screening and resources [23].

With regard to completing the recommended work-up for suspicious injuries, the decision support tools made it very easy to order the appropriate tests, particularly a skeletal survey which is recommended by the AAP as the standard of care for suspected abuse [13]. While small, the significant increases in our skeletal survey rates signify a crucial step towards ensuring appropriate workups in identifying potential abuse cases that might otherwise be missed, leading to more timely interventions and improved outcomes for vulnerable children. We found increases in use of skeletal surveys between 5% and 15%, although overall rates were still relatively low. A QI project at Rady’s Children Hospital San Diego found that ordered/planned skeletal surveys among children < 24 months with nonaccidental injury increased by 17% (change from 74% to 91%) [24]. Unfortunately, our initial data shown that there was extremely low use of skeletal surveys, but the positive news is that our interventions improved skeletal survey use for all presenting injuries. The low rates may be due to the fact that none of the presenting injuries are in themselves pathognomonic for abuse, although in infants they do indicate a high risk for abuse [13]. While these presenting injuries should lead to further evaluations including skeletal survey, the optimal skeletal survey rate for each presenting injury remains a topic of investigation as evidence suggests occult fractures are noted on skeletal surveys for only 1–6% of infants with skull fracture but 23–34% with significant head injury [25]. However, it is clear that continued work is necessary to ensure the use of the educational modules, clinical support tools, and child abuse specialists. In addition, while the AAP has outlined recommendations for the evaluation of suspected child abuse, a recent study showed substantial variation across US children’s hospitals in the protocols used to assess suspicious injuries [26], suggesting further efforts are needed to standardize tools and practices for evaluation of suspected child abuse across the US.

As shown in a recent analysis of over 700 skeletal surveys, in over half of the positive cases of occult skeletal trauma the finding influenced the diagnosis of abuse [13]. In this way skeletal surveys can confirm some suspected abuse. However negative findings on the skeletal survey cannot rule out abuse, and clinical assessment becomes key to the determination. Support from child abuse specialists can help in these more challenging cases [15]. Our goal of early and appropriate care management of suspected abuse dovetails with a statewide collaborative in Ohio that found within 2 years of their initiative, which included education resources for clinicians as well as updated clinical decision support tools, reinjury rates significantly decreased [27]. Although we did not assess reinjury rates, our expectation is that the increased adherence to the standard of care and timely intervention will reduce recurrent abuse. This is an important avenue for future assessment.

Overall, this program was determined to be successful within our healthcare system and implementation is planned for additional departments. However, there are some limitations to note. The program components were introduced over nearly a 2-year period, potentially reducing their impact as the webinars in 2021 became more distant. In addition, there was no assessment after each of the components so we cannot determine which one was responsible for improvements. Second, the increased use of skeletal surveys primarily captures only one type of maltreatment, physical abuse, and we do not have good measures for improvement in appropriate work-ups for sexual abuse, emotional abuse, or neglect. The training modules did provide information about signs of emotional abuse, sexual abuse, and neglect, but it is more difficult to examine a metric of standardized follow-up care for those types. We did track overall child maltreatment diagnoses to see if they increased after the interventions, but we did not see any appreciable change. This is likely because we are addressing both the issues of under- and over-reporting. We will likely need continued refreshers for our educational modules so that the information stays in the forefront as clinical clinicians see patients. Lastly, we did not evaluate the component of adding Child Abuse specialists. This was primarily because they were limited to only two medical centers and the caseload was too low to test improvments. In the future we hope to show the benefit of these specialists as they are made available to our entire healthcare system.

Conclusion

This quality improvement program encompassed a multi-component approach by integrating ongoing education and clinical decision support tools to improve the use of the standard of care for suspected child abuse. This program transitioned the care of child abuse from a case-by-case approach, which relied heavily on individual clinical judgment, to a proactive, standardized, system-supported process incorporating specialists to provide additional expertise in the management of child abuse cases. Unlike previous efforts in our healthcare system that were led by individual abuse champions in each medical center, which were smaller in scale and only focused on one initiative at a time, this regionally led comprehensive program addressed multiple facets simultaneously, ensuring a more robust and consistent approach to improve adherence to standard of care, and treatment of our vulnerable pediatric population.
