Computed tomography‐based radiomics and clinical‐genetic features for brain metastasis prediction in patients with stage III/IV epidermal growth factor receptor‐mutant non‐small‐cell lung cancer
Authors: Mei Zheng, Xiaorong Sun, Haoran Qi, Mingzhu Zhang, Ligang Xing
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the value of computed tomography (CT)‐based radiomics combined with clinical‐genetic features in predicting brain metastasis in patients with stage III/IV epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)‐mutant non‐small‐cell lung cancer (NSCLC).MethodsThe study included 147 eligible patients treated at our institution between January 2018 and May 2021. Patients were randomly divided into two cohorts for model training (n = 102) and validation (n = 45). Radiomics features were extracted from the chest CT images before treatment, and a radiomics signature was constructed using the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator regression. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was used to describe the differences in brain metastasis‐free survival (BM‐FS) risk. A clinical‐genetic model was developed using Cox regression analysis. Radiomics, genetic, and combined prediction models were constructed, and their predictive performances were evaluated by the concordance index (C‐index).ResultsPatients with a low radiomics score had significantly longer BM‐FS than those with a high radiomics score in both the training (p < 0.0001) and the validation (p = 0.0016) cohorts. The C‐indices of the nomogram, which combined the radiomics signature and N stage, overall stage, third‐generation tyrosine kinase inhibitor treatment, and EGFR mutation status, were 0.886 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.823–0.949) and 0.811 (95% CI 0.719–0.903) in the training and validation cohorts, respectively. The combined model achieved a higher discrimination and clinical utility than the single prediction models.ConclusionsThe combined radiomics‐genetic model could be used to predict BM‐FS in stage III/IV NSCLC patients with EGFR mutations.Keywords: brain metastasis, epidermal growth factor receptor, non‐small‐cell lung cancer, prediction model, radiomics

INTRODUCTION

Brain metastasis (BM) is the most common site of spread in non‐small‐cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and occurs in about 50% of patients with advanced NSCLC. 1 Patients with epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations are more at risk of developing BM. About 40%–50% of advanced NSCLC cases in Asia have EGFR mutations, 2 and approximately 39%–63% of patients diagnosed with NSCLC with EGFR mutations present with BM. Moreover, patients with EGFR mutations also have a higher lifetime risk of developing BM than patients with wild‐type EGFR. 3 , 4 Before the development of targeted therapies, the median survival time of NSCLC patients with BM ranged between 3 and 6 months, and about 40%–50% of these patients died from BM. 5 Our research and that of other teams found that the first‐line application of EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) can significantly reduce the incidence of BM, delay the onset of BM, and prolong the median progression‐free survival (PFS) in patients with BM. 6 , 7 However, patients treated with targeted therapies often develop drug resistance. As a result, NSCLC patients resistant to first‐generation TKIs continue to have poor survival, with a median PFS of only 8.4 months, even after switching to third‐generation TKIs. 8 Therefore, the prognosis of patients with advanced NSCLC with EGFR mutations may be improved by predicting the risk of developing BM and via the provision of timely targeted therapies.

Radiomics involves the high‐throughput extraction of numerous features from radiographic images and could be used to capture intra‐tumoral heterogeneity in a non‐invasive way. 9 However, previous studies have also shown that various clinical and genomic features could also be used to predict the incidence of BM in NSCLC. 10 , 11 In addition, several biomarkers based on radiomics signatures have been reported to correlate with clinical outcomes and underlying genomic patterns. 12 In recent years, the value of radiomics features in predicting tumor progression and distant metastasis in NSCLC has been investigated. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 However, it remains uncertain if these radiomics features, in conjunction with other clinical‐genetic features, could improve the prediction accuracy for the development of BM in stage III/IV NSCLC patients with EGFR mutations.

In this retrospective study, we aimed to explore whether the combination of a radiomics signature from computed tomography (CT) images with clinical‐genetic features could improve the prediction accuracy for the development of BM in stage III/IV NSCLC patients with EGFR mutations.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients

The workflow of this study is shown in Figure 1. This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of our institution and waived the requirement of acquiring informed consent from patients. We collected 373 patients with pathologically confirmed stage III/IV NSCLC with EGFR mutations treated at our institute between January 2018 and May 2021. The inclusion criteria were as follows: pathologically confirmed stage III–IV NSCLC (the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM Staging Manual, 8th Edition); diagnosed with EGFR mutation via targeted next‐generation sequencing (NGS); patients without other neoplastic diseases; available pretreatment thoracic CT images on the picture archiving and communication system performed less than 1 month before the pathologic sampling were collected; and availability of a CT, magnetic resonance image (MRI) or positron emission tomography/computed tomography of the brain acquired at the time of diagnosis and follow‐up to confirm the status of BMs. The exclusion criteria were as follows: BM at initial diagnosis; multiple lesions; unsatisfactory resolution of the thoracic CT images, and lung lesions smaller than 1 cm, which did not allow for accurate tumor delineation and feature extraction; loss to follow‐up. The patients were randomly allocated in a 7:3 ratio to the training and validation cohorts.

The workflow of this study. CT, computed tomography; EGFR, epidermal growth factor receptor; ICC, intraclass correlation coefficient; LASSO, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator; NSCLC, non‐small‐cell lung cancer.

The primary outcome of the study was brain metastasis‐free survival (BM‐FS), defined as the time from the diagnosis of NSCLC to the date of BM diagnosis or the last known date when the patients were brain metastasis‐free. The follow‐up deadline was until May 2023. Patients were followed up with cranial MRI/CT every 3–6 months after diagnosis of NSCLC for the first 2 years and then annually or until symptoms develop. The first 2‐year follow‐up was selected because previous studies have shown that the median time from diagnosis of locally advanced NSCLC to first failure in the brain was 12 months, and most patients developed BM within 2 years of diagnosis. 17 , 18 The baseline clinical characteristics, including gender, age, smoking history, pathological type, AJCC pathological stage (T stage, N stage, and overall stage), third‐generation TKI treatment, and chemotherapy, were extracted from the electronic medical records. Mutation analyses of EGFR and tumor protein P53 (TP53) were performed via targeted NGS.

CT acquisition and segmentation

Pretreatment diagnostic chest contrast‐enhanced CT images were acquired with a 64‐multidetector CT scanner (Definition AS+, Siemens SOMATOM) using a slice thickness of 5.0 mm, a tube voltage of 120 kVp, and a tube current‐exposure time product of 160 mAs. All images were reconstructed with a sharp kernel (b60) and were displayed using the standard lung (width 1600 HU, level −600 HU) and mediastinum (width 400 HU, level 40 HU) window settings. The contrast‐enhanced CT images were acquired after the injection of an intravenous bolus dose (1.5–2 mL/kg) of iodinated contrast agent (300 mg/mL) into the antecubital vein at a rate of 2.0 mL/s via a high‐pressure syringe.

The CT images were imported into the open‐source software 3D Slicer (version 5.0.3, https://www.slicer.org) and saved in the Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine format. A region of interest (ROI) encompassing the primary lung tumor was delineated slice by slice on the enhanced CT images by an experienced radiologist, and subsequently confirmed by a senior radiologist. When encountering disagreements, the senior radiologist guided the segmentation to completion. The lung window was used to identify the tumor–lung borders, while the mediastinal window was used to outline the tumor regions adjacent to soft tissues. The ROIs delineated on each CT slice were used to construct the volume of interest (VOI).

Feature extraction

Radiomics features were extracted for each VOI using the SlicerRadiomics extension of the 3D Slicer software. The extracted radiomics features were classified into first‐order, shape, texture, and wavelet‐transformed features. The first‐order features describe the voxel intensity distribution within the VOI, and they are typically calculated based on intensity histograms. Shape features describe the spatial characteristics of the VOI itself, such as volume and sphericity. Texture features describe the spatial relationship of voxel intensity, including gray‐level cooccurrence matrix (GLCM), gray‐level dependence matrix, gray‐level run length matrix (GLRLM), gray‐level size zone matrix, and neighborhood gray‐tone difference matrix. Wavelet‐transformed features can be extracted after a series of wavelet filtration is applied to the images. The wavelet transform decomposes the original image into low and‐high frequencies, focusing on the features on different frequency ranges within the tumor volume. 19

Feature selection and signature construction

The features were first standardized by Z‐score normalization to reduce the bias caused by different index dimensions. Subsequently, 20 patients were randomly selected for VOI segmentation, and the radiomics features were extracted again. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated to assess the intra‐observer and inter‐observer reproducibility of extracted radiomics features. Features with an ICC above 0.8 were selected for further feature selection. Spearman's correlation analysis was performed and in cases where the correlation coefficient between two features exceeded 0.9, only one of the features was included in the analysis. This step aimed to mitigate the influence of highly correlated variables and enhance the interpretability and stability of the subsequent analyses. Finally, the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression analysis with 10‐fold cross‐validation was utilized to select the optimal radiomics features in the training cohort. The area under the curve (AUC) of a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to calculate the prediction accuracy of the radiomics signature. The radiomics score (rad‐score) for each patient was calculated to reflect the risk of BM by weighting the linear combination of the selected features by the regression coefficients. The patients were then divided into high‐ and low‐risk groups according to the cut‐off value of the ROC curve of the training cohort. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was used to assess the correlation between the radiomics signature and BM‐FS.

Clinical‐genetic characteristics selection

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were used to identify the clinical‐genetic risk factors associated with BM‐FS. For multivariable analysis, a variable selection method with selection criteria of p ≤ 0.1 was considered, and interactions among features significant at 0.05 were also included.

Construction and validation of models

BM‐FS prediction nomogram based on combined radiomics‐clinical‐genetic (R + C + G) features was developed for the training cohort. The performance and extensibility of the models were verified in the validation cohort. In both the training and validation cohorts, the prediction accuracy of the models was quantified by the Harrell concordance index (C‐index). The calibration curves were used to evaluate the consistency between the predicted and actual results for BM‐FS. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was used to evaluate the net clinical benefit of the models under different threshold probabilities.

Statistical analyses

The statistical analyses were performed using the software R (version 4.2.1, https://www.r-project.org), IBM SPSS (version 25.0, https://www.ibm.com), and MedCalc (version 22.009). Variations in the clinical characteristics between the training and validation cohorts were assessed using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test. SPSS software was used to perform the chi‐square test, Fisher's exact test, and Cox regression analysis. MedCalc software was used to determine the ROC cut‐off value for the Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and perform the comparison of the ROC curves among three prediction models. Nomogram, DCA, and calibration curves were presented using R software. The “glmnet” package in the R software was used for the LASSO regression analysis.

A total of 851 radiomics features were extracted from the CT images. The inter‐ and intragroup correlation analyses revealed 637 features with outstanding stability (ICC > 0.8). After excluding the highly correlated features (Spearman correlation >0.9), 189 related radiomics features were retained. Finally, the 14 best features were selected by the LASSO regression analysis (Figure 2a) and were used to build the radiomics signature. The radiomics signature on the training cohort achieved AUCs of 0.816 and 0.780 on the training and validation cohorts, respectively (Figure 2b). Construction and validation of models

The developed R + C + G nomogram is shown in Figure. 3a. The C‐index in the training cohort was highest for the R + C + G model (C‐index 0.886, 95% CI 0.823–0.949), followed by those of the radiomics (R) model (C‐index 0.829, 95% CI 0.746–0.912) and clinical‐genetic (C + G) model (C‐index 0.758, 95% CI 0.663–0.853). In the validation cohort, the C‐index was highest for the R + C + G model (C‐index 0.811, 95% CI 0.719–0.903), followed by those of the C + G model (C‐index 0.746, 95% CI 0.615–0.877) and the R model (C‐index 0.710, 95% CI 0.553–0.867).

DISCUSSION

BM is a common cause of mortality in patients with NSCLC with EGFR mutations. Predicting the risk of developing BM could enable clinicians to tailor the follow‐up procedure and hence facilitate the delivery of earlier interventions such as TKIs. Numerous radiomics and clinical features have been linked with the development of BM. 16 , 20 , 21 , 22

Rahman et al. 11 developed a clinical and genomic model to predict BM in non‐early NSCLC and obtained reasonable predictive performance. Liao et al. 23 used a comprehensive model based on radiomics features and EGFR status to effectively predict the risk of death after radiosurgery in lung cancer patients with BM. However, to our knowledge, few models based on radiomics and clinical‐genetic features are currently available to predict BM in advanced NSCLC patients with EGFR mutations. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to evaluate whether a nomogram based on radiomics and clinical‐genetic features could improve the prediction of BM‐FS in stage III/IV NSCLC patients with EGFR mutations in relation to models based solely on radiomics and clinical‐genetic features.

We developed a 14‐feature radiomics signature based on shape, texture, and wavelet‐transformed features extracted from CT images. Studies have shown that among all radiomics features, texture features are most closely related to tumor heterogeneity and prognosis, while wavelet‐transformed texture features have been associated with early BM in lung cancer patients with specific driver gene mutations. 14 Moreover, shape, GLRLM, and GLCM were associated with EGFR mutations in NSCLC, 24 while GLCM and ClusterShade have been identified as good predictors for distant metastasis in NSCLC. 25 , 26 These features could be used to determine the risk of developing distant metastasis, including BM in NSCLC with EGFR mutations. However, further studies are needed to explore the scalability and general applicability of these radiomics features in NSCLC with mutations in other driver genes or distant metastases in other sites.

Our results showed that the AJCC pathological N stage and overall stage, third‐generation TKI treatment, and EGFR mutation status were independent risk factors for BM. Lymph node metastasis and distant metastasis are known prognostic factors for advanced lung cancer. 21 , 27 , 28 EGFR mutations have also been reported as a potential risk factor for BM, 29 and PFS and survival were better in the del19 mutant group than in the L858R group. 30 , 31 Studies have also shown a significantly longer median PFS and overall survival (OS) in patients treated with osimertinib as first‐line therapy compared with patients treated with first‐line EGFR‐TKIs. 32 , 33 Osimertinib also demonstrated an advantage over first‐ and second‐generation TKIs in treating and preventing BM. 34

The model based on the radiomics signature achieved a high C‐index (0.829) in the training cohort. The radiomics signature successfully divided patients into high‐ and low‐risk BM groups. However, with the addition of the four clinical‐genetic features (N stage, overall stage, third‐generation TKI treatment, EGFR mutation status), the model's prediction ability improved significantly (C‐index 0.886). The DCA also confirmed that the combined radiomics‐genetic model had a higher clinical utility than the other two models. This was also confirmed in the validation cohort.

Our study had several limitations that had to be acknowledged. First, this study was based on a small dataset from a single institute, thus limiting the generalizability of the research findings, therefore further prospective multicenter studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate the model's prediction accuracy. Second, the study was retrospective, therefore there may be some bias in the selection of patients. The standard protocol for enhanced CT imaging at our institution used a slice thickness of 5.0 mm due to resource constraints, although thinner slices provide more detailed image data. Future studies could consider evaluating the impact of varying CT imaging slice thicknesses on the outcomes of radiomics analysis. Finally, the radiomics features were assessed at a single point in time. However, the radiomics features can change (delta radiomics) as the disease progresses or following treatment, therefore further research is required to assess whether delta radiomics may have a higher predictive value for tumor heterogeneity and BM than features extracted at a single point in time.

CONCLUSION

In this study, we established a CT image‐based radiomics signature for the stratification of BM risk in patients with stage III/IV NSCLC with EGFR mutations. The nomogram based on radiomics and clinical‐genetic features may help clinically guide the development of personalized treatment plans to reduce the risk of developing BM and improve survival.
